Knowledge associated with attitudes and practice in the management of post-prostatectomy erectile dysfunction: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Can Zhu, Hao Geng, Wei Zhang, Tao Zhou, Chengxiang Liu, Qinglin Ye, Xiansheng Zhang, Ci Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe authors investigated the knowledge, attitudes, and practices about erectile dysfunction (ED) after radical prostatectomy (RP) among urologists and andrologists.MethodsThis study invited urologists and andrologists from more than 150 medical centers across China to participate in this cross-sectional survey. We used an anonymous and web-based research database and a quota sampling approach. Questionnaires were distributed and analyzed from December 2023 to March 2024.ResultsIn total, 921 participants were eligible for this study. Among eligible participants, 476 participants [51.7%] scored at least 6 points of a total score of 10 points in the knowledge test, and 650 participants [70.6%] of the participants scored at least 6 points in the attitude test. Among 476 participants who scored at least 6 points, 402 participants [84.5%] were cognizant of the clinical guidelines of ED and implemented them in clinical practice; 153 participants [32.1%] assessed the patient's erectile dysfunction before performing radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer; 91 participants [19.1%] utilized the technique of preserving the neurovascular bundles during radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer, which were significantly higher than rates among 445 participants with scoring less than 6 points (147 participants [33.0%]; 45 participants [10.1%]; 24 participants [5.4%]). The most significant challenges in the management of ED after RP were lack of sufficient experience (58.7%), deficiency in knowledge within the domain (53.9%), and rarity of effective treatments and medications (41.2%).ConclusionPhysicians generally possess insufficient knowledge about ED after RP. Insufficient sexual health knowledge is closely associated with the attitudes and practice patterns of physicians. The lack of understanding and experience about ED among physicians may be a key reason why patients struggle to receive comprehensive sexual education and penile rehabilitation services after RP. Physicians should undergo more in-depth education in sexual medicine to sufficiently respond to the escalating professional demands in the field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01967-5.

Introduction

In 2020, there were more than 1.4 million new cases of prostate cancer and more than 370,000 cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. The incidence and mortality of prostate cancer are relatively low but growing rapidly in China. Affected by many socio-economic factors, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing has not been extensively adopted by patients in China. Coupled with the disparities in tumor biology [2], the incidence of prostate cancer and high grade prostate cancer was lower in the Chinese cohort than the Western cohorts at any given prostate specific antigen level. This situation increased the metastasis rate at the time of prostate cancer diagnosis in China, with 42% of patients presenting with metastatic cancer [3, 4]. Nevertheless, a comprehensive meta-analysis, pooling data from 17 population-based cancer registries across the country, has demonstrated that the five-year survival rate between 2012 and 2015 was estimated to be 66.4% [5]. Prostate cancer is recognized for its generally favorable prognosis, and localized disease can be cured by RP. However, with the increase in long-term survival rates, RP has been found to substantially undermine patients' quality of life, commonly leading to ED and urinary system complications. In China, RP is considered the primary treatment for patients with prostate cancer [6]. ED, a prevalent postoperative complication, postoperative ED is widely recognized as inevitable in the majority of cases, with an incidence ranging from 14 to 82% [7–12].

The spectrum of penile rehabilitation strategies after RP predominantly features the administration of oral phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5I), vacuum erection devices (VEDs), intracavernosal injection (ICI) therapy, medicated urethral erection systems (MUSE), and the combinations of these therapeutic approaches [9]. When the aforementioned treatments are ineffective, penile prosthesis implantation is considered a definitive treatment that a patient may wish to undergo [13]. Worldwide, RP constitutes the second most frequent indication for implanting penile prosthesis, with an increasing trend in implant procedures [14]. Despite the absence of exact figures, an upward trajectory in prosthesis implantation can be observed in China. Nonetheless, this trend does not correspond to an upsurge in penile prosthesis implantation after RP. The divergence in these trends is multifactorial and affected by the traditional cultural factors of China and the high medical expenses incurred by prosthesis implantation [15]. Moreover, the absence of sufficient patient education is a pivotal factor. It is not uncommon for patients and their partners to anticipate the immediate restoration of effective erections after initiating penile rehabilitation; however, the disappointment due to the failure of initial treatments can lead to emotional desolation [16]. ED is a common sequela of RP, yet this issue has not garnered enough attention. A study with 331 patients with localized prostate cancer, 69% of whom were subjected to RP, highlighted significant unmet needs. Within a year after diagnosis, 82% of patients needed supportive care that remained unfulfilled. Diminished quality of life and the presence of psychological distress were the main concerns. Moreover, sexual needs were most frequently unmet, with 58% of patients reporting this issue. This prevalence suggests that ED, particularly after radical surgical intervention for prostate cancer, can undermine the quality of life of patients [17]. However, there is a significant lack of sex education and penile rehabilitation for patients with prostate cancer after surgery [18]. Furthermore, the leading role of physicians in care and rehabilitation is extremely limited. One of the major reasons for this phenomenon may be the lack of adequate sexual knowledge, inactive attitudes, and the lack of practical experience among urologists and andrologists.

This multicenter study aimed to provide a representative survey of the sexual knowledge, attitudes, and practices of radical prostate cancer surgery among urologists and andrologists from more than 150 hospitals in China.

Methods

A multicenter cross-sectional study of urologists and andrologists was conducted between December 2023 and March 2024 in 31 provinces of China. This study was approved by the independent Ethics Committee of the coordinating centers (SL-YX2024-048). We surveyed all invited urologists and andrologists using a secure, anonymous, and web-based research database (Wenjuan Star, free version; Changsha Ranxing Information Technology). The survey questionnaire was first released on December 6th, 2023, and was accompanied by an invitation letter explaining the topic and purpose of this study. There was also a URL link to the web-based questionnaire. The purpose of this survey in the invitation letter was stated as "A survey of Chinese urologists and andrologists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding erectile dysfunction after radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer". To increase the response rate, an electronic reminder was sent to non-respondents three to seven days after the initial invitation.

The preliminary questionnaire was developed under the guidance of the Chinese Anti-Cancer Society and following the European Association of Urology's Sexual and Reproductive Health Guidelines, the American Urological Association's ED Guidelines, and the European Association of Urology's Prostate Cancer Guidelines. Before the formal survey, the early questionnaire was preliminarily tested to assess its flow, content, validity, and clinical utility. The questionnaire was divided into four main sections(Supplement 1): (1) basic information about the responders (10 items), (2) knowledge of ED after RP for prostate cancer (10 items), (3) attitudes toward the treatment of ED after RP for prostate cancer (5 items) (4) practice of treating ED after RP for prostate cancer (6 items). To quantitatively explore the associations among knowledge, attitudes, and practice patterns related to the management of post-prostatectomy erectile dysfunction, a 10-point system was adopted to score for the knowledge and attitudes sections, and a 60% threshold for the knowledge and attitudes tests was adopted. The detailed scoring method is described in the Supplement 1. After finalizing the questionnaire, 10 urologists and andrologists were invited to complete the survey to assess minimum time to complete the questionnaire, and the minimum time was determined to be 2 min.

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the characteristics of participants. One-way ANOVA and independent samples t-tests were employed to verify whether there was a significant effect of sample characteristics on the total score of each scale. Mann–Whitney U tests (for pairwise comparisons) or Kruskal–Wallis tests (for multi-group analyses) were utilized instead of parametric alternatives to verify significant effects of sample characteristics on ordinal/non-normally distributed scale scores, with post-hoc Dunn's tests incorporating Bonferroni correction where applicable. Statistical significance was set at p < 0 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

to produce the mosaics and explore the relationship between knowledge and practice patterns, we selected four representative practice patterns (P1 P2 P4 P6) and categorized the knowledge scores into four groups (0–2.5; 2.5–5.0; 5.0–7.5; 7.5–10).

Participants who met all of the following criteria were included in the analysis: 1) licensed physicians specializing in urology or andrology; 2) providing direct patient care in the eligible specialties; and 3) completing all items in our survey using more than 2 min. Participants were excluded if they shared the same IP address, did not complete the questionnaire, completed the questionnaire in less than 2 min, or were graduate students or interns. Initially, 2683 urologists and andrologists were invited to participate in this survey, and 1052 participants responded. However, 131 respondents were excluded, including 88 individuals who answered the questionnaire in less than 2 min, 23 individuals who no longer worked in clinical practice, and 20 individuals who were graduate students or interns.

Results

In total, 921 eligible urologists and andrologists were included in the final analysis, with a response rate of 34.9%. Among all respondents, 900 (97.7%) were male and 21 (2.3%) were female. In addition, 486 participants (49.4%) were aged 30 to 45 years. Regarding the attitude test, 650 urologists and andrologists (70.6%) scored at least 6 points out of 10 points. In total, 921 eligible participants were classified into 2 groups with a cutoff value of 6 points. Participants who scored at least 6 were categorized as having positive attitudes toward the diagnosis and treatment of ED after prostate cancer. Conversely, a score of fewer than 6 indicated a lack of positive attitudes. Significant differences existed between participants who scored 6 or more points and those who scored less than 6 points in terms of age, practice setting, time in practice, percentage of patients in practice with ED, and offering andrology subspecialty. For instance, participants with longer practice were more likely to have attitude scores of 6 or more (< 5: 37 participants [13.7%] vs. 66 participants [10.2%]; 5 ~ 10: 63 participants [23.2%] vs. 102 participants [15.7%]; 10 ~ 15: 61 participants [22.5%] vs. 129 participants [19.8%]; > 15 110 participants [40.6%] vs. 353 participants [54.3%] P < 0.001). Similarly, on the knowledge test, participants who scored at least 6 had a good understanding of ED after RP, while those who scored fewer than 6 lacked the necessary knowledge. Except for gender and subspecialty, all subgroups showed significant differences in the knowledge testing statistics (P < 0.001). Demographic characteristics of respondents

To analyze the association between attitudes and knowledge, we compared attitude scores and specific answers between groups scored at least 6 points the knowledge test and scored fewer than 6 points of a total score of 10 points the knowledge test. In the latter group, 247 participants (55.5%) passed the attitude test, which was significantly lower than that in the group with 6 scores (403 participants [84.7%]; P < 0.001). More than 60% of participants in both groups agreed that "patients with sexual health-related problems are recommended to consult an andrologist". Significant differences were found in all the remaining four attitude questions. Interestingly, 254 (53.3%) of participants with at least 6 points of knowledge test agreed that sexual health issues were not important or should be a priority. In contrast, 168 (37.7%) of participants with a poor knowledge score agreed that sexual health issues were not important or should be a priority. Associations between attitude and knowledge among respondents

We then compared the distribution of the groups' responses to each attitudinal question and found significant differences across the six practice patterns. As shown in Fig. 1, there was a significant positive correlation between the four groups in terms of being familiar with ED guidelines and adopting clinical practice following the ED guidelines. Almost all participants with knowledge scores greater than 7.5 clearly understood and followed clinical practice guidelines. Compared to other subgroups, a markedly greater proportion of participants with knowledge scores greater than 7.5 chose that all patients should be assessed for sexual function before prostate cancer surgery (Fig. 2). Discussion

In the knowledge test, this survey indicated that only 51.7% of urologists and andrologists scored at least 6 points of a total score of 10 points (≥ 6), a figure consistent with the outcomes reported by Tang et al. [19]. These results imply a deficiency in physicians' understanding of ED subsequent to RP. Physicians with higher knowledge scores are more likely to have better practice. Therefore, high-quality and continuous education programs should be made available to Chinese urologists and andrologists to address this knowledge gap.

A significant proportion of Chinese physicians with scoring at least 6 points of a total score of 10 points consider sexual health issues as less critical or not a priority (53%), compared to 37.7% of those with poor knowledge scores. This perception may reflect a tendency among Chinese urologists and andrologists to prioritize the prolongation of life over the quality of life in the context of prostate cancer, a perspective originating from traditional Chinese cultural and societal beliefs. In this cultural framework, ED is often an underemphasized aspect of healthcare. Based on traditional Chinese norms, masculinity is characterized by professional success, honor, autonomy, and family roles, rather than being characterized by an active sex life or physical attractiveness [20]. Treatment of ED markedly affects individuals' identity and social standing. The misconception is that ED is not an important life-threatening problem after RP. Not only patients but also many urologists and andrologists believe that the importance of ED is significantly undermined by an innate fear of tumors and an excessive focus on tumor control. Furthermore, Chinese men consider ED to be undignified, and discussing ED can be embarrassing for physicians and patients [20]. Consequently, Chinese men are unlikely to discuss their ED with others. However, if they discuss their sexual health issues, they prefer to talk to a friend or traditional healer rather than a medical professional [21]. Therefore, both physicians and patients need to be adequately educated about sexual health, and there is a long way to develop effective and personalized healthcare programs based on traditional culture, with the joint efforts of the government, societies, physicians, and media [22].

It is generally accepted that for a successful treatment of ED, comprehensive education should be provided to couples who want to treat ED. Redefining and exploring intimate relationships and sexual activities while not achieving insertion is necessary for couples coping with ED. Studies have highlighted the positive aspects of newly established intimacy in relationships where the capacity for penetrative sex has been compromised. These findings imply that individuals suffering from prostate cancer can significantly benefit from psychosexual education, potentially enhancing the success of penile rehabilitation and facilitating adaptation to altered sexual behaviors [16]. In China, a small proportion of patients with prostate cancer attend follow-up consultations regarding ED, and an even smaller subset receives penile rehabilitation. This observation indicates a deficiency in the sexual education and support provided to patients in the postoperative period. It is also imperative to assess the lack of sexual knowledge among urologists and andrologists and evaluate their perceptions and attitudes toward sexuality.

Physicians are often at the forefront of clinical practice, with their extensive knowledge about sexuality, positive attitudes, and effective communication skills being crucial for promoting the recovery of postoperative ED. A study with over 500 patients, 93% of whom underwent RP, indicated that those who developed ED after surgery were more likely to seek treatment from their treating specialists [23]. It has been observed that education in sexual medicine is insufficient in many countries, a gap that extends to urology residents who will conduct RP in the future, as well as some specialists in the field of sexual medicine [24–26]. It was reported that only approximately 38–43% of urologists recommended penile rehabilitation for all patients after surgery [25, 27], selective recommendations were made by roughly half of the physicians, either for patients with robust preoperative erectile function or upon patients’ request [28].

This survey revealed a significant correlation between the sexual knowledge and attitudes of Chinese urologists and andrologists and their clinical practices in managing ED associated with RP. It also illuminated a notable deficiency in experience and knowledge about sexuality, along with insufficient effective treatments and medications, as the main challenges in addressing ED. Given the scarcity of sexual health education and penile rehabilitation for patients after prostate cancer surgery, continuous education in sexual medicine is imperative for urologists and andrologists. We aimed to establish a collaborative model led by urology and andrology experts, in conjunction with an interdisciplinary team, including oncologists, psychotherapists, and physiotherapists, to implement a patient-centered, personalized care plan. This model can provide sexual health education and penile rehabilitation to all patients who undergo RP, regardless of their sexual activity status.

It should be noted that this survey had certain limitations. Primarily, the survey adopted a quota sampling approach, potentially leading to sampling errors. Secondly, the response rate of 34.9% was comparatively low, raising concerns about potential response bias. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that certain Chinese andrologists also function as internists, lacking specialization in prostate cancer management, which limits the accuracy of their responses in reflecting the real clinical scenario. Finally, it is critical to recognize that the selected survey questions only provide a partial representation of the clinical landscape. Hence, whether these findings can accurately reflect the comprehensive sexual knowledge, attitudes, and practice of urologists and andrologists dealing with ED after prostate cancer remains to be validated.

Conclusion

The results revealed a general deficiency among Chinese urologists and andrologists in knowledge about ED after RP. This gap in sexual health knowledge was correlated with the attitudes and practice patterns. The insufficient understanding of sexual health and insufficient relevant clinical experience among these specialists contribute to the absence of comprehensive sexual education and penile rehabilitation programs for patients after RP. Therefore, urologists and andrologists should engage in collaborative sexual medicine education with their patients, tailored to address the sexual health needs of RP patients. Such an approach is essential for promoting long-term survival and quality of life.
