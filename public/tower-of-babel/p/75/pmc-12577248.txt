Prevalence, pattern, and factors associated with self-reported disability among the tribal population in Jawadhi Hills, Tamil Nadu: a census
Authors: Jackwin Sam Paul G, Sarah Sunil Joseph, Prathibha Obed, Anuradha Rose, Venkata Raghava Mohan, John Paul D, Samuel Prasanna J Vinothkumar, Amala Vinodh, Anika Amritanand, Padma Paul, Vinod Joseph Abraham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Jawadhi Hills region in Tamil Nadu, India, is home to predominantly tribal communities facing severe health challenges, including high infant and maternal mortality. Data on disability within these populations are limited. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of disability and examine associated factors in this region.MethodsA census was conducted across 395 hamlets in the Tiruvannamalai and Vellore districts. Trained field workers collected data on sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and living conditions using a structured questionnaire. Disability was assessed in individuals aged five years and above using the Washington Group Short Set on Functioning (WG-SS).ResultsAmong 58,598 individuals aged ≥ 5 years, the prevalence of perceived functional disability, defined as “a lot of difficulty” or “unable to do” in ≥ 1 domain, or “some difficulty” in ≥ 2 domains, was 2.89% (95% CI: 2.75%–3.03%). Moderate to severe disability was most commonly reported in the domains of mobility (1.64%, 95% CI: 1.54% − 1.75%.), vision (0.89%, 95% CI: 0.81% − 0.96%), and hearing (0.70%, 95% CI: 0.63% − 0.77%). The highest prevalence of disability was observed in the ≥ 85 years age group (33.59%). Logistic regression showed that increasing age, lower educational attainment, unemployment, chronic conditions (diabetes, hypertension), caste, and poor housing conditions were significantly associated with higher odds of disability.ConclusionsThis is the first census-based estimate of disability in a tribal population in the Jawadhi Hills. The findings reveal a notable burden of mobility-related impairments and strong links between disability and social disadvantage. The results support the integration of disability assessment into existing health programs, particularly those addressing non-communicable diseases, and call for expanded access to rehabilitation services in tribal areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24957-6.Keywords: Tribal population, Perceived disability, Washington group short set (WG-SS), Census

Background

Approximately 15% of the global population lives with a disability [1]. According to the United Nations (UN) Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD), people with disabilities include those who have long-term physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairments that may hinder their full, effective, and equal participation in society [2].

According to the 2011 census, there are 26.8 million people with disabilities in India, accounting for 2.21% of the total population. Approximately 2.45% of persons with disabilities in India are from scheduled castes (SCs), and 2.05% are from scheduled tribes (STs) [3].

The Jawadhi Hills region, located across the Tiruvannamalai and Vellore districts of Tamil Nadu, is characterised by hilly rugged terrain and difficult accessibility. It is predominantly home to tribal communities, recognised as scheduled tribes by the government, on the basis of the criteria established by the Lokur Committee. These criteria include having primitive traits, a distinctive culture, geographical isolation, shyness of contact with the broader community, and socioeconomic backwardness [4].

The health indicators for the tribal population in the Jawadhi Hills were notably worse than the state averages. The infant mortality rate (IMR) in the region is 73 per 1,000 live births, whereas it is 24 per 1,000 live births in Tamil Nadu. Similarly, the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) is 170 per 100,000 live births in the Jawadhi Hills, which is significantly higher than Tamil Nadu’s rate of 97 per 100,000 live births [5]. Additionally, ocular morbidity among children under 15 years of age in the Jawadhi Hills stands at 10.8%, with most cases attributed to vitamin A deficiency [6]. From a pilot in the same region using the WHO “Training in the community for people with disabilities, guide for local supervisors” [7], persons with perceived visual disability were seen at a referral clinic, and among those who presented, cataracts (31%) and refractive errors (19%) were the most common ocular morbidities [8]. Other forms of disability within this community have not been thoroughly studied. The study was designed in response to the region’s poor overall health indicators, its geographically inaccessible setting, and preliminary findings from a pilot study indicating a high prevalence of undetected perceived disability.

The Washington Group Short Set (WG-SS) is a standardised six-question tool developed by the Washington Group on Disability Statistics that measures activity limitations to identify individuals at risk of disability globally [9]. The WG-SS is based on the WH Organisation’s International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF), which recognises disability as a result of functional limitations and environmental barriers. The questions are focused on six functional domains, walking, seeing, hearing, cognition, self-care, and communication, capturing common participation limitations. Data from the WG-SS inform policy decisions aimed at promoting equal participation in education, employment, and civic life.

For the past 40 years, the Christian Medical College (CMC) in Vellore has been actively involved in the Jawadhi Hills. The Community Medicine Department, in collaboration with Ophthalmology, Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, and the Christoffel Blinden Mission, has provided services to persons with disabilities (PWDs) in urban, rural, and tribal areas of Vellore district. The institution also conducted a census and disability survey of the region to guide resource allocation, strengthen curative and rehabilitative services at both facility and community levels, build human resource capacity, and plan future interventions.

For this study, we used data from the census of individuals aged five years and above. The dataset included household income, demographic characteristics, substance use, noncommunicable diseases with their risk factors, and disability status. Our objective was to estimate the prevalence of disability and identify its associated factors.

Methods

Study design

In a geographically dispersed setting like the Jawadhi Hills, sampling may lead to systematic bias due to inaccessibility and terrain. Also, from a service point of view, it was important to identify any individual in the entire population who may have perceived disability which can be rectified with medical intervention. This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted to establish a health and demographic surveillance system (HDSS) for Jawadhi Hills, which spans the Tiruvannamalai and Vellore districts of Tamil Nadu, India. Data were collected from 395 hamlets between 2022 and 2023, with the majority collected in the first six months. We have reported our findings in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [10].

Participants

All individuals residing in the 395 hamlets during the survey period were included. Residents were categorised as permanent, temporarily present, or temporarily absent. No exclusion criteria were used to ensure the inclusion of all household members in the study area.

Study tools

The household surveys used a structured questionnaire to collect sociodemographic data, including caste, education, occupation, and chronic health conditions. Disability prevalence was estimated via the WG-SS. The WG-SS assesses six functional domains: vision, hearing, mobility, cognition, self-care, and communication. Responses included ‘No difficulty,’ ‘Some difficulty,’ ‘A lot of difficulty,’ and ‘Cannot do at all.’ Disability was classified on the basis of the Weeks et al. criteria: this is the most conservative and widely used classification, here individuals with “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” in any domain were categorised as having a disability, whereas responses of “no difficulty” or “some difficulty” in all domains were classified as having no disability. Adults with one or more “don’t know” or “refused” responses but no responses of “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” were treated as missing. Using this classification, the tool’s sensitivity ranged from 9% to 62%, and its specificity ranged from 86% to 99.7% [11].This limitation is recognized and accepted given its measures perceived disability, not clinically diagnosed impairment.

Procedure

Seventy Field Research Assistants (FRAs) participated in four training sessions, with minimal absenteeism. Community-based pilot testing was conducted after sessions 1, 2, and 3. Pilot data were cleaned, and queries were raised and addressed during the following sessions. Based on reassuring findings from the third pilot, enumeration commenced after the final training session. Supervisors monitored fieldwork, resolving doubts via phone or in person during sessions when connectivity was poor. FRAs mapped the study area and recorded each household as occupied, vacant, or demolished. Functional disability was assessed using the WG-SS administered during a door-to-door household census. Responses were obtained from a single member from each household typically the head of household or caregiver. As per WG guidelines, proxies are accepted in census settings [12]; however, FRAs were trained to ensure proxies were familiar with the individual’s daily functioning. No formal validation of proxy accuracy was conducted, which may introduce reporting bias, particularly in subjective or less observable functional domains.

Data were recorded on paper forms and entered weekly into an electronic database. Discrepancies and missing data were flagged for follow-up. Regular quality checks ensure the accuracy and completeness of the results. Multiple visits were made over a period of 18 months; all households were covered during this time. Refusals were negligible.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed via STATA/IC 15 (StataCorp, 2017) and the syntax provided by the Washington Group on Disability Statistics for the WG Short Set on Functioning (Washington Group on Disability Statistics, n.d.-a, n.d.-b, n.d.-c). Bivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between disability status and potential predictors, including sociodemographic characteristics and self-reported chronic conditions. Crude odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in bivariate analyses were entered into a multiple logistic regression, after checking for collinearity among the variables using the variance inflation factor and reported adjusted OR with 95% CI. Variables with p < 0.05 were considered significant. The dependent variable was binary (presence or absence of self-reported disability in any domain). The final model included the following covariates: age group, marital status, caste, individual education level, occupation type, self-reported diabetes, self-reported hypertension, and the highest level of education within the household.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the manuscript.

Results

This census covered a total of N = 64,419 individuals from 16,775 households, with an average household size of 4.13 ± 1.53. Of these, n = 58,598 (90.87%) were aged 5 years and above and included in the disability analysis. The population pyramid of the census data (2022), shown in Fig. 1, has a broad base that narrows sharply at the top, indicating a population with high birth and mortality rates. During the census, two individuals were identified as transgender. Owing to the small sample size, they were not included in further analysis, as meaningful statistical inferences could not be drawn.

According to Tables 1 and 2, the overall prevalence of having at least “a lot of difficulty” in any domain was 2.89%, with the highest reported difficulty in ‘Mobility’ (1.65%), followed by ‘Vision’ (0.89%). The prevalence rates of hearing, cognition, self-care, and communication were as follows: hearing (0.70%), cognition (0.25%), self-care (0.14%), and communication (0.13%). The prevalence of having at least one domain with “cannot do at all’ was 0.37%, affecting 219 individuals (Table 2). As age increases, so does the reported prevalence of each disability type, with the largest jumps typically occurring after 60 years of age, indicating that late adulthood is a critical period for the onset or recognition of functional difficulties. Figure 2 illustrates that vision-related disabilities often occur early in life, while mobility-related challenges emerge in the mid-to-late twenties and increase progressively with age.

Table 3. presents the sociodemographic profile of the study population. The study included 58,598 individuals, with a mean age of 31.05 years (SD: 17.42). Most participants were aged 25–44 years (35.86%), and the sex distribution was nearly equal (51.40% male, 48.60% female). The population was predominantly Hindu (99.20%) and primarily comprised Malayali scheduled tribes (92.49%). Educational levels were low, with 48.34% having no formal education and 27.65% having completed primary school. Most individuals (68.4%) were unskilled workers engaged in seasonal labour.

Table 4. presents factors associated with perceived disability among individuals aged 5 years and above (n = 58,598). Significant associations were observed with age group, sex, marital status, education, caste, occupation, self-reported diabetes and hypertension, highest household education, and housing type. The odds of disability increased markedly with age and were higher among individuals with chronic conditions such as diabetes (aOR: 2.31, 95% CI: 1.54–3.44) and hypertension (aOR: 2.48, 95% CI: 2.07–2.98). Lower educational attainment and poorer housing conditions were also associated with greater odds, for instance individuals living in thatched houses had significantly higher odds compared to those in houses with terraces (OR: 2.73, 95% CI: 2.25–3.32). These findings reflect the complex interplay of health, social, and structural determinants of disability.

Discussion

This population-based study assessed the prevalence and correlates of self-reported disability in the tribal population of Jawadhi Hills, Tamil Nadu. Using the Washington Group Short Set (WG-SS) tool, we found that 2.89% (95% CI: 2.75–3.03) of individuals aged ≥ 5 years reported mobility impairments being the most common (1.64%, 95% CI: 1.54%, 1.75%.), followed by vision (0.89%, 95% CI: 0.81% − 0.96%).

Our findings align with national disability estimates for rural India, which range between 2% and 6% [13]. However, among individuals aged ≥ 45 years, disability prevalence in Jawadhi Hills was notably higher at 23%. This exceeds estimates from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI), which reported 15.4% of tribal elders having three or more functional limitations. This has been partly explained by shorter life expectancy and higher physical activity among tribal groups [14, 15]. Yet, more targeted studies show that disability among tribals may be severely undercounted. In comparison, studies in Khammam district, Telangana, found over half of tribal elders with at least one limitation, and vision screening among the Dongaria Kondh in Odisha revealed that 12.4% had visual impairments [16, 17]. In our setting, the high proportion of mobility-related issues may reflect the local way of life. About 68.4% of residents work in physically demanding jobs, such as seasonal labour, or farming, often on steep terrain. These factors contribute to early wear and tear. Also, disability is sometimes viewed as a spiritual or moral issue, leading many to seek help from traditional healers before approaching health services, which can delay care. Additionally, the perception of mobility impairments may be influenced by the region’s difficult terrain, which makes walking, travelling, and daily movement more challenging [18].

As seen in many studies, age was strongly associated with disability. Individuals aged ≥ 85 years had adjusted odds of disability exceeding 15 times those aged 25–44.This aligns with the known rise in senility-related conditions like degenerative joint pathologies, decreased vision, and cognitive decline. Gender differences noted in unadjusted data diminished after accounting for age and other factors, suggesting gendered patterns of occupation and exposure rather than inherent vulnerability. This is consistent with other tribal studies, such as one in Telangana, which found only minimal gender differences in disability profiles [16].

Educational status showed a clear gradient: nearly half (48.4%) of those with any disability had no formal schooling. This pattern reflects broader associations between education, health literacy, and healthcare access. Globally and in India, education is a protective factor for both physical and mental health outcomes [18–21].

Compared to Indigenous groups in high-income countries, the overall prevalence of disability in Jawadhi Hills appears lower. For example, 25.3% of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islanders in Australia, 21% of Māori in New Zealand, and over 30% of First Nations and Métis in Canada live with some form of disability [22–24]. These differences are often tied to social and economic exclusion, limited healthcare access, and historical trauma. In the U.S., American Indian and Alaska Native adults experience nearly double the disability rate of the general population (16.3% vs. 8.9%) [25]. Across all these contexts, common barriers include poverty, low levels of education, poor access to care, and social stigma. However, direct comparison is limited by differences in definitions, survey methodologies, and health system coverage. Our study adds to a small but growing body of work using the WG-SS in low- and middle-income settings [26].

In this study, individuals with diabetes and hypertension had significantly higher adjusted odds of reporting disability (aOR of 2.30 and 2.47, respectively). The strong association between chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension with disability highlights the intersection of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) and disability. Both conditions are known to cause micro and macrovascular complications such as neuropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular diseases, all of which can contribute to physical disability. In a previous study conducted in a similar population in Vellore in 2021, the prevalence of diabetes and hypertension among the tribal population was found to be 7.4% and 36.5%, respectively. Notably, 62% of diabetes cases and 68% of hypertension cases were previously undiagnosed [27]. This highlights a limitation of relying on self-reported data, as many cases of chronic illness may go undetected, leading to an underestimation of their true impact on disability in the population.

One of the key strengths of this study is the large sample size, covering a population of over 64,000 individuals, which provides a robust estimate of disability prevalence and associated factors. Additionally, the focus on a marginalised and under researched tribal population addresses a critical gap in the literature. It employs a validated tool suitable for international comparisons.

However, the study has a few limitations First, its cross-sectional design prevents causal inference between the identified factors and disability. Second, the use of self-reported and proxy responses, while endorsed by the Washington Group for census contexts, is a practical necessity but not without limitations. Proxy respondents may unintentionally underreport mild, intermittent, or less visible functional difficulties (such as cognition and communication), potentially leading to misclassification and an underestimation of true disability prevalence. This is especially important given that functional limitations and chronic conditions were self-reported and not clinically validated. When clinical impairment was assessed in people self reporting disability by the WS tool in the report by Boggs et al. from five population based surveys, “some or worse” difficulty identified people with clinical impairments with a sensitivity range of 44% to 85%. This was further qualified to being best for distance visual impairment (67–85%), followed by (64–84%) for mobility and lastly for hearing (44–83%) [11]. Third, the WG-SS tool, while widely used, does not capture all types of disabilities, particularly mental health and episodic conditions. Additionally, the study was not primarily focused on disability, so key contextual factors such as access to rehabilitation, assistive devices, and healthcare quality were not assessed. Finally, some subgroups were too small for meaningful analysis, and missing data in certain domains may have influenced prevalence estimates.

Conclusion

This study provides insights for public health planning in the Jawadhi Hills, where disability particularly among older adults is both under-recognized and poorly addressed. Using th WG-SS tool, we identified mobility and vision-related impairments as the most prevalent, with steep increases after age 45, often among those facing chronic illness, poor housing, and limited access to care. Following enumeration, targeted follow-up camps were conducted across all hamlets, leveraging CMC Vellore’s established base hospital in Veerapanur and mobile medical units, which currently deliver regular check-ups and prescriptions. Building on this infrastructure, we recommend a structured Community-Based Rehabilitation (CBR) program tailored to the tribal context, anchored in local leadership and delivered through existing health workers such as ASHAs. This should include early screening, provision of assistive devices, home-based rehabilitation, and continuity of care for NCDs, especially diabetes and hypertension through mobile clinics. Community-led Disabled People’s Organizations, supported by Panchayat and health system stakeholders, can drive early identification, reduce stigma, and ensure service delivery is culturally acceptable and sustainable. Interventions must integrate health, housing, education, and livelihood support to reduce structural barriers that compound disability.

Future research should examine ageing-related disability trajectories and assess the feasibility, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness of tribal CBR models.
