A dual-channel hyperspectral classification method based on NAS and transformer
Authors: Wenbin Liu, Juanjuan Li, Yiliang Zeng, Na Meng, Jiahong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Transformer networks present excellent performance in capturing long distance dependencies between different locations in the input sequence and are highly capable on a global scale. Recently, several neural architecture search (NAS) algorithms have been proposed for hyperspectral image (HSI) classification, which further improves the accuracy of HSI classification to a new level with more attention paid to local information. However, current two-channel network methods cannot focus on both local and global information of hyperspectral images, which leads to a decrease in the classification accuracy of this type of data. In this paper, a two-channel network named CTmixer-NAS is proposed for hyperspectral image classification. Combining the advantages of NAS and Transformer networks, local and global information is captured simultaneously. In the Transformer branch, the fusion of information at different self-coding layers is proposed. In the CNN branch, a high-performance network structure is automatically designed using NAS, which improves the accuracy of hyperspectral classification. The proposed approach saves labor cost, and the most suitable network structure can be searched according to different datasets, which makes the searched network structure present better generalization performance. CTmixer-NAS achieves the best performance in five hyperspectral datasets in comparative experiments.

Introduction

Hyperspectral imagery, a type of remote sensing imagery, refers to images of objects photographed from a distance by high-resolution satellite sensors, which record the magnitude of electromagnetic waves reflected from the objects. Each pixel in a hyperspectral image has hundreds of contiguous narrow spectral bands ranging from the visible spectrum to the short-wave infrared spectrum. The spectral information of different bands can describe different aspects of the photographed object, which is usually regarded as a 3D cubic structure. It has a wide array of practical applications, including land cover mapping, urban planning, precision agriculture, surface change detection, and mining surveys.

Hyperspectral classification plays a vital role in earth sciences and remote sensing. Hyperspectral image classification specifically refers to the assignment of category labels to each pixel point in a hyperspectral image by extracting its potential features. In the early investigations on hyperspectral image classification, the prime focus was placed on the spectral vectors of individual pixels, known as the spectral information of a hyperspectral image. Feature extraction and classification were performed on the spectral information of individual pixels. In previous studies, support vector machines1, random forests2, and other methods were used to learn features with discriminative properties from a training dataset to classify hyperspectral images. However, as the image quality of hyperspectral images may be distorted by factors such as illumination and shadows, in some situations, the same object may have different spectral information, and different objects may have the same spectral information. Therefore, focusing only on spectral features and overlooking spatial correlations, such as when using stacked auto-encoders3, deep belief networks4, and 1-DCNN5, leads to low accuracy in classifying hyperspectral images. Many classification methods based on spectral–spatial information, such as the null spectral residual network6 and full convolutional neural network7–9 have been proposed. They take more structural information into account and realize the joint processing of spectral and spatial information, providing better classification accuracy.

In recent years, many deep learning-based methods have been applied to hyperspectral image classification and have made tremendous progress. In particular, CNNs have demonstrated remarkable performance in hyperspectral classification tasks. Considering the cubic structure of hyperspectral data, Xu proposed the direct use of a 3D-CNN network, which expands the idea of multiscale feature fusion and dilated convolution from the spatial dimension to the spectral dimension, and combine 3D convolution and residual connection10. However, owing to the problem of gradient vanishing, the deep neural network training remains a challenge. Song proposed a deep feature fusion network by deepening the 2D-CNN11, which favorably solves the problems of gradient vanishing and gradient explosion by introducing residual blocks as a method to train a deeper network while ensuring good performance.

Although CNNs can capture local information better than other methods in many visual domains, diverse network structures can achieve good classification results when facing different data types. However, the above neural network are designed manually and rely heavily on the knowledge and experience of the people applying them. The deeper the network structure, the more difficult it is to design an optimally structured network when processing hundreds of spectral bands and a considerable amount of data. In addition, a variety of hyperparameters need to be tuned, such as the size of the convolution kernel and jump layer connection, which increases the difficulty of manually designing the network structure. Furthermore, the limited convolutional kernel does not effectively obtain the global information of hyperspectral images, while the CNN is not adept at dealing with hyperspectral sequence data and other problems. As a result, the CNN yields an insufficiently high classification accuracy and not fast enough computation time when dealing with hyperspectral data.

Fortunately, an increasing number of scholars are now interested in the design of automated network architectures. For example, neural architecture search (NAS)12 has achieved good results in image classification tasks. First, the differentiable architecture search (DARTS)13,14 was introduced for hyperspectral classification, and auto-CNN was proposed. Subsequently, a three-dimensional asymmetric convolutional search space was proposed, which markedly improved the classification performance. Owing to a significant increase in the amount of data and computation load, powerful network characterization capabilities may suffer from overfitting. Due to the special characteristics of hyperspectral data, the spectral range and spatial resolution differ for different types of hyperspectral data. Therefore, it is still challenging to study an NAS-based automatically designed network structure15,16.

The use of a transformer network17–19 solves some bottlenecks problem that it is difficult for CNN network to effectively capture long-distance dependencies and integrate global information20–22 during hyperspectral image classification to a certain extent. Furthermore, this network, which is based on the attention mechanism, offers significant advantages in dealing with continuous data. It leverages a self-attention mechanism to attract global dependence on a sequence of inputs. It can simultaneously obtain global information easily. Although the transformer network can solve the problem of long-range correlation between image pixels (there is a certain correlation between pixels that are far away from each other), it cannot effectively capture local feature information, the layer-hopping connection plays a pivotal role in the transformer network. The introduction of residual blocks improves gradient propagation and mitigates the loss of critical information. However, as simple layer-hopping occurs only in each encoder, the connections between different layers are weak.To address the aforementioned problems, herein, we propose a dual-channel neural network search model based on a CNN23,24 transformer networks25, namely, CTmixer-NAS. In particular, we have two foci: (1) Improving the accuracy of hyperspectral image classification by utilizing the CNN to fully capture local information and the transformer to fully capture global information. (2) Optimizing the network structure and reducing the time required for hyperspectral image classification by designing a suitable optimization space and choosing an optimal search strategy, thus creating a satisfactory network model. Under the cube-to-pixel framework, the NAS-based dual-channel network is designed by considering both the local and global information, as well as the spatial-spectral correlation for hyperspectral image classification. Proposed methodology

In this study, we propose CTMixer-NAS, a dual-channel NAS-based network for hyperspectral image classification, whose overall architecture is depicted in Fig. 1. It consists of three components: A data preprocessing module, a transformer branch, and an NAS-based CNN network branch. First, considering the low spatial resolution of hyperspectral images, group-parallel residual blocks are established to obtain the relevant spectral-spatial information of hyperspectral images. Second, the transformer network is designed to increase the layer-hopping connection mechanism in different encoder coding layers, which improves the ability of the network to capture global information. Meanwhile, an NAS-based CNN network branch is established, which includes the search of a super network architecture and final network optimization, designing network operators to create a new search space, optimizing the network structure based on the search strategy to better capture local information, and shortening the model classification time. Finally, an average pooling layer is used to facilitate the embedding of the convolutional operations, and the classification results are obtained after using a linear classifier.

(A) Data preprocessing

(1) Set of parallel residual blocks

The group-parallel residual block can improve the information transfer and representation ability during feature extraction to a certain extent. It introduces a residual connection method to realize the direct transfer of information. Inside the group-parallel residual block, the input features are divided into several groups, and the features in each group are connected by a residual connection with the outputs of the previous layer to enable the input features to be directly transferred to the subsequent layers and to strengthen the information flow between different layers. The flow of information between different layers is then enhanced. Group convolution divides a spectral band into multiple groups using convolution operations that can be viewed as multiple parallel CNNs26–28. Specifically, this structure divides the input features into multiple groups and applies independent convolution operations to each group. The outputs of each group are then fused and summed with the input features using residual concatenation to obtain the final output feature map. Its structure is shown in Fig. 2.

(2) Group pixel embedding

Unlike the discrete temporal ordering in a classical transformer or Vision Transformer (ViT), hundreds or thousands of spectral channels in a hyperspectral image produce near-continuous spectral features at subtle intervals. Hyperspectral images are rich in spectral information with strong correlations between neighboring bands. Each pixel contains spectral information from multiple bands; therefore, the data dimensions are extremely high. This leads to increased computational complexity and the problem of a “dimensionality catastrophe”. Pixel-wise classification treats each pixel as an independent sample to be classified, which means that each pixel is assigned a classification label that can more accurately capture subtle spectral variations and spatial information, improving the accuracy of classification. Owing to the correlation and dependency between bands, a pixel neighbor matrix is constructed to capture the similarity between bands. Search strategy

The DARTS-based network architecture is used to design the proposed network architecture, which is based on the principle of making the search space of the network architecture continuous and derivable through differentiable operations. This is obtained by first defining a hypergraph, where the nodes represent the operations in the network (e.g., convolutional pooling), and the edges represent the direction of data flow. The weight of each operation is then represented by introducing a set of parameters learned through gradient optimization, during which DARTS32 attempts to learn a mixture of operations to determine the best network architecture. By alternating between a search phase and a subnetwork-training phase, DARTS can automatically discover network architectures that are suitable for a given task. A notable advantage of this approach is that it significantly reduces the amount of manual work involved in designing network architectures while still achieving a promising performance.

The core logic of DARTS is that “each edge simultaneously learns the weights of multiple types of candidate operations.” The dashed lines in the leftmost subfigure represent the state at the initial stage of the search; the second subfigure from the left also represents the initial stage of the search. The third subfigure from the left depicts the training process, where DARTS optimizes the “mixed weights” of operations—causing the weights of high-gain operations to increase (with solid lines gradually becoming prominent) and the weights of low-gain/high-cost operations to decrease (with dashed lines weakening). The rightmost figure shows the state after search convergence, where only the operations with the highest weights are retained (solid lines), forming the final CNN architecture and reflecting NAS’s screening result for “performance-cost balance.”

From the results of this experiment, it can be seen that the introduction of layer-hopping connections between different layers increases the classification accuracy for hyperspectral images. The purpose of using cross-attention fusion (CAF) hopping connections is to achieve a large difference between shallow and deep features. However, when the layer-hopping connection is too large, it may lead to incomplete fusion and potential loss of information.

(3) Number of attention layers

To verify the impact of the number of attention layers on experimental results, this article carried out ablation experiments, for the IndianPines dataset, with the number of attention layers set to 2, 4, 6, and 8 respectively. Verification was performed using OA, AA, and Kappa coefficient as evaluation metrics.Detailed results are presented in Table 7.

As can be seen from the results, when the number of attention network layers is 4, the hyperspectral classification accuracy reaches the highest. This is because the attention mechanism of shallow networks struggles to capture the complex spectral-spatial long-range dependency information in hyperspectral images, resulting in insufficient feature expression capability and thus lower classification accuracy. When the number of network layers is excessively large, deep networks tend to suffer from gradient vanishing and gradient exploding, leading to inadequate feature propagation.

(4) Number of attention heads

In Transformer networks, the multi-head attention mechanism can enhance the model’s representational capability by increasing the number of heads; however, an excessive number of heads may lead to performance degradation. The number of heads is independent of the size of the model weights, but it affects training efficiency and the representational space.for the IndianPines dataset,This paper designed ablation experiments targeting the number of attention heads, with specific details presented in Table 8.

As can be seen from the results, the hyperspectral classification accuracy reaches the highest when the number of attention heads is 8. This is because when the number of attention heads is 2, 4, or 6, a small number of heads fails to meet the complex feature requirements of hyperspectral data; whereas an excessively large number of attention heads leads to an increase in parameters, resulting in issues of feature redundancy and waste of attention resources.

The CTMixer-NAS dual-channel network proposed in this study significantly enhances the classification results. On the one hand, by integrating the information from different channels, the dual-channel network can better capture the correlations and interactions in the data, which can improve the classifier’s ability to recognize complex patterns and features and thus increase the classification accuracy. On the other hand, as the CNN network is good at capturing local spatial features while the transformer network is good at processing sequence data and capturing global correlations, a network that combines the two can capture spatial and local information in hyperspectral data more comprehensively to improve the classification accuracy. Finally, as the NAS algorithm can search for the best network structure to optimize the performance of a given task, the designed CNN architecture can be optimized automatically and thus improve the classification accuracy.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a CTmixex-NAS module that combines a CNN and transformer dual-channel network to maximize the retention of global and local information, thus improving hyperspectral classification accuracy. The experimental results on four typical datasets reveal that the proposed method can achieve a satisfactory classification performance. Compared with a few popular hyperspectral classification algorithms and the two-channel network structure, the method proposed in this paper has a great improvement in classification accuracy.

In addition, the introduced layer-hopping connection proves the feasibility of cross-attention fusion (CAF) hopping connection and improves the classification accuracy. The result of the ablation experiments also proves that the proposed module and network structure are very useful for hyperspectral classification. The NAS-based auto-searched network structure greatly improves the performance of the proposed network, but the search space requires continuous update and optimization, the improvement of which belongs to future work.
