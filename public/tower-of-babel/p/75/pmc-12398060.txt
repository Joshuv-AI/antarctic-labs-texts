Silicon vs. selenium: A comparative study in mitigating osmotic and drought stress in durum wheat (Triticum durum Desf.)
Authors: Chaima Ajmi, Imen Bouhaouel, Afef Othmani, Hajer Slim Amara, Heidar Meftahizadeh, Sourour Ayed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSilicon (Si) and selenium (Se) are recognized as beneficial elements that may significantly improve plant’s tolerance to drought stress. However, their action and efficiency on germination, biometric and physiological attributes are rarely compared on durum wheat under water-deficit conditions. This study highlights the effect of Si (15 mg L−1) and Se (1 and 2 mg L−1) seed priming on germination kinetic of four durum wheat varieties (‘Karim’, ‘Maali’, ‘INRAT100’, and ‘Dhahbi’) under osmotic stress (150 g L−1 PEG6000). In addition, a pot experiment was undertaken in the year 2019/2020 to examine the fertigation benefits of these elements on growth and physiological durum wheat performance, using the same concentrations under two water regimes (70 and 30% field capacity). Except for phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids contents, our results indicated that osmotic/drought stress induced considerable reductions in germination rate, root, shoot and leaf physiological attributes. However, these drastic impacts were altered through seed priming and fertigation of Si and Se (2 mg L−1). Se seed priming was more effective than that of Si, and inversely for the fertigation. These two nutrients Si and Se stimulated the elongation of roots (23.82%, 9.48%) better than that of shoots (20.55%, 10.44%), but improved the shoot fresh (27.79%, 10.37%) and dry weight (32%, 18.13%) better than that of roots. Specific interactions between studied traits were noted according to the water regime. In addition, genotype-specific effects of Si or Se were observed. The variety ‘Karim’, the lowest performing genotype, was the most receptive to stimulators. The positive changes resulting from Si and Se applications highlights the potential of these elements in boosting durum wheat plants to successfully acclimate to water-deficit conditions.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07167-5.Keywords: Fertigation, Seed priming, Mineral compounds, Water-deficit stress

Introduction

Durum wheat (Triticum turgidum L. ssp. durum [Desf.] Husn.) is considered as the basic staple food of the Mediterranean diet. It serves as a raw material for semolina, couscous, pasta, burghul, and several other culinary traditions of the Mediterranean regions [1]. However, with climatic events of recent years, drought is a serious abiotic stress that sharply hampers durum wheat production, notably in semi-arid and arid areas [2, 3]. Numerous studies have forecasted an increase in the severity, extent, and duration of drought across various parts of the world triggered by warmer temperatures and water scarcity [4, 5]. In this sense, Zhao et al. [6] noted that global wheat yields are expected to decline sharply by 6.0 ± 2.9% for each 1 °C rise in temperature as a result of climate change. In general, drought stress leads to several morphological, physiological, and biochemical changes; e.g., reduction in water potential, photosynthesis rate and photosynthetic pigments, antioxidants generation, ion uptake, growth hormones, root development, stem stretching, etc [7]. Nonetheless, various adaptive mechanisms can be triggered against dehydration [8, 9].

One of the most sustainable and eco-friendly options and strategies that have been served to enhance crop performance is to supply plants with mineral compounds such as silicon (Si), selenium (Se), magnesium (Mg), etc. Thereby, finding ways to improve drought tolerance are very useful for durum wheat production, especially for sensitive varieties [10]. Although Si and Se have not yet been recognized as essential elements in the plant growth processes [11], they have been considered as multi-talented elements and their exogenous application attracted much more attention [12, 13]. In fact, the use of these elements has been shown to enhance plant tolerance to abiotic stress much more effectively and swiftly than with genetic improvement strategies [14]. Literature reports confirmed the beneficial effects of Si and Se fertilization on plant growth and development, physiological processes like photosynthesis and antioxidant system and yield [15, 16]. These elements induced abiotic stress tolerance of various crops/plants namely rice [17], maize [18], and barley [19].

Si is a widespread constituent and the second most abundant element in the soil after Oxygen, primarily occurring in the form of silicate minerals. Through chemical and physical weathering, these minerals are transformed into their soluble form, monosilicic acid [Si(OH)₄], which can be absorbed by plants to varying extents, mainly via the epidermal and exodermal cells of lateral roots [13, 16, 20]. Monocot species can accumulate Si up to 10% of the plant mass and it is a structural integrity component of the cell walls in some species [13, 21]. Most research on wheat revealed that Si alleviates the harmful drought consequences at various developmental levels under laboratory and field conditions [22, 23]. The divergence of the observed Si effects may be associated with growth conditions, stage of plant growth, mode of application, and/or to variation in plant species and genotypes [24, 25]. The potential benefits of Si are more obvious in varieties/species that actively accumulate this element in their aerial parts and notably in their roots [26, 27]. This effect has been attributed to subsequent deposition of silica gel on the surface of foliar, stem, and other tissues to protect plants from different adverse conditions [25].

Se application is also an innovative approach used to safeguard crop plants against drought stress [28]. Se can be taken up by plants as selenate (SeO42−), selenite (SeO32–), or as organic Se compounds namely selenomethionine [29]. In oxic soils (pH + pE > 15), SeO42– is the most dominant form, whereas SeO32−, is more prevalent in anaerobic soils with a neutral to acidic pH [30]. The uptake of Se by plants depends on the form, the concentration, and the species’ ability to absorb and metabolize this element, as well as the presence of competing ions [31]. Additionally, the toxicity or benefits of Se are closely tied on the applied concentration and type of plant [28, 32, 33], with 4.9 mg kg−1 as toxic threshold for wheat [34]. Se ions possess the ability to diffuse through epidermal cells, hence becoming part of the plant body [35]. Under drought stress, previous studies revealed that supplemental Se improved grain weight, biomass yield and grain yield by 26%, 12% and 24% respectively, in wheat [36, 37].

Although the positive role of Si and Se was proved in most studies, the exact underpinning mechanisms to improve drought tolerance are unknown [38]. However, it has been demonstrated that Si or Se application enhance the photosynthetic activity by the preservation of plastid and chloroplast membrane’s structure and fluidity, and cellular integrity, increasing leaf chlorophyll and carotenoids, reducing electrolyte leakage of mitochondrial electron transport, and enhancing exchange of gas [39–43]. Si and Se fertilization can also improve the water use efficiency by roots, limit the water loss from tissues [44], delay senescence [45], and increase plant yields [46]. Additionally, Si and Se has been proven to decrease oxidative damage, especially by enhancing reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity [20, 46, 47]. During drought, it improved secondary metabolite antioxidants such as phenols and flavonoids in plants [43, 48–50]. In particular, Se acts also as an antioxidant agent at low concentrations and adequate doses [51].

Numerous modes of application of Si and Se might be used including seed coating or priming, soil application, and foliar spray [28, 52, 53]. Seed priming is known as an effective and pragmatic approach for increasing seed vigor, synchronization of germination, as well as seedlings growth [54]. Otherwise, fertigation or soil application, proved as an efficient technique to enhance growth of wheat especially at tillering stage [22, 46]. Several reports mentioned the positive impact of Si or Se on the aerial and root parts. Currently, comparison of the action of these elements on these durum wheat parts, in particular on root traits, and discerning the better type of application and the developmental stage of this species suitable for the application under drought conditions are less known. Our objective is to investigate the separate effects of Si and Se on the germination process using priming technique and fertigation method on root, shoot and leaf physiological attributes at stem elongation stage (Z39) [55] of durum wheat under osmotic and drought stress.

Materials and methods

Used durum wheat varieties

To study the impact of silicon (Si) and selenium (Se) fertilization on durum wheat (Triticum turgidum L. ssp. durum [Desf.] Husn.), four Tunisian modern varieties (‘Karim’ [control], ‘Maali’, ‘INRAT100’, and ‘Dhahbi’), given by the Tunisian national breeding program, were selected based on data available regarding their performances under drought stress. In addition, ‘Karim’ and ‘Maali’ were the most widely cultivated varieties in Tunisia, even though ‘INRAT100’ and ‘Dhahbi’ are two newly developed varieties that have been marketed for farmers since the 2021–2022 cropping season. Experiment 1

Effect of Si and Se seed priming on durum wheat germination

Seed sterilization and nutri-priming

All durum wheat seeds underwent sterilization by treatment with 10% of sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, then thoroughly rinsed three times with sterile distilled water for 5 min duration each [56–58]. Then, seeds were primed for 12 h [59] with Si or Se. Water-soluble Si and Se treatments were prepared by dissolving sodium silicate powder (Meta) extra pure (Na2SiO3.9H2O) and sodium selenate (Na2SeO4) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), respectively. Specifically, seeds were primed using a solution-to-seed weight ratio of 5:1 (v/w), meaning that 5 mL of solution was applied per gram of seeds. This ratio was chosen to ensure sufficient contact and uniform hydration without excess dilution. In this study, 30 seeds were used per replication for each treatment, with three replications conducted per treatment. Based on previous studies, the most efficient Si concentration (15 mg L−1) was chosen [16], while two Se concentrations (1 and 2 mg L−1) were used [56]. After priming treatment, seeds were rinsed in three successive 5-minute intervals with sterile distilled water. Thereafter, seeds were labeled and air-dried on blotting paper at ambient room temperature (24 °C) overnight.

Experimental setup, and plant growth and treatment conditions

Ten priming seeds of each variety were placed on sterile filter paper (12–15 μm) in a 90 mm diameter Petri dish moistened with 4 ml of Si or Se solution (Table 2). To impose osmotic stress, the durum wheat seeds were exposed to 150 g L−1 of polyethylene glycol (PEG6000, with a molecular weight of 6000 g/mol and a purity of over 99.0%, was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). to obtain an osmotic pressure of −3.0 MPa. The completely randomized block design with three replications (n = 3) was employed for the three-way factorial experiment which included two levels of osmotic potential (0 [control] and 150 g L−1 PEG6000), four nutriments treatments (0 mg L−1 Si or Se, 15 mg L−1 Si, and 1 and 2 mg L−1 Se), (Table 2) and four varieties (‘Karim’, ‘Maali’, ‘INRAT100’, and ‘Dhahbi’) as main factors. Seeds were germinated in a dark growth chamber, maintaining 50% relative humidity and a day/night temperature of 22 ± 2 °C [60].

Seed germination measurement

The germination kinetics was assessed by daily counting the number of germinated seeds until the 7th day of growth [16]. The germination was considered to have occurred when the radicle length extended for at least 2 mm [52].

Determination of phenol, flavonoid and carotenoid contents

For the assessment of the phenol and flavonoid content, a methanol extraction was performed following the method outlined by Li et al. [61]. 1 g of fresh leaves samples harvested from each plot were ground with liquid nitrogen then extracted with 25 ml methanol solution (80%, v/v) and treated with ultrasonication for 20 min, then centrifuged. The obtained extract was kept at −20 °C for the determination of the total phenolic and flavonoid content and all determinations were carried out in triplicate expressed as the mean value. Total phenolic compounds (TPCs) were determined using the colorimetric method Folin–Ciocalteau. 100 µl of extract was mixed with 50 µl of the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (2 N) (Sigma–Aldrich Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA). After 5 min, 400 µl of sodium carbonate (1 M) and 1 ml of deionized water were added. The obtained mixture was kept for 90 min in the dark at 37 °C. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 740 nm. A standard curve with different concentrations of gallic acid was prepared, and the content of TPCs was expressed as gallic acid equivalent standard of fresh weight (mg GAE g–1 FW) [62]. Flavonoid content (TFC) was performed using aluminum chloride assay. Briefly, 2 ml of methanolic extract was mixed with 0.2 ml Sodium Nitrite (NaNO2; 5%: w/v), 0.2 ml of Aluminum Nitrate solution (Al (NO3)3; 10%: w/v), and 2 ml Sodium hydroxide solution (NaOH; 1 M). After an incubation period of 15 min, the absorbance of the mixture was recorded at 510 nm. A standard curve using different concentrations of rutin was prepared, and TFC content was quantified as milligrams of rutin equivalent gram–1 of fresh weight (mg RE g–1 FW) [61].

Carotenoid content was estimated based on the protocol described by Farman et al. [63]. 1 g of samples of fresh ground leaves were mixed with acetone solution (80%), then centrifuged during 15 min at 3,000 rpm. Experiment 2

Effect of Si and Se fertigation on durum wheat biometric and physiological traits

Greenhouse management, and plant growth and treatment conditions

The assay was performed during the growing season 2019–2020 in a semi-closed environment with sowing carried out in the middle of the month of Decembre. During the experiment, the minimum and the maximum air temperature and relative humidity ranged from 10 to 35 °C and relative humidity varied between 57% and 70%.

Durum wheat varieties were grown in plastic pots (21 cm diameter × 25 cm height) filled with 5 kg of sterilized and washed fine sand (electric conductivity = 0.84 dS m−1 and pH = 7.54). Sterilized soil was used in the greenhouse experiment to eliminate the influence of soil-borne pathogens, pests, and microbial variability, which could otherwise interfere with plant growth, water stress responses, or nutrient uptake. Using sterilized soil allowed us to standardize the growing conditions across all treatments. More precisely, ten seeds sterilized as previously described, were sown in each pot. Later, the seedlings were thinned to five individuals per pot after emergence.

Applied treatments

During the experiment, two soil moisture regimes were established to simulate different levels of water availability. From the first day of the trial until the stem elongation stage (Zadoks growth stage Z39), pots were irrigated using tap water to maintain either 70% of field capacity (FC), representing moderate water stress (control), or 30% of FC, representing severe water stress. The field capacity of the soil was pre-determined using the gravimetric method. To maintain these target moisture levels throughout the experiment, pot weights were monitored daily. The amount of water lost through evapotranspiration measured using the gravimetric method and was replenished by weighing each pot every two days and restoring water to the designated FC percentage.

Water-soluble Si (15 mg L−1 Na2SiO3.9H2O) and Se (1 and 2 mg L−1 Na2SeO4) treatments were applied at the initial and at the final of the tillering phase (Z21 and Z29, respectively [55]) via fertigation method. The completely randomized block design including three replications for each treatment was followed (Table 3).

Treatments with water-soluble silicon and selenium were applied via fertigation method at two key developmental stages: at the beginning of tillering (Z21) and at the end of tillering (Z29), as defined by the Zadoks scale [55]. The silicon treatment consisted of sodium metasilicate (Na₂SiO₃·9 H₂O) at a concentration of 15 mg L⁻¹, and selenium was applied in the form of sodium selenate (Na₂SeO₄) at two concentrations: 1 mg L⁻¹ and 2 mg L⁻¹. Each treatment consisted of 100 mL of solution per pot, applied via manual soil application (fertigation) directly to the root zone. This volume ensured uniform and adequate coverage without causing leaching or waterlogging.

The experiment followed a completely randomized block design (CRBD) with three replications per treatment. Each treatment group (combination of Si, Se level, and water regime) included a sufficient number of pots to allow for statistical analysis of plant growth parameters, physiological traits, and yield-related data.

Root, shoot and leaf physiological traits measurements

After four weeks, precisely, the chlorophyll index (SPAD value) was recorded at stem elongation stage (Z39) by chlorophyll meter SPAD 502 Plus (Minolta, Japan). This index was determined on flag leaves of the five plants per replicate. Additionally, the flag leaf area index (cm2) of the five plants per pot was measured using electronic planimeter AM300 (ADC Bioscientific Ltd, UK). At two months post-sowing (mid-March), the samples were harvest for the measurement of the shoot and root length (cm) and the fresh weight (g) were determined. Afterward, shoots and roots were dried at 65 °C for 72 h to determine the dry weight (g).

Data analysis

ANOVAs variance analysis was conducted using General Linear Model (GLM) to assess the impact of Si/Se treatments, osmotic/drought stress, durum wheat varieties, and their respective interactions on germination kinetics, and biometric and physiological attributes. Tukey’s multiple range test was applied to the datasets at the 0.05 significance level. These statistical analyses were realized using SAS software (version 9.4). The Pearson coefficients (r) were determined to evaluate correlations between the biometric and physiological attributes for each treatment (Si or Se) under both control and stressed conditions using the “Cor” function and were subsequently visualized in the “Corrplot” package in R (version 3.4.3).

Results

Effect of Si and Se seed priming on durum wheat germination

The osmotic stress negatively affected the seed germination, particularly during the first three days (Table 4; Fig. 1). The results showed no significant difference under control conditions (0 g L−1 PEG6000), while a significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) was obtained between treatments (control, Si, and Se) for the first days using 150 g L−1 of PEG6000. Under stressed conditions (150 g L−1 of PEG6000), Se (2 mg L−1 followed by 1 mg L−1) was the most effective treatment for durum wheat germination, while Si induced an overall lower germination rate, especially for the first days. In addition, it was observed that the effect of Se/Si priming is mainly reflected in a faster germination rate as shown by Dhahbi seeds primed with Si and Se, which achieved a maximum germination percentage two days earlier than the non-primed seeds.

Under the two osmotic levels, the effect of these elements was genotype-dependent. Notably, Se (2 mg L−1) was the most performing treatment for ‘Karim’ and ‘Dhahbi’ germination especially for the first three days.

Effect of Si and Se seed priming on phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids content in durum wheat leaf

In durum wheat leaves phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids contents were significantly affected by different treatments (osmotic stress × Si seed priming × Se seed priming), varieties and treatments × varieties interaction (Table 5). Compared to the control treatment, osmotic stress increased phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids contents. Under no osmotic stress conditions, INRAT 100 variety had the highest value of phenols (66.50 mg/100 g FW) using 1 mg L−1 Se and flavonoids (36.98 mg/100 g FW) by 2 mg L−1 Se as seed priming treatment (Fig. 2). However, the highest value of carotenoids (1.66 mg/100 g FW) was obtained in Maali variety by 15 mg L−1 Si treatment. Under osmotic stress conditions, INRAT 100 variety showed the highest phenols content (65.53 mg/100 g FW) with 15 mg L−1 Si. While, Maali variety delivered the highest (3.98 mg/100 g FW) carotenoids content with 1 mg L−1 Se. Overall, Se seeds priming treatment improved phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids content especially with a concentration of 2 mg L−1 Se, and Maali displayed the best performance across the investigated cultivars.

Effect of Si and Se fertigation on durum wheat biometric and physiological traits

The results revealed significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) among the three water regimes (WR) for all biometric and physiological traits. Similarly, the effects of Si and Se treatments (T) were significant across all measured parameters. Varietal differences (V) were also observed for most traits, except for shoot length (Table 6). In most cases, the interactions between water regime and treatment (WR × T) and between water regime and variety (WR × V) were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05), whereas the interaction between treatment and variety (T × V) and the interaction between the three factors (WR × T × V) were not significant. These ANOVA results suggested that the effect of Si/Se treatments and varietal responses to these elements depend on the water regime. Conversely, the response of varieties appears to be stable across different treatments.

Under control conditions (70% FC), Si treatment enhanced more the shoot dry weight (28.54%), the root (18.92%) and shoot (14.38%) length, the root fresh weight (8.47%), the chlorophyll index (6.38%), and the leaf area index (4.98%) compared with the control treatment (i.e., without Si) (Table S1, Figs. 3 and 4). The addition of 2 mg L−1 Se induced the greatest increase rate for the shoot fresh weight (37.74%), and the root/shoot ratio (9.44%). Nonetheless, 1 mg L−1 of Se enhanced considerably the root dry weight (62.24%). The three treatments had a differential effect on the above- and belowground parts of the plant. In fact, Si fertigation stimulated the root elongation (18.92%) more than that of shoots (14.38%), but increased the shoot fresh (27.13%) and dry weight (28.54%) more than the root fresh (8.47%) and dry weight (13.72%). The same trend was also noted for Se using 1 or 2 mg L−1. However, 1 mg L−1 Se showed a great accumulation of root dry weight.

Under limited irrigation (30% FC), Si was the most efficient treatment for the shoot dry weight (33.66%), the root (30.00%) and shoot (28.04%) length, the shoot (28.41%) and root (10.91%) fresh weight, the chlorophyll index (7.75%), the leaf area index (6.50%), and the root/shoot ratio (2.34%), while 2 mg L−1 of Se showed the highest value for the root dry weight (10.15%) (Table S1, Figs. 3 and 4). A similar trend was obtained with 2 mg L−1 of Se, but both shoot and root length were improved in a comparable manner. Conversely, the beneficial effect of 1 mg L−1 Se was more pronounced on the shoot length, fresh and dry weight.

Genotype-specific Si/Se responses were observed either in presence of severe (10% FC) or control (70% FC) water stress, based on root, shoot, and physiological attributes (Table S1, Figs. 3 and 4). Overall, ‘Karim’ showed a better response to Si and Se (2 mg L⁻¹) treatments under both conditions, unlike ‘Maali’, ‘INRAT100’, and ‘Dhahbi’. However, a similar beneficial effect on the three varieties was observed with 1 mg L⁻¹ Se under 70% FC.

Discussion

Durum wheat is mainly a rainfed crop in Tunisia, recently exposed to severe drought conditions which impedes the plants growth and development and reduces the crop productivity [64]. In particular, the precipitation in semi-arid and arid regions does not exceed 200 mm and remains markedly lower than the water requirements of the durum wheat crop needs [65]. Drought effects on plants are largely determined by its severity, duration and the growing stages, as it impacts plant growth and development from the onset to the later developmental stages. Recently, Tunisia has faced erratic early-season rainfall, a common feature of the North African Mediterranean climate, affecting early stages of wheat growth. Our findings revealed that osmotic stress had negative impacts on durum wheat seed germination. In fact, drought inhibits water uptake during the germination process, leading to delayed emergence. In this context, Panda et al. [66] reported that limited water conditions hinder the ATP production and respiration, which in turn impairs water absorption via the seed coat and hampers seed imbibition, ultimately affecting radicle emergence. In the current research, Si and Se priming did not significantly increase the germination percentage of durum wheat, in the absence of stress. This observation implies that under favorable conditions, the intrinsic physiological mechanisms of seeds are sufficient for a successful germination, and priming often provides no substantial additional benefit. Similar results were obtained by (Rocha et al. [67], Gama et al. [68], Du et al. [69]. By contrast, under osmotic stress, both Si and Se priming significantly improved germination performance during the initial days. This early improvement may stem from the role of Si and Se in the upregulation of the antioxidative system to mitigate oxidative damage and the induction of a better osmotic balance to maintain membrane stability during stress-induced imbibition. This result may suggest that at an early stage the primed seeds were better equipped to initiate metabolic activity and water uptake under limited water availability [20, 46, 47]. Nevertheless, after the fifth day of germination no significant differences in germination percentage between control and primed treatments were shown. This observation potentially suggests that the priming effect was primarily on the germination speed (e.g. Dhahbi seeds primed with Si and Se achieved maximum germination percentage two days earlier than the non-primed seeds). Indeed, in adverse conditions, Se/Si priming would promote pre-germination metabolic activity, allowing seeds to initiate physiological processes in advance and thus regulating germination within a shorter period Feghhenabi et al. [62]. In this context, 2 mg L−1 of Se showed a good performance for most durum wheat varieties in the early days. This outcome could be therefore attributed to the ability of Se application to activate early metabolic pathways involved in germination, promote de novo synthesis of germination-promoting substances, improve α-amylase activity and antioxidant defense ability, and increase the content of osmotic substances [54, 60]. Additionally, it was observed that the different priming treatments triggered distinct genotypic responses. This differential response of the durum wheat cultivars may be due to their genetic potential, as some cultivars might exhibit sensitivity to these minerals compared to others. Recently, great attention has revolved on the role of phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids in response to different abiotic stress. They function as antioxidants and inhibit ROS generation in stressed plants. In wheat their accumulation is correlated to drought tolerance [70]. In the current study, phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids contents were increased in response to osmotic stress. Similar results were reported by [48] in wheat. The Si and Se seed priming increased the concentration of phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids in durum wheat leaves under stressed and no stressed conditions. These results suggest that both Si and Se might promote the antioxidative system and enhance plant tolerance to oxidative stress resulting from water deficit [71], Consequently, the enhanced antioxidant capacity observed in Se and Si primed seeds may have contributed to a more efficient and rapid germination process. This effect is particularly relevant during the initial hours of germination, a critical phase involving imbibition and metabolic activation, when seeds are highly sensitive to environmental stresses such as early drought. In such conditions, the rapid activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms may improve cellular readiness and protect against oxidative damage, thereby supporting improved germination kinetics reflected by faster and more synchronized germination rather than changes in final germination percentages.

Root architecture traits such as root length, root biomass, and root distribution are crucial in the screening of plant adaptation to water deficit stress [72]. In this study, the fertigation method was used for investigating the impact of Se and Si supplementation on aerial and root traits under control (70% of FC) and severe drought stress (30% of FC). Drought stress exhibited significant reduction in root/shoot length, fresh and dry weight, and leaf physiological traits (chlorophyll index and leaf area index) of durum wheat.

Similar results were obtained by Rahnama et al. [73] who reported that drought stress decreased root traits. These results might be explained by the effect of drought conditions on the plant’s initial responses which involves stomatal closure to minimize transportational water loss. This event results in a decrease in the photosynthetic rate and carbon assimilation, a reduction in the cell division process leading to short stems, impaired root systems and reduced biomass accumulation [74–77].

Furthermore, our investigation showed that root traits, especially root dry weight and root length, were the most affected trait by water deficiency. Similar results were obtained by Khalil et al. [78] and Licaj et al. [3]. In this context, Shi et al. [79] reported that root traits undergo faster and more severe morphological changes under drought compared to shoot traits, due to direct exposure to the dry soil, and therefore to the variations in soil water potential.

After one month of growth (Z39), supplementary Si (15 mg L−1) resulted in significant increases in growth and physiological attributes under control and drought conditions. As reported by Maghsoudi et al. [80], Parveen et al. [81], and Tahir et al. [82], this effect was clearly more pronounced under stressed conditions. The observed superiority of Si application in enhancing most morphological and physiological traits can be attributed to its multi-faceted protective role. The beneficial role of Si is related mainly to the formation of a silica structure that acts as a protective barrier, which reduces water loss by limiting leaf transpiration while allowing light to penetrate for photosynthesis, ultimately boosting vegetative growth [83].

Si treatment remained more effective than Se treatments under both conditions (70% and 30% FC). Similar findings were obtained by Sattar et al. [39] for wheat grown under water-deficit stress conditions.

The current study divulged commonalities and differences in the mode of action of Si/Se on the areal and root parts of the plant. For instance, Si and Se (2 mg L−1) promoted more the elongation of roots than that of shoots under both conditions (control and 30% FC), while improving the shoot fresh and dry weight more than the roots. This boosting effect of these elements on root elongation more than shoot elongation can be attributed to their role in enhancing root cell wall strength and flexibility, improving water and nutrient uptake efficiency, which promotes root elongations. As roots, being in direct contact with soil, are the first organs to sense and respond to soil conditions, so Si mediated improvements in root structure and function can rapidly promote root elongation to explore more soil volume. In particular, as obtained in our study, more profuse and deeper root systems are an important factor in water and nutrient uptake and are often viewed as desirable traits for drought adaptation [84, 85]. Conversely, the greater increase in shoot fresh and dry weight compared to roots could be explained by an enhancement of water, macro- (including P, K, Ca, and Mg) and micronutrients (such as Fe, Cu, and Mn) uptake via the improved roots [73]. These results highlight the involvement of Si and Se in promoting and improving root architecture without compromising the vegetative system development.

Specific interactions between studied parameters were obtained according to the water regime. For instance, the root dry weight was correlated positively with shoot and root fresh weight, and leaf area index under both conditions (70 and 30% FC). However, root dry weight was exceptionally correlated with shoot dry weight and chlorophyll index under control conditions (70% FC). Shoot dry weight was also correlated positively with shoot and root fresh weight, chlorophyll index and leaf area index under control conditions, while no significant correlation was obtained under limited irrigation (30% FC). Our results are in part consistent with previous findings [86] showing that an increase in root weight may cause a simultaneous increase in other growth traits. As suggested by Narayanan et al. [87], the increase of root mass might have contributed to the increased shoot dry weight. In turn, the surplus of photoassimilates as a result of increased shoot growth might be allocated to roots that increased root dry weight.

Nonetheless, the absence of some correlations under severe water stress (30% FC) might be attributed to drought-adaptive traits of each variety. This study indicated also that root elongation was independent of shoot elongation. Sanguineti et al. [88] suggest that root weight and length are genetically controlled by different sets of genes in comparison to that of shoot length. Furthermore, no relationship between the elongation of the aerial and root parts and their biomass accumulation was noted. In the same sense, some studies have reported that decreased plant height genes had no impact on root diameter and root dry weight [89, 90]. Conversely, Arruda et al. [91] and Mahmonir et al. [92] reported that wheat root length was significantly and positively correlated with shoot length, and root and shoot biomass accumulation.

In this investigation, distinct and consistent variations were exhibited among durum wheat varieties in their reaction to Si/Se seed priming and fertilization. Although ‘Karim’ was the lowest performing genotype for growth and physiological attributes, it was the most receptive genotype to Si and Se. A comparable result was reported by Ayed et al. [16] following Si seed priming and foliar application of twenty-four durum wheat promising lines developed by ICARDA, while Othmani et al. [93] obtained conversed results.

Over all, substantial differences were obtained between the two assays. Se application was more effective on germination of durum wheat varieties, while Si was the promising treatment for growth and physiological performance. These results suggest that the beneficial effect of Si or Se is not universal. However, the obtained disparities may be partly due to methodological aspects including the type of applied stress (i.e., osmotic or drought stress), the mode of nutritional elements application (i.e., seed priming or fertigation) and the developmental stage.

Conclusions

Si (15 mg L−1) and Se (2 mg L−1) seed priming and fertigation provided effective strategies to mitigate the negative effects imposed by osmotic/drought stress on germination percentage, and root, shoot leaf physiological attributes and phenols, flavonoids and carotenoids contents of four durum wheat varieties. Notably, Se was the best treatment for germination, while Si was more effective for growth and physiological performance. These two elements acted in a similar way on the aerial and root parts of the plants, but some specific interactions depended on the applied water regime. Genotype-specific Si/Se responses were observed, and ‘Karim’ was the most receptive variety to these elements in different conditions especially in drought stress. These findings highlight the potential of selenium and silicon as promising eco-friendly approach to mitigate the adverse effects of drought and osmotic stress, and their use is strongly recommended in stress-prone environments.
