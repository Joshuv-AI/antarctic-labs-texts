Delayed at the Door: Impact of Pandemic Response Policies on Emergency and Critical Care in Korea: An Interrupted Time-Series Analysis
Authors: Ho Kyung Sung, Kyung-Shin Lee, Jeehye Lee, Min-hwan Oh, Jin Young Kim, Young Kyun Choi, Jae Young Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic strained emergency and critical care systems globally, limiting access. Although Korea has been recognized for effective containment, the impact of the pandemic and its response policies on critical care accessibility remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide interrupted time-series analysis of patients admitted from emergency department (ED) to intensive care unit (ICU) in Korea (January 2015–December 2023). The study period was divided into pre-pandemic, pandemic phase 1 (January 2020–March 2022; strict infection control with segregated COVID-19 care), and pandemic phase 2 (April 2022 onwards; reintegration of COVID-19 care). Negative binomial regression was used for ED-to-ICU admissions, and seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average models for the proportion of ED length of stay (EDLOS) ≥ 6 hours and in-hospital mortality.ResultsAt pandemic onset, ED-to-ICU admissions declined immediately by 6.66% (95% confidence interval [CI], –9.25 to –3.98) and continued to fall by 0.54% per month (95% CI, –0.69 to –0.39) throughout phase I. Despite this, in-hospital mortality rose by 0.08% monthly (95% CI, 0.02 to 0.14), while EDLOS ≥ 6 hours increased by 0.35% per month (95% CI, 0.24 to 0.47). In phase 2, ED-to-ICU admissions rebounded with a 1.22% monthly increase (95% CI, 0.96 to 1.48), while mortality (–0.10% monthly, 95% CI, –0.20 to –0.01) and EDLOS ≥ 6 hours (–0.98% monthly, 95% CI, –1.18 to –0.77) declined.ConclusionDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, marked changes in Korea’s ED-to-ICU admission and in-hospital mortality were observed, reflecting the consequence of pandemic response policies. While infection control was essential, strict segregated COVID-19 care system likely limited ICU access for non-COVID patients, contributing to reduced admissions and increased mortality. These findings underscore the need for policies that balance rigorous infection control with flexible ICU management and effective patient flow strategies during public health emergencies.

INTRODUCTION

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic placed an extraordinary burden on healthcare systems worldwide, particularly on emergency and critical care systems (ECCSs).1 The extent of these challenges varied across countries, depending on public health strategies, infrastructure, and response.2 In many regions, including the United States (US), the United Kingdom (UK), and Europe, emergency department (ED) visits declined sharply due to lockdowns and public fear of infection.34 Concurrently, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions fluctuated as hospitals faced resource reallocation, overcrowding, and shifting priorities.5

Korea has been internationally recognized for early COVID-19 containment through extensive testing, tracing, quarantine, and centralized healthcare management.6 Unlike many other countries that experienced systemic healthcare disruptions, Korea reportedly maintained access to primary care and other essential healthcare services.7 A study analyzed nationwide data indicates that overall healthcare utilization returned to pre-pandemic levels by late 2022, with minor disruptions for older adults and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.8

Although overall healthcare utilization remained stable, critical care access encountered significant constraints, particularly due to ED processing delays and ICU bed reallocation. ECCSs operate under unique constraints, where ED overcrowding, ICU capacity limitations, and resource reallocation policies may disproportionately affect critically ill patients.9 Studies from Korea have reported a significant decline in ED-to-ICU admissions and an increase in in-hospital mortality during the early pandemic phase, suggesting that pandemic-related policies, such as mandatory polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for SARS-CoV-2 and the reallocation of ICU beds exclusively for patients with COVID-19, may have contributed to critical care capacity constraints.1011 These findings contrast with reports that Korea’s healthcare system remained stable, raising questions about whether ECCSs experienced greater strain than previously recognized.

Safe and effective critical care is essential for mitigating the consequences of global health crises like pandemics.12 However, limited research has explored the pandemic’s sustained impact on ECCSs, particularly regarding the intersection of pandemic response policies and critical care access.13 This study aimed to quantify the COVID-19 response policies’ impact on ED-to-ICU admissions, critical care accessibility, and patient outcomes across different pandemic phases in Korea.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This study used an interrupted time series (ITS) analysis to assess the COVID-19 pandemic and response policies’ impact on Korea’s ECCSs from 2015 to 2023. The study period was divided into three phases based on major shifts in Korea’s pandemic response: pre-pandemic (before January 2020), pandemic phase 1 (January 2020–March 2022), and pandemic phase 2 (April 2022 onwards).

The first breakpoint (January 2020) corresponded to the confirmation of the first COVID-19 case and subsequent surge in Korea. In response, a segregated system was implemented to separate patients with and without COVID-19 in EDs and ICUs.14 Concurrently, PCR testing became mandatory for all ED patients before admission. The government also designated a substantial number of ICU beds in tertiary hospitals exclusively for patients with COVID-19, thereby reducing ICU availability for other patients.10

The second breakpoint (April 2022) marks the transition of COVID-19 care into routine hospital operations, with decentralized care and hospital capacity reallocation.15 Despite a decline in daily cases, the healthcare facility burden remains substantial. Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the policy changes, and Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates the trends in monthly COVID-19 cases and related mortality.

Data source and study population

We obtained data from the National Emergency Department Information System (NEDIS). NEDIS, established under Article 15 of the Emergency Medical Service Act, is a nationwide ED-based database designed to evaluate ECCSs in Korea. The system collects data on all ED visits, including demographics, ED level, arrival mode, arrival and departure times, triage scores, diagnostic codes, ED disposition, and clinical outcomes. Throughout the study period, the participation rate of EDs contributing to NEDIS data remained consistently high, ranging from 96% to 100% (Supplementary Table 2).

We extracted data on patients aged ≥ 18 years with ED-to-ICU admissions. Patients with missing information on the ED presentation date and time were excluded. Details of the NEDIS variables and data-processing procedures are available elsewhere.1116

Study outcomes

We analyzed changes in key ECCS performance indicators across pandemic phases, focusing on the monthly ED-to-ICU admission numbers, in-hospital mortality rate, and patient proportions with ED length of stay (EDLOS) ≥ 6 hours per month. EDLOS ≥ 6 hours was included because it is a widely used performance indicator for evaluating ED processes and is associated with increased in-hospital mortality among ED-to-ICU admissions.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were compared across the three study phases. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s χ2 test, whereas continuous variables were summarized as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The ten most common primary diagnoses in each phase were identified.

The optimal SARIMA parameters were selected using the auto.arima() function, based on the Akaike Information Criterion. Ljung-Box tests confirmed no significant autocorrelation in residuals. Robust standard errors were applied to account for the heteroscedasticity.

For ED-to-ICU admissions, results were reported as relative percentage changes, whereas for in-hospital mortality and EDLOS ≥ 6 hours, absolute percentage changes were used, as these outcomes are inherently expressed as percentages.

Recognizing the potential for heterogeneous effects of the pandemic and policy transitions across different populations, we conducted subgroup analyses. These analyses evaluated whether the observed changes in ICU admissions, in-hospital mortality, and EDLOS varied significantly based on key patient characteristics, including age, sex, comorbidities, triage scores, and ED levels. We also tested the pre-specified breakpoint robustness (January 2020 and April 2022) for outcomes using the Bai-Perron test as a data-driven approach to detect statistically significant structural changes without pre-specified breakpoints.17

All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were two-tailed, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The Ethics Committee of the Chungnam National University Sejong Hospital approved this study (CNUSH-2025-01-003). The requirement for patient consent was waived because the identities of all patients in the NEDIS were concealed.

RESULTS

Between 2015 and 2023, 1,918,446 adult patients were admitted from the ED to the ICU. The median age increased from 69 years (IQR, 56–79) pre-pandemic to 71 years (IQR, 59–81) in phase 2, with the proportion of patients aged ≥ 80 years rising from 24.1% to 31.2% (Table 1). The proportion of EMS arrivals increased from 42.4% pre-pandemic to 52.3% in phase 2, whereas inter-hospital transfers declined from 35.0% to 26.8%. Median EDLOSs increased in phase 1 and slightly decreased in phase 2, while the proportion of EDLOS ≥ 6 hours reached 32.0% in phase 1 before decreasing to 26.0% in phase 2. Monthly median EDLOS trends are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. The in-hospital mortality rate increased from 13.7% pre-pandemic to 15.2% in phase 1 and 15.5% in phase 2.

Table 3 presents the ITS analysis of ED-to-ICU admissions. A significant decline in ED-to-ICU admissions was observed in phase 1, with a 6.66% reduction (95% CI, –9.25 to –3.98) in January 2020, followed by a 0.54% monthly decrease (95% CI, –0.69 to –0.39%). In phase 2, ED-to-ICU admissions increased by 1.22% per month (95% CI, 0.96 to 1.48%) (Fig. 1). The decline was more pronounced among older adults. ICU admissions for patients aged ≥ 80 years decreased by 10.33% (95% CI, –14.15 to –6.33%) in phase 1, with a 0.63% monthly decline (95% CI, –0.86 to –0.39%). In phase 2, admissions increased by 7.16% (95% CI, 1.51 to 13.14%) at breakpoint 2, followed by a 1.36% monthly rise (95% CI, 0.96 to 7.76%). By acuity on ED arrival, ICU admissions among patients assessed as requiring immediate resuscitation (triage level 1) decreased by 5.82% (95% CI, –9.11 to –2.41%) at the onset of phase 1, followed by a monthly decline of 0.44% (95% CI, –0.64 to –0.25%). However, during phase 2, admissions rebounded, increasing by 2.27% per month (95% CI, 1.96 to 2.59%). By ED type, Level 1 EDs, which manage the most critical patients, experienced a 17.20% decline (95% CI, –23.80 to –9.99%) in phase 1, with a 1.72% monthly decrease (95% CI, –2.16 to –1.27%). In contrast, admissions increased by 1.22% per month (95% CI, 0.46 to 1.99%) after breakpoint 2.

ITS analysis results for EDLOS ≥ 6 hours are presented in Table 4. The proportion of ED-to-ICU-admitted patients with EDLOS ≥ 6 hours increased by 3.75% (95% CI, 1.69 to 5.82%) in phase 1, with a 0.35% monthly rise (95% CI, 0.24 to 0.47%). This trend reversed in phase 2, showing a 4.37% decline (95% CI, –6.65 to –2.09%) at breakpoint 2, followed by a 0.98% monthly decrease (95% CI, –1.18 to –0.77%) (Fig. 2). Among patients aged ≥ 80 years, EDLOS ≥ 6 hours increased by 6.96% (95% CI, 4.59 to 9.32%) in phase 1, with a 0.33% monthly rise (95% CI, 0.20 to 0.47%). In phase 2, this proportion declined by 6.77% (95% CI, –9.52 to –4.02%) at breakpoint 2, followed by a 1.03% monthly decrease (95% CI, –1.27 to –0.79%). A similar pattern was observed among patients with triage level 1, with a 4.38% increase (95% CI, 0.64 to 8.11%) in phase 1, followed by a 6.14% reduction (95% CI, –10.29 to –1.99%) in phase 2.

DISCUSSION

We investigated changes in ED-to-ICU admissions, EDLOS, and in-hospital mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic using nationwide ED data in Korea. In phase 1 of the pandemic, the government implemented a segregated COVID-19 care model that included mandatory PCR testing for ED patients prior to admission and the reallocation of ICU beds to COVID-19 care. Over this period, there was an immediate and sustained decline in ED-to-ICU admissions, coupled with prolonged EDLOS and a modest increase in mortality. These trends were more pronounced among older adults and critically ill patients assessed as requiring immediate resuscitation. By phase 2, as strict infection control measures eased and COVID-19 care was reintegrated into routine hospital operations, ICU admissions rebounded, EDLOS shortened, and in-hospital mortality began to decline—even as patient volume continued to rise.

Our findings contrast with those from healthcare systems where the surge of COVID-19 cases placed extreme pressure on critical care capacity. Several studies have demonstrated that ICU strain was associated with increased mortality among critically ill patients. A study of COVID-19 patients admitted to 88 US ICUs found that as COVID-19 ICU load increased, mortality risk rose significantly. When COVID-19 ICU load reached 75–100% of total ICU capacity, mortality risk was 67% higher, and when ICU capacity was exceeded, it more than doubled.18 In the UK, higher ICU burden during the pandemic was associated with increased mortality among COVID-19 patients.1 In Japan, national critical care demand was independently associated with increased mortality among mechanically ventilated COVID-19 patients.19 Beyond COVID-19 patients, ICU strain also negatively impacted critically ill non-COVID-19 patients. In the UK, non-COVID-19 patients admitted during peak ICU strain had a 16–30% higher risk of death.1 Similar findings were reported in Brazil and the Netherlands.2021 In Australia, ICU strain was associated with higher mortality, with increased after-hours discharges, readmissions, and interhospital transfers contributing to worse outcomes.22 In contrast, COVID-19-related ICU admissions in Korea accounted for only 0.67% of total ICU admissions in phase 1 and 1.93% in phase 2. These findings, reflecting the relatively low burden of COVID-19 cases in Korean ICUs, suggest that the decline in ED-to-ICU admissions and the increase in EDLOS observed in phase 1 were not driven by a surge in critically ill COVID-19 patients, as seen in other countries.

The initial decline in ED-to-ICU admissions and prolonged EDLOS during phase 1 likely stemmed from both demand- and supply-side factors. On the demand side, patients—particularly older adults—may have avoided emergency care due to fears of in-hospital infection, shaped in part by Korea’s MERS experience.23 A nationwide survey revealed that a substantial proportion of the population delayed or avoided seeking medical care during the pandemic,24 and national data showed a marked drop in ED visits for injuries following the implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions.25 While these behavioral shifts reduced ED volume, they do not fully explain the sustained decline in ICU admissions.

On the supply side, Korea’s adoption of a segregated COVID-19 care model introduced multiple barriers to critical care access during the first wave of the pandemic. This model, which aimed to minimize cross-infection and preserve ICU capacity for COVID-19 cases, entailed both institutional and procedural constraints that affected ICU utilization system-wide. Among the institutional constraints, ICU beds were preemptively designated for COVID-19 patients, with estimates indicating that 1–4% of total ICU beds were allocated for this purpose.10 Although the proportion was relatively small, many of these beds were held in reserve even when unoccupied,10 and some acute hospitals were converted into COVID-19–only facilities.2627 These structural decisions effectively reduced the available ICU capacity for non-COVID patients. In parallel, the model imposed significant procedural constraints. A particularly consequential measure was the mandatory PCR testing required prior to ICU admission,28 which delayed triage and bed assignment—especially for patients presenting with fever or respiratory symptoms.29 This policy likely contributed to the sharp increase in the proportion of patients experiencing EDLOS ≥ 6 hours during phase 1, as critically ill individuals waited for test results before ICU transfer could be initiated, a situation likely intensified during surges in COVID-19 cases which increased overall testing demand.

Taken together, these institutional and procedural constraints created substantial bottlenecks in the ED-to-ICU transfer process. ICU beds that were technically available often remained unused due to policy-imposed restrictions or clearance delays, while patients accumulated in the ED. Consequently, EDLOS increased markedly across patient groups, may have contributed to delays in definitive care and partially explain the observed rise in in-hospital mortality. These effects were especially pronounced among older adults and those requiring immediate resuscitation—two groups particularly vulnerable to deterioration during extended ED stays.3031 These observations are consistent with prior evidence showing that prolonged ED boarding is associated with clinical deterioration, delayed interventions (e.g., intubation, vasopressors, emergent surgery), and worse outcomes.16303233 The concurrent reallocation of EMS resources to COVID-19 transport duties further strained system responsiveness, reducing inter-hospital transfers and EMS-facilitated ICU admissions.34

In contrast, phase 2 marked a turning point in Korea’s pandemic strategy, with relaxed infection control policies and the reintegration of COVID-19 care into routine operations. The reclassification of COVID-19 to a Class 2 infectious disease and the lifting of mandatory isolation requirements signaled a shift toward routine care delivery.35 At the same time, mandatory PCR testing for ED admissions was replaced by clinician-directed risk assessments, thereby alleviating one of the key triage bottlenecks identified in phase 1.36 As previously segregated ICU beds were gradually reintegrated into general use and emergency medical service capacity was restored, inter-hospital transfers and ED-to-ICU admissions increased, accompanied by shorter EDLOS and modestly lower in-hospital mortality.

Together, these findings suggest that ICU accessibility and patient flow disruptions played a more critical role in pandemic-related mortality patterns than ICU strain. While studies from other regions have largely attributed increased mortality risk to ICU strain, our results highlight the importance of maintaining timely ICU access and minimizing ED boarding times during public health crises.

Our study's implications are twofold. Operationally, it highlights the critical need for flexible ICU admission protocols during health emergencies to mitigate prolonged EDLOS, a surrogate for delayed care and increased mortality. Optimizing ED-to-ICU admissions should involve real-time bed monitoring, enhanced patient flow, and dynamic admission criteria based on clinical assessment rather than rigid policies.373839 From a policy perspective, our findings support a balanced pandemic response. While infection control is crucial, strict segregation policies, like those in Korea’s phase 1, can cause ‘collateral damage’ by limiting ICU access for non-COVID-19 patients, potentially increasing their mortality and EDLOS.40 Our findings suggest that this segregated system, while primarily aimed at controlling infection spread, could have inadvertently contributed to the observed increase in in-hospital mortality and prolonged EDLOS for these non-COVID patients. However, such segregation may also curb overall infection spread and reduce direct COVID-19 ICU burden, a dual effect requiring careful evaluation.41 Future public health strategies should integrate contingency plans that preserve critical care capacity across all patient groups. Key approaches include: First, implementing dynamic ICU occupancy and patient flow management, featuring flexible targets and real-time data-driven admission criteria, potentially enhanced by adaptive forecasting.424344 Second, establishing regional resource-sharing networks and coordination centers, exemplified by Maryland’s successful Critical Care Coordination Center.38 Third, adopting measures to ensure ICU capacity resilience, such as reserving beds for non-pandemic emergencies or developing adaptable facility designs.4546 These strategies are urgent for Korea, given its low ICU bed-to-population ratio, rapidly aging population, and regional disparities in critical care capacity, emphasizing the importance of proactive and flexible ECCS resource management to mitigate potential bottlenecks during future pandemics.474849

Our study has several strengths. First, the nationwide ED data provides a uniquely broad and representative perspective on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on critical care access and patient outcomes. Second, this is the first study in Korea to longitudinally observe the full trajectory of pandemic impacts through multiple phases, examining not only the initial effects but also the sustained consequences on the ECCS. Additionally, the use of ITS analysis allowed us to systematically evaluate pandemic-related policy shifts over time.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study could not directly quantify specific ICU capacity constraints due to data limitations. The NEDIS dataset did not provide granular information regarding ICU bed capacity dedicated to non-COVID-19 patients, their specific ICU occupancy rates, or data on ED crowding and staffing levels. While COVID-19-related ICU admissions were a small fraction of the total, more detailed data on non-COVID ICU capacity fluctuations would be valuable. We also lacked data on pandemic-driven ICU workforce reallocations. Some hospitals created separate COVID-19 units outside the ICU and reassigned ICU staff,26 which may have affected non-COVID care. Future research should integrate such real-time hospital capacity and staffing metrics to better capture dynamic resource strain. Second, despite the strengths of the ITS analysis in adjusting for underlying temporal trends, we cannot rule out the possibility that other unmeasured confounders influenced the observed changes in our outcomes. For instance, concurrent variations in vaccination rates, the prevalence of different COVID-19 variants, advancements in COVID-19 treatment protocols, or shifts in public awareness and healthcare-seeking behavior could have independently affected ED-to-ICU admission, EDLOS, and in-hospital mortality. Our findings provide important evidence on the impact of COVID-19 response on ECCS, but future studies using methods are required to confirm causality. Lastly, while our study considers causal inference, its observational nature limits definitive conclusions. Further research using counterfactual models, such as synthetic control or difference-in-differences analysis, is needed to validate these associations and assess long-term policy impacts.

Our study found that ICU admission and mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic varied based on pandemic response policies. This finding emphasizes the need for carefully considered pandemic response strategies that balance infection control measures while maintaining critical care access. To prevent similar disruptions in future public health crises, healthcare systems should ensure that ICU admission criteria remain adaptable and that ED-to-ICU transfers are not hindered by overly rigid care segregation. Policies should focus on minimizing prolonged EDLOS, avoiding unnecessary restrictions on ICU access, and ensuring that critically ill patients receive timely intensive care.
