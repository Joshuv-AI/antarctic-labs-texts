The Sympathetic Nervous System Is Necessary for Development of CD4+ T-Cell Memory Following Staphylococcus aureus Infection
Authors: Jessica M Reel, Jumana Abbadi, A Jesse Bueno, Katarazyna Cizio, Richard Pippin, D Annie Doyle, Laura Mortan, Jeffrey L Bose, Maureen A Cox
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLymph nodes and spleens are innervated by sympathetic nerve fibers that enter alongside arteries. Despite discovery of these nerve fibers nearly 40 years ago, the role of these nerves during response to infection remains poorly defined. We have found that chemical depletion of sympathetic nerve fibers compromises the ability of mice to develop protective immune memory to a Staphylococcus aureus infection. Innate control of the primary infection was not impacted by sympathectomy. Germinal center formation is also compromised in nerve-depleted animals; however, protective antibody responses are still generated. Interestingly, protective CD4+ T-cell memory fails to form in the absence of sympathetic nerves after S aureus infection.Keywords: CD4 T-cell memory, immunity, infection, neuroimmunology, Staphylococcus aureus

There is increasing evidence that the nervous system participates in immune responses [1 , 2] and that deficits or changes in the neuronal response renders individuals susceptible to infection. Patients with peripheral nerve damage are much more susceptible to bacterial infection, especially Staphylococcus aureus, the most common pathogen isolated from these patients [3, 4]. Peripheral nerve damage can include sensory nerves, motor nerves, and autonomic nerves including sympathetic nerves [5–7]. Staphylococcus aureus also produces toxins to specifically manipulate the nervous system [8], driving activation of sensory nerves at the site of infection [9, 10] and in some cases loss of neuronal signaling in the periphery [11]. Overall, these findings suggest a role for the nervous system in protection from S aureus infection, which the pathogen attempts to subvert through the production of toxins. How the nervous system influences the course of S aureus infection, and which nerves mediate this effect, is still unknown.

The secondary lymphoid organs are predominantly innervated by the sympathetic nervous system [1, 12–15]. Immune cells express adrenergic receptors, particularly the β2 adrenergic receptor (β2AR) [16], and therefore can communicate with these nerves directly. The impact of this signaling during infection is complicated and incompletely understood. During viral infection, sympathetic signaling limits the cytotoxic response and slows viral control [17–19]. Similarly, mice lacking the β2AR are more resistant to Listeria monocytogenes [20, 21], indicating that the sympathetic nervous system limits immune activity. In sepsis models, adrenergic signaling limits inflammation and accelerates control of infection [22, 23]. In multiple studies, the sympathetic nervous system restrains the production of proinflammatory cytokines associated with a type 1 response, including interferon-γ [24–26], tumor necrosis factor–α [27], and interleukin 12 [28]. Protection from extracellular pathogens like S aureus is largely driven by neutralizing antibody, neutrophils, and the CD4+ T-helper 17 (Th17) response [29–36], so the impact of sympathetic nerves during S aureus infection is unknown.

The sympathetic nervous system drives the mobilization of neutrophils from the bone marrow to the periphery in response to pathogen signals [37–39]. Sympathectomy also impairs the formation of germinal centers in the spleen following vaccination [40]. Given that neutrophils and antibody are critical mediators of protection against S aureus, we hypothesized that the sympathetic nerves have a protective role during local S aureus infection. Interestingly, we found that both neutrophil and antibody responses were intact in sympathectomized mice. Instead, we found that loss of sympathetic nerves has a profound effect on the ability to generate a protective CD4+ memory T-cell response to S aureus.

METHODS

Mice and Sympathectomy

C57Bl6/J and CD45.1 (B6.SJL-PtprcaPepcb/BoyJ) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories. Sympathectomy was carried out by 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) treatment as previously described [17, 18]. Control mice were treated with vehicle alone. Mice were infected 2 weeks after the final 6-OHDA or vehicle treatment. Depletion of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)–positive nerves was confirmed in a subset of popliteal lymph nodes. Experiments were conducted in both male and female cohorts, and no sex-dependent differences in response were observed. All animal experiment adhered to Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines.

Bacteria and Infection

For each experiment, S aureus strain UAMS-1 were streaked onto tryptic soy agar (TSA) and grown overnight. Single colonies were selected and then grown overnight in tryptic soy broth (TSB). On the day of infection, 100 mL of TSB was inoculated with overnight culture, and bacteria were grown until they entered log phase growth as measured by absorbance 600 readings on a spectrophotometer (BioRad). Colony-formingunits (CFU) per milliliter was estimated from the A600 reading. Bacteria were diluted into sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to obtain inoculum to infect mice with either 105 CFU (primary infection) or 106 CFU (recall infection and transfer recipients) in a volume of 50 μL. All animals in an experimental cohort were infected with the same inoculum. The inoculum was plated on TSA and the actual infectious dose determined the following day. The range of infectious inoculum for primary infection across all experiments is 1–5 × 105 CFU of S aureus. The range of infectious inoculum for recall infection across all experiments is 1–5 × 106 CFU of S aureus. Recall infections were established in the contralateral foot to the primary infection 28 days after the primary infection.

Bacterial Burden Quantification

Infected footpads were removed and weighed. The footpad was disrupted either mechanically with disruptor beads in PBS alone or via digestion in Collagenase IV and DNase I in PBS for 30 minutes at 37°C and then mechanically disrupted through a 70 μM cell strainer. Disruption of the footpad was conducted in a volume of 1 mL. From this 1 mL volume, 100 μL of the disrupted footpad suspension was either directly plated on TSA or serially diluted and then plated, and allowed to grow overnight. The CFU per mL was determined. Total CFU was then divided by the footpad mass to determine the CFU/g of footpad in each mouse.

Chemokine Quantification

Mice were infected with S aureus in the left hind foot and the draining and nondraining popliteal lymph nodes isolated 12, 18, and 24 hours postinfection into PINK bead lysis tubes (Next Advance, Troy, New York) containing 1 mL of PBS with Halt protease inhibitor (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, Illinois). Lymph nodes were homogenized using a TissueLyser (Qiagen) and homogenates were snap frozen and stored at −80°C until thawed for the assay. Concentration of chemokines was quantified using the BioLegend LegendPlex Mouse Proinflammatory Chemokine Panel (BioLegend, San Diego, California).

Inflammatory Cell Migration

Mice were infected with S aureus in the left hind foot. Mice were injected with 108 FluoSpheres carboxylate 0.2 μM yellow-green microspheres (Life Technologies, Eugene Oregon) in PBS in both hind feet either at the time of infection, or 48 hours later. Mice were euthanized 24 hours after bead injection, and the draining and nondraining popliteal lymph nodes isolated. Lymph nodes were stained for flow cytometric markers to identify innate immune populations, and the number of Fluosphere+ innate immune cells was quantified and compared between draining and nondraining lymph nodes to measure migration of inflammatory cells.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Immuno plates (Nunc, Thermo Scientific, catalog number 442404) were coated with 10 μg/mL of S aureus peptidoglycan or lipoteichoic acid overnight at 4°C, then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS-T for 2 hours at room temperature (RT). Mouse sera were 2-fold serially diluted in PBS-T and loaded into coated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) plates. Naive mouse sera served as a negative control. Samples were incubated in the coated ELISA plates overnight at 4°C. Wells were washed with PBS-T and incubated for 1 hour with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat antimouse IgG1 (1:10 000) (Southern Biotech). Wells were washed again with PBS-T and developed for 5 minutes at RT with 2,2'-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) substrate (SeraCare). ABTS reactions were stopped with 10% wt/vol sodium dodecyl sulfate in H2O. Endpoint antibody titers were determined by measuring the optical density at 405 nm.

Adoptive Transfer

Draining inguinal and popliteal lymph nodes from 5 infected mice were pooled and processed for isolation of CD4+ T cells. CD4+ T cells were enriched using a magnetic separation kit (Stem Cell). Purified CD4+ T cells were counted, and cell counts normalized to inject equal numbers of enriched cells from mock-treated or 6-OHDA–treated mice. Cells were injected intravenously (IV). An aliquot of the purified cells was separated prior to injection and evaluated for CD4 and CD44 by flow cytometry. The actual number of antigen-experienced CD4+ T cells transferred was determined in each experiment. The range for the numbers of CD4+ T cells transferred was 5000–10 000 cells (mock-treated donors) or 10 000–30 000 cells (6-OHDA–treated donors). Recipient mice were infected 24 hours after transfer with a recall dose (1–5 × 106 CFU) of S aureus.

Serum Transfer

Serum was isolated with serum separator tubes (BD Biosciences) 21 days postinfection (dpi). Serum from 5 donors was pooled. Recipient C57Bl/6 mice were injected IV with 300 μL of pooled serum from either mock-treated or 6-OHDA–treated donors. Control mice were injected with 300 μL of normal mouse serum (Southern Biotech). Recipient mice were infected 3 hours after transfer with a recall dose (1–5 × 106 CFU) of S aureus.

Flow Cytometry

Popliteal and inguinal lymph nodes were dissociated by mechanical disruption through a 70 μM nylon mesh. Single-cell suspensions were stained with antibodies to surface antigens for 30 minutes at 4°C. Cells were washed and fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde. For transcription factor staining, cells were fixed overnight with True-Nuclear Transcription fixative (BioLegend). Cells were then stained the following day for transcription factors. Antibodies and concentrations used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescent Staining

Lymph nodes were isolated and snap-frozen in optimum cutting temperature medium in liquid nitrogen, and 10-μM frozen sections were cut on a cryostat. For germinal center staining, slides were warmed and dried for 30 minutes, washed 3 times in PBS for 5 minutes, and then blocked for 1 hour at RT with 10% normal goat serum, 5% BSA, 1% fish gelatin, and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS. Sections were stained with IgD-AF647 (BioLegend) and Ki67-FITC (Thermo Fisher) in PBS containing 10% goat serum, 5% BSA, and 1% fish gelatin for 90 minutes at RT. Slides were washed 3 times for 5 minutes in PBS before staining with DAPI for 1 minute. Slides were washed in PBS and sections mounted in Prolong Gold.

For TH staining, sections were warmed to RT and rehydrated in double-distilled water. Slides were blocked with 10% normal goat serum with 5% BSA, 1% fish gelatin, and 0.5% triton X-100 in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) for 2 hours. Block was removed and slides stained with a polyclonal rabbit antirat TH (Thermo Fisher OPA1-04050) at a 1:500 concentration overnight in a humidity chamber. Slides were washed 3 times for 10 minutes in HBSS, then stained with AlexaFluor488-conjugated donkey antirabbit polyclonal (BioLegend, 406416) at a 1:100 concentration for 1 hour. Slides were washed 4 times in HBSS for 5 minutes. Slides were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 1 minute, rinsed in HBSS, and mounted in Prolong Gold.

Sections were imaged on a Leica M205-MFC THUNDER microscope at ×10 magnification.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. For comparisons between 2 normally distributed groups, a Student t test was performed. For nonnormally distributed groups, a Mann–Whitney U test was performed between 2 groups. For groups of 3, a Kruskal–Wallis test was performed with Dunn post hoc analysis between groups. Pearson correlations were performed for bivariate analyses.

RESULTS

We chemically depleted sympathetic nerves with 6-OHDA, as described previously [17], and confirmed deletion of sympathetic nerves in the popliteal lymph node (Supplementary Figure 1). We then infected mock-treated and sympathectomized mice and evaluated bacterial control in a primary and secondary infection. While we did not observe any defect in the control of primary infection (Figure 1A), we found that sympathectomized mice were not protected from a second S aureus infection (Figure 1B). Animals were reinfected in the contralateral footpad 28 days after the primary infection with 10-fold higher bacterial titers. Naive mice were challenged with the same bacterial inoculum as previously infected mice. As expected for a recall response, neurologically intact previously infected mice showed rapid control of S aureus infection, with an average of a 10-fold reduction in bacterial titers compared to a primary infection (Figure 1B). In contrast, sympathectomized mice did not exhibit any protection from a secondary infection (Figure 1B), and in many instances had a higher bacterial burden compared to primary infection.

Primary control of S aureus is heavily dependent upon a rapid neutrophil response [29–31]. We found similar recruitment of neutrophils to the footpad during the early stages of primary infection (Figure 1C). This recruitment occurs despite the well-described role of the sympathetic nervous system in regulating neutrophil trafficking [37, 39, 41], indicating that presence of infection is sufficient to drive neutrophil recruitment even in the absence of sympathetic signaling. Recruitment of neutrophils to both the site of infection and the draining lymph node is primarily driven by CXCL1 produced at the site of infection [31]. We noted a modest decrease in CXCL1 in the draining popliteal lymph node 12 hours postinfection in sympathectomized mice, which recovers to the levels seen in mock-treated cohorts by 18 and 24 hours postinfection (Supplementary Figure 2A). Measurement of multiple other proinflammatory chemokines revealed no difference in the draining popliteal lymph node early during infection (Supplementary Figure 2A). Accordingly, recruitment of inflammatory cells from the site of infection to the draining lymph node was not impaired by sympathectomy (Supplementary Figure 2B). These data suggest that chemical sympathectomy does not impact the initial innate immune response to S aureus infection.

Recall immune responses are characterized by faster and more robust recruitment of immune cells to the site of infection, driving accelerated control. During a secondary infection, memory CD4+ T cells produce interleukin 17 (IL-17), enhancing neutrophil recruitment. Antibody to the pathogen also forms immune complexes to neutralize toxins, directly bind to pathogens, and facilitate recruitment of innate immune cells. These processes act in addition to the pathogen-derived signals driving neutrophil recruitment. We compared the recruitment of neutrophils and CD4+ T cells to the infected footpad in primary and recall responses. The total number of either neutrophils or CD4+ T cells present in the footpad was not different in any of the 4 groups (Supplementary Figure 3); however, presence of pathogen is also a major driving factor for immune cell recruitment. We normalized to the pathogen titers in the footpad to determine the relative recruitment of either neutrophils or CD4+ T cells per bacteria. In primary infection and in sympathectomized recall mice, we observe approximately 1 neutrophil per bacteria and 1 CD4+ T cell for every 10 bacteria in the infected foot (Figure 1D). In contrast, neurologically intact mice that had previously been infected had significantly better recruitment of both neutrophils and CD4+ T cells relative to their bacterial burden, with 10 neutrophils for every bacterium and 1 CD4+ T cell for every bacterium (Figure 1D). These results indicate the sympathetic nervous system is critical for formation of adaptive immune memory that can then respond to a secondary infection.

Due to the defect in immune memory in sympathectomized mice, we evaluated the B- and T-cell response after S aureus infection. Draining popliteal lymph nodes were sectioned and stained for germinal centers 14 dpi. While robust germinal centers were observed in all neurologically intact mice, sympathectomized mice formed small abortive germinal centers or had no discernable germinal centers at all (Figure 2A and 2B). These results corroborate previous vaccination studies in sympathectomized mice [40]. Interestingly, despite the profound loss of germinal centers, antibody titers to 2 S aureus antigens, peptidoglycan and lipoteichoic acid, were not different 21 dpi (Figure 2C and D). Germinal centers are critical sites for affinity maturation of antibody responses; therefore, the quality of the antibody response may still be impacted, even if the quantity of antibody is not. We adoptively transferred serum from intact or sympathectomized mice 21 dpi (Figure 2E). Control mice received naive mouse serum. Serum recipients were challenged 3 hours after transfer with a recall dose of S aureus, and bacterial titers in the footpad were measured 7 days postchallenge. Serum from both intact and sympathectomized mice provided robust protection from infection, with a 2-log reduction in bacterial burden compared to mice receiving naive mouse serum (Figure 2F). These results indicate that the antibody response to S aureus infection is intact in sympathectomized mice 21 dpi, despite the failure to form germinal center responses.

Memory responses to S aureus are mediated by both B cells and CD4+ T cells; therefore, we quantified antigen-experienced CD44hiCD4+ T cells in the footpads and draining popliteal and inguinal lymph nodes 21 dpi. Notably, we observed a decrease in both the total number of CD44hiCD4+ T cells and in RORgT+ Th17 cells in the footpads of sympathectomized mice 21 dpi (Figure 3A). In the draining popliteal and inguinal lymph nodes, we did not find any differences in the number of total CD44hiCD4+ T cells or in the number of CD44hiRORgT+ Th17 or FoxP3+ regulatory T cells between our sympathectomized and neurologically intact mice (Figure 3A). We do not have a marker for pathogen specificity in the CD4+ T cells. As the CD4+ T cells in the footpad are all likely to be responding to S aureus infection, the diminution in the number of CD4+ T cells in the footpad could indicate poor generation or retention of S aureus–specific memory CD4+ T cells.

We tested the protective capacity of CD4+ memory T cells with an adoptive transfer experiment (Figure 3B). Antigen-experienced CD4+ T cells were isolated from the draining lymph nodes of either intact or sympathectomized mice 21 days after S aureus infection. These CD4+ T cells were transferred into recipient CD45.1 mice, and recipients challenged 1 day posttransfer with a recall dose of S aureus. Control mice were injected with PBS alone (Figure 3C). CD4+ T cells from neurologically intact mice provided some protection from infection compared to control mice 7 dpi (Figure 3B). In contrast, T cells from sympathectomized mice did not provide any protection from infection (Figure 3B). We compared the number of CD45.2+ donor cells recovered in the draining popliteal lymph node of recipient mice to the bacterial burden in the foot of the T-cell recipients. In mice that received CD4+ T cells from intact mice, the number of donor cells recovered negatively correlated with bacterial burden (Figure 3C), indicating that the presence of these cells was protective. In contrast, in mice that received CD4+ T cells from sympathectomized mice, there was no statistically significant correlation between the number of donor cells recovered and bacterial control (Figure 3C), indicating that the CD4+ T cells primed in the absence of sympathetic nerves were not effective at combating the infection. The Th17 cytokine IL-17 is a strong chemoattractant for neutrophils. We measured the relative recruitment of neutrophils to the footpad with regard to bacterial burden in T-cell recipient mice. We found that the mice that had received memory CD4+ T cells from intact mice had a significant enhancement in neutrophil recruitment to the infected footpad compared to mice that received either memory CD4+ T cells from sympathectomized mice or no T cells at all (Figure 3D).

DISCUSSION

In our current study, we found that an intact sympathetic nervous system is necessary for the development of CD4+ T-cell immune memory following S aureus infection. CD4 T cells are critical for protection from S aureus infection; patients with uncontrolled human immunodeficiency virus or hyper-IgE syndrome, which compromises Th17 responses, are at high risk for recurrent severe infections [42, 43]. Furthermore, patients with neurological damage resulting from either advanced diabetes, spinal cord injury, or stroke are also at high risk for severe, frequent S aureus infections [3, 4, 44]. While each of these conditions also can drive pressure sores, and vascular defects in the case of diabetic foot ulcers, our results would suggest that the neurological damage itself may inhibit the formation of memory CD4+ T-cell responses, rendering these patients susceptible to recurrent infections.

How sympathetic signaling promotes memory CD4+ T-cell formation is less clear. While there is controversy over the role of sympathetic signaling in antigen presentation [17, 45], our results from the primary infection would suggest this is not defective in our model. While we do not have a measure of S aureus specificity for our CD4+ T cells, we did not find defects in the number of activated CD4+ T cells in either the draining lymph nodes or footpads early after infection (Figure 3A, Supplementary Figure 3B). This would suggest that the initial priming of the T-cell response may be intact.

In CD8+ T cells, adrenergic signaling is known to inhibit T cell receptor–dependent functions [46, 47], and it has been shown that this signaling acts, in part, by altering the metabolic reprogramming of the CD8+ T cells to a less glycolytic state [47]. Elevated aerobic glycolysis is associated with poor formation of memory CD4+ [48] and CD8+ [49, 50] T cells. It is possible that sympathetic signaling during infection similarly metabolically reprograms a subset of responding CD4+ T cells, biasing their entry into the memory pool and promoting their secondary proliferative capacity.

Our study confirms previous work that demonstrated that depletion of sympathetic nerves impairs the formation of germinal centers in the spleen [40]. While we did not find an early impact on antibody production, we do not know if these antibody responses are long-lived, or if the antibody response in sympathectomized mice will deteriorate over time. These 2 studies together highlight the critical role of the sympathetic nervous system in the formation of germinal centers in lymph nodes and spleen, both in response to infection and in response to vaccination. Future work will be needed to determine if direct adrenergic signaling to B cells is necessary for the formation of germinal centers, or if these defects are due to the aberrations we have observed in the CD4+ T-cell response in sympathectomized mice.
