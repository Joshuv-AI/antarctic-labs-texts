Neural sensitivity to peer hierarchies: Risks for depressive symptoms for low status adolescents
Authors: Jimmy Capella, Nathan A Jorgensen, Seh‐Joo Kwon, Maria T Maza, Mitchell J Prinstein, Kristen A Lindquist, Eva H Telzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPopularity is increasingly salient and impactful in adolescence, and unpopular youth are at significantly greater risk of experiencing depressive symptoms. However, adolescents can vary in their neural sensitivity to social stimuli, such that some adolescents may be more impacted by social status than others. We examined whether adolescents' neural sensitivity to popular and unpopular peers moderates the extent that their own social status is related to trajectories of depressive symptoms across 5 years (6th–10th grades). During an fMRI scan, adolescents from the southeastern United States (n = 116, 61 female, 29% White Non‐Hispanic, 36% Hispanic, 22% Black/African American, 11% multiracial, 2% other; M age = 13.59, SD = 0.59) viewed pictures of their popular and unpopular classmates based on sociometric nominations from their social networks. Results indicated that heightened amygdala sensitivity to unpopular peers moderated the relations between participants' own popularity and their depressive symptoms, leading to increased depressive symptoms for unpopular youth. These findings suggest that individual differences in sensitivity to peer hierarchies impact the degree to which adolescents are negatively impacted by their own level of popularity.Keywords: adolescence, depression, fMRI, popularity

INTRODUCTION

Adolescence is a sensitive period for social development wherein peer hierarchies become particularly salient, and attaining social status is highly valued (LaFontana & Cillessen, 2010). Heightened sensitivity to peer hierarchies plays a significant role in shaping goals and behavior (Crone & Dahl, 2012), with high and low status conferring benefits and risks for a various social behaviors. For example, higher status adolescents typically have lower internalizing symptoms (Sandstrom & Cillessen, 2006), whereas low status is associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms (reviewed in Platt et al., 2013). Notably, these associations are not uniform across all adolescents. For instance, adolescents can vary in their level of concern about their own status within the peer group. While some are particularly focused on whether they are accepted or rejected by peers, others place less value on their own social standing. This varying level of concern, known as peer importance, has been shown to moderate the relations between status and outcomes, such that heightened peer importance enhances the likelihood of internalizing symptoms among low status adolescents (Prinstein & Aikins, 2004).

Since individuals' level of peer importance can enhance or buffer against detrimental outcomes for low‐status adolescents, it is important to better understand how adolescents perceive and integrate information about status within their social environments. Recently, neuroscientists have begun to explore the neural processes by which individuals perceive social status in their real world social networks (reviewed in Capella & Telzer, 2024). In the present study, we examined whether adolescents' neural sensitivity to their high and low‐status peers impacted the relations between their own social status and their longitudinal trajectories of depressive symptoms.

Sensitivity to social status and depressive symptoms

Adolescents with low social status (i.e., those who are chronically rejected or disliked by their peers) are more likely to report increased depressive symptoms (reviewed in Platt et al., 2013). This association is likely bidirectional—lower status prospectively predicts depressive symptoms among adolescents (Nolan et al., 2003; Prinstein & Aikins, 2004; Sentse et al., 2010; Witvliet et al., 2010), and depressive symptoms can increase the risk of later peer rejection (Vernberg, 1990). For low‐status youth, heightened peer importance—the degree to which individuals are concerned with their social standing within their peer groups—significantly increases the likelihood of depressive symptoms (Prinstein & Aikins, 2004). Among rejected or unpopular youth, peer importance may increase the risk of depression in a variety of ways. For example, low‐status youth who wish to increase their social standing may engage in status‐seeking behaviors to achieve social goals (Crone & Dahl, 2012; Nesi & Prinstein, 2019) and experience frustration, cognitive dissonance, and sadness when those goals are not met (Pelham & Swann, 1989; Prinstein & Aikins, 2004). Another possibility is that peer importance heightens the salience of interactions with high‐status youth and increases the negative impacts of rejection or victimization from these peers (de Water et al., 2017). Lastly, given that adolescents are particularly sensitive to social evaluation and peer rejection (Somerville, 2013), there may also be value in attending to which peers are particularly low in status, such that they can avoid status losses that could result from associating with those peers or mimicking their behavior (Dijkstra et al., 2013). Thus, studying the processes by which adolescents perceive and integrate information about status in their networks may further illuminate the effects of peer importance on depressive outcomes.

Neural sensitivity to social status

Studying the neural mechanisms by which individuals perceive status in their peer groups may provide insights into which low‐status adolescents might be at greater risk for developing depressive symptoms. Theories of neurobiological sensitivity to social contexts posit that individual differences in neural sensitivity to the social environment contribute to individual differences in outcomes, such that those who are most sensitive will be more impacted by positive and negative contexts (Schriber & Guyer, 2016). These individual differences can be captured by examining neural activation or connectivity in relevant socio‐affective regions in response to salient social stimuli, and can ultimately inform our understanding of variations in adolescent outcomes (Do et al., 2020; Schriber & Guyer, 2016). While low social status is an indicator of how negative one's social environment is (Prinstein, 2017), adolescents might vary in the degree to which they attribute salience to or value these peer hierarchies and are subsequently impacted by them. Thus, heightened neural response to information pertaining to social status, particularly in regions associated with salience or valuation, may contribute to increased risks for low‐status youth.

Recent research has begun to probe the neural mechanisms by which individuals perceive social status in their networks. Previous studies among older adolescents (Morelli et al., 2018) and young adults (Parkinson et al., 2017; Zerubavel et al., 2015), as well as previous analyses of early adolescents from the current study sample (Capella et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2023), indicated that regions commonly implicated in salience and valuation, including the ventral striatum (VS), amygdala, insula, and cingulate cortex, spontaneously encode information about the social status or social connectedness of individuals' peers. Given that these regions are considered to signal the salience and motivational significance of stimuli (Adolphs, 2010; Van Duijvenvoorde et al., 2016) and are extensively implicated in functional networks related to social behavior (Bickart et al., 2014; Fareri & Delgado, 2014), sensitivity to social status in these regions might signal the differential importance of high and low‐status peers in relation to individuals' thoughts and social behaviors.

During early adolescence, neurobiological changes in regions implicated in salience and valuation increase the importance of peer hierarchies, norms, and feedback (Blakemore & Mills, 2014; Foulkes & Blakemore, 2016; Nelson et al., 2016; Somerville, 2013). While heightened neural sensitivity to social stimuli is a normative feature of adolescence, hypersensitivity to experiences, such as peer evaluations or rejection, has been associated with depressive symptoms (Silk et al., 2012, 2014). Moreover, sensitivity to cues related to acceptance and rejection is considered to specifically play a role in the negative outcomes of adolescents who are chronically rejected (i.e., low status) in their peer contexts (Platt et al., 2013). Sensitivity to high or low social status in regions such as the VS, amygdala, insula, and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) might similarly reflect heightened salience of information pertaining to acceptance or rejection in the peer environment and potentially relate to adolescents' susceptibility to the negative effects of low status.

The current study

In the current study, we examined how individual differences in neural responses to high and low‐status peers moderate the link between adolescents' own status to predict longitudinal trajectories of depressive symptoms. From a sample of over 900 adolescents, data were collected across an accelerated longitudinal design, such that four waves of data collection spanned sixth through tenth grades (Cohort 1: 6th–9th grade, Cohort 2: 7th–10th grade). Across each wave of data collection, we obtained sociometric ratings of peer status and self‐reported depressive symptoms. During the second wave of data collection, a subset of these participants completed a novel fMRI task, the “Classmates Task,” wherein they viewed yearbook photos of their classmates, such that participants saw sequences of high‐status (e.g., most popular) and low‐status (e.g., least popular) peers from their school grade.

We tested the links between adolescents' own status and their neural sensitivity to high‐ and low‐status peers on trajectories of depressive symptoms across adolescence. We examined neural sensitivity in the VS, amygdala, anterior insula, and dACC given their role in tracking social status in adolescents and young adults (Capella et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2023; Morelli et al., 2018; Parkinson et al., 2017; Zerubavel et al., 2015). We hypothesized a significant interaction between neural response to high‐status peers in these regions and participants' own status, such that greater activation in these regions when viewing the most popular peers from their school will increase the risk of depressive symptoms for low‐status youth. Given the minimal research evaluating how individuals track information about low peer status (Koski et al., 2015), our analyses concerning neural response to adolescents' least popular peers are more exploratory. However, it may be the case that we identify similar effects for neural sensitivity to adolescents' least popular peers, particularly in regions sensitive to rejection‐related information such as the insula and dACC (Premkumar, 2012), as heightened neural responses to these peers could represent a greater attunement to the risks and consequences of low status in the peer context.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were recruited from three rural public middle schools in the southeast United States (N = 1385) as part of a 5‐year longitudinal study. See Figure 1 for a flowchart of participants recruited, enrolled, and excluded for the present analyses, which utilize data collected across 4 waves when participants were in the 6th (cohort 1) and 7th (cohort 2) grade (wave 1, 2016–2017 school year), 7th (cohort 1) and 8th (cohort 2) grade (wave 2, 2017–2018 school year), 8th (cohort 1) and 9th (cohort 2) grade (wave 3, 2018–2019 school year), and 9th (cohort 1) and 10th (cohort 2) grade (wave 4, 2019–2020 school year). Adolescents and parents gave written assent/consent in accordance with the university's Institutional Review Board.

Flowchart detailing the recruitment process and exclusion criteria that resulted in the final analysis sample.

At wave 1, 6th and 7th graders (N = 1385) were invited to participate, and 76.4% (n = 1059) of families returned consent forms. Of these families, 88.3% (n = 935) gave consent for their child to participate in yearly data collection. Of the 935 original participants, a random selection of 284 (30.3%) were screened and invited to participate in a concurrent fMRI study. Ninety‐one participants were ineligible due to learning disabilities, braces, head trauma, or other MRI contraindications; 45 were eligible but did not participate because of scheduling difficulties or lack of interest. A final sample of 148 adolescents completed a scanning session that took place between November 2016 and September 2017. An additional 30 participants were recruited in wave 2 to account for attrition. Participants completed annual fMRI scans, each 1 year apart. A total of 135 participants completed the Classmates task at wave 2. Of these, 3 were excluded from analyses for excessive head motion (>2 mm in any direction), 15 were excluded due to an error in calculating the sociometric ratings of target images in the Classmates task (see the “fMRI Task” subsection for more details), and 1 was excluded due to being an extreme outlier in neural data (>9 SD), resulting in a final sample of 116 adolescents (61 female, 55 male) ages 12–15 at scan (M age = 13.59, SD = 0.582). Participants were from diverse racial/ethnic backgrounds (42 Hispanic/Latino, 34 White, 25 Black/African American, 13 multiracial, 2 Asian, Native American, or Pacific Islander), in line with the overall demographics of the larger school sample (Analysis Sample/School Sample: Hispanic/Latino: 36%–30%; White: 29%–34%, Black/African American: 22%–22%; Multiracial: 11%–11%, Asian, Native American, or Pacific Islander: 2%–3%).

Procedures

Data used in the current study include self‐reported depressive symptoms and sociometric ratings of social status collected at school during waves 1, 2, 3, and 4, as well as the Classmates fMRI task collected at wave 2. For the school visits, adolescents were compensated $5 for completing the surveys and sociometric data. For the fMRI visit, adolescents were compensated with a monetary remuneration of $90, a $20 gift card for performing well in the scanner (e.g., minimum motion), an extra $25 as a bonus for each additional wave they completed (e.g., $25 at wave 2, $50 at wave 3), snacks during the visit, and a meal. Parents were compensated with a monetary remuneration of $50, as well as a meal, compensation for gas, and parking.

Measures

Sociometric ratings of social status

Sociometric procedures were used to measure popularity as part of the larger study, and sample sizes for each school/grade group can be found in the Table S1. During the school‐based assessment, participants were given a full list of their peers within their school and grade level and were asked to identify (1) who is the most popular, and (2) who is the least popular. There was no limit to the number of peers they could nominate. Based on the peer nominations from the school‐based assessment, we calculated popularity (z‐score of “most popular” minus z‐score of “least popular”) scores for each participant in the school. This z‐score is based on their relative rating compared to other students within their school and grade. These ratings of popularity for each wave were used as time‐varying covariates in the final model. In addition, the nominations collected during wave 1 were used to create the stimuli in the fMRI task, which was administered at wave 2. Across all waves, nominations were collected between 2 and 4 months into the school year (October 29th–December 18th), providing participants with enough time to become acquainted with their peers.

Depressive symptoms

Participants completed a modified version of the Short Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (Angold et al., 1995). Adolescents reported on the degree to which 9 feelings or experiences applied to them in the previous 2 weeks on a 3 point scale (0 = not true, 1 = sometimes true, 2 = mostly true). The scale included items about symptoms such as mood (e.g., “I felt miserable or unhappy,” “I didn't enjoy anything at all”), and self‐worth (e.g., “I hated myself,” “I felt I was a bad person”). A total sum score for all items was calculated (Cronbach's α: .88–.92 across waves).

fMRI task

During the Classmates task, adapted from Parkinson et al. (2017), participants viewed yearbook photos of their peers from school. The yearbook photos (i.e., targets) used in the task were selected based on the sociometric data from the previous year (wave 1), because of the time required to process the sociometric data and create the scan task. However, we conducted correlations between wave 1 and wave 2 for popularity to establish that popularity was highly stable across years (i.e., High Popularity: r = .67, p < .001; Low Popularity: r = .76) in line with prior research (Dijkstra et al., 2013). It is important to note that the task gauged sensitivity to two dimensions of social status, popularity (whether one is viewed by their peers as being popular, regardless of whether they are well liked) and social preference (whether one is well‐liked by their peers), but the current study solely focuses on popularity, since enhancing popularity is particularly valued by adolescents and has the greatest potential to influence outcomes (Gommans et al., 2017). To be selected as a target for the task, the peer needed to have a sociometric z‐score between 1 and 5 (representing 1–5 SD above the mean on popularity/social preference in their school and grade) or between −1 and −5 (representing 1–5 SD below the mean on popularity/social preference in their school and grade). One version of the task was created for each grade level within each school (three middle schools, each with 2 grades, resulting in six versions total). The task had four conditions: High social preference (i.e., z‐score between 1 and 5 on social preference), low social preference (i.e., z‐score between −1 and −5 on social preference), high popularity (e.g., z‐score between 1 and 5 on popularity), and low popularity (i.e., z‐score between −1 and −5 on popularity). Within each condition, there were 10 targets, and we aimed for an equal number of boys and girls within each condition. Due to a data management error, z‐scores for the targets from one of the schools (two of the six task versions) were incorrect and did not necessarily fall within the criteria (i.e., z‐score between ±1 and 5). Popularity and social preference scores were recalculated for the target images in these task versions. For one group of participants (n = 20), there were a sufficient number of target images that still fit the criteria for each condition (High Popularity = 9, Low Popularity = 8, High Social Preference = 8, Low Social Preference = 10), so these participants were included. For the other group, there were an insufficient number of target images fitting the criteria (e.g., as few as 5) in the conditions, so these participants (n = 15) were excluded. Across all versions of the task, the average z‐score within each condition was approximately 2 (Table S2). There was no overlap in the targets across conditions, so each target belonged to only one sociometric category and appeared in only one condition. No participants were included as targets so that no participant would see their own image. Target photos were obtained from school yearbooks from the previous school year, the same year the sociometric ratings were collected. Yearbook photos were digitized into JPEG images.

The task was programmed in E‐Prime and presented across two runs. Each run consisted of eight blocks, with two blocks per condition, each containing 10 faces. Within each run, participants were presented with the eight blocks in a randomized order. The order in which their faces were shown was fixed within the block, with the order pre‐selected based on a randomization algorithm. We used an n‐back task design (Parkinson et al., 2017) to ensure that the participants were paying attention, such that each block contained one target that appeared twice in a row. Participants were instructed to press with their right pointer finger when a target was repeated. Due to the limited number of peers who satisfied our criteria for high and low status (greater or less than one standard deviation from the average) and in order to achieve the power for neuroimaging analysis on visual stimuli, we repeated each peer face across the conditions, in line with previous studies (e.g., Parkinson et al., 2017; Zerubavel et al., 2015). Having participants passively view faces was also in line with these previous studies in young adults, allowing us to probe the spontaneous neural processes adolescents use to encode social status information. The repeated targets were fixed in the task and balanced so that no target was shown more than another (i.e., when a target was shown twice in one block, that target was absent from the next block). Thus, participants saw each face 4 times in total (2 in each run), with each condition having 40 total trials each. Each target face was presented for 1750 ms, separated by a jittered inter‐trial interval (M = 2300 ms, range: 565.8–4936.8 ms). Each block lasted approximately 40.5 s, and the total time of each run was 336 s.

fMRI data acquisition and preprocessing

Imaging data were collected using a 3 Tesla Siemens Prisma MRI scanner. The Classmates Task was presented on a computer screen and projected through a mirror. A high‐resolution structural T2‐weighted echo‐planar imaging (EPI) volume (TR = 2000 ms; TE = 25 ms; matrix = 92 × 92; FOV = 230 mm; 37 slices; slice thickness = 3 mm; voxel size 2.5 × 2.5 × 3 mm3) was acquired coplanar with a T2‐weighted structural matched‐bandwidth (MBW), high‐resolution, anatomical scan (TR = 5700 ms; TE = 65 ms; matrix = 192 × 192; FOV = 230 mm; 38 slices; slice thickness = 3 mm). In addition, a T1* magnetization‐prepared rapid‐acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE; TR = 2400 ms; TE = 2.22 ms; matrix = 256 × 256; FOV = 256 mm; sagittal plane; slice thickness = 0.8 mm; 208 slices) was acquired. The orientation for the EPI and MBW scans was oblique axial to maximize brain coverage and to reduce noise. Preprocessing was conducted using FSL (FMRIB's Software Library, version 6.0; www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) and included the following steps: Skull stripping using BET (Smith, 2002); motion correction with MCFLIRT (Jenkinson et al., 2002); spatial smoothing with Gaussian kernel of full width at half maximum (FWHM) 6 mm; high‐pass temporal filtering with a filter width of 128 s (Gaussian‐weighted least‐squares straight line fitting, with sigma = 64.0 s); grand‐mean intensity normalization of the entire 4D dataset by a single multiplicative factor; and individual level ICA denoising for motion and physiological noise using MELODIC (version 3.15; Beckmann & Smith, 2004), combined with an automated signal classifier (Tohka et al., 2008; Neyman–Pearson threshold = .3). For the spatial normalization, the EPI data were registered to the T1 image with a linear transformation, followed by a white‐matter boundary based transformation (BBR; Greve & Fischl, 2009) using FLIRT, linear and nonlinear transformations to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) 2‐mm brain were performed using Advanced Neuroimaging Tools (ANTs; Avants et al., 2011), and then spatial normalization of the EPI image to the MNI. Quality check during preprocessing and analyses ensured adequate signal coverage.

fMRI data analysis

Individual‐level, fixed‐effects analyses were estimated using the general linear model convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function in SPM12. We modeled the task as an event‐related design with four separate conditions wherein sociometric ratings were used as parametric modulators: high popularity, low popularity, high social preference, and low social preference. Intertrial jitters were not explicitly modeled and thus served as an implicit baseline. The absolute value of the sociometric rating for the target (i.e., social preference score for the low and high social preference conditions; popularity score for the low and high popularity conditions) was included as a parametric modulator (PM) at the trial level. Importantly, the sociometric ratings ranged from relatively lower to higher scores within each of the four conditions, which allowed us to examine whether adolescents linearly track peer status at the neural level within each condition (e.g., linear tracking of peers with sociometric popularity between 1 and 5 SD above average for the high popularity condition, and between 1 and 5 SD below average for the low popularity condition, Table S2). Within each block, one target was repeated as an attention check. The repeated target was treated as a separate condition and was modeled as a contrast of no interest. TRs with motion greater than .5 framewise displacement were modeled as a nuisance regressor.

fMRI regions of interest

We extracted parameter estimates for the bilateral VS, amygdala, insula, and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex. These regions were selected given their consistent association with tracking status in adults (Morelli et al., 2018; Parkinson et al., 2017; Zerubavel et al., 2015) and overall social valuation and salience in adolescents (Blakemore & Mills, 2014). We used bilateral ROIs that were defined using the Harvard‐Oxford Atlas. For each participant, we extracted parameter estimates from these ROIs for the high popularity condition and the low popularity condition separately for each participant. In order to retain as much data as possible, parameter estimates that were above or below 3 standard deviations from the mean were winsorized to 3 standard deviations.

Data analysis plan

Growth curves for depressive symptoms were fitted using a five‐timepoint multilevel model, where grades were nested within individuals. Starting with an unconditional growth model, analyses followed a formal model‐building procedure to determine the inclusion of random intercepts and slopes, as well as whether there is heteroscedastic error across grades. Intercepts for growth trajectories were centered at grade 6. The inclusion of random slopes and heteroscedastic error across grades was sequentially fit and tested via a likelihood ratio test (i.e., difference between two models in their degrees of freedom) and level of significance of p < .05. Next, given the differences in depressive symptom trajectories between adolescent boys and girls (Angold et al., 1998; Nolen‐Hoeksema & Girgus, 1994), participant sex was included as a dichotomous between‐person, time‐invariant (level 2) predictor, resulting in a cross‐level sex‐by‐grade interaction to assess sex differences in depressive trajectories over time. We chose to use sex, a time‐invariant predictor, rather than self‐reported gender, which can change over time, in order to maximize model parsimony. Three participants in our sample self‐reported a gender that did not align with their sex (options included “non‐binary” or “self‐identify” depending on the wave of data collection) at some point during the study. Sensitivity analyses excluding these participants did not significantly change our findings, so these individuals were included in our analyses.

Next, participants' own status (i.e., peer nominated popularity) was added as a within‐person, time‐varying (level 1) predictor to evaluate how individuals' status at each timepoint influences their trajectories of depressive symptoms.

Lastly, participants' sensitivity to their most and least popular peers, measured through separate parametric modulator conditions in the classmates task, was included as a time‐invariant predictor that interacts with their own status. This allowed us to test the primary research question of the study, whether neural sensitivity to social status moderates the effect of low social status on trajectories of depressive symptoms. These final models were run separately for each condition (high popularity and low popularity) and each ROI (VS, amygdala, insula, and anterior cingulate cortex), for a total of eight separate tests: VSHighPop, VSLowPop, AmygdalaHighPop, AmygdalaLowPop, InsulaHighPop, InsulaLowPop, dACCHighPop, dACCLowPop. Within conditions, results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). To ensure that significant results weren't being unduly influenced by participants' familiarity or friendship with target individuals in the Classmates task, we ran supplemental analyses controlling for the number of target individuals that participants identified as a “close friend” (Table S3).

Given that social status is calculated as a standardized rank‐order variable, interpreting meaningful within‐person effects for this measure is particularly complex. For example, longitudinal fluctuations in the measure could result from either meaningful changes in status or those associated with measurement, including differences in total nominations, number of students, or school group membership from year to year. Thus, our primary analyses do not disaggregate between and within‐person effects. However, we ran supplemental analyses disaggregating these effects (Curran & Bauer, 2011) and found results in line with our primary findings (Tables S4.1 and S4.2; Figure S1A,B). Lastly, sensitivity analyses excluding the 11 participants who missed >1 response on the n‐back attention check (2 misses: n = 10; 3 misses: n = 1; Table S5) did not significantly change our results, so these participants were included in the final analyses.

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics and trajectories of depressive symptoms

Means and standard deviations for depressive symptoms at each timepoint are shown in Table 1. There were no significant racial/ethnic differences for popularity or depressive symptoms at any timepoint. To examine longitudinal changes in depressive symptoms from 6th to 10th grade we conducted an unconditional multilevel growth model. Likelihood ratio tests indicated that the inclusion of random slopes significantly improved model fit (χ 2 = 8.900, df = 2, p = .011), whereas allowing for heteroscedastic errors did not (χ 2 = 5.8179, df = 4, p = .213). Thus, the following models included random intercepts and random slopes for grade, while maintaining the assumption of homoscedastic errors. There was no significant main effect of grade in the unconditional model (Table 2), but there was a significant sex by grade interaction (γGradexSex = 0.82, p = .016, Table 3) when sex was included as a time‐invariant predictor. Simple slopes analyses revealed that females' depressive symptoms significantly increased from 6th to 10th grade (B = 0.52, p = .027), whereas males' depressive symptoms did not significantly change over time (B = −0.30, p = .221, Figure 2). There was no significant main effect of participants' own peer‐nominated popularity (B = −0.14, p = .578), nor a significant sex by popularity interaction when including popularity as a time‐varying covariate (B = 0.50, p = .144, Table 4).

Interactions between neural sensitivity to popular and unpopular peers and participants' own popularity

To assess our primary research question, we included estimates of neural sensitivity to popular and unpopular peers from the VS and amygdala as a time invariant predictor in eight separate models (VSHighPop, VSLowPop, AmygdalaHighPop, AmygdalaLowPop, InsulaHighPop, InsulaLowPop, dACCHighPop, dACCLowPop). This resulted in a cross‐level interaction between neural sensitivity and participants' own popularity on trajectories of depressive symptoms. After correcting for multiple comparisons, there was a significant interaction between neural sensitivity to the least popular peers in both the amygdala the insula and participants' own popularity (Table 5: γPopularxAmygdalaLowPop = − .8547, p = .0034; Table 6: γPopularxInsulaLowPop = −1.0217, p = .0195). We probed this interaction by conducting simple slopes analyses (Figure 3a) at amygdala values at −1SD (−.630, B = 0.33, p = .25), mean (−.111, B = −0.11, p = .64), and +1SD (.407, B = −0.55, p = .045) as well as insula values at −1SD (−.512, B = 0.084, p = .75), mean (−.140, B = −0.30, p = .23), and +1SD (.232, B = −0.67, p = .037). Additionally, we employed the Johnson‐Neyman technique (Figure 3b) to evaluate levels of amygdala and insula sensitivity wherein popularity significantly predicted depressive symptoms. The Johnson‐Neyman significance intervals (AmygdalaLowPop: [−1.14, 0.38], InsulaLowPop: [−1.80, 0.14]) indicated that individuals with high amygdala or insula sensitivity to their least popular peers exhibited a significant negative effect of popularity on depressive symptoms, such that lower levels of popularity were associated with increased depressive symptoms. Slopes and intervals were calculated with males as the reference group in models, and post‐hoc analyses evaluating a three‐way interaction between sex, sensitivity to the least popular peers, and popularity did not indicate a significant sex difference in the interactions between amygdala sensitivity nor insula sensitivity to the least popular peers and popularity (γPopularxAmygdalaLowPopxSex = −0.41, p = .469, γPopularxInsulaLowPopxSex = −1.27, p = .150). Significant results held when controlling for the number of targets in the Low Popularity condition that participants considered to be a “close friend” (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

Social status is a salient feature of adolescents' social environments that can play a significant role in the developmental trajectory of outcomes such as depressive symptoms. However, the effects of status on internalizing symptoms are not uniform, such that individual differences in the salience of peer hierarchies can exacerbate the negative effects of low status. This study assessed whether neural sensitivity to high‐ and low‐status (i.e., popular and unpopular) peers from adolescents' school networks moderated the relations between participants' own status and trajectories of depressive symptoms. We found that lower status was associated with heightened depressive symptoms throughout adolescence, but primarily only for adolescents with high neural tracking of unpopularity in the amygdala and insula. These results suggest that neural sensitivity in the amygdala and insula may influence the degree to which their own social status negatively impacts their development, perhaps because these regions signal the social salience of low status in their social environments.

Amygdala and insula sensitivity to unpopular peers moderate the relations between popularity and depressive symptoms

While adolescence is viewed as a period wherein high status is particularly valued (LaFontana & Cillessen, 2010), it is also a time in which individuals are hypersensitive to social evaluation (Somerville, 2013) and peer rejection (Blakemore & Mills, 2014; Platt et al., 2013). Such sensitivities are not uniform across adolescents, however, and individual differences in neural sensitivity to these stimuli have been theorized to underlie differences in how adolescents navigate and are impacted by their social environments (Do et al., 2020; Schriber & Guyer, 2016). We found that heightened neural sensitivity to unpopular peers in the amygdala and insula was associated with increased risks for depressive symptoms among low status adolescents.

Heightened amygdala and insula sensitivity to unpopular peers among participants in our study might indicate a tendency for these adolescents to be more cognizant of which peers are of low status so that they can avoid the negative effects of unpopularity themselves. Given that the amygdala and insula are involved in detecting salient cues in the environment (Adolphs, 2010; Uddin, 2015), greater response to low‐status peers in these regions may signal that low status is particularly salient and may therefore be a threatening or undesirable outcome. Indeed, previous studies have implicated amygdala function in adolescents' goals of avoiding peer rejection. For instance, adolescents with greater avoidance‐related status goals (i.e., endorsed efforts or behaviors that minimize peer rejection) exhibit heightened amygdala‐MTG functional connectivity in response to negative feedback (Davis et al., 2023). Similarly, adolescents with a higher insula response to peer rejection self‐report greater distress during these experiences (Masten et al., 2009). Given that amygdala and insula sensitivity to unpopular peers may signal a heightened focus on the negative aspects of low status in the peer environment, these neural responses may be particularly detrimental for adolescents who are unpopular themselves. For example, both amygdala (Silk et al., 2014) and insula (Jankowski et al., 2018) responses to rejection‐related cues are higher among youth with depression, highlighting the potential role of these regions' response to rejection‐related information as a neural biomarker for depressive symptom risk. These risks are considered to be particularly impactful among low‐status youth, who face more consistent rejection and victimization in their peer environments (Platt et al., 2013). Heightened amygdala and insula sensitivity to unpopular peers in our study may follow a similar mechanism, thus heightening the risks of depressive symptoms for low‐status youth.

Addressing null results

Notably, we did not identify a significant interaction between participants' peer nominated popularity and their sensitivity to their most popular peers in any of our regions of interest. Previous work examining the role of self‐reported peer importance emphasized the potential role of valuing status gains in moderating the relations between low status and depressive symptoms (Prinstein & Aikins, 2004). This focus on high status being particularly salient or valued may have aligned with neural sensitivity to high‐status peers in our task. However, our results suggest that neural sensitivity to the most unpopular peers may be more relevant for depression than neural sensitivity to peers who have the highest status. This finding, which might reflect a heightened focus on the threatening nature of low status, aligns with the notion that individuals with depression often exhibit cognitive and attentional biases for negative information, as found in behavioral (Platt et al., 2017) and neuroimaging (Jiang, 2024) studies. Moreover, while our fMRI task and other tasks that have measured neural sensitivity to social status do not explicitly measure reward responses, heightened neural sensitivity of high‐status peers has been theorized to reflect the rewarding or motivational nature of high status (Morelli et al., 2018; Zerubavel et al., 2015). However, adolescents with depression often exhibit blunted neural responses to reward (Silk et al., 2012), indicating that higher sensitivity to high‐status peers may be the more normative neural response. On the other hand, it is important to exercise caution when interpreting null effects, and, given that social goals related to gaining status and avoiding rejection are not mutually exclusive (Lee, 2018; Pelletier‐Baldelli et al., 2023), there may still be a role for heightened prioritization of gaining status in risks for depressive symptoms.

Additionally, we did not identify any significant moderating effects for VS or dACC sensitivity to participants' least popular peers. While the VS, amygdala, insula, and dACC have all been implicated in social salience detection (Seeley, 2019), the VS is hypothesized to play a greater role in detecting stimuli one might want to approach or engage with (such as popular peers), whereas the amygdala and insula are hypothesized to detect stimuli one might want to avoid (such as unpopular peers) (Ernst, 2014). Although analyses for the dACC were null, future work could explore the cingulate cortex in greater regional specificity, given that regions including the anterior, middle, and posterior cingulate cortex have all been implicated in social status detection (Capella et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2023; Parkinson et al., 2017).

Lastly, our models account for differential trajectories of depressive symptoms by sex, such that females exhibited longitudinal increases in depressive symptoms whereas males did not. The interaction between amygdala sensitivity and popularity was significant when controlling for sex, and was not found to differ significantly between the sexes in a post‐hoc three‐way interaction analysis. However, there are theoretical reasons to believe that females would be particularly sensitive to the negative effects of unpopularity, given their heightened levels of socioevaluative concern (i.e., concern or preoccupation with peer judgments of oneself) in adolescence (Rudolph & Conley, 2005). While we did not identify a significant three‐way interaction between sex, popularity, and amygdala sensitivity, nor a significant interaction between sex and popularity alone, these null results may simply stem from a lack of power due to a smaller sample size (n = 116, 61 females), and should be explored in future research.

Contributions, limitations, and future directions

This study has several strengths. While prior work has evaluated how status‐related goals can moderate the relations between low status and depressive symptoms in adolescence (Prinstein & Aikins, 2004), this study provides a novel contribution by incorporating the neural mechanisms by which adolescents perceive the status of peers in their school networks. By leveraging robust sociometric data, our study employs an fMRI task with high ecological validity to assess how adolescents detect salient information from their social environments. Moreover, this sociometric data allows us to consider how adolescents are differentially situated in these contexts, enabling us to probe complex interactions between individuals' perceptions and the influences of their peer environments. Lastly, by utilizing a longitudinal dataset that spans 5 years of adolescence, our models estimate how these factors influence long‐term trajectories of depressive symptoms.

Nonetheless, this study has some limitations. Even though our study utilizes longitudinal measures of individuals' status and depressive symptoms to cover a wide span of early adolescence, we only incorporate neural data from one timepoint. While this approach falls in line with theories of adolescents' neural sensitivity to social contexts (Schriber & Guyer, 2016), which emphasize that between‐person differences in neural function can inform our understanding of differences in responses to environmental contexts, it does not account for the possibility that neural sensitivity to social status may change over time. Indeed, this notion is supported by behavioral evidence, which indicates that the valuation of status peaks in early adolescence (LaFontana & Cillessen, 2010), and neuroimaging evidence, which demonstrates developmental changes in the processing of faces (Scherf et al., 2013) and other social stimuli (Blakemore & Mills, 2014). Moreover, since our neural data was collected at the second wave of our study, we cannot ascertain whether heightened sensitivity to low‐status peers is an antecedent or consequence of heightened depressive symptoms among some unpopular youth. Thus, future research should examine whether neural sensitivity to social status changes across adolescence, and whether these changes are related to trajectories of depressive symptoms for unpopular youth.

Additionally, given that our task consists of the passive viewing of faces, we cannot fully ascertain the cognitive processes reflected by heightened amygdala and insula sensitivity to low‐status peers. While our results dovetail with previous work identifying these regions' roles in encoding salient social information related to overall social goals (Cunningham & Brosch, 2012; Davis et al., 2023) and experiences of peer rejection (Achterberg et al., 2017; Blakemore & Mills, 2014; Silk et al., 2014), future work is needed to more explicitly link neural tracking of unpopularity with cognition related to rejection or one's own social status. Incorporating measures of adolescents' social goals (Ryan & Shim, 2006) and their personal experiences of rejection or victimization could provide valuable mechanistic information that further contextualizes our findings. For example, while unpopularity, peer rejection, and victimization are often correlated with one another (Sandstrom & Cillessen, 2006), instances of rejection and victimization are particularly salient and can significantly increase the risk of depressive symptoms (Platt et al., 2013). Similarly, while we ran supplementary analyses to control for participants' friendships with peer stimuli in the Classmates task, there may be other relationships between the participants and the peer targets (e.g., target images being participants' bullies, former friends, etc.) that could influence neural responses in our task. Other confounds, such as differences in facial attractiveness, trustworthiness, or overall familiarity (Scherf et al., 2013) could also influence amygdala or insula responses to these images.

Lastly, we did not identify any differences in popularity or depressive symptoms between adolescents with different racial and ethnic identities. However, previous work suggests that these measures may be influenced by both individuals' racial and ethnic identities (Patil et al., 2018; Prinstein, 2017) and by differences in the overall class composition (Stevens et al., 2020). Moreover, while we chose target images for the Classmates task that reflect the diversity of the overall sample, adolescents' individual identities and class compositions may also impact their response to in‐group and out‐group faces (Carollo et al., 2023). Thus, future work could explore how neural sensitivity to popular and unpopular peers may differentially impact adolescents from varying demographic backgrounds, and whether neural sensitivity to status varies depending on their racial/ethnic identities and those of their peers.

Overall, this research suggests that the neural mechanisms by which adolescents track social status in their peer contexts can influence the degree to which they are negatively impacted by their own ranking within peer hierarchies. In early adolescence, low status and other forms of peer rejection can contribute to the risk of depression and other negative outcomes (Platt et al., 2013). Given the alarming increases in depressive symptom onset across early and middle adolescence (Costello et al., 2005; Hankin et al., 1998), this study advances a critical line of research that identifies neural processes and social factors implicated in trajectories of depressive symptoms.
