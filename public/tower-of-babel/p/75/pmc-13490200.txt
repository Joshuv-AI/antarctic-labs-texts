Improving early recognition and treatment of sepsis in paramedic services and emergency departments: a pan-Canadian mixed methods application of the Theoretical Domains Framework
Authors: A J E Carter, B L Bigham, J Goldstein, J Greene, M Harrison, A Steenbeek, J Curran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEfforts to implement evidence-based guidelines for sepsis care (Surviving Sepsis, CAEP guideline) have met with limited success and mortality from sepsis remains high. We sought to understand barriers and facilitators from the frontline perspective of emergency nurses, physicians, and paramedics.MethodsIn an explanatory sequential mixed methods approach, a 55-question survey was developed using the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) and scoping review (reported separately) and distributed via Canadian emergency, nurse, physician, and paramedic organizations in English and French. Purposeful recruiting of interviewees followed, using an interview guide informed by the scoping review and survey. Recordings were transcribed, and audit trails were maintained. A coding tree was developed and findings from the thematic analysis were mapped to TDF constructs, triangulating findings from all data sources.ResultsData consisted of 545 surveys from paramedics (37%), nurses (20%), and physicians (27%), spanning all provinces and the territories in Canada, and 15 interviews (4 nurses, 6 physicians, 5 paramedics).Six themes emerged: access block, recognition by the public, resources (human, physical), leveraging scopes of practice (paramedics, nurses), recognition by healthcare, and the importance of communication. Many of these map to the TDF domain of Environment, Context, and Resources. There was support for guidelines (88% physician/93% paramedic/89% nurse), but interprofessional differences include awareness/understanding the guidelines (92% physician/72% paramedic/84% nurse) and trust in the evidence behind the guidelines (70% physician/85% paramedic/94% nurse). Reinforcement strategies at the point of care were more highly supported (74%/76%/81%) than education (physician 59%, paramedic 85%, nurse 78%).ConclusionThe themes of this work confirm many of the issues known from previous publications, but also added key insights such as access block and recognition by the public. It also reinforces that raising awareness/more education is not a strong facilitator for physicians or nurses.The frontline perspective adds key insights that can improve sepsis care by overcoming key barriers and leveraging facilitators that have been highlighted as important in the complex healthcare implementation environment that is the ED.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s43678-026-01119-0.Keywords: Emergency medical services, Emergency department, Prehospital, Sepsis management

Introduction

Most sepsis patients present to healthcare through emergency departments (EDs), many arriving via paramedics. Recognizing and managing sepsis is challenging; delay to treatment increases mortality. [1] Multiple efforts including the Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) and the Canadian Association of Emergency Physicians (CAEP) campaign have not reduced the 20–50% mortality [2–5].

Decades of research have examined strategies such as raising awareness, best practice advisories, and warning scores, yet gaps in care remain. [6] Further, solutions applied in large-city academic EDs, where most knowledge translation and quality improvement studies occur, may not apply to community hospitals and remote nursing stations. Research on a single solution does not account for the interprofessional nature of sepsis care, the interdependency of the health system, or the public’s interaction with it [7]. Sepsis treatment cannot improve without a better understanding of barriers and facilitators; culture will not change unless we ask questions about culture. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) provides a structured approach to uncover reasons why effective interventions are not being adopted, through an exploration of constructs of knowledge, beliefs, skills, role, processes, and context. Some examples of past TDF applications include barriers to uptake of transfusion, and pre-operative testing guidelines [8–10] The objective of this study was to examine barriers and facilitators affecting the adherence to best practice (SSC/CAEP guidelines) from the frontline perspective of paramedics, nurses, and physicians in Canadian emergency healthcare settings. Findings are a key addition to the published literature in that “traditional methods of review … provide little or no clue as to why the intervention worked or did not work” [11]; frontline perspectives and an understanding of culture are key to successful implementation [12].

Methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Nova Scotia Health Research Ethics Board (File # 1,028,156). Investigators used an explanatory sequential mixed methods design to investigate the factors influencing healthcare professionals’ adherence to best practice. The survey questions were built on a scoping review (under review), and the interview questions built on both survey and review, structured around the TDF [13, 14]. The mixed methods approach is detailed in Fig. 1.

Mixed methods approach

This mixed methods study is reported in accordance with Lee et al.’s checklist for reporting mixed methods research [15].

Participants and data collection

Population:

Nurses and physicians working in Canadian emergency departments, and paramedics working in Canadian ground ambulance services.

Survey

Survey design:

A 55-question survey was developed by the study team, (online supplement) with questions derived from the TDF and Kaur, Smyth and Williamson (2012) model. [16] Questions were presented under each domain of the TDF and were scored on a 7-point Likert scale from Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree. Additional questions built on a scoping review (under review), with potential items presented on a 7-point Likert scale from Strong Barrier to Strong Facilitator. Open-ended questions provided the opportunity to add barriers or facilitators not listed, name the single top barrier, comment on personal experience applying strategies to any of the barriers, and anything else the respondent thought would be helpful. For validation, five raters from the target population scored survey questions for relevancy and clarity on a 4-point scale, and questions were revised until they achieved a score of 4 for both. The survey was delivered using the Opinio platform in English and French.

Recruitment:

The support of the several large national associations representing the population of interest (National Emergency Nurses Association, CAEP, Paramedic Chiefs of Canada, Association des médecins d’urgence du Québec (AMUQ), Society of Rural Physicians of Canada (SRPC) and McNally paramedic research group) was enlisted to recruit participants. Each of these organizations distributed email invitations in September 2022, with a reminder email after 2 weeks. A link to the survey was also shared via social media. The first page of the survey provided a scripted informed consent, indicating also that return of the survey indicated consent.

Interviews

Interview design:

Building on the scoping review and survey findings, the study team developed a semi-structured guide grounded in the TDF. The guide consisted of 13 questions with optional prompts, which allowed more detailed and in-depth responses to beliefs, knowledge of and attitudes toward the sepsis guidelines, as well as the top barriers and facilitators, and asked about strategies to resolve these issues.

Recruitment:

At the end of the survey and via social media, those interested emailed the research team and booked a qualitative, semi-structured telephone interview. A scripted consent was read to the individual before beginning the interview and recording.

Data collection:

Interviews were conducted by a masters-trained research coordinator with experience in qualitative interviewing, between March 2023 and January 2024. Recordings were transcribed verbatim, and thematic analysis was managed in QRS NVivo11. One team member (MH) conducted the first ten interviews; a change in study personnel required a second interviewer to add five interviews to enrich physician representation (AS).

Data analysis

Analysis of the study data followed a mixed methods approach, through which the different phases were built on previous phases, and the final analysis triangulated and integrated data by merging findings from all data sources. Results were presented under the themes and mapped back to the TDF, with supporting data points from all sources.

Quantitative results were evaluated using descriptive statistics. For the qualitative data, an initial coding tree was developed from transcribed interviews by MH and reviewed by the principal investigator (AC). Broad themes were identified inductively from the interviews and surveys, which were then mapped to the 14 constructs of the TDF, and reviewed by the study team. We practiced reflexivity on several levels, including personal, methodologic, and contextual. The study team comprised two emergency physicians, three paramedics, one research assistant, and two nurses (five who identify as women, one man, one man, one person of color). An audit trail was maintained (e.g., field notes). Peer debriefing was used. One participant accepted the offer to review their transcript/coding (member checking).

Triangulation of results

The survey and interview results showed convergence and saturation, with six themes emerging: access block and wait times, recognition by the public and professionals, leveraging scopes of practice across roles, communication, training, and resource constraints. Table S1 (online) shows the survey results for the 14 TDF domains. Most respondents agreed that guidelines improve outcomes, felt they had the skills to provide care aligned with the SSC/CAEP guidelines, and that it was part of their professional role. Respondents were open to learning more about sepsis. Social influence had minimal impact on their practice. Confidence in the evidence supporting SSC guidelines was highest among nurses (94%) and lowest among physicians (70%) who cite concerns about overtriage and overtreatment. Confidence in managing septic patients was nearly universal among physicians (99%) and nurses (100%), but lower for paramedics (78%).

Access block (patients are unable to gain access to hospital beds due to a lack of capacity) emerged as the top barrier for all three professions, with 79%/77%/76% (medicine/paramedic/nursing) citing this as a moderate or extreme barrier. Bed block relates to questions from the TDF domains Environment, Context, and Resources and Social and Professional Role and is a key factor in the timely initiation of treatment, as many EDs require a physician order to start antibiotics/fluid resuscitation, which may require the patient to be placed in an ED bed.

“ the biggest delay usually isn’t making the decision to give antibiotics but in actually having the space to see the patient.” (interview 3, physician)

“If there’s septic shock, they would get a CTAS 2, they end up being with other CTAS 2’s and wait.” (interview 4, Nurse)

Recognizing sepsis and its severity is challenging. Respondents placed “patient/public recognition of the seriousness of sepsis” at the top of their ranking of moderate-to-extreme barrier (physicians 65%, paramedics 77%, nurses 75%), with “patient/public recognition of the symptoms of sepsis” closely following (physicians 65%, paramedics 73%, nurses, 69%). It was noted that solutions were challenging, since sepsis is complex and its signs overlap with many conditions. This theme did not map easily to the TDF, but highlights the importance of the complex interaction between the public and the healthcare system.

“I saw a patient last night who had clearly had chills and fever and altered mental status and was probably septic… Her partner (called) 911, and was completely surprised when I thought she had infection… there’s a misunderstanding of what sepsis is and how dangerous it is” (interview 6, physician).

Resource constraints such as physical and human resource issues, speed of laboratory results, medical record systems, bed availability and patient flow, human resources, and several important issues related to rural practice were highlighted. This relates to the TDF domain of Environment, Context, and Resources.

Challenges related to lack of adequate healthcare providers/staffing was the top identified barrier when asked for additional barriers in the survey (n = 27), particularly nursing shortages and workload, and high turnover leading to less experienced nurses.

“With the current staffing problems across Canada, one of the barriers is also junior/inexperienced staff and lack of available mentorship due to short staffing.” (survey, Nurse)

Lack of resources in rural and remote communities was specifically highlighted, for example imaging services, transport, specialty services (e.g., ICU), physician availability, etc.

“Some communities have a significant barrier to access appropriate level of care for most acute presentations including sepsis. More than 30 fly in communities with limited access to healthcare professionals, diagnostics etc. often leads to sepsis being missed or mistreated” (Paramedic, survey).

When the ICU is in (big city) and they’re like “Ohh, do you have a central line?” And I’m like, “OK, so I can do a central line, but none of the nurses have done one, ever”…-and also, I’m running the entire department on my own. So, like no… The challenge is when the ICU has expectations of you, that are not at all connected to your reality. (physician 5, interview).

There were some differences in the responses to the TDF domain-based questions between rural and urban participants (Online Supplement Fig. 1). Awareness and understanding of the guidelines were lower in rural settings, as was confidence, and agreement is within scope of practice. Reinforcement/cues at the point of care was favored more highly in the urban setting. There were also some differences in the perception of barriers and opportunities (Online Supplement Table 2). Availability of definitive treatment (43% vs 26%), and of an emergency physician (50% vs 45%), was more a barrier in the rural setting. Offload delay was much more of an issue in the urban setting. (86% vs 60%).

Leveraging scopes of practice relates to TDF domains of Skills, Social, and Professional Role and Behavioural Regulation. Professions differ in their response to: “Implementing the SSC guidelines is within my scope of practice” (physician 99%, paramedic 63%, nurse 88%). Nearly half of all survey respondents felt that timely treatment initiation in the prehospital setting was a moderate-to-extreme barrier (48%/ paramedics 38%/nurses 54%). Additional education was embraced particularly by paramedics, with 84% agreeing/strongly agreeing with the TDF statement “More training would prompt me to implement the guidelines”. Paramedics report the lowest level of satisfaction with how consistently they offer excellent care to septic patients (40% agree/strongly agree). Early antibiotics, bloodwork, fluids, and alerts to hospitals were noted as within scope of paramedics, but not often employed. Similar opportunities exist at the time of triage for nurses to initiate laboratories, fluids, and antibiotics, or alerting physicians.

“It wouldn’t traditionally be in the paramedic field … you’d say well how long are you going to be with them, do you really think we’re going to improve outcomes starting a half hour earlier. But we’re not starting a half hour early now, we’re starting potentially 6 h earlier.” (interview 14, Paramedic).

Recognition of sepsis by healthcare providers was a moderate or extreme barrier for 48% of physicians/51% of paramedics /40% of nurses, noting a lack of point-of-care testing, accurate sepsis screening tools, and laboratory result delays. Interviewees felt improving sepsis triage was a high priority, yet reported system issues such as bed availability, resulting in delayed care despite recognition, especially in the urban setting. Respondents suggested that sepsis should have its own protocol to bypass queues and allow timely care, similar to protocols for ST-elevated myocardial infarction, trauma and stroke. Some highlighted the elderly and cancer patients as being less recognizable. Reassessing patients already triaged was also mentioned.

“Not everyone fits the mold … tachycardia, hypotension, …. They don’t always show up at first… so I think case finding is one of the more difficult parts.” (interview 2, physician)

Interestingly, education and increasing awareness were not supported by physicians or nurses as promising strategies. Instead, all three professions endorsed visual reinforcement and cues which stem from the TDF domain of Reinforcement (less so in the rural setting as noted above). Some specific strategies were proposed, with strong agreement across groups: “Reinforcement strategies at the point of care (visual cues, alerts, etc.) would prompt me to implement the guidelines” (physicians 74%, paramedics 76%, nurses 81%). Training was an important interprofessional difference, as noted in response to this TDF question: “More training would prompt me to implement the guidelines” (physicians 59%, paramedics 85%, nurses 78%).

Communication: interprofessional conflict relates to the TDF domain of Social and Professional Role. Tensions between professions were commented on frequently. Issues between prehospital and hospital providers included a lack of trust or undervaluing paramedic clinical judgment. While many highlighted pre-alerts to notify hospitals of suspected sepsis, some paramedics interviewed felt when they suspect sepsis, their concerns are not always taken seriously by hospital staff, requiring advocacy for their patient to be seen quickly.

“It’s frustrating to recognize sepsis and communicate with the ED staff as the triage nurses generally think that we don’t know what we’re talking about and tend to ignore what we say.” (survey, paramedic).

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

This study adds a unique element to the published literature on barriers to best practice in sepsis care, by the addition of frontline perspective gathered via a pan-Canadian survey and key informant interviews with physicians, nurses, and paramedics to reveal six themes, mapped to the TDF. This frontline perspective has surfaced barriers which have been un-/underrecognized in published literature and may provide a key element to successful implementation. Access block is a significant concern, especially in the urban setting. Recognition of sepsis is challenging for both the lay public and healthcare providers. Leveraging scopes of practice for paramedics and nurses was proposed, while underscoring the need for communication and interprofessional respect. Training and visual cues, with very specific feedback about what would and would not be appealing, were highlighted as well as the “clunkiness” of the guidelines. Resources, particularly having a lack of experienced nurses, and the unique circumstances of rural practice, were noted as challenges. Respondents presented several solutions to these issues. A study like this using a framework to explore beliefs, resources, environment, and attitudes is essential to understand why decades of research have yet to move treatment forward. This study draws on the strengths of a realist approach and participatory research, and acknowledges the critical role of context, including cultural norms, rapport between practitioners, and system factors [17].

Comparison to previous studies

Several key issues not emphasized in the literature review were highlighted as extremely important by qualitative responses; the most important of these was the access block. Participants noted alerts, triage tiers, and tools for early recognition would not make any difference if the patient must wait to be seen [18]. At the provider level, this raises a new angle to the discussion of paramedics and/or triage nurses having sensitive and specific tools to identify sepsis, and the ability to act on those findings with the initiation of appropriate evidence-based care. [19] The interprofessional conflict faced by paramedics who find they do not have the same language or tools and that their findings may not be acted on is an enormous barrier to moving opportunities forward for earlier identification and treatment. It also raises system-level change. For example, systems-of-care approaches have incorporated designated teams or spaces for disease-specific emergencies (e.g.. ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, trauma, stroke) to address wait time/access. Another area of divergence between literature and survey/interview findings is the value of education/training; it is frequently studied in the literature and yet the physician support for this is quite low (59% endorse this).

Several strategies were endorsed by survey/interview participants and are also supported by the literature. Paramedic early recognition can be communicated with ED staff at triage or as a formalized pre-alert. [20, 21] When prehospital alerts are implemented, EDs are more successful at achieving the guideline recommendation of 1 h to antimicrobial treatment [22]. A meta-analysis found mortality benefit with administration of prehospital antibiotics in patients with sepsis [23], but a randomized controlled trial concluded that although EMS education improved early recognition and care, administration of prehospital antibiotics had no clear mortality benefit when compared to ED antibiotics, though factors such as short time to ED care and low illness severity may have played a role [24]. Other examples of leveraging scopes of practice include early administration of antibiotics/sepsis care by nurses at the point of triage. All-hours access to the pharmacy, having a pharmacist on the team, and having antimicrobials ergonomically positioned in the ED have been shown to facilitate care, decreasing time to treatment, lengths of admission, readmission rates, and increases in survival [25]. Technology can help automate and enhance alerts with the ability to predict the development of sepsis; a machine-learning algorithm to identify sepsis led to a 60% decline in mortality rates in one center [26]. However, the impact of such systems largely depends on adoption and appropriate utilization by clinicians. Alert fatigue is also a real concern; overly sensitive alerts are tuned out [27].

Strengths and limitations

Limitations

Response bias is an inherent limitation of survey and interview research. Our convenience sample is without a denominator, given the overlap of distribution lists and social media. While covering a range of experience and geography, our sample is likely not proportionate to population size. The study took place in the context of the Canadian healthcare system—the results and recommendations may differ elsewhere. The interviewer was a member of the study team, which could lead to interviewer, response, or confirmation bias.

Strengths

Our broad, multimodal sampling approach worked to overcome selection or sampling bias. The findings represent a large number of Canadian practitioners, achieved saturation and convergence, and triangulated with our scoping review. The use of multiple coders and review by the team, and the practice of reflexivity, as well as diversity among team and respondents, works to balance any biases introduced by the relationship of the interviewer to the study.

Health systems implications

An easy strategy to overcome lagging implementation is to look for strategies that have been published in the literature and assign merit based on volume of publication, but this approach could miss critical barriers in these complex systems. Overcoming the barriers described here, particularly the system-level issues such as access block and lack of retention of experienced nurses, will require system-level effort and commitment, and a shift in culture. An interprofessional sepsis system of care addressing key themes as outlined in this work would have significant potential to overcome barriers that have led to poor uptake of evidence-based sepsis guidelines to date. Our findings provide insight into specific solutions—physicians are the least interested in training, but any training should be brief, focused, and asynchronous; FOAM and easily accessible infographics were suggested. Nurses, and particularly paramedics, were interested in further education. Physicians, paramedics, and nurses put more emphasis on reminders/reinforcement at the point of care—not just alerts that the person might have sepsis, but reminders to assist with following the steps of a guideline that is seen as “clunky”. System-level solutions may be far more fruitful than targeting individual clinicians.

Research implications

Rural settings remain underrepresented and deliberate efforts must be made to include these centers. Realist approaches such as this study, where “boots on the ground” perspectives are sought, are critical to successful implementation. Any attempts at trialing these complex interventions must be evaluated, and that knowledge must be disseminated.

Conclusion

Our triangulation provides deep insight into the barriers to implementation of sepsis guidelines across Canada, as well as promising solutions. Overcoming these will require a systems-of-care approach to improve sepsis care that deploys public awareness campaigns, leverages technology to help with recognition and resource limitations, works to overcome (and excel within the limits of) access block, enables practitioners to perform at top of scope, and establishes care pathways and expert teams to care for patients with sepsis.
