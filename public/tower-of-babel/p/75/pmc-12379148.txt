The impact of green infrastructure on ecosystem quality based on explainable machine learning: a case study of Shanxi Province, China
Authors: Yuxiang Zhang, Wei Kong, Guoqing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Green infrastructure (GI) is a critical strategy for maintaining ecological security and sustainable development amidst rapid urbanization. This study examines changes in GI within the ecologically vulnerable Loess Plateau region, particularly in response to complex human activities, including ecological restoration projects. Utilizing Explainable Machine Learning methods, we explore the impact of GI’s coverage-feature-form on ecosystem quality. The findings indicate that over the past two decades (2000–2022), there has been a general improvement in ecosystem quality within the study area due to large-scale ecological restoration efforts. While core GI areas expanded, the reduction of other morphological types led to a more fragmented landscape with significant spatial heterogeneity. Crucially, our XGBoost models demonstrate that morphologically minor components, such as bridge and islet types, exert a disproportionately strong influence on ecosystem quality. These results highlight that ecosystem health is determined not only by the amount of green space but also by its specific features and spatial arrangement. We therefore advocate for integrating the coverage-feature-form framework into future urban planning and ecological restoration to optimize GI performance and enhance ecosystem resilience.

Introduction

The increasing frequency of phenomena such as global warming and extreme weather has drawn significant attention to the ecological crises we face today. Concurrently, the accelerated pace of urbanization is rapidly transforming surface landscapes1,2. A notable consequence of this transformation is the extensive conversion of natural or semi-natural habitats into impermeable surfaces3. Such landscape changes not only lead to habitat loss and a decline in biodiversity4,5 but also alter environmental factors like radiation and moisture levels6–8. These alterations exacerbate issues such as urban heat island effects, air pollution, and public safety hazards9–11. Consequently, these challenges significantly impair the efficiency of urban landscape functions and the overall health of ecosystems, posing a substantial threat to the advancement of human society12–14.

Green infrastructure (GI) is increasingly recognized as a vital approach for maintaining ecosystem health and stability during rapid urbanization15–17. It encompasses all natural, semi-natural, and artificially constructed green spaces, as well as water bodies and wetlands, which can create essential connections within urban areas18. As an indispensable environmental management tool in contemporary cities, GI provides valuable habitats and vital ecosystem services17,19–21. Particularly in regions under significant population pressure, the rise in population density not only leads to greater occupation of urban space but also corresponds to an increased demand for ecosystem services. Therefore, in the context of rapid urbanization, scientific planning and management of green infrastructure becomes a critical issue.

Numerous studies have provided evidence demonstrating that various types of GI, including forests, grasslands, and water bodies, can promote ecological restoration and enhance living environments11,22,23. For instance, it is well-established that increasing GI can mitigate the urban heat island effect24. Specifically, expanding green spaces and water areas in urban settings can lower surface temperatures, while the expansion of built environments tends to have the opposite effect. Additionally, many studies utilizing landscape pattern metrics (LPM) have found that the structure and spatial distribution of landscapes are closely linked to improvements in biodiversity and overall ecosystem performance25,26. Research focusing on urban morphology has further confirmed that the specific forms of GI within a region can significantly impact the environment11,27. For example, studies conducted in Nanjing, China, have shown that elements like loops and bridges can alleviate urban heat by enhancing connectivity28. However, there remains a significant research gap in comprehensively integrating GI coverage, landscape characteristics, and morphological types and their complex effects on ecological environments. This study aims to address this gap by investigating the insufficient integration of GI coverage, feature, and form as a central rationale. By aligning our research objectives with robust methodologies, we aim to provide practical insights for urban ecological management.

China’s urbanization process represents a significant phenomenon in human history, characterized by its unprecedented scale and speed. Over the past 30 years, rapid urbanization has not only transformed the economy but also consumed substantial urban space and ecological resources, leading to severe environmental pollution and ecological crises29,30. In response, Chinese cities are increasingly prioritizing the planning and construction of GI. A prominent example is the Grain-to-Green Program (GTGP), a large-scale ecological restoration initiative that has been implemented for over 20 years31,32. According to statistics from the National Forestry and Grassland Administration, from 1999 to 2019, this program resulted in the addition of 515 million acres of forest and grassland across 25 provinces, accounting for more than 4% of the global increase in green space during the same period33. Such initiatives have significantly altered landscape composition and the morphology of GI. While studies have shown that these measures can mitigate soil and water loss by increasing greenery34, they may also exacerbate drought conditions35. Therefore, further comprehensive research is needed to explore whether changes in GI lead to improvements in ecological environments.

This study employs a dual approach, integrating LPM with Morphological Spatial Pattern Analysis (MSPA), to comprehensively identify and quantify the coverage, features, and morphological types of GI. Focusing on regions where the GTGP has been implemented, this research spans a period of 22 years. The objectives of this study are fourfold: (1) to assess the changes and improvements in ecosystem quality during the implementation of the GTGP from 2000 to 2022; (2) to identify the changes of coverage and features in GI throughout this period; (3) to recognize and quantify the changes in the morphological types of GI; and (4) to reveal the impacts of GI’s coverage–feature–morphology types factors on ecosystem quality. The findings of this research will provide insights into the effectiveness of GI construction in improving ecological environments and inform the scientific planning of GI to achieve more favorable outcomes for ecosystem health. This will provide a scientific reference for the layout and management of GI in future urban planning or environmental management.

Materials and methods

To achieve our objectives, we develop a four‐stage integrative framework that systematically links GI form and landscape pattern metrics with regional ecosystem quality and its underlying response mechanisms. First, we quantify ecosystem quality dynamics by applying the RSEI. Next, we characterize GI elements through MSPA and calculate key landscape pattern metrics to capture the structural and configurational attributes of green spaces. We then employ interpretable machine learning techniques to elucidate how individual GI features drive variations in ecosystem quality and to uncover the primary mechanisms of influence. Finally, leveraging the insights gained from these models, we formulate targeted recommendations for optimizing GI design and management, thereby enhancing the resilience and overall health of regional ecosystems. Specific details of each step are provided in the following sections.

Study area and data sources

Shanxi Province is situated in the central part of the Yellow River basin, extending from 34°31’ to 40°44’ north latitude and from 110°15’ to 114°32’ east longitude (Fig. 1). This province is located on the northeastern edge of the Loess Plateau, characterized by its distinctive loess soil and dramatic topography. The region features a diverse landscape, with elevations higher in the northeast and lower in the southwest, encompassing several prominent mountain systems and basins, such as the Lüliang and Taihang mountain systems, along with basins like the Fenhe river basins. The province of Shanxi is characterized by a semiarid weather pattern, receiving yearly precipitation of roughly 468 mm36. Shanxi Province covers an area of 156,700 km2, comprising 11 major cities and 117 county-level administrative units. By the end of 2023, approximately 34.66 million people reside here, with the population continuing to grow38. The region has a long history of human activity, with significant agricultural and industrial demands for natural resources. Holding nearly 40% of China’s total coal reserves, Shanxi has historically been a key energy production base39. However, extensive land cultivation and ongoing resource extraction have led to serious ecological degradation, including soil erosion, water resource depletion, and significant air pollution issues.

In response to these environmental challenges, various ecological restoration initiatives have been implemented to improve the region’s environmental quality and promote sustainable development. These efforts have included large-scale vegetation restoration, the conversion of sloping croplands to forests, and integrated approaches to address mining impacts and industrial pollution. These projects have significantly altered the vegetation and landscape of Shanxi, consequently impacting the overall health of the ecosystem.

Given that landscape changes often induce spatiotemporal heterogeneity in GI, satellite-derived remote sensing data provide a robust foundation for analysis. This study integrates land use data and ecological remote sensing indicators—widely recognized in ecology, urban planning, and remote sensing research—to assess GI dynamics over time. Specifically, GI was identified and landscape metrics were computed using the China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD)40. This widely used dataset, generated via deep learning methods on Landsat imagery, provides annual 30-m resolution land cover data for China from 1990 to the present. For the computation of the ecosystem quality, we employed the MOD09A1 and MOD11A2 datasets (2000–2022) from the U.S. Geological Survey, accessed via the Google Earth Engine platform (https://code.earthengine.google.com/). To supplement environmental covariates, monthly precipitation data (1 km spatial resolution) were sourced from the Science Data Bank (https://www.scidb.cn/), while the terrain data was obtained from the SRTM 90 m Digital Elevation Database (https://bigdata.cgiar.org/). Slope data were generated through DEM surface analysis in ArcGIS 10.2. All raster datasets were preprocessed by clipping to the study area and reprojecting to UTM Zone 49N.

Evaluation of ecosystem quality

The Remote Sensing Ecological Index (RSEI) functions as a comprehensive environmental assessment tool that synthesizes four key variables to evaluate ecosystem conditions. Through the incorporation of principal component analysis, RSEI minimizes researcher bias in the assessment process. Multiple research investigations have validated RSEI’s effectiveness in delivering impartial environmental assessments31,41. Considering these advantages, our research employed RSEI methodology to evaluate ecosystem conditions across Shanxi Province during the 2000–2022 timeframe.

The RSEI offers a comprehensive assessment by integrating four key ecological components: greenness, humidity, heat, and dryness42. Each of these factors plays a critical role in understanding the overall condition of the ecological system. Greenness indicates the health and density of vegetation, serving as a vital indicator of ecosystem vitality. Humidity reflects moisture levels, which are essential for plant growth and overall ecosystem sustainability. Heat represents temperature variations that can influence biological activity and environmental stressors, while dryness assesses aridity levels that can impact water availability and vegetation health. Together, these components provide a nuanced view of ecological dynamics, helping to highlight areas of environmental improvement or degradation. By analyzing these indices, researchers can better understand how human activities and natural processes affect ecosystem health.

The greenness index can be quantified using Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) or Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) metrics. Given the heterogeneous plant distribution across the Loess Plateau landscape, we selected EVI for analysis. Based on previous research experience and the actual conditions of the study area, we consider regions with RSEI values below 0.3 to have poor quality, while areas with values above 0.7 are regarded as having good quality. Other regions fall into the moderate category. Finally, to better establish the relationship between GI morphological types and characteristics and ecosystem quality, the analysis results of RSEI and MSPA were assigned values within a 6 km × 6 km grid.

MSPA and mapping of GI

MSPA is an effective method utilized in landscape ecology and remote sensing for the quantitative examination of spatial patterns in digital imagery43. This approach employs mathematical morphology, a technique analyzing spatial patterns based on geometric structures, to assess landscape configurations. Using morphological operations, MSPA classifies and quantifies spatial features such as patches, corridors, and matrices. MSPA not only maps these elements but also evaluates their structural attributes—critical for assessing Green Infrastructure (GI), where spatial connectivity and distribution directly inform urban planning and ecosystem management.

Land cover features such as forest, shrubland, grassland, and aquatic systems were categorized as primary GI components (foreground), while artificial surfaces, cropland and other non-vegetated land types were designated as non-GI elements (background). The 30 m CLCD land cover data was employed to extract GI components, a resolution that facilitates the identification of fine-scale GI types and detailed green space structures. This dichotomy was applied to land use data spanning 2000–2022, generating binary raster datasets for spatial analysis. Subsequent processing and visualization were performed using Guidos Toolbox to assess GI patterns. Consequently, the green infrastructure categories were organized into seven distinct, non-overlapping types based on their landscape morphology (Table S1): core, islet, perforation, edge, loop, bridge, and branch44. After accurately identifying and classifying the morphological categories, we calculated the proportion of each GI type within each 6 km × 6 km grid and presented the research findings through visual mapping.

The relative contributions of GI’s types and features factors to RSEI

Existing research indicates that the coverage, morphology, and landscape characteristics of GI can significantly influence its ecological functions. However, the relationships among these factors are quite complex. Based on a review of various GI characteristics and considering the temporal and spatial variability of ecosystem quality, we developed separate models for different GI forms and characteristics for the years 2000 and 2022. These models aim to reveal their specific impacts on ecosystem quality.

In this study, we employed a Machine Learning algorithm, the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). It is an advanced gradient boosting framework for decision trees, to develop our explaining model. This approach constructs a strong learner by combining multiple weak models, utilizing Classification and Regression Trees as the base classifiers47. It is recognized as one of the fastest decision tree algorithms currently available48. The incorporation of regularization parameters effectively mitigates overfitting by penalizing tree complexity49. This capability, along with the model’s interpretability, makes it a more attractive choice compared to other classification techniques like neural networks. In addition to delivering robust predictive performance, this framework facilitates clearer evaluations of variable importance, enhancing our understanding of how different factors influence the model’s predictions. This method was selected to address the non-linear relationships between GI factors and RSEI values. The dataset was partitioned into a 70% training set and a 30% test set. To optimize performance and prevent overfitting, a grid search with fivefold cross-validation was employed for hyperparameter tuning to achieve the optimal model fit. The model’s predictive accuracy was evaluated using R-squared (R2) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).

Ultimately, we created three models that reflect the relative impacts of the forms and characteristic factors of GI in 2000, 2010 and 2022 on ecosystem quality. Both models incorporated the proportion of GI and seven different morphological types in the grid, along with eight distinct landscape characteristic factors such as Shannon diversity, as explanatory variables. The Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) tool was utilized to interpret the model outputs and determine the influence of each factor on ecosystem quality. The entire process was implemented in Python.

Results

Spatiotemporal variation of ecosystem quality

The spatial distribution of the RSEI reveals significant spatial heterogeneity in ecosystem quality across Shanxi Province, particularly between its eastern and western regions (Fig. 2). Throughout the study period, Shanxi Province generally exhibited a pattern of poorer ecological conditions in the west and relatively better conditions in the east. In 2000, RSEI values in the western regions, specifically along the eastern bank of the Yellow River, were consistently below 0.3. Indeed, a comparison of RSEI values over the 22-year period reveals a general improvement in ecosystem quality across Shanxi Province, with the average RSEI increasing from 0.47 to 0.52 in 2010, and subsequently to 0.59 in 2022. The proportion of areas classified as having fair ecosystem quality (RSEI ≤ 0.3) decreased significantly from 9.45% of the province in 2000 to 8.13% in 2010, and only to 0.91% in 2022. Notably, many areas along the Yellow River corridor have recovered to a moderate level (0.3 < RSEI ≤ 0.7). Concurrently, the area classified as having good ecosystem quality (RSEI ≥ 0.7) expanded by a factor of 1.21.

A grid-based difference analysis (Fig. 2d) indicates that ecosystem quality improved in 88.4% of the study area during 22-year period. However, not all regions exhibited sustained improvement over this timeframe. In the initial phase from 2000 to 2010, the northern regions showed a discernible downward trend, but this fluctuation was masked by improvements over the subsequent decade. The most substantial improvements were concentrated in the southwestern Lüliang Mountains and the downstream Fen River valley. However, a marked degradation was observed in plain regions, particularly in areas with larger urban centers or concentrated settlements, such as the central urban agglomeration.

Evolution and morphology of GI

During the study period, GI constituted an average of 73.47% of the study area and was primarily distributed across extensive regions, excluding the Fen River valley and alluvial plains. Following the classification of GI into seven morphological types using MSPA (Fig. 3), core was identified as the most prevalent type, accounting for an average of 47.93% of the total GI. Edge and bridge followed, representing 24.16% and 16.06% of the total GI, respectively. In 2000, core accounted for 41.70% of the GI, primarily concentrated in higher-elevation mountainous regions, including the Wutai Mountain World Heritage Site. Edge was more frequently observed in lower-elevation river valleys, particularly concentrated in the Shangdang Basin in the southeast. Branch exhibited a relatively higher prevalence along the Yellow River corridor in the northwest. Although islet constituted a small proportion of the total GI, it displayed localized concentrations in lower-elevation river valleys. Other GI types exhibited a sporadic and dispersed distribution pattern across the study area.

Although core remained the most prevalent GI morphology (54.17%) and exhibited some expansion in areas with > 75% coverage, its overall spatial distribution pattern did not undergo drastic changes and remained primarily concentrated in mountainous regions. The reduction in edge and bridge was particularly notable, with both types experiencing area losses exceeding 34%, especially the loss of many areas with > 50% coverage. Specifically, the highly concentrated pattern of edge in the east was disrupted: the substantial amount of edge that was clustered in the Shangdang Basin in the southeast in 2000 had largely disappeared by 2010, and had almost completely vanished by 2022. In fact, all six GI types other than core experienced a reduction over the 22-year period. Not only did the more abundant types undergo significant reductions, but even the initially least abundant islet decreased by 4.54%, representing the most direct cause of the overall GI reduction.

A comparison of the spatial distribution differences of GI and different landscape morphology types among 2000, 2010 and 2022 (Fig. 4) corroborates the changes and reveals further details. Although core is the most prevalent GI type and increased in most areas of Shanxi Province, the reduction of core in localized regions is of greater concern. This is because the areas where core decreased are primarily concentrated in the southern section of the Lüliang Mountains (which even includes a national nature reserve) and around towns in river valley areas. The reduction of bridge and edge was more pronounced than other types and occurred across a wider area. In the eastern and southern regions, their decrease dominated the overall GI reduction trend; in the western regions, their changes were, to some extent, complementary to the changes in core, resulting in a relative balance in the total amount of GI.

Mechanisms of morphology and characteristic factors of GI on ecosystem quality

In Fig. 7, the order in which different factors are arranged indicates the extent of their influence on ecosystem quality. The optimal models for all selected years achieved R2 values exceeding 0.65. Regardless of whether it was 2000, 2010 or 2022, PLf was the most important influencing factor, while the influence of NPg was the weakest. Among these factors, PLf exhibits a positive correlation with ecosystem quality. The influence of other factors on ecosystem quality changes with the complex variations in landscape morphology and characteristics over time.Fig. 7SHAP summary plot of landscape metrics from XGBoost models in 2000, 2010 and 2022.

SHAP summary plot of landscape metrics from XGBoost models in 2000, 2010 and 2022.

In 2000 (Fig. 7a), among the GI morphology type factors, bridge, core, and islet had a significant influence on ecosystem quality. Most of the grids with a high percentage of bridge were distributed in the range where the SHAP value was less than 0, indicating that they had a negative impact on ecological quality. Although core dominated the GI composition, it did not have an overall positive impact on the ecosystem. Although islet was scarce, its percentage had a stronger influence on ecosystem quality than most characteristic factors. In addition, the diversity characteristics and quantity percentage of GI had negative and positive influences on ecosystem quality, respectively.

In 2010 (Fig. 7b), PLf remained the most significant influencing factor. The importance of LPIf was similar to that in 2000. The overall trend in 2010 was positioned between those of 2000 and 2022, indicating a transitional effect. Additionally, the importance of Shannon increased during this period.In 2022 (Fig. 7c), Shannon became the second most important factor influencing ecosystem quality and continued to have a negative impact on ecosystem quality. Compared to 2000, the influence of the LPI and PL of grassland on ecosystem quality significantly increased. The influence of bridge weakened but remained negative. The influence of the branch increased, and its impact on ecosystem quality became significantly more positive.

Through Supplementary Figs. 1–3, we can validate the research results presented in Fig. 7 and discover more specific impacts of morphological and characteristic factors on ecosystem quality. Forest is not only a very important influencing factor, but when the PLf is greater than 20%, its positive impact on the ecosystem is very significant. Although PLg does not have a strong explanatory power for habitat quality, it does not have a positive impact on habitat quality when it exceeds 90%. The impact of GI diversity on ecosystem quality did not show a prominent pattern in 2000, but in 2010 and 2022, when the Shannon is greater than 1.0, its impact on habitat quality is negative. In addition, when NPg exceed 5.0, its impact on ecosystem quality changes from a previously negative one to a more positive one. Similar changes towards a more positive impact also occur when the percentage of branch is greater than 5% or the percentage of islets is greater than 12%.

Discussion

The equal importance of protecting islets and cores

Core areas are large GI patches that provide contiguous habitats as source areas within the ecological network, which is crucial for constructing ecological security patterns and protecting biodiversity50. The study found that despite the overall reduction in GI, the study area maintained the size and spatial stability of core areas. This is consistent with previous studies on Shanxi Province44. The study area has been fully participating in the GTGP since 2000, resulting in the conversion of a large amount of cultivated land into forest, especially in mountainous regions. As shown in Fig. 6, over the 22 years after 2000, the LPI and PL of forest increased in most parts of Shanxi. This significant change in land use due to ecological restoration strategies has not only led to changes in the characteristics of GI but has also directly protected the size of core areas. It can be inferred that GTGP is clearly effective in protecting core areas as ecological source areas. Furthermore, machine learning methods intuitively reflect the positive effect of increased PLf on improving ecosystem quality (Supplementary Fig. 1–3). Therefore, protecting forest-dominated core through ecological restoration projects such as GTGP is a simple and effective way to improve the ecological environment in Shanxi Province. Our study supports the local government in continuing the implementation of the GTGP to maintain the scale of GI and enhance ecosystem quality. Future urban and rural planning and ecological managers should also pay attention to maintaining the proportion of core in the landscape.

More attractively, our research found that islet is not less important than core simply because of its small size. In 2000, the degree of influence of islet on ecosystem quality ranked 4th among all influencing factors, second only to core, which occupies the largest proportion in space (Fig. 7). Although the islet experienced a significant reduction in size over the 23 years, potentially leading to a decline in its influence, its role in improving ecosystem quality has become more positive. Supplementary Fig. 3 shows that when the percentage of islet in a 6 km × 6 km grid exceeds 10%, ecosystem quality improves. In the past, related studies in island biogeography have confirmed that even small habitat patches play important ecological functions51,52, such as improving biodiversity and supporting pollination services53,54. Small habitat fragments can serve as crucial stepping stones within a larger landscape. To maximize beta diversity and distribute ecological risk effectively, these fragments should be sufficiently dispersed across a broad geographical area55. This research suggests that even small GI patches can contribute positively to ecosystem quality, potentially functioning in a similar manner.

Restoration and construction of ecological corridors

Ecological corridors are narrow strips of vegetation or waterways that have formed and possess specific ecological functions. These ecological zones exhibit unique linear shapes, distinguishing them from the surrounding matrix or isolated habitat patches56. Previous research has demonstrated that ecological corridors can provide a “conduit function” by facilitating the movement of organisms between habitat patches57. They also offer valuable ecosystem services and support systemic flows58,59. Unlike traditional ecological studies, our research does not directly identify linear forest or rivers as corridors. Instead, we use MSPA to identify truly connected linear GI elements—bridges, branches, and loops—as the current ecological corridors. However, the study reveals a concerning trend: these ecological corridors experienced a significant reduction in area between 2000 and 2010, with an even more pronounced decline by 2022. Therefore, we hypothesize that while the ongoing GTGP implementation is beneficial for maintaining the total GI area, it primarily contributes to increasing the core area, without effectively supporting the corridors.

Furthermore, our study moves beyond a focus on specific species or ecosystem services, adopting a more comprehensive indicator: RSEI. We found that bridges have a significant impact on ecosystem quality (Fig. 7), although this impact tends to be negative. Based on the actual environment of the study area, we can observe that bridges are mainly located in the mountainous areas in the western part and along the banks of the Yellow River. These are typical areas with highly fragmented landscapes, accompanied by a lack of core elements. So, we suspect that the negative impact reflected by bridges in the XGboost model mainly stems from the lack of completeness of the core habitats. We call for ongoing optimization of bridge planning while maintaining the scale of core areas to enhance the integrity of GI’s functions.

Branches, on the other hand, have become increasingly important over the 20-year landscape change period, showing a growing tendency to exert a positive influence (Supplementary Figs. 1–3). Currently, if the proportion of branches exceeds 5% at the raster scale, the RSEI tends to be greater than 0. Therefore, we strongly urge environmental managers to strengthen the restoration and construction of ecological corridors represented by branches. Before formulating ecological management strategies and urban planning schemes, it is essential to accurately identify existing ecological corridors using scientific methods. Based on the coverage–feature–morphology types framework, a precise assessment of the current situation should be conducted to uncover specific issues. Given that human activities, such as rapid urbanization, significantly influence RSEI60, particular attention must be paid to avoiding urban expansion that encroaches upon existing branches in urban and densely populated areas. For Shanxi Province, it is important to ensure that the proportion of branches in GI is above 5%. Other regions should also refer to this experience when developing ecological restoration strategies, giving sufficient attention to areas where corridors are insufficient or show a decreasing trend.

Designing GI through a coverage-feature-morphology system to improve environmental quality

Balancing rapid economic development and urbanization with the imperative of maintaining ecological security and a livable environment is a key challenge for urban managers and ecologists alike. Constructing ecological networks through GI is a promising strategy to address this challenge. Ecological network construction involves identifying the critical components and structure of ecological processes across multiple stages, optimizing patterns and combining types where necessary to ensure the integrity and fluidity of natural ecological processes61. In traditional ecological research, the construction of ecological networks emphasizes different levels of thresholds. Many studies focusing on GI have concentrated on coverage, validating the positive effects of large-area GI, represented by protected areas and national parks, on biodiversity conservation and ecosystem services. The significance of landscape characteristics and configuration for environmental improvement is increasingly attracting attention. However, GI designs based solely on LPM cannot be mapped geographically, making it difficult to provide managers with direct decision-making tools. At the landscape and regional scales, managers require a more comprehensive evaluation framework, while at the local scale and within urban areas, additional morphological factors must also be considered62.

Our research combines MSPA with LPM to coverage–feature–morphology types framework, providing a more comprehensive GI evaluation system. This approach directly identifies specific phenomena and issues, enabling managers to make informed planning and management decisions based on local conditions. In Shanxi Province, for example, the government has invested in various costs, including ecological compensation, tax exemption, and technical guidance, to attract farmers to participate in the GTGP for more than 20 years. However, the GI changes brought about by such huge investments are mostly reflected in the increase of the core area, while islets and corridors have continued to shrink. This reflects the problem of early ecological restoration strategies that emphasized coverage but neglected pattern. Therefore, for densely populated and ecologically fragile areas such as Shanxi Province, a more comprehensive evaluation and design is more accurate and effective. It is advisable to improve vegetation structure and enhance landscape heterogeneity by supplementing diverse local plants and shrubs. This approach will enhance the habitat quality within the core and facilitate its function as a species pool for fostering greater biodiversity.In urbanized areas (e.g., Taiyuan, Jinzhong, Datong, Changzhi, etc.), small GI patches, mainly islets, should be supplemented artificially. Because these areas face land resource scarcity and population inflow due to urban expansion, they are more likely to have insufficient GI construction space and ecological security risks. Therefore, increasing islets can save land resources while exerting the positive effect of GI on improving ecosystem quality. At the same time, the reasonable configuration of small patches in space is also more likely to promote exposure equity.Prioritizing the establishment of linear landscapes within GI is essential. Relying on the natural geographical pattern, major ecological corridors across administrative units can be established along major rivers such as the Fen River and Sanggan River. More importantly, in areas where urban expansion is occurring and between core areas, linear semi-natural habitats should be actively designed and constructed artificially, such as designing rich transition zones at road and farmland boundaries, to enhance ecological flow between urban centers and surrounding core areas.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the spatiotemporal variations in ecosystem quality and GI in Shanxi Province, revealing significant ecological trends over a 22-year period. Our findings reveal a notable overall improvement in ecosystem quality, yet pronounced spatial heterogeneity persists, with western regions lagging behind the east. While core GI types remain dominant, a concerning reduction in edge and bridge types indicates declining landscape connectivity and integrity, a trend particularly acute in urbanized and low-elevation areas suffering from high fragmentation.

Mechanistically, forest patches proved critical, exhibiting a strong positive correlation with higher ecosystem quality. The observed transition of core GI to isolated islets highlights the pressing threat of fragmentation to ecosystem resilience. Therefore, this study underscores the urgent need for tailored management strategies focused on enhancing woodland areas and restoring GI connectivity. Future efforts must prioritize integrating ecological restoration across both urban and rural landscapes to build resilience and ensure sustainable land management in the face of environmental changes.
