Distinct difference of pancreatic tissue-specific microbiome in autoimmune pancreatitis and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Koh Nakamaru, Takashi Ito, Tsubasa Shimogama, Takuya Shijimaya, Tomomitsu Tahara, Jumpei Yamazaki, Ayaka Orino, Masataka Masuda, Shinji Nakayama, Tsukasa Ikeura, Makoto Naganuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) is a form of chronic pancreatitis that may be difficult to distinguish from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Emerging evidence suggests the substantial involvement of gut microbiome dysbiosis in various disorders, including pancreatic diseases. This study investigates the differences in pancreatic tissue-specific microbiomes between AIP and PDAC. Pancreatic tissues were obtained from patients with type 1 AIP (n = 17) or PDAC (n = 24) via ultrasound-guided tissue acquisition and subjected to 16S rRNA sequencing. The sequences were used to determine the bacterial alpha diversity and characterize the microbiome structures related to different sample groups. The pancreatic microbiome in PDAC exhibited increased bacterial alpha diversity compared to that in AIP. The abundances of 16 bacteria were significantly different between the AIP and PDAC groups, and most of them (15/16) were increased in PDAC relative to that in AIP. The bacterial index calculated using these bacteria had a favorable discriminative ability for these different groups, with an area under the curve value of 0.91. Functional analysis demonstrated that the pentose phosphate pathway, gondoate biosynthesis, and several pathways related to adenosine biosynthesis were positively associated with the bacterial index. The findings from this preliminary study indicate that pancreatic microbiome, as a new diagnostic alternative, may potentially help with the differential diagnosis of AIP and PDAC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44821-w.Keywords: Pancreatic microbiome, Pancreatic tissue, Autoimmune pancreatitis, 16S rRNA sequencing, Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomaSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Microbiology

Introduction

Autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) is a rare and specific form of pancreatitis1,2. AIP is characterized by a dramatic response to steroids, and thus the pathogenesis of this disease appears to involve autoimmune mechanisms. AIP can be classified into two types: type 1 and type 2. Both types share similarities in symptoms, imaging features, and treatment, but differ considerably in terms of pathological manifestations. Type 1 is thought to involve the pancreas in immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD) and is characterized by lymphoplasmacytic infiltration in IgG4-positive plasma cells, stromiform fibrosis, and obliterative phlebitis. Meanwhile, type 2 is characterized by granulocytic epithelial lesions3.

The typical imaging features of AIP consist of diffuse pancreatic enlargement, sometimes accompanied by a capsule-like rim and narrowing of the pancreatic duct. However, a subset of AIP cases manifests as focal lesions, with the majority of these lesions located in the pancreatic head. Discriminating such lesions from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is crucial but considerably challenging. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are widely used to distinguish PDAC from AIP; however, their diagnostic accuracy has not been satisfactory, with variable sensitivity (for example,12–83% for CT)4. Histological assessment is the gold standard for diagnosis. However, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) techniques, including EUS-guided tissue acquisition (EUS-TA), have limited diagnostic sensitivity and sampling artifacts5. Serological markers such as carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) and IgG4 have been used for differentiating PDAC and AIP. Approximately 5–10% of PDACs are Lewis antigen negative, resulting in normal CA19-9 levels6. Some PDACs exhibit elevated IgG4 levels7,8, whereas most type 2 AIP cases and a minority of type 1 AIP cases do not show elevated IgG4 levels. However, some patients with AIP, especially those with jaundice, may also present with elevated CA19-9 levels9. These findings indicate that these serological markers may not be sufficient to precisely differentiate between PDAC and AIP. Given the limited diagnostic accuracy of current approaches for discriminating AIP from PDAC, the establishment of novel biomarkers based on a deeper knowledge of disease pathogenesis may provide more accurate alternatives for differential diagnosis of the two diseases.

Recent metagenomic studies have suggested that bacterial communities in the gastrointestinal tract, referred to as the gut microbiota, play a significant role in regulating host physiology. Changes in the gut microbiome composition, termed dysbiosis, play an important role in various disorders10–12, including gastrointestinal disorders13 and pancreatic diseases such as chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer14,15. Dovhalyuk et al.16 have shown that the fecal metabolites, presumably derived from gut microbes in patients with AIP are different from those in chronic pancreatitis, despite both diseases causing chronic fibroinflammatory changes in the pancreas. Indeed, a previous study also found significant differences in in gut microbiota profiles between AIP and chronic pancreatitis. Namely, the proportions of Bacteroides, Streptococcus and Clostridium species were higher in patients with chronic pancreatitis than in AIP17. A multinational study demonstrated shared gut microbial signatures associated with PDAC risk in Asian and European cohorts18. Above results have indicated that the gut microbiome can be a powerful source of biomarkers for both AIP and PDAC.

Importantly, intestinal barrier disruption worsens experimental AIP by facilitating the translocation of pathogenic bacteria from the gut to the pancreas. These results indicate that the “gut–pancreas axis” may be involved in pathogenesis of AIP19. However, oral pathogenic bacteria may also reach the pancreas through circulation or transductal transmission from the biliary tract and can contribute to pancreatic carcinogenesis. This concept was supported by the detection of the Fusobacterium species, an oral bacterial group of the human microbiome, in the pancreatic cancer tissue of patients with a worse prognosis20. Evidence suggests that not only the gut microbiome but also the pancreatic microbiome may serve as potential diagnostic markers, reflecting their pathogenic contribution. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the differences in the tissue-specific microbiomes of AIP and PDAC using pancreatic tissues obtained via EUS-TA.

Materials and methods

Tissue samples and genomic DNA extraction

This study included 17 patients with type 1 AIP and 24 patients with PDAC treated at the Kansai Medical University Hospital. AIP was diagnosed based on the clinical diagnostic criteria21. All patients with PDAC were histologically diagnosed using tissue samples obtained via EUS-TA. For all AIP cases, pancreatic samples were obtained using EUS-TA. During EUS-TA, tissue samples were cut into two pieces. One part of the sample was used for histological assessment and the other was immediately suspended in a lysis solution containing 25 mM EDTA (pH 8.0) and 2% SDS. Genomic DNA was extracted using the standard precipitation method. Briefly, specimen was incubated overnight in a lysis solution containing proteinase K at 55 °C. The obtained lysate was precipitated and washed first using isopropyl alcohol and then with 80% ethanol, followed by resuspension of the isolated genomic DNA in TE buffer (pH 8.0). Histological assessment confirmed that all PDAC tissues contained more than 50% cancerous tissue, whereas all AIP tissues contained more than 50% pancreatic tissue. We initially invited 18 patients with type 1 AIP and 24 patients with PDAC but excluded one AIP patients due to low genomic DNA quality. Kansai Medical University approved the study protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent for study enrolment and publishing of information and images. The study was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

Using this DNA, the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F (5'-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 805R (5'-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC) targeting the V3–V4 hypervariable region. The universal sequence of the Illumina adapter were included in the 5’ end of the primers. Samples underwent denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 40 s; and a final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were purified and processed using the Nextera XT Index kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), followed by an additional 12 cycles of PCR. The concentrations of all DNA libraries were normalized and then pooled for next-generation sequencing using a MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc.) and the MiSeq Reagent Kit version 3 (2 × 300 bp Paired-End Reads, Illumina Inc.).

The sequencing results were processed using the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME) pipeline (Ver.2023.2). Primer-trimmed sequences were clustered into amplicon sequence variants using the q2-dada2 plugin, and sequences with anonymous bases and chimeras were filtered. Bacterial alpha diversity was calculated using the amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table in QIIME2. The mean relative abundances (percentage of all reads) of each group were compared at the genus level. The co-abundance network was determined and visualized using Cytoscape 3.10.3 (https://cytoscape.org/) based on compositional data using Spearman correlation algorithms. Microbial diversity was visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) using ArrayTrack (http://www.fda.gov/ScienceResearch/BioinformaticsTools/Arraytrack/). Microbial functions were predicted using PICRUSt2 in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; https://www.kegg.jp/) database.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between the two groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. The correlation of continuous variables between the two groups was assessed using the Spearman correlation analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The discriminatory potential of different sample groups based on microbial status was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC).

Bacterial compositions in AIP and PDAC tissues

Tissue samples from all the 17 and 24 patients with AIP and PDAC, respectively, were available for 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. After quality filtering, 254,660 sequences (mean; 6,221 per sample) were obtained. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered based on the Greengenes v.13.8 reference database on the QIIME website (http://qiime.org/home_static/dataFiles.html).

Initially, the bacterial alpha diversity measures were compared between the AIP and PDAC groups using the following three indicators: Shannon index, observed features, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity. All three indicators were significantly higher in the PDAC group than in the AIP group (Fig. 1).

Bacterial alpha diversity measures between autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) groups. Statistical analysis was performed using Mann–Whitney U test.

We then searched for specific bacterial taxa associated with the different tissue groups. At the genus level, the abundances of 16 bacteria were significantly different between the AIP and PDAC groups. The abundance of Staphylococcus was significantly higher in the AIP group than in the PDAC group, whereas the abundances of the remaining 15 bacteria were significantly higher in the PDAC group than in the AIP group (Fig. 2a). The identification of multiple bacteria in specific tissue types indicated the existence of community-wide virulence properties involved in other diseases22,23. A correlation analysis of the 16 significantly altered bacteria (Fig. 2b) revealed complex co-occurring interactions between these 16 taxa and several hub genera, most notably Streptobacillus. This finding supports the synergistic contribution of the bacterial network to diseases, which may also serve as a potential diagnostic tissue marker. To confirm this, we performed PCoA on these 16 taxa. This analysis separated the majority of the AIP samples from the PDAC samples (Fig. 2c). We then combined these 16 taxa and calculated their respective bacterial indexes22,23. The bacterial index was defined as the sum of the number of increased bacteria in the PDAC groups divided by the abundance of Staphylococcus. The bacterial index had a favorable discriminative ability (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2d), with an AUC of 0.91 (Fig. 2e). We also investigated whether the bacterial index was associated with the clinicopathological characteristics of patients with AIP and PDAC (Table 2). Although the bacterial index was significantly higher in females, no association was found between the bacterial index and the remaining clinicopathological characteristics of the patients with AIP and PDAC.

Functional analysis of the microbiome

Finally, we performed predictive functional analysis using PICRUSt2 based on bacterial information to discriminate PDAC from AIP. Significant correlations between the metabolic pathways were determined with respect to the bacterial index (Fig. 3). The top ten significant metabolic pathways with positive correlations included the pentose phosphate pathway, gondoate biosynthesis, and several pathways related to adenosine biosynthesis.

Discussion

Emerging evidence suggests the substantial involvement of gut microbiome dysbiosis in various disorders of the digestive tract, including chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer14–17. Our results demonstrated distinct structural differences in the pancreatic microbiomes of AIP and PDAC using tissue specimens obtained via EUS-TA. The distinct microbial structure in PDAC was characterized by higher bacterial alpha diversity measures than that in AIP. Since specific microbial changes in the AIP and PDAC groups were characterized by increased taxa in PDAC (15 out of 16) and decreased taxa (1 out of 16) in AIP, changes in alpha diversity measures in the specific groups of samples seemed to reflect changes in specific microbiomes in different groups of tissues. During EUS-TA procedure, pancreatic tissues are obtained penetrating other organ such as gastric mucosa, the possibility of contamination of microbes from other organ should be considered. Moreover, antibiotics are routinely administrated before EUS-TA for all patients, which may affect microbiome status. On the other hand, previous study demonstrated that the intra-tumoral microbiome profile of PDAC tissues obtained by EUS-TA had similar alpha-diversity and taxonomic profiles with those obtained by surgical biopsy, suggesting that EUS-TA can be another valid and valuable tool for studying tissue-specific microbiome in pancreatic diseases because the effect of contamination and administrated antibiotics might be small24. In this study, all EUS-TA procedures were performed penetrating gastric wall to obtain pancreatic tissue. Thus, the difference of microbiome structures between AIP and PDAC would be attributed to the disease-associated pancreatic microbiome of both diseases rather than the contamination of other organ, while it should be also noted that influence of contamination from gastric mucosa could not be completely excluded. Regarding the origin of the pancreatic microbiome, the translocation of pathogenic bacteria to the pancreas from the gut has been suggested to contribute to the immunopathogenesis of AIP19,25. In the experimental mouse model of AIP, Staphylococcus sciuri (S. sciuri) mono-colonized mice treated with polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C)), a prototypical TLR3 ligand developed more severe AIP than the germ-free mice. Importantly, development of severe AIP in S. sciuri monocolonized mice treated with poly(I:C) was accompanied by a marked accumulation of plasmacytoid dendritic cells that produce type I type I interferon and interleukin-33 in the pancreas19, suggesting that S. sciuri plays a pathogenic role in aggravating experimental AIP. Interestingly, our result also demonstrated significantly higher abundance of genus Staphylococcus in the AIP group, while this result was obtained in comparison with the PDAC group. Causal effect of this bacteria in the development of AIP need to be further evaluated.

Meanwhile, it has also been suggested that oral pathogenic bacteria may also reach the pancreas through circulation or transductal transmission from the biliary tract26 and can contribute to pancreatic carcinogenesis20. Detection of the Fusobacterium species, an oral bacterial group of the human microbiome, in the pancreatic cancer tissue has been associated with a worse prognosis20. An analysis of the pancreatic microbiome in intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN), which is known risk factor for PDAC revealed that the presence of specific bacterial strains reduced the cytotoxic effect of gemcitabine that is a key chemotherapeutic agent for PDAC27. It has also been demonstrated that microbial metabolites derived from pathogenic microbiota in IPMN activate human mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells and restrict the bacterial infection in a tumor-like spheroid28. These results suggest that not only the gut microbiome but also the pancreatic microbiome may have clinical implications in the management of pancreatic malignancy.

Comparison of AIP and PDAC pancreatic microbiomes identified 16 genera with significant changes between both groups; however, none of these overlapped with the pancreatic cancer-associated gut microbiome, as shown in a recent multinational study comparing patients with pancreatic cancer and healthy controls17. Because of the ethical issue, our study could not investigate pancreatic microbiome in healthy subjects using EUS-TA samples, which needs to be considered as a limitation. Since our study compared AIP and PDAC groups, our result can be best viewed as an evidence that the bacteria identified in our results would be useful for discriminating PDAC from AIP, but not from healthy subjects.

Complex co-occurrences and interactions were observed among the 16 AIP/PDAC-discriminating bacteria. This indicates the existence of community-wide virulence properties in several diseases22,23,29. Indeed, PCoA of these 16 taxa separated the majority of AIP samples from PDAC samples, suggesting that combining these taxa may better differentiate the two groups. The bacterial index calculated using these bacteria had a favorable discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.91. This supports the synergistic contribution of the bacterial network to pancreatic diseases, further suggesting that the combination of tissue-specific bacteria may serve as a potential diagnostic marker, as shown in other studies22,23,29. Despite recent advances in diagnostic strategies, the differential diagnosis of AIP and PDAC in a subset of cases remains challenging, even when using imaging modalities4, histological assessment5and serological markers6–9. Our preliminary findings provide evidence that pancreatic microbiome could be a diagnostic alternative to differentially diagnose AIP from PDAC. In the current clinical setting, the gold standard for differential diagnosis of AIP from PDAC would be histopathologic analysis, while there are considerable cases with inconclusive histopathologic diagnosis by the EUS-TA5. Thus, clinical utility of our result can be best viewed as an additional clinical test in patients who could not achieve conclusive diagnosis by histopathologic assessment by the EUS-TA.

Distinct differences in the pancreatic microbiome between the AIP and PDAC groups were further supported by functional correlations between metabolic pathways and the bacterial index. The top ten significant metabolic pathways with positive correlations included the pentose phosphate pathway, gondoate biosynthesis, and several pathways related to adenosine biosynthesis. Because the bacterial index positively correlated with PDAC-related bacteria, our results suggest that these metabolic pathways are relevant to the PDAC tissue-specific microbiome. One might speculate that the pentose phosphate pathway confers more severe oxidative stress, leading to host somatic changes towards carcinogenesis, which is crucial for cancer because it provides NADPH for reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defense and ribose-5-phosphate for nucleic acid synthesis30,31. Gondoate biosynthesis has also been linked to carcinogenesis through the activity of the gut and oral microbiome, specifically in the context of oral squamous cell carcinoma and potentially intestinal cancer32. The functional association highlighted pathway-specific changes by altered microbiome and raises the possibility of treating PDAC and its premalignant conditions using biologic therapy, such as probiotics and prebiotics, as shown in other diseases33.

This study has some limitations. First, functional analysis was predicted using PICRUSt2 and specific pathways were not identified by direct metabolite detection. Well-designed future studies should investigate and validate the role of these predicted specific pathways in pancreatic tumorigenesis. Second, this retrospective study included a small cohort in a single cultural and geographical setting (Japan), and its findings should be confirmed in diverse populations. Third, we did not find a robust correlation between disease-discriminating bacteria and the clinicopathological characteristics of AIP and PDAC, possibly due to the limited number of samples. Since microbiome profile have been associated with responses against prednisolone treatment in AIP34 as well as the neoadjuvant chemotherapy in PDAC35, further case studies are required to evaluate the utility of this bacterial index in various clinical settings. Fourth, we could not compare the pancreatic microbiome of patients with AIP and PDAC with that of healthy subjects owing to the lack of tissues obtained via EUS-TA. Finally, our study mainly reports the findings of correlative analyses without experimental validation of the microbial mechanisms. Therefore, the contribution of the specific bacteria and pathways identified in our study to disease pathogenesis requires further validation using in vivo and ex vivo systems. Nonetheless, this is the first report that compares the pancreatic microbiome in AIP and PDAC, and provides preliminary evidence for further exploration of the potential use of the microbiome for differential diagnosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our result showed distinct difference of pancreatic tissue-specific microbiome in AIP and PDAC. The findings from this preliminary study indicate that pancreatic microbiome, as a new diagnostic alternative, may potentially help with the differential diagnosis of AIP and PDAC.
