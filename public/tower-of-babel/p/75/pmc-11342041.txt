The impact of Anatomage table on writing of medical students' case reports: A comparative study
Authors: Ekaterina Kldiashvili, Saif Al‐Rustum, Josette Denekens, Herman van Rossum
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsAcademic performance is essential in medical education, yet comprehensive training programs addressing this need are lacking. Simulation‐based learning has emerged as a valuable tool for developing clinical skills, with the Anatomage table offering a virtual platform to enhance learning experiences. This study aimed to assess the impact of utilizing the Anatomage table on the academic performance and similarity of case reports among seventh‐semester medical students.MethodsA total of 68 students participated, with 25 using paper‐based medical history and 43 using the Anatomage table. Descriptive statistics and Turnitin were employed for data analysis.ResultsStudents using the Anatomage table exhibited higher academic performance and lower similarity percentages compared to those using paper‐based medical history.ConclusionThese findings highlight the benefits of integrating advanced technology into medical education to enhance learning outcomes and prepare students for clinical practice. Further investigation is required to improve the effectiveness of simulation‐based learning in enhancing academic performance of medical students.

INTRODUCTION

Research skills are a fundamental requirement in the field of medicine, as healthcare professionals are expected to be lifelong learners capable of evaluating and analyzing data and comprehending the scientific principles underlying medical processes. Regardless of whether a medical school graduate pursues a research career, they must possess the ability to critically evaluate online information and design and execute their own research studies. 1 The World Federation for Medical Education has established standards to evaluate research competence in medical students, highlighting the importance of research training in medical education. 2 Similarly, the Association for Medical Education in Europe emphasizes the role of active student participation in research activities to enhance their understanding of research methodology. 3 Therefore, it is crucial that medical education programs incorporate training and activities that foster the development of research and science communication skills among students. Despite this demand, there is a lack of comprehensive and targeted training in medical settings. 4

Simulation‐based learning (SBL) is a critical educational approach used for developing clinical skills in medical education. 5 , 6 , 7 The need for SBL arises from patient safety concerns and the complexity of diagnostic procedures. Opportunities for practicing important clinical skills are often insufficient, and certain clinical conditions are rare and difficult to predict, making it challenging to provide students with training opportunities. 8 Additionally, enlisting patients for educational purposes can be difficult. The Anatomage table, developed by the California‐based three‐dimensional medical technology company Anatomage, addresses these challenges by offering a virtual platform for students to practice clinical skills. 9 This tool presents a digital, life‐sized human cadaver suitable for virtual dissection exercises, supplemented by a case library with over 1000 radiology images of real patients.

Case reports, which present medical conditions experienced by patients, play a significant role in medical education by allowing students to share their medical experiences. While case reports are of minor significance in evidence‐based medicine, they are valuable for educational purposes. 10 However, despite the educational value of preparing case reports, undergraduate medical students often neglect this task.

This study aims to investigate the impact of the Anatomage table on the academic performance and similarity percentage of case reports prepared by VII semester medical students. We hypothesize that students who utilize the Anatomage table will demonstrate better performance in formulating case reports compared to those who do not use it. The study's conceptual framework focuses on the integration of SBL in enhancing research and communication skills. The primary research question is: How does the use of the Anatomage table influence the quality and originality of case reports among medical students? Briefly, we hypothesize that the innovative use of the Anatomage table will lead to improved academic performance and a lower similarity percentage in case reports, reflecting higher originality and understanding.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 68 students of participated in the study, 25 students who prepared the case report through review and analysis of paper‐based medical history, and 43 students who performed the same assignment by individual review and analysis of Anatomage table case. These students were in their seventh semester of a single‐step medical curriculum, with educational backgrounds including courses such as “Academic Writing,” “Introduction to Medical Research,” and “Critical Thinking.” Additionally, they were enrolled in clinical subjects and possessed knowledge in basic and diagnostic sciences, albeit without prior exposure to the Anatomage table. In our study, the students' backgrounds and abilities are similar across the two groups, therefore a sample size of 68 students is statistically relevant as it could adequately capture the variability within the population. Furthermore, a sample size of 68 students provides a reasonable level of confidence in the study outcomes, considering our study objectives.

To ensure proper organization of the assignment, a list of 52 clinical conditions was shared with both groups of students in advance as a Google Sheet document. Students were allowed to select the topic of their interest from the list, but duplication was not permitted. Basic concepts and instructions 11 , 12 on how to write the case report were presented to the students, along with the evaluation criteria and principles of submission and evaluation. The students were given a period of 14 days to prepare and submit their case reports, and Turnitin, an internet‐based service provided by Turnitin LLC, was used to collect and evaluate the papers submitted by both groups.

The similarity percentage of the submitted case reports was checked using Turnitin, with a maximum allowable similarity percentage of 20%. In cases where the similarity percentage was found to be higher, the submission was not considered for evaluation. It is worth noting that Turnitin was used for both submission and evaluation, and no additional training on its usage was provided to the students. The results of the study will shed light on the effectiveness of Anatomage table as a tool for improving the academic performance of medical students in case report writing.

The statistical methods employed in the study involved a combination of descriptive analyses to examine the differences in academic performance and similarity percentages between the two groups of students. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the student cohorts, including their academic performance scores and similarity percentages. Measures such as average, mean, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges were computed to provide a comprehensive overview of the data.

Ethical clearance was secured from the Bioethics International Committee of the Petre Shotadze Tbilisi Medical Academy (identification code: 20220503/01, Tbilisi, Georgia). All procedures adhered to the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, revised in 2013, with participants receiving comprehensive study information and providing written informed consent before inclusion.

RESULTS

Before the study, students were assessed based on their overall performance in various educational activities and assignments. These assessments included assignments related to academic writing, introduction to medical research, critical thinking, and clinical subjects. The students' performance in these assignments provided an indication of their proficiency in research skills, analytical thinking, and ability to communicate medical information effectively. For example, students might have been tasked with writing literature reviews, conducting small research projects, analyzing case studies, or presenting findings from clinical observations. These assignments were designed to assess students' ability to gather and evaluate information, critically analyze research findings, and communicate their insights effectively. The selection rate for inclusion in the study was determined based on the similarity percentage of the students' assignments. Papers with a similarity percentage of more than 20% were excluded from the study to ensure the integrity and originality of the data analyzed.

The assessment of academic performance in the study was based on a comparison between the group of students who prepared case reports using the Anatomage table and those who utilized paper‐based medical history. The results indicated that the group utilizing the Anatomage table demonstrated higher academic performance and lower similarity percentages compared to the paper‐based medical history group (Figures 1 and 2; Table 1).

DISCUSSION

The findings of our study underscore the potential benefits of incorporating advanced technology, such as the Anatomage table, in medical education for preparing case reports. Our results indicate that students who utilized the Anatomage table demonstrated superior academic performance and lower levels of similarity in their case reports compared to those who relied on paper‐based medical history.

The use of the Anatomage table offers several advantages, including enhanced understanding of clinical cases and the development of critical thinking and problem‐solving skills, which are essential for medical students. 13 By providing a virtual platform for interactive learning and virtual dissection exercises, the Anatomage table enables students to engage with medical concepts in a more immersive and detailed manner.

Conversely, students who prepared case reports using paper‐based medical history exhibited lower academic performance and higher levels of similarity. This disparity may be attributed to the limitations of traditional resources and the lack of visualization tools available to these students. 14 , 15 The higher variability in performance within the paper‐based group suggests potential challenges related to individual learning styles or disparities in the quality of available resources. 16

Overall, our findings underscore the importance of integrating innovative technologies, such as the Anatomage table, into medical education to enhance learning outcomes and prepare students for the complex challenges of clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

The use of SBL could be an effective tool in medical education for facilitation of research and science communication skills development among medical students. Application of the functionalities of the Anatomage table can assist the students by aiding the better understanding of medical problems and formulating research questions. However, further research is needed to develop the best practice model of research and science communication skills development among medical students by usage of SBL application.

LIMITATIONS

The limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader student populations. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the applicability of the results to other educational settings with different resources and teaching methodologies.

Furthermore, while efforts were made to control for confounding variables, it is possible that unmeasured factors could have influenced the outcomes observed. For instance, variations in individual learning styles or prior exposure to similar educational technologies could have impacted students' performance differently. Another limitation is the reliance on self‐reported data for some variables, such as academic performance and similarity percentages, which may introduce bias or inaccuracies. Additionally, the study focused solely on academic performance and similarity in case reports, overlooking other potential outcomes or factors that could influence students' learning experiences. Lastly, the study design was cross‐sectional, which limits our ability to establish causality or infer long‐term effects of using the Anatomage table on student learning outcomes. Longitudinal studies with larger and more diverse samples would provide more robust evidence regarding the effectiveness of incorporating such technology in medical education.
