The Effect of Earthquake‐Related Anxiety Level on Nurses' Potential for Medical Errors: A Cross‐Sectional Study on the Disaster of the Century in Turkey
Authors: Reva Gündoğan, Sebahat Kuşlu, Sevim Güler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe study was planned to examine the effect of nurses' earthquake anxiety levels on their tendency to make medical errors.DesignThe research was conducted as a descriptive and cross‐sectional study.MethodsThe research was conducted with 172 nurses who worked in Adiyaman Training and Research Hospital, Turkey, between 25 September 2023 and 20 February 2024. The data of the study were collected with the ‘Personal Information Form’, ‘Earthquake Anxiety Scale’ and ‘Medical Error Tendency Scale’. Number, percentage, mean and standard deviation were used in the statistical analysis of the data. The t‐test was applied for the comparisons of two independent groups, and the ANOVA test was applied for the comparisons of more than two groups. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the scales. p < 0.05 was accepted as the significance level.ResultsIn the study, the Earthquake Anxiety Scale total mean score was found to be 110.49 ± 39.66 (medium level), and the Medical Error Tendency Scale total mean score was found to be 225.74 ± 16.73. It was determined that the anxiety levels of nurses working in intensive care were significantly higher than nurses working in internal, surgical and emergency services (p < 0.05). In addition, the Medical Error Tendency Scale's subscales of patient monitoring and material safety mean scores were found to be statistically significantly different between nurses working on mixed (day–night) shifts compared to nurses working on day shifts (p < 0.05). It was determined that there was a statistically significant weak and positive correlation between the Earthquake Anxiety Scale and the hospital infection subscale of the Medical Error Tendency Scale (p < 0.05). It was determined that there was no statistically significant correlation between the total mean scores of the Earthquake Anxiety Scale and the Medical Error Tendency Scale (p > 0.05).Patient or Public ContributionThis study's findings were significantly shaped by the contributions of the nurses who experienced the earthquake and volunteered their time and experiences. Their input was invaluable in understanding the impact of earthquake‐related anxiety on their professional performance and potential for medical errors. The participants provided critical insights that informed the study's conclusions and recommendations, which aim to improve support systems and reduce medical errors in disaster‐prone regions. The engagement and openness of these nurses underscore the importance of frontline healthcare workers' perspectives in research that seeks to enhance.ConclusionsIn the present research, it was found that nurses were moderately affected by the earthquake. Depending on this result, it can be recommended to determine the anxiety level of nurses who experienced an earthquake, to provide guidance and consultancy services accordingly, to increase awareness of medical errors to prevent medical errors, to provide in‐service training on patient safety and error reporting systems and to constantly evaluate them.Keywords: earthquake, earthquake anxiety, medical error, nurse

Background

Derived from the Latin word ‘mala‐praxis’, malpractice is used to describe professional errors involving an unreasonable lack of skill or care (Vera Carrasco 2022). Medical malpractice is defined as an act or situation that causes harm to a patient, from a delay in recovery to death, by going beyond the normal course of the disease. This occurs when healthcare professionals providing healthcare services fail to perform standard medical procedures negligently. This can manifest as the application of an incorrect treatment plan, the failure to provide treatment to the patient or a combination of the two (Turan et al. 2017; Vera Carrasco 2022; Yigitbas et al. 2016). Medical errors result in prolonged treatment, additional costs due to the treatment of new injuries or complications, moral damage, loss of morale and motivation of healthcare professionals, distrust towards healthcare professionals and dissatisfaction with the healthcare system in society (Menon et al. 2020).

Although the provision of health services is intended to be error‐free, the inherent complexity of health services and the necessity of providing health services with a crowded team may lead to medical errors, which are considered one of the most important indicators of patient safety (Kandemir and Yuksel 2020). A study conducted with Iranian nurses found that 54% of them had experienced a medication error (Fathizadeh et al. 2024). It has been found that 36.2% of nurses at a university hospital in Egypt have committed medical errors (Araby et al. 2018). In a study conducted with surgical nurses in Turkey, it was found that 62.6% of nurses encountered medical errors during their professional life (Karacabay et al. 2020). Although these rates are reported rates, it is thought that the actual rate is much higher due to the influence of stigma, fear of blame, fear of legal consequences and fear of punishment (Afolalu et al. 2021; Ta'an et al. 2020).

Disasters are defined as natural or human‐caused events that disrupt the daily lives of individuals and communities, resulting in physical, psychosocial and economic losses (Gizzi 2022; Sonmez 2022). Earthquakes represent one of the most significant natural disasters to affect our country, with the potential to cause individual and societal‐level mental health problems as a result of their devastating effects (Soqia et al. 2023). In the context of an earthquake, individuals are confronted with a natural disaster that poses a threat to their own lives and that of their relatives, with the potential for death or serious injury (Sonmez 2022). Irritability and outbursts of anger, extreme startle reactions, rapid distraction, difficulty in falling asleep and staying asleep and physical reactions that occur in environments reminiscent of an earthquake are among the symptoms of increased anxiety (Casalvera et al. 2024; World Health Organization 2022). It has been reported that individuals may experience these symptoms in the early stages. However, when anxiety symptoms persist and become excessive and prolonged, individuals may experience difficulty distinguishing between what is dangerous and what is not, and a state of hypervigilance may be observed. This may contribute to an increased tendency to make mistakes in a person's social and professional lives (Sonmez 2022).

On 6 February 2023, a 7.8 magnitude earthquake occurred in the province of Kahramanmaraş, Türkiye, followed by a second earthquake of 7.6 magnitude on the same day. These extremely destructive earthquakes resulted in massive loss of life and property (United Nations 2023). The disaster affected 11 provinces, causing more than 50,000 deaths and injuring tens of thousands of people. Due to its devastating impact and lasting consequences, it has been referred to as the ‘Disaster of the Century’ (Cifci and Kilinc 2024).

Nursing is a profession characterised by a high workload and exposure to multiple stressors, including emotional demands, complex patient care and shift‐based working conditions (Costa et al. 2020; Velana and Rinkenauer 2021). Such factors may increase psychological stress and cognitive load, which can negatively affect attention, decision‐making processes and safety‐related behaviours. In disaster contexts such as earthquakes, these existing stressors may be further intensified by personal loss, trauma exposure and disrupted working conditions. This may contribute to increased anxiety levels among nurses, which in turn may impair cognitive functioning and increase the likelihood of medical errors. Therefore, understanding the relationship between earthquake‐related anxiety and the tendency to make medical errors is critical for ensuring patient safety. Despite the growing body of literature on disaster‐related mental health outcomes and medical errors, there is limited evidence examining the relationship between earthquake‐related anxiety and nurses' tendency to make medical errors, particularly in post‐disaster settings. In light of these considerations, the research was designed to examine the correlation between nurses' earthquake anxiety levels and their tendency to engage in medical errors.

Methods

Objective of the Study and Research Design

The research was designed as a descriptive, cross‐sectional study to investigate the effect of nurses' earthquake anxiety levels on the tendency towards medical errors.

Execution Period of the Study

This research was conducted between 25 September 2023, and 20 February 2024.

Population and Sample

The population of the research consisted of 300 nurses working at the Adiyaman Training and Research Hospital, Turkey. The sample consisted of nurses who met the inclusion criteria. The minimum number of samples required to take part in the research was calculated with the sample calculation method and was calculated as 169 nurses with a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level. The sample in this research consisted of 172 nurses. A convenience sampling method was used, and all eligible nurses who met the inclusion criteria and voluntarily agreed to participate were invited to take part in the study.

Data Collection

Data were collected online through prepared forms. Before sending the survey form to individuals, they were informed about the scope and purpose of the research and who could participate via social networking sites (Instagram, WhatsApp and e‐mail), and then asked whether they voluntarily agreed to participate. A survey form was sent to nurses who voluntarily agreed to participate in the research. The survey form consists of the ‘Person Information Form’, ‘Earthquake Anxiety Scale’ (EAS) and ‘Medical Error Tendency Scale’ (METS).

Personal Information Form

This form consists of a total of 14 questions developed by researchers based on the literature containing sociodemographic characteristics and earthquake information (Bal and Akgul 2023).

EAS

The EAS was developed by Bal and Akgul (2023). The scale consists of a total of 34 items. There is no reverse coding in the scale items. The EAS was determined as a five‐point Likert‐type rating scale consisting of the options ‘strongly disagree—1, disagree—2, undecided—3, agree—4 and strongly agree—5’. The lowest score on the items is 34, and the highest score is 170. It can be said that as the score obtained from the scale increases, earthquake anxiety also increases. The earthquake anxiety level obtained by adding up the points given to the items in the scale is ‘Low’ if it is between 34 and 70 points, ‘Medium’ if it is between 71 and 120 points, and ‘High’ if it is between 121 and 170 points (Bal and Akgul 2023).

METS

The METS was developed by Ozata (2009) in 2010 to measure nurses' tendency towards medical errors. Validity and reliability of the scale were assessed (Ozata 2009). The scale consists of 49 items and 5 subscales. The scale is a 5‐point Likert‐type scale (1—never, 2—very rarely, 3—sometimes, 4—usually and 5—always) and includes 18 questions about medication and transfusion applications, 5 questions about falls, 12 questions about hospital infections, 9 questions about patient monitoring and material safety, 5 questions about communication and in total, the scale has 49 questions. A minimum of 49 and a maximum of 245 points can be obtained on the scale. A low score on the scale indicates a high tendency to medical error, and a high score indicates a low tendency to medical error (Yigitbas et al. 2016).

Ethics

This research was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Principles. The necessary ethics committee approval was received from the Non‐Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Gaziantep Islam Science and Technology University in Turkey (Decision Number: 324.32.03). In addition, before sending the survey form, nurses were informed about the purpose and scope of the research and were informed that they could withdraw from the research at any time. Participants who agreed to participate in the study voluntarily were included. The data collected was used only for scientific purposes, and confidentiality was emphasised.

Data Analysis

The SPSS 22.0 package programme was used to evaluate the data. Normality assumptions were at a satisfactory level, and it was decided whether the variables were normally distributed or not according to Skewness‐Kurtosis values. Accordingly, it was determined that the variables were suitable for normal distribution (Tabachnick et al. 2013). In this regard, number, percentage, mean and standard deviation values regarding sociodemographic characteristics were examined. The t‐test was used for the comparison of two independent groups, and the ANOVA test was applied for the comparison of more than two groups. When a significant difference was detected as a result of the ANOVA test, the post hoc multiple comparison Tukey test was used to determine which groups caused this difference. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the scales. The significance level was accepted as p < 0.05.

Results

The mean age of the 172 nurses who participated in the research is 36.06 ± 8.92, the number of children is 1.49 ± 1.23 and their experience (years) is 14.01 ± 9.47. Most of them are women (89.0%), married (69.8%), have a bachelor's degree (70.9%), work in mixed shifts (47.1%) and in internal units (37.2%). 77.9% of them have not experienced any disasters before, 84.3% of them experienced the earthquake in their own houses, 98.3% of them were not trapped under debris, 52.9% of them lost a relative and 83.1% of them were living in their houses after the earthquake (Table 1).

The EAS total mean score was found to be 110.49 ± 39.66. When looking at the EAS total mean scores, it was observed that the rate was highest in nurses working in intensive care (131.79 ± 28.86) and lowest in those who had previously experienced a disaster (100.82 ± 43.17) (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

The METS total mean score was found to be 225.74 ± 16.73. The total mean score of the METS's subscale of medication and transfusion application was found to be 85.93 ± 4.54 (18–90), hospital infections was found to be 55.77 ± 4.38 (12–60), patient monitoring and material safety was found to be 39.61 ± 4.75 (9–45), falls was found to be 22.35 ± 2.77 (5–25) and communication was found to be 22.53 ± 2.38 (5–25). According to these results, the lowest mean score among the METS's subscales was obtained from the patient monitoring and material safety (39.61 ± 4.75) (Table 1). Considering the METS mean scores, nurses working on the night shift had a lower score in the subscale of medication and transfusion applications than others (84.94 ± 4.65), nurses working in intensive care had a lower score in the subscale of hospital infections than others (54.38 ± 4.72), vocational high school of health graduates had a lower score in the subscale of patient monitoring and material safety than others (38.0 ± 5.19), nurses working in intensive care had a lower score in the subscale of falls than others (21.67 ± 2.76), nurses who experienced the earthquake in somebody's house had a lower score in the subscale of communication than others (21.75 ± 2.63) and nurses working in intensive care had a lower METS total mean score (221.54 ± 15.49) than all other participants. Additionally, nurses working on mixed (day–night) shifts have statistically significantly lower total mean score in the METS's subscale of patient monitoring and material safety than nurses working on day shifts (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

When the correlation between nurses' EAS and METS total mean scores was examined, it was determined that there was a statistically significant, weak and positive correlation between the EAS and the METS's subscale of hospital infections. It was determined that there was no statistically significant correlation between the EAS and METS total mean scores (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, nurses' earthquake‐related anxiety levels were found to be at a moderate level, while their tendency to make medical errors was low. In addition, no statistically significant relationship was found between earthquake anxiety and medical error tendency.

In the present study, the mean score on the EAS was found to be 110.49 ± 39.66, which placed the total mean in the ‘medium level’ range on the scale evaluation. In a study conducted by Canli and Yilmaz (2023) in Turkey, the total mean score of the EAS in individuals who had experienced an earthquake was found to be 129.08 ± 26.90, which was classified as a ‘high level’ (Canli and Yilmaz 2023). Furthermore, in this study, the total mean score of the EAS for those who witnessed the earthquake from a distance was calculated as 83.46 ± 37.06, and a statistically significant difference was found between the two groups. This situation demonstrates the impact of experiencing an earthquake in proximity on earthquake anxiety in both studies. The discrepancy in the EAS total mean scores between the two studies can be attributed to the fact that this study was conducted in an earthquake‐prone region, whereas the other study was conducted in a province of migration. It is plausible that people's anxiety levels may have increased with migration and the difficulties they encountered while leaving their place of residence.

In the present research, the METS total mean score was found to be 225.74 ± 16.73. In the research, it can be said that nurses have a low tendency to make mistakes. This result is similar to the findings of national research (Celik Durmus et al. 2022; Kiymaz and Koc 2018; Sabanciogullari et al. 2021). In the literature, in terms of the units in which nurses work, the METS total mean score and the total mean scores of the subscales of hospital infections, patient monitoring and material safety, and falls of nurses working in the surgical unit were found to be lower and statistically different (Celik Durmus et al. 2022; Karakurt and Uysal 2020). Although there was no statistically significant difference in this study, it was observed that the total mean scores of nurses working in intensive care units were lower than all other participants. In addition, the total mean scores of nurses working in intensive care units in the subscales of medication and infusion applications and patient monitoring and material safety were lower than those of nurses working in other units, and the total mean scores in the subscales of hospital infections and falls were lower than all nurses participating in the study. One of the data that draws attention in the study is that the EAS total mean score of intensive care nurses (131.79 ± 28.86) was higher than all participants, and their anxiety levels were found to be statistically significantly higher than those working in internal, surgical and emergency services (p < 0.05), while the METS total mean score (221.54 ± 15.49) was lower than all participants. However, no statistically significant relationship was found between EAS and METS total mean scores. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted descriptively and with caution.

In the study, the mean total scores of nurses with a vocational high school degree in health (38.0 ± 5.19) and a bachelor's degree (39.33 ± 5.04) were low on the patient monitoring and material safety subscales. In a similar study, a comparable mean score was reported (39.57 ± 6.71) (Celik Durmus et al. 2022). These findings suggest that nurses with different educational levels have a similar tendency regarding medical errors in patient monitoring and material safety. However, other studies have reported no significant difference between educational level and medical error tendency (Uysal and Karakurt 2020; Yildiz 2021). In contrast, some studies have indicated that higher education levels are associated with increased awareness and more positive attitudes towards medical errors (Aktan and Atay 2021). Furthermore, no statistically significant relationship was found between earthquake anxiety and educational level (Canli and Yilmaz 2023).

In the study, the mean total score for the communication subscale (21.75 ± 2.63) among those who lived in another house after the earthquake was low, indicating a high probability of nurses making medical errors in this subscale. In a study, the lowest mean total score was on the METS communication subscale (22.83 ± 3.69), which aligns with this research finding (Celik Durmus et al. 2022). In Kahriman and Ozturk (2016) study, when the mean scores of the METS and its subscale of communication of nurses who made medical errors were compared with those of nurses who did not make medical errors, there was a statistically significant difference; that is, nurses largely experienced communication insufficiency/communication problems, and nurses with communication insufficiency experienced communication problems. It was found that the probability of making medical errors was significantly higher among nurses who did so than among those who did not. In the same study, it was stated that nurses made medical errors due to doctors' orders not being understood, orders being taken verbally or by phone, and a lack of communication among nurses, other medical professionals and doctors on the team (Kahriman and Ozturk 2016). In the study conducted by Santas et al. (2020), the lack of communication between nurses and the high number of medical errors caused by communication problems support the findings of this study (Santas et al. 2020). As a result, a lack of communication may increase the possibility of nurses making medical errors (Sayilan and Boga 2018). Based on this study and the literature, it is recommended to improve the communication skills of nurses who are most in contact with individuals receiving health care. In addition, it may be recommended to raise nurses' awareness of language problems, lack of empathy, negative body language and similar issues that may interfere with communication. This finding may be explained by displacement‐related stress and disruption of social support systems following the earthquake, which may negatively affect interpersonal communication and teamwork among nurses.

In some studies, the incidence and rate of medical errors in hospital infections ranked second only to medication errors (Isci and Altuntas 2019; Ta'an et al. 2020). In our research, unlike these studies, we found that as earthquake anxiety increases, the mean total score of the METS hospital infections subscale also increases. Given that higher METS scores indicate a lower tendency to make medical errors, this finding suggests a lower error tendency in this subscale. When the relationship between nurses' EAS and METS total mean scores was examined, a statistically significant, weak positive correlation was found between EAS and the METS subscale on hospital infections. Similar to our research, Cerit et al.'s study found a statistically significant, negative and weak correlation between nurses' work‐related tension levels and the METS's subscales of hospital infections and medication and transfusion applications. A statistically significant, negative and very weak correlation was found between the other subscales (Cerit et al. 2022). Given the weak nature of this association, this finding should be interpreted with caution. In line with this, it is plausible that poor hygiene conditions following the earthquake may have increased sensitivity to the development and spread of infections.

Conclusions and Recommendations

Unexpected earthquakes not only shake the earth but also deeply affect institutions, social structures, individuals and professional practices. In this study, nurses were moderately affected by the earthquakes; however, no significant relationship was observed between nurses' earthquake anxiety levels and their tendency to commit medical errors. Despite experiencing anxiety, nurses were able to fulfil their professional roles and responsibilities effectively, even in the post‐earthquake period. It is crucial to identify the individual and systemic factors that may contribute to medical errors among nurses affected by such large‐scale disasters.

Limitations of the Study

The most important limitation of this research is that it was conducted in a single centre located in one of the provinces most severely affected by the earthquake. Therefore, the results cannot be generalised to all healthcare professionals in Türkiye. In addition, data collection began 7 months after the earthquake, which may have influenced the participants' recall and emotional responses. Furthermore, other psychological and occupational factors, such as burnout, stress or workload intensity, could have affected nurses' anxiety levels or their tendency to make medical errors, but these variables were not measured in this study. Furthermore, the lack of assessment of the duration of active work in the hospital following the earthquake may have influenced anxiety levels and error rates, constituting one of the study's limitations. Future research should consider including such variables to better understand the multifactorial nature of anxiety and error tendencies among healthcare workers in post‐disaster contexts.
