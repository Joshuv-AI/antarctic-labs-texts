Workplace difficulties in early-stage relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis with low physical disability
Authors: Ángel Pérez-Sempere, Elena García-Arcelay, Jacobo Caruncho, Antonio Candeliere-Merlicco, Aida Orviz, Jesús Martín-Martínez, Raquel Piñar Morales, Elena Álvarez-Rodríguez, Eva M Pacheco, Laura Borrega, Ignacio Casanova, Ana B Caminero, José L Sánchez-Menoyo, Montserrat Gómez-Gutiérrez, Olga Carmona, Carmen Calles, Miguel A Hernández, Pablo López-Muñoz, Inés González-Suárez, Héloise Joly, Jorge Maurino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated perceived workplace difficulties in 128 individuals with early-stage relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis and low physical disability. Significant workplace challenges were reported by 40.6% of participants. External barriers, including workplace inflexibility and professional-domestic life imbalance (median 23-item multiple sclerosis (MS) Work Difficulties Questionnaire-MSWDQ-23 score: 31.3, interquartile range 6.3–56.3), represented the highest burden. Women reported higher difficulty scores than men (mean MSWDQ-23 total scores: 29.9 vs. 21.2, p = 0.010). Multivariable analysis identified fatigue (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.17–1.61) and anxiety (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.05–1.38) as the only independent predictors of significant workplace difficulty (p < 0.001). These findings demonstrate a significant dissociation between physical disability and functional outcomes. Vocational vulnerability is driven by non-motor symptoms rather than overt neurological impairment. Proactive clinical screening for these factors is essential to mitigate work instability and preserve long-term professional trajectories.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) predominantly affects young adults during their peak productive working years.1–4 In early-stage relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS), significant workplace difficulties and reduced employability often emerge despite low physical disability. 3 Employed individuals with RRMS exhibit higher rates of absenteeism and presenteeism (reduced on-the-job productivity) than healthy controls, which diminishes health-related quality of life.3–6

Workplace barriers are multifactorial, encompassing demanding job characteristics, inadequate workload management and insufficient support regarding disability rights.1,7,8 Consequently, adjustments such as flexible scheduling and workload modifications are frequently essential for job retention.7–9 While clinical assessments often focus on overt physical impairment, “invisible” symptoms, specifically fatigue, cognitive impairment, and anxiety, are primary determinants of work-related dysfunction.9–11 This study aimed to assess perceived workplace difficulties and identify clinical predictors in people with early-stage RRMS and low physical disability.

Methods

We conducted a multicenter, observational, cross-sectional study across 16 neuroimmunology units in Spain. Between 29 October 2024 and 4 April 2025, we consecutively enrolled adults with RRMS during routine follow-up visits. Inclusion required a confirmed RRMS diagnosis within the previous 3 years, current use of disease-modifying therapy, and an Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score between 0.0 and 5.0. We excluded candidates with insufficient language proficiency to provide informed consent or complete the required assessments. The Research Ethics Committee of Euskadi (CEIm-E, Spain) approved the study protocol. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Study measures

Perceived workplace challenges were evaluated using the 23-item MS Work Difficulties Questionnaire (MSWDQ-23). 12 This validated instrument assesses three distinct domains: physical (motor function and fatigue), psychological/cognitive (memory, concentration, and emotional impact), and external barriers (financial concerns, workplace support, and the professional-domestic life balance). Scores are calculated as percentages (0–100%), with higher values representing a greater perceived burden. For individuals not currently employed at the time of assessment, the questionnaire evaluates perceived barriers to employment maintenance based on their most recent professional experience. 12

We quantified overall symptom severity with the SymptomMScreen and assessed cognitive processing speed via the Symbol Digit Modalities Test. Psychological distress was measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, the Beck Hopelessness Scale, and the Decision Regret Scale. Fatigue was captured with the five-item Modified Fatigue Impact Scale, while health-related quality of life was assessed through the MS Impact Scale (MSIS-29). Finally, we utilized the MS Knowledge Assessment Scale for disease literacy, the Mishel Uncertainty of Illness Scale for trajectory uncertainty, and the Decisional Conflict Scale (SURE) to evaluate decisional conflict.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics, reported as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. Differences between patients with and without significant workplace difficulties were compared using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney tests, depending on data distribution. In accordance with established clinical thresholds, we defined significant workplace difficulty as a total score ≥32, a cutoff previously associated with an elevated risk of job loss. 13 Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent clinical predictors of significant workplace difficulties, with these difficulties serving as the dependent variable.

Results

Among 128 evaluable individuals, 40.6% (n = 52) reported significant workplace difficulties (Table 1). The median MSWDQ-23 total score was 26.2 (IQR 7.7–42.4). The highest perceived burden was attributed to external barriers (median score: 31.3, IQR 6.3–56.3), followed by psychological/cognitive (median score: 25.0, IQR 6.8–40.9) and physical (median score: 18.8, IQR 4.7–34.4) barriers. Women reported significantly higher total difficulty scores than men (29.9 vs. 21.2, p = 0.010), driven primarily by elevated psychological and external barrier scores. People with significant difficulties exhibited higher levels of fatigue, anxiety, depression, hopelessness, and illness-related uncertainty (Table 1). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, fatigue (OR = 1.372, 95%CI 1.170–1.608; p < 0.0001) and anxiety (OR = 1.205, 1.054–1.378: 95%CI p < 0.0001) remained the only factors independently associated with significant total workplace difficulties, as defined by the MSWDQ-23 total score.

Discussion

The management of early-stage RRMS increasingly prioritizes the preservation of long-term social and professional outcomes.1,4,6,8 Our study identified a critical “disability paradox": while clinical measures like the EDSS showed minimal impairment, over 40% of patients reported significant workplace difficulties. The finding that overt EDSS-defined physical disability underestimates early vocational vulnerability confirms that traditional clinical outcome measures often fail to capture aspects of health that matter most to individuals living with MS. 4 As noted by the Global PROMS Initiative, standard measures lack the sensitivity to detect “invisible” symptoms such as fatigue and psychological distress, which are primary drivers of daily functional impairment. 14 In this context, the MSWDQ-23 serves as a valuable tool to document subtle worsening in domains not captured by legacy measures, shifting the focus toward identifying clinically meaningful disease states that align with real-world patient needs.4,12,13

A key finding of our study was that external barriers constituted the highest perceived burden. While physical and psychological barriers focus on “internal” symptoms, external barriers measure how MS interferes with a patient's life roles and the support they receive from their surroundings. 13 These barriers refer to the environmental, social, and logistical factors, such as workplace inflexibility, lack of support, and the challenge of balancing professional and domestic duties, that impede employment maintenance. 7 Our results align with recent qualitative evidence highlighting that the workplace climate and the quality of relationships between individuals with MS and their colleagues are fundamental to job retention. 8 A supportive environment and empathetic leadership not only facilitate the disclosure of the diagnosis but also mitigate the fear of professional discrimination.

The multivariable analysis identifies fatigue and anxiety as the primary drivers of vocational challenges, aligning with evidence that fatigue is a critical determinant of employment status even in low-disability cohorts.9,10 Furthermore, we observed a significant gender disparity, with women reporting higher difficulty scores in psychological and external domains. Women with MS are at higher risk of “work drift,” shifting from full-time to part-time employment due to the cumulative impact of fatigue, mood disturbances, and domestic obligations.11,15

Our study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of temporal relationships or causal inference between clinical factors and workplace difficulties. Second, the cohort consisted primarily of people with early-stage RRMS and minimal physical disability (median EDSS 1.5), which restricts the generalizability of these findings to the broader MS population or those with progressive phenotypes. Third, the recruitment from specialized neuroimmunology units in Spain may introduce selection bias, as these participants may not fully reflect the sociodemographic or occupational characteristics of other regions or healthcare systems. Finally, the use of self-reported measures may introduce recall bias and does not account for external macroeconomic factors.

Conclusion

Workplace difficulties affect over 40% of people with early RRMS and are driven by “invisible” symptoms rather than overt physical impairment. Early screening with the MSWDQ-23 is a complementary strategy to initiate timely interventions and preserve professional trajectories.
