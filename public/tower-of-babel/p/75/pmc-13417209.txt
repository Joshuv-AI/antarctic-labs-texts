Tunable Thermotropic Liquid Crystals Using Amphiphilic Sequence-Defined Macromolecules
Authors: Irene De Franceschi, Valentin P. Beyer, Steve Huband, Nezha Badi, Sébastien Perrier, Filip E. Du Prez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liquid crystals (LCs) are a class of materials that combine molecular order and fluidity, making them crucial for technologies from displays to sensors. However, polymeric LC materials typically lack molecular precision, limiting systematic control over their phase behavior. Here, we report a modular synthetic strategy to prepare amphiphilic columnar liquid crystals based on hydrophobic di- and trialkylated galloyl cores attached to a sequence-defined polar oligomer grown by a thiolactone-based iterative protocol. This approach enables independent variation of (i) polar monomer type (e.g., hydroxyethyl acrylate and N,N-dimethylacrylamide), (ii) oligomer length (from 1-mer to tetramer), and (iii) core topology (two or three C18 chains). Differential scanning calorimetry, polarized optical microscopy, and X-ray scattering show that the number of hydrophobic C18 chains on the aromatic core is the dominant parameter governing mesophase stability. Derivatives with three C18 chains exhibit higher melting and isotropization temperatures and more pronounced transitions from lamellar crystals to hexagonally packed columnar mesophases than their two C18 analogues. The chemistry and length of the sequence-defined polar block further tune crystallization and mesophase behavior, i.e. hydrogen-bonding heads increase thermal stability, whereas bulkier PEG-like heads reduce crystallinity and can destabilize mesophases. Increasing oligomer length reduces crystallization enthalpies and can introduce competing liquid-crystalline states. These results demonstrate that the liquid crystalline behavior can be encoded at the molecular level, providing a basis for designing responsive and sequence-programmed LC materials.

Introduction

Liquid crystals (LCs) are materials that exist between the solid and liquid states, retaining even in the fluid state a degree of anisotropic order and forming intermediate mesophases. This combination of molecular order and mobility makes LCs highly valuable in materials science, especially for the design of responsive , and self-organizing materials. , When the transition from a crystalline solid to a LC phase is driven by temperature, the material is classified as a thermotropic liquid crystal. Such materials are widely used in optical displays, organic electronics, sensor coatings, photovoltaics, and other advanced technologies. ,

Among them, columnar liquid crystals (CLCs) are distinguished by rigid aromatic cores that promote π-π stacking and columnar order, while peripheral flexible chains adjust intermolecular spacing. − These columnar assemblies can further organize into hexagonal or rectangular arrays, providing strong anisotropy in the liquid state. , The introduction of polar or hydrogen-bonding groups can influence the packing of the columnar assemblies by promoting microphase segregation or driving structural responses to external stimuli. , This was extensively discussed in studies by Tschierske, Serrano, , Laschat and coworkers, which demonstrate how competing intermolecular interactions govern LC symmetry and phase transition. In such materials, nanosegregation between chemically incompatible fragments (e.g., aromatic cores vs aliphatic or polar chains) is a key driving force for mesophase organization, analogous to behavior observed in block copolymer self-assembly. Incorporating both hydrophobic and polar or ionic segments can further promote segregation and allow access to more complex morphologies, including for example bicontinuous or hierarchical nanostructures reported in advanced LCs.

A widely studied structural motif in CLCs is based on symmetrically trisubstituted phenolic cores such as gallic acid (3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid) and related derivatives. − Upon etherification or esterification, these molecules bear three regularly arranged side chains and typically self-assemble into columnar mesophases. Although often historically described as “discotic”, such mesogens are more rigorously classified as polycatenar or taper-shaped systems, lacking a strictly planar disk like core but still forming columnar stacks through cooperative packing. Percec and coworkers , extensively investigated gallic acid esters with long alkyl substituents for creating Janus dendrimers, demonstrating their ability to form a broad range of discotic mesophases. − Work by Marchi-Artzner and coworkers further showed that related structures can display lyotropic LC behavior and quantum dot-like properties, enabling their use as nanostructured carriers in drug delivery. ,

Complementary contributions from Swager highlighted the role of π/conjugated motifs in stabilizing columnar assemblies with electronic functionality, while Lee and coworkers demonstrated how subtle variations in side-chain architecture can tune columnar packing and long-range order. Furthermore, scattering studies by Gruner explained how nanoscale segregation and periodicity in such systems can be quantitatively controlled.

Beyond small-molecule LCs, many LC materials are polymer-based. However, LC polymers (LCPs) commonly lack molecular precision and often show broad thermal transitions and poor batch-to-batch reproducibility. Most rely on statistical copolymerization or controlled/living polymerization, which hampers systematic structure–property studies due to the inherent dispersity and incomplete control over monomer sequence and composition. Early studies using stepwise synthesis of monodisperse LC polyethers demonstrated that mesophase stability and thermal transitions are strongly influenced by chain length. More recently, bent-core mesogenic oligomers (also called banana-shaped LCs) prepared by iterative exponential growth strategy (IEG) showed that chain length can dictate supramolecular curvature and mesophase organization. Despite these advances, precise control of LC behavior, particularly the ability to program narrow temperature windows and phase behavior, remains challenging.

Sequence-defined macromolecules (SDMs) offer a way to address these limitations. When attached to aromatic cores, they provide absolute control over length, monomer composition, and functional group placement, enabling the introduction of side chains with well-defined dipoles and hydrogen-bonding interactions. The thiolactone-based iterative protocol developed in our group for SDMs is compatible with alkylated gallates and enables rapid and scalable preparation of such amphiphilic LC structures. ,

In this work, we combine aromatic cores with sequence-defined polar oligomers to systematically investigate how monomer type, length and core topology (two versus three C18 tails) influence mesophase formation. By framing these structures within the broader context of columnar liquid crystal and soft materials, this modular approach provides a set of molecularly precise amphiphiles that reveal how LC behavior can be programmed through controlled variations in hydrophobic packing, polarity, and chain architecture.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Amphiphilic Sequence-Defined Liquid Crystals

The amphiphilic molecules prepared in this work consist of a hydrophobic multiarm aromatic core and a sequence-defined polar head. For the hydrophobic part, methyl gallate (i.e., methyl 3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoate) and protocatechuic methyl ester (i.e., methyl 3,4-dihydroxybenzoate) were selected as phenolic precursors. After alkylation with long hydrophobic C18 chains under standard Williamson conditions (i.e., potassium carbonate, 1-bromooctadecane, DMF, reflux), the corresponding tri- (3C18) and dialkylated (2C18) aromatic cores were obtained in quantitative yields (Figure and S1–S2). These structures exhibit good thermal stability and their polarity allows easy purification of the subsequently attached SDMs, as previously reported.

Schematic representation of the platform used in this work: A) Hydrophobic support starting from methyl gallate (left) or protocatechuic acid (right) after alkylation using K2CO3 and C18H37Br in DMF under reflux (i), hydrolysis in 1 M KOH/EtOH for 3 h (ii) and amidation using NH3.Cl-Tla with EDC.Cl, DMAP and NEt3 (iii) in CHCl3; B) Thiolactone protocol performed on hydrophobic support 3C18-Tla or 2C18-Tla via (i) ring-opening and thiol–ene reaction with ethanolamine (2 equiv) and acrylate or acrylamide (3 equiv) together with dithiothreitol (DTT, 1 equiv) and DMAP (0.1 equiv) and (ii) chain extension using CDI (2 equiv) to activate the alcohol, followed by substitution with NH3.Cl-Tla and NEt3 in CHCl3 (iii); C) Hydrophilic monomers used in step (i) of the cycle in the thiol–ene reaction, namely acrylates DMAEA, HEA, MEEEA and AA protected by ethylene vinyl protecting group, and DMA acrylamide.

The polar headgroups were introduced by growing short sequence-defined oligomers (from 1-mer to 4-mer) using a thiolactone-based iterative protocol. Introduction of the first thiolactone (Tla) moiety (Figure A) was achieved by hydrolysis of the methyl ester (Figure S3–S4), followed by amidation using homocysteine thiolactone hydrochloride (NH3·Cl-Tla) in the presence of ethyl diisopropylcarbodiimide chloride (EDC·Cl), dimethylaminopiperidine (DMAP) and triethylamine (NEt3) (Figure S5–S6). Oligomer growth followed an iterative two-step cycle consisting of (i) Tla ring opening with ethanolamine and one-pot thiol–ene addition of an acrylate or acrylamide, and (ii) reinstallation of the thiolactone unit by activation of the terminal alcohol with carbonyldiimidazole (CDI) and subsequent coupling with NH3.Cl-Tla (Figure B).

In order to study the influence of the polar head, several Michael acceptors were used, including dimethylaminoethyl acrylate (DMAEA), dimethylacrylamide (DMA), hydroxyethyl acrylate (HEA), 2-(2-(2-methoxyethoxy)­ethoxy)­ethyl acrylate (MEEEA) and acrylic acid (AA) (Figure C, S7–S10). In the following, compounds are named using the format xC18-(M)n, where x is the number of C18 chains on the aromatic core (i.e., 2C18 or 3C18), M is the Michael acceptor incorporated in the sequence-defined segment, and n is the oligomer length (1 to 4). The thiol–ene reactions performed with the acrylates proved to be fast and did not generate any side products, as previously reported. With DMA, incomplete conversion and disulfide formation were initially observed (Figure S11), but the addition of dithiothreitol (DTT) as a reducing agent and DMAP as a basic catalyst yielded complete reaction without side products in less than 1 h (Figure S12–S13). Because free acrylic acid tends to dimerize, it was introduced as a protected ethyl vinyl ether acrylate derivative and deprotected with a mild TFA concentration (5% in DCM) after oligomer growth (Figure S14–S17).

In our previous reports, reinstallation of the thiolactone moiety was achieved through either an isocyanate-functionalized thiolactone, which generates a urethane backbone or via a carboxylic acid-containing thiolactone, yielding an ester backbone. Here, we selected the urethane backbone for its higher thermal resistance compared to the ester and because it introduces an additional hydrogen-bonding interaction. To avoid the isocyanate route (which requires triphosgene), we employed a new method to form the carbamate bonds: the hydroxyl moiety was activated with CDI (Figure S18), followed by the introduction of NH3·Cl-Tla in the presence of triethylamine (NEt3), as shown in Figure S19­(ii). Compounds obtained after the reintroduction of the thiolactone moiety follow the same nomenclature as the previous series, but with n = 1.5, 2.5, or 3.5 (e.g., xC18-(M)1.5). The fractional value indicates that the compound corresponds to the intermediate obtained after thiolactone reinstallation, prior to the introduction of a new acrylate monomer.

Thermal Characterization of Amphiphilic Sequence-Defined Oligomers

The thermal properties of the synthesized structures were investigated by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) to identify the presence of crystalline and liquid-crystalline phases. These macromolecules typically show a characteristic pattern consisting of a first high-enthalpy melting transition associated with crystalline domains, followed by a smaller endothermic peak at higher temperatures, corresponding to isotropization, confirming the presence of a liquid-crystalline phase.

As a starting point, the hydrophobic 3C18 and 2C18 methyl ester cores (3C18 or 2C18-MeO) without polar headgroups were analyzed (Figure S20 and S21). Both displayed melting transitions but no isotropization peak, indicating the absence of a liquid crystalline phase. On the other hand, after introducing a thiolactone moiety and installing a polar headgroup, the thermal behavior changed markedly (Figure S22-S26). For example, 2C18-(DMAEA)1 is semicrystalline at room temperature and, upon heating, exhibits a main endothermic melting transition, followed by a second, smaller transition at higher temperature attributed to isotropization (Figure A and Figure S26B). This demonstrates that the material forms a mesophase between the melting (T m) and isotropization temperatures (T i). The lower enthalpy of isotropization compared to melting is consistent with a transition from an ordered fluid mesophase to a fully isotropic liquid. Interestingly, during cooling, the liquid-crystalline phase reforms, showing a thermal hysteresis of approximately 5 °C, which is slightly larger than typically observed for simple isotropic transition. This suggests the involvement of kinetic effects or structural reorganization during phase formation at 10 °C/min in DSC, followed by crystallization at T c upon further cooling.

Heating and cooling scans were conducted at 10 °C min–1, after verifying on a representative sample that the transition temperatures showed negligible dependence on the heating rate. Additional measurements performed at lower rates (5 and 2 °C min–1) indeed demonstrated that T c and T m values remained essentially unchanged (Figure S27–S28). At slower heating rates, the isotropization transition becomes progressively less apparent due to its low enthalpy, which is spread over a broader time interval, reducing peak intensity. For this reason, the 10 °C min–1 heating rate was selected for the main data set, ensuring optimal detection of phase transitions.

To further assess the thermal stability of the materials under the experimental conditions used in DSC and SAXS, particular attention was paid to the reproducibility of the structural response upon thermal cycling. The SAXS profiles collected during heating and cooling were found to be fully reversible, with no evidence of irreversible changes such as loss of intensity, peak broadening or disappearance of characteristic features. In particular, the original crystalline phases were consistently recovered upon cooling. Although long-term thermal degradation or stability under prolonged exposure was not specifically investigated in this study, all investigated materials contain amide, urethane and ester functionalities, which are generally known to exhibit good thermal stability over moderate time scales. This structural integrity upon thermal cycling is confirmed by repeated DSC heating and cooling cycles on representative samples (e.g., 3C18-(DMAEA)1), showing no significant changes in transition temperatures or peak shapes.

Polarized optical microscopy (POM) was further used to confirm the existence of LC behavior. Interestingly, below T m, 2C18-(DMAEA)1 showed irregular crystalline textures (Figure S29). Upon heating into the LC range (i.e., between T m and T i), birefringent domains consistent with a smectic-type arrangement appeared (Figure B, top).

At temperatures close to T i, the 2C18-(DMAEA)1-based LC displayed birefringent domains (Figure B bottom and S30), before melting completely to an optically isotropic liquid. These results are in agreement with the DSC data and indicate well-defined LC organization. This phenomenon can occur due to pretransition effects where fluctuations in local order can be large and some domains could have high local ordering, giving strong birefringence and color variations under a polarized microscope.

A comparison of all the amphiphilic macromolecules bearing DMA, MEEEA, AA, HEA, DMAEA headgroups on 2C18 and 3C18 cores shows that hydrophobic packing plays a dominant role in determining the thermal behavior (Figure S22–S26, Table ). Increasing the number of C18 chains from two to three results in generally higher T m and T c values, as well as larger melting enthalpies (ΔHm), reflecting more efficient packing and increased stability of the crystalline phase. In contrast, the stability of the liquid crystalline mesophase is more directly reflected by the isotropization temperature (T i), which is systematically higher for the 3C18 series (typically 130–140 °C). This indicates that the three-arm architecture not only enhances crystalline order but also promotes a more thermally stable mesophase. The increased symmetry and higher density of alkyl chains in the 3C18 cores contribute to van der Waals interactions and microsegregation, thereby stabilizing the LC organization over a wider temperature range. These findings highlight qualitative structure–property trends rather than predictive rules, offering an exploratory basis for identifying molecular factors that govern liquid crystalline behavior.

As a support to these calorimetric observations and in order to gain molecular-level insight into the phase behavior, temperature-dependent ATR-FTIR measurements were performed. In fact, the evolution of the amide band could provide direct evidence for the partial retention of molecular order above T m due to H-bonding, supporting the presence of a liquid-crystalline phase.

Because DSC alone does not isolate a single contribution from polarity, hydrogen bonding, and steric effects, temperature-dependent ATR-FTIR measurements were carried out on a representative sample (3C18-(DMAEA)1) to illustrate the evolution of hydrogen bonding with temperature, both below and above T m (Figure S31). Below T m, the Amide I band is intense (∼1630 cm–1), indicative of strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding in the crystalline phase. Immediately above T m, an expected sudden decrease in intensity is observed, reflecting the partial disruption of this network. In the liquid-crystalline phase, the Amide I band progressively shifts to higher wavenumbers (up to ∼ 1680 cm–1) and decreases in intensity, consistent with weaker, more dynamic hydrogen bonding.

The nature of the polar head affects thermal properties, which can be observed specifically in the case of HEA and AA, both possessing hydroxyl and carboxylic acid groups, respectively, capable of hydrogen bonding and therefore leading to higher T m and T i values. For instance, 3C18-(HEA)1 exhibits the highest T i at 147.0 °C. In the case of DMAEA, despite being less capable of hydrogen bonding, specifically the 2-arm variant shows a ΔH i of 3.6 kJ/mol higher than the 3-arm, indicating a more enthalpically demanding isotropization process compared to the corresponding 3-arm analogue. On the other hand, the 2C18-(MEEEA)1 and 3C18-(MEEEA)1 suggest that the ethylene glycol units disrupt regular packing, hindering crystallization and mesophase formation.

Despite the differences in the polar head nature, ordered structures in the mesophase require the presence of both a hydrophobic and polar head part, if compared to the control 3C18 and 2C18 esters. The LC behavior likely arises from (i) efficient packing and strong interactions of the alkyl chains, (ii) π–π stacking of the aromatic core supported by the proximal amide functionality, and (iii) hydrophobic/polar phase separation.

While the results discussed above focus on monomeric structures and highlight the role of the hydrophobic core and polar headgroup, the modular synthetic strategy employed here also enables a systematic investigation of oligomer length as an additional molecular parameter to explore thermal and mesomorphic properties. Indeed, the iterative synthesis described above allowed us to investigate the effect of oligomer length on the resulting material properties. Subsequently, four tetramers were selected as model structures for studying the effect of the oligomer length (vide infra): i.e., 3C18-(DMA)4, 2C18-(DMA)4, 3C18-(MEEEA)4, and 2C18-(MEEEA)4 (Figure ). Tetramers were selected as a representative upper limit of oligomer length, allowing us to capture the cumulative effect of chain extension on phase behavior without introducing excessive synthetic complexity. While DMA provides compact, polar headgroups expected to favor ordered phases, MEEEA introduces longer PEG-like segments expected to increase disorder.

To this end, we examined a set of four oligomers: 3C18-(DMA)4, 2C18-(DMA)4, 3C18-(MEEEA)4, and 2C18-(MEEEA)4 (Figure A–D). We initially focused on the DMA-containing series, whose side-chains are significantly more compact than MEEEA analogues, and therefore expected to promote tighter packing and more stable ordered phases.

The oligomer samples were again analyzed by DSC (heat–cool cycles at 10 °C/min) to assess trends in crystallization behavior (Figure ). The 3C18-(DMA)n series was first investigated to evaluate the effect of the number of repeating units (n = 1 to 4). While the crystallization temperature (T c) decreases systematically with increasing oligomer length, no clear trend was observed for the melting temperature (T m), suggesting that the reduced T c primarily reflects kinetic hindrance to crystallization rather than a decrease in thermodynamic stability of the crystalline phase (Figure B and C). This trend may be attributed to increased molecular flexibility and reduced crystal order for higher oligomer lengths. A similar trend was observed in the crystallization enthalpy (ΔH c), which decreased as the hydrophilic oligomer block grew longer, consistent with lower packing efficiency. Additionally, the isotropization temperature became less clearly detectable in longer oligomers, suggesting either a shift to higher temperatures or a diminished enthalpic contribution that obscures the appearance of a second thermal transition.

In contrast, the PEG-like side chains in the MEEEA series are expected to enhance phase separation between blocks due to their increased polarity and flexibility, while also potentially disrupting mesophase order. To verify this behavior, 3C18-(MEEEA)n oligomers with n = 1–4 (Figure D) were analyzed under the same DSC conditions. Interestingly, the sequence-defined MEEEA oligomers exhibited trends similar to the DMA series, with decreasing T c (Figure E) and ΔH c (Figure F) as the chain length increased, again indicative of increased disorder in the crystalline domains. Upon heating, multiple melting peaks were observed, with peak T m values increasing with chain length. This may reflect slow reorganization of imperfect crystals during the heating ramp, where defect-rich crystals gradually anneal into more ordered structures. Alternatively, these multiple peaks could arise from polycrystallinity within the sample.

To differentiate between these two hypotheses, we performed modulated DSC analysis. Interestingly, even at lower heating rates, only partial annealing was observed, suggesting limited crystal perfectioning (Figure S36–S38). Accordingly, the presence of multiple crystalline domains or polymorphic forms is proposed as the most likely explanation.

XRD Studies to Determine Phase Transitions

To understand how these amphiphilic oligomers pack at the nanoscale level and to rationalize the trends obtained by DSC, we analyzed their self-assembly using azimuthally averaged small-and wide-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS/WAXS). Measurements were carried out at room temperature with heating and cooling ramps at 0.5 °C min–1, covering the entire range from crystalline (C) to liquid crystalline to isotropic (I) states.

We first examined 2C18-(DMAEA)1, selected as a reference because it displays sharp and well-defined thermal transitions in DSC, and a clear LC texture in POM. At room temperature, the SAXS profile is characteristic of a highly ordered crystalline lamellar phase, with a primary reflection at q 0 = 0.09 Å–1, followed by higher-order peaks consistent with a one-dimensional lamellar lattice (i.e., q, 2q, 3q, 4q). Using the formula d = 2π/q 0, the lamellar spacing is d ≈ 7.0 nm. This crystalline lamellar organization likely results from interdigitation of alkyl chains driven by van der Waals interactions, combined with π-π stacking of the aromatic cores.

The polar DMAEA headgroup is expected to position in the polar sublayer of the lamellae, segregated from the alkyl-rich region (Figure A). The lamellar order persists up to the melting transition, after which the SAXS pattern changes within a narrow temperature window to the first reflections of a hexagonal columnar LC phase, identified by the characteristic peak positions (i.e., q, √3q, 2q, √7q). The lattice parameter “a” (i.e., center-to-center distance between neighboring columns) can be calculated using the formula a = 4π/√3q 0, indicating close-packed cylindrical domains in the mesophase.

A) Molecular structure of 2C18-(DMAEA)1 with schematic behavior at T < T m, T m < T < Ti, T > Ti; B) Stacked SAXS data for 2C18-(DMAEA)1 from 60 to 130 °C (heating at 0.5 °C min–1): crystalline lamellae (blue) transform into hexagonal columnar mesophase (green), followed by an isotropic liquid (red); C) WAXS stacked below T m (40 °C) and above Ti (120 °C); two impurity peaks appear at around 2 Å–1.

Formation of this phase can be attributed to increased chain mobility after melting, which enables reorganization driven by microphase separation and interactions among the polar headgroups. Upon further heating, isotropization occurs, evidenced by the disappearance of the hexagonal lattice reflections in SAXS due to the breaking of low interactions existing between the cylinders (Figure B).

To further support the proposed columnar organization, an approximate effective packing analysis was performed for representative hexagonal columnar LC phases. The hexagonal lattice parameter a was calculated from the primary SAXS reflection using a = 4π/(√3q10), and the corresponding column cross-sectional area was estimated assuming hexagonal packing using Acol = (√3/2)­a. Because the WAXS reflection disappears in the LC state, experimentally resolved intracolumnar periodicity is not available. Therefore, the effective molecular occupancy Zeff was estimated semiquantitatively using reasonable density assumptions and a plausible liquid-like molecular slice thickness. The resulting values support physically reasonable columnar packing (Table S1). WAXS provides complementary information: in the crystalline phase, 2C18-(DMAEA)1 shows a single peak at q = 1.50 Å–1 (Figure C), whereas no sharp WAXS peaks are observed in the LC or isotropic states, consistent with a loss of long-range 3D crystalline order upon melting. The transition temperatures identified by SAXS agree with DSC, with a slight shift that can be attributed to the different experimental conditions (open heating stage and/or different heating rates; 0.5 °C min–1 in SAXS versus 10 °C min–1 in DSC). Upon cooling, the I → LC → C sequence is recovered, and the recrystallized lamellar reflections become sharper and more intense, consistent with thermal annealing, leading to improved structural coherence.

Different polar heads are expected to induce only minor changes in the SAXS profiles, mainly through shifts in transition temperatures and small variations in lamellar spacing d and lattice constant a. Consistent with this expectation, 2C18-(HEA)1 behaves similarly to 2C18-(DMAEA)1, showing a lamellar crystalline phase at low temperature and a transition to a hexagonal columnar LC phase upon heating, with only minor changes in q, T m, and T i. The full data set is reported in the Supporting Information (Figure S39–S57).

We then compared 2C18 and 3C18 derivatives (Figure A) to probe the role of core topology in supramolecular order. Already at first inspection, the additional hydrophobic chains strongly affect the SAXS patterns. For 2C18-(DMAEA)1 (Figure C), the reflections are comparatively broad in all phases, and identifying higher-order peaks (e.g., 2q 0 in the lamellar phase and √3q 0 in the hexagonal phase) is challenging due to the weaker signal. This indicates limited long-range order in the crystalline phase, which carries over into the LC state. In contrast, 3C18-(DMAEA)1 (Figure D) and 3C18-(HEA)1 (Figure S39) display much sharper lamellar reflections, indicating a more ordered crystalline phase and consistent with the higher T m measured by DSC. Upon heating, these materials undergo a well-defined C → LC transition into a stable hexagonal columnar phase that persists to at least 125 °C (Figure D). Because T i is not reached within this interval, a second ramp to 150 °C was performed for the 3C18 series, revealing a gradual isotropization near the upper temperature limit. Although broad scattering features remain visible at the highest temperatures, complementary POM observations confirm isotropization. These residual signals are therefore attributed to short-range molecular correlations and local density fluctuations in the isotropic melt rather than to the persistence of long-range liquid-crystalline order. The narrower peak widths and the higher transition temperatures of the 3C18 derivatives indicate that the extra alkyl chain strengthens van der Waals interactions and promotes a more rigid, symmetric supramolecular network, thereby increasing mesophase stability.

Having established the dominant role of hydrophobic packing, we next examined the influence of the polar head length using the 3C18-(DMA)n and the 2C18-(DMA)n oligomer series, with n = 1 to 4. For 3C18-(DMA)1, SAXS shows a sharp C → LC → I sequence similar to 3C18-(DMAEA)1 and 3C18-(HEA)1, with a lamellar crystalline phase transforming into a hexagonal columnar mesophase above T m. As the oligomer length increases, the lamellar spacing d in the crystalline state gradually increases (i.e., q 0 decreases), consistent with expansion of the polar region and reduced crystalline order. For some intermediate liquid-crystalline states observed for the longer oligomers, the available SAXS reflections were insufficient for unambiguous indexing. These phases are therefore discussed tentatively as liquid-crystalline phases with unresolved symmetry rather than as distinct new mesophases. Specifically, in the 3C18-(DMA)n series, q0 decreases from 0.0890 Å–1 for n = 1, to 0.0803 Å–1 for n = 2, 0.069 Å–1 for n = 3, and 0.066 Å–1 for n = 4, corresponding to an increase in the lamellar spacing from approximately 7.1 to 7.8, 9.1, and 9.5 nm, respectively. This increase is consistent with progressive expansion of the oligomer-rich microsegregated domain. A similar trend is observed for 2C18-(DMA)n series (Figure B).

The phase behavior becomes increasingly complex for longer DMA oligomers and is summarized in the phase diagrams (Figures C, D and A). For example, 3C18-(DMA)3 exhibits a broadened C → LC transition near 80 °C and a splitting of reflections, consistent with coexistence of multiple LC domains (q 0 = 0.10 and q 0 2 = 0.12 Å–1). This effect is further enhanced for 3C18-(DMA)4, which undergoes a two-step transformation, first into an intermediate LC phase and then into a second LC phase, without reaching isotropization up to 125 °C (Figures C, D and S43). These results indicate that longer polar oligomers disrupt the molecular packing within the mesophase and suggest coexistent LC states within a single sample, which could be ascribed to different crystallization rates or heterogeneous local interactions. In parallel, the hexagonal columnar ordering becomes less well-defined when increasing the oligomer length: the reflections broaden and the extracted lattice parameter a significantly diminishes, indicating either smaller ordered domains and/or wider distribution of local packing environments. Peak broadening is also evident in the crystalline state as n increases, suggesting smaller crystallites or increased structural heterogeneity.

The 2C18-(DMA)n series shows qualitatively similar behavior but with lower transition temperatures, and T i is readily observed around 110–120 °C (Figures S44–S46).

Here, the C → LC transition still proceeds from lamellar to hexagonal columnar order, but the changes in spacing are smaller, consistent with reduced symmetry and weaker hydrophobic packing in the two-arm cores (Figure S47).

Finally, we examined the impact of a bulkier polar head at different length using 3C18-(MEEEA)n and 2C18-(MEEEA)n series. For short heads (n = 1), the structure melts directly from a lamellar crystal into an isotropic liquid, and no LC phase is detected. For longer oligomers (n > 2), a transition from a lamellar C phase to a lamellar LC phase emerges, but its stability is markedly reduced, with T i around 115 °C even for the more stable 3C18 derivatives (Figure B). Thermal cycling also affects crystallization: after melting and cooling, SAXS patterns show broader and less intense lamellar reflections, indicating that bulky polar heads hinder recrystallization and promote polymorphic rearrangements. For n = 3, the C > LC transition becomes poorly defined and peak broadening increases.

Notably, 2C18-(MEEEA)1 exhibits sharper crystalline order than 3C18-(MEEEA)1, reversing the trend observed in DMA-based structures (Figure S55). This inversion suggests that steric congestion from the MEEEA headgroup can outweigh the packing advantage typically provided by the third alkyl arm. These observations align with DSC, where sharper melting and crystallization features correlate with narrower, more intense SAXS reflections. The recurring lamellar-to-hexagonal columnar transition (DMA series) and lamellar-to-lamellar transition (longer MEEEA series) confirm that these amphiphilic oligomers form well-defined nanoscale lattices whose symmetry and stability are dictated by core topology and polar-block design.

Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated a modular and versatile strategy to synthesize amphiphilic liquid crystalline materials by attaching sequence-defined polar oligomers to multiarm aromatic cores. The combination of a thiolactone-based iterative protocol with di- and trialkylated galloyl derivatives provides precise control over monomer type, oligomer length and core topology.

The thermal and structural characterization establishes clear structure–property relationships. The number of C18 chains on the aromatic core has the strongest effect on phase stability: three-arm derivatives consistently display higher melting and isotropization temperatures and more stable lamellar-to-hexagonal transitions than two-arm analogues, reflecting tighter hydrophobic packing and higher substitution symmetry. In contrast, the higher ΔHi values observed for two-arm structures indicate more cooperative, entropy-driven isotropization, highlighting the role of structural flexibility in LC organization. The nature of the polar headgroup also influences the phase behavior: hydrogen-bonding units such as HEA and AA increase the thermal stability of both the crystalline and LC phases, whereas bulkier PEG-like monomers such as MEEEA reduce crystallinity and can suppress LC formation. Increasing the length of the sequence-defined polar block lowers T c and crystallization enthalpy and can introduce competing mesophases, particularly in the three-arm DMA-based systems.

These results show that LC behavior in this class of amphiphiles can be programmed at the molecular level, with hydrophobic packing, headgroup structure, and polar block length acting as independent parameters to tune thermal windows and mesophase organization. This approach provides therefore a strong basis for designing responsive LC materials with sequence-controlled molecular structures.
