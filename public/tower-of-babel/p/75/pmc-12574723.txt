Psychological impact of first vs. repeated cesarean sections: A comparative study on postpartum depression, anxiety, and stress
Authors: Minoli P. Doshi, Nimesh C. Parikh, Nilima D. Shah, Vinod M. Darji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Pregnancy impacts women’s physical and emotional well-being, with cesarean births potentially causing stress, anxiety, and depression. Previous childbirth experiences significantly shape views on subsequent pregnancies.Aim:To Study the Prevalence of Depression, Anxiety, and Stress In Women Who Underwent Their First CS As Compared To those with repeated CS.Materials and Methods:It is a cross-sectional descriptive study conducted in a Tertiary Care Hospital of Ahmedabad. Obstetric-Gynaecological Department. It included 130 women, 65 who had their first CS and another 65 who had subsequent sections. After taking informed consent, Participants’ sociodemographic and clinical information was acquired using a Performa, and their anxiety, depression, and stress levels were measured using three scales: GAD-7, EPDS, and PSS. Statistical analysis was performed using Excel and the statistical software SPSS.Results:Anxiety was significantly higher in rCS women (38.46%) than in fCS women (21.53%) (P = 0.03), while depression (26.15% vs. 16.92%) and stress (38.46% vs. 26.15%) were more common in rCS but not statistically significant. Depression correlated with maternal age (P = 0.0002) and education (P = 0.044), anxiety with emergency cesarean (P = 0.01), and stress with unplanned pregnancy (P = 0.04).Conclusion:The findings of this study emphasize the need for interventions like mental health support programs, stress management strategies, educational initiatives and early detection of mental health issues during prenatal care to improve maternal well-being.

Motherhood marks a profound transition in a woman’s life, encompassing a complex interplay of physical, emotional, and psychological changes. While generally celebrated as a period of joy and fulfillment, pregnancy and childbirth can also be fraught with emotional upheaval and psychological distress, often influenced by the birth experience itself. Although traditionally postpartum psychiatric illnesses have been categorized into postpartum blues, depression, and psychosis, recent evidence underscores that anxiety disorders are often underdiagnosed and more prevalent than depression during the perinatal period.[1] Stress has been highlighted as an independent construct involving sustained arousal and functional impairment.[2]

Among the obstetric factors influencing maternal mental health, mode of delivery—particularly cesarean section (CS)—has emerged as a critical determinant. Studies have consistently associated emergency CS with elevated levels of postpartum stress, anxiety, and depression.[34] Emergency procedures may intensify psychological distress due to lack of preparation and heightened concerns for maternal and fetal safety. Psychological vulnerability, including prior psychiatric history, has also been linked with greater susceptibility to postpartum depression after CS.[5]

Sociodemographic variables further modulate mental health outcomes. Advanced maternal age, marriage duration, lower income, and education levels have all been variably associated with increased psychiatric morbidity.[6] Some evidence suggests that higher socioeconomic status may not always be protective, with educated women and those from urban settings occasionally reporting higher distress levels.[7] Additionally, cultural perceptions of CS—viewed in certain societies as a status-enhancing or less painful alternative to vaginal birth—may influence maternal expectations and psychological responses.[8]

Parity plays a nuanced role in shaping childbirth experiences. First-time CS (fCS) deliveries are associated with heightened anxiety and uncertainty,[9] whereas repeated CS (rCS) deliveries may involve more postoperative pain due to inadequate analgesia or prior surgical complications.[10] A prior CS strongly predicts future CS deliveries, highlighting the cyclical nature of obstetric decision-making.[11] Despite the growing global CS rates, there remains a paucity of studies directly comparing psychological outcomes between women undergoing fCS and rCS, particularly in the context of anxiety, depression, and stress.

In this backdrop, this study aimed to examine and compare the continuum of psychological distress—namely anxiety, depression, and stress—among women who underwent first versus repeated CSs, while also exploring associated sociodemographic and obstetric correlates. The findings aim to fill a critical gap in existing literature and inform more personalized postpartum psychological care strategies.

PROCEDURE

After getting approval from the Institutional Review Board and permission from the Head of the Department of Obstetric-Gynaecological Department of Tertiary Care Hospital attached to the Medical College, the study was conducted as follows: a total of 130 women – 65 who underwent their first CS and 65 who underwent their subsequent cesarean section were included in the study after taking their informed consent. Data was collected at the Tertiary Care Hospital affiliated with the Medical College from March 2022 onwards.

The proforma included:

A standardized semi-structured proforma for sociodemographic and other clinical details.

Edinburgh postnatal depression scale: EPDS

The EPDS, crafted by Cox et al.,[12] is a specialized 10-item self-rated questionnaire for postpartum depression screening. It demonstrates robust reliability with a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 98% at a threshold of 10.5. Questions 1, 2, and 4 follow a scoring pattern of 0, 1, 2, or 3, while questions 3, 5, and 10 are reverse scored. The scale’s maximum score is 30, and a score of 10 or above indicates possible depression.

Generalized anxiety disorder assessment scale -7 (GAD-7)

The GAD-7, developed by Spitzer et al.[13] in 2006, is a self-rated 7-item scale designed to identify probable cases of generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). Administered across diverse populations, it is a freely usable tool with good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92) and validity (internal consistency of 0.83). This questionnaire evaluates the patient’s health status over the preceding 2 weeks through seven self-report anxiety items. A GAD-7 score of 4 or higher serves as a reasonable threshold for indicating the presence of anxiety.

Perceived stress scale-10 (PSS-10)

Developed in 1983 by Cohen et al.,[14] the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) assesses the subjective components of unwanted, non-specific stress. A self-rated 10-item scale, it is freely administered across diverse populations, with good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82) and validity. Participants rate feelings and thoughts over the past month on a five-point scale, yielding scores ranging from 0 to 40, where higher scores indicate elevated perceived stress. Four items (4, 5, 7, 8) employ reverse scoring, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of stress levels. A PSS-10 score of 13 or higher serves as a reasonable threshold for indicating heightened stress, making it a valuable tool in stress assessment.

Statistical analysis

Data was tabulated and a master chart was prepared using Microsoft Excel Office. Statistical analysis was performed using Excel and the statistical software SPSS. Tests of significance such as Pearson’s Chi-square test and Fisher exact test were used for value <5 to find significance. Significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Women in the rCS group were slightly older, with a mean age of 29.8 ± 3.2 years compared to 26.5 ± 2.9 years in the fCS group (P = 0.002). Educational attainment was lower in the rCS group (P = 0.044). No significant differences were noted in socioeconomic status or employment status [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Previous literature has extensively explored the psychological outcomes associated with cesarean deliveries. One study reported that 25% of women undergoing planned cesareans experienced general anxiety, while 31.3% expressed concern for their baby’s health.[8] In contrast, another study found that first-time cesarean recipients were more likely to experience heightened anxiety.[9] A history of adverse childbirth experiences has also been associated with increased postpartum anxiety, reinforcing the findings of the present study.[1] Additionally, multiparous women may encounter more postoperative pain, potentially contributing to elevated anxiety levels.[10] Higher stress levels have also been reported in women undergoing cesarean births compared to those who had vaginal deliveries, aligning with current results.[15] However, specific comparisons of stress between first-time (fCS) and repeated cesarean sections (rCS) remain scarce.

Psychological vulnerability factors such as increasing maternal age and longer marriage duration have shown mild correlations with postpartum depression.[1] Geographic and sociocultural variations may also influence outcomes. For instance, in a study conducted in Istanbul, women with high school or university education reported higher depression scores.[16] Similarly, in the current study, depression was more prevalent among homemakers, those living in joint families, and women from specific income groups across both fCS and rCS categories—although no statistically significant intergroup differences were observed. To date, no published studies have directly examined depression correlations with sociodemographic factors specific to fCS and rCS.

Other researchers found no clinically significant association between demographic or obstetric variables and postpartum psychological outcomes.[34] Furthermore, previous study identified links between postpartum and prenatal depression, as well as sleep disturbances in both mothers and their infants[4]—similar to current observation that a history of psychiatric illness was associated with postpartum depression.

Delivery mode also plays a critical role. Emergency cesareans were linked with higher depression scores in earlier studies,[17] consistent with current finding that 72.73% of fCS women who experienced depression had undergone emergency deliveries. Additionally, having a female newborn has been associated with increased anxiety symptoms.[6] In the current study, stress was more common among mothers of female infants in the fCS group (57.14%). Unplanned pregnancies have also been shown to correlate with higher anxiety levels,[3] which mirrors the current observation of increased anxiety in women with unplanned deliveries—57.14% in fCS and 76% in rCS.

Moreover, consistent with previous studies,[12] the current study found that multiparas, much like primiparas, often experience inadequate analgesia and more severe postoperative pain, likely due to a history of previous surgical procedures.

Differences between current study and existing literature may be attributed to several factors, including geographic location, cultural context, methodological design, and time of data collection. The diversity of healthcare systems, population characteristics, and assessment tools used across studies calls for caution when generalizing findings. This emphasizes the importance of interpreting results within their specific contexts.

Limitations

While current study adds valuable insights into postpartum psychological distress, its limitations—namely, a small sample size, cross-sectional design, and single-center setting—limit the generalizability of findings. Future research involving larger, more diverse populations across multiple hospitals and cultural backgrounds is essential to validate and expand upon these results.

CONCLUSION

To address the observed variations in postpartum mental health outcomes between women undergoing first cesarean section (fCS) and repeated cesarean section (rCS), several interventions can be implemented. Firstly, personalized counseling sessions should be provided, emphasizing coping strategies and mental well-being. Additionally, targeted educational programs should be developed, addressing the psychological impact of multiple cesarean sections and the potential association with heightened anxiety and depression. Establishing support groups where women can share experiences may prove beneficial. Clinical practices should incorporate routine mental health assessments during postpartum visits to identify and address any emerging concerns promptly. Furthermore, raising awareness among family members, particularly husbands, about providing emotional support during the postpartum period is crucial. Integrating these interventions into standard postnatal care protocols can contribute to improved mental health outcomes for women undergoing cesarean sections.
