Modernizing Pesticide Risk Assessment: Technical Advancements as the Path toward Global Food Security
Authors: Heidi B. Irrig, David J. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper examines how U.S. pesticide science and regulatory frameworks contribute to global food security. It details the use of USDA and FDA monitoring data alongside modernized dietary models (FDD/FCID) to improve the transparency, reproducibility, and potential accuracy of risk assessments. While domestic systems show high compliance, the paper highlights persistent challenges in global harmonization, including the hazard- versus risk-based regulatory divide and emerging methodologies like GECDE. Ultimately, achieving consistent global food safety alignment requires bridging regulatory differences through transparent, validated data and international coordination to support trade, consumer confidence, and an abundant food supply for all.

Introduction

The Washington DC location of the 2025 American Chemical Society’s Agrochemicals (AGRO) Division Fall meeting provided the perfect setting for examining how U.S. leadership in pesticide science and regulation contributes to global food security. , The symposium “Pesticide Regulation, Global Trade: U.S. Leadership in Food Security” brought together experts from government, industry, and agricultural trade to examine how coordinated regulation and international harmonization support exports while maintaining food safety.

Given the complex landscape of pesticide regulation and food safety, the symposium examined these challenges through two complementary lenses: data systems (Session One) and policy frameworks (Session Two). This approach allowed participants to review current food safety efforts, international coordination activities, and the technical frameworks that underscore the U.S. contribution to global food security.

The data session demonstrated how FDA and EPA are advancing and modernizing pesticide risk assessment through integrated databases and granular food disaggregation, moving from broad food categories to ingredient-level precision. The policy session focused on balancing agricultural production and market competitiveness with environmental protection, while highlighting collaborative efforts to reduce trade barriers through harmonized international pesticide residue standards and access to detailed trade data.

This paper (Table ) underscores the prominent role of the United States in securing a safe and abundant global food supply. It details how the nation achieves this through the strategic utilization of comprehensive pesticide monitoring data and the development of advanced dietary models, which together enable rigorous and transparent risk assessments.

Theme 1: Strengthening the Foundation of Data Infrastructure and Monitoring: USDA PDP and FDA Programs

Two complementary systems were presented: the USDA Pesticide Data Program (PDP), which generates residue data for dietary risk assessment and regulatory decision-making, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) pesticide residue monitoring program, which applies those standards in practice by enforcing U.S. EPA tolerances in the food supply.

The USDA Pesticide Data Program

Established in 1991, PDP provides statistically based, nationally representative surveillance of pesticide residues in foods consumed by the U.S. public, with particular emphasis on commodities important in the diets of infants and children. Unlike enforcement programs, PDP was designed to generate high-quality data for EPA risk assessments and U.S. government decision-making rather than support regulatory action against violators.

PDP data are a core input into EPA’s dietary exposure modeling. EPA combines PDP residue data with NHANES/WWEIA (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey/What We Eat in America) − consumption data to estimate exposures across population groups. This integration allows EPA to evaluate both acute and chronic dietary risks, set or revise tolerances, and make science-based registration decisions. Transparency is a defining feature of PDP. The program issues detailed annual reports, data summaries, and statistical appendices that are freely accessible to the public. These reports – which provide breakdowns by commodity, pesticide, and residue level – are widely used not only by regulators but also by researchers, growers, and consumer groups. In 2023 alone, PDP analyzed more than 9,800 samples. Results showed that over 99% of residues were below EPA tolerance levels, and nearly 40% of samples had no detectable residues. This robust, publicly available data underpins EPA’s dietary risk assessments, assists FDA enforcement targeting, supports international MRL alignment at Codex, and provides growers and commodity groups with credible evidence to market U.S. produce as safe and compliant.

The FDA Pesticide Monitoring Program

FDA’s program complements PDP by focusing directly on enforcement of EPA-established tolerances. While PDP supports exposure assessment, FDA’s program focuses on enforcing tolerances and preventing violative shipments. Its data set – extending back decades – provides an unparalleled view of long-term compliance trends. FDA results consistently show high compliance among U.S. foods, whereas imported products exhibit violation rates three to five times higher. Most violations reflect the absence of established U.S. tolerances rather than unsafe residue levelsa regulatory gap that complicates international trade.

Like PDP, FDA emphasizes transparency. It publishes annual pesticide monitoring reports with analyses, data tables, and electronic statistical files, all publicly available, with archives back to 1987. , It also provides raw monitoring data through the Compliance Data Portal, enabling independent analysis. A 2021 review in Food Additives and Contaminants, Part A summarized the result of more than 56,000 samples collected between 2009 and 2017, underscoring the program’s breadth and consistency.

Further, FDA can rapidly adapt its monitoring program to emerging food concerns on a case-by-case basis as circumstances arise. For example, in 2016 FDA initiated a focused glyphosate monitoring program in soybeans, corn, milk, and eggs, later expanding the sampling to other commodities. It has also conducted targeted surveys on sensitive foods such as infant formula, reflecting its flexibility in responding to evolving public and regulatory concerns.

Bringing USDA and FDA Together

Together, USDA’s PDP and FDA monitoring programs form the backbone of the U.S. pesticide monitoring system. PDP provides the residue data used in EPA dietary risk assessments, while FDA enforces tolerance compliance in the food supply. Their publicly available data sets support regulatory decision-making, consumer confidence, and international discussions on pesticide residue standards.

Theme II: Innovation – Modernizing Dietary Exposure Assessment Tools through Alignment of FDA’s TDS/FDD and EPA’s FCID

While monitoring provides the foundation, the integration of such surveillance data into dietary exposure models is what makes pesticide risk assessment informative and actionable. Two closely linked initiatives were highlighted – FDA’s modernization efforts of the Total Diet Study (TDS) , through its Food Disaggregation Database (FDD), and EPA’s update of its Food Commodity Intake Database (FCID). Together, these advances move dietary risk assessment from “foods-as-consumed” toward ingredient-level modeling, providing more accurate, transparent, and policy-relevant estimates of exposure. Both agencies emphasized that standardized “recipes” for disaggregating complex foods into raw commodities are essential to link consumption survey data (e.g., from USDA’s WWEIA) with residue monitoring results to produce data-informed dietary exposure assessments which are key in evaluating – and acting upon, when needed – real-world risks.

FDA’s Total Diet Study (TDS) and Food Disaggregation Database (FDD)

The TDS is among the longest-running dietary monitoring programs in the world. Initiated in 1961, the program expanded to include nutrients, pesticides, toxic elements, and other contaminants. Its core mission remains unchanged: to estimate dietary exposures for the U.S. population and subgroups across a broad range of chemicals.

TDS traditionally operated by collecting food from 24 regional locations across the country and preparing foods as typically consumed. Each year, the program analyzed roughly 3,000 individual food items, which were then composited into representative samples for laboratory analysis. Over time, however, the fixed composite approach of the TDS proved increasingly mismatched with the granularity of modern dietary surveys, such as USDA’s WWEIA, part of NHANES. These surveys capture thousands of foods, preparation methods, and consumption patterns, but the TDS sampling program lacked the resolution to integrate them directly.

To bridge this gap, FDA developed the FDD, which expands the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS) into more than 11,000 standardized recipes. By disaggregating foods into raw ingredients, the FDD enables commodity-level exposure modeling for foodborne chemicals such as pesticides and toxic elements. This innovation preserves analytic concentration data from TDS while allowing computational modeling to capture modern dietary patterns more faithfully. In addition, the FDD is particularly useful for initiatives such as FDA’s Closer to Zero program, which seeks to reduce children’s exposures to toxic elements (e.g., arsenic, lead, and cadmium). Foods such as fruit purees, mixed cereals, or snack items can now be broken down into their individual components, providing ingredient-specific exposure estimates for sensitive populations. Transparency is also central to this modernization; like PDP data, FDA makes TDS and FDD data and documentation publicly available to ensure the data remains trustworthy and accessible.

EPA’s Update of the Food Commodity Intake Database (FCID)

EPA’s FCID plays a central role in its Office of Pesticide Programs dietary risk assessment, pesticide tolerance-setting, and cumulative exposure analyses. Originally developed in its current form using 2005–2010 consumption data, , it translated survey foods from USDA’s WWEIA into raw commodities through a labor-intensive recipe-building process. While thorough, this manual approach was unsustainable for routine updating and risked becoming outdated as consumption patterns shifted over time.

To modernize dietary exposure tools, EPA aligned its FCID with FDA’s FDD using a semiautomated “crosswalk” powered by BM25 text-matching algorithms. Moving to ingredient-level modeling replaces labor-intensive manual procedures, improving transparency, efficiency, and interagency consistency. A public release of the updated FCID is anticipated in 2026. As with the 2005–2010 version (https://fcid.foodrisk.org), the new database will include comprehensive documentation and FAQs.

Bringing FDA and EPA Together

FDA’s FDD and EPA’s updated FCID together represent a major modernization of U.S. dietary exposure assessment. By enabling foods to be disaggregated into ingredients and linked with monitoring data, the system improves transparency, reproducibility, and potential accuracy of pesticide exposure estimates used in regulatory decision-making.

Just as the PDP and FDA monitoring programs exemplify transparency and trust in surveillance, the FDD–FCID alignment illustrates innovation and interagency collaboration in modeling. In 2026, it is expected that the U.S. will have in place a more reproducible, flexible, and transparent dietary exposure data infrastructure, one that is better able to inform regulatory decisions, anticipate emerging risks, and support consumer confidence in food safety.

Theme III: Advanced Risk and Residue Methodologies

Building on residue monitoring and dietary exposure data, the EPA conducts aggregate risk assessments for pesticides as required by the Food Quality Protection Act (FQPA). , The statute directs EPA to evaluate aggregate exposures across the food, water, and residential pathways, and ensure such combined exposures result in “reasonable certainty of no harm”. The FQPA also specifically directs that EPA explicitly consider increased susceptibility in infants and children and to retain an additional child-protective safety factor unless reliable data demonstrate that a different factor would be adequately protective. These provisions are intended to ensure a reasonable certainty of no harm for infants and children by accounting for potential increased susceptibility, including pre- and postnatal toxicity, as well as data uncertainties related to exposure and toxicological completeness.

In situations where multiple pesticides act by a common mechanism of toxicity, the FQPA statute also requires that the Agency cumulate these exposures in a cumulative risk assessment (CRA) across chemicals with a similar mechanism of toxicity. A central principle in CRA is the identification of pesticides that share a common mechanism of toxicity through which pesticides are grouped into “Common Mechanism Groups” (CMGs) where evidence supports a shared toxicological pathway. Recognizing the inherent complexity, data-intensity, and resource requirements of establishing CMGs and conducting CRAs, EPA has published a tiered “CRA Screening Framework”. This framework uses available toxicological data, chemical structure information, and mechanism/mode-of-action evidence to determine whether a cumulative assessment is warranted and – if so – the appropriate level of refinement. In practice, this screening approach helps prioritize effort, limits unnecessary analyses, and focuses cumulative assessments on situations where combined exposures are most likely to be relevant for regulatory decision-making. Despite ongoing challenges related to the number of chemicals and data needs, this systematic approach underscores EPA’s commitment to comprehensive pesticide safety.

In addition to advances in monitoring and exposure modeling, the session discussed the use of predictive models as a potential complement to or substitute for conventional pesticide residue data generation through field trials. These models were introduced as a robust, reliable, and regulatory-ready solution to optimize pesticide use and comply with export market residue standards. Several limitations of the current GLP field-trial paradigm were noted, including high costs (often exceeding $100,000 per crop), long study timelines (typically 12–18 months), and restricted coverage of crops, geographies, and use patterns. Together, these constraints hinder innovation and product approvals and can slow product development, limit the number of use scenarios evaluated, and complicate efforts to align residue data with international regulatory requirements.

As a sophisticated alternative, predictive models are proposed to forecast pesticide residues across all use scenarios at lower cost and increased reliability. If validated, these models could accelerate product approvals and help growers comply with international residue standards. These approaches could also modernize residue regulation and support science-based registration decisions. Ongoing work focuses on extending model applicability to additional crops (or crop varieties) and active ingredients, continued validation, and engagement with regulators and stakeholders to explore circumstances under which model-based approaches could be used alongside traditional residue studies and, eventually, to pilot model-based registration. Additional exploratory domains could include uncertainty propagation and extreme weather/regional variations, and application differences.

Theme IV: Divergent Global Frameworks

Despite significant U.S. progress in monitoring and exposure modeling, the symposium emphasized that domestic achievements alone cannot overcome barriers to global trade and regulatory alignment. Several talks explored how differences in methodology, data use, and regulatory philosophy across regions create persistent obstacles to harmonization.

Comparing U.S. and European Risk Assessment Models

A comparative analysis of dietary risk assessment models highlighted the depth of divergence between the U.S. system and those in use in other regions around the globe. The U.S. system, using tools such as Dietary Exposure Evaluation Model (DEEM) and the Cumulative and Aggregate Evaluation System – Next Generation (CARES-NG) model, relies on tiered refinements: residue data are adjusted using processing factors, probabilistic approaches are employed, and diverse consumption data sets (with eight defined population groups) are integrated. Toxicological end points are adjusted where appropriate to protect sensitive populations with factors that are specific to those subpopulations (e.g., FQPA) mandates providing additional safety factors for infants and children unless evidence is available for their nonuse.

By contrast, the European Union’s PRIMo models (versions 3.1 and 4.0) typically rely on more conservative default approaches, in which toxicological reference values are derived from the most sensitive end point and applied uniformly across all subpopulations. Such a hazard-based framework emphasizes the intrinsic toxicological properties of a substance without considering exposure magnitude, whereas a risk-based framework evaluates both toxicity and exposure together. Consequently, differences in population definitions and survey designs can produce markedly different exposure estimates from identical residue data, creating technical barriers to trade.

The Global Estimate of Chronic Dietary Exposure (GECDE) Approach at Codex

Recent Codex discussions on dietary exposure assessment illustrate both the need for modernization and the challenges of achieving international consensus. The Joint FAO/WHO Meeting on Pesticide Residues (JMPR)the international scientific body tasked with evaluating the safety of pesticide residues and providing advice to the Codex Committee on Pesticide Residues (CCPR)has explored replacing the current International Estimated Daily Intake (IEDI), based on GEMS/Food cluster diets, with the Global Estimate of Chronic Dietary Exposure (GECDE-mean) approach. The rationale is that the current cluster-diet approach is limited in its ability to characterize exposure for sensitive populations – such as children and females of childbearing age – and high-end habitual consumers. While JMPR reported good concordance between GECDE-mean and GECDE-high approaches and individual food-consumption survey data, CCPR members and observers have requested additional information on the method’s transparency, validation across a broader range of diets, and potential implications for Codex MRLs.

These concerns underscore that changes to international tools must be scientifically justified and developed through clear communication between JMPR as risk assessor and CCPR as risk manager. Recognizing these issues, CropLife International submitted a Conference Room Document to CCPR56 requesting procedural assurance prior to any transition. Furthermore, an independent initiative is now evaluating GECDE’s parameters and validating outputs against national data sets like U.S. NHANES. The GECDE case demonstrates that the acceptance of global tools depends on transparent methodologies and stakeholder engagement to ensure they meet the concerns and needs of global food safety governance with varying views of risk assessment.

The Broader Implications

Varying global regulatory approaches can undermine even sophisticated national systems. Differences in Maximum Residue Limits across countries – such as those identified by FAO for rice – create trade disruptions, increase compliance costs, and contribute to consumer confusion and reduced trust in regulatory institutions. Without greater international alignment, even technically robust national systems cannot ensure consistent global food safety governance.

In conclusion, the U.S. demonstrates important leadership in pesticide regulation, through transparent monitoring systems, publicly accessible residue data, and increasingly refined dietary exposure and risk assessment tools. The USDA PDP and FDA’s monitoring programs provide complementary foundations for regulatory decision-making, enforcement, consumer confidence, and international discussions of pesticide residue standards, demonstrating consistently high compliance in U.S. foods. At the same time, modernization efforts such as the FDA’s TDS with FDD and EPA’s updated FCID illustrate how exposure assessment is moving toward greater granularity, reproducibility, and policy relevance by linking contemporary food consumption data with commodity-level residue information.

Despite these advances, the broader challenge remains: technical strength at the national level does not, by itself, ensure international alignment which can create trade barriers and undermine regulatory harmonization. The effectiveness of even the most sophisticated national systems is diminished without greater international alignment. Differences in monitoring systems, dietary exposure models, residue definitions, population groupings, data transparency, MRL-setting practices, as well as the balance between hazard-based and risk-based decision frameworks can all lead to divergent regulatory outcomes. The ongoing Codex discussion of GECDE further illustrates that globally applicable methods must be transparent, validated, fit for purpose, and developed through clear communication between scientific assessors, risk managers, and stakeholders.

Thus, the central lesson is not simply that one regulatory philosophy differs from another or that a concerted effort is required to bridge regulatory differences and foster greater harmonization of standards worldwide. It is that global food safety governance depends on the credibility, comparability, and acceptance of the data and models used to support decisions. Continued investment in transparent monitoring, modern exposure assessment, validated predictive tools, and international dialogue will be essential to reduce unnecessary trade barriers, maintain consumer confidence, and support a safe and abundant global food supply.
