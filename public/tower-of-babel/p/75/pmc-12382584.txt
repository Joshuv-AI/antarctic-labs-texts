Parental quality of life and its predictors in Jordanian families with children with cerebral palsy
Authors: Mohammad Al-Wardat, Husam Magableh, Khader A Almhdawi, Mohammad Yabroudi, Mohammad Etoom, Hashem Abu Tariah, Auwal Abdullahi, Nihad Almasri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground The lifelong care responsibilities associated with cerebral palsy (CP) may place significant physical and psychological burdens on parents, which might hinder their quality of life (QOL). Despite their importance, factors affecting parents’ QOL in CP cases remain understudied globally, including Jordan. This study aimed to assess the QOL of parents of children with CP and investigate its predictors.MethodsSociodemographic factors were evaluated in this cross-sectional study for both the parents and their children with CP. The parents’ QOL and musculoskeletal pain were assessed using the Beach Centre Family Quality of Life Scale (BCFQOL) and the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire. For a comprehensive assessment of psychological well-being and sleep quality, the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index were administered. The significant predictors of parents’ QOL were determined using multivariable linear regression analysis.Results150 parents of children with CP participated in this study. Participants reported a moderate to high overall level of satisfaction with BCFQOL. The highest satisfaction was observed in the family interaction and parenting, while the lowest was in emotional well-being. The regression model explained approximately 35% of the variance in parents’ QOL (R²=0.35, F=5.568, p<0.001). Factors significantly predicting higher levels of parents’ QOL were absence of child mental health symptoms (β=0.176 (95% CI 0.611 to 9.340), p=0.026), parents who are not smokers (β=0.2 (95% CI 1.468 to 11.608), p=0.012) and parents who do not report pain in the past 12 months (β=0.184 (95% CI 1.038 to 12.303), p=0.021).ConclusionsParents of children with CP showed acceptable levels of QOL. A higher level of QOL was associated with the absence of child mental health symptoms, and parents who are not smokers or do not report pain. The findings highlight the need for mental health interventions, healthier habits and musculoskeletal pain management to improve parents’ well-being.Keywords: Cerebral Palsy, Caregivers, Rehabilitation

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is associated with a spectrum of impairments and diverse clinical presentations resulting from brain injuries that can affect motor function, postural control and balance. In addition, children with CP may experience sensory, perceptual and cognitive deficits, as well as sociointeractional and attitudinal challenges.1 The global prevalence of CP in high-income countries is estimated as 1.5 per 1000 live births,2 while the estimation is 1.8 per 1000 live births in Arabic-speaking countries.3 The incidence of CP in Jordan is around 1.5–3.0 cases per 1000 live births.4 The primary movement disorders associated with CP usually include spasticity, ataxia, dyskinesia or a combination of these deficits affecting different body parts. Additionally, children with CP often exhibit significant limitations in manual dexterity and functional abilities, along with delays in gross and fine motor skills, social interactions and language development.5 Consequently, these complications may exert a detrimental influence on the overall quality of life (QOL) experienced by children diagnosed with CP and their families.1 6

The rehabilitative management of CP requires a collaborative, multidisciplinary team approach that actively involves parents (particularly mothers) who play a pivotal role throughout the treatment journey.7 As children with CP grow, parental responsibilities often become increasingly demanding, contributing to the heightened stress levels experienced from the time of diagnosis.8 In Australia, previous research found that around 98% of primary caregivers continued to experience chronic sadness for years after their child’s CP diagnosis.9 Mothers, in particular, face an elevated caregiving burden and may be at high risk of depression compared with mothers of children without developmental impairments.10

The emotional and psychological impacts on caregivers are profound. Caregiving for children with disabilities has been associated with increased unhappiness, emotional distress, anxiety, despair, heightened apprehension, compromised physical health, lack of family support and diminished QOL for primary caregivers.11 These adverse effects are consistently reported across various developing countries, including India, Malaysia and Malawi.6 10 Such findings highlight the pervasive challenges faced by families of children with CP and the significant implications of parental mental health and QOL.12

In Arab countries, evidence from Egypt,13 Kuwait14 and Saudi Arabia14 has shown that mothers often experience significant fatigue along with poor QOL and low satisfaction levels. However, the degree of parental burden in these studies varied considerably depending on socioeconomic factors, which limits the generalisability to other Arab contexts, including Jordan. Therefore, investigating the QOL of parental caregivers in Jordan is crucial for informing healthcare sectors and policy-makers.

The psychological status of parents is also thought to play a crucial role in influencing both their own and their children’s QOL.15 Previous studies have highlighted the intricate interplay between parental mental health and various dimensions of family well-being.16 Parents experiencing high levels of stress, anxiety or depression often show diminished emotional availability, which may impair their ability to provide a supportive and nurturing environment for their children.17 This can affect the psychosocial development of children and potentially lead to long-term consequences for their QOL.16 Moreover, parental psychological well-being is closely linked to the quality of parent–child relationships, with strong correlations observed between parental mental health and overall family cohesion.18

This study examines the QOL of parents of children with CP in Jordan, an area that has not been comprehensively investigated using standardised outcome measures. Given the essential role of parents in caregiving, the study explores key factors influencing their QOL, including sociodemographic characteristics, mental health (depression, anxiety and stress), sleep quality and musculoskeletal health. The research acknowledges Jordanian caregivers’ unique challenges, such as cultural, socioeconomic and healthcare system differences. Findings from this study aim to enhance support strategies for parents of children with CP.

Materials and methods

Design and sampling

This cross-sectional study was conducted following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.19 The study aimed to assess the QOL among caregivers of children with CP in Jordan.19

The sample size was determined using G*Power software, based on an estimated 20 predictors and an effect size of 0.15. A minimum of 147 participants was required to ensure a statistical power of at least 80%.20

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were parents of a child with CP, aged 18 years or younger, who were permanent residents of the same household and proficient in Arabic. Parents were excluded if they had been recently separated or widowed (within the last 6 months) to minimise the potential confounding effects of acute grief and major recent life stressors on their QoL and mental health assessments. In addition, we excluded parents with severe mental health conditions or who had a physical disability preventing ambulation. Eligible parents were approached in person during visits to healthcare centres and schools, with support from healthcare providers and school staff. Detailed information about the study was provided, and all participants gave written informed consent before enrolment.

Data collection

The researcher identified eligible parents in collaboration with healthcare providers, rehabilitation centres and special education centre staff. The diagnosis of CP was confirmed through medical records, with a neurologist’s confirmation serving as a required criterion. During these visits, the study’s objectives were explained to potential participants. Individual evaluation interviews were conducted from January to June 2023, lasting approximately 30–45 min each. To ensure independent responses, each parent was interviewed separately. A research assistant facilitated the interviews by reading the questionnaire items and response categories verbatim to maintain consistency and accuracy in data collection.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and caregivers were not directly involved in the design of the study; however, the development of the research question and outcome measures was informed by their priorities, experiences and preferences, as identified in previous literature and preliminary discussions with caregivers. Patients were not involved in recruitment or the conduct of the study. The study results will be disseminated to participants through summary reports shared with healthcare centres and institutions involved in recruitment, as well as through academic publications and presentations.

Assessments and outcome measures

The study used a self-reported questionnaire, which included the following components:

Demographics information

The demographic section collected details about the parents, such as age, nationality (Jordanian parents and non-Jordanian mothers or fathers married to Jordanian citizens), marital status, place of residence, educational level, monthly income, occupation, smoking status, presence of chronic diseases and the primary child caregiver (father or mother).

CP characteristics

Information on the children with CP included their current age, chronicity of diagnosis, type of CP, school attendance, use of assistive devices and any related conditions (physical, mental, speech, hearing, swallowing, visual and bladder control problems). Data on surgeries, type of rehabilitation, insurance coverage and the child’s level of Gross Motor Function were also collected. The Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) was used to assess the child’s gross motor abilities. The classification was performed by a specialised physical therapist experienced in paediatric neurorehabilitation. The GMFCS ranges from level I (walking without restrictions) to level V (fully reliant for mobility). The GMFCS system has been found to have good psychometric properties.21

Standardised outcome measures

The following tools were used to measure parental QOL, sleep quality, mental health symptoms and pain:

Beach Centre Family QOL Scale (BCFQOL): This was the primary outcome measure in this study, assessing parental QOL.22 The BCFQOL consists of 25 items across 5 subscales: family interaction, parenting, emotional well-being, physical/material well-being and disability-related support. The BCFQOL Scale’s major answer format is satisfaction. The items are rated on a 5-point satisfaction scale, where 1=very dissatisfied, 3=neither satisfied nor dissatisfied and 5=very satisfied. The scores range from 25 to 125; higher scores indicate better BCFQOL. The BCFQOL is reliable and valid for use among Arabian families of children diagnosed with CP.22

DASS-21 consists of 21 items, with seven items for each of the three mental health symptom categories.23 For each subscale, scores above specific cut-offs indicate the presence of symptoms: depression (>9), anxiety (>7) and stress (>14). Higher scores indicate that symptoms are more severe. DASS-21 is validated in the Arabic language and has shown good psychometric properties.23

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were presented as means and SD for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. A multivariable linear regression analysis with a stepwise feature was used to identify the factors influencing BCFQOL. This approach allowed us to systematically include or exclude predictor variables based on their statistical significance, thus refining the model to highlight only those variables with the strongest association with BCFQOL.

A significant level of alpha is set at 0.05 for all statistical analyses. The statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software V.23.0 (SPSS).

Results

Demographic characteristics of parents and children diagnosed with CP

This study successfully involved 150 parents of children with CP. Of the 180 eligible parents approached, 30 declined to participate, resulting in a response rate of 83.3%. No participants were lost during the interview stage. Most participants (80.7%) were females, with an average age of 34.84 (±8.18) years. Although both mothers and fathers were invited to participate, the majority of respondents were mothers, as they are typically the primary caregivers and more often present during recruitment visits.26 The sample mostly consisted of Jordanian parents (88%), residing in the northern (73.3%) and urban areas (68.7%). Education levels varied, with 40% holding bachelor’s degrees and 6.7% holding a community college diploma (2 years after high school), and the majority (97.3%) were married. Mothers (81.3%) were primarily responsible for childcare. Employment status varied, with 63.3% not employed, while monthly incomes ranged from 100 to 2000 Jordanian dinars, with a mean of 518.2 (±311.7) Jordanian dinars (US$731.20). Parents’ health characteristics

As shown in figure 1, the musculoskeletal symptoms were prevalent among the participating parents. Low back pain was the most common reported symptom, affecting 69.3% (figure 1A). The mean depression, anxiety and stress scores were 9.62, 7.08 and 7.68, respectively. According to the DASS-21 scoring system, these mean scores indicate that, on average, participants experienced mild to moderate levels of depression and anxiety and normal levels of stress. Specifically, a depression score of 9.62 is just above the cut-off (>9), suggesting depressive symptoms; an anxiety score of 7.08 is at the cut-off (>7), indicating anxiety symptoms and a stress score of 7.68 is below the cut-off (>14), suggesting no significant stress symptoms on average. Furthermore, based on the DASS-21 cut-off values, 34.7% of participants had depression, 41.3% had anxiety and 14.0% had stress. The mean PSQI score for the participants was 7.87, with 70% of them reporting sleep quality deficits (PSQI score >5). Parents reported the highest satisfaction in family interaction and parenting according to BCFQOLS. However, parents experienced the lowest satisfaction in emotional well-being. Financial well-being and disability support also had moderate levels of satisfaction. Overall, the parents’ satisfaction was moderate to high (figure 1B).

Predictors of QOL

Significant predictors of parents’ QOL included absence of child mental health symptoms (β=0.176 (95% CI 0.611 to 9.340), p=0.026), parents who are not smokers (β=0.2 (95% CI 1.468 to 11.608), p=0.012), parents who do not report pain in the past 12 months (β=0.184 (95% CI 1.038 to 12.303), p=0.021). The regression model accounted for approximately 35% of the variance in QOL among parents of CP-diagnosed children (r²=0.35, F=5.568, p<0.001). The variance inflation factors in the regression model were between 1.01 and 1.02, which indicates no concern regarding collinearity (table 3).

Discussion

The main aim of this cross-sectional study was to assess the QOL of parents who have children diagnosed with CP. Additionally, we aimed to investigate potential associations between QOL and various factors, including parents’ sociodemographic factors and depression, anxiety, stress, sleep quality and musculoskeletal impairments, along with CP children’s sociodemographic and medical factors. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively examine the determinants of QOL in Jordanian parents facing the unique challenges of raising a child diagnosed with CP.

Our findings revealed a significant relationship between QOL and specific factors, namely the absence of child mental health symptoms, parents who were non-smokers and parents who had not reported pain in the past 12 months. These factors explained 35% of the parents’ QOL variance in the final regression model. The study’s findings revealed a negative association between the QOL of parents of children diagnosed with CP and the presence of self-reported pain in the past 12 months, as measured by the NORDIC scale. Supporting this, we found that more than half of the children with CP in our sample used mobility aids, reflecting the substantial physical caregiving demands and their impact on parents’ daily activities and overall well-being. Additionally, around two-thirds of these children scored levels 3–5 according to the GMFCS, indicating a higher degree of physical impairment. Consistent with our findings, a recent systematic review reported that caregivers of children with CP who have severe physical disabilities experience significantly lower QOL.27 This parallel observation highlights the influence of the child’s physical condition and level of dependency on the well-being and QOL of their caregivers. Specifically, the relationship between musculoskeletal pain, child dependency and the caregiving experience highlights the multifaceted dynamics affecting parents raising children with CP. This dynamic can be explained by the increased demands placed on parents in assisting with their children’s activities of daily living, often involving lifting and physical support. Consequently, this can lead to overuse of the elbow joints, resulting in physical strain for caregivers. This finding aligns with a previous study that found a high prevalence of musculoskeletal pain among mothers of children with microcephaly, impacting their QOL.28 Similarly, Kaya et al29 identified a link between lower back pain among mothers of children with CP and its impact on their QOL.29 Additionally, Kavlak et al30 observed an association between worsening functional levels in children with CP and increased upper extremity, lower back and neck problems, along with higher anxiety levels in mothers. These factors were associated with a negative impact on the mothers’ overall QOL.30

The study revealed a significant association between smoking status and the QOL of parents raising children with CP. This finding can be explained by the well-documented adverse effects of smoking on physical and mental health, including respiratory problems, cardiovascular diseases, cancer and increased levels of stress and anxiety, all of which may collectively diminish overall well-being.31 32 Our result aligns with previous research in general populations and among adults with disabilities, which also reported a negative association between smoking and QOL.33 34 Additionally, the higher prevalence of smoking among these parents may be related to their demanding caregiving responsibilities, with smoking potentially used as a coping mechanism to manage stress. This pattern is supported by the documented correlation between problem-solving and smoking behaviour.35

The results of this study identified a statistically significant association between the presence of parents-reported mental health issues in their children with CP and their parents’ QOL. Families raising children with mental health challenges often contend with elevated levels of stress and caregiving responsibilities.36 The act of parenting and caring for a child with mental health issues can be emotionally and physically challenging.37 Additionally, the financial burden associated with providing treatment and support services for children with mental health problems can strain family finances, further impacting parental QOL.38 This association highlights the intricate interplay between the psychological and emotional aspects of CP parents and the challenges of coping with the added demands posed by their child’s mental well-being.39 The identified relationship between mental health issues in children with CP and parental QOL highlights the importance of implementing comprehensive support and interventions. These interventions should not only address the physical needs of the child but also prioritise their psychological well-being.

This study’s regression model did not reveal any significant relationships between other factors, except for parental smoking habits, children’s mental health problems and persistent elbow joint pain lasting 12 months. Indeed, these results may have been influenced by cultural factors within Jordanian society, which is known for its conservative values and potential hesitancy among participants to disclose personal information. Cultural barriers may have limited the detection of significant relationships, as participants might be uncomfortable sharing private details even in a research context. Additionally, the Muslim community’s strong belief in predestination could have led participants to perceive candid responses as challenging God’s will, potentially affecting their willingness to express grievances. In line with our results, a previous study conducted in other Muslim-majority countries such as Malaysia has also highlighted the potential impact of religious beliefs on qualitative research.40 However, contradictory findings from Sudan suggest the need for more comprehensive research databases in Arab and Muslim countries.41 Future studies should explore significant factors in-depth, examining their interconnections with additional dependent variables to gain a nuanced understanding of caregiving dynamics in these contexts.

Based on the findings of this study, clinicians, especially physical and occupational therapists, should consider the physical demands of caregiving when developing rehabilitation plans for children with CP. Addressing musculoskeletal pain, such as elbow or back pain, can improve overall family well-being. Interventions that involve parents in therapy may also enhance treatment outcomes and caregiver mental health. Healthcare facility managers can support caregiver well-being by integrating ergonomic training and musculoskeletal injury prevention into caregiver education sessions. Including support services for stress and smoking cessation may enhance caregiver QOL and reduce long-term health costs. Incorporating caregiver-focused services into national CP rehabilitation strategies—such as access to mental health services, smoking cessation programmes and periodic health assessments—can improve caregiver health and reduce burden. Future research should explore factors such as social support, coping strategies and cultural influences on caregiver QOL. Mixed-method and longitudinal studies are encouraged to gain deeper insights into caregiver experiences and to track changes over time.

This study has several strengths, including an adequate sample size determined through power analysis, which enhances statistical reliability, and the use of validated, standardised self-report instruments that allowed for a comprehensive assessment of psychological and physical health. As the first study of its kind in Jordan, it provides valuable local insight into the QOL of caregivers of children with CP. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The voluntary recruitment of participants through healthcare centres and schools may have introduced selection bias, potentially underrepresenting caregivers with limited access to services or greater caregiving burdens. The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretation and does not capture changes in caregiver well-being over time. In addition, the absence of a comparison group of parents with typically developing children limits contextual interpretation of findings. Relying on self-reported data may have introduced response bias, especially in culturally sensitive areas such as smoking or psychological distress. The study also did not account for other children in the household, which could influence caregiver strain, and it lacked a qualitative component that might have offered deeper insight into caregivers’ lived experiences. Finally, findings may not be generalisable beyond the Jordanian context without further comparative research.

Conclusions

Parents in Jordan reported moderate to high satisfaction with their overall QOL. Higher level of QoL was associated with the absence of child mental health symptoms, and parents who are not smokers or parents who do not report pain. Recognising these factors is essential for developing targeted interventions to improve both parental and child outcomes. These findings provide valuable insights for healthcare providers and policy-makers to support CP caregivers in Jordan and guide future research.
