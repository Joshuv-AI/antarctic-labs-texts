Central Message

Wedge neocarina modification preserves the conceptual advantages of the Miyamoto method while maintaining tracheo–left main bronchial continuity and enabling tailored stoma creation.

Carinal reconstruction remains one of the most technically demanding procedures in thoracic surgery.1 The wedge neocarina modification is presented as an alternative to the established Miyamoto technique.2 It was developed to optimize the reconstruction process for extended right upper-sleeve lobectomy involving the lower trachea and carina.

The ethics committee of Chiba University Graduate School of Medicine approved this research (no. M10309) on June 8, 2022. Informed consent regarding the provision of clinical information, such as images and videos, from the patient whose operative video is presented was obtained on July 8, 2022.

Operative Techniques

Resection

A wedge-shaped resection of the lower trachea and left main bronchus is made, preserving the left tracheobronchial angle. Ventilation Strategy

To maintain continuous left-lung ventilation throughout the procedure, an endotracheal tube is advanced directly into the left main bronchus (Figure 1, C).

Reconstruction

The process begins with continuous parachute sutures (specifically using 4-0 absorbable suture) starting from the deepest part of the tracheobronchial junction. Surgeons intentionally create an open stoma, which is sized to fit the stump of the intermediate bronchus (Figure 1, D). Although temporary stay sutures are used to assess tension, minimal mobilization of the left main bronchus is usually sufficient to ensure a low-tension anastomosis. Proximation and anastomosis of the intermediate bronchus is made using a parachute suture. A pericardiotomy can be used if required to reduce tension (Figure 1, E).

Finalization

Once the intermediate bronchus is anastomosed to the stoma, the endotracheal tube is retracted to the midtrachea to verify bilateral ventilation.

Comment

The original Miyamoto method is a reconstructive strategy in which the trachea and left main bronchus are first approximated, followed by implantation of the intermediate bronchus into the reconstructed airway,2 offering several important advantages in comparison with the Barclay method (Figure 3, A),3 such as exposure through a right thoracotomy, distribution of anastomotic tension, and applicability. Unlike the original method, the principal advantage of the present modification is preservation of tracheo–left main bronchial continuity throughout reconstruction. This maintains the native left tracheobronchial angle, facilitates airway orientation, and avoids excessive mobilization of the left main bronchus. In addition, stable left-lung ventilation can be maintained by advancing the endotracheal tube directly into the left main bronchus during reconstruction.Figure 3Differences among procedures. By closing the wedge-shaped defect with bilateral running sutures, a stoma of predetermined size and location can be created before implantation of the intermediate bronchus. Unlike the original Miyamoto technique, the final airway opening can therefore be tailored to the diameter and orientation of the bronchial stump. In the modified technique, the proximal resection line is longer than in the original Miyamoto technique. However, this additional length contributes directly to stoma formation and does not increase anastomotic complexity. Conversely, approximation of the distal airway is facilitated because continuity between the trachea and left main bronchus is preserved (Figure 3, B).

Conclusions

Wedge neocarina modification preserves the conceptual advantages of the Miyamoto technique while maintaining tracheo–left main bronchial continuity and enabling tailored stoma creation.
