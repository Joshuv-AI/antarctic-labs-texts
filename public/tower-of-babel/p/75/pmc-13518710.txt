Letter to the Editor Regarding “Aflibercept 8 mg versus Faricimab Treat-and-Extend for Diabetic Macular Edema or Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration: A Bayesian Fixed-Effect Network Meta-analysis of Clinical Trials”
Authors: Georgios D. Panos
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

I read with interest the article by Friedman et al., “Aflibercept 8 mg versus Faricimab Treat-and-Extend for Diabetic Macular Edema or Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration: A Bayesian Fixed-Effect Network Meta-analysis of Clinical Trials” [1]. The question addressed is clinically important, particularly as treatment burden remains central in the management of diabetic macular oedema (DMO) and neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). However, several methodological issues warrant a more guarded interpretation of the authors’ conclusions.

My main concern is that the reported advantage in injection frequency may be driven, at least in part, by structural differences in trial design rather than by a true comparative durability signal. The aflibercept 8 mg studies and the faricimab studies did not use equivalent treatment algorithms. The loading phases differed, with fewer initial injections in the aflibercept 8 mg programme, and the maximum extension interval also differed, reaching up to 24 weeks for aflibercept 8 mg but only up to 16 weeks for faricimab. In addition, the interval-adjustment criteria were not aligned across the trials. Under such conditions, comparing cumulative injection numbers risks conflating protocol architecture with therapeutic effect.

A second issue concerns the assumptions underlying the network meta-analysis itself. The network was sparse, with only three trials contributing to each disease-specific comparison, and there were no closed loops. Although the authors justify the use of a fixed-effect model on statistical grounds, the underlying clinical heterogeneity remains substantial. Differences in eligibility criteria, prior treatment exposure, disease activity rules and timing of outcome assessment challenge the transitivity assumption required for valid indirect comparison.

Third, the evidentiary basis was not fully balanced across treatment nodes. At the time the network meta-analysis was conducted, the faricimab data were drawn from contemporaneously published peer-reviewed reports, whereas the aflibercept 8 mg 2-year data from PHOTON and PULSAR were incorporated from clinical study reports. Although the 96-week PHOTON and PULSAR results have since been published, the analysis itself relied on evidence sources that were not uniform in provenance or level of external scrutiny at the time of modelling [2, 3]. This asymmetry warrants caution when interpreting the robustness of the indirect comparison.

Further limitations also deserve emphasis. In DMO, the proportion of previously treated eyes varied across trials, yet no adjustment for prior anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) exposure was performed. The analysis also treated central retinal thickness and central subfield thickness as interchangeable, despite possible differences in definition and measurement. Moreover, percentage central subfield thickness (CST) change for faricimab appears to have been derived indirectly rather than reported directly, which adds another layer of uncertainty to the anatomical comparisons.

For these reasons, the conclusion that aflibercept 8 mg required significantly fewer injections while maintaining similar efficacy should, in my view, be framed more cautiously. The findings are hypothesis-generating, but they should not be taken as firm comparative evidence in the absence of head-to-head trials or carefully controlled real-world comparative studies.
