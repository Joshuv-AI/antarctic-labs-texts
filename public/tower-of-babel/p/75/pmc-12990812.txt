Screen Time Matters: The Impact of Screen Time on the Risk of Personal–Social Development Outcomes Among Preschool Children in Indonesia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Ikeu Nurhidayah, Thalia Salsabilla, Emma Aprilia Hastuti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid increase in screen time among preschool children has become a growing public health concern, as digital gadgets are increasingly integrated into children’s daily lives. Excessive screen exposure may reduce opportunities for direct social interaction and experiential learning, thereby increasing the risk of adverse personal–social development outcomes. Personal–social development is a critical component of early childhood growth, enabling children to interact effectively with peers and their environment. This study aimed to examine the association between the duration and intensity of screen time and the risk of suboptimal personal–social development among preschool-aged children.MethodsA quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted involving 98 parents of preschool children recruited from a single preschool setting in Bandung, Indonesia. Data were collected using structured, parent-reported questionnaires assessing screen time duration and frequency, and the risk of suboptimal personal–social development, based on a previously applied personal–social development instrument in the Indonesian context. Personal–social development scores were categorized into optimal (lower risk) and suboptimal (higher risk) based on an established cut-off. Screen time variables were classified according to risk-based thresholds. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable analyses were performed to examine associations while adjusting for child age and parental education.ResultsThe findings showed a statistically significant association between screen time duration and the risk of suboptimal personal–social development (p < 0.001), as well as between the intensity of gadget use and the risk of suboptimal personal–social development (p < 0.001). Children with prolonged daily screen time (≥120 minutes) had a higher risk of suboptimal personal–social development (53.1%) compared with those with limited screen exposure. Similarly, children who used gadgets frequently (>3 times per day) were at greater risk of suboptimal personal–social development than those with moderate or minimal use.ConclusionProlonged duration and high intensity of screen time are significantly associated with an increased risk of suboptimal personal–social development among preschool children. These findings highlight the importance of parental monitoring and the establishment of appropriate limits on screen use during early childhood. Health professionals, educators, and policymakers should work collaboratively to promote balanced digital habits and screen time awareness as part of early childhood development strategies in the digital era. This study contributes to achieving SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 4 (Quality Education), particularly Target 4.2 on early childhood development.Keywords: screen time, personal–social development, preschool children, early childhood, digital media use, SDG 3, SDG 4

Background

Screen time has become an integral part of young children’s daily lives worldwide, including in low- and middle-income countries such as Indonesia.1,2 Screen time refers to the amount of time spent using screen-based devices, including smartphones, tablets, televisions, and computers, for activities such as watching videos, playing games, or using applications.3 Although digital devices may offer educational and entertainment value, excessive or poorly regulated screen exposure during early childhood has raised concerns regarding developmental outcomes.2

Early childhood represents a critical developmental period during which foundational personal–social skills are established. During the preschool years, children develop independence, emotional regulation, empathy, cooperation, and the capacity for social interactions.4 These early competencies are critical, as behavioural and social tendencies formed in this period often persist and shape later developmental trajectories.5

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that preschool children aged 2–5 years should engage in no more than one hour of screen time per day, emphasizing that less is better for optimal health and development.3,6 However, international evidence indicates that only 25–35% of preschool-aged children meet these recommendations.7 Although some scholars have questioned the strength of the evidence underlying existing guidelines,8 there is growing consensus that further empirical research is needed, particularly focusing on specific developmental domains and contextual factors.

Previous studies have linked higher screen time with behavioural difficulties, emotional dysregulation, and sleep problems among preschool-aged children.8,9 Evidence also indicates that poorer personal–social development may be associated with longer screen time through caregivers’ permissive attitudes and difficulties in limit setting.2 The increasing presence of screen-based devices in children’s daily lives has therefore raised growing concern about their potential developmental risks. In Indonesia, the proportion of five-year-old children using screen-based devices increased from 38% in 2011 to 80% in 2015,10 indicating a rapid shift toward digital use from an early age. While educational applications may enhance creativity and support cognitive development when used appropriately, excessive or unsupervised screen time has been associated with lower levels of physical activity, reduced attention, and fewer opportunities for meaningful face-to-face social interaction.8,11

Personal–social development is fundamental to school readiness and long-term mental well-being.12 In contemporary literature, personal–social development is understood as a component of personal, social, and emotional development (PSED).13 PSED is one of the three prime areas within the Early Years Foundation Stage (EYFS).13 According to the EYFS framework, children’s personal, social, and emotional development is essential for leading healthy and happy lives and serves as a fundamental foundation for their cognitive development.13

The development of personal and social skills enables children to benefit optimally from learning experiences within their environments.14 Through this developmental process, children gain a better understanding of themselves and demonstrate increasing self-regulation.15 Other aspects of personal and social development necessary for effective functioning in everyday life, including the school context, include children’s interactions with others and their social problem-solving abilities.13,16 Delays in this domain may impair communication, behavioural regulation, and emotional functioning, thereby limiting children’s participation in learning and social activities.17 Globally, developmental disparities in early childhood remain a critical concern. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately 250 million children in low- and middle-income countries, equivalent to 43% of the population, do not achieve age-appropriate developmental milestones due to poverty, inadequate stimulation, and limited access to early interventions.6 In Indonesia, data from the 2018 Indonesian Basic Health Research Survey indicated that 69.9% of children achieved expected personal–social developmental milestones. Although this represents an improvement, the proportion remains lower than that reported in several neighbouring countries, including Vietnam (91.2%), Kazakhstan (82.1%), and Thailand (79.4%).18

Recent studies suggest that excessive screen time and exposure to digital technology may increase the risk of suboptimal personal–social development in children.2,8,11,19 Specifically, screen time intensity, defined as the frequency of daily screen-based device use, may influence developmental outcomes alongside total duration.20 Muppalla et al suggested that increased screen time may reduce the quantity and quality of interactions between children and their caregivers, which, in turn, may influence language development and contribute to suboptimal social and emotional development in early childhood.11 Unregulated exposure to digital media may reduce opportunities for direct social interaction and hands-on learning experiences.2 In Indonesia, digital engagement has become embedded in everyday family life, with approximately 79.7% of the population actively using social media, exceeding rates in neighbouring countries such as the Philippines (78%), Malaysia (72%), and China (67%).21,22

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends that children aged 2–5 years engage in no more than one hour of high-quality screen time per day and only with active parental supervision.23,24 However, in many low- and middle-income settings, including Indonesia, these recommendations are challenging to implement due to evolving family routines and limited parental awareness.2 The COVID-19 pandemic further intensified children’s reliance on digital devices, particularly among preschool-aged children.20,25 The widespread adoption of remote and hybrid learning blurred the distinction between educational and recreational screen use, making it more difficult for caregivers to monitor duration and content.20,25 Many young children reportedly spend more than three hours per day on digital devices, often for entertainment rather than educational purposes.20

Most existing evidence on screen time and personal–social development has been generated in high-income countries.2 Recent studies have shown that longer screen time among preschool children is associated with behavioural difficulties and poorer personal–social development, often mediated by caregivers’ permissive attitudes and challenges in setting limits.2 In contrast, evidence from low- and middle-income countries such as Indonesia remains limited, despite a larger population of young children and rapidly increasing access to digital devices. Differences in socio-economic conditions, parental awareness, and caregiving practices suggest that findings from developed countries may not be directly transferable. This highlights the need for context-specific research examining the risk of personal–social developmental outcomes associated with screen time among preschool children in Indonesia.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the risk of suboptimal personal–social developmental outcomes associated with the duration and intensity of screen time among preschool children in Indonesia. Specifically, this study investigated whether prolonged daily screen exposure and higher frequency of screen use increase the likelihood of personal–social developmental risk among preschool-aged children in Bandung, Indonesia. Based on previous empirical evidence, this study hypothesized that preschool children with longer daily screen time and higher screen use intensity have a greater risk of suboptimal personal–social development than those with shorter daily screen time and lower screen use intensity.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional design to examine the association between screen time exposure (duration and intensity) and personal–social developmental outcomes among preschool-aged children. Data on all variables were collected at a single point in time, allowing for the assessment of patterns and risk associations without inferring causality.26

Setting and Study Period

The study was conducted at a preschool in Bandung, Indonesia, between July 2022 and February 2023. The selected preschool serves children from diverse urban families with varying socio-economic backgrounds, providing a relevant context for examining screen time exposure and personal–social development within an Indonesian urban setting.

Participants and Sampling

The study population consisted of parents or primary caregivers of preschool children aged 3–6 years enrolled at the selected preschool. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling based on the following inclusion criteria:

Being the parent or primary caregiver of a child aged 3–6 years,

Being the parent or primary caregiver of a child aged 3–6 years,

Having a child who used screen-based devices, and

Having a child who used screen-based devices, and

Providing written informed consent.

Providing written informed consent.

Parents of children with diagnosed developmental disorders were excluded to minimize potential confounding effects on personal–social development outcomes.

Data Collection Procedures

Data were collected using structured, self-administered questionnaires completed by parents during school hours. Prior to data collection, the researcher explained the study objectives and procedures to all participants. Written informed consent was obtained, and participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time without penalty. To ensure confidentiality and anonymity, each questionnaire was assigned a unique identification code (R1-R98). All data were stored securely and accessed only by the research team. Conducting data collection in person allowed parents to ask questions and seek clarification, thereby enhancing data accuracy and completeness.

Instruments and Measures

Screen Time Duration

Screen time duration was assessed using a researcher-developed questionnaire informed by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) screen time recommendations. Parents were asked:

On a typical day, how long does your child spend using screen-based devices (such as smartphones, tablets, televisions, or computers)?

Responses were reported in minutes per day and categorized into two groups: <120 minutes/day and ≥120 minutes/day. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends limiting screen time to no more than one hour per day for preschool children.27 The present study applied a 120-minute cut-off as a risk-based threshold, consistent with previous studies indicating that screen exposure exceeding two hours per day is associated with increased developmental and behavioural risks among young children.1,19,28 This categorization also reflects commonly observed screen use patterns in the local context and contextual feasibility in Indonesia, as supported by prior national studies reporting higher average screen exposure among preschool children.

Screen Time Intensity

Screen time intensity was operationalized as the frequency of daily screen-based device use. Parents were asked: “How many times per day does your child usually use screen-based devices?”. Based on classifications proposed by Putriana et al,20 frequency was categorized as rare (once per day), moderate (two to three times per day), and frequent (more than three times per day). Frequent use is defined as device use occurring more than three times per day. The term “frequent” was chosen instead of absolute descriptors (eg, “always”) to better reflect variability in daily use patterns. This approach aligns with previous literature emphasizing the importance of habitual and repeated screen exposure in understanding developmental and behavioural outcomes in young children.1,20

Screen-Based Application Type (Contextual Information)

As part of the questionnaire, caregivers were also asked to report the types of screen-based applications their children most frequently use, such as video streaming platforms, games, or educational applications. This information was collected to provide a contextual understanding of children’s screen exposure patterns. Application type and content quality were not treated as independent variables and were not included in the statistical analysis.

Personal-Social Development

Personal–social development was assessed using an 18-item parent-reported questionnaire adapted from a previous study.10 The questionnaire has previously been applied and validated within the Indonesian population.10 The instrument was adapted from earlier research conducted among Indonesian preschool children, ensuring its cultural relevance and contextual appropriateness for assessing personal–social development in local settings.10 The questionnaire assesses multiple dimensions of personal–social development, including self-centred behaviour, power-related behaviours (defined as behaviours reflecting dominance, control over peers, or insistence on authority), defiance, conflict, cooperation, aggression, and teasing or mocking.

Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 4 = strongly disagree), yielding total scores ranging from 18 to 72. Higher scores indicate greater risk of personal–social developmental problems. Scores were categorized based on the mean cut-off from the original instrument. Scores ≥44.10 were classified as suboptimal personal–social development (higher risk), while scores <44.10 were classified as optimal personal–social development (lower risk).10 This classification reflects relative developmental risk within a screening context. The validity test showed r-values ranging from 0.515 to 1.000, indicating that all 18 items are valid. Reliability testing produced a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.768, indicating that the questionnaire is reliable.10 The questionnaire underwent pilot testing with a small sample of parents (10) to ensure clarity, comprehension, and cultural appropriateness.

Participants Characteristic

Caregiver and child characteristics, including caregiver age, education level, employment status, and child age and sex, were collected using a structured questionnaire. These variables were included as covariates in the analysis to account for potential confounding effects.

Data Analysis

Data were coded, cleaned, and analyzed using SPSS version 26. Data were coded, cleaned, and analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Descriptive analyses were used to summarize participants’ characteristics and key study variables, including screen time exposure and personal–social development. Chi-square tests were conducted to examine the associations between screen time variables (duration and intensity) and categories of personal–social development, with p<0.05 set as the criterion for statistical significance. Binary regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with personal–social development scores, adjusting for child age, sex, and parental education.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Dharma Husada Institute of Health Sciences (Approval No. 174/KEPK/SDHB/B/VIII/2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and relevant national guidelines and regulations. Participants were informed about the study’s purpose, assured of confidentiality, and advised that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 98 parents of preschool-aged children participated in this study. Most respondents were mothers (87.8%), the majority of whom were homemakers, while fathers were predominantly private employees (38.8%) or self-employed (41.8%). Most parents had completed senior high or vocational education (mothers: 68.4%; fathers: 63.3%).

The children’s ages ranged from 3 to 6 years, with a mean age of 4.96 years (SD = 0.96). The largest proportion of children was five years old (38.8%), followed by six years old (33.7%). Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Screen Time Duration and Intensity

More than half of the children (52; 53.1%) were classified as having high screen time duration (≥120 minutes per day), while 46 children (46.9%) had low screen time duration (<120 minutes per day). Regarding screen time intensity, 43 children (43.9%) were categorized as frequent users (more than three times per day), 31 children (31.6%) as moderate users (two to three times per day), and 24 children (24.5%) as rare users (once per day or less). Smartphones were the most commonly used screen-based devices (85.7%), and most children shared devices with parents or siblings (65.3%). YouTube was the most frequently accessed application (57.1%), followed by TikTok and online games. Gaming-related videos constituted the most commonly viewed content. In terms of developmental outcomes, 46 children (46.9%) demonstrated optimal personal–social development (lower risk), while 52 children (53.1%) were categorized as having suboptimal personal–social development (higher risk) based on the established cut-off score (Table 2).

Association Between Screen Time Duration and Personal–Social Development Risk

An assessment of personal–social development revealed a strong association with daily screen time duration (Table 3). All children in the low-duration group (<120 minutes/day) demonstrated optimal personal–social development (46/46; 100%). Statistical analysis confirmed a highly significant association between screen time duration and risk of personal–social development (p < 0.001). Children with greater daily screen exposure were more likely to be categorized as having suboptimal personal–social development than those with lower screen time (Table 3).

Association Between Screen Time Intensity and Personal–Social Development Risk

A similar pattern was observed when screen time intensity was examined (Table 3). Among children with rare screen use (≤1 time/day), the majority demonstrated optimal personal–social development (21/24; 87.5%). In the moderate-use group (2–3 times/day), 11 of 31 children (35.5%) were classified as having suboptimal development. Among frequent users (more than three times per day), a large proportion of children (38/43; 88.4%) demonstrated suboptimal personal–social development. The association between screen time intensity and personal–social development risk was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Higher frequency of daily screen exposure was associated with an increased likelihood of suboptimal personal–social development (Table 3).

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrated a significant association between both duration and frequency of screen exposure and personal–social development among preschool children. Children with longer daily screen time and higher frequency of screen use were more likely to exhibit suboptimal personal–social development. These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that excessive screen exposure in early childhood is associated with challenges in social interaction, emotional regulation, and adaptive behaviors.11,19,20 From a developmental perspective, early childhood represents a critical period during which personal–social skills are influenced through repeated, reciprocal interactions with caregivers, peers, and the surrounding environment.2

More than half of the children in this study (53.1%) were exposed to screen time exceeding 120 minutes per day, and a similar proportion showed suboptimal personal–social development. This pattern aligns with existing recommendations, including those from the American Academy of Pediatrics, which advise limiting screen time for young children.27 While the present study cannot establish causality, prolonged screen exposure may reduce opportunities for children to engage in direct social interaction with caregivers and peers, which are essential for developing cooperation, communication, and social understanding.23 Screen time may function as a contextual factor that alters children’s daily interactional environments, thereby influencing developmental outcomes.11

It is important to note that the perfect separation observed in screen time duration (<120 vs ≥120 min/day) may not fully reflect reality. While children whose parents reported <120 minutes/day appeared to all have optimal personal–social development, this may be influenced by potential parental reporting bias, the dichotomous scoring system used for the personal–social development instrument, or the relatively small, single-site sample. Future studies could incorporate observational methods or multiple informants to improve measurement accuracy and reduce the risk of reporting bias.

Previous literature suggests that excessive screen use may reduce opportunities for meaningful parent–child interaction, particularly when digital devices are used to occupy or soothe children.24,29 Face-to-face interaction, shared play, and verbal engagement have been shown to support emotional development and social adjustment in early childhood. A reduction in these interactions may help explain the association observed between higher screen time and poorer personal–social outcomes in this study, although caregiver behaviors were not directly measured.

Although the present study did not formally assess the type or quality of digital content as analytical variables, descriptive observations indicated that children commonly accessed entertainment-based applications such as YouTube and gaming platforms. Prior studies have reported that frequent exposure to fast-paced, entertainment-focused, or aggressive content may be associated with increased irritability, impulsivity, and reduced self-regulation in young children.30 Prior research suggests that the type of media content matters for children’s development. Passive, fast-paced digital content, such as watching videos without interaction, tends to offer limited opportunities for active engagement and language stimulation compared with interactive, child-directed media, which can support verbal expression and cognitive processing when combined with parental involvement.31,32 Recreational and highly stimulating digital content has been associated with reduced self-regulation and increased irritability or impulsivity in young children, possibly because it promotes sustained attention to screens rather than real-world social interactions that are essential for social development.31,32 These findings indicate that the quality and interactivity of screen content should be considered when assessing the developmental impacts of digital media exposure.

Early childhood social development relies heavily on real-world interaction, including the ability to interpret facial expressions, tone of voice, and body language.23,33 Excessive engagement with digital devices may limit these experiences and, over time, be associated with reduced opportunities to practice empathy and cooperative behaviors. While parents in this study reported supervising their children’s gadget use, supervision was largely passive and did not necessarily involve active engagement or guidance. This observation, although not quantitatively measured, is consistent with previous research indicating that passive supervision alone may be insufficient to mitigate the potential risks of excessive screen exposure.27

Caregivers are encouraged to implement specific strategies rather than general limit-setting.34 These include co-viewing digital content with children to guide understanding and interaction, establishing screen-free zones in the home to promote uninterrupted play and family interaction, and scheduling regular playdates or outdoor activities to encourage peer socialization.11 Such structured approaches can help mitigate potential developmental risks associated with prolonged or high-frequency screen exposure while fostering personal–social skills.11

Furthermore, community nurses and healthcare professionals can play a pivotal role by integrating screen-time counseling into well-child visits. During routine check-ups, nurses can assess screen use patterns, provide personalized guidance to caregivers, and educate families on the importance of balanced digital media use and active interaction with children to promote optimal personal–social development.

Overall, this study’s findings highlight the relevance of screen time duration and intensity as correlates of personal–social development in preschool children. These results contribute to the growing body of evidence highlighting the need for balanced, developmentally appropriate use of digital media during early childhood. These findings are also relevant to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 4 (Quality Education).35 By identifying excessive screen exposure as a modifiable factor associated with personal–social development, this study supports SDG Target 3.4 on promoting mental health and well-being, as well as SDG Target 4.2 on ensuring quality early childhood development and school readiness. Addressing screen time in early childhood may therefore contribute to broader efforts to enhance child health and developmental outcomes.

Implications for Practice and Recommendations

The findings of this study suggest several practical implications for parents, educators, and healthcare professionals. Rather than offering prescriptive guidelines, this study highlights the importance of awareness regarding the potential risks associated with prolonged and frequent screen time use in preschool children. Parents may benefit from guidance on monitoring screen time, encouraging alternative play activities, and engaging more actively with children during media use.

Educators and early childhood practitioners can support social development by promoting peer interaction, cooperative play, and non-digital learning activities within preschool settings. From a healthcare perspective, nurses and health workers may consider incorporating brief discussions about screen use into routine child health visits, particularly to raise awareness and support informed decision-making by families. Future research using longitudinal or mixed-methods designs is needed to understand the directionality of these associations better and to explore how contextual factors, such as caregiver involvement and content type, influence child development outcomes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that both the duration and frequency of screen exposure are associated with a higher risk of suboptimal personal–social development among preschool children. Prolonged duration combined with frequent screen use may further increase this risk. Although parental supervision was not directly assessed, these findings may have implications for how screen time is managed in early childhood settings. These findings support integrating screen-time education into early childhood health programs, with nurses, educators, and caregivers promoting structured digital routines, co-viewing, screen-free zones, scheduled playdates, and alternative social and physical activities. Future research should explore caregiving strategies and interventions that mitigate the developmental risks associated with excessive or unstructured screen use.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study provides valuable evidence from an Indonesian preschool population. A key strength is the examination of both screen time duration and frequency, allowing a more comprehensive assessment of children’s screen exposure patterns. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The personal–social development instrument used has been previously applied in Indonesian populations and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, although further refinement and validation are still needed. We recommend that future studies employ more comprehensive, standardized developmental assessments and multi-method approaches to further validate and expand upon these findings. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal conclusions. Screen time data were based on parental self-report, which may be subject to recall bias. The extreme separation observed in screen time duration (<120 vs ≥120 min/day) may reflect parental reporting bias, the rigid cut-off score for personal–social development, or the small, single-site sample. Future research should include larger, multi-site samples and triangulate parental reports with objective measures to provide more robust estimates of the relationship between screen time and early childhood development. In addition, caregiving practices and parental supervision were not directly measured, and information on application type and content was limited to description.
