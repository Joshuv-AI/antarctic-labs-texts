Serum anti-Müllerian hormone levels in women following bariatric surgery: insights from a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammad Sharifi, Mohammad Jalaeian Dehghani, Mohammad Amin Karimi, Ali Jafari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of bariatric surgery on serum anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels in women, highlighting potential implications for ovarian reserve and fertility management.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase databases up to February 2025. The included studies were observational in design (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional) and provided quantitative pre- and post-operative AMH data. Quality assessment was performed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and data were analyzed using a random-effects meta-analysis.ResultsA total of 14 studies were included in the systematic review, with 591 participants contributing to the quantitative meta-analysis. The pooled results indicated that bariatric surgery did not significantly alter AMH levels overall (WMD = 0.05 ng/mL; 95% CI: −0.66, 0.75; p = 0.897), with substantial heterogeneity among effect estimates (I² = 93.9%, p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses suggested significant AMH reductions in studies with baseline AMH > 3 ng/mL, upper age limit ≤ 35 years, longer follow-up, retrospective design, smaller sample size, and South Asian populations, while a significant increase was observed in the fertile subgroup.ConclusionsBariatric surgery was not associated with a significant overall change in serum AMH levels. Findings suggest variable AMH responses across clinical and methodological contexts; however, given substantial heterogeneity and limited reproductive outcome data, these changes should be interpreted as dynamic endocrine biomarker responses rather than direct evidence of altered ovarian reserve or fertility potential.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02394-9.Keywords: Anti-Müllerian hormone, Bariatric surgery, Ovarian reserve, Polycystic ovary syndrome, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Obesity is defined as a body mass index (BMI) of 30 kg/m2 or more by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. It has become a major, rapidly escalating global health concern [4] and poses a serious public health challenge with significant medical, psychological, and economic consequences. These include an increased risk of various conditions such as cancer, gastrointestinal and respiratory disorders, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and type 2 diabetes [2, 3]. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, approximately 603.7 million individuals worldwide had obesity in 2015. The prevalence of obesity was notably higher in women than in men and demonstrated a rising global trend [4]. According to annual data from the Health Survey for England (HSE), the prevalence of clinical obesity among women aged 16–44 years increased from 12.4% in 1993 to 20.2% in 2010 [2]. Obesity is regarded as a potentially life-threatening condition, as it is a major risk factor for several chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, osteoarthritis, and various cancers [6]. Additionally, obesity is associated with reduced fertility, contributing to a lower likelihood of conception, increased risk of miscarriage, irregular menstrual cycles, and diminished efficacy of fertility treatments [7–9].

Bariatric surgery (BS) has been shown to be the most effective and sustainable form of weight reduction therapy for individuals with severe obesity, typically defined as a BMI of 35 kg/m² or higher [10]. It has been associated with significant improvements or resolution of obesity-related comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and obstructive sleep apnea [11, 12]. Regardless of the type of surgery performed, bariatric surgery provides long-lasting benefits in weight loss, weight control, and favorable outcomes for obesity comorbidities compared with nonsurgical therapies [13, 14]. According to earlier studies, bariatric surgery helps women with obesity lose weight while improving their ovulatory processes and controlling their menstruation [15]. Furthermore, several studies have indicated that metabolic surgery may benefit patients with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) by improving menstrual irregularities, hirsutism, fertility, and associated metabolic disturbances [16, 17]. BS is highly beneficial for women with obesity and PCOS, contributing to significant weight loss, restoration of regular menstruation, reduction in serum androgen levels, and improvements in metabolic parameters, as reported in several retrospective studies and a limited number of prospective studies [18–21].

Granulosa cells of follicles undergoing gonadotropin-independent development secrete anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), a member of the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily. Its production begins with the recruitment of follicles from the primordial pool to the primary stage and ceases once follicles reach their final size and differentiation. In women, AMH levels peak after puberty and gradually decline with age. Several studies have demonstrated that AMH is a reliable biomarker of ovarian age and reserve and is also a valuable marker for the diagnosis and clinical management of PCOS [20, 22–24]. In normo-ovulatory women, AMH levels correlate with the number of antral follicles and decline with age, reflecting the diminishing pool of oocytes [25]. A reduction in AMH levels in this population typically signifies decreased fertility potential. Conversely, women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) often present with elevated AMH levels due to an increased number of small antral follicles, which is characteristic of the syndrome [26]. In PCOS, high AMH is associated with impaired folliculogenesis and may contribute to anovulatory infertility. Thus, monitoring changes in AMH levels can be instrumental in evaluating the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions, such as bariatric surgery, on ovarian function and fertility in women with obesity and PCOS [18, 27].

Numerous studies have investigated the effect of BS on serum AMH levels; however, their findings remain inconsistent. In women with obesity of reproductive age, some prospective studies have shown a postoperative decline in AMH levels following sleeve gastrectomy [18], and a reduction exceeding that expected from normal age-related decline after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass [28]. In contrast, two cohorts of women with obesity undergoing sleeve gastrectomy demonstrated either a significant increase [29] or stable AMH levels [30] over 6 months. These discrepant patterns underscore a highly variable and still unclear association between bariatric surgery and circulating AMH concentrations. To the best of our knowledge, no meta-analysis to date has specifically focused on the effect of BS on AMH levels in women. Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically review the available evidence and perform a meta‑analysis to evaluate the impact of bariatric surgery on serum AMH concentrations in women.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The methodology was designed to align with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, ensuring transparency and adherence to established research standards [31]. The protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251020248. Since this study was a systematic review of published observational studies, ethical approval was not required. However, the review was conducted in accordance with best practices for systematic reviews of observational studies, ensuring ethical considerations in data handling and reporting.

Search strategy and study selection

A comprehensive search strategy was implemented across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science, covering studies published from database inception to February 2025. The search terms were carefully selected to capture studies related to bariatric surgery and AMH levels in women. These included keywords such as “bariatric surgery,” “weight loss surgery,” “gastric bypass,” “sleeve gastrectomy,” “anti-Müllerian hormone,” and “AMH,” combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR). Detailed search strings, including database-specific syntax, and applied filters for PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science are provided in Supplementary Table 1. A summary of excluded full-text articles and the reasons for exclusion is presented in Supplementary Table 2. Reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were manually searched to identify additional eligible studies not captured in the electronic search.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) observational design (cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional), and (2) reported serum AMH levels before and after surgery. Studies needed sufficient quantitative data, such as pre- and post-surgery means and standard deviations of AMH levels, to allow for effect size calculation. Exclusion criteria included (1) studies involving male participants or non-human subjects (2), studies lacking pre- and post-surgery AMH level data, and (3) case reports, letters, editorials, and conference abstracts without full text. Studies with duplicate data or overlapping populations were excluded to prevent bias.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers (M.Sh. and M.J.D.) screened titles and abstracts of the retrieved studies to identify potentially relevant articles. Full texts of these articles were then obtained and assessed for eligibility by M.Sh. and M.J.D., with reasons for exclusion documented. Data from eligible studies were extracted using a standardized form, capturing study characteristics (author, year, country, study design, type of bariatric surgery, and follow-up duration), participant characteristics (age, body mass index [BMI] at baseline and, when available, at follow-up, fertility status, polycystic ovary syndrome [PCOS] status), and outcome measures (mean serum AMH levels before and after surgery, standard deviations, and sample sizes). Fertility status (fertile, infertile, or mixed) was extracted as reported by the original studies, based on their recruitment setting and/or explicit labeling of participants as fertile or infertile. No additional reclassification by the review authors was performed. AMH concentrations were extracted as reported in the original articles. If studies reported AMH levels at multiple post-surgery time points, the latest available measurement was used for standardization. To ensure comparability across studies, AMH values were harmonized to a common unit of ng/mL. For studies reporting AMH in pmol/L, we applied the conversion factor AMH (ng/mL) = AMH (pmol/L) ÷ 7.14. All pooled effect estimates are therefore expressed in ng/mL. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary (A.J.).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [32], which evaluates selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and ascertainment of exposure or outcome, with a maximum score of 9 points. Two independent reviewers (M.Sh. and M.J.D.) applied the NOS, with disagreements resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (A.J.). Studies scoring ≥ 8 points were classified as high quality, scores between 4 and 7 indicated medium quality, and scores below 4 were considered low quality. This classification informed subgroup analyses and helped identify potential sources of bias.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata software, version 17.0. All records retrieved from the databases were imported into EndNote (version 21.5; Clarivate) for reference management and initial duplicate removal. The screening and data extraction process were organized and tracked using Microsoft Excel. The mean difference in serum AMH levels (post-surgery minus pre-surgery) was calculated for each study as the effect size. If a study reported mean AMH levels pre- and post-surgery with standard deviations but did not provide the standard deviation of the difference, it was estimated using the formula: SD_difference = sqrt (SD_pre² + SD_post² − 2RSD_pre*SD_post), assuming a correlation coefficient (R) of 0.5 if not reported. The weighted mean difference (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was used as the effect size measure. Random-effects meta-analyses were then conducted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to estimate the between-study variance (τ²), which provides more robust and conservative uncertainty estimates than the traditional DerSimonian–Laird method, particularly with few studies. In a few small study arms, both baseline and follow-up SDs were reported as exactly zero, yielding a change SD and standard error of zero. Such zero-variance arms produce unrealistically large inverse-variance weights and artificially precise pooled estimates. To avoid this artifact, arms with SD = 0 at both time points were excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis but considered qualitatively in the narrative synthesis. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic; values above 50% indicate substantial heterogeneity and warrant further investigation through subgroup analyses.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity based on the following factors: PCOS status was categorized as “with PCOS,” “without PCOS,” or “mixed PCOS/non-PCOS populations” “Mixed PCOS/non‐PCOS populations” were defined as studies that enrolled both women with a clinical PCOS diagnosis and women without PCOS, based solely on author‐reported participant characteristics, without reporting separate stratified results; fertility status, studies were categorized as including predominantly fertile women, infertile women (e.g., recruited from infertility clinics or explicitly described as infertile), or mixed populations according to the original study definitions; age range (≤ 35 years, > 35 years, postmenopausal); mean age categories (< 30 years, 30–34.9 years, ≥ 35 years); BMI status (< 40 kg/m², 40–44.9 kg/m², ≥ 45 kg/m²); geographic region (Middle East, Europe, South Asia, North America); study design (prospective vs. retrospective); follow-up duration (≤ 26 weeks vs. >26 weeks); sample size (≤ 20 vs. >20 participants); AMH measurement method (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA] vs. chemiluminescent immunoassay [CLIA]); study quality (high vs. medium/low, based on Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [NOS] scores); type of bariatric surgery (laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy [LSG], Roux-en-Y gastric bypass [RYGB], mixed procedures); and baseline AMH levels (categorized as ≤ 3 ng/mL and > 3 ng/mL, based on clinical relevance and distribution of study data).

To further investigate the sources of heterogeneity and assess the influence of continuous moderators, meta-regression analysis was performed. This analysis examined the linear relationship between the effect size (change in AMH levels) and study-level covariates, including baseline AMH levels, mean age, baseline BMI, and follow-up duration. Meta-regression was conducted using a random-effects model to account for residual heterogeneity not explained by the moderators. Additionally, a non-linear dose-response meta-analysis was conducted to explore the potential non-linear relationship between baseline AMH levels and the change in AMH post-surgery.

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot asymmetry and Begg’s test. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the robustness and stability of the results in the presence of potential outliers.

Results

Study selection

The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. A comprehensive literature search across multiple databases identified a total of 359 studies, retrieved from PubMed (n = 48), ISI Web of Science (n = 81), Scopus (n = 103), Embase (n = 124), as well as Google Scholar and manual reference searches (n = 3). After removing 161 duplicate records, irrelevant publications, and animal studies, 198 studies underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 160 articles were excluded based on predefined eligibility criteria, leaving 38 full-text studies for further assessment. Subsequently, 24 studies were excluded due to the absence of relevant outcome measures, as detailed in Supplementary Table 2. Ultimately, 14 studies, encompassing a total of 591 participants, were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis.

Study characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The studies included in this analysis were published between 2008 and 2025 and were conducted across multiple countries, including Egypt [30], France [33], Greece [34], India [18, 35], Iraq [36], Italy [27, 37, 38], Russia [39], Spain [40], Sweden [28], Turkey [29], and the USA [23]. These studies were distributed across four major geographic regions: the Middle East [29, 30, 36], Europe [27, 28, 33, 34, 37–40], South Asia [18, 35], and North America [23]. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 45 years, and follow-up durations ranged from 9 to 104 weeks. Sample sizes ranged from 4 to 64 participants. Anti-Müllerian hormone levels were assessed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [18, 23, 27–30, 36] and chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) [33, 38, 40].

Overall effect of bariatric surgery on AMH levels

The meta-analysis, which incorporated multiple studies evaluating the impact of bariatric surgery on AMH levels, found no significant overall effect. The pooled WMD was 0.05 ng/mL (95% CI: -0.66, 0.75; p = 0.897), indicating that bariatric surgery did not result in a statistically significant reduction in AMH levels. However, the analysis revealed considerable heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 93.9%, p < 0.001), suggesting substantial variability in study findings (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analyses

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH by PCOS status

Subgroup analysis based on PCOS status revealed no statistically significant association between bariatric surgery and AMH levels in individuals with PCOS (WMD = -0.06 ng/mL, 95% CI: -2.71, 2.60; p = 0.967; I² = 85.6%, p < 0.001), in mixed PCOS/non-PCOS populations (WMD = -0.98 ng/mL, 95% CI: -2.01, 0.04; p = 0.060; I² = 76.0%, p = 0.002), or in non-PCOS populations (WMD = 0.89 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.43, 2.21; p = 0.185; I² = 89.9%, p < 0.001). Moreover, studies with unreported PCOS status also showed no significant association (WMD = 0.33 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.25, 0.91; p = 0.260; I² = 93.8%, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by fertility status

Based on subgroup analysis by fertility status, studies including fertile women showed a significant postoperative increase in AMH levels (WMD = 4.59 ng/mL, 95% CI: 2.87, 6.32; p < 0.001; I² = 0.0%). In contrast, no significant changes were observed in infertile individuals (WMD = 0.25 ng/mL, 95% CI: −0.77, 1.27; p = 0.628; I² = 91.5%, p < 0.001) or in studies where fertility status was not explicitly stated (WMD = − 0.37 ng/mL, 95% CI: −0.98, 0.25; p = 0.241; I² = 86.3%, p < 0.001).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by age range

Analysis by age range indicated a significant reduction in AMH levels among individuals with an upper age limit ≤ 35 years following bariatric surgery (WMD = -1.32 ng/mL, 95% CI: -2.24, -0.40; p = 0.005; I² = 81.4%, p = 0.001). However, no significant effect was observed in studies with an upper age limit > 35 years (WMD = 0.44 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.31, 1.19; p = 0.250; I² = 89.1%, p < 0.001).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by mean age

Stratification by mean age categories revealed no significant association between bariatric surgery and AMH levels in individuals aged 30–34.9 years (WMD = 0.11 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.48, 0.71; p = 0.707; I² = 91.0%, p < 0.001), or those younger than 30 years (WMD = 0.11 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.70, 1.92; p = 0.907; I² = 88.9%, p < 0.001).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by BMI status

Subgroup analysis based on BMI showed no significant impact of bariatric surgery on AMH levels among individuals with a BMI of 40–44.9 kg/m² (WMD = 0.07 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.87, 1.01; p = 0.888; I² = 94.2%, p < 0.001), those with a BMI ≥ 45 kg/m² (WMD = 0.44 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.02, 1.91; p = 0.554; I² = 79.9%, p < 0.001), or those with a BMI < 40 kg/m² (WMD = -0.68 ng/mL, 95% CI: -2.68, 1.33; p = 0.508; I² = 91.5%, p < 0.001).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by geographic region

The regional analysis identified a significant reduction in AMH levels among South Asian populations (WMD = -0.84 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.52, -0.15; p = 0.017; I² = 73.0%, p = 0.011). However, no significant effects were observed in studies conducted in Europe (WMD = -0.24 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.11, 0.63; p = 0.587; I² = 93.3%, p < 0.001), or the Middle East (WMD = 2.04 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.60, 4.67; p = 0.130; I² = 88.9%, p < 0.001).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by study design

Subgroup analysis by study design revealed no significant association in prospective studies (WMD = 0.27 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.75, 1.28; p = 0.610; I² = 94.9%, p < 0.001). In contrast, retrospective studies demonstrated a significant reduction in AMH levels following bariatric surgery (WMD = -0.29 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.57, -0.01; p = 0.042; I² = 33.9%, p = 0.182).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by follow-up duration

Studies with follow-up durations exceeding 26 weeks demonstrated a significant reduction in AMH levels (WMD = -0.81 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.56, -0.06; p = 0.034; I² = 71.6%, p < 0.001). Conversely, studies with follow-up durations of 26 weeks or less showed no significant effect (WMD = 0.64 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.34, 1.61; p = 0.201; I² = 95.0%, p < 0.001).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by sample size

Analysis based on sample size indicated no significant association when studies with more than 20 participants were considered (WMD = 0.39 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.66, 1.45; p = 0.466; I² = 95.7%, p < 0.001). In contrast, studies with 20 or fewer participants demonstrated a significant reduction in AMH levels following bariatric surgery (WMD = -0.46 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.89, -0.04; p = 0.033; I² = 34.3%, p = 0.166).

Effects of bariatric surgery on AMH change by measurement method

No significant association was observed when AMH levels were measured using chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) (WMD = 0.15 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.80, 2.10; p = 0.880; I² = 91.0%, p < 0.001), ELISA (WMD = -0.02 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.19, 1.15; p = 0.975; I² = 84.9%, p < 0.001), or unspecified/undisclosed assays (WMD = 0.33 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.23, 0.90; p = 0.249; I² = 89.8%, p < 0.001).

Effects of study quality on outcome

Studies with a quality score below 8 showed no significant effect on the outcome (WMD = 0.03 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.34, 1.40; p = 0.964; I² = 87.0%, p < 0.001). Similarly, studies with a quality score of 8 or higher demonstrated no significant effect (WMD = 0.08 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.80, 0.96; p = 0.862; I² = 94.6%, p < 0.001).

Effects of bariatric surgery type on AMH change

When categorized by surgical procedure, studies assessing mixed procedures showed no significant effect (WMD = -0.35 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.70, 0.00; p = 0.052; I² = 0.0%, p = 0.548), and studies investigating only LSG (WMD = 0.15 ng/mL, 95% CI: -0.58, 0.88; p = 0.684; I² = 95.8%, p < 0.001) or combined LSG/RYGB procedures (WMD = 1.12 ng/mL, 95% CI: -1.50, 3.73; p = 0.402; I² = 88.1%, p < 0.001) showed no significant effect.

Effects of baseline value on AMH change

Subgroup analysis was conducted to assess the influence of baseline AMH levels on changes in AMH following bariatric surgery, with studies categorized based on a baseline AMH threshold of 3 ng/mL. In studies where baseline AMH levels exceeded 3 ng/mL, a statistically significant decrease in AMH levels was observed post-surgery (WMD = -0.89 ng/mL; 95% CI: -1.54, -0.25; p = 0.007; I² = 79.1%, p < 0.001). In contrast, studies with baseline AMH levels ≤ 3 ng/mL showed a non-significant increase in AMH levels post-surgery (WMD = 1.04 ng/mL; 95% CI: -0.03, 2.10; p = 0.057; I² = 94.3%, p < 0.001).

Publication bias assessment

The funnel plot analysis did not reveal any substantial publication bias (Fig. 3). Furthermore, statistical assessments using Begg’s test (p = 0.059) indicated no small-study effects, reinforcing the robustness and reliability of the meta-analysis findings.

Funnel plot of the impact of bariatric surgery on AMH

Sensitivity analysis

Given the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to examine the stability of the pooled estimate. Sequential removal of individual studies did not materially alter the overall effect estimate, indicating that the finding was not disproportionately influenced by any single study.

Meta-regression and non-linear dose-response analysis

Meta-regression analysis was performed to assess the linear relationships between the effect sizes of AMH changes following bariatric surgery and key covariates: baseline AMH levels, baseline age, baseline BMI, and follow-up duration. A significant negative association was identified between baseline AMH levels and post-surgery AMH change (Coefficient = -0.55, p = 0.006), indicating that lower baseline AMH levels were associated with greater post-surgery AMH improvement. No significant linear relationships were observed for baseline age (Coefficient = 0.20, p = 0.614), baseline BMI (Coefficient = 0.49, p = 0.236), or follow-up duration (Coefficient = -2.52, p = 0.357) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

A non-linear dose-response regression model was applied to further explore the relationship between baseline AMH levels and AMH changes post-surgery. The analysis revealed a significant non-linear association (Coefficient = 6.15, p < 0.001), with the dose-response curve indicating a maximal gain in AMH at baseline levels of approximately 1.0–1.5 ng/mL, peaking at around 1.2 ng/mL (Supplementary Fig. 3). Above this range, the effect size declined, crossing zero at approximately 3 ng/mL and becoming negative thereafter. No significant non-linear associations were found for baseline age (Coefficient = -330.77, p = 0.178), baseline BMI (Coefficient = 1.28, p = 0.229), or follow-up duration (Coefficient = 9.52, p = 0.122).

Discussion

Summary of findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis represents the first comprehensive investigation into the effects of bariatric surgery on AMH levels in women, incorporating an extensive subgroup analysis to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. A total of 14 studies, encompassing 591 participants, were included in this evaluation. The overall analysis demonstrated no statistically significant effect of bariatric surgery on AMH levels (WMD = 0.05 ng/mL, 95% CI: −0.66, 0.75; p = 0.897), although substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I² = 93.9%, p < 0.001). However, subgroup analyses revealed that AMH responses varied according to population characteristics and study-specific parameters. When stratified by PCOS status, no significant postoperative changes in AMH levels were observed among women with PCOS, women without PCOS, mixed PCOS/non-PCOS populations, or studies with unreported PCOS status. In analyses stratified by fertility status, AMH levels increased significantly among fertile women, whereas no significant changes were observed among infertile women or in studies with unstated fertility status.

Age-stratified analysis indicated that bariatric surgery was associated with a significant decline in AMH levels in studies with an upper participant age limit of ≤ 35 years (WMD = − 1.32 ng/mL, 95% CI: −2.24, − 0.40; p = 0.005), whereas no significant association was observed in studies with an upper age limit > 35 years. When categorized by mean age or BMI, no statistically significant changes in AMH levels were observed across the assessed subgroups. Geographical differences were also evident, with studies conducted in South Asian populations reporting a significant reduction in AMH levels (WMD = − 0.84 ng/mL, 95% CI: −1.52, − 0.15; p = 0.017). In contrast, studies conducted in Europe and the Middle East did not identify significant effects. Methodological subgroup analyses further showed significant AMH reductions in retrospective studies (WMD = − 0.29 ng/mL, 95% CI: −0.57, − 0.01; p = 0.042), studies with follow-up durations > 26 weeks (WMD = − 0.81 ng/mL, 95% CI: −1.56, − 0.06; p = 0.034), and studies with sample sizes ≤ 20 participants (WMD = − 0.46 ng/mL, 95% CI: −0.89, − 0.04; p = 0.033). By contrast, no significant postoperative changes were observed when studies were stratified by AMH measurement method, study quality, or type of bariatric procedure.

Notably, subgroup analysis by baseline AMH levels highlighted differential responses. Studies with baseline AMH levels > 3 ng/mL showed a significant postoperative reduction in AMH (WMD = − 0.89 ng/mL, 95% CI: −1.54, − 0.25; p = 0.007; I² = 79.1%), whereas those with baseline AMH levels ≤ 3 ng/mL exhibited a non-significant tendency toward increased AMH (WMD = 1.04 ng/mL, 95% CI: −0.03, 2.10; p = 0.057; I² = 94.3%). These findings suggest that preoperative AMH concentration may be an important source of variability in postoperative AMH response. However, the substantial heterogeneity observed in both baseline AMH subgroups warrants cautious interpretation.

The meta-regression analysis demonstrated a significant negative linear relationship between baseline AMH and the magnitude of AMH change after bariatric surgery (coefficient = − 0.55; p = 0.006), suggesting that baseline AMH concentration may partly explain between-study variability in AMH response. No significant linear relationships were observed for baseline age, baseline BMI, or follow-up duration. The non-linear dose-response analysis further supported a significant non-linear association between baseline AMH and postoperative AMH change (coefficient = 6.15; p < 0.001), with the predicted AMH response peaking at baseline values of approximately 1.0–1.5 ng/mL, around 1.2 ng/mL, and crossing zero at approximately 3 ng/mL. No significant non-linear associations were found for baseline age, baseline BMI, or follow-up duration.

Taken together, these findings suggest that bariatric surgery is not associated with a consistent overall change in serum AMH levels, although postoperative AMH responses may vary across clinical and methodological contexts. The observed subgroup and meta-regression findings should be interpreted cautiously because they were accompanied by substantial heterogeneity and were derived from observational studies with variable sample sizes, follow-up durations, AMH assays, and participant characteristics. Importantly, AMH changes after bariatric surgery should be viewed primarily as dynamic endocrine biomarker responses rather than direct evidence of structural ovarian reserve depletion or improvement. Comparison with previous studies

AMH level

Our findings are consistent with those reported by Monteiro et al. [41], who conducted a prospective study involving 22 women of reproductive age with obesity. Their investigation revealed no statistically significant alterations in AMH levels six months after bariatric surgery despite a considerable reduction in body weight. Specifically, the median AMH level slightly declined from 3.02 ng/mL to 2.95 ng/mL, representing a non-significant decrease of 2.32% (p = 0.783) [41]. Consistent with these findings, Lofty Elshwaikh et al. [30] conducted a prospective cohort study involving women aged 18 to 40 years with severe obesity and a BMI ranging from 36 to 42 kg/m², were referred for bariatric surgery. The study assessed AMH levels before and six months after the procedure, revealing no statistically significant association (p = 0.322). These results align with the findings of the present study and those reported by Merhi et al. [23] and Mileno et al. [37], who observed no significant changes in AMH levels following bariatric surgery.

Conversely, Nilsson-Condori et al. assessed the impact of bariatric surgery on AMH levels in a cohort of 48 women. They documented a significant decline in AMH concentrations at both six and twelve months postoperatively, with median levels decreasing to 19.5 pmol/L and 18.0 pmol/L, respectively (p = 0.001). Similarly, Bhandari et al. conducted a study on 75 reproductive-age women undergoing bariatric surgery for morbid obesity. They reported a substantial postoperative reduction in AMH levels across both study groups, with a statistically significant decline (p < 0.001).

PCOS status

Our analysis examined changes in AMH levels following bariatric surgery across different PCOS groups and found no substantial postoperative alterations, aligning with previous studies [23, 27, 30, 33, 35–37, 39, 40]. In women with PCOS, AMH levels showed a slight decline, whereas non-PCOS individuals exhibited relatively stable levels with no notable changes after surgery. In studies where PCOS status was not explicitly reported, AMH levels fluctuated slightly but without a clear pattern. These findings suggest that bariatric surgery does not have a consistent impact on AMH levels across PCOS subgroups.

On the other hand, Nilsson-Condori et al. [28] reported a significant decline in AMH levels at six and twelve months post-surgery in 48 women who underwent bariatric surgery (p = 0.001) [28]. These findings align with those of Bhandari et al. [18], who similarly observed a marked reduction in AMH concentrations, particularly among participants with PCOS (p < 0.001) [18].

Fertility status

Our analysis evaluated the influence of bariatric surgery on AMH levels, considering differences in fertility status. Overall, no significant postoperative changes in AMH were observed across the study population. Among infertile women, findings were inconsistent, with some studies reporting stable AMH levels [23, 35, 37] while others noted a slight increase [34]. In contrast, studies examining fertile women indicated a postoperative increase in AMH concentrations. In cases where fertility status was not explicitly stated, AMH levels exhibited fluctuations without a discernible pattern.

Meanwhile, some studies, such as that of Buyukkaba et al. [29], documented a significant increase in AMH levels following bariatric surgery in fertile women. In contrast, Condori et al. [28] and Bhandari et al. [18] observed a significant decrease in AMH levels in populations with undefined fertility status. These findings suggest that postoperative AMH responses may vary across different fertility-status groups, although these AMH changes should not be interpreted as direct evidence of altered ovarian reserve or fertility potential.

Plausible mechanisms underlying AMH changes

One possible explanation relates to the improvement in insulin resistance and androgen excess after bariatric surgery. Chronic hyperinsulinemia, a common feature of obesity and PCOS, may influence ovarian AMH production by stimulating granulosa cells and upregulating AMH expression. Elevated AMH may inhibit aromatase activity, disrupt estrogen synthesis, and impair follicular maturation. Bariatric surgery may improve insulin resistance and reduce circulating insulin levels, thereby attenuating insulin-mediated stimulation of granulosa-cell AMH production [42–44]. Improved insulin sensitivity may also increase hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) synthesis, reduce free insulin-like growth factor (IGF) and androgen levels, and indirectly contribute to moderation of AMH concentrations [45, 46]. This pathway is particularly relevant in PCOS, where hyperandrogenemia can increase the pool of small antral follicles and may directly stimulate granulosa-cell AMH production [44, 47, 48]. Postoperative reductions in total and free testosterone may therefore reduce androgen-driven AMH secretion, consistent with clinical observations showing correlations between reductions in AMH and androgens after surgery [49, 50].

Adipose-derived endocrine signals may provide another explanatory link between bariatric surgery and AMH variation. Obesity is associated with reduced adiponectin and elevated leptin levels, both of which may disrupt ovarian endocrine function. Adiponectin receptors are expressed in granulosa cells, oocytes, and cumulus cells, and adiponectin may enhance insulin and gonadotropin responsiveness, regulate steroidogenesis, and counteract excessive androgen production by theca cells [51–53]. Post-bariatric weight loss increases adiponectin and reduces leptin levels, potentially creating a more favorable endocrine milieu for granulosa-cell function, folliculogenesis, and AMH modulation [47, 54, 55]. Ghrelin, which is suppressed after sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, may also influence reproductive tissues, although clinical studies have not demonstrated a strong direct correlation between postoperative ghrelin changes and AMH levels [56–59]. Thus, adipokine and gut-hormone changes may contribute to reproductive endocrine modulation, but their direct contribution to postoperative AMH changes remains uncertain.

Changes in AMH after bariatric surgery may also reflect altered granulosa-cell secretion and follicular dynamics rather than structural depletion of ovarian reserve. Through reductions in insulin resistance and androgen excess, bariatric surgery may decrease AMH secretion per follicle, with clinical studies reporting AMH reductions of approximately 20–35% within 6–12 months after surgery and, in one study, a one-third reduction at one year independent of the magnitude of weight loss [33]. Importantly, such declines should not necessarily be interpreted as ovarian reserve depletion. In women with PCOS, AMH is often elevated because numerous small antral follicles produce excessive AMH but fail to mature appropriately [25, 60–62]. Postoperative metabolic and hormonal improvement may partly alleviate this follicular arrest, with some studies reporting reductions in antral follicle count from polycystic to more physiologic ranges [63, 64]. For example, one PCOS-dominant cohort showed a decline in mean AFC from approximately 26 at baseline to 17 one year after bariatric surgery [63]. Therefore, postoperative AMH reductions in high-AMH or PCOS-dominant populations may represent normalization of aberrant follicular activity rather than loss of the primordial follicle pool.

The ovarian microenvironment may also improve after bariatric surgery through reductions in lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Obesity-related inflammation, local cytokine release, and advanced glycation end products may adversely affect follicular viability and ovarian function [65–68]. After bariatric surgery, systemic inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein may decline, while adiponectin-related anti-inflammatory effects may increase within ovarian tissues [69, 70]. Reduced adipose-derived estrone may also help restore gonadotropin balance and luteinizing hormone dynamics, thereby supporting more favorable reproductive endocrine function [71, 72]. Bariatric surgery may further improve PCOS-related endocrine dysfunction by enhancing insulin sensitivity, in some cases before substantial weight loss occurs [72, 73]. Caloric restriction and gut-hormone changes may reduce insulin levels within days to weeks, interrupting the insulin-androgen cycle that promotes ovarian theca-cell androgen production and suppresses SHBG [74–76]. These changes may reduce androgen production, lower LH-driven stimulation, and increase SHBG synthesis, with studies reporting significant declines in total testosterone and, in some cases, normalization of androgen levels after surgery. The resulting improvement in hyperandrogenemia may contribute to better follicular development and clinical improvements such as reduced acne and gradual attenuation of hirsutism [77, 78].

Bariatric surgery may also influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis and gut-reproductive hormone crosstalk. Obesity and PCOS are associated with altered gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulsatility, relative LH excess, insulin resistance, hyperleptinemia, androgen excess, and obesity-related hyperestrogenemia, all of which may contribute to impaired follicular maturation and anovulation [74, 75, 79]. Bariatric surgery may improve gonadotropin regulation by reducing insulin and androgen levels and modifying gut-hormone signaling, including glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) and peptide YY (PYY), which may influence central neuroendocrine pathways [80, 81]. It is also associated with reductions in inflammatory markers, including CRP, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), along with increases in anti-inflammatory adipokines such as adiponectin. These changes may support a more favorable ovarian steroidogenic environment, improve insulin sensitivity, and potentially affect endometrial function and implantation potential [82, 83]. Beyond these metabolic effects, bariatric surgery substantially alters gastrointestinal hormone profiles, increasing postprandial GLP-1 and PYY while suppressing ghrelin and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP). GLP-1 and PYY may influence GnRH neurons and gonadotropin release, while reduced ghrelin may further support ovulatory function; however, this gut-reproductive axis remains an indirect and incompletely established explanation in the context of AMH change [76, 84, 85].

These endocrine and metabolic changes may help explain why bariatric surgery is often associated with improved menstrual cyclicity and reproductive function, particularly in women with obesity-related anovulation or PCOS. By improving HPO-axis regulation, bariatric surgery may support more physiologic GnRH pulsatility and gonadotropin signaling, thereby facilitating ovulatory function [36, 86]. Previous meta-analytic evidence has reported a marked decline in menstrual irregularities after bariatric surgery, with anovulation rates decreasing from approximately 82% before surgery to 15% after surgery [80]. Improvements in insulin sensitivity, gut-hormone profiles, systemic inflammation, and androgen excess may further support follicular development, ovulation, and endometrial receptivity [87–90]. Nevertheless, because the present meta-analysis did not directly evaluate ovulation, pregnancy, live birth, miscarriage, or time to conception, fertility-related interpretations should remain indirect and hypothesis-generating.

Finally, part of the observed heterogeneity may reflect differences in AMH measurement and study populations rather than true biological divergence. Some studies measured AMH using ELISA, whereas others used CLIA or did not specify the assay method. These methods differ in analytical sensitivity and may produce variable AMH estimates, particularly at lower concentrations. In addition, studies were conducted across Europe, the Middle East, North America, and South Asia, where genetic background, lifestyle factors, obesity phenotype, PCOS prevalence, metabolic profiles, and baseline AMH distributions may differ [91, 92]. These variations reinforce the need for standardized AMH assays, consistent follow-up intervals, and concurrent assessment of reproductive and metabolic outcomes in future studies.

Strength limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis has several important strengths. The review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO, supporting methodological transparency and reducing the likelihood of selective reporting. It also provides a focused synthesis of the available evidence on the effect of bariatric surgery on AMH levels, an area in which individual studies have reported inconsistent findings. The use of subgroup analyses allowed AMH responses to be examined across clinically and methodologically relevant factors, including PCOS status, fertility status, baseline AMH level, age, BMI, geographic region, follow-up duration, surgical procedure, assay method, study design, and study quality. This stratified approach helped explore potential sources of variability and allowed a more clinically nuanced interpretation than the overall pooled estimate alone. In addition, the inclusion of studies from multiple regions, including the Middle East, Europe, South Asia, and North America, enhances the applicability of the findings across diverse clinical settings. The use of sensitivity analysis, meta-regression, and non-linear dose-response analysis further strengthened the assessment of heterogeneity, the robustness of the pooled estimate, and potential modifiers of postoperative AMH response.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this meta-analysis. A primary limitation is the substantial heterogeneity among included studies (I² = 93.9%, p < 0.001), which may reduce the precision and interpretability of the pooled estimates. This variability likely reflects clinical and methodological diversity, including differences in study design, sample size, follow-up duration, surgical technique, PCOS prevalence, baseline AMH levels, and AMH measurement methods. Uncontrolled confounders, such as lifestyle factors, concurrent medications, postoperative nutritional status, and baseline metabolic health, were also not uniformly reported and may have influenced AMH trajectories. In addition, we did not calculate a formal inter-reviewer agreement statistic, such as Cohen’s κ, which may limit the quantitative assessment of consistency between reviewers during screening and quality appraisal.

Although the subgroup analyses were pre-specified in our PROSPERO protocol (CRD420251020248), they should be considered exploratory because of the high heterogeneity observed in most subgroups, variation in subgroup sample sizes, and the observational nature of the included evidence. These analyses may help identify potential effect modifiers, such as fertility status, age, and baseline AMH levels, but they require confirmation in future studies using standardized methodologies. To account for heterogeneity, we used a random-effects model, conducted subgroup and sensitivity analyses, and performed meta-regression, which identified baseline AMH as a significant contributor to variability (coefficient = − 0.55, p = 0.006). Nevertheless, residual heterogeneity persisted, likely reflecting unmeasured differences in AMH assays, reproductive phenotype, metabolic status, comorbidities, and postoperative care.

Another important limitation is that AMH was the only ovarian biomarker consistently available for quantitative synthesis. The included studies did not uniformly report antral follicle count, follicle-stimulating hormone, ovulation, pregnancy, live birth, time to conception, or other clinically meaningful reproductive outcomes. Therefore, AMH changes in this meta-analysis should be interpreted as biomarker-level findings rather than direct evidence of altered ovarian reserve, fertility potential, or clinically meaningful ovarian compromise. In addition, although subgroup analyses were conducted according to baseline AMH categories, the available data did not allow a clinically validated threshold-based analysis of diminished ovarian reserve. This distinction is important because AMH is influenced not only by follicle number but also by metabolic, hormonal, and PCOS-related factors, including insulin resistance, androgen excess, adipokine alterations, and granulosa-cell function.

Finally, the mechanistic pathways discussed above should be considered hypothesis-generating. The included studies did not directly test the biological mechanisms linking bariatric surgery to AMH changes, and the available evidence does not allow firm conclusions about whether postoperative AMH changes reflect functional ovarian endocrine modulation, changes in follicular dynamics, or structural ovarian reserve changes. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized AMH assays, complementary reproductive markers, and clinically meaningful reproductive outcomes are needed to clarify the interpretation and clinical relevance of AMH changes after bariatric surgery.

Clinical implications

Patient counseling

Bariatric surgery may influence reproductive endocrine markers, including AMH, in variable ways, necessitating individualized patient counseling. Women considering weight-loss surgery should be informed that while the procedure generally enhances metabolic health and hormonal balance, its effects on AMH may vary according to individual factors, including age, baseline AMH level, fertility status, and PCOS status. For some patients, bariatric surgery may be associated with improved menstrual cyclicity and reproductive endocrine profiles, although AMH changes alone should not be considered definitive evidence of improved reproductive potential. Conversely, studies including younger women, particularly those with an upper age limit of ≤ 35 years, and studies conducted in South Asian populations demonstrated postoperative declines in AMH levels. These AMH declines should be interpreted cautiously and should not be used alone to infer reduced ovarian reserve or a shortened reproductive window. Rather, they may support individualized reproductive counseling when considered alongside age, menstrual pattern, fertility history, PCOS status, and complementary markers such as AFC and FSH when available.

Importantly, patients should be reassured that fluctuations in AMH levels do not necessarily correspond to immediate changes in fertility. For instance, women with PCOS may experience a decrease in previously elevated AMH levels as metabolic and hormonal parameters improve; however, such changes should be interpreted alongside clinical indicators such as menstrual regularity and ovulatory status. Overall, fertility responses to bariatric surgery are highly variable, necessitating thorough discussions about individual expectations and reproductive goals.

Additionally, it is critical to inform patients that fertility may return rapidly following bariatric surgery. Many women experience the resumption of regular menstrual cycles within weeks to months postoperatively, often before substantial weight loss has occurred. Women with obesity-related anovulation or PCOS may find themselves ovulating regularly and capable of conception shortly after surgery. Therefore, clinicians must provide comprehensive counseling regarding pregnancy timing and contraception. If immediate pregnancy is not desired, effective contraceptive measures should be emphasized to prevent unintended conception during this period of enhanced fertility. Conversely, for patients who wish to conceive, healthcare providers typically recommend delaying pregnancy for approximately 12 to 18 months post-surgery to allow for weight stabilization and optimal nutritional status, promoting a healthier pregnancy. Individualized counseling regarding conception timing is essential to ensuring favorable maternal and fetal outcomes.

Fertility management

The implications of bariatric surgery for fertility management and family planning should be integrated into comprehensive reproductive care strategies for patients with obesity. Preconception planning is essential, with individualized discussions regarding the optimal timing of pregnancy following surgery. For instance, women in their mid-30s with obesity may benefit metabolically and reproductively from bariatric surgery, particularly through improvements in weight, insulin resistance, menstrual cyclicity, and obesity-related anovulation. However, discussions about the timing of pregnancy should be individualized and guided by age, reproductive goals, menstrual history, PCOS status, nutritional status, and, when available, independent reproductive assessments such as AFC, FSH, and AMH interpreted in context. Patients with clinically concerning reproductive histories or abnormal complementary assessments, such as low AFC, abnormal FSH, persistent menstrual dysfunction, or infertility history, may benefit from referral to reproductive specialists for individualized counseling regarding conception timing and fertility options.

Fertility specialists should be actively involved in these discussions, helping patients evaluate assisted reproductive technologies (ART) such as in vitro fertilization (IVF) or egg freezing, considering their age and hormonal changes. Importantly, clinicians should emphasize that bariatric surgery frequently enhances fertility for individuals with obesity-related infertility. Weight loss can restore ovulatory function and improve natural conception rates. In anovulatory women, particularly those with PCOS, bariatric surgery has been associated with a marked reduction in anovulation rates, leading to the resumption of regular menstrual cycles in the majority of patients. This restoration of spontaneous ovulation may obviate the need for fertility interventions in some cases, warranting a reassessment of prior fertility management plans.

For patients who continue to require ART postoperatively, metabolic improvements following bariatric surgery may enhance treatment success. Weight loss and subsequent hormonal stabilization have been associated with improved ovarian response and endometrial receptivity, potentially increasing the success of ovulation induction and IVF. Emerging evidence suggests that infertile women with obesity who undergo bariatric surgery may experience better ART outcomes compared to their pre-surgical attempts. Consequently, bariatric surgery should be regarded as an adjunctive strategy in fertility management for appropriate candidates, not only promoting overall health but also fostering a more favorable reproductive environment. Clinicians should ensure multidisciplinary coordination of care, including optimizing nutrition, timing of reproductive interventions, and leveraging periods of improved fertility to maximize the likelihood of successful conception and pregnancy.

Reproductive health follow-up

Given the dynamic reproductive endocrine changes that may occur after bariatric surgery, reproductive health follow-up should be individualized rather than based on AMH changes alone. In routine post-bariatric care, clinicians should discuss menstrual patterns, pregnancy intentions, contraception, nutritional status, and symptoms suggestive of reproductive dysfunction. Additional reproductive assessments, such as AMH, FSH, or AFC, may be considered when clinically indicated, particularly in patients with infertility history, persistent menstrual irregularity, advanced reproductive age, or specific pregnancy-planning concerns. This approach avoids overinterpreting isolated AMH changes while still supporting appropriate reproductive counseling.

Reproductive health discussions should be incorporated into routine post-bariatric care for patients of reproductive age, with emphasis on pregnancy intentions, contraception, menstrual regularity, and the recommended timing of conception after surgery. Patients should be informed that fertility may improve after weight loss and metabolic improvement, and unintended pregnancy may occur if effective contraception is not used. At the research level, longer-term prospective studies are needed to determine whether postoperative AMH changes are associated with clinically meaningful reproductive outcomes.

In addition, standard post-bariatric care, including nutritional supplementation and metabolic monitoring, remains important for patients who may become pregnant. Particular attention should be given to micronutrients such as iron, folate, calcium, and vitamin D, because nutritional deficiencies may adversely affect pregnancy outcomes. This reinforces the need for coordinated post-bariatric and reproductive counseling rather than AMH-driven ovarian surveillance.

Future research directions

The substantial heterogeneity observed in our meta-analysis underscores the critical need for more comprehensive and standardized research investigating the complex relationship between bariatric surgery and AMH levels. Future studies should prioritize developing robust, longitudinal research designs that address the significant variability in current findings, particularly focusing on the nuanced interactions between surgical interventions and reproductive endocrine parameters.

Methodological refinement should be a primary focus for subsequent research. Future investigations should aim to establish uniform protocols for AMH measurement, including standardizing the timing of measurements, selecting consistent assay methods, and defining precise follow-up intervals. Given the significant variations observed across different measurement techniques and follow-up durations, developing a consensus on optimal research methodology would substantially enhance the comparability and reliability of future studies. Additionally, researchers should explore advanced molecular and endocrinological techniques to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying AMH level changes, potentially incorporating proteomics, metabolomics, and advanced hormonal profiling to provide deeper insights into the complex physiological responses to bariatric surgery.

The observed differential effects across various subpopulations necessitate more targeted research strategies. Future studies should design comprehensive investigations that systematically explore the impact of bariatric surgery on AMH levels across diverse demographic and clinical subgroups, including detailed analyses stratified by age ranges, polycystic ovary syndrome status, fertility profiles, and specific surgical techniques. Particularly, the notable variations were observed in different age groups and geographic regions. Genetic polymorphism studies and personalized medicine approaches could provide valuable insights into individual variability in hormonal responses to surgical interventions.

A critical avenue for future research involves expanding the scope of investigation beyond AMH levels to comprehensively assess reproductive and metabolic outcomes. Future studies should incorporate complementary ovarian reserve and reproductive parameters, including AFC, FSH, ovulation, pregnancy, live birth, time to conception, and menstrual regularity, to clarify whether AMH changes translate into clinically meaningful reproductive outcomes. Longitudinal studies should integrate multiple endocrine markers, fertility parameters, and long-term reproductive health outcomes to develop a more holistic understanding of bariatric surgery’s impact on women’s reproductive potential. This should include prospective studies with extended follow-up periods, detailed assessment of hormonal dynamics, and comprehensive evaluation of fertility outcomes, potentially incorporating advanced reproductive technologies and detailed clinical assessments.

The geographic and methodological limitations identified in our current meta-analysis present clear opportunities for future research. Multicenter international collaborations could help address the current geographic disparities, particularly the limited representation from certain regions. Such collaborative efforts should focus on developing standardized research protocols, creating comprehensive databases, and implementing rigorous quality control measures. Moreover, researchers should design studies that explicitly address current knowledge gaps, including investigating the potential mechanisms underlying changes in AMH levels, exploring the long-term reproductive implications of bariatric surgery, and developing predictive models to help clinicians better understand and manage the reproductive consequences of surgical interventions.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that bariatric surgery was not associated with a significant overall change in serum AMH levels in women. However, subgroup and meta-regression analyses suggested that postoperative AMH responses may vary according to baseline AMH concentration, age-related eligibility criteria, follow-up duration, study design, sample size, and geographic region. In particular, studies with higher baseline AMH levels (> 3 ng/mL) showed significant AMH reductions, whereas those with lower baseline AMH levels (≤ 3 ng/mL) showed a non-significant tendency toward increased AMH. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because AMH is influenced by metabolic and hormonal factors and because the available evidence did not consistently include AFC, FSH, ovulation, pregnancy, live birth, or other reproductive outcomes. Future well-designed prospective studies with standardized AMH assays, complementary reproductive markers, and long-term fertility outcomes are needed to clarify the clinical significance of AMH changes after bariatric surgery.
