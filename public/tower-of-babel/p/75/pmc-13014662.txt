Prostatic malakoplakia mimicking prostate cancer in an elderly male with prostatitis: A rare but important differential
Authors: Marco Rosario, Darshan Sitharthan, Warick Delprado, Jonathan Kam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the case of an 81-year-old male presenting with lower urinary tract symptoms and a PSA of 39 ng/mL, initially attributed to Escherichia coli prostatitis. Despite PSA normalization with antibiotics, MRI demonstrated a PIRADS 5 lesion in the left transition zone. Histopathological evaluation following transperineal biopsy revealed prostatic malakoplakia. This rare granulomatous condition can mimic prostate cancer both clinically and radiologically. Our case highlights the importance of histological confirmation in atypical presentations to avoid misdiagnosis and overtreatment.Keywords: Prostatic malakoplakia, Prostatitis, PIRADS 5, PSA, Prostate cancer mimic, Transperineal biopsy

Introduction

Malakoplakia is a rare granulomatous inflammatory condition that commonly affects the genitourinary tract, but prostatic involvement is uncommon and often misdiagnosed as malignancy due to its imaging features and elevated PSA levels. It is strongly associated with chronic infection, especially with gram-negative bacteria like Escherichia coli.1 Characterized histologically by the presence of Michaelis-Gutmann bodies, malakoplakia may mimic prostate cancer on digital rectal exam (DRE), multiparametric MRI, and serum PSA levels.2,3 We present a case of prostatic malakoplakia that radiologically appeared as a PIRADS 5 lesion, emphasizing the importance of biopsy for diagnostic clarity.

Case presentation

An 81-year-old man presented with mixed lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). Initial PSA was markedly elevated at 39 ng/mL in the setting of a urinary tract infection caused by E. coli. He was treated with Augmentin, with gradual symptomatic improvement.

Past medical history included ischemic heart disease (with a coronary stent and pacemaker), hypertension (on prazosin), long-term testosterone replacement therapy, a 25-pack-year smoking history, and use of aspirin.

Initial ultrasound revealed an enlarged prostate measuring 67 mL, with a post-void residual of 40 mL and no signs of hydronephrosis. He had preserved renal function on biochemistry. His International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) was 15 with a quality-of-life score of 3. PSA levels continued to trend downward; 1 month later- 7.2 ng/mL and 2.3 ng/mL 3 months later.

Despite normalization of PSA, he had an abnormal DRE and thus a multiparametric MRI was performed, which revealed a PIRADS 5 lesion measuring 15 × 12 × 24mm in the left transition zone (Fig. 1), with the prostate itself equating to 35 cc in total volume. Given the imaging findings, a transperineal biopsy was performed under antibiotic cover with cephalexin based on asymptomatic E. Coli growth in his most recent urine culture.

Histopathology

Microscopic examination demonstrated sheets of histiocytes with eosinophilic cytoplasm and pathognomonic Michaelis-Gutmann bodies — round, basophilic, targetoid inclusions within macrophages — consistent with a diagnosis of prostatic malakoplakia (Fig. 2). No evidence of carcinoma was observed.

The patient remained clinically well and asymptomatic from a urinary standpoint. A repeat urine culture continued to show E. coli with white cells, confirming the persistence of subclinical infection. He was placed on a prolonged course of antibiotics to clear the infection.

Discussion

Malakoplakia was first described in 1902 and is thought to result from defective lysosomal digestion of bacteria within macrophages, particularly in immunocompromised or chronically infected individuals.1 Prostatic malakoplakia is exceedingly rare, with most cases misidentified as carcinoma on imaging.2

Multiparametric MRI is a cornerstone in prostate cancer diagnosis, but inflammatory and infectious conditions such as malakoplakia can lead to false-positive PIRADS scores.4,5 In this case, a PIRADS 5 lesion prompted concern despite normalized PSA and clinical improvement. Biopsy proved essential for diagnostic clarification.

Histologically, malakoplakia is characterized by the presence of Michaelis-Gutmann bodies, which are partially mineralized remnants of bacterial degradation within histiocytes.3 Special stains such as von Kossa and Prussian blue can assist in their identification.

Treatment typically involves prolonged, targeted antibiotics. In this case, the patient was managed conservatively without need for definitive surgical intervention.

Conclusion

Prostatic malakoplakia, although rare, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of elderly men presenting with elevated PSA and abnormal imaging, especially in the setting of recurrent UTIs. Biopsy remains essential for distinguishing this benign inflammatory condition from prostate cancer and avoiding unnecessary oncologic treatment.
