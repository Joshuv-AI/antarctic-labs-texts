Cost-Effectiveness of Colonoscopy for Screening: What Matters Most?
Authors: Fumiaki Ishibashi, Sho Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Worldwide, population-based systematic screening programs for the disease, which is essential to address this burden, rely primarily on fecal immunochemical testing (FIT). However, this test has multiple limitations, including an inability to detect right-sided CRCs and low individual adherence to follow-up colonoscopy among those with positive FIT results. Thus, primary colonoscopy screening is currently being explored as a more reliable strategy. Although randomized controlled trials published to date have not conclusively demonstrated beneficial effects of colonoscopy screening on mortality, limited evidence has suggested that improved participation rates in such programs may increase their effectiveness. That said, the evaluation of cost-effectiveness is another critical consideration when assessing these programs. To date, 11 simulation studies have compared the cost-effectiveness of colonoscopy-based screening with alternative strategies, of which six found that the colonoscopy-based screening strategy to be more cost-effective. Notably, among seven studies that assessed strategies based on repeat colonoscopies at regular intervals, five demonstrated favorable cost-effectiveness. However, among the 11 simulation studies, only five modeled the adenoma–carcinoma sequence, and only three accounted for interval cancers, limiting their clinical applicability. Moreover, repeated colonoscopy screening may increase the total number of procedures, potentially straining healthcare resources. Thus, in addition to evaluating mortality reduction, careful consideration of cost-effectiveness is essential in assessing the feasibility of implementing colonoscopy screening programs in public health practice.Keywords: Colonoscopy, Screening, Colorectal neoplasms, Cost-effectiveness analysis

INTRODUCTION

According to GLOBOCAN (Global Cancer Observatory) data, colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide.1 The burden of CRC affects not only the elderly but also the working-age population, making its prevention and control an important public health priority.2 Consequently, several countries have implemented population-based CRC screening programs organized by national or local governments, with the strategic goal of reducing mortality from CRC.3

In principle, the implementation of such public health policies requires strategies that maximize population-level benefits, minimize potential harms, and demonstrate favorable cost-effectiveness. However, in many countries where CRC screening is conducted, cost-effectiveness has not been thoroughly considered prior to the formulation of screening strategies. This issue is particularly relevant for colonoscopy-based screening strategies, which have recently attracted increasing attention for their feasibility. Compared with fecal immunochemical testing (FIT), which has traditionally been the mainstay of screening, colonoscopy involves substantially higher per-test costs, necessitating careful evaluation of its cost-effectiveness.

In this review, we provide an overview of the CRC screening strategies using FIT or colonoscopy and summarize the current evidence regarding the cost-effectiveness of the colonoscopy-based screening.

EFFICACY AND LIMITATIONS OF POPULATION-BASED CRC SCREENING USING FIT

The primary objective of CRC screening is to reduce CRC-related mortality. Among various modalities, FIT is currently the most widely implemented strategy. Repeated use of FIT enhances cumulative sensitivity, and given its low cost, FIT is commonly conducted annually or biennially in organized screening programs.4 For quantitative FIT, a hemoglobin concentration threshold can be set, allowing adjustment of the sensitivity–specificity balance. This is particularly important to avoid overburdening colonoscopy resources for follow-up examinations after positive FIT results.

The effectiveness of FIT in population-based screening has been well demonstrated in various studies. In a prospective cohort study of more than one million individuals participating in a nationwide FIT-based CRC screening program in Taiwan, the relative rate (RR) of CRC mortality was reduced to 0.38 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.35 to 0.42), indicating substantial effectiveness.5 In the same program, a follow-up period of up to 17 years showed that long-term FIT screening reduced CRC mortality by 40% (adjusted RR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.57 to 0.64).6

Despite this high effectiveness, FIT has certain limitations. First, its sensitivity for CRC in the right-sided colon is relatively low, because the longer intestinal transit time promotes degradation of hemoglobin. In the large Taiwanese cohort study, the FIT-based screening program significantly reduced mortality from left-sided CRC, but the effect was limited for right-sided CRC.6 Similarly, a nested case-control study of over two million adults showed that the FIT-based screening program reduced mortality from left-sided CRC (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.58; 95% CI, 0.48 to 0.71), but not from right-sided CRC (adjusted OR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.69 to 1.01).7 Moreover, because the sensitivity of a single FIT round is limited, repeated screening at annual or biennial intervals is essential. Consequently, program effectiveness declines if adherence decreases. A microsimulation study varying adherence from 0% to 100% showed that annual FIT with complete adherence (100%) reduced CRC mortality by approximately 76.3%, whereas effectiveness dropped to 63.1% at 50% adherence.8

MORTALITY REDUCTION EFFECT OF COLONOSCOPY-BASED CRC SCREENING

The ultimate goal of cancer screening is to reduce cancer-related mortality. In the case of CRC, the detection and removal of adenomas are essential indicators because they have been shown to lower the future risk of CRC-related death.9 However, these are not the final objectives themselves. To evaluate the effectiveness of a screening modality, it is essential to objectively measure or estimate the extent to which it reduces CRC mortality.

Given the limitations of the FIT-based screening program, the strategy of using colonoscopy as a primary screening tool has been explored. The NordICC study, conducted in Norway, Poland, Sweden, and the Netherlands, was the first randomized controlled trial (RCT) to evaluate whether population-based invitation to colonoscopy screening reduces CRC incidence and mortality.10 In the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, colonoscopy screening significantly reduced the cumulative incidence of CRC (RR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.70 to 0.93), but did not significantly lower CRC mortality at 10 years (RR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.64 to 1.16). This lack of mortality benefit was mainly attributable to the low participation rate, with only 42% of individuals in the invited group undergoing colonoscopy. In the per-protocol analysis, which included only those who actually underwent colonoscopy, CRC mortality was reduced by approximately 50%, highlighting that, similar to FIT screening, ensuring high participation is crucial for the population-level effectiveness of colonoscopy screening.

The COLONPREV trial, conducted in Spain, was a non-inferiority RCT comparing the effectiveness of FIT versus colonoscopy in reducing CRC mortality.11 At 10 years, ITT analysis showed CRC mortality of 0.22% in the colonoscopy group and 0.24% in the FIT group, with an absolute risk difference of –0.02%, confirming the non-inferiority of FIT-based screening against colonoscopy-based screening. However, the participation rate for the FIT group was higher than that for the colonoscopy group (39.9% and 31.8%, respectively), potentially affecting the results. The CONFIRM trial in the United States and the Akita pop-colon trial in Japan are designed to test the superiority of colonoscopy over FIT; however, final results have not yet been published.

Thus, there is no definitive evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of colonoscopy-based screening in reducing CRC mortality. Nonetheless, colonoscopy remains a highly attractive option due to its high diagnostic accuracy and lower miss rate compared with other modalities. The final results of ongoing RCTs are warranted.

COST-EFFECTIVENESS OF COLONOSCOPY-BASED CRC SCREENING

Alongside establishing the effectiveness of colonoscopy as a screening modality for CRC, estimating its cost-effectiveness is of critical importance. Various simulation models have been employed for this purpose. Macrosimulation models calculate cost-effectiveness by applying different strategies to a hypothetical cohort, summing overall costs and health outcomes. Such models, often based on Markov state-transition frameworks, assign transition probabilities between health states and allow relatively simple model construction. In contrast, microsimulation models simulate outcomes at the individual level under complex assumptions, tracking detailed endpoints such as cancer-related mortality. The limitation of microsimulation lies in its difficulty in visualizing aggregate costs and benefits for an entire cohort. Thus, balanced consideration of both approaches is necessary for informed policy decision-making.12

To date, 11 studies have compared the cost-effectiveness of colonoscopy-based screening with alternative strategies (Table 1).13-23 Among these, seven used macrosimulation models and four employed microsimulation models. Reports span diverse regions, including Japan, China, Thailand, Kuwait, France, Germany, and the United States. These reports analyzed the cost-effectiveness of colonoscopy-based screening over alternative screening strategies in terms of incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs). ICER is calculated using the difference in costs and effectiveness, generally quantified by quality-adjusted life year, of the two targeted strategies. Although more costly, a colonoscopy-based strategy can remain cost-effective if greater effectiveness lowers the ICER. Of these 11 studies, six concluded that colonoscopy alone was the most cost-effective screening strategy.

When focusing on screening frequency, five of seven studies evaluating strategies based on repeated colonoscopy concluded that colonoscopy-based screening was highly cost-effective. In contrast, among four studies assessing a single-colonoscopy strategy, only one favored a colonoscopy-based screening strategy. However, in this study, the comparator FIT strategy was also limited to a single test.18 The question of whether colonoscopy should be performed once or at regular intervals has been specifically investigated. One macrosimulation study using a Markov model reported that a single colonoscopy achieved nearly the same reduction in CRC incidence as repeated colonoscopy (65.8% vs 67.2%), with superior cost-effectiveness for the single-colonoscopy strategy.24 However, given the inherent limitations of macrosimulation, further multifaceted analyses are required to enhance external validity and to assess individual-level benefits. A meta-analysis integrating 56 studies comprehensively evaluated colonoscopy-based screening strategies from the perspective of net monetary benefit, demonstrating that annual FIT or colonoscopy every 10 years were cost-saving strategies.25

The detailed model settings should be carefully compared to draw conclusions (Table 2).13-23 Most studies applied a time horizon of 20 to 35 life years; however, one study adopted a very short horizon of 10 life years, limiting its clinical applicability. Regarding discount rates, all studies except two microsimulation models used 3%, 3.5%, or 5% per cycle, which is generally acceptable in modeling. Although incorporating the adenoma–carcinoma sequence is critical for simulating the clinical trajectory of patients, only five of eight macrosimulation studies explicitly modeled this sequence, and of these, only three accounted for interval cancers. Lack of incorporating quality metrics, such as adenoma detection rate, also affects the results of analyses. Additionally, only four of 11 studies included complications associated with colonoscopy and treatment. Furthermore, colonoscopy costs varied widely across studies. In particular, studies from China and France reported an extremely high cost for colonoscopy, while three studies from Japan reported the lowest cost (Table 1), which reduces the generalizability of the simulation results. The primary reason why these studies did not demonstrate a decrease in the cost-effectiveness of the colonoscopy-based strategy is largely attributable to the high cost of colonoscopy, and it is also necessary to consider the influence of the regional healthcare system. Therefore, given that each study has limitations inherent to its respective model, a collective interpretation of the findings is necessary when applying the results to real-world clinical practice.

Colonoscopy also enables risk stratification at the time of initial screening. Individuals with high-risk adenomas, dysplasia, or CRC are identified as being at elevated risk for subsequent cancer development, necessitating tighter surveillance to detect metachronous cancers. A cost-effectiveness analysis of surveillance strategies following baseline colonoscopy showed that high-intensity surveillance—performing colonoscopy 5 years after low-grade adenoma resection and 3 years after high-grade adenoma resection—was more cost-effective than low-intensity surveillance with longer intervals.26 Similarly, a simulation study stratifying patients into average-risk and high-risk groups found that colonoscopy every 10 years was optimal for average-risk individuals, while 3-year intervals were most cost-effective for high-risk patients.27

One microsimulation study evaluated a strategy incorporating multi-target stool DNA testing; however, because this test was considerably more expensive than FIT (236.88 USD vs 14.34 USD) and ultimately required subsequent colonoscopy, its cost-effectiveness was lower than that of the colonoscopy- and FIT-based strategies.20 If the costs of these evolving modalities decrease in the future, the results of cost-effectiveness analyses may change.

As noted, macrosimulation models have limitations in accommodating complex individual-level heterogeneity. A microsimulation study that incorporated competing causes of death and stratified populations by CRC risk concluded that a single colonoscopy at age 60 was optimal for low-risk individuals, whereas 5-yearly colonoscopies from ages 40 to 85 were optimal for high-risk individuals.28

BEYOND THE COST-EFFECTIVENESS

Overall, in terms of pure cost-effectiveness, colonoscopy-based strategies—particularly those involving repeated screening—have often been shown to outperform alternative strategies. However, repeated colonoscopy inevitably increases the total number of procedures required across a cohort.29 Indeed, Sekiguchi et al.21 reported that while colonoscopy was the most cost-effective strategy, the required number of colonoscopies was more than double that of other approaches. Beyond financial costs, resource burden on healthcare personnel and facilities must also be considered, particularly in settings with limited capacity. Potential harms associated with an increased number of colonoscopies must also be taken into account. A microsimulation study by Buskermolen et al.,30 although not primarily a cost-effectiveness analysis, provided an essential comparison of mortality reduction and harms across strategies. This study demonstrated that colonoscopy achieved greater mortality reduction than FIT or sigmoidoscopy, while the rates of harms—including perforation, bleeding, gastrointestinal complications, and cardiovascular events—were similar across modalities.

CONCLUSIONS

Colonoscopy-based screening is a promising strategy to efficiently detect CRC, although its effectiveness in reducing CRC incidence and mortality has yet to be shown in RCTs. Several studies suggest that it is superior in terms of cost-effectiveness compared to alternative strategies, which potentially justifies its implementation as a population-based screening policy. Nevertheless, repeated colonoscopies increase procedural volume, resource demands, and potential harms, which must be balanced against their benefits. Policy decisions should therefore consider not only economic efficiency but also feasibility, equity, and healthcare capacity. Future research, utilizing robust simulation models and real-world data, is essential to optimize screening strategies tailored to population risk profiles.
