Comparison of accuracy between Navbit Sprint and AR-CT-based navigation in total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Yohei Naito, Shine Tone, Gai Kobayashi, Masahiro Hasegawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A portable navigation system that constructs a three-dimensional coordinate system by laterally tilting the operating table without anterior superior iliac spine registration has been introduced for total hip arthroplasty (THA). This study compared the accuracy of intraoperative navigation values in THA between a novel portable navigation system and an augmented reality (AR)-computed tomography (CT)-based navigation system. Thirty-four primary cementless THAs were retrospectively analyzed. All procedures were performed using an anterolateral approach in the supine position. Using AR-CT-based navigation, coronal pelvic tilt was adjusted to 0°, and sagittal and axial tilts were recorded. Table tilting was then performed to complete portable navigation registration. Acetabular cups were implanted using AR-CT-based navigation, and intraoperative measurements were obtained using the portable system. Postoperative CT measured radiographic inclination (RI) and anteversion (RA). Navigation error was defined as the absolute difference between intraoperative measurements and postoperative CT values. The association between portable navigation error and pelvic tilt at registration was also examined. Mean postoperative RI and RA were 39.3° ± 3.1° and 15.4° ± 3.1°, respectively, with all components within the Lewinnek safe zone. Mean navigation errors for AR-CT-based navigation were 1.9° ± 1.2° (inclination) and 1.9° ± 1.4° (anteversion). Portable navigation showed higher errors: 2.6° ± 2.7° (inclination) and 4.3° ± 2.9° (anteversion). Anteversion error was significantly greater with portable navigation and positively correlated with axial pelvic tilt at registration. Portable navigation showed lower anteversion accuracy than AR-CT-based navigation. Axial pelvic tilt at registration may affect anteversion accuracy with portable navigation.

Introduction

Favorable accuracy of acetabular cup placement in total hip arthroplasty (THA) using portable navigation systems has been reported, and most systems establish a three-dimensional (3D) coordinate system by registering both anterior superior iliac spines (ASISs) and the gravitational axis1–9.

Navbit Sprint (Navbit Pty Ltd., Sydney, Australia) is a newly developed portable navigation system that adopts a table tilt registration method and constructs a three-dimensional coordinate system composed of a table rotational axis, an axis perpendicular to the rotational axis, and the gravitational axis, without ASIS registration10. However, because the pelvic orientation is determined at the time of table tilt registration, variations in pelvic tilt may influence navigation accuracy.

HoloNavi One (HoloNavi Medical Technology Inc., Ichinomiya, Japan) is an augmented reality (AR)- and computed tomography (CT)-based navigation system. Preoperative CT images were obtained from the pelvis to the knee joint using a multi-slice CT scanner and a 3D templating system (ZedHip; Lexi, Tokyo, Japan). HoloNavi One has been shown to achieve high accuracy in acetabular cup placement and to allow intraoperative measurement of pelvic tilt in the coronal, sagittal, and axial planes2,11,12.

We hypothesized that the accuracy of intraoperative navigation values is higher with HoloNavi One than with Navbit Sprint, and that pelvic tilt at the time of table tilt registration influences the accuracy of Navbit Sprint. This study compared the accuracy of intraoperative navigation values between the two systems and evaluated the effect of pelvic tilt at registration on the accuracy of the Navbit Sprint.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Mie University Graduate School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (Reference: H2018-083). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. For H2018-083, which covered the review of clinical outcomes using existing clinical data, the Mie University Graduate School of Medicine Institutional Review Board waived the requirement for written informed consent, and both participation and publication of anonymized data and images were performed under an opt-out policy.

Thirty-four primary cementless total hip arthroplasties performed using both Navbit Sprint and HoloNavi One were retrospectively reviewed. The cohort consisted of 6 men and 28 women, with a mean age of 66 years and a mean body mass index of 24 kg/m². The diagnoses included osteoarthritis in 31 hips (Crowe group 1, 30; group 2, 113; osteonecrosis of the femoral head in 2 hips; and a femoral neck fracture in 1 hip (Table 1). All procedures were performed using an anterolateral approach with the patient in the supine position. All the hips received the R3 acetabular system (Smith and Nephew).

For the HoloNavi One, the reference marker was fixed to the iliac crest using two fixation pins (3.2 mm in diameter) (Fig. 1a). The anterior pelvic plane (APP) was registered by pointing at the bilateral anterior superior iliac spine (ASISs) and pubic tubercle(s), with the probe in the supine position. After exposing the acetabulum, the initial paired-point matching was performed by registering three points within the acetabulum. Subsequently, surface matching was performed by palpating approximately 30 points in the intra-acetabular region. For pelvic tilt measurement, the coordinate system of the reference marker attached to the operating table was identical to the 3D coordinate system defined by the table’s longitudinal axis, transverse axis, and vertical axis. By simultaneously recognizing both the pelvic reference marker and the operating table reference marker, pelvic tilt within this coordinate system could be continuously measured. Coronal tilt was defined as 0° when the bilateral ASISs were parallel to the transverse axis of the operating table, whereas axial tilt was defined as 0° when the bilateral ASISs were parallel to the horizontal axis of the operating table. Sagittal tilt was displayed relative to the functional pelvic plane (FPP), and its initial value was preoperatively measured using ZedHip. The FPP was defined as the horizontal plane of the functional pelvic zero position, in which the pelvis in the supine position on the CT scan table was axially rotated until the bilateral ASISs were aligned in the same horizontal plane14.

For the Navbit Sprint, the navigation unit was rigidly fixed to the pelvis using a pelvic mount, with an additional 3.2-mm percutaneous pin placed adjacent to the pins used to fix the reference marker HoloNavi One (Fig. 1a). Registration was performed using the table tilt method. First, by using the operating table at a level position, the gravitational axis was acquired to define the vertical reference. Subsequently, the operating table was tilted laterally by approximately 10° to each side in the supine position, and the rotational axis of the operating table was recorded as the table rotational axis (Fig. 1b). Using these two measured axes, the gravitational axis and table rotational axis, the third axis perpendicular to the rotational axis was mathematically calculated, thereby defining a 3D coordinate system. In the present study, the coronal pelvic tilt was manually adjusted to 0° during table tilt registration using HoloNavi One. The sagittal and axial pelvic tilts at the time of registration were recorded simultaneously (Fig. 1c). To evaluate the effect of pelvic deviation from the functional pelvic zero position during Navbit Sprint registration on navigation accuracy, axial and sagittal pelvic tilt deviations were analyzed. For axial tilt, because the functional pelvic zero position was defined as 0°, the intraoperative axial tilt value displayed during registration was directly used as the deviation from the zero position. For sagittal tilt, because the initial displayed value represented the functional pelvic zero position, deviation from the zero position was calculated as the difference between the sagittal tilt value displayed during registration and the initial sagittal tilt value.

After registration, the HoloNavi One reference marker was attached to the acetabular cup holder after pelvic and acetabular registration. Acetabular cup implantation was performed with reference to the FPP. Radiographic inclination (RI) and anteversion (RA) were displayed during cup insertion, and the target angles were set at 40° and 15°, respectively. The cup was implanted according to the planned values using the HoloNavi One (Fig. 2a).

After acetabular cup implantation, the HoloNavi One reference marker was removed from the acetabular cup holder. The navigation unit of the Navbit Sprint was then attached to the acetabular cup holder, and the RI and RA were measured with reference to the FPP (Fig. 2b).

Postoperative CT was performed two weeks after surgery, and the RI and RA were measured using ZedHip. The absolute differences between the postoperative CT measurements and the angles displayed intraoperatively by the HoloNavi One and Navbit Sprint were defined as navigation errors.

Based on previous data, the difference in cup anteversion accuracy (mean ± standard deviation) between the CT-based navigation group and the accelerometer-based navigation group was 3.8 ± 3.6°15. The power calculation indicated that 15 cases would be necessary for the study (α = 0.05, power = 0.8). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess the intra- and interobserver reliability. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare paired samples. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were used to assess the correlations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The mean postoperative RI and RA were 39.3° ± 3.1° and 15.4° ± 3.1°, respectively, and all the cups were positioned within the Lewinnek safe zone. The intraobserver reliability of postoperative cup orientation measurements was excellent, with ICCs of 0.963 for RI and 0.951 for RA. The interobserver reliability was also excellent, with an ICC of 0.942 for RI and 0.934 for RA. Navigation errors for HoloNavi One were 1.9° ± 1.2° for inclination and 1.9° ± 1.4° for anteversion, whereas those for Navbit Sprint were 2.6° ± 2.7° and 4.3° ± 2.9°, respectively. There was no significant difference in inclination error between the two navigation systems. In contrast, the anteversion error of Navbit Sprint was significantly larger than that of HoloNavi One (Table 2). HoloNavi One demonstrated no cases with navigation error > 5° in either inclination or anteversion. In contrast, Navbit Sprint showed 3 cases (8.8%) with inclination error > 5°, including 1 case (2.9%) > 10°, and 9 cases (26.5%) with anteversion error > 5°, including 1 case (2.9%) > 10° (Table 3).

The mean sagittal tilt at registration of Navbit Sprint was 0.1° ± 1.1° posterior (absolute value, 0.8° ± 0.7°), and the mean axial tilt was 3.2° ± 2.3° external rotation (absolute value, 3.4° ± 2.2°). A significant positive correlation was observed between the anteversion error of the Navbit Sprint and the absolute value of the axial tilt (R = 0.556, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The main findings of this study were that the anteversion error of the Navbit Sprint, defined as the difference between intraoperative navigation values and postoperative CT measurements, was significantly greater than that of the HoloNavi One, and that axial pelvic tilt at registration adversely affected the accuracy of the Navbit Sprint.

In THA, cup orientation should be defined within a 3D coordinate system composed of the axis connecting the ASISs, the gravitational axis, and a third axis orthogonal to these two axes. With table-tilt-based registration, however, the coordinate system is constructed from the axes of table rotation, the gravitational axis, and a third orthogonal axis. Therefore, if the ASIS axis in the coordinate system does not coincide with that represented in the table tilt-derived coordinate system, accurate cup placement may not be reliably achieved.

Tetsunaga et al. reported that the mean absolute navigation errors of the Navbit Sprint were 2.1° and 2.0° for inclination and anteversion, respectively, in a study in which table tilt registration was performed in the supine position, followed by cup implantation in the lateral decubitus position. The favorable accuracy in that report was induced by careful adjustment of the pelvic orientation using a steel square ruler and level guide at the time of registration16. In contrast, although coronal pelvic tilt was adequately corrected during table tilt registration, the residual axial pelvic tilt was significantly associated with coordinate system misalignment and contributed to increased anteversion error.

Previous studies have reported favorable accuracy for portable navigation systems that register the ASISs. AR-Hip (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) and Naviswiss (Naviswiss AG, Brugg, Switzerland) allow ASIS registration in the supine position and can subsequently be used for surgery in both the supine and lateral decubitus positions. HipAlign (OrthAlign, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA) allows the ASISs to be registered in the supine position, and surgery is performed only in the supine position. For AR-Hip, Naviswiss, and HipAlign used in the supine position, the reported navigation errors range from 1.7° to 3.7° for RI and from 1.9° to 3.8° for RA1–9, with 95–98.4% of acetabular components positioned within the safe zone5,7. In contrast, HipAlign in the lateral decubitus position demonstrated inferior accuracy, with reported navigation errors of 3.0°–4.6° for RI and 4.4°–6.8° for RA15,17,18. This difference has been attributed to the inability to register the ASISs, which require registration based on the body axis in the lateral decubitus position. Furthermore, a study using Naviswiss reported that registration of the ASISs in the supine position, followed by fixation in the lateral decubitus position, yielded higher accuracy than registration based on the body axis performed directly in the lateral decubitus position5. Construction of the 3D coordinate system without ASISs registration may be one potential factor contributing to the reduced accuracy of Navbit Sprint. However, because this was not directly proven by the present study design, this could not be definitively established. Although the difference in mean navigation error in anteversion between the two systems was not large, the variability in navigation accuracy observed with Navbit Sprint may indicate a potential for outlier cup placement.

The present study had some limitations. First, because acetabular cup implantation was performed using HoloNavi One, this study did not directly compare independent cup implantation accuracy between the two navigation systems. Second, the small sample size may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the present findings. In addition, although sample size was determined by a power analysis based on previously published data15, the characteristics of the reference study, including its relatively small sample size and large standard deviation, may have affected the precision of the estimation. Third, the retrospective design of this study may have introduced selection bias. Fourth, because all procedures were performed using the anterolateral supine approach, the generalizability of the present findings to other surgical approaches remains uncertain. Fifth, it did not evaluate whether the use of navigation systems led to improved clinical outcomes or implant survival in THA. However, a previous study using data from the Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry reported that the 10-year cumulative revision rate was significantly lower in navigated THA (2.4%) than in non-navigated THA (4.2%), and that the revision rate due to dislocation was also significantly lower in navigated THA (0.4% vs. 0.8%)19. These findings suggest a potential association between navigation and improved long-term outcomes. Sixth, although this is the first report evaluating Navbit Sprint in THA performed in the supine position, coronal pelvic tilt was corrected using HoloNavi One during table tilt registration, and therefore the registration methodology was not fully independent, potentially reducing external validity and real-world applicability. Finally, changes in sagittal pelvic tilt during registration were minimal in this cohort, limiting the ability to assess the impact of these changes on navigation accuracy.

Conclusion

In THA performed in the supine position, Navbit Sprint demonstrated significantly larger anteversion errors in intraoperative navigation values relative to postoperative CT than AR-CT-based navigation did. Axial pelvic tilt during registration may negatively affect the anteversion accuracy when using the Navbit Sprint.
