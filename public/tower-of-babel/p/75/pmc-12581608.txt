Transcription factor MdWRKY50 correlates with spur-type formation in apple by modulating endogenous gibberellin biosynthesis
Authors: Tianci Yan, Kun Shi, Ruoxue Li, Yixue Bai, Xiaodong Zheng, Dongqian Shan, Awei Jiang, Chanyu Wang, Haixia Zhang, Zehui Hu, Yanzhao Sun, Xiangshu Piao, Yan Guo, Jin Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The spur-type trait is an important breeding goal for apple (Malus × domestica Borkh.) cultivar, due to its advantages in yield and orchard mechanical management. MdWRKY50 encoding a transcription factor were identified to have dramatically lower expression in spur type. RNA interference of it leads to significantly shortened internodes in transgenic apple plants, while its overexpression resulted in opposite phenotype. The shortened internode of transgenic apple with RNA interfered MdWRKY50 was in good correlation with the bioactive gibberellin (GA) level. Furthermore, MdWRKY50 was proved to directly binds to the promoter of MdGA3ox, encoding a rate-limiting biosynthetase in GA biosynthesis, as confirmed by Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) and Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA). This binding up-regulated the expression of MdGA3ox. The low expression of MdWRKY50-MdGA3ox regulatory pathway is conserved across six spur-type apple cultivars in contrast to the high expressions across six standard-type apple cultivars. Our findings established a MdWRKY50-MdGA3ox module regulating GA-mediated internode elongation and coorelating with spur-type formation, which might contribute to the molecular breeding of spur-type cultivars adapted to high-density, mechanized apple orchards.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07496-5.

Introduction

Spur-type apple trees, characterized by a compact architecture and reduced size compared to the open-canopy form of standard-type varieties, enables early flowering and more concentrated flower bud differentiation, leading to increased fruit yield. Additionally, spur-type trees with shorter, thicker internodes, produce shorter branches and reduce pruning costs [1]. Spur-type, as an ideal tree architecture for modern orchards growth, is also a major breeding objective in apple cultivation. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying its formation remain largely unclear.

The spur-type of apple cultivars with shortened internode might result from reduced cell division and elongation, which are governed by a complex regulatory network involving multiple hormones. Decreased hormone level of auxin, cytokinin, brassinosteroids (BR), GAs and increased level of Jasmonic Acid (JA), Abscisic Acid (ABA) were detected in spur-type apple cultivar ‘Fuji’ and ‘Yanfu No.6’, respectively [2–4]. Auxin promotes cell elongation, while cytokinin stimulates cell division [5, 6]. Additionally, BRs can act synergistically with GAs to enhance internode elongation [7, 8]. GAs might be critical to promote elongation of internode cells, thereby regulating stem growth [9]. For instance, the contents of GA₃ and GA₄ are lower in the spur-type cultivar ‘Liquan spur’ compared with the standard-type cultivar ‘Yanfu No. 8’, whereas the changes in ZR, ABA, and IAA levels were less pronounced than those observed for GA [10]. Importantly, exogenous GA₃ application partially rescues the shortened internode phenotype in the spur-type cultivar ‘McIntosh Wijcik’, thereby confirming the pivotal role of GAs in internode elongation [11]. These findings indicate that while multiple hormones contribute to spur-type formation, the deficiency in GA might be the dominant determinant underlying this architectural trait.

GA levels are critical determinants of cell elongation and overall plant growth [12]. In higher plants, GA biosynthesis typically occurs in three sequential stages, among which GA3-oxidase (GA3ox) catalyzes the final, rate-limiting step to form bioactive GAs [13]. GA3ox has been identified through metabolomic and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) as a major candidate gene in height regulation of several woody and herbaceous species, including mango (Mangifera indica L.), rice (Oryza sativa L.) and maize (Zea mays L.). Overexpression of GA3ox conferred transgenic tabacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) altered internode length [14, 15].

There are a few researches on the spur-type formation. Several microRNAs were found to be involved in this formation with unknown mechanism. In a spur-type mutant of ‘Fuji’ apple, a deletion of a miniature inverted-repeat transposable element (MITE) in the promoter region of the transcription factor gene MdTCP11 altered DNA methylation levels, resulting in increased expression of MdTCP11, which subsequently suppressed GAs biosynthesis, leading to a compact growth habit [10]. But there is not report if MdTCP11 directly inhibits GAs biosynthesis. However, to date, no transcription factors have been reported to directly regulate GA biosynthetic enzymes in apple. In citrus, the transcription factor BBX22 has been shown to inhibit internode elongation and reduce plant height by directly activating GA2ox8, a gene involved in GA catabolism [16]. In woody plants such as poplar, the transcription factor PagKNAT2/6b suppresses the expression of GA20ox, resulting in dwarfism [17]. Studies in herbaceous plants identified different WRKY transcription factors, which either positively or negatively regulates GA-mediated plant growth. In rice, OsWRKY36 promotes the expression of OsSLR1, a repressor of GA signaling, thereby inhibiting internode elongation [18]. In maize, mutation of ZmWRKY92 leads to a marked reduction in GA content and a dwarf phenotype [19]. However, no reports have documented WRKY involvement in regulating internode length in woody perennials.

In this study, we observed that the expression of MdWRKY50 was significantly higher in six standard-type apple cultivars compared to six spur-type cultivars, suggesting a potential role for MdWRKY50 in affecting spur-type architecture. Overexpression of MdWRKY50 led to significantly increase in internode length, consistent with elevated levels of bioactive GA. Conversely, RNA interference of MdWRKY50 expression resulted in shortened internodes. Further analyses revealed that MdWRKY50 specifically binds to the promoter region of MdGA3ox, directly activating its expression. Notably, MdGA3ox transcript levels were also higher in standard-type cultivars relative to spur-type ones, indicating MdWRKY50-MdGA3ox pathway is conserve across spur-type and standard-type apple cultivars. Our findings elucidate a molecular mechanism by which MdWRKY50 regulates internode elongation in apple through modulation of the GA biosynthetic pathway. Moreover, this provides a theoretical foundation and candidate gene for spur-type apple breeding.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Shoot tips (5–8 mm below the apical region, including the shoot meristem without any leaves or petioles) from six spur-type cultivars (Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Huaguan Spur’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Han Fu’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Fukushima Spur’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Miyazaki Spur’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Mutsu Spur’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Scarlet’) and six standard-type cultivars (Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘ChangFu No. 2’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Mutsu’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Liangxiang’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Red kuo kuang’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Jonagold’, Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Feizhiyi’) were collected at at the autumn shoot elongation stage in August 29th. All samples were obtained from the Beidaihe New Area Apple Breeding Base, located in Nanlizhuang Village, Qinhuangdao City, Hebei Province, China (39.7469° N, 119.3120° E). Immediately after collection, leaves were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until use. Sampling was conducted with permission from the management authority of the breeding base, and in accordance with institutional, national, and international guidelines for experimental research on cultivated plants. All cultivars used are domesticated varieties and are not listed under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES).

The ‘GL-3’ (Malus × domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Royal Gala’) plants were cultured on subculture medium containing 0.2 mg L−1 IAA, 0.3 mg L−1 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA) and 0.1 mg L−1 GA3 at 23 °C under a 16 h:8 h light/dark cycle with a light intensity of 100 µmol m−2 s−1, and were subcultured every four weeks. ‘GL-3’ plants were rooted and transplanted as described previously [20]. Apple calli induction and culture were conducted as described previously [21].

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qPCR) analysis

qPCR was used to measure the expression levels of MdWRKY50, MdGA3ox, and other GA biosynthetic and catabolic genes, including MdCPS, MdKS, MdKO, MdKAO, MdGA20ox, and MdGA2ox. In addition, the MdWRKY genes with dramatical expression alternation in stem tissues were also examined. Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR reactions were performed as previously described [22]. MdActin was used as the internal reference gene. All primers used in this study are listed in Supporting Information Table S1. Each experiment was independently repeated two times.

Generating transgenic ‘GL-3’ apple plants and calli with altered MdWRKY50 expression

To generate transgenic apple plants MdWRKY50-OE, the full-length coding sequence (without termination codon) of MdWRKY50 was amplified by PCR and cloned into pBI121 vector under the control of the CaMV35S promoter, resulting in the overexpression construct 35 S::MdWRKY50-GUS. To suppress the expression of MdWRKY50, a pair of sense and antisense fragments targeting MdWRKY50 were cloned into the Gateway-compatible RNA interference vector pK7WIWG2D, yielding the RNAi construct pK7WIWG2D-MdWRKY50. Each construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105. Subsequently, Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of ‘GL-3’ apple was conducted following the protocol described previously [23].

To generate transgenic apple calli with altered MdWRKY50 expression, the coding sequence (CDS) of MdWRKY50 lacking the stop codon was amplified and subcloned into the Gateway-compatible binary vector pMDC83 under the control of the CaMV35S promoter, generating the overexpression plasmid 35 S::MdWRKY50. For RNA interference, sense and antisense fragments of MdWRKY50 were cloned into the pZH01 vector to construct the RNAi plasmid pZH01-MdWRKY50. Both constructs were separately introduced into EHA105. Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of apple calli was then performed according to the protocol [24].

Histological analysis of internode tissues

For histological examination, the internodes of both transgenic and non-transgenic control apple plants were harvested and immediately fixed in FAA solution at 4 °C for a minimum of 24 h to ensure complete tissue penetration. Following fixation, the samples were dehydrated through an ethanol series, embedded in paraffin wax, and sectioned into 8–10 μm thick slices.

The paraffin sections were subsequently deparaffinized and rehydrated. The slices were stained by Servicebio Company (Wuhan, China) [25]. Stained sections were observed under a Nikon Eclipse compound microscope (Instech Co., Ltd., Kanagawa, Japan). Images were captured using the integrated digital imaging system to document cellular structure. All samples were processed and analyzed in parallel to ensure consistency across experimental groups.

Detection of bioactive GA levels

The extraction, purification, and quantification of endogenous bioactive GA3 and GA₄ were conducted using an indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method, following the protocol described previously [26]. Three biological replicates were analyzed per sample, and GA levels were calculated based on the standard curve and expressed as ng/g FW.

The exogenous GA4 and paclobutrazol (PAC) application and phenotype observation

To evaluate the regulatory role of GA in MdWRKY50-mediated growth, the response of transgenic apple plants to exogenous GA₄ and its inhibitor PAC was observed under controlled conditions. One month after transplantation to soil, transgenic apple plants MdWRKY50-OE and MdWRKY50-RNAi were selected for treatment with non-transgenic plants as control.

For growth inhibition, the MdWRKY50-OE lines were foliar-sprayed daily with 100 µM PAC (a GA biosynthesis inhibitor). Conversely, to restore growth in the MdWRKY50-RNAi lines, exogenous GA₄ solution (100 µM) was applied to the leaves by daily-spraying. Additionally, treatments included PPBo (p-Phenoxyphenyl boronic acid, 100 µM), IAA (100 µM) were also carried out. All treatments were performed at the same time of day to minimize circadian effects. Each treatment group included at least three independent biological replicates. All measurements were conducted under identical greenhouse conditions (16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, 25 °C, relative humidity 50–60%).

ChIP-qPCR assay

To investigate the direct binding of MdWRKY50 to the promoter regions of candidate target genes, ChIP-qPCR was conducted. The recombinant protein GST-MdWRKY50 was expressed in Escherichia coli (BL21) and purified using glutathione affinity chromatography as described previously [27]. Polyclonal anti-MdWRKY50 antibodies were raised in rabbits against the purified GST-MdWRKY50 fusion protein and affinity-purified for ChIP.

ChIP assays were performed using the anti-MdWRKY50 polyclonal antibody according to the protocol [28]. Briefly, approximately 2 g of apple leaf tissue was crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde under vacuum to preserve protein-DNA interactions. Chromatin was isolated, sonicated into ~ 200–500 bp fragments, and immunoprecipitated with anti-MdWRKY50 antibody or normal rabbit IgG. DNA was obtained from the immune complexes and used as the template for qPCR amplification. Primers were designed to amplify specific regions containing W-box elements within the promoter sequences of putative MdWRKY50 target genes (Table S2). Each sample was analyzed three independent biological replicates.

EMSA

EMSA were conducted to evaluate the direct binding of MdWRKY50 to the W-box cis-elements within the promoter of MdGA3ox. The assay was performed using the LightShift™ Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the method described previously [28].

For the DNA binding assay, biotin-labeled double-stranded DNA probes containing the wild-type W-box sequence from the MdGA3ox promoter were synthesized. In parallel, unlabeled competitor probes were also prepared to assess binding specificity. All probe sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Each binding reaction (20 µL total volume) was carried out by incubating purified protein with biotin-labeled probe at room temperature for 20 min in binding buffer. The DNA-protein complexes were separated on a 6% native polyacrylamide gel and then transferred to a nylon membrane. After crosslinking, the membrane was incubated with streptavidin-HRP conjugate and visualized using chemiluminescence.

Detection of gene expression in the transiently transformed apple calli

To assess the regulatory effect of MdWRKY50 on MdGA3ox expression, a promoter-reporter construct was generated by amplifying the promoter region of MdGA3ox and inserting it upstream of the β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene in the binary vector pCAMBIA1301, resulting in the construct ProMdGA3ox::GUS.

Apple calli were transiently co-transformed with Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 harboring three plasmids: 35 S::MdWRKY50-GFP, ProMdGA3ox::GUS, and 35 S::LUC (LUC: luciferase, as an internal control). As a negative control, apple calli were co-transformed with 35 S::GFP, ProMdGA3ox::GUS, and 35 S::LUC. After 48 h of incubation under dark conditions at 24 °C, transformed calli were harvested for reporter activity assays. GUS and LUC activities were quantified as described previously [28]. GUS activity was normalized to LUC activity to control for variation in transformation efficiency, and the GUS: LUC ratio was used as the final measure of promoter activity. Each transformation and quantification experiment were independently repeated two times.

Results

MdWRKY50 expression correlates with internode length in apple cultivars

To identify WRKY transcription factors potentially involved in the regulation of apple tree architecture, we conducted expression analysis of 15 MdWRKY genes previously reported to have dramatical expression alternation in stems, using stem tissues from twelve apple cultivars (Fig. S1), including six spur-type cultivars (‘Huaguan Spur’, ‘Han Fu’, ‘Fukushima Spur’, ‘Miyazaki Spur’, ‘Mutsu Spur’, and ‘Scarlet’.) characterized by short internodes and compact growth, and six standard-type cultivars (‘ChangFu No. 2’, ‘Mutsu’, ‘Liangxiang’, ‘Red kuo kuang’, ‘jonagold’, and ‘Feizhiyi’) exhibiting normal elongated growth patterns.

Among the candidate genes analyzed, MdWRKY50 showed a consistent positive correlation between transcript abundance and plant height across all tested cultivars (Fig. 1A). The concordance between MdWRKY50 expression at both transcript and protein levels, coupled with its differential expression between spur- and standard-type cultivars, suggested a potential role for MdWRKY50 in regulating shoot elongation and tree architecture. Based on these findings, MdWRKY50 was selected for subsequent functional characterization in transgenic apple lines.

Functional characterization of MdWRKY50 in transgenic apple plants

To elucidate the functional role of MdWRKY50 in affecting apple shoot architecture, we generated transgenic ‘GL-3’ apple lines MdWRKY50-OE and MdWRKY50-RNAi. Three overexpression lines and two RNAi lines were selected for phenotypic and cytological analyses (Fig. S2).

One month after transplantation, distinct morphological differences were observed among the transgenic lines. MdWRKY50-RNAi plants displayed a spur-type growth habit with shorter shoots and internodes, whereas MdWRKY50-OE plants exhibited elongated shoot growth (Fig. 2A, B). Quantitative measurements indicated that the total number of nodes was comparable across all lines, suggesting that the observed differences in plant height were not due to altered node initiation (Fig. 2C). To further investigate the cellular basis underlying the architectural changes, histological examination of longitudinal stem sections was performed. In MdWRKY50-RNAi plants, cortical cells were significantly shorter and therefore more cells appeared per unit area compared with wild-type controls. Conversely, in MdWRKY50-OE lines, cortical cells were visibly elongated and fewer in number (Fig. 2F, G).

MdWRKY50 modulates GA biosynthesis to regulate internode elongation in apple

Given the well-established role of phytohormones in regulating plant architecture, we investigated whether MdWRKY50 influences hormone homeostasis, particularly in relation to internode elongation. Endogenous levels of GA3/4, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and brassinosteroids (BR) were quantified in MdWRKY50-OE lines and wild-type control plants.

While IAA and BR concentrations showed no significant differences between MdWRKY50-OE and control plants (Fig. S3), a marked elevation in bioactive GAs levels was observed in MdWRKY50-OE lines. Specifically, GA₃ levels in MdWRKY50-OE-1 and MdWRKY50-OE-2 reached 6.318 ng/g and 7.168 ng/g, respectively, both significantly higher than that of the control (5.091 ng/g). In contrast, GA₃ contents were markedly reduced in MdWRKY50-RNAi-1 and RNAi-2 lines, with levels of 3.868 ng/g and 3.072 ng/g, respectively (Fig. 3A). A similar trend was observed for GA₄: all three MdWRKY50 overexpression lines exhibited elevated GA₄ levels ranging from 3.716 to 4.718 ng/g, significantly exceeding the control value of 2.957 ng/g. To further verify the effect of altered GA level on spur-type formation in transgenic apple lines, we applied paclobutrazol (PAC, a GA biosynthesis inhibitor) to MdWRKY50-OE plants. This application of PAC led to severe growth inhibition within 14 days, resulting in a stunted phenotype resembling that of MdWRKY50-RNAi lines (Fig. 3C). Conversely, treatment of MdWRKY50-RNAi plants with exogenous GA₄ partially rescued the shortened internode, as evidenced by visible shoot elongation in newly developed part (Fig. 3C).

Node number remained unchanged among treated and untreated plants (Fig. 3D), indicating that the observed growth differences were attributable to changes in internode elongation rather than meristem activity. After 35 days of treatment, PAC-treated MdWRKY50-OE plants exhibited significantly reduced shoot height, whereas MdWRKY50-RNAi plants treated with GA₄ displayed markedly increased internode length (Fig. 3E).

To determine whether MdWRKY50 directly regulates these target genes, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) using a polyclonal anti-MdWRKY50 antibody. Enrichment of the P1 promoter fragment containing the W-box motif of MdGA3ox was significantly higher in MdWRKY50-OE lines compared to controls (Fig. 4B), confirming in vivo binding. No enrichment was observed for the promoters of other GA-related genes (Fig. S4).

We further validated the specific interaction between MdWRKY50 and the MdGA3ox promoter in vitro using EMSA. GST-tagged MdWRKY50 bound specifically to the MdGA3ox promoter probe, and this binding was competitively inhibited by excess unlabeled probe (Fig. 4C), confirming direct and specific interaction.

We next assessed the regulatory effect of MdWRKY50 on MdGA3ox expression using transient expression assays in apple calli. Three days after Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, the GUS : LUC activity ratio was significantly higher (2.76-fold) in calli co-expressing proMdGA3ox::GUS, 35 S::MdWRKY50-GFP, and 35 S::LUC compared to controls (Fig. 4D). These results indicate that MdWRKY50 positively regulates MdGA3ox transcription through direct promoter activation.

To evaluate the functional necessity of MdWRKY50, we analyzed MdGA3ox expression in transgenic apple calli overexpressing or silencing MdWRKY50. MdWRKY50 expression levels were significantly altered in these lines relative to controls (Fig. S5). In MdWRKY50-OE lines, MdGA3ox transcript levels increased more than 8-fold, while RNAi-mediated knockdown of MdWRKY50 reduced MdGA3ox expression to about 40% of control levels (Fig. 4E).

Consistent with the expression trends observed for MdWRKY50, MdGA3ox transcript levels were significantly higher in standard-type cultivars compared with spur-type cultivars. This co-expression pattern was consistently observed across all the detected cultivars, indicating that the transcriptional activity of MdGA3ox is positively correlated with MdWRKY50 expression in apple plants.

Discussion

The spur-type apple cultivar, characterized by shortened internodes and compact plant architecture, contributing to increased yield and reduced pruning cost, is a main breeding object for modern orchard [29]. It is important to explore the critical regulatory genes for spur-type and reveal the underlying mechanism for the molecular breeding of spur-type apple cultivar.

The WRKY transcription factor family members have been reported to govern development by regulating the biosynthesis, transportation and signaling of various plant hormones [21, 24, 27, 30]. But it remains unreported if WRKY members control spur-type formation. The WRKY members were screened and MdWRKY50 was identified to express itself differently between the six and the other six spur-type cultivars. The dramatically low MdWRKY50 transcriptional levels and MdWRKY50 content correlated well with the spur-type in all the test cultivars with shorter internode length (Fig. 1A, B). RNA interfered MdWRKY50 conferred transgenic apple shortened internode, whereas its overexpression significantly promoted internode length (Fig. 2A-C), suggesting the crucial role of MdWRKY50 in affecting apple spur-type formation.

Previous studies reported that in paraffin sections, cells in the spur-type cultivar ‘Liquan spur’ were significantly shorter than those in the standard-type cultivar ‘Yanfu No. 8’, while the cell number within the same area was markedly higher in ‘Liquan spur’ [10]. To validate whether MdWRKY50 plays a similar role in regulating cellular morphology, we examined the paraffin sections of stems from MdWRKY50-OE, MdWRKY50-RNAi, and wild-type apple plants. The transgenic plants with RNA-interfered MdWRKY50 exhibited shorter cells in stem longitudinal sections compared to the non-transformed control, which corresponded with their shortened nodular stems. In contrast, the MdWRKY50-OE lines displayed longer cells and significantly increased internode length (Fig. 2D, E). Our quantitative analysis of longitudinal stem sections showed that spur-type lines contain significantly shorter cells but a higher number of cells per unit area compared with standard-type varieties (Fig. 2F, G). These findings strongly suggest that cell length and density are key determinants of internode length in apple trees, consistent with previous studies [10].

The formation of spur-type apples is a complex trait co-regulated by multiple phytohormones [31]. To investigate whether MdWRKY50 influences spur formation by modulating hormone levels, we measured IAA, BR, and GA levels in transgenic apples. The GA3 and GA4 content was significantly elevated in MdWRKY50-OE plants, whereas IAA and BR levels remained unchanged (Figs. 3A, B; S3). GA4 treatment increased internode length in MdWRKY50-RNAi plants, while paclobutrazol (PAC, a GA biosynthesis inhibitor) suppressed cell elongation in MdWRKY50-OE lines and therefore shortened the internode length (Fig. 3C-E). These findings collectively supported that MdWRKY50 regulates internode elongation mainly by promoting GA accumulation. MdTCP11 is well-known in promoting GA-mediated spur formation, it remains unknown if MdWRKY50 participate in MdTCP11 regulated network. In addition, there are also other critical pathway regulating hormone biosynthesis and signaling, including JA, BR pathways, forms a critical regulatory network for spur-type development or dwarfing in apple. The JA-MdILL6 module suppresses xylem and vessel development, ultimately restricting internode elongation for spur-type formation [2]. Similar regulation of hormone-mediated development by other WRKY was also reported in apple, MdWRKY9-MdDWF4 has been reported to inhibit BR biosynthesis for apple dwarfing [28].

To elucidate how MdWRKY50 regulates GA-mediated spur-type formation, we analyzed the expression of GA metabolic genes. Overexpression of MdWRKY50 significantly upregulated multiple GA biosynthetic genes (MdCPS, MdKO, MdGA20ox, MdGA3ox), while MdGA2ox remained unaffected (Fig. 4A). ChIP-qPCR and EMSA assays confirmed that MdWRKY50 directly binds to the promoter of MdGA3ox (Fig. 4B, C). Additionally, GUS reporter assays demonstrated that MdWRKY50 activates MdGA3ox promoter-driven GUS expression (Fig. 4D, E), further validating its transcriptional activation function. MdCPS, MdKO, MdGA20ox might be regulated by MdWRKY50 indirectly.

MdWRKY50 is the first transcription factor identified to regulate GA biosynthesis via directly promoting MdGA3ox expression. Our finding of this MdWRKY50-MdGA3ox pathway might be critical in affecting spur-type resulted from shortened cell, because there are accumulated reports on the tight correlation of cell size the with expression of GA3ox, which has been proved to be the rate-limiting key enzyme in GA biosynthesis [32]. Reduction of GA3ox function invariably leads to shortened internode length, a typical GA-deficient phenotype. In alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a natural recessive mutation in the MsDWF1 gene (encoding GA3ox) conditions a pronounced small-cell phenotype [33]. Similarly, in sponge gourd (Luffa acutangula (L.) Roxb), the dwarf plants with smaller cells have a 4-kb transposon insertion in the first intron of GA3ox gene, leading to mis-splicing and drastically lower active GA levels, therefore severely reduced internode elongation. The application of GA₃ restores normal vine length, confirming this shortened internode is caused by GA deficiency, demonstrating that the stunted growth is directly attributable to insufficient GA due to the GA3ox loss-of-function [34].

Furthermore, in tested six spur-type apple cultivars, both MdWRKY50 and its downstream target gene MdGA3ox were consistently expressed at significantly lower levels compared to standard-type cultivars (Fig. 5). This coordinated downregulation suggests that the MdWRKY50-mediated GA regulatory pathway is suppressed in spur-type apple cultivars, which may represent a key molecular basis underlying reduced internode elongation and compact plant architecture. Notably, the consistent expression trend across diverse genetic backgrounds suggests this mechanism is conserved cross apple germplasms, highlighting the potential of these genes as molecular markers for early selection and engineering compact-growth cultivars [10].

Besides spur-type formation, GA deficiency is also closely associated with the development of columnar apple. We hypothesize that MdWRKY50 and MdGA3ox may also serve as candidate genes in the regulatory pathway underlying columnar apple formation. Nevertheless, further experiments are required to confirm the expression patterns of MdWRKY50 and MdGA3ox in columnar apple.

Conclusion

Taken together, our results support a model in which MdWRKY50 directly promotes transcription of MdGA3ox, thereby increasing GA biosynthesis and modulating cell elongation and internode length to shape spur-type architecture in apple (Fig. 6).
