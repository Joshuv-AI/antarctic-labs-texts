Diagnostic value of systemic immune-inflammation composite index combined with triglyceride-glucose index in type 2 diabetes patients with coronary heart disease: a retrospective diagnostic model study
Authors: Run Li, Wanyu Deng, Wei Zhang, Yong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of coronary heart disease (CHD) among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has increased substantially. Early identification of high-risk individuals is critical for improving clinical outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic value of the systemic immune-inflammation composite index (SIICI) and the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index for CHD in patients with T2DM, and to develop and validate a combined diagnostic model incorporating these indices.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 599 patients with T2DM who underwent coronary angiography and divided them into CHD (n = 371) and non-CHD (n = 228) groups based on angiographic findings. Using stratified 7:3 sampling according to CHD status, the cohort was randomly split into training (n = 439) and validation (n = 160) sets. Clinical and laboratory data were collected. Univariate logistic regression (P < 0.1) followed by backward stepwise multivariate logistic regression was performed to construct the diagnostic model. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsCompared with the non-CHD group, patients with CHD had significantly higher median levels of SIICI [5.24 (3.54, 8.05) vs. 3.75 (2.63, 4.76), P < 0.001] and TyG index [9.36 (8.96, 9.77) vs. 8.89 (8.60, 9.27), P < 0.001]. Multivariate logistic regression identified SIICI (OR = 1.347, 95% CI: 1.190–1.524) and TyG (OR = 2.843, 95% CI: 1.794–4.507) as independent risk factors for CHD. The combined model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.840 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.803–0.877) in the training set, with a sensitivity of 72.8% and specificity of 80.4%; in the validation set, the AUC was 0.851 (95% CI: 0.794–0.908), with a sensitivity of 75.8% and specificity of 80.0%. Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement (Hosmer–Lemeshow test: P = 0.597). DCA revealed positive net clinical benefit across a threshold probability range of 10–56%.ConclusionsBoth SIICI and TyG are independent risk factors for CHD in patients with T2DM. The combined diagnostic model exhibits excellent discriminative ability, good calibration, and stable generalizability. While head-to-head comparisons with traditional risk scores are needed, this model offers a non-invasive tool that integrates inflammatory and metabolic information for cardiovascular risk stratification in T2DM patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06006-1.

Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major risk factor for coronary heart disease (CHD), and their co-occurrence significantly increases the risk of adverse cardiovascular events [1]. Early identification of patients with T2DM at high risk for CHD is essential for precision prevention and optimized clinical management. Although coronary angiography remains the gold standard for CHD diagnosis, its invasive nature and high cost limit its use in large-scale screening. Therefore, exploring novel composite biomarkers derived from routine laboratory tests that integrate multiple pathophysiological mechanisms has become a research priority.

Patients with T2DM often exhibit concurrent metabolic disturbances and chronic inflammation, which interact to form a “metabolic-inflammatory axis” that synergistically drives atherosclerosis [2]. The interaction between the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) and systemic inflammation has been implicated in diabetic complications. For example, in T2DM patients with CHD, elevated levels of ACE, ACE2, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) have been reported, suggesting a close interplay between RAS dysregulation and inflammatory activation [3]. Moreover, a recent overview of RAS in diabetic nephropathy highlights the dual pathways (ACE/Ang II/AT1R and ACE2/Ang 1–7/MasR) that mediate both protective and deleterious effects in diabetic vascular disease [4]. These findings support the rationale for combining inflammatory and metabolic markers in cardiovascular risk assessment.Traditional inflammatory markers, such as white blood cell count, provide limited reflection of systemic inflammation. Recently, composite inflammatory indices have gained attention due to their accessibility and low cost. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) [5] and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) [6] reflect platelet-neutrophil and monocyte-neutrophil mediated inflammation, respectively; however, neither integrates the interaction between monocytes and platelets—a core pathological mechanism in diabetes-accelerated atherosclerosis: activated platelets directly bind monocytes, forming monocyte-platelet aggregates that promote monocyte subendothelial migration, foam cell formation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release [7]. Wang et al. [8] proposed the systemic immune-inflammation composite index (SIICI), which integrates neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and platelets (all cell counts in ×10⁹/L), providing a multidimensional reflection of the “metabolic-immune-thrombotic” inflammatory network. Compared with SII and SIRI, SIICI theoretically captures the metabolic disturbance-driven, monocyte-platelet axis-centered vascular inflammatory burden more sensitively in patients with T2DM, making it a promising novel marker for CHD risk assessment.

For metabolic evaluation, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index– calculated as Ln[fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL) / 2], where triglycerides (mmol/L) are converted to mg/dL (×88.5) and fasting glucose (mmol/L) to mg/dL (×18) – has been widely validated as a reliable surrogate marker of insulin resistance (IR), which is the core metabolic defect linking T2DM and cardiovascular disease [9]. Combining these two indices may enhance diagnostic performance for CHD in patients with T2DM. This study aimed to evaluate the independent diagnostic value of SIICI and TyG for CHD in patients with T2DM, explore their correlation with coronary artery disease severity (assessed by the Gensini score), and develop and validate a combined diagnostic model incorporating SIICI, TyG, and other key clinical factors, thereby providing a new quantitative tool for clinical risk stratification.

Methods

Study population

We enrolled 599 T2DM patients who underwent coronary angiography at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between January 2022 and December 2024. T2DM diagnosis was based on the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes (2024 edition) [10]. CHD diagnosis followed the 2024 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Chronic Coronary Syndromes [11]. Exclusion criteria were: history of primary cardiomyopathy, valvular heart disease, or other severe cardiac diseases; any malignancy; diseases affecting the immune or hematological systems; severe liver or kidney failure. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (approval number: PJ2025-12-70).

Sample size estimation

Following the events per variable (EPV) rule proposed by Peduzzi et al. [12], each candidate predictor requires at least 10–15 outcome events to ensure stable logistic regression parameter estimates. With 10 candidate variables and requiring 15 events per variable, at least 150 CHD patients were needed. Our study included 371 CHD patients, meeting the requirements for model development and internal validation.

Data collection

Data collected included

Demographic and clinical characteristics

age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, alcohol consumption, hypertension history, diabetes duration, and antidiabetic medications(including metformin, sulfonylureas, α-glucosidase inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 [DPP-4] inhibitors, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 [SGLT2] inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 [GLP-1] receptor agonists, and insulin). (Due to the retrospective design relying on routine medical records, pre-admission medication histories, including specific types, doses, and durations of statins and aspirin, were incomplete and thus not included in the final analysis.)

Laboratory parameters

Fasting venous blood samples were collected on the morning after admission to measure fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), lipid profile [total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)], lipoprotein(a) [LP(a), mg/L] and complete blood count [white blood cell (WBC), neutrophils (NE), lymphocytes (LYM), monocytes (Mon), platelets (PLT)]. All tests followed standard operating procedures with regularly calibrated instruments.left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF, %).

Coronary angiography results

Interpreted independently by two interventional cardiologists. CHD was defined as ≥ 50% diameter stenosis in at least one major epicardial vessel (left main, left anterior descending, left circumflex, or right coronary artery). Coronary artery disease severity was quantified using the Gensini scoring system [13], weighting scores based on stenosis degree and lesion location.

Quality control: Variables with < 5% missingness were handled by multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE). For continuous variables, predictive mean matching was applied; for binary variables, logistic regression imputation was used. Five imputed datasets were generated, and pooled estimates were calculated for subsequent analyses. Critical variables with missing data were traced for supplementation from original medical records; cases with unsupplementable missing data were excluded. The proportion of missing data for each key variable was as follows: HbA1c 3.2%, LDL‑C 2.1%, HDL‑C 1.8%, and other complete blood count parameters < 1%.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.1. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman correlation analysis assessed associations of SIICI and TyG with Gensini score.

Model development and validation

The total sample was randomly split into training (n = 439) and validation (n = 160) sets at a 7:3 ratio. In the training set, univariate logistic regression was performed (P < 0.1 for entry into multivariate analysis). Backward stepwise multivariate logistic regression (removal criterion P > 0.05) was used to construct the combined diagnostic model. Regression coefficients (β), odds ratios (OR), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. To assess the impact of SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists on model stability, we excluded patients using these medications (n = 141) and re-constructed the model in the remaining population (n = 458). SIICI and TyG were entered as continuous variables. To evaluate the dose-response relationship between SIICI and CHD risk, we categorized SIICI into tertiles for trend test (P for trend). Collinearity diagnostics were performed for all continuous variables before multivariate regression (variance inflation factor [VIF] < 3 indicated no severe multicollinearity).

Model performance evaluation

ROC curves were plotted to calculate AUC and 95% CI. Calibration curves were plotted, Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was performed, and Brier score was reported. The optimal cutoff was determined by Youden index, and sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to assess clinical utility by calculating net benefit at different high-risk threshold probabilities.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 599 T2DM patients, 371 (62.0%) were in the CHD group and 228 (38.0%) in the non-CHD group. Compared with non-CHD patients, CHD patients had higher proportions of males, current smokers and drinkers, hypertension prevalence, and longer diabetes duration. Regarding antidiabetic medications, CHD patients had significantly lower usage rates of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists. For laboratory parameters, CHD patients had significantly higher levels of FBG, HbA1c, TG, LDL-C, neutrophil count, and monocyte count, while HDL-C and lymphocyte count were significantly lower (all P < 0.05). SIICI [5.24 (3.54, 8.05) vs. 3.75 (2.63, 4.76), P < 0.001] and TyG index [9.36 (8.96, 9.77) vs. 8.89 (8.60, 9.27), P < 0.001] were significantly higher in CHD patients. Gensini score, SII, SIRI, and AIP were also significantly higher in the CHD group (all P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Comparison of training and validation sets

The total cohort was randomly split into training (n = 439) and validation (n = 160) sets. No statistically significant differences were observed in any baseline characteristics between the two sets (all P > 0.05), indicating good matching for model development and validation (Additional file 1: Table S1).

Correlation analysis

In CHD patients, Spearman correlation analysis showed that SIICI was strongly positively correlated with Gensini score (r = 0.681, P < 0.001), which was stronger than correlations for SII (r = 0.282, P < 0.001), SIRI (r = 0.515, P < 0.001), AIP (r = 0.302, P < 0.001), and TyG (r = 0.417, P < 0.001). (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Diagnostic value of SIICI and TyG for CHD

Univariate logistic regression analysis

In the training set (n = 439), univariate logistic regression showed that current drinking, diabetes duration, FBG, HbA1c, elevated TG, decreased HDL-C, elevated LDL-C, SIICI, SII, SIRI, AIP, and TyG were significantly associated with CHD risk (all P < 0.05) (Additional file 2: Table S2).

Dose-response analysis of SIICI

When SIICI was categorized into tertiles (Q1–Q3) with Q1 as reference, multivariate logistic regression with progressive adjustment (Model 3: adjusted for demographics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, glucose metabolism, inflammatory cell counts, and lipid profile) showed that patients in the highest tertile (Q3) had a 4.023-fold higher CHD risk (95% CI: 2.197–7.368, P < 0.001) with a significant linear trend (P for trend < 0.001). The middle tertile (Q2) showed no significant difference compared with Q1 (Table 3).

Multivariate logistic regression and final model construction

A total of 15 candidate variables with P < 0.1 in univariate analysis were initially entered into the backward stepwise regression. Current drinking, diabetes duration, HDL-C, LDL-C, SIICI, and TyG remained as independent predictors. Smoking and hypertension history lost significance after adjustment (P > 0.05), likely because their effects on CHD risk are largely mediated by insulin resistance (TyG), dyslipidemia (HDL‑C, LDL‑C), and systemic inflammation (SIICI), which are already included in the model. Spearman correlation analysis showed that SIICI was moderately correlated with SII (r = 0.62) and SIRI (r = 0.58), but VIF values remained < 3, and SIICI retained independent significance in the final model, indicating no severe multicollinearity. The final combined diagnostic model was: Logit(P) = − 11.525 + 1.020 × current drinking + 0.106 × diabetes duration – 1.667 × HDL-C + 1.013 × LDL-C + 0.298 × SIICI + 1.045 × TyG (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Simplified bedside scoring rule

Based on the regression coefficients, each independent predictor was assigned integer points: current drinking (+ 5), diabetes duration (per year: +1), HDL‑C (per 0.5 mmol/L decrease below 1.0: +4), LDL‑C (per 1.0 mmol/L increase above 2.0: +5), SIICI (per 2‑unit increase: +3), TyG (per 0.5‑unit increase: +4). A total score ≥ 22 indicates a high probability of CHD (> 70%). This simplified rule can be used without a nomogram.

Model performance and validation

Discrimination

In the training set (n = 439), the combined model achieved an AUC of 0.840 (95% CI: 0.803–0.877). Using the Youden index, the optimal cut‑off value was determined as 0.738, yielding a sensitivity of 72.8%, specificity of 80.4%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 86.2%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 65.0%. In the validation set (n = 160), the same model produced an AUC of 0.851 (95% CI: 0.794–0.908), with an optimal cut‑off of 0.688, sensitivity of 75.8%, specificity of 80.0%, PPV of 84.7%, and NPV of 69.3%. These results confirm the stable and excellent discriminative ability of the combined model across independent datasets.(Table 5; Fig. 3).

Discussion

Chronic hyperglycemia in patients with T2DM can induce low-grade inflammation and lipid metabolism disorders, forming a “metabolic-inflammatory” vicious cycle that synergistically drives the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis (AS) [14, 15]. Insulin resistance (IR) is the central hub of this cycle: on one hand, IR directly promotes inflammation by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and exacerbating oxidative stress [16]; on the other hand, it leads to lipid metabolism abnormalities (elevated LDL-C, decreased HDL-C), while abnormal lipids can reciprocally activate inflammatory pathways, further damaging the vascular endothelium and accelerating AS [17, 18]. Clinical studies have also confirmed that T2DM patients with CHD often exhibit more severe coronary atherosclerosis [19]. Therefore, early identification of such high-risk individuals and implementation of personalized interventions are of significant clinical importance.

This study demonstrated that SIICI and TyG index were significantly elevated in T2DM patients with CHD, and both were independent risk factors for CHD (SIICI: OR = 1.347; TyG: OR = 2.843), suggesting the central role of metabolic disturbances and chronic inflammation in AS. The TyG index is a reliable surrogate marker of IR, reflecting abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism. IR, along with its accompanying hyperglycemia and free fatty acids, drives AS by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and exacerbating oxidative stress, serving as the upstream metabolic driver of the inflammatory response [16].Mechanistically, the insulin-resistant state represented by TyG may regulate SIICI expression through the following pathways: hyperglycemia and free fatty acids can induce platelet activation, increase P-selectin expression, and promote the formation of monocyte-platelet aggregates, thereby directly elevating the “monocyte × platelet” component of the SIICI formula. Concurrently, in the insulin-resistant state, the expression of chemokine receptors (such as C-C chemokine receptor type 2 [CCR2]) on monocytes is upregulated, making them more readily recruited to the vascular intima and enhancing their polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, further amplifying the local inflammatory response [20]. In the unique metabolic environment of diabetes, persistent stimulation by hyperglycemia and free fatty acids predisposes immune cells (particularly monocytes/macrophages) to differentiate into a pro-inflammatory phenotype and enhances their interaction with activated platelets, collectively forming an amplified “metabolic-immune-thrombotic” inflammatory network. Elevated SIICI directly reflects the activation state of this network.The central role of monocytes in diabetic vascular inflammation is further evidenced by recent work in diabetic retinopathy, where PXN+ monocytes were shown to accelerate inflammaging via the SELPLG-SELL chemotaxis axis and RAS modulation [21]. This lends biological plausibility to the inclusion of monocyte count in the SIICI formula and supports the concept that monocyte‑platelet interaction is a key driver of atherosclerosis in T2DM.The pathogenic role of inflammatory pathways in myocardial injury is further supported by recent experimental evidence showing that disruption of the caspase‑1/IL‑1β axis alleviates myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury through improvement of mitochondrial homeostasis and reduction of pyroptosis [22].Therefore, SIICI is not merely a manifestation of inflammatory burden but can be regarded as an integrative indicator of the “metabolic stress-driven immune-thrombotic inflammatory axis.“Furthermore, the correlation between SIICI and the severity of coronary artery disease (Gensini score) in this study (r = 0.681) was significantly stronger than that of TyG (r = 0.417) and traditional inflammatory indices SII (r = 0.282) and SIRI (r = 0.515). This suggests that while metabolic disturbance (TyG) acts as an upstream initiating factor, the sustained immune-inflammatory activation reflected by SIICI represents the effector pathway directly exacerbating coronary plaque burden.

In clinical practice, widely used tools such as the Framingham Risk Score and ASCVD Risk Score primarily rely on traditional risk factors and may have limitations in identifying “residual cardiovascular risk” in T2DM patients who have achieved target lipid control through lipid-lowering therapy [23]. This residual risk is largely driven by persistent metabolic abnormalities and low-grade inflammation. In contrast, the combined diagnostic model developed in this study, centered on SIICI and TyG, directly integrates information from both the inflammatory and metabolic axes, reflecting systemic “immune-thrombotic-inflammatory” status and insulin resistance. Therefore, it may help identify high-risk patients missed by traditional scores but with persistently active metabolic-inflammatory axes, providing a complementary tool for risk stratification in this population.Compared with existing scores, all variables in this model are derived from routine blood tests, requiring no additional examinations, facilitating its application in daily clinical practice and primary healthcare settings. The combined diagnostic model established in this study demonstrated excellent discriminative ability, with AUCs of 0.840 and 0.851 in the training and validation sets, respectively. Compared with recent similar studies, such as the model constructed by Chen et al. [24] for predicting CHD in T2DM patients (AUC = 0.81) and the model incorporating TyG established by Mirjalili et al. [25] (AUC = 0.83), our model demonstrated comparable or superior discriminative performance.Similarly, Lin et al. constructed a machine‑learning nomogram for diabetic osteoporosis that achieved an AUC of 0.925, further demonstrating the feasibility of nomogram‑based diagnostic tools in diabetic complications [26].In a similar approach, Guo et al. developed a predictive model for diabetic nephropathy combining blood pressure, glycemic, and renal markers in elderly hypertensive patients with type 2 diabetes [27].This may be attributed to the novel combination of the composite inflammatory index SIICI and the classic metabolic index TyG, simultaneously capturing the two core pathophysiological processes of “inflammation” and “metabolism,” thereby containing richer risk information than single-type indicators.Additionally, all enrolled patients in this study underwent coronary angiography, ensuring a definitive CHD diagnosis, which may have facilitated the model’s learning of stronger discriminant features. Moreover, this study employed rigorous univariate screening (P < 0.1) and multivariate backward stepwise regression, resulting in a final model with only six strong predictors, ensuring efficacy while avoiding overfitting. However, the AUC value of this model does not represent a substantial advantage over some excellent studies (e.g., the model by Zhang et al. combining coronary CTA features and serum markers achieved an AUC of 0.89) [28]. This suggests that the performance of blood-based models has reached a relatively high level, and future improvements may require integrating imaging features, genetic information, or more novel specific biomarkers.This model closely aligns with the core mechanism (inflammation-metabolism axis) of CHD in T2DM, potentially offering better identification capability for early-stage, atypical, or “lipid-normal” high-risk patients. It enables rapid individualized risk assessment through nomograms or simplified scoring tables.The nomogram is intended for two main clinical scenarios: as a non‑invasive screening tool in outpatient settings for T2DM patients without typical angina, to identify those at high risk of CHD who may benefit from further diagnostic workup; and for risk stratification before invasive coronary angiography, helping to prioritize patients with a high predicted probability. Our composite index SIICI, which captures neutrophil‑lymphocyte‑monocyte interactions, may partially reflect such immune dysregulation.

In head‑to‑head comparisons with each individual biomarker, the combined model consistently outperformed SIICI, TyG, SII, SIRI, and AIP. In the training set, the combined model achieved an AUC of 0.840 (95% CI: 0.803–0.877), which was substantially higher than that of SIICI alone (0.701, 95% CI: 0.653–0.749) or TyG alone (0.717, 95% CI: 0.669–0.765). Similar results were observed in the validation set, with the combined model yielding an AUC of 0.851 (95% CI: 0.793–0.908) compared with 0.722 (95% CI: 0.645–0.799) for SIICI and 0.673 (95% CI: 0.589–0.756) for TyG. These findings underscore that integrating metabolic (TyG) and inflammatory (SIICI) information with other clinical predictors captures a broader spectrum of pathophysiological mechanisms than any single biomarker, leading to improved risk discrimination. Furthermore, the combined model also compared favorably with recent similar studies, such as the model by Chen et al. [24] based on multiple inflammatory factors (including IL-6, TNF-α) for predicting CHD in T2DM (AUC = 0.81), and the model by Mirjalili et al. [25] using TyG combined with traditional risk factors (AUC = 0.83). This superior performance may be attributed to the novel combination of the composite inflammatory index SIICI and the classic metabolic index TyG, simultaneously capturing the two core pathophysiological processes of “inflammation” and “metabolism,” thereby containing richer risk information than single-type indicators. All enrolled patients underwent coronary angiography, ensuring a definitive CHD diagnosis, which may have facilitated the model’s learning of stronger discriminant features. Moreover, rigorous univariate screening (P < 0.1) and multivariate backward stepwise regression resulted in a final model with only six strong predictors, ensuring efficacy while avoiding overfitting. The nomogram is intended for two main clinical scenarios: as a non‑invasive screening tool in outpatient settings for T2DM patients without typical angina, to identify those at high risk of CHD who may benefit from further diagnostic workup; and for risk stratification before invasive coronary angiography, helping to prioritize patients with a high predicted probability.Our subgroup analyses further demonstrated that the combined model maintained good diagnostic accuracy across different ages, sexes, diabetes durations, and hypertension statuses, with no significant AUC differences between any two levels of these subgroups. Notably, the model performed slightly better in non‑hypertensive patients (training AUC = 0.883; validation AUC = 0.894) than in hypertensive patients (training AUC = 0.799; validation AUC = 0.820), although the differences did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that while the model is broadly applicable, clinicians should be aware that additional risk factors beyond those captured by our model may contribute to CHD in hypertensive T2DM patients. Nevertheless, the consistent performance across subgroups supports the model’s utility as a non‑invasive screening tool for a wide spectrum of T2DM patients.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design inherently carries selection bias. Second, all indicators were measured at a single time point, failing to reflect dynamic changes and their relationship with long-term cardiovascular events. Whether longitudinal changes in SIICI or TyG — such as a rising trajectory — could improve dynamic risk stratification or predict future cardiovascular events remains unknown and warrants further prospective investigation.Third, despite adjusting for multiple confounders, residual confounding (e.g., dietary structure, physical activity, psychological stress, medication adherence) may still exist. Fourth, some antidiabetic medications (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists) and lipid-lowering agents (e.g., statins) possess definite anti-inflammatory and metabolic-improving effects, potentially influencing TyG, SIICI, and the outcome, constituting potential residual confounding. Although we controlled for the potential impact of SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 receptor agonist use through multivariate adjustment and sensitivity analysis, the retrospective design precluded obtaining information on medication exposure duration, dosage, and adherence. Other unmeasured confounders (e.g., dietary structure, physical activity, statin use intensity) may still exist. Furthermore, due to the retrospective design, data on statin and antiplatelet agent use (including type, dose, and duration) were incomplete in the medical records and could not be reliably included in the analysis. Both drug classes have well‑known effects on inflammation, lipid profiles, and platelet function, which are core components of SIICI and the TyG index. Specifically, statins exert cholesterol‑lowering and pleiotropic anti‑inflammatory effects, potentially reducing both SIICI (by decreasing platelet activation and monocyte chemotaxis) and TyG (by improving insulin sensitivity and triglyceride levels). Antiplatelet agents (e.g., aspirin, clopidogrel) may directly inhibit platelet function, thereby affecting the platelet count component of SIICI. The absence of adjustment for these medications may introduce residual confounding. Importantly, the likely direction of such confounding would be towards the null (i.e., an underestimation of the true association) because use of these drugs would lower inflammatory and metabolic risk markers, thereby attenuating the observed relationship between SIICI/TyG and CHD. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Future prospective studies should systematically collect detailed medication histories (including drug type, dose, duration, and adherence) to enable more precise adjustment. Fifth, while the diagnostic model performed well in internal validation, its applicability across different regions, healthcare facility levels, and ethnic populations requires further validation through multicenter prospective studies.This study’s all patients were recruited from a single tertiary hospital and underwent coronary angiography based on clinical suspicion of coronary heart disease (CHD). This selection strategy inevitably introduces referral bias, as the study population predominantly consists of individuals with a higher pre‑test probability of CHD. Consequently, the reported diagnostic performance of the combined model may not be directly generalizable to unselected patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus in primary care or community settings, where the prevalence of CHD is lower and the spectrum of disease severity may differ. Therefore, external validation in multi‑center, prospective cohorts that include outpatient T2DM populations without prior referral for angiography is essential to confirm the robustness and applicability of our findings.Sixth, we did not compare our model with traditional risk scores (e.g., Framingham, ASCVD) because our database lacked required variables (systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive treatment status and race‑specific parameters). Moreover, those scores predict future cardiovascular events, whereas our model is designed for cross‑sectional diagnosis of CHD. Direct comparison was therefore not feasible or methodologically appropriate. Future prospective studies are needed to address this issue.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms the independent and synergistic diagnostic value of the novel inflammatory index SIICI and the classic metabolic index TyG for CHD in T2DM. The significant correlation between them provides empirical support for the “metabolism-inflammation axis” theory, and their combined application enables a more comprehensive assessment of patient risk. The combined diagnostic model developed herein exhibits excellent performance and is based entirely on readily available routine clinical indicators, offering an efficient, convenient, and non-invasive tool for cardiovascular risk stratification in T2DM patients. This facilitates early identification of high-risk individuals and precision prevention. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed for external validation and to further explore the model’s predictive ability for hard cardiovascular endpoints.
