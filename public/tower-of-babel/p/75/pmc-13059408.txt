Association between demographic-socioeconomic factors and grouped trajectory patterns of peer support needs in parents of preterm infants: a longitudinal exploration
Authors: Yingjia Qian, Canyu Zhong, Yaru Tang, An Qin, Wenjuan Tang, Tingting Xu, Daqiao Zhu, Wenzhe Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePreterm poses substantial physical and psychological burdens on parents. Peer support could alleviate such burdens. However, longitudinal evidence on the trajectory patterns of peer support needs among parents of preterm infants, as well as how demographic-socioeconomic characteristics influence these patterns, remains limited.MethodsThis longitudinal study recruited 76 parents of preterm infants from three tertiary hospitals in Shanghai, China. Participants completed the Peer Support Needs Scale for Parents of Preterm Infants at four key time points from birth to two weeks post-discharge. Conditional Latent Class Growth Modeling was used to identify distinct trajectory patterns of peer support needs. Parental demographic-socioeconomic characteristics were included as covariates to assess their influence on trajectory group membership. Descriptive and univariate analyses were also conducted, and model fitting was evaluated through multiple fit indices.ResultsParticipants were categorized into three distinct trajectory groups based on the overall longitudinal patterns of change in their peer support needs across all time points: High-level Stable (22.37%), Moderate-level Slowly Declining (67.11%), and Low-level Rapidly Declining (10.53%). Parents with higher age and financial difficulties were more likely to be classified into the High-level Stable Group (OR = 10.72, P = 0.003; OR = 5.86, P = 0.024) or the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group (OR = 7.71, P = 0.009; OR = 4.08, P = 0.046).ConclusionsThese findings reveal that peer support needs among parents of preterm infants are dynamic and heterogeneous. Parental age and financial difficulties among demographic-socioeconomic factors could influence peer support needs. Stage-specific, tailored peer support should be provided based on individual characteristics.

Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as childbirth occurring before 37 completed weeks of gestation, is a leading cause of neonatal mortality worldwide [5]. Although the prevalence of preterm birth showed a little decrease from 2010 to 2020, with an average annual rate of reduction of − 0.14%, there were still approximately 13.4 million infants born preterm in 2020, accounting for 9.9% of all live births globally [43]. Preterm infants are frequently admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) for specialized treatment due to immature immune and organ systems [9, 17, 25], which can exert considerable burdens on their parents. For instance, the health challenges faced by preterm infants often exacerbate parental psychological distress, such as anxiety and depression, which in turn may diminish maternal breastfeeding motivation, contribute to postpartum fatigue, and strain family relationships [11, 16, 34, 36, 44, 61]. Moreover, the physical separation between parent and infant due to NICU hospitalization can hinder the development of parental role identity, potentially impairing the infant’s recovery and long-term developmental outcomes [30, 34, 36]. Empirical evidence suggests that adequate social support can enhance parental self-efficacy and psychological well-being, thereby enabling parents to better cope with the challenges associated with preterm birth [8, 41].

Peer support, a specific form of social support, refers to informational, emotional, or material assistance provided by individuals within a social network who share similar experiences or demographic characteristics (e.g., gender, age) [27, 50]. This type of support can facilitate access to parenting-related knowledge and resources for parents of preterm infants, alleviate negative emotions, and enhance parenting confidence [27]. Despite its documented benefits, peer support in practice is often constrained by resource shortages or low compatibility between peers [27, 56]. These limitations highlight the need for a comprehensive understanding of parents' peer support needs to deliver support services that are more resource-efficient and demand-responsive [10].

Our previous study suggested that the key events parents of preterm infants might experience and the event-triggered emotions differed over time from preterm birth to discharge, implying potential changes in the level of their support needs [23]. Similarly, parents in another study expressed that the type and amount of support evolved throughout their infant’s hospitalization [29]. However, although these studies have presented the potential changing trends of the level of support needs, they were predominantly cross-sectional and qualitative in design, which could not provide the quantitative evidence. Previous longitudinal studies have demonstrated the value of this design in tracking changes in the overall level of support needs among patients with chronic illnesses (e.g., heart failure and cancers) [49, 59, 65, 66]. However, few longitudinal studies exploring the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants were reported. Furthermore, these studies have revealed heterogeneous trajectories in patients’ support needs over time. For instance, in patients with heart failure, three distinct trajectories of care needs have been identified based on the evolving trend: (1) a mild increase trajectory, (2) a decline trajectory, and (3) a persistently high trajectory [49]. Therefore, we hypothesized that distinct patterns or subgroups of change might also exist in the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants, which could be detected through trajectory patterns analyses. Such analyses could facilitate researchers and practitioners in uncovering population heterogeneity and identifying vulnerable subgroups [42]. Therefore, the present study primarily aims to address the following research question: What are the longitudinal grouped trajectory patterns of peer support needs among parents of preterm infants from birth to two weeks after discharge?

Demographic-socioeconomic factors refer to a set of variables that reflect individual characteristics, including race, ethnicity, age, gender, educational attainment, and income status [14]. These factors are independent of the experimental environment, function as key control variables, and are closely related to health inequities [14, 21]. Previous studies have indicated that demographic-socioeconomic factors could influence the support needs of parents of preterm infants. For example, fathers may have greater emotional support needs than mothers [31],parents with a higher education level demonstrated a higher level of peer support needs [35]. However, how these factors influence the grouped patterns of support needs remains unclear. Understanding the influence of these factors may help the healthcare providers to predict the individual’s potential support needs at different timepoints, and their changing trend, and provide the opportune and personalized interventions, which help optimize the allocation of limited resources [10, 19]. Therefore, the present study hypothesizes that demographic-socioeconomic factors are associated with parents’ membership in the identified trajectory groups of peer support needs.

Given the lack of longitudinal research that applies grouped trajectory patterns analyses and the critical role of peer support for parents of preterm infants, the present study aims to identify distinct grouped trajectory patterns of peer support needs and the influence of demographic-socioeconomic factors on them from the time of the infant's birth to shortly after hospital discharge. By employing longitudinal analytical methods, this study addresses current gaps in the literature and contributes to enhance the understanding of how peer support needs change over time.

Methods

Participants

A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit participants from the NICUs of three tertiary hospitals in Shanghai, China, from December 2022 to February 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) one of the biological parents of preterm infants, typically the parent most involved in daily caregiving; (2) infants within 3 days of birth; (3) parents aged 18 years or older; (4) infants requiring hospitalization and medical care for more than 3 days after birth; (5) Parents who were awake and conscious at the time of recruitment, able to comprehend a brief verbal explanation of the study-related information (e.g., the study purpose, procedures, and questionnaire items) provided by the researcher and to answer simple questions confirming their understanding (e.g., what the study was about and what they would be asked to do), and able to communicate fluently in Mandarin or the local language; and (6) parents provided informed consent to take part in this study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) parents of preterm infants whose condition deteriorated during hospitalization, leading to treatment abandonment or transfer to another facility; and (2) parents who were not the primary caregivers after the infant’s discharge.

Sample size

Considering the longitudinal design of this study on the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants, the sample size was estimated to be 5 to 10 times the number of observed variables [46]. This study included 8 observed variables. Considering the possibility of invalid or incomplete questionnaires and potential participant loss, a 10% attrition rate was incorporated into the sample size calculation. Data missingness and imputation

Among the 76 participants initially enrolled, 8 cases (10.53%) exhibited partial missingness across the four assessment time points (T0-T3). Reasons for attrition included the death of infants, transfer to other hospitals, and time constraints. These participants did not complete all follow-up surveys but had at least one valid time point of data collection. The multiple imputation method was used to address missing data. Based on the missing-data pattern and variable type, the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method with a maximum of 10 iterations was performed in SPSS 27.0. The imputed values for continuous variables were constrained to their observed range, while those for categorical variables were restricted to valid categories with a minimum rounding unit of 1. The Cronbach's alpha of the scale before and after imputation was greater than 0.9, indicating good internal consistency and that the imputation process did not introduce systematic bias.

Outcomes and measurements

General information questionnaire

The researchers designed a questionnaire to collect the potential independent variables based on literature review, consisting of two sections: (1) infant and maternal characteristics, included sex of infant, gestational age at birth, birth weight, comorbidities at birth, parity, type of pregnancy, mode of delivery, mode of conception, and pregnancy complications; (2) demographic-socioeconomic factors of the parents, included parental role, parental age, employment status, educational level, medical payment type, annual household income, financial difficulties, place of residence. Financial difficulty was defined as 'Subjective Financial Distress,' a commonly used indicator assessing whether parents perceived their household income as insufficient to cover medical costs and daily living expenses [13, 26]. To ensure analytical stability, this variable was collapsed into two categories: “Present” (indicating perceived insufficiency and burden) and “None” (indicating financial adequacy).

Peer support needs scale for parents of preterm infants(PSNS-PPI)

This scale was self-developed and was constructed based on an extensive literature review, qualitative interviews, and expert consultations [47]. The scale is composed of 39 items covering five dimensions: needs for disease-related knowledge support (9 items, e.g., "I need to learn from other preterm infants’ parents about the baby’s diseases"), daily care knowledge support (9 items, e.g., "I need to learn from other preterm infants’ parents about feeding"), informational support (7 items, e.g., "I need to learn from other preterm infants’ parents about available resources such as websites, mini-programs, or mobile apps related to preterm infants"), emotional support (7 items, e.g., "I need encouragement and feeling of hope from other preterm infants’ parents"), and social support (7 items, e.g., "I need to interact with other preterm infants’ parents to build new social relationships"). All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not important at all”) to 5 (“very important”), with higher scores indicating stronger perceived needs for peer support. The scale demonstrated strong reliability and validity. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.982, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.838. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.85 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) was 0.96. These results indicate that the scale is a reliable tool for evaluating the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants.

Procedures

Following preliminary screening and eligibility assessment conducted by the head nurses of the NICUs, participants who met the inclusion criteria were provided with standardized instructions. The purpose and significance of the study, the number of survey rounds, detailed guidance for questionnaire completion, and key considerations (e.g., answering truthfully and avoiding missing key information) were clearly communicated. Upon receiving informed consent, eligible participants were formally enrolled and assigned a unique code. The baseline survey time was recorded along with a schedule for follow-up assessments. Questionnaire links were distributed via a secure online platform according to each participant's follow-up timeline. Based on previous research findings [24], data collection occurred at four critical time points: the day of enrollment (T0, immediately following preterm birth),15 days to 1 month post-birth (T1, the clinical fluctuation period); within two days after being informed of the infant’s hospital discharge (T2, the stabilization phase); and 15 days post-discharge (T3, the home adaptation phase). The T0 questionnaire included both the General Information Questionnaire and the Peer Support Needs Scale for Parents of Preterm Infants, whereas the T1-T3 assessments comprised only the peer support needs scale.

During the data collection process, researchers were available in real time to answer participants' questions and monitor the completion progress. If a questionnaire was not submitted in a timely manner, the participant was reminded to submit it. Since all questionnaire items were required, there were no missing items. Upon receipt, the responses were assessed for logical consistency. If illogical patterns or uniformly repetitive answers were detected, participants were contacted for clarification. If necessary, participants were asked to complete the questionnaire again. The research team also conducted regular cross-validation of responses across different time points to detect discrepancies or anomalies. This process was implemented to ensure the authenticity and completeness of the data.

Statistical analysis

The data were exported, and a database was established using SPSS version 27.0. Descriptive statistics were first conducted to summarize the general characteristics of preterm infants and their parents, as well as the scores on the peer support needs scale. Normality tests were performed for all quantitative variables. Variables conforming to a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), whereas those with non-normal distributions were reported as median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages [n (%)].

Subsequently, we employed a conditional Latent Class Growth Model (LCGM) [7] to identify potential trajectory groups of peer support needs among parents of preterm infants. Parental demographic-socioeconomic characteristics (e.g., gender, age, and financial difficulties) were incorporated in the model as time-invariant covariates to explore their influence on trajectory class membership. A linear growth trajectory was specified, and covariate effects were analyzed through an integrated multinomial logistic regression component. LCGM can identify subgroups of individuals with similar developmental trajectories from longitudinal data, thereby revealing population heterogeneity. As LCGM does not account for individual-level variability, it involves fewer parameter estimations, resulting in a simpler model structure, lower computational burden, and higher classification accuracy, which is particularly advantageous for studies with small sample sizes [7, 58]. The number of latent classes was increased sequentially from one to five. Model selection was based on the comparison of several fit indices, alongside theoretical and practical interpretability [33]. Fit indices included the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample Size-Adjusted BIC (aBIC), Entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMRT), Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), and Posterior Probabilities. Lower values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC indicate better model fit. Entropy values range from 0 to 1, with higher values suggesting greater classification certainty. LMRT and BLRT assess whether a model with k classes provides a better fit than one with (k–1) classes, with P-values < 0.05 indicating a statistically better fit. Posterior Probabilities reflect the accuracy of individual classification, with values exceeding 0.70 considered acceptable for reliable subgroup assignment [12, 22, 33].

Then, the influences of parental demographic-economic factors on the trajectories of peer support needs were analyzed. To control for potential confounding effects related to infant and maternal health status, descriptive and univariate analyses were performed on infant and maternal characteristics. The trajectory classifications derived from the LCGM were used as grouping variables to assess whether there were significant differences in infant health status across the identified trajectory groups. For univariate analysis, normally distributed quantitative variables were compared using independent sample t-tests (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA (for more than two groups). For non-normally distributed quantitative data, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 or Fisher's exact test.

The LCGM was constructed in Mplus version 8.3, and descriptive statistics and univariate analyses were performed in SPSS version 27. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

The ethics related to this study complied with the Helsinki Declaration. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University [grant number SJUPN-202132]. The purpose of the research was clearly explained to the patients. All participants were adults (aged over 18 years) and provided written informed consent to participate in this study. We distributed the questionnaire only to those who understood the study's purpose and consented to participate. All patients’ privacy were protected and respected.

Influence of parental demographic-socioeconomic characteristics on trajectory classification

With C3 (Low-level Rapidly Declining Group) designated as the reference category, the results of conditional LCGM indicated that parental age and financial difficulties were significant predictors of trajectory group membership (P < 0.05). Specifically, older parents were more likely to be classified into the High-level Stable Group (OR = 10.72, P = 0.003) or the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group (OR = 7.71, P = 0.009). Additionally, those reporting financial difficulties were more likely to fall into the High-level Stable Group (OR = 5.86, P = 0.024) or the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group (OR = 4.08, P = 0.046).

Due to insufficient sample size, employment status was fixed automatically by the model and could not be tested for its effect. Other covariates did not show statistically significant associations with trajectory classification (P > 0.05). Discussion

This study employed a longitudinal design to examine the trajectory groups of level of peer support needs among parents of preterm infants from the time of NICUs admission to two weeks post-discharge. The results identified three distinct trajectory groups of peer support needs. Among the demographic-socioeconomic factors, parental age and financial difficulties emerged as significant predictors of trajectory groups membership (P < 0.05). The findings highlighted that the demographic-socioeconomic factors should be paid attention to when we provide support for the parents, other than the disease-related factors that the clinical practitioners focus on.

Trajectories of peer support needs among parents of preterm infants

This study identified three distinct trajectory groups in the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants. Among these, the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group accounted for the largest proportion (67.11%). This suggests that most parents initially had a moderate level of peer support need, possibly due to the availability of existing support resources. The gradual decline in support needs observed during follow-up may be associated with changes in negative emotional states and postpartum fatigue experienced by these parents. The separation from their infants during medical treatment often elicits various negative emotions in parents, such as anxiety and depression [52, 57], which in turn heighten the need for psychosocial support [54]. Longitudinal studies have shown that such negative emotions tend to decrease over time [37, 60], potentially leading to a corresponding reduction in the need for peer support. Postpartum fatigue, defined as a decline in physical and mental functioning following childbirth [6], has been shown to be closely linked to depressive symptoms according to a recent meta-analysis [62]. Previous research has found that the trajectory of postpartum fatigue in most mothers is characterized by high levels in the early postpartum period, followed by a gradual decline over time [64]. This trend is, to some extent, similar to the peer support need trajectory with the largest proportion in this study—the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group. These findings suggest a potential dynamic covariation among postpartum fatigue, depressive symptoms, and peer support needs in parents of preterm infants. However, as the present study focused on the impact of demographic and socioeconomic factors on the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants, we had not incorporated assessments of parents’ emotional characteristics, which limited the ability to examine the true effects of these factors on peer support needs. Future research could further investigate the temporal interplay among these factors to clarify key influencing pathways, thereby laying a theoretical foundation for the development of more targeted support strategies.

In addition, the participants of this study were the primary caregivers among parents of preterm infants, with a relatively high proportion of fathers (67.1%). The gender imbalance may be related to the custom of postpartum confinement in East Asian cultures. Postpartum confinement typically takes place during the first month after childbirth, during which mothers stay at home and limit physical activity in accordance with traditional practices to alleviate postpartum discomfort (such as pain) and promote recovery [55]. During this period, caregiving and other responsibilities are primarily undertaken by fathers, which may explain the gender bias observed in the study sample. This imbalance may also help explain why the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group accounted for the largest proportion. Previous studies have shown that when infants are hospitalized in the NICU, fathers often experience a range of negative emotions, and their needs for social and spiritual support are frequently unmet [4]. However, due to factors such as stigma and social expectations, fathers often tend to conceal their support needs to some extent [1]. In addition, fathers often face systemic barriers, such as hospital policies and sociocultural norms that emphasize maternal involvement in caregiving, which limit their ability to fulfill expectations of an active caregiving role. The lack of formal and recognized opportunities for participation may also lead fathers to perceive themselves as being excluded from the caregiving process, resulting in a certain degree of emotional detachment and giving rise to distinct psychological and caregiving-related support needs [4]. Under the combined influence of these factors, fathers tend to exhibit a moderate level of peer support needs, which may have consequently influenced the findings of this study. Paradoxically, the challenges faced during the NICU experience may also strengthen fathers’ family bonds and enhance their emotional solidarity, which in turn may reduce their perceived need for peer support over time [4].

Parental demographic-socioeconomic factors influencing the trajectories

This study found that parental age and financial difficulties were influential factors affecting the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants, whereas no other demographic or socioeconomic factors were identified as significant predictors. A cross-sectional study showed that educational level was one of the factors influencing the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants. Parents with a higher educational level tended to have greater peer support needs, which may be related to the fact that individuals with higher education are more likely to adopt positive coping strategies [35]. The discrepancy between these findings and the present study may be attributed to differences in research design. Moreover, a previous study examining the trajectory patterns of supportive care needs of stroke patients reported that patients with lower education and lower income reported fewer supportive care needs [63]. The reason may be related to differences in the study populations and in the categories of supportive needs. The cited study focused on patients themselves, whereas the present study targets the primary caregivers of preterm infants. Moreover, compared with peer support, the scope of supportive care needs is broader, encompassing medical, psychological, social, and informational dimensions of support. Such support can be provided by a wider range of sources, including not only peers but also healthcare professionals [51]. Therefore, variations in research focus and conceptual frameworks may account for the observed differences in findings.

Parental age

This study found that the age of parents of preterm infants was a significant factor influencing the classification of peer support need trajectories (P < 0.05). Individuals with higher age were more likely to be classified into the High-level Stable Group or the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group. Age is known to be a critical factor affecting maternal and fetal health. A higher maternal age is associated with an increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes and a higher incidence of diseases among preterm infants, such as respiratory distress syndrome, necrotizing enterocolitis, and retinopathy of prematurity [48, 53], which may elevate parenting stress and negative emotions, thereby intensifying the need for external support. Previous studies have shown that professional psychological interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and expressive writing can effectively alleviate psychological distress in parents of preterm infants [45]. Given the constraints of peer support, future research could investigate the potential of combining professional psychological interventions with peer support. This combined model may offer a more comprehensive and tailored strategy to address the diverse and evolving support needs of this population.

In addition, the reason why parents with younger age are more likely to belong to the Low-level Rapidly Declining Group may be related to differences in social media adoption. Previous studies have shown that social media can provide reliable and diverse forms of peer support, including informational and emotional support, and can facilitate individuals’ social connections [40]. Compared with parents of older age, parents with younger age are more likely to use social media to connect with peers [15]. Future research could explore how parents of preterm infants seek peer support through social media and the factors influencing this process, as well as examine how digital approaches can be leveraged to facilitate peer support.

The findings of this study differ from those of another study [35], which found no statistically significant effect of age on peer support needs. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in measurement tools and research design. The referenced study employed a cross-sectional design and used a peer support needs scale comprising three dimensions: peer support, information needs, and intimacy needs. In contrast, the present study adopted a longitudinal design and utilized a scale encompassing four dimensions: Daily Care Knowledge, Information Support, Emotional Support, and Social Support. This broader scope of measurement may have provided a more comprehensive assessment, which could explain the observed differences in results.

Financial difficulties

This study found that parents of preterm infants experiencing financial difficulties were more likely to be classified into the High-level Stable Group or the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group (P < 0.05).

Preterm infants not only impose significant financial burdens on their families during hospitalization due to the costs associated with medical treatment, but also incur ongoing economic demands related to post-discharge care. These sustained expenses place additional strain on both families and society, particularly on parents already experiencing financial hardship [2, 32]. These objective financial hardships and the resulting subjective economic distress are collectively referred to as financial toxicity [20, 39]. Studies have shown that financial toxicity can impair individuals' treatment adherence, communication skills, and social functioning [20, 38, 39]. For individuals who previously lacked peer support, exposure to financial toxicity further exacerbates the difficulty in accessing such support. This leaves them in a persistent state of high need and contributes to the development of a High-level Stable Group trajectory.

Given that fathers constituted a relatively large proportion of the sample in the present study (67.1%), this gender imbalance among caregivers may also contribute to the heightened sensitivity of the identified peer support need trajectories to financial difficulties. A qualitative study focusing on fathers of preterm infants found that a substantial proportion of fathers described experiencing stress related to financial difficulties during their infants’ hospitalization in the NICU. These financial difficulties were reported to intensify negative emotional responses, such as anxiety and depression. Moreover, the combined burden of financial strain and routine hospital arrangements was described as disrupting intra-family communication and exacerbating overall family stress [4]. These factors may be associated with a higher likelihood that fathers of preterm infants experiencing financial difficulties are classified into either the High-level Stable Group or the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group.

Meanwhile, although parents of preterm infants with moderate levels of support needs may possess a certain degree of peer support resources that could help alleviate their demands, such support is typically limited to the provision of knowledge, skills, or informational resources. It rarely extends to sufficient financial assistance, failing to effectively mitigate their economic hardship [27]. In this context, the cumulative economic burden resulting from the treatment of preterm infants leads to escalating survival pressures—such as paying for medical expenses and maintaining daily living costs—for their parents. According to the Theory of Existence, Relatedness, and Growth [3], these pressures may prevent parents from adequately fulfilling their Existence Needs. This may compel them to reorient part of their peer support needs (Relatedness Needs) toward meeting basic survival demands (Existence Needs), thereby contributing to the trajectory pattern observed in the Moderate-level Slowly Declining Group.

Implications

This study reveals the trajectory groups of peer support needs among parents of preterm infants from hospitalization to the early post-discharge period, and explores their associations with demographic-socioeconomic characteristics. These findings offer important theoretical and practical implications.

On one hand, this study addresses a gap in existing literature by demonstrating significant heterogeneity in the trajectories of peer support needs, suggesting that peer support should not be treated as a static or fixed intervention. Instead, support strategies should be adjusted over time and tailored to different levels of need. Although the peer support needs scale employed in this study encompasses multiple dimensions, in this exploratory investigation we emphasized the overall trajectory of peer support needs to delineate general developmental patterns and their associations with demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. An RCT study [18] discussed that providing support to mothers with greater support needs appeared to be more effective in improving their caregiving capacity for their infants than supporting those with lower support needs. It also suggested that overlooking parental demographic characteristics in the allocation of support may lessen the effectiveness of the intervention [18]. Therefore, the results of this study demonstrated that both trajectory identification and group classification were valuable in capturing the dynamic patterns of parents’ peer support needs, providing a meaningful foundation for future fine-grained research. From a practical perspective, these findings helped healthcare professionals gain a general understanding of parents’ needs for peer support, indicating that the overall demand varied over time and underscoring the importance of offering timely and adaptive support across different stages. In addition, this study found that the peer support needs of parents of preterm infants may be influenced by multiple factors. Research focusing solely on demographic or socioeconomic characteristics may therefore be insufficient to explain variations in parents’ peer support needs. Building on this, future research could assess parents’ peer support needs in conjunction with other factors, such as their psychological well-being, to further explore how these elements interact and shape support requirements and outcomes.

On the other hand, the significant predictive effects of parental age and financial difficulties on support need trajectories underscore the importance of incorporating demographic-socioeconomic characteristics of individuals in the design and implementation of peer support services. In light of these findings, for older parents at a high risk of preterm birth, it is essential to conduct needs assessments for peer support early in the antenatal period to identify those with moderate to high levels of support needs in advance. Corresponding peer support should then be initiated during pregnancy to ensure timely engagement. Because peer matching for older parents is inherently challenging, initiating support at this earlier stage allows more effective matching with peers of similar age and experiences, as the pool of potential peers tends to become smaller once preterm birth occurs. Early intervention may also facilitate emotional adjustment and help meet their support needs more promptly. Moreover, a recent study targeting individuals with psychiatric disabilities introduced an intervention model that integrates peer support with economic empowerment [28]. It proposed that peer supporters could assist recipients in overcoming financial difficulties through six key pathways: engagement in financial wellness goals, financial hope building, financial wellness action planning, financial skills-building, navigation of financial services and asset-building supports, and offering mutual emotional and social support [28]. Future research may adapt this framework for parents of preterm infants.

Limitations

Although the participants were recruited from three well-recognized tertiary hospitals in Shanghai that serve a large and diverse patient population from across the country, the use of convenience sampling from a single city may still limit the broader generalizability of the findings. Variations in healthcare infrastructure, cultural practices, and access to support services across regions may shape parents' experiences and peer support needs. One major limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which may constrain the robustness of model fit indices and the generalizability of the identified latent classes. In the present analysis, model selection was informed by a combination of information criteria and classification quality indices, yielding a three-class solution with high internal classification accuracy, as indicated by an entropy value of 0.933 and average posterior class membership probabilities ranging from 97.0% to 98.1%. Nevertheless, these fit indices and classification measures can be sensitive to sampling variability and may overestimate classification precision in small samples. Consequently, they may not fully capture the stability or generalizability of the latent class structure in independent populations. Therefore, the identified trajectory classes should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive population-level classifications. Future studies employing larger samples and independent validation are needed to further assess the robustness and external validity of these findings. Furthermore, this study did not control for the potential confounding effects of parents’ psychological status, such as stress, anxiety, or depressive symptoms. These psychological factors are known to influence how individuals perceive and seek support [54]. The omission of these variables may have introduced residual confounding into the analysis. Financial hardship was measured only once at baseline and considered time-invariant, which could limit the ability to account for possible temporal fluctuations in economic status. Finally, although the PSNS-PPI demonstrated strong internal consistency and test–retest reliability, the scale’s responsiveness to change over time was not formally assessed. This limits our ability to confirm whether observed longitudinal changes reflect true shifts in peer support needs. Future research is needed to examine the sensitivity of the PSNS-PPI to intra-individual change across time.

Conclusion

This exploration study identified three distinct longitudinal trajectory groups of peer support needs among parents of preterm infants, highlighting the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of support needs over time. The majority of parents exhibited a moderate level of support need at the outset, which gradually declined. Parental age and financial difficulties were significant predictors of trajectory classification. These findings underscore the need for adaptive, stage-specific peer support interventions that account for the influence of demographic and socioeconomic factors on support dynamics. However, cautions were needed when applying and interpreting these findings considering the limitation of this exploration study.
