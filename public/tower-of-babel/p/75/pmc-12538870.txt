Association between obesity and the intersection of depression, sex, and race in Brazilian adults: a cross-sectional analysis of population-based survey
Authors: Thais Cristina Marquezine Caldeira, Daniela Silva Canella, Thays Maria Sant’Anna Rosa, Ivy Evangelista Costa Ramos, Luiza Eunice Sá da Silva, Rafael Moreira Claro, Taciana Maia de Sousa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA bidirectional relationship between obesity and depression is reported; racial and sex inequities may contribute to this relationship in low- and middle-income countries.MethodsCross-sectional study with data from 2019 Pesquisa Nacional de Saude (Brazilian National Health Survey), aiming to investigate the association of obesity and the intersection between depression, sex, and race/skin color in Brazilian adults (n = 87,497). Depression was investigated using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 and obesity by the Body Mass Index (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²). Sex and race/skin color were analyzed separately and combined: white men; white women; black men; black women. Odds Ratios (OR) were estimated using Logistic regression models adjusted by sociodemographic data. Obesity was the outcome, while depression and its intersections with sex and race/skin color were explanatory variables. Individuals without depression were the reference group in all models.ResultsDepression prevalence was 10.8%, and obesity prevalence was 21.3%. Depression was associated with a higher chance of presenting obesity in the total population (OR:1.37; p < 0.001). Women with depression had higher chance to present obesity (OR:1.55; p < 0.001) compared to those without depression. Black individuals with depression (OR:1.37; p < 0.001) and white individuals with depression (OR:1.38; p < 0.001) had similar higher chances to present obesity. Considering the intersection between sex and race/skin color, compared to individuals without depression, black women with depression presented 59% higher chance of obesity (OR:1.59; p < 0.001), followed by white women with depression (OR:1.50; p < 0.001).ConclusionsDepression was associated with an increased chance of obesity with this relationship varying by sex and race/skin color. The strongest association was observed among black women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24882-8.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of both obesity [1] and depression [2] represents a significant public health concern worldwide, given their profound impacts on individual health, quality of life, and burden placed on health systems. Since 1990, obesity rates have more than doubled among adults and quadrupled in children and adolescents (ages 5 to 19). In 2022, over 1 billion people globally were affected by obesity [1]. Depression similarly presents an alarming situation, affecting 5% of the global adult population. In 2023, it was estimated that around 280 million people were living with depression [2]. The association between these two conditions has been studied, with evidence suggesting that factors such as physical impairment and emotional eating contribute to the development of obesity in individuals suffering from depression [3]. Notably, a meta-analysis found a stronger association from depression to obesity, particularly among women [3].

Racial and gender inequities further complicate this relationship, especially in low- and middle-income countries such as Brazil [4]. For instance, medical diagnosis of depression is significantly more frequent in women (14.7%) compared to men (5.1%), and in white individuals (12.5%) compared to black individuals (8.2%)5. Similarly, disparities in obesity prevalence are evident, with 29.5% of women and 30.9% of black individuals affected, compared to 21.8% of men and 27.0% of white individuals [5].

Intersectionality theory provides a critical framework for understanding how overlapping social characteristics—such as race, gender, and socioeconomic status—interact to influence health outcomes [6, 7]. Incorporating intersectionality into population health research presents several challenges, including how to quantify these intersecting relationships, at what levels (micro and macro) they should be examined, and understanding how these factors are embodied in individuals’ lived experiences [8]. However, this approach allows for the identification of health inequities that might be overlooked when race or gender are analyzed independently [6, 7].

In this sense, finds on the interplay between depression, obesity, race, and gender may provide important information to support the equitable addressing of public health issues. Thus, this cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the association between obesity and the intersection of depression, sex, and race/skin color in Brazil, contributing to a broader understanding of health inequities and their underlying causes.

Materials and methods

Design and population sample

This is a cross-sectional study based on data collected from the 2019 Pesquisa Nacional de Saúde (PNS) (free translation: National Health Survey). The PNS is a nationwide household survey conducted by a partnership between Ministry of Health and the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics [5]. PNS relies on a clustered sampling strategy in three steps. The first step consists of a census tract or a group of tracts, the primary sampling units. The households are then selected within each census tract, the secondary sampling units. Finally, within each household, a resident, aged 15 years or older, is randomly chosen based on the list of residents made at the time of the interview. More information about the methodology is available in the official publication of the research [5].

For this study, data from Brazilian adults (≥ 18 years) who self-declared race/skin color black (including black or brown) and white were used (n = 87,497).

Data organization

The indicators of central interest for the present study are those related to obesity and depression. Obesity was determined based on the Body Mass Index (BMI), calculated using self-reported weight and height [body weight (Kg) divided by the square of height (m) (Kg/m²)] and classified according to the World Health Organization criteria, with a BMI cutoff of ≥ 30 kg/m². Depression was investigated using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ9), with a cutoff score of ≥ 10. PHQ9 consists of nine questions that assess the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks [8].

To complement the analyses, the sociodemographic characteristics of the individuals were included: biological sex (men | women); race/skin color (black | white); age group (18–24 | 25–34 | 35–44 | 45–54 | 55–64 | 65 years and over); education (up to 8 | 9–11 | ≥ 12 years); per capita income (minimum wage) (less than 1 | 1 to 2 | 3 to 4 | 5 and more) and presence of a partner/spouse (married or in a stable union: yes | no).

Data analysis

The population was described using distribution percentages (%) and 95% confidence intervals (CI95%), stratified by sex and race/skin color.

To explore the intersectionality of sex and race/skin color, indicators were analyzed both separately and in combination: white men; white women; black men; black women. From this organization, the separate and combined categories were used to describe the presence of obesity among individuals with and without depression.

Crude and adjusted Odds Ratios (OR) were estimated using Logistic regression models. The adjusted models included the following variables: age, education, per capita income, and presence of partner/spouse. Obesity was the outcome variable in models, and four explanatory variables were considered: (1) depression, (2) the intersection between depression and sex, (3) the intersection between depression and race/skin color, and (4) the intersection between depression sex and race/skin color.

Additionally, the interaction between depression and race/skin color associated with obesity was investigated using Logistic regression for the total population (adjusted by, sex, age, education, per capita income and presence of partner/spouse) and stratified by sex (adjusted by age, education, per capita income and presence of partner/spouse).

Descriptive analysis of the prevalence of obesity (BMI ≥ 30) and its different classes (Class I: BMI 30–34.9; Class II: BMI 35–39.9; and Class III: BMI ≥ 40) according to depression severity levels assessed by the PHQ-9 (Mild: 4–9; Moderate: 10–14; and Moderately severe/Severe: ≥15) were conducted, in order to explore the gradient of association between depression and obesity. Analyses were stratified by sex and race/skin color to examine variations in the prevalence of obesity classes across depression levels within these strata of interest and are available in the Supplementary Material.

Individuals without depression served as the reference category in all models. Significance was determined using 95%CI and p value ≤ 0.05. All analyses were performed with Stata version 18, using the survey module for complex samples.

The PNS was approved by the Brazilian Committee on Ethics in Research with Human Beings, Ministry of Health (nº. 3.529.376) and the database is available for public access and use (https://www.ibge.gov.br/estatisticas/sociais/saude.html).

Results

Data from 87,497 Brazilian adults (≥ 18 years) who self-declared race/skin color black (including black or brown) and white were analyzed. Characteristics of the studied population according to sex and race/skin color groups are presented in Table 1. No significantly different distribution was observed according to sex and race/skin color, and over 50% of the population was aged between 25 and 54 years. The highest prevalences of lower education level (up to 8 years of schooling) were observed among men (51.4%) and black individuals (55.5%). Additionally, the prevalence of per capita income above 5 minimum wage was higher among the white population, while the prevalence of per capita income below 1 minimum wage was more frequent in the black population (58.7%). The highest prevalence of depression was identified among women (15.0%) (Table 1).

The prevalence of obesity in the studied population was 21.3%, being higher among women (23.1%) than men (19.2%), with no significant differences for race/skin color. Obesity was more frequent among individuals with depression (26.8%) than those without depression (20.6%). The highest prevalence of obesity was observed among black women with depression (28.9%) (Table 2).

Depression was associated with a higher chance of presenting obesity in the total population (adjusted OR:1.37; p < 0.001). Compared to individuals without depression, women with depression had a 55% higher chance to present obesity (adjusted OR:1.55; p < 0.001), with no significance for men with depression. Black individuals with depression (adjusted OR:1.37; p < 0.001) and white individuals with depression (adjusted OR:1.38; p < 0.001) had similar higher chances to present obesity. Considering the intersection between sex and race/skin color, compared to individuals without depression, black women with depression presented 59% higher chance of obesity (adjusted OR:1.59; p < 0.001), followed by white women with depression (adjusted OR:1.50; p < 0.001), with no significance for white or black men with depression (Table 3).

The interaction analyses showed that the presence of depression increased the chance of obesity in both sexes, with a slightly higher magnitude among women (adjusted OR 1.45; 95% CI 1.22–1.73) compared to men (adjusted OR 1.41; 95% CI 1.04–1.86). In addition, black women had higher chance of obesity compared to white women (adjusted OR 1.14; 95% CI 1.04–1.26). However, there was no significant effect of the interaction between race/skin color and depression on the chance of obesity in the total population or in the sex-stratified analyses (Table 4).

Discussion

With a sample of over 87,000 adults, this study examined the association between obesity and the intersection between depression, sex, and race/skin color in Brazil. Depression was associated with higher chances of obesity in the total population, with this association being particularly pronounced among black women. Our study broadens the understanding of the relationship between depression and obesity, while highlighting the distinct and combined influences of race/skin color and sex on this association. These findings provide crucial data to guide public health policies aimed at combating the rise of obesity in low- and middle-income countries, such as Brazil.

In studies exploring the relationship between obesity and race/skin color in women, Brazil and the United States of America (USA) are key local for research, with the USA producing the highest number of publications [9]. National data from the USA, 2017 to 2018, showed that obesity prevalence did not differ significantly between men and women. However, non-Hispanic Black adults had the highest obesity compared to Whites, non-Hispanic Asians and Hispanics. Consistent with our findings, non-Hispanic black women had in the USA had the highest obesity prevalence when compared to black men and women of other ethnic backgrounds. Moreover, severe obesity (class III, BMI ≥ 40) was more prevalent among women and non-Hispanic Blacks individuals [10]. Despite similarities in racial classification between Brazil and the United States, socio-historical contexts differ significantly [9], which must be considered when comparing obesity prevalence from an ethnic-racial perspective.

Social determinants

Several factors, such as income, education, occupation, and gender, contribute to racial inequalities that are reflected in obesity rates [9]. They result in violations of rights and social marginalization, which black women subjected to sexism and racism face. Research in North America suggests that obesity is linked to schooling, age, gender and physical activity, with gender being the sole factor associated with obesity among black individuals [11]. This underscores the vulnerability of black women and the importance of sex/gender and race/skin color in understanding health inequalities. While socioeconomic variables may confer advantages among white individuals, they fail to protect black women from obesity risk. In Brazil, obesity in black women is associated with poor diet quality, a lower prevalence of consumption of in natura or minimally processed foods and a higher prevalence of ultra-processed foods [12]. It is also associated with the territory in which individual lives and body image, social inequalities and health care [13, 14].

The trend towards lower prices for ultra-processed foods could contribute to their increase in consumption, particularly among economically disadvantaged groups. The trend in the consumption of ultra-processed foods is more pronounced among Black individuals and populations with lower income [15], which may exacerbate the obesity epidemic if not addressed through targeted interventions. Residential environment also plays a role, with factors like social cohesion and perceived violence influencing obesity in lower-income neighborhoods, particularly for women [13].

Previous studies have demonstrated a positive association of depression and obesity. A meta-analysis found that depressed individuals had a 58% increased risk of obesity [16]. The association between obesity and depression varies by ethnicity [17], and factors such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical activity [18], help explain the differences in the relationship between depression and obesity. Additionally, racism, discrimination, stress, education, income, and economic difficulties faced by individuals can mediate the relationship between race/skin color and physical and mental health [19].

The correlation between depression and obesity is stronger among females [20], which cloud be explained by psychological, interpersonal and physiological differences [21]. Studies in Brazil have shown that Black individuals face higher risk of weight gain/increased BMI, particularly women [22]. This relationship is also repeated in depression, in which women and Black individuals are more affected [19].

The treatment and control of depression depend on a set of health actions, which face significant challenges, particularly regarding gender and race/skin color. Women and Black individuals often encounter greater barriers to accessing mental health services due to socioeconomic, geographic, and cultural factors [19]. Additionally, when these services are available, they may be inadequate or of lower quality. Stigma associated with depression is another obstacle, which can be amplified by factors such as gender and race/skin color [23].

Psychosocial mechanisms help explain the strong association between obesity and depression, particularly among Black women. Weight-related discrimination, internalized stigma, bullying, and unrealistic weight-loss expectations are among the factors that contribute to worse mental health outcomes [24]. Stigma, in addition to directly impacting psychological well-being, reduces the pursuit of healthcare and engagement in treatments, often centered on biomedical approaches that neglect social and psychological dimensions [25]. This scenario is especially worrying in Brazil, where precisely the groups most exposed to stigma, women, low-income individuals, and Black populations, also show the largest recent increases in the prevalence of obesity [26]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that interventions and conversations focused exclusively on weight loss can generate frustration and intensify depressive symptoms, especially when based on unrealistic expectations [27].

It is essential to recognize racism as a structural condition with a profound impact on population health. In addition to the mechanisms of racial oppression that affect the human body [28], racial inequality interferes with access to treatment, diagnoses, and healthcare. Therefore, these inequalities should be interpreted beyond a simple association between race/skin color and health, acknowledging racism as an active psychosocial mechanism that operates through discrimination and mediates this relationship by structuring opportunities and resources and generating stressors that affect mental health and access to health services [29]. In the Brazilian context, the underdiagnosis of depression among Black individuals highlights barriers to access, unequal treatment, and the persistence of structural racism [30], reinforcing the need for public policies that expand access to mental health care and address racism as a central determinant of health disparities. Moreover, the stigmatization of Black women’s bodies, framed historically within racist narratives, has shaped public health’s focus on weight as if it were the primary issue, often obscuring the broader sociostructural determinants that truly underlie health inequities [31].

Policy implications

Despite the importance of these issues, there are notable gaps in the literature regarding care strategies and health promotion specifically targeting black women [19, 32]. This highlights the need to incorporate gender and race/skin color intersectionality in public mental health policies to address the specific needs of these populations. In this context, the “World Health Organization Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030” emphasizes the importance of inclusive and equitable mental health policies and services, as well as the integration of mental health into all levels of care, with a focus on vulnerable populations, and efforts to combat stigma and discrimination [33].

In Brazil, public policies analyses concerning obesity recognized the relevance of social markers such as gender/sex and other social determinants. However, these policies often fail to address how these determinants intersect to produce inequalities. For example, while gender/sex is discussed in relation to reproductive cycles and increased vulnerability to obesity, race/skin color is frequently absent from discussions on obesity care. Consequently, the discourse in public health policies and documents tends to lack a comprehensive exploration of the combined effects of determinants like race/ethnicity and gender/sex on health outcomes [34]. Recently, the “Strategy for Obesity Prevention for Brazilians 2024–2034” was opened for public consultation [35]. This document aims to develop public policies addressing obesity as a social issue, adopting an intersectoral and intersectional approach. It highlights the social factors contributing to obesity, particularly among vulnerable populations affected by race/color, gender, and social class. The Strategy involves a multisectoral effort, recognizing the importance of food environments, social engagement, and stigma as key areas of action. Although it does not directly address the relationship between obesity and depression, it acknowledges the psychological effects associated with obesity, which are often linked to depression and have a significant impact on individual health. This underscores the need for mental health to be considered in the broader context of public health interventions aimed at combating obesity [35].

The prevention and treatment of obesity involves lifestyle changes, which can also reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety. Lifestyle interventions for overweight and obesity in women of reproductive age can reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety [36]. Despite this, there are concerns about the management of obesity in primary health care. Less than a quarter of the Basic Health Units in Brazil had psychology professionals, highlight an insufficient capacity to meet the growing demand for mental health services related to the obesity epidemic [37]. This scenario indicates that care for obesity in primary health care remains limited.

Limitations

The present study has some limitations. The PNS was not designed to be representative of race/skin color groups, so the analytical categories for this information may be under or overestimated. However, to minimize race/color biases, our analysis focused only on white and black individuals, as the proportion of other ethnic groups was too small. It is also important to reinforce that the distribution of race/skin color in the PNS is very similar to that observed in the 2024 Census. This study also advances due to its focus on the intersection of race/skin color and sex in the context of health inequalities, differing from other studies that include this variable as an analytical variable that is not incorporated into the discussions. Our study presents the intersection between these variables as a key factor in understanding disparities in obesity and depression in the Brazilian population. This research contributes to the monitoring of global health indicators by the Ministry of Health through the PNS and can support the development of public health policies within the Sistema Único de Saúde (free translation: Unified Health System) [7] aimed at addressing obesity and meeting the needs of diverse social identities. We also acknowledge that the use of self-reported data in this study may impact the findings. BMI was calculated from self-reported weight and height, which may lead to underestimation of obesity prevalence, does not capture differences in body composition, and may be subject to reporting bias related to age and sex [38]. Using BMI in a categorized form may also be more accurate than continuous values [39]. However, self-reported anthropometric data is widely used in large-scale health surveys and have been shown to provide reliable estimates in population-based studies [40, 41]. Furthermore, the reproducibility of self-reported BMI has been tested in studies conducted in the Brazilian population, supporting its use in epidemiological research [42, 43]. Furthermore, the PNS has a cross-sectional design, so our findings do not allow us to determine causality. However, the study aims to indicate that both conditions assessed are interconnected and correlated with gender and race and should be assessed and treated in an integrated manner.

While we do not view this as a limitation of the study, we emphasize that, although the data were collected based on biological sex, we chose to use the concept of gender in our discussion. This is due to the well-established understanding that health outcomes are influenced by both sex and gender, and these concepts are interconnected in complex ways. Gender and sex interact and shape the social and biological determinants of health [44]. The use of the gender construct allows for a more comprehensive analysis of health inequalities, reflecting not only biological differences but also the social expectations, roles, and norms that impact the health of men and women in different ways [44].

Finally, although our study used obesity prevalence as the primary outcome, it is important to recognize the limitations of a normative, weight-centric approach. The use of BMI as the sole criterion for obesity is problematic, as this measure is a limited indicator of health and does not account for body composition, fat distribution, or body diversity [45]. A more inclusive weight and health paradigm should consider how social and psychological factors, including racism, stigma, and depression, directly influence health behaviors such as eating patterns and physical activity [24, 25]. While BMI remains a relevant indicator for population surveillance [45], future research should integrate structural and psychosocial determinants to more comprehensively understand the complex interplay between obesity, mental health, and social inequalities. Future studies applying multilevel approaches could further investigate the influence of contextual characteristics, such as socioeconomic conditions of census tracts or regional context, on the relationship between depression and obesity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, depression was found to be associated with a higher likelihood of obesity, particularly among black women. These results underscore the intersectional nature of gender and racial inequalities in Brazil, revealing the disproportionate impact of both conditions on this population. Therefore, public health policies must prioritize addressing these disparities by developing targeted interventions aimed at the most vulnerable groups.
