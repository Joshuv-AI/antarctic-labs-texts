The pathoanatomy of cancer: order in paradox
Authors: Michael Höckel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe pathoanatomy of local cancer spread is approached by the ontogenetic cancer field model, deduced from aspects of evolution, development, immunity, and wound healing/regeneration in multicellular animals, postulating: (1) Cancer cells canonically respond to the disruption of tissue homeostasis caused by their uncontrolled proliferation with progressive de-differentiation, which paradoxically enlarges the cancer wound instead of healing it. (2) The de-differentiating cancer cells adhere in reverse sequence to a principle of topological order laid down during embryonic development by the metazoan cell type differentiation trajectory.The model predicts with high anatomical accuracy ontogenetic stage-dependent permissive tissue territories for local cancer propagation, i.e., cancer fields, as demonstrated for cancers of the alimentary and genital tracts. Assuming the ‘export’ of the local cancer field into the draining lymph nodes by tissue macrophages and regulatory T cells, the model is also applicable for regional carcinoma spread. The clinical translation into cancer field surgery has led to a marked reduction of locoregional recurrences and to increased cure rates.Keywords: Cancer, Pathoanatomy, Multicellular animals, De-differentiation, Cell fate

Introduction

Despite significant progress in cancer prevention, early diagnosis, and treatment current global cancer statistics demonstrate an increase in the incidence and mortality of cancer in general.1 Roughly half of patients with cancer die of their disease. In Western societies such as the European Union and the United States cancer is the second-most common cause of human deaths, affecting one in four to five persons.2 Resective surgery, the leading cancer treatment, is indicated in more than 70% of all patients suffering from carcinomas.1,3 Insights into the pathoanatomy of locoregional carcinoma spread are of fundamental importance to perform cancer surgery with optimal results.4 The majority of operations for local cancer control is currently carried out as wide excision, i.e., resection of the macroscopic tumour circumferentially surrounded by a metrically defined microscopically cancer-free tissue margin based on a model of isotropic diffusion-like local tumour propagation. However, the metrical definition of the necessary minimal margin width is often vague or debated.5

Regional carcinoma surgery is increasingly carried out with diagnostic intention by sentinel node biopsy. If regional control is mandatory to cure the patient with node-positive carcinoma, radiotherapy of the complete draining lymph node basin(s) with or without additional lymph node dissection is the clinical standard due to the tenet that the regional metastatic pattern is not predictable beyond the sentinel nodes.5 However, the isotropic diffusion-like model as a general principle of local cancer propagation is not compatible with clinical facts and the radiotherapy of whole lymph node regions with or without adjuvant resection of nodes bears the risk of an overtreatment.6 Rethinking the pathoanatomy of both local and regional carcinoma is therefore warranted with high priority.

The anatomical complexity of local tumours is already evident at the macroscopic scale. Complexity increases if the microscopic scale with interstitial, intravasal, and perineural cancer propagation is considered as well (Fig. 1). Likewise, the full topological picture of regional carcinoma spread including metastases in intercalated lymph nodes, ‘in-transit metastases’, and metastases in tertiary lymphoid tissues in addition to the metastases in basin nodes is quite complex. Nevertheless, the pathoanatomy of locoregional carcinoma propagation adheres to an ordering framework resulting from principles of metazoan development and regeneration.

Local tumour spread is not random

If the isotropic diffusion-like model of local cancer spread was valid, the width of the cancer cell-free resection margin must be related to the probability of local recurrence. A narrow margin should predict a higher rate of local relapses compared to a wider margin. However, although tested with many cancer series, a robust predictive significance of the cancer-free resection margin could not be demonstrated for tumour entities such as breast, vulvar, and cervix carcinoma.6

Assuming the existence of anatomically distinct permissive territories for local tumour propagation, i.e., cancer fields, provides an explanation for this fact: A narrow resection margin at the border of a cancer field indicates a low risk of local recurrence, whereas the probability for a local relapse at the site of a narrow margin width within the cancer field is high. When the minimal margin width is evaluated without its anatomical context, which is the case in current clinical practice, a robust predictive association cannot be demonstrated.

The existence of permissive and non-permissive tissue for the local propagation of individual carcinomas is histopathologically evident in specimens of surgical resection where tumours do not invade immediately adjacent tissues despite large tumour volumes. Examples are shown in Fig. 2.

Another argument comes from tumour landscapes, i.e., anatomical maps coded for the frequency of the tissues infiltrated by a tumour entity. Locally advanced cervix cancers infiltrate the bladder ten times more often than the rectum and the bladder mesentery/adventitia seven times more often than the mesorectum, although both organs within their mesotissues are directly adjacent to the malignant tumour.7 Vulvar cancers having originated in the interlabial sulcus invade medially the abutting labia minora three times more often than laterally the abutting labia majora. Likewise, vulvar cancers derived from the vestibulum infiltrate the lateral labia minora three times more often than the medial vagina. In contrast, vulvar carcinomas originating from the labia minora and the glans skin invade the adjacent interlabial sulcus laterally and the vestibulum medially with exactly the same probability.8

How is this obvious non-randomness, i.e., the order of local cancer propagation established? If transformed cell types are ‘experienced’ in distinguishing permissive and non-permissive tissue environments, they must have achieved this ability during the embryonic development of the corresponding normal cell type. The temporo-spatial regulation of cell types, i.e., the topological interference of populations of distinct cell types within the organism during development, is a cardinal feature in multicellular animals, the metazoa.9, 10, 11

Aspects of metazoan evolution and development: human ontogenetic anatomy

Malignant solid tumours, phenotypically characterized by uncontrolled cell proliferation and progressive destruction of tissue organisation, have been detected in almost all extant metazoan phyla with only a few exceptions.12,13 They occurred also in extinct animals evident from cancerous alterations in excavated bones.14

The evolution of metazoa has been traced to the pre-Cambrian period more than 600 million years ago.9 A phenotypic comparison between the extant choanoflagellates, akin to the unicellular metazoan common ancestor, extant porifera (sponges), akin to the last metazoan common ancestor, and cnidaria, the earliest phylum in the phylogenetic animal tree prone to cancer, provides clues to topological principles in the ontogenesis of multicellular animals that should be valid for malignant tumorigenesis as well. Choanoflagellates exhibit one cell type (i.e., a self-reproducing somatically inheritable cell state) and five additional cytotypes (i.e., temporarily stable cell states without self-reproducing ability). The different cell states can transit to each other either uni- or bi-directionally.9,10

All multicellular animals develop their mature organisms from the single fertilized egg, the zygote, through progressive cell fate transitions of progenitor cell types to the terminal cell types according to their species-specific ontogenetic tree. Porifera derive from just two somatically inheritable cell types, flagellated choanocytes and archeocytes, following the cleavage of the zygote. Both cell types can trans-differentiate into each other. The first-order topology of the animal is represented by one sheet of choanocyte- and one sheet of archeocyte-derived populations, more or less distended by a fluid-filled space (Appendix Fig. S1). Archeocytes differentiate post-mitotically into about ten terminal non-self-reproducible cytotypes including immune cells. Second-order topology in the mature animal forms different meta-structures by repetition and variation of the first-order topology elements according to the building plan of the sponge type.15 Both choanocytes and archeocytes are still totipotent in the adult state.16

Hydra, a species of the phylum cnidaria, which appeared two bifurcations further from the unicellular–multicellular transition in the phylogenetic tree, represents the most primitive animal in which cancer has been detected.17,18 After the cleavage and gastrulation stages its ontogenetic tree bifurcates into three self-replicating multipotent somatic cell types establishing the first-order topology of the organism as an internal sheet of endodermal epithelial cell types separated from an external sheet of ectodermal epithelial cell types by an acellular slit-shaped space generated by the basement membranes of the two epithelia (Appendix Fig. S2).19 Multiple interstitial cytotypes are dispersed between the endodermal and ectodermal cell layers. Second-order topology according to the hydra's building plan with two perpendicular axes generates a tube with a ring of tentacles around the mouth preserving the tripartite first-order topology in the body wall.

These evolutionary aspects of the early multicellular animals demonstrate the canonical association between a cell type and the anatomical territory of its population, i.e., the spatial position within the organism. The fate progression of a cell type from the zygote to the terminal state is accompanied by spatial specification of the anatomical territory of its population for each developmental step. The topology of a cell type's anatomical territory of an earlier developmental stage is the foundation of the topologies related to the later stages following the building plan of the organism.

In order to depict ontogenetic anatomy, the principle of cell fate progression, i.e., the branched trajectory of cell type differentiation coupled to the specification of the corresponding cell population's anatomical territory, is applied to the development pursued from the phylotypic phase through the post-phylotypic, pre-determination, and post-determination phases to the mature state. Prior to determination, delaminating and migrating cell types adapt their topological identity in response to their immediate environment as observed manifold during metazoan morphogenesis.20, 21, 22 Moreover, change of a cell type induced by a dominant surrounding of cells from the abutting population of a different cell type has been proposed as embryonic mechanism of boundary formation.23

As a consequence of the topological plasticity of pre-determination cell types, the permissive anatomical territory of an individual cell is not only confined to the cell type's home territory but also includes the adjacent home territories of other cell types, whose topological identities and fates can be adopted by the reference cell type. This integrative anatomical territory is defined here as morphogenetic field for a given cell type at a given developmental stage. After determination, the individual cells of a cell type are restricted to their anatomical home territory, termed compartment.24 Further cell fate progression allocates the cell type progeny to subcompartments of even smaller scales.

Ontogenetic anatomy maps the mature derivatives of the morphogenetic fields for a cell type's differentiation trajectory starting at the phylotypic phase to the mature state. Ontogenetic anatomical compartments can differ markedly from tissues attributed to organs or regions in medical textbooks and anatomical atlases. For example, the ontogenetic vulvar compartment includes the complete gynaecologic perineum and even the anterior sector of the anal skin but excludes the skin of the labia majora lateral to the interlabial sulcus.8 The knowledge of the ontogenetic anatomy of the female subperitoneum enables its precise dissection instead of the crude ‘space – ligament’ approach following current anatomical perception.6

In summary, this brief survey of metazoan evolution and development highlights the association of self-reproducing cell types with the anatomical home territories of their populations. The spatial dynamics of the home territories concomitant with the cell type differentiation trajectories taking the pre-determination topological plasticity into consideration are the foundation of ontogenetic anatomy. Below, I will show that the pathoanatomy of local cancer spread can be comprehended from the ontogenetic anatomy of the cell type from which the cancer originated.

Aspects of metazoan wound healing and regeneration: cell type de-differentiation

The evolution of multicellular animals as self-generating (ontogenetic) biological systems of cell types and their anatomical home territories had to establish mechanisms of immune surveillance and wound healing to ensure their sustained existence. The regenerative capability of multicellular animals is more or less inversely correlated to increased evolutionary complexity. Animals that evolved early in the phylogenetic tree, such as sponges and cnidaria, are able to regenerate the complete organism from single cells. Planaria, early phyla of bilateralia, can regenerate the whole worm from amputated fragments.25 Sea cucumbers are able to restore their complete alimentary tract.26 In the phylum of vertebrates, urodele amphibians such as salamanders, newts, and frogs in the tadpole stage fully regenerate their limbs and the tail after amputation, an ability lost in the common ancestor of amniota (i.e., reptiles, birds, mammals).27 Regeneration capacity is further limited in mammals, in which complete organ or appendage regeneration is no longer possible, but certain tissues such as skin, liver, intestine, stomach, lung, nerves can be restored to some extent after injury.28 Otherwise tissue restoration after damage is accomplished by scar formation, i.e., fibrotic tissue covered by epithelium.

A common feature in metazoan wound healing across all phyla is the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and the associated de-differentiation of adult cells to previous developmental states with increasing topological and cytotype plasticity at the wound site.29,30 The de-differentiated cells proliferate and re-differentiate into the previous cell type or trans-differentiate into a different cell type demanded by the tissue to be restored. EMT and the topological plasticity achieved by de-differentiation extends the permissive anatomical territory of the cells, which is necessary for their recruitment and the exertion of their regenerative functions.

The possible extent of de-differentiation, i.e., distance on the reversed cell type differentiation trajectory, varies within the metazoan phyla and species. This is illustrated here for the phylum of vertebrates with some examples. To regenerate a limb after amputation in salamanders, cell type de-differentiation has to reach the state of limb bud formation in the developmental history coinciding with the phylotypic phase of the embryo.31,32 The de-differentiated cells retain their positional and cytotype memory.33 For the regeneration of liver tissue after chronic injury in mammals biliary epithelial cells de-differentiate to bi-phenotypic cells that re-differentiate both into biliary cells and hepatocytes.34 Progenitor cells, which bifurcate to hepatocytes and biliary cells, exist in the post-phylotypic phase of the normal liver development in mammals. After peripheral nerve damage in mammals, Schwann cells de-differentiate to a pre-determination state in order to repair the nerve.35

Homeostatic epithelial restoration in tissues with fast replenishment, such as skin and intestinal epithelia, is accomplished by dividing stem cells continuously substituting the missing cells. However, in extensive and sustained wounds disruption of the homeostasis induces mainly post-determination de-differentiation of terminal cell types.36, 37, 38

The mechanisms limiting the reversed developmental distances observed with the de-differentiation capacity of cell types during wound healing and regeneration accompanying the phylogenetic progress are poorly understood to date.39 The de-differentiation suppression in mammals coincides with the emergence of certain tumour suppressor genes, which therefore have been proposed to also constrain mammalian regeneration.40

Metazoan tissue repair and regeneration is regulated by the innate immune system and from the evolution of vertebrates with jaws onward also by the adaptive immune system.41 Tissue-specific macrophages of the innate immune system respond to the disruption of tissue homeostasis mediated by damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) with a spatio-temporarily controlled spectrum of functions including pro-inflammatory and phagocytizing activity as well as immuno-suppressive and pro-regenerative functions.42,43 The latter manifests itself inter alia by the activation of regulatory T cells, tissue-specific remodelling of extracellular matrix, induction of angiogenesis, and release of proliferation and differentiation factors acting on precursor and stem cells by recapitulation of tissue-specific developmental programmes.44

The adaptive immune system contributes to vertebrate tissue repair and regeneration by means of tissue-specific regulatory T cells (Tregs).45,46 The pro-regenerative actions of this subset of CD4+ T cells are exerted in addition to the prevention of autoimmunity by restraining inflammation and immune responses. By encountering their cognate self- or tolerized antigens under conditions of disrupted tissue homeostasis and inflammation, activated Tregs expand clonally and interact with resident epithelial, mesenchymal, and endothelial cells to initiate and sustain repair and regeneration in a tissue-specific context.47 Particularly, Tregs, when chronically exposed to stress, stimulate the proliferation of tissue-resident stem cells and precursor cells at the site of tissue damage.48,49 Moreover, activated tissue Tregs promote angiogenesis by interaction with endothelial cells and remodel the extracellular matrix favouring invasion and proliferation of de-differentiated cells by interaction with resident fibroblasts.46,50

The vicious cycle of cancer progression: the cancer paradox

The model of tumour progression as a vicious cycle considers malignantly transformed cells as previous normal cells in which genetic alterations cause uncontrolled proliferation and loss of de-differentiation suppression. Uncontrolled proliferation disrupts the local tissue homeostasis, which initiates a wound healing reaction involving both the host organism and the transformed cells.

The macroscopic and microscopic similarities of a local malignant tumour and a non-healing wound have already been observed by Rudolf Virchow in the 19th century.51 Harold Dvorak fuelled this argument with striking cellular and molecular evidence.52 EMT and cell type de-differentiation of resident somatic cells within the wound environment are canonical responses in metazoa aimed at repairing and regenerating the damaged or lost tissue and affect also the transformed cells. However, contrary to normal cells, the cancer cells do neither cease proliferation nor suppress further de-differentiation in order to re-differentiate in a coordinated manner with other cells participating in the repair or regeneration process. Due to the loss of de-differentiation suppression, wound healing-induced de-differentiation of cancer cells increases their permissive anatomical territory and consequently extends the cancer wound. Therefore – paradoxically – the mechanisms that evolved in metazoa to restore tissue homeostasis spatially expand the cancer wound and increase cancer cell stress, which again provokes further de-differentiation thus propelling the vicious cycle (Fig. 4). The increasing ontogenetic dissociation between the progressively de-differentiating cancer cells and the adjacent normal cells with restricted de-differentiation potential explains the pathoanatomical complexity of advanced cancers limiting their surgical control. Progressive metabolic disturbance, particularly hypoxia, experienced by the cancer cells due to the uncoordinated blood supply and waste substance removal in the expanding cancer wound selects cancer cell states with increased metastatic potential and resistance towards non-surgical therapies as well.53

The cancer paradox. Unsuppressed de-differentiation of the epigenetically unstable cancer cells as canonical mechanism of wound healing/regeneration paradoxically extends and aggravates the cancer wound, which is generated by the uncontrolled proliferation of the cancer cells.

Pursuing the concept that malignant solid tumours exploit canonical metazoan mechanisms of immuno-suppression, repair, and regeneration suggests a role of the innate and adaptive immune systems by means of pro-regenerative tissue-specific macrophages and regulatory T cells for local cancer propagation and for regional spread of carcinomas. The destructive growth of the local cancer causes a sustained massive liberation of DAMPs, tissue self-antigens, and tolerized cancer antigens, which are drained together with tissue-specific macrophages and Tregs through afferent lymphatics into the regional lymph nodes. Interacting with the lymphatic endothelial cells, particularly in the marginal sinus and with other stromal cells of the node, sustained DAMP-stimulated macrophages and cognate self- and tolerized tumour antigens-encountering Tregs exert immuno-suppressive and pro-generative functions.53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60 Clonally expanding activated Tregs and macrophages secrete mitogens, trophic and differentiation factors specific for the stem and precursor cells, i.e., de-differentiated cells of the tissues of their residence.42, 43, 44,48,49 Cancer cells, which represent de-differentiated states of these cell types, should be particularly sensitive to these factors. It is reasonable to assume that the extracellular matrix remodelling directed by the tissue-specific macrophages in the lymph node transmits signals of topological tissue identity (although this has not been proven yet to the knowledge of the author). Based on these considerations the regional cancer field model postulates the ‘export’ of the local cancer field to the draining lymph nodes (Appendix Fig. S6). Certainly, additional mechanisms such as angiogenesis and lymph angiogenesis contribute to the pre-metastatic conditioning of the lymph nodes draining local carcinoma and additional intrinsic mechanisms of the cancer cells effect metastasis formation.

Cancer cell de-differentiation reverses the hierarchy of the corresponding normal cell's differentiation trajectory: the order of cancer

During embryonic development the morphogenetic field of a progeny population emerges within the borders of the precursor cell population's field. The ontogenetic cancer field model claims that the topological hierarchy established during cell fate progression, i.e., the cell type differentiation trajectory, is reversed during the malignant progression of the cancer cells, i.e., their successive de-differentiation. The organism allocates anatomically defined mature tissue territories to cancer cells at a specific state of de-differentiation that have been derived from the morphogenetic field of the normal cell at the corresponding state of differentiation.

This creates a principle of order to which the local pathoanatomy of a cancer and the pattern of regional spread in carcinomas adhere despite of the chaotic elements of tumorigenesis. From the anatomical extension of a cancer at its diagnosis the state of malignant progression, the ontogenetic tumour stage (oT) and consequently, the overall permissive local tissue of a cancer, its stage-associated cancer field, can be deduced (Fig. 5).

The cancer field model requires that the mature tissue, which has been derived from a morphogenetic field associated with a normal cell type at a specific developmental state, exhibits cues that can be interpreted as permissive by the transformed cells having de-differentiated to the corresponding state of malignant progression. The fixed relation of a metazoan cell type to its anatomical home territory is preserved throughout the developmental history as ‘positional memory’ and should also exist in the transformed state.61,62

The regional cancer field model suggests that immuno-suppressive and regenerative responses of the innate and adaptive immune systems to a local carcinoma ‘export’ the local cancer field to the draining lymph nodes. All lymph nodes downstream directly connected to the tissue infiltrated by a carcinoma can develop first-line metastases depending inter alia on the tissue-specific macrophage and Treg cell function and on the amount of tumorigenic carcinoma cells. The potential pattern of second- and third-line lymph node metastasis is determined by the lymphatic network, functional states of the lymph nodes, lymph flow, and existing macrometastases. The ontogenetic anatomy of the lymphatic system mapping lymph territories and basins matured from topographically distinct lymph node anlagen and lymph sacs/plexus, provides clues to the lymphatic network of a specific tumour, which are relevant for the pattern of its regional metastatic spread.

Clinical evidence

The relative frequency of anatomically distinct tissues to be infiltrated by cancer cells (cancer landscapes) as assessed for cervix carcinoma and for vulvar carcinoma mirrors the developmental distances of the infiltrated tissues to the tissue of cancer origin as predicted by the cancer field model.7,8 Other models of anisotropic local tumour spread tested so far including mechanical barriers have not been compatible with the observed topology of cervix carcinoma spread.7 Although the cancer cells’ sensing of topological tissue identity cues from surfaces and substrates is most probable in the context of the cancer field model, different mechanisms of pattern formation cannot be excluded yet. Comprehending the pathoanatomy of cancer spread from the knowledge of the ontogenetic anatomy of the normal tissue from which the malignant tumour originated is of particular importance with opaque mesostructures of the internal organs located in the sub- and retroperitoneum, such as urogenital mesentery, mesureter, visceral rectal fascia, mesorectum, mesocolon. In addition of being directly infiltrated by the local tumour, these tissues may contain intercalated first-line lymph nodes and tertiary lymphatic structures prone to metastasis formation. These mesotissues often abut essential neurovascular structures. Conventional surgical anatomy, which is not founded on the knowledge of the developmental history, predisposes to cancer recurrence and unnecessary morbidity. Cancer field surgery, such as total mesorectal excision (TME),63 complete mesocolic excision (CME),64 total mesometrial resection (TMMR)65,66 achieved a dramatic reduction of locoregional recurrences and morbidity and enhanced survival.

From the ontogenetic cancer field model it is evident that the complete oT3b cancer field of rectal carcinoma, i.e., the tissues matured from the post-phylotypic morphogenetic field, is resected with TME, whereas TMMR can extirpate only the oT2 cancer field of cervix carcinoma in total. The resection of oT3a cervix carcinomas would demand the inclusion of the bladder. Likewise, cancer field surgery of oT3a and oT3b bladder carcinomas necessitates the inclusion of Müllerian organs. External tissues subjected to the contingency of conventional anatomy, such as the perineum, are precisely delineated by ontogenetic anatomy with regard to local cancer spread. Applying ontogenetic anatomy for the surgical therapy of vulvar carcinoma by vulvar field resection and immune network-directed lymph node dissection without adjuvant radiotherapy achieved treatment outcomes superior to conventional surgery with adjuvant radiotherapy.8

According to the cancer field model the anatomical site and features of the late morphogenesis should correspond to those of the early tumorigenesis. Indeed, epithelial–stromal interactions late in the organogenesis, e.g., the glandular outpouchings in the development of the uterine cervix,67 mucosal basement membrane dissolution in the development of the bowel,21 and the canalisation of the vestibular plate in the development of the vulva68 are reflected by epithelial–stromal interactions as earliest morphological events in cancer (EMT and invasion).

The regional cancer field model involving tissue-specific macrophage- and Treg cell-mediated immuno-suppressive and pro-regenerative mechanisms explains the preferential location of micrometastases below the floor of the subcapsular and transverse sinuses of the draining lymph node.69 The model is also compatible with the relevance of the lymph territories and basins developed from distinct lymph node anlagen and lymph sacs to determine the pattern of regional carcinoma spread. Early cervix carcinomas first metastasize to lymph nodes of the iliac lymph territory before nodes of the mesenteric and lumbar lymph territories are colonized by cancer cells, whereas late stage cervix carcinomas may seed the first generation of metastases in nodes of the iliac, lumbar, and mesenteric lymph territories.70 Likewise, early rectal carcinoma involves primarily both intercalated and basin nodes of the mesenteric lymph territory, whereas locally advanced caudally located cancers can also metastasize into the lateral nodes of the iliac lymph territory.71 In line with the cancer field model claiming the ‘export’ of the local cancer field to the draining lymph nodes, carcinomas can form regional metastases even in the earliest stage of malignant progression infiltrating an ontogenetic subcompartment. In contrast, malignant progression of the cancer cell in terms of de-differentiation to the phylogenetic state and beyond is necessary for the colonisation of distant tissues. Consequently, distant metastases are usually late events in the clinical disease course of carcinomas.

Critique and outstanding questions for future research

The ontogenetic cancer field model was set up to comprehend and predict the pathoanatomy of locoregional carcinoma spread with the aim to improve cancer diagnosis and surgical therapy. It is founded on topological and morphological features and arguments. Molecular mechanisms are beyond the scope of the model. Nevertheless, recent progress in unveiling the complexity of gene regulatory networks would allow to test at least some aspects at the molecular level. Although genetic mutations and Darwinian selection are instrumental to transform a normal cell into a cancer cell characterized by uncontrolled proliferation and the loss of de-differentiation suppression, the model claims that the ‘driving forces’ of malignant progression – at least with regard to anatomic topology – are the canonical metazoan mechanisms of tissue repair and regeneration. Topological cancer cell progression is claimed to be hierarchically constrained by cues imprinted by the normal cell type's differentiation trajectory to explain the order of locoregional cancer propagation. However, genetic mutations and phenotype selection could influence the disease course of the cancer within the constraints of cell type de-differentiation by e.g., doubling time of tumour mass, onset of metastasis formation, sites of distinct metastases.

I have denoted the vicious cycle of malignant progression as paradox because it is based on canonical mechanisms that have evolved in metazoa to heal and not to destroy and finally kill the organism. Surely, in the context of the cancer cell's behaviour these mechanisms are not paradoxical, they rather execute a biological logic.

The states of somatically inheritable cell types pursuing their differentiation trajectory during the development of the organism and the states of the transformed cell type's malignant progression are temporary stable manifestations of their epigenome. 3D chromatin conformations for a specific genotype during cell fate progression are associated with the expression of programmes exerting decreased plasticity and increased specificity. These are proposed to be reversed in malignant progression,72,73 which is in accordance with the demonstration of similar transcriptomes of localized and distant metastatic states of prostate and colon carcinomas and of developmental states of their normal cell types.74,75

Finally, based on the cancer field model a reason for the ‘resistance’ of sponges towards cancer contrary to hydras can be hypothesized. Malignant tumours manifest from transformed cells if the latter are permanently ontogenetically dissociated from the cells of their environment, i.e., de-differentiated cancer cells existing in tissues of less differentiated or undifferentiated cells. In sponges, transformed cells would exhibit the same ontogenetic state as the archeocytes or choanocytes present in the mature animal, which both are totipotent and can trans-differentiate into each other.15,16 Uncontrolled proliferating cancer cells would therefore produce new sponges but not tumours. In hydras, however, cancer cells can de-differentiate beyond the state of the normal cells causing an ontogenetic dissociation between the cancer cells and the cells of their environment, the prerequisite for tumorigenesis.17,18

The cancer field model has been formulated and validated to date for certain carcinomas of the abdomen, pelvis, and female perineum. More research is warranted to exclude alternative explanations for the observed pathoanatomy of carcinomas, to specify or correct anatomical details, and to expand the model to other tumour entities. Clinically, the most benefit from applying the model to predict prognosis and optimize surgical therapy can be expected for carcinomas diagnosed at the spectrum of intermediate stages, which are the majority of cancer cases at present.1, 2, 3
