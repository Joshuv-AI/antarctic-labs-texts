Evaluation of X-ray Personal protective equipment for Orthopaedic Surgeons in theatrE (EXPOSEd) study
Authors: Sophie Donoghue, Clara Miller, Karen Chui, Hannah Sevenoaks, Deborah M Eastwood
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo evaluate the accessibility and utility of current radiation personal protective equipment (PPE) provision and understand the experiences and radiation protection practices offered to all levels of Trauma & Orthopaedic (T&O) surgeons in the UK.MethodsAn incentivized electronic survey was sent to all UK T&O surgeons during a six-week period in 2024. Responses to 27 multiple-choice questions were collected and free-text responses analyzed by theme.ResultsA total of 677 responses from 196 UK NHS hospitals were received: 228 recepients (33%) were female, 455 (67%) were resident doctors, and 222 (33%) were consultants, representing a response rate of 38% (341/897) for trainees and 11% (222/2114) for consultants. Overall, 171 (25%) agreed they could find appropriate lead gown PPE; 80 (12%) had been given dosimeters, although few had received feedback; 173 (26%) received mandatory training, but many more (328, 48%) had sought training individually. When undertaken, it was felt useful. Symptoms attributed to ill-fitting PPE were common and more likely to be reported by females (odds ratio 2.5). In total, 372 (55%) did not feel protected by their current PPE, and 569 (84%) were concerned about the effects of ionizing radiation (IR) on their health. Thematic analysis confirmed concerns relating to PPE availability, education, and inconsistent advice and guidance across hospitals. Health concerns and poor morale were also highlighted.ConclusionEmployers must address the lack of compliance with the UK legislation, IR Regulations 2017 (IRR17), in relation to access to PPE, training, and monitoring. Introduction

Ionizing radiation (IR) is a crucial adjunct to Trauma & Orthopaedic (T&O) surgery. IR can cause direct tissue injury, irreparable DNA damage, and is a risk factor for cancer and cataracts.1,2 Existing literature highlights the increased prevalence of all-cause cancer in T&O surgeons3-5 with several studies showing up to a 3.9-fold increase in breast cancer prevalence in female T&O surgeons compared to the general USA population3,6-8 and to other surgeons using IR.5,8

The UK IR Regulations 2017 (IRR17) define employers' legal duties regarding safe delivery of IR:9 they are required to 'risk assess, monitor, and adapt practice to ensure they are delivering the lowest amount of radiation to their employees', ensure 'appropriate training,' and provide 'adequate and suitable personal protective equipment' (PPE). Suitable PPE is described by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA).10 They advise T&O surgeons to wear gowns of lead weight equivalence (LWE) of 0.25 mm of the wrap around type, with protective eyewear and head/face protection.10 A 0.35 mm LWE should be worn when the radiograph source operates above 100 kV, such as during lumbar spine imaging.10

Despite the risks, legislation, and guidelines, several regional studies have shown insufficient access to appropriate radiation PPE.11-13 This issue is widespread, with similar results found internationally and across specialties.14-18 Previous studies also highlight the lack of IR training and dose monitoring.12,19 This study aimed to evaluate current radiation PPE provision, and document the experiences and practices of UK T&O surgeons.

Methods

A UK-wide cross-sectional incentivized survey of T&O doctors, designed and delivered through the online platform Google Forms (Google, USA), was conducted over a six-week period (26 June 2024 to 7 August 2024). A total of 28 questions assessed accessibility and utility of current radiation PPE provision and explored experiences, practices, and training in IR (Appendix 1 in the Supplementary Material).

The survey was distributed through British Orthopaedic Association (BOA) and British Orthopaedic Trainees Association (BOTA) mailing lists, training programme directors, BOTA regional representatives, sub-speciality societies, and a social media campaign on X.

Respondents

Overall, 677 responses were received, of which 448 were male (66%).

Statistical analysis

Survey responses were analyzed in Google Sheets and Excel (Microsoft, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as mean, range, and percentages. Further analyses are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. A thematic analysis of free-text responses was completed using Braun and Clarke’s six phase method:20 one researcher completed all phases, two researchers completed steps 4 to 5. Raw data was tagged with code then underwent serial refinement of themes (Appendix 2 in the Supplementary Material).

In total, 54 surgeons across 40 hospitals (8%) had access to axillary protection, and 28 across 21 hospitals (4%) had access to lead glasses. A total of 66 people (10%) had been measured for and advised on appropriate lead gown size.

Risk assessment and monitoring

Overall, 80 individuals (12%) had been provided with individual dosimeters, but only 22 (28%) received feedback on their IR exposure. A total of 23 respondents covering all grades had no knowledge of this equipment. In total, 55 (8%) had been offered an IR risk assessment at their place of work.

A total of 496 (73%) were unaware of who to escalate to regarding radiation risk concerns: 259 individuals (38%) had raised issues; and 176 (68%) felt these concerns were addressed inappropriately.

Education

Overall, 130 (19%) correctly identified 0.25 mm as the minimum LWE thickness gowns recommended by IAEA for T&O surgeons: 173 (26%) reported receiving mandatory IR training on starting at their current hospital; and 328 (48%) had sought out formal IR training. Of all those who had undertaken IR training, 248 (62%) felt it was relevant to their practice.

Adverse effects

A total of 600 of 677 participants reported experiencing at least one symptom which they attributed to the adverse effects of ill-fitting lead gowns (Table IV). Females had a significantly increased OR of 2.5 (95% CI 1.3 to 4.7) of experiencing at least one effect.

Perceptions of risk

When asked if they felt adequately protected by their workplace PPE, over half (55%) disagreed; 284 (76.3%) of those that felt unprotected were residents; and 569 (84%) were concerned about IR effects on their health (Figure 4).

Thematic analysis of free text comments

A total of 170 respondents commented on concerns regarding radiation PPE and suggested improvements. Frequency and categorization of themes are shown in Figure 5.

Count of respondents per theme from qualitative analysis to the free-text question: 'Please add comments if you have any particular concerns regarding radiation safety in orthopaedics or suggestions on how this issue could be addressed'. PPE, personal protective equipment.

PPE: Concerns regarding poor fitting/inadequate radiation PPE were most frequent. Rotational and female staff felt disadvantaged.

Health and morale: Comments on IR effects on health were also frequent. Several reported being diagnosed with cancer and/or having concerns about ongoing risks related to cancer. Others reported physical illnesses which they attributed to the ill-fitting radiation PPE.

Pregnancy-related concerns highlighted inconsistent guidance, risk assessment, and monitoring. Some reported improved PPE access during pregnancy.

Poor radiation PPE provision was linked with a sense of lack of responsibility and support from employers. A few highlighted positive experiences.

Barriers: Funding was cited as both a barrier and a solution to provision. Safety concerns led some to purchase personal PPE, but others were unable due to funding, storage availability, and local policies.

Education and national guidance: Recommendations of standardized UK radiation protection guidance were common, including minimum equipment requirements, dosimetry monitoring with feedback, and better training. Personalized fitted equipment was considered a way to overcome disproportionate access barriers for long-term rotational workers.

Discussion

The EXPOSEd survey on radiation PPE among T&O surgeons is the largest in Europe and second largest worldwide.21 The high response rate emphasizes the importance of this issue and highlights areas of concern.

Perceptions of risk

Overall, 569 respondents (84%) were concerned about the health effects of IR, and 372 (55%) felt unprotected by the PPE available in their workplace. This is similar to an international study, where 31% of T&O surgeons reported significant concern and 48% slight/moderate concern about their exposure.22

Those closest to the beam (surgeon/scrub nurse) and radiosensitive tissues such as the breast, eyes, and thyroid are at particular risk.23-25 Tissues with high physiological turnover during pregnancy or lactation are vulnerable to IR.26,27 In males, breast tissue is located directly behind the nipple, but in females, breast tissue extends into the axilla.28 The upper outer quadrant of the breast is the commonest site of breast cancer and numerous studies have reported increased prevalence of breast cancer in female orthopaedic surgeons.3,6-8,29

The image intensifier C-arm in orthopaedic theatres is often used in the lateral position with the surgeon’s arm abducted, exposing the axilla.30 This produces higher radiation scatter compared to anteroposterior position. The anthropomorphic study by Valone et al31 found the upper-outer quadrant of the torso was exposed to a higher dose of scatter radiation compared to the lower-inner quadrant. An in vivo study confirmed the axillary area receives the highest dose of body radiation.32 This highlights the need for well-fitting wraparound lead gowns with high axillary coverage.

Access to appropriate PPE

The long-term stochastic effects of radiation are reduced by wearing appropriate PPE.33,34 Radiation exposure is reduced by 94% when wearing a lead gown and 97% when wearing a thyroid collar.32,34 While previous studies have assessed compliance and access to radiation PPE, no other study has assessed adequacy of radiation PPE in accordance with international guidelines.19

The IAEA PPE guidelines for T&O surgery state that a well-fitting, 0.25 mm thickness wraparound lead gown is required, with 0.35 mm thickness for lumbar spine procedures.10 Protective eyewear and head/face protection is also recommended. Across all demographic groups, only 25% of respondents were always able to find such a gown; 39% were able to access a thyroid shield; and 4% accessed lead glasses.

Lack of PPE provision has been reported frequently. Raza et al19 found 29% of orthopaedic surgeons felt their hospital provided inadequate PPE. Similar findings are reported among orthopaedic surgeons in the East of England, where more than half reported lead gowns were not available in a range of sizes, nor in good condition. Interventional radiology trainees also experienced difficulties obtaining appropriate PPE, with 51% unable to find appropriately fitting lead gowns once a week or more.33 There is a consistent lack of thyroid shields across different specialities and countries.13,14,35

The literature on cataract rates in T&O surgeons is limited. While one study showed a ten-fold reduction in radiation exposure in hip and pelvic surgery when wearing lead glasses, another showed the ocular radiation exposure in trauma surgeons did not exceed annual recommended dose limits.33,36 The causal effect of IR exposure and cataract development is known; there are no negative effects from wearing lead glasses, thus their routine use as per IAEA guidance seems sensible.

In all, 8% of respondents had access to axillary protection. One study demonstrated that current lead gowns do not adequately protect the axilla but failed to show improved axillary protection when testing axillary wings.37 More research is required in this area.

A total of 132 surgeons had personal PPE, most provided by hospitals for consultant staff. Rotational staff are disproportionately disadvantaged, with poorer access to equipment despite evidence that junior surgeons are exposed to higher radiation doses, linked to their relative inexperience.23,38

Risk assessment and monitoring

A notably small number of respondents (80, 12%) had personal body dosimeters, and 23 individuals (3%) had never heard of this equipment. Similarly, a recent deanery survey found 73% had never been offered a dosimeter in their careers and in Ireland, only 15% had ever worn dosimeters.11,12 This is in contrast to interventional radiology residents, who are 99% compliant with dosimeter wear.5 This disparity may be due to reduced awareness of the importance of monitoring in T&O or better enforcement of legislation and attitudes towards radiation safety in interventional radiology.

Few risk assessments were provided to participants (8%), and many did not know who to inform if they had concerns relating to PPE, a finding echoed in other studies.12,18 As staff working regularly in close proximity of IR, the workforce should be afforded personal dose monitoring with timely assessments and feedback.

Education

Few individuals (173, 26%) had received IR training when starting their current post. Other studies have shown similarly poor rates for IR training from 38% to 62%.12,13,19,35 In total, 328 of our study population (48%) had undertaken their own formal IR training. Overall, 62% (247/398) felt the training was relevant to their practice. This contrasts with other studies where satisfaction with radiation training was low, ranging from 19% to 54%.19,39,40 Our relatively higher rates of satisfaction may be influenced by selection bias inevitable in voluntary surveys. Those more interested in radiation safety are perhaps more likely to undertake their own IR training and in turn, complete this survey. Despite concerns raised in 2021 by Raza et al,19 there is still no formal requirement for orthopaedic surgeons to undertake IR training, although this is compulsory for other specialties, and disappointingly in our study a significantly higher proportion of surgeons had received no training compared to those surveyed in 2021: a negative trend. Incorporation of standardized radiation safety training into the T&O curriculum would clearly mitigate these issues and has been shown to reduce radiation exposure.41

Adverse effects

A total of 600 respondents (89%) reported at least one adverse effect which they felt related to ill-fitting lead gowns. Females were twice as likely to experience adverse effects. These responses are subjective. Work-related musculoskeletal disorders are a prominent cause of injury among professionals.42 The prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders in T&O surgeons ranges from 37% to 97%, with 9% to 33% of surgeons requiring leave, work modification, or early retirement.43 A total of 55.6% of Irish T&O residents experienced back pain and 30% felt this was exacerbated by their PPE.13 Similarly, 56% of radiology residents have back pain with those wearing ill-fitting lead gowns twice as likely to experience pain.16 This has personal and financial implications for staff and organizations.

A recent study simulated the physiological effects of wearing different styles of lead gown: wearing a one-piece tabard resulted in significantly higher heart rates, temperature, and lower comfort scores compared to wearing a two-piece lead gown.44 Higher temperatures in operating theatres have been shown to be associated with discomfort, frustration, and fatigue, affecting surgical performance.45,46 Surgeons require well-fitting gowns to facilitate a comfortable environment for optimum operating conditions, and to prevent musculoskeletal problems.

Limitations

Any survey is limited by respondent bias. The responses to some questions were subjective but highlighted the concerns of our colleagues. Individuals with previous negative experience with access to radiation PPE may have been more likely to complete this survey. However, the large number of responses and broad demographics of respondents in our study should lessen these effects. The survey was tested internally but not formally validated. Statistical power calculations were not conducted.

Recommendations and future directions

The concerns raised in this survey should be addressed. All NHS hospitals should ensure adequate numbers of thyroid shields, lead glasses, and lead gowns are available in a variety of sizes and kept in good condition. Employers should also consider investing in axillary protection. Individual risk assessments for IR should be conducted on commencement at a new hospital, and advice given on the size of gown to be worn. This will identify any ongoing deficiencies in stock.

Standardized training on the use of IR in T&O theatres should be developed nationally and incorporated into mandatory curriculum requirements and at all hospital inductions. Training should include contact information for local radiation protection supervisors and advisors. There should be clear, concise, auditable and standardized national guidelines on style, LWE of gown and other radiation PPE that should be worn. Dosimeters should be given to all surgeons using IR with doses recorded regularly and feedback given.

Further research is needed to better understand the prevalence and relative risk of cancer in doctors who work with ionizing radiation and advice from the UK Health Security Agency has been sought.

In conclusion, an overwhelming proportion of T&O doctors are concerned about the risks of IR and feel unprotected by the current availability of PPE. Current provision does not meet international guidelines. Doctors are not routinely receiving training on radiation safety and are frequently not monitored for dose exposure. Prompt steps must be taken to ensure our workforce is trained, protected, and monitored, as is the employer’s legal obligation.

Take home message

- The current Trauma & Orthopaedic workforce is concerned about both the perceived and real lack of relevant information concerning radiation risk in the workplace, personal protective equipment availability, and appropriateness.

- These factors discourage doctors from entering our profession and leave those in the profession feeling unsafe.
