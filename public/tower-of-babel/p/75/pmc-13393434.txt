CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of MsMLO1 confers enhanced powdery mildew resistance in alfalfa
Authors: Zhuoting Hou, Qi Li, Yongqiang Wang, Hang Zong, Weiyue Zhao, Yiling Dong, Huanli Liu, Qing Wu, Jianghua Chen, Wen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Powdery mildew, a disease primarily caused by Erysiphe pisi, is one of the most devastating foliar diseases of alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a globally important forage crop. The MLO gene is a well-known susceptibility factor for powdery mildew in many plant species, but its role in alfalfa remains unclear. In this study, we used the CRISPR/Cas9 system to edit MsMLO1 in alfalfa cultivar 'Aohan'. We designed an sgRNA based on a conserved target site in the exon region of this gene, and introduced the genome-editing vector into alfalfa via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation. Among the regenerated plants, we obtained a homozygous mutant (Msmlo1-3) with all four haplotypes edited, as well as two heterozygous mutants (Msmlo1-1 and Msmlo1-2). In disease-resistance assays, the homozygous mutant exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to E. pisi, with markedly reduced hyphal growth and conidial density compared to wild-type (WT) plants, whereas heterozygous mutants displayed no obvious resistance. Importantly, the Msmlo1-3 mutant showed no detectable growth penalties or altered agricultural traits grown in a greenhouse for six months. Transcriptome analysis revealed that the MsMLO1 mutation affects a broad gene regulatory network, including genes encoding transcription factors (MYB, AP2/ERF, WRKY) and genes involved in phyto hormone signaling, cell wall remodeling, secondary metabolism, and reactive oxygen species pathways. Therefore CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of MsMLO1 conferred robust and durable powdery mildew resistance without compromising normal plant growth, highlighting the potential of MsMLO1 as a target for breeding disease-resistant in alfalfa.

Dear Editor,

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is a globally important forage crop that plays a crucial role as livestock feed. However, alfalfa production in China is threatened by more than 40 diseases, which systematically infect its leaves, stems, and roots, severely constraining industrial development [1]. Among these, powdery mildew, primarily caused by Erysiphe pisi, is one of the most prevalent foliar diseases of alfalfa. In later stages of this disease, the pathogen spreads systemically, leading to significant yield reductions. This disease is common in alfalfa cultivation regions worldwide, posing a serious threat to forage yield and quality [2]. In recent years, the development of CRISPR-mediated genome-editing technology has provided a tool to precisely and efficiently modify genes involved in plant responses to pathogens [3].

Editing host susceptibility genes to enhance plant resistance to pathogen infection has emerged as a promising approach for disease-resistance breeding. Loss-of-function mutations in S genes can successfully block pathogen infection and confer durable resistance to plants. The MLO gene was first discovered in barley (Hordeum vulgare) [4]. Loss-of-function mutations in MLO genes enhance resistance to powdery mildew in barley, leading to durable resistance against this disease. MLO genes regulate susceptibility to powdery mildew in plants such as Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), grape (Vitis vinifera), wheat (Triticum aestivum), rose, apple (Malus domestica), and pepper. MLO loss-of-function mutants in various crops generated through chemical mutagenesis, RNA interference, TALEN and CRISPR/Cas9 technologies [5] exhibited enhanced powdery mildew resistance. While knocking out MLO enhanced resistance to powdery mildew, in wheat, it negatively affected plant yield [6,7]. However, whether MLO regulats susceptibility to powdery mildew in the legume alfalfa remains unknown.

Here, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 to edit MsMLO1 in alfalfa, to investigate its role in disease resistance and potentially enhance resistance to powdery mildew in this crop. Using MLO amino acid sequences from multiple species known to be involved in powdery mildew susceptibility (including Medicago truncatula MtMLO1, Arabidopsis thaliana AtMLO2, Pisum sativum PsMLO1, Rosa hybrida RhMLO1, Nicotiana tabacum NtMLO1, Solanum lycopersicum SlMLO1, and Triticum aestivum TaMLO), a BLAST search was performed against the protein set annotated from the reference genome of the alfalfa cultivar ‘XinJiangDaYe’. A candidate protein with the highest similarity was identified and designated MsMLO1. Phylogenetic analysis further showed that MsMLO1 clustered with MtMLO1 in the same clade, indicating a close phylogenetic relationship between them. (Fig. S1–S2). Using the reference genome of alfalfa cultivar XinJiangDaYe [8], we identified a conserved target site within the first exon region of MsMLO1 gene across the four allelic sets and used it to design an sgRNA. After cloning this sequence into a backbone vector containing a hygromycin resistance expression cassette and an SpCas9 gene expression cassette to obtain the MsMLO1 gene editing vector (Fig. 1A), we introduced this vector into alfalfa 'Aohan' via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation. We extracted genomic DNA from the leaves of these transgenic seedlings using the CTAB method and amplified the target region by PCR using MsMLO1-F and MsMLO1-R primers (Table S1). After an initial screening of the PCR products via Sanger sequencing, we subjected samples with mutations at the target site to second-generation high-throughput sequencing to precisely identify the mutation types and calculate the editing efficiency. Among T0 generation plants, we successfully identified MsMLO1 with various types of gene editing. Among these, Msmlo1-1 and Msmlo1-2 were heterozygous mutants, with 3 and 2 out of the 4 haplotypes edited, respectively; Msmlo1-3 was a homozygous mutant, with all 4 haplotypes edited (Fig. 1B). This sequence change alteration causes a frameshift in the coding region, which is predicted to result in premature translational termination of MsMLO1, thereby producing a severely truncated protein (Fig. S3).

To identify the powdery mildew isolate used for inoculation, the fungus was isolated from infected alfalfa leaves, and genomic DNA of the isolate was extracted, and the rDNA-ITS region was amplified by PCR using the universal primers ITS1 and ITS4. The resulting sequence was subjected to BLAST analysis against the GenBank database and used to construct a phylogenetic tree. The results showed that the sequence shared the highest similarity (97%) with the rDNA sequence of Erysiphe pisi (Fig. S4). Based on this, the powdery mildew isolate used for inoculation was identified as Erysiphe pisi. We performed infection assays by inoculating Msmlo1-1, Msmlo1-2, Msmlo1-3, and WT alfalfa plants with powdery mildew spores collected from alfalfa leaves infected with this isolate. The homozygous mutant exhibited significantly enhanced disease resistance, whereas no discernible resistance was observed in the heterozygous mutants (Fig. S5). To further evaluate powdery mildew resistance in the homozygous Msmlo1-3 mutant, we collected leaf samples at 3, 7, and 14 days post-inoculation (dpi). Following decolorization and staining, we quantified the number of hyphae per unit leaf area was quantified under an optical microscope.

The Msmlo1-3 mutant displayed strong resistance to powdery mildew. At 3 dpi, no hyphae were observed on mutant leaves, whereas on WT leaves, the powdery mildew fungus formed rapidly developing haustoria, with hyphae elongating and some showing branching. At 7 dpi, only sparse hyphal growth was visible on the mutants; by contrast, WT leaves exhibited extensive hyphal elongation, increased branching, and additional haustoria for nutrient acquisition. At 14 dpi, hyphal expansion remained limited on mutant leaves, while on WT leaves, the hyphae had formed a dense, interconnected network covering the entire leaf surface. Consistent with these hyphal phenotypes, conidial density was significantly lower on mutants than on WT leaves, indicating that pathogen growth and development were effectively suppressed (Fig. 1C). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the hyphal counts were markedly lower on Msmlo1-3 than on WT at both 3 and 7 dpi (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, macroscopic examination at 14 dpi revealed abundant sporulating lesions on WT leaves, particularly on the abaxial surfaces, whereas mutant leaves remained largely symptom-free (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these findings pathologically validate the robust and durable powdery mildew resistance conferred by the Msmlo1-3 mutation.

Notably, mutant plants cultivated under greenhouse conditions for six months showed no obvious growth abnormalities. There were no significant differences (P > 0.05) between the mutant and WT in key agronomic traits such as plant height, fresh weight, branch number and stem node number (Fig. S6). This suggests that CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing improved disease resistance without causing observable adverse effects on plant growth and development. This result contrasts with findings in wheat, where MLO gene mutations often lead to phenotypic changes and yield reduction. This phenotypic difference may be attributed to the distinct allelic combinations in the mutant plants. Indeed, the 6-bp deletion is an in-frame mutation that results in the loss of only two amino acids and is generally not sufficient to completely impair MLO protein function. In contrast, the −2 bp and −4 bp frameshift mutations introduce premature stop codons, leading to loss of protein function. In this study, three independent mutant lines (Msmlo1-1, Msmlo1-2, and Msmlo1-3) were obtained, each carrying a distinct combination of alleles (Fig. 1B). Analysis of the proportions of mutant sequencing reads revealed that in Msmlo1-3, about 73.83% of the reads carried 2-bp or 4-bp deletions (loss-of-function), while only 26.17% had the 6-bp deletion (in-frame deletion); in Msmlo1-1, about 47.63% of the reads had 2-bp deletions (loss-of-function), 26.09% had 6-bp deletions, and 25.26% were wild-type reads without mutation; and in Msmlo1-2, approximately 25.30% of the reads had 2-bp deletions (loss-of-function), 26.18% had 6-bp deletions, and 48.52% were wild-type reads. Phenotypic analysis showed that all three lines exhibited resistance to powdery mildew compared with the wild type, and the line with the highest proportion of loss-of-function alleles (Msmlo1-3) displayed the strongest resistance (Fig. 1C–E and Fig. S4). These results indicate that even partial loss of MsMLO1 function is sufficient to confer effective resistance to powdery mildew. In summary, these results indicate that the loss of-function Msmlo1 mutants generated in this study hold good potential for breeding disease-resistant alfalfa.

Additional RNA-seq data will help elucidate the molecular mechanism of MsMLO1-mediated resistance to Erysiphe pisi. To investigate the transcriptional changes associated with MsMLO1 mediated resistance, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA seq) on leaf samples of wild type and Msmlo-3 mutant plants following inoculation with Erysiphe pisi, with three biological replicates. Among the identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were several genes encoding transcription factors, including members of the MYB, AP2/ERF and WRKY families (Fig. S7). These transcription factors play important roles in plant defense against pathogens [[9], [10], [11]], suggesting they might act as key regulators in the MsMLO1-mediated resistance pathway. In addition to transcription factor genes and DEGs involved in phytohormone signaling, DEGs associated with diverse biological processes were also identified, including cell wall remodeling, secondary metabolism, light signaling, calcium signaling, growth and development, DNA/protein modification, transporters, and defense responses. These findings point to the existence of an extensive regulatory network involving MsMLO1 during plant defense against Erysiphe pisi (Fig. 1F, Fig. S7–8). Future studies should focus on validating the roles of these DEGs in MsMLO1-mediated resistance and their associated signaling pathways. Such investigations will deepen our understanding of the functional framework of MsMLO1 and identify novel targets for enhancing resistance to Erysiphe pisi.

In this study, we successfully generated specific mutations in the alfalfa MsMLO1 gene using sgRNA-guided CRISPR/Cas9. Phenotypic experiments and gene expression analysis confirmed that the mutants exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to powdery mildew, a major disease of alfalfa. These results have important implications for breeding alfalfa, the fourth largest crop in the United States.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

All alfalfa materials were asexually propagated and cultivated under controlled greenhouse conditions at 23 °C day/18 °C night, with a of 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod and 60% relative humidity. Both WT and transgenic alfalfa plants were maintained in 2-gallon (approximately 7.6 L) pots, watered weekly, and fertilized monthly with Peters 10-5-10 fertilizer.

Identification of the MsMLO1 gene in alfalfa

Based on previously reported MLO amino acid sequences from species such as Arabidopsis thaliana and Triticum aestivum, a BLASTp search was performed against the alfalfa genome to identify candidate homologous genes, with an E-value threshold of ≤ 1e−5. Subsequently, multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MUSCLE, and poorly aligned regions were removed using TrimAl to retain reliable conserved domains. Finally, a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed from the trimmed amino acid sequence matrix using IQ-TREE 2. The best-fit amino acid substitution model was automatically selected by ModelFinder, and branch support was assessed using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates.

CRISPR/Cas9 vector construction

A CRISPR/Cas9 vector harboring a single sgRNA, driven by the Medicago truncatula MtU6 promoter, was constructed to induce targeted mutations in the selected MsMLO1 gene in alfalfa (Medicago sativa). The CRISPR/Cas9 backbone vector was provided by the Sanjie Institute of Forage. Pairs of oligos including guide sequences were synthesized as primers, annealed, and cloned into the BsaI-digested CRISPR/Cas9 vector using T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs, Beijing). The CRISPR/Cas9 vector containing the guide sequence was transformed into competent Escherichia coli DH5α cells. Colony sequencing was performed using T-F primers (Table S1) to confirm successful cloning. Individual colonies were propagated in liquid LB medium supplemented with 50 mg·L−1 kanamycin, and plasmid DNA was extracted from the cultures using a TIANprep Mini Plasmid Kit (Tiangen, China) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Stable alfalfa transformation and transgene confirmation

Various CRISPR/Cas9 constructs were transferred into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 via electroporation for plant transformation Agrobacterium [8]. Finally, regenerated plants were transferred to soil and grown to maturity in a greenhouse. Genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissues of the regenerated materials using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method, and the presence of the transgene was confirmed was using specific primers (Table S1).

Detection of target gene mutations

Genomic DNA was extracted from alfalfa plants using the CTAB method [12]. Transgenic plants were screened by PCR for the presence of the hygromycin resistance gene. The MsMLO1 gene was subsequently amplified from transgenic plants carrying the hygromycin resistance marker, and the amplified fragments were sequenced by Sanger sequencing at Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi'an, China). To accurately assess the MsMLO1 mutations, the transgenic lines Msmlo1-1, Msmlo1-2, and Msmlo1-3 were selected. MsMLO1 gene was amplified from these plants, and the amplicons were subjected to next-generation sequencing (NGS) at Biorun (Wuhan, China). To exclude potential sequencing errors, only mutations with a read frequency >0.1% were subjected to further analysis.

Molecular identification of the pathogen

To determine the taxonomic status of the powerdy mildew isolate used in this study, conidia were collected from alfalfa leaves infected with powdery mildew provided by the Sanjie Institute of Forage. Genomic DNA was extracted from the pathogen using a fungal DNA extraction kit (Pyeast Biotechnology). The rDNA-ITS region was amplified by PCR using the universal primers ITS1 and ITS4 (Table S1) with the extracted DNA as a template. The PCR products were separated by electrophoresis in a 1% agarose gel, excised and purified, and subjected to bidirectional Sanger sequencing at Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi'an, China). The sequences were compared against the NCBI database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) using BLAST to determine their homology with known sequences. Based on reference sequences of powdery mildew species from GenBank, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method in MEGA 12.0 software. Branch support was assessed with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Inoculation and evaluation of reactions against powdery mildews

The powdery mildew isolate (Erysiphe pisi) used to infect alfalfa leaves was provided by the Sanjie Institute of Forage as described in Section 1.6. Powdery mildew spores were collected from infected alfalfa leaves using a soft brush, suspended in sterile distilled water, and adjusted to a concentration of 2 × 105 spores/mL. This spore suspension was uniformly sprayed onto leaves of Msmlo1-3 and WT plants for inoculation. Leaf samples were collected at 3, 7, and 14 dpi.

Histological analysis

Erysiphe pisi infected leaves were collected at 3, 7, and 14 dpi and submerged in ethanol-acetic acid solution (3:1 v/v) to remove chlorophyll [13]. The treated leaves were stained with 250 μg/mL trypan blue in lactoglycerol solution (lactic acid:glycerol:water 1:1:1 [v/v/v]) for 15 min, and rinsed in the same solution at room temperature as described by Vogel and Somerville [14]. The samples were mounted on slides and examined under an opyical microscope at 100× magnification to observe and quantify hyphal development and conidia.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Leaf samples from control (WT) and mutant lines (Msmlo1-3) were collected at 0 dpi and 14 dpi, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored for subsequent analysis. Three biological replicates were harvested for each genotype at each time point. Total RNA was extracted from alfalfa leaves tissues using TRIZOL reagent (Ambion, http://www.ambion.com). To remove the residual DNA, the extracted RNA was treated with RNase-free DNase I (New England Biolabs, https://www.neb.com) for 30 min at 37 °C. Total RNA was quality-checked using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN ≥7.0). Ribosomal RNA was removed using Ribo-Zero, and the remaining RNA was fragmented to approximately 300 nt segments with a NEBNext Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module. Strand-specific libraries were constructed following the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit following the manufacturer's protocol. The libraries were quality-checked and quantified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads, yielding at least 6 Gb of raw data per sample. The raw reads were processed with fastp (v0.20.1) to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, generating clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the Medicago sativa reference genome (https://figshare.com/projects/whole_genome_sequencing_and_assembly_of_Medicago_sativa/66380) using HISAT2 (v2.0.4). Based on the alignment results, transcripts were assembled with StringTie (v2.1.4) and quantified using Kallisto (v0.48.0). The tximport R package was then used to convert transcript-level abundances into a gene-level expression matrix.

Data analysis

Agronomic traits and disease severity data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics software (version 20.0, IBM, Armonk, NY). The mean values and standard deviation of the mean (SD) were calculated from three replicates. Statistical significance was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by a post-hoc Turkey's test at P < 0.05.
