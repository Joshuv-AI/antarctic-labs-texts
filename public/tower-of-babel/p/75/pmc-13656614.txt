Persistent cognitive dysfunction in patients with migraine: a case series
Authors: Kenichi Kashihara, Yasuko Takeda, Minoru Irisawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive symptoms, including impaired concentration, slowed thinking, and forgetfulness, are common in patients with migraine. Although these symptoms are most prominent during the headache and postdrome phases, some patients experience persistent cognitive symptoms that extend beyond individual migraine attacks and interictal periods. Cortical spreading depression, a key pathophysiological mechanism of migraine, may contribute to neurological and cognitive manifestations and can be visualized indirectly using arterial spin-labeled magnetic resonance imaging (ASL-MRI). We investigated the clinical characteristics of persistent cognitive symptoms in patients with migraine, evaluated cerebral perfusion using ASL-MRI, and assessed the effects of migraine-preventive therapy.Case presentationWe present a cohort of 12 consecutive patients with migraine who exhibited cognitive symptoms for over one month, without identifiable physical, neurological, cognitive, or psychiatric disorders. These patients underwent ASL-MRI while experiencing cognitive symptoms. Their ages ranged from 15 to 37 years, comprising five men and seven women. Eleven patients reported cognitive symptoms as their primary complaint during their initial visit, with only three presenting with concomitant headaches on that day. The cognitive symptoms persisted beyond migraine attacks and/or interictal phases, with durations ranging from two months to eight years. All patients displayed regional hypoperfusion in the right or left cerebral hemisphere on ASL-MRI. Treatment with migraine-preventive medications led to improvements in cognitive symptoms. The consistent presence of regional hypoperfusion and the favorable response to migraine-directed therapy suggest that migraine-related cerebral hypoperfusion may contribute to persistent cognitive dysfunction.ConclusionsCognitive symptoms associated with migraine may persist for months to years, even in the absence of ongoing headache attacks. Regional cerebral hypoperfusion may reflect these persistent symptoms, and ASL-MRI may be a useful tool for detecting this abnormality. Migraine should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with unexplained, persistent cognitive symptoms, particularly when a history of migraine exists.Keywords: Arterial spin labeling, Aura, Cognitive symptom, Cortical spreading depression, Interictal phase, Persistent, Migraine, Postdrome, Psychiatric symptom, Hypoperfusion

Background

Migraine is a prevalent, disabling, and recurrent neurological disorder [1] characterized by a prodrome (e.g., fatigue, drowsiness, neck stiffness, and nausea), aura (e.g., scotoma, flashing lights, paresthesia, and motor dysfunction), headache phase, and postdrome (e.g., asthenia, tiredness, somnolence, and cognitive difficulties) [2]. Among these symptoms, cognitive dysfunction is the second most disabling aspect of a migraine attack [3]. Cognitive dysfunction occurs frequently during the prodromic (30%), headache (38%), and postdromic (40%) phases of migraine [4, 5]. Cognitive symptoms in patients with migraine consist of executive dysfunction and impairments in multitasking, inhibitory control, working memory, processing speed, visual-spatial processing, immediate and sustained attention, attention shifting, word retrieval, and verbal learning [3, 5]. Patients may report problems with concentration, difficulty in thought processing, reduced intellectual clarity or “fog,” diminished attention span, challenges in reading and writing, and difficulties with speech [5, 6]. Research supports cognitive impairment in patients with migraine across different phases of the disorder [7]. Worse cognitive performance is reported during the ictal phase compared with controls, which can persist into the postictal period [7].

Cortical spreading depression (CSD) is characterized by a slowly propagating wave of depolarization and inhibition in cortical neurons and glial cells throughout the cerebral cortex. It has been shown to sensitize trigeminovascular neurons via the peripheral release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which is implicated in the development of headaches [1, 2, 8]. CSD is thought to play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of migraine aura [2, 8, 9]. Perfusion imaging during the ictal phase using arterial spin-labeled (ASL) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in patients with migraine shows common perfusion alteration. This is characterized by initial hypoperfusion after symptom onset followed by subsequent hypoperfusion, which appears to be in line with the pathophysiological concept of CSD [10]. Although the direction and significance of these perfusion changes remain uncertain across studies [10, 11], they likely represent aspects of migraine-related pathophysiology. Thus, cognitive symptoms potentially related to migraine may be associated with such pathophysiology and, therefore, may be visualized using ASL-MRI. In a previous report, we described several patients with migraine who exhibited regional brain hypoperfusion lesions associated with neurological, cognitive, and/or psychiatric symptoms [12]. Notably, three of these patients experienced prolonged non-headache symptoms, such as hemiplegia, lasting over a week.

In the present study, we report on patients with migraine who exhibited persistent cognitive symptoms that extended beyond a single migraine attack and/or persisted throughout multiple interictal phases. To investigate the background of these persistent cognitive symptoms, we conducted evaluations using brain MRI, including ASL-MRI, and assessed the effects of migraine-preventive medications on these cognitive symptoms.

Patients and methods

We report on 12 consecutive patients with migraine and persistent cognitive symptoms who visited Okayama Neurology Clinic between April 2024 and July 2025. The diagnosis of migraine was made according to the diagnostic criteria of the International Classification of Headache Disorders, third edition [13]. No patient had been diagnosed with migraine before visiting our clinic. These patients presented with long-lasting cognitive symptoms persisting for over a month, without any identifiable neurological, physical, cognitive, or psychiatric disorders apart from migraine. For differential diagnosis, each patient underwent formal neurological testing and psychiatric interviews conducted by specialists. Each patient also underwent MRI, magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), and ASL-MRI while exhibiting cognitive symptoms. Patients aged > 50 years were excluded from the study due to the potential for prodromal dementia or other forms of cognitive decline. We also assessed the clinical characteristics of these patients and their therapeutic responses to migraine-preventive medications in relation to cognitive symptoms.

MRI

MRI, MRA, and ASL-MRI were conducted at Okayama Kyokuto Hospital to identify any organic brain diseases that could account for the cognitive symptoms. ASL-MRI was acquired at 1.5 and 2.5 s following the initiation of the labeling phase using a 3.0-Tesla MRI (Signa 750 W, GE Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan; pulse sequence: 3D spiral FSE, TR = 4,642 ms, TE = 10.7 ms, BW = ± 62.5, post-labeling delays of 1,525 ms and 2,525 ms, slice thickness = 4 mm/36 slices, matrix 512 × 6, NEX: 2, scan time: 2 min and 19 s). All patients concurrently underwent conventional MRI, including T2-weighted, diffusion-weighted, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging, as well as MRA, to differentiate between various organic etiologies such as Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, brain tumors, moyamoya disease, and epilepsy. ASL-MRI evaluations were conducted by M.I., who was blinded to the patients.

Ethical conduct statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The appropriate Ethics Review Board of Okayama Kyokuto Hospital approved this study (IRB No: KyoIR_2014004). Written informed consent was obtained from all of the patients, or from their parent in cases where the patients were aged < 20 years, to participate in the present study and for the publication of their MRI images.

Patient 1

A 34-year-old woman visited the Okayama Neurology Clinic with the complaint of severe photophobia. She had experienced recurrent headaches since the age of 15 years. Severe photophobia began at 31 years of age, which was accompanied by an increase in headache frequency to 10 days per month, as well as difficulties with concentration and comprehension, disturbed attention, forgetfulness, apathy, and daytime sleepiness. Frequent dizziness commenced at 32 years of age. Due to the persistence of photophobia, cognitive symptoms, hypersomnia, and frequent headaches over a three-year period, she sought consultation at our clinic. Based on her complaints and normal neurological examinations, migraine was diagnosed, and valproic acid was initiated. The frequency of her headaches decreased to less than half with a daily dosage of valproic acid up to 1000 mg. However, her cognitive symptoms, hypersomnia, dizziness, and photophobia did not improve and, in fact, worsened 11 months after her initial visit. MRI and MRA studies conducted at that time revealed normal findings; however, ASL-MRI demonstrated marked hypoperfusion in the right hemisphere of the cerebrum and cerebellum (Figure 1-1). A subcutaneous injection of galcanezumab, an anti-CGRP monoclonal antibody (mAB), further alleviated her headaches, cognitive symptoms, dizziness, and hypersomnia within one month, although photophobia persisted with reduced intensity.

Patient 2

A 23-year-old man presented to our clinic with a 1.5-month history of concentration difficulties, asthenia, and fatigue. He had a prior history of recurrent headaches for 11 years, which had resolved prior to the onset of his cognitive symptoms. Additionally, he reported a fuzzy sensation, recurrent dizziness, and abdominal pain. Migraine was diagnosed, and valproic acid was initiated at a daily dosage of 200 mg. Conventional MRI and MRA conducted two months later were normal. However, ASL-MRI detected regional hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–2). His concentration difficulties resolved 3.5 months after the initial visit, shortly after the daily dosage of valproic acid was increased to 400 mg. However, his chronic cognitive symptoms recurred at age 24 following an eight-month cessation of valproic acid.

Patient 3

A 20-year-old man presented to our clinic with complaints of daytime sleepiness and difficulties with concentration and cognitive processing. His history revealed that recurrent headaches began at the age of 5 years and are typically accompanied by motion sickness and abdominal pain. He reported the onset of orthostatic hypotension at 9 years of age and experienced challenges waking up in the morning starting at 18 years of age. At 19 years of age, approximately 10 months prior to his first visit, he began struggling with concentration and cognitive clarity, which also coincided with symptoms of apathy, depressive mood, anxiety, palpitations, hyperventilation attacks, daytime sleepiness, and insomnia. He expressed feeling overwhelmed by the demands of balancing his studies with club activities at the university. He then sought psychiatric help and received a diagnosis of depression. Treatment with standard antidepressant medications over the course of three months failed to improve his mood. However, daily administration of 25 mg lamotrigine had improved his depressive mood, although other symptoms remained unaddressed over the following five months. Therefore, he took a leave of absence from the university, returned to his hometown, Okayama, and sought assistance from our clinic.

During his initial visit, he reported the aforementioned cognitive, psychiatric, and sleep disorders, in addition to dizziness. He had experienced recurrent headaches and dizziness, as well as abdominal pain, over the past seven years. The headaches occurred two to three times per week without visual aura. Conventional MRI and MRA of the brain showed no abnormalities. However, ASL-MRI revealed hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–3).

After a daily regimen of 200 mg valproic acid was initiated, he experienced significant improvement in his symptoms, including headaches, hypersomnia, cognitive symptoms, psychiatric issues such as anxiety and hyperventilation attacks, and dizziness within two months.

Patient 4

A 16-year-old woman visited our clinic with complaints of headache and difficulties with concentration and cognitive processing, which had commenced one week prior to her visit. She reported easy fatigability that began three weeks earlier. Additional symptoms included a fuzzy feeling in her head, photophobia, shoulder stiffness, and episodes of fainting upon standing. Her heart rate increased by more than 30 beats per minute when moving from a supine to an upright position. She had no previous history of headaches and was suspected to have migraine and orthostatic dysregulation. A daily dosage of 200 mg valproic acid was initiated, which was subsequently increased to 400 mg. Within two weeks, the frequency and severity of her headaches decreased, and they ultimately resolved within five months. However, cognitive symptoms, including difficulties with concentration, a fuzzy sensation, forgetfulness, fatigue, asthenia, and orthostatic dysregulation, persisted. An ASL-MRI conducted five months after the onset of cognitive symptoms demonstrated a disparity in the left cerebral hemisphere (Figure 1-4). An increase in the daily dosage of valproic acid to 600 mg resulted in the resolution of all symptoms, including orthostatic dysregulation, within seven months of the onset of cognitive symptoms.

Patient 5

A 15-year-old woman presented to our clinic with complaints of abnormal visual patterns resembling a sandstorm, as well as cognitive symptoms including difficulty concentrating and a persistent fuzzy feeling. She reported that her headaches began at the age of 6 years, are generally accompanied by nausea and vomiting, and occur once or twice a year. At 13 years of age, she experienced difficulties waking up in the morning, which prevented her from attending junior high school for over two years. Visual disturbances, described as continuous visual snow, began at 14 years of age, along with episodes of palpitations, easy fatigability, and photophobia. Visual snow is a persistent visual disturbance characterized by the perception of continuous snow or flickering dots, which frequently occurs in association with migraine [14]. Seven months later, she developed cognitive symptoms and agitation. She sought treatment at our clinic one year after the onset of her cognitive symptoms, and a diagnosis of migraine was established. Conventional brain MRI and MRA results were normal; however, ASL-MRI indicated hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–5). Treatment with valproic acid alleviated her cognitive symptoms and difficulties waking up and reduced the severity of her visual snow. Three months after discontinuing the medication, her cognitive symptoms relapsed, coinciding with a migraine attack. During this episode, the cognitive symptoms were associated with a headache phase and resolved within one day.

Patient 6

A 22-year-old man presented with dizziness and cognitive symptoms, including a fuzzy feeling, difficulty concentrating, and disorganized thoughts. He reported recurrent headaches since the age of 20 years, along with episodes of abdominal pain, orthostatic hypotension, vertigo, and dizziness. Based on these symptoms, migraine was diagnosed. Brain MRI and MRA results were normal, but ASL-MRI indicated hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–6). Treatment with lomerizine at a dosage of up to 20 mg daily resulted in significant improvement within 6 weeks. The treatment was subsequently transitioned from lomerizine to valproic acid because of the worsening of headache attacks. However, eight months later, the cognitive symptoms reappeared. A daily dosage of 800 mg of valproic acid led to improvements in both cognitive symptoms and headaches.

Patient 7

A 37-year-old woman presented to our clinic with complaints of word amnesia, forgetfulness, dysarthria, difficulty concentrating, challenges in multitasking, stuttering, phonophobia, pain behind the eyes, palpitations, and daytime sleepiness. She had a history of mild, recurrent headaches that began at 30 years of age, occurring on average once a week. Her cognitive symptoms emerged 13 months prior to her first visit to our clinic, following the birth of her child. Due to these symptoms, she sought help from a psychiatric clinic, where she was prescribed lamotrigine for a diagnosis of depression but without any improvement. Initially, her cognitive symptoms worsened at night for the first 18 months, then persisted without diurnal fluctuation. Neurological examinations conducted during her first visit revealed no abnormalities. An ASL-MRI performed 19 months after the onset of cognitive symptoms detected slight regional hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–7). Treatment with lomerizine resulted in significant improvement in her cognitive and neurological symptoms, hypersomnia, phonophobia, and other migraine-related symptoms.

Patient 8

A 19-year-old man presented to our clinic with a 2.5-month history of forgetfulness, difficulty concentrating, increased errors, apathy, and daytime sleepiness. He reported a history of recurrent headaches, dizziness, and abdominal pain persisting for the past seven years. The headaches occurred once or twice a month and did not include visual aura. A daily dosage of valproic acid up to 400 mg alleviated the headaches but did not improve the cognitive symptoms. Conventional MRI and MRA performed one month after the initial visit were normal; however, ASL-MRI revealed mild hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–8). An increase in the daily dosage of valproic acid up to 600 mg resulted in a reduction of cognitive symptoms, somnolence, recurrent abdominal pain, and headaches.

Patient 9

A 37-year-old woman sought treatment at our clinic due to hypersomnia, daytime sleepiness, and forgetfulness. She experienced difficulty driving due to somnolence and reported challenges with concentration and calculation and increased work-related errors. She had suffered from recurrent headaches, occurring weekly, since the age of 20 years. Insomnia and daytime sleepiness began at 21 years of age, significantly impacting her ability to work in an office setting. Despite a diagnosis of idiopathic hypersomnia and daily treatment with up to 300 mg modafinil at a hospital, she noted no improvement. Cognitive symptoms, including forgetfulness and difficulty with concentration and calculation, emerged at 30 years of age and worsened at 36 years of age, alongside insomnia, apathy, and increased daytime sleepiness. This deterioration coincided with her resumption of office work seven months prior to her visit to our clinic.

At the initial consultation, her predominant complaints were morning hypersomnia and forgetfulness. The cognitive symptoms persisted throughout the day, while the sleepiness resolved by the afternoon. ASL-MRI demonstrated mild hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–9), suggesting that her cognitive symptoms were likely related to migraine. Treatment with 600 mg of valproic acid was ineffective. However, subcutaneous injections of fremanezumab, an anti-CGRP mAB, resulted in marked improvement in forgetfulness, difficulty concentrating, hypersomnia, insomnia, and headaches within a month.

Patient 10

A 29-year-old man presented to our clinic with a 4-year history of forgetfulness and concentration difficulties, with mild fluctuations in severity. He also reported recurrent headaches since 20 years of age, accompanied by orthostatic hypotension, photophobia, and abdominal pain. The headaches were mild and occurred on average twice a month. Neurological examination revealed postural tremor in the hands. ASL-MRI indicated hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–10). Migraine and essential tremor were diagnosed. A daily regimen of 400 mg of valproic acid led to a reduction in cognitive symptoms over the course of a month, allowing him to return to work.

Patient 11

A 17-year-old woman presented to our clinic with complaints of frequent headaches occurring three to four times per week, along with vertigo and phonophobia. She reported that her recurrent headaches had begun at the age of 10 years, accompanied by visual aura and orthostatic hypotension. At the age of 13 years, she experienced significant depressive mood, apathy, hyperventilation attacks, and wrist-cutting, as well as mild cognitive symptoms, including difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness, and a sensation of mental fog, hypersomnia, and general weakness in the morning, which hindered her ability to attend school. Consequently, she sought help from a psychiatrist and received a diagnosis of depression. Treatment with antidepressants resulted in partial improvement of her symptoms; specifically, her behavioral and psychological symptoms decreased, while cognitive symptoms persisted.

A diagnosis of migraine was established. Daily treatment with 200 mg of valproic acid reduced the frequency of her headaches to one or two episodes per month, and both vertigo and phonophobia resolved within a month. Thus, the treatment was subsequently completed, although her cognitive symptoms remained unsolved.

At 18 years, she again presented to our clinic with an acute onset of weakness in her left hand, as well as a worsening of her difficulties with concentration, cognitive processing, speech, and forgetfulness. Notably, the frequency of her headaches continued to be once or twice a month. MRI and MRA results were unremarkable, except for evidence of hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere on ASL-MRI (Fig. 1–11). A daily dosage of valproic acid up to 400 mg did not alleviate her cognitive symptoms. However, a subcutaneous injection of fremanezumab led to improvements in her cognitive symptoms and left-hand monoplegia, accompanied by the successful discontinuation of psychotropic medications.

Patient 12

A 25-year-old woman presented to our clinic with complaints of weakness in her left lower leg, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, recurrent headaches, daytime sleepiness, apathy, and episodes of fainting upon standing. The recurrent headaches began at the age of 21 years, accompanied by abdominal pain and fainting spells. She had received a diagnosis of orthostatic dysregulation, but treatment for hypotension proved ineffective. The frequency and severity of her headaches worsened at the age of 23 years, coinciding with her employment. During this period, fatigue, concentration difficulties, forgetfulness, daytime sleepiness, apathy, and orthostatic hypotension emerged or intensified. Due to these debilitating symptoms, she took a one-year leave of absence from work. She consulted a psychiatrist regarding her fatigue and hypersomnia, with no improvement.

At the age of 25 years, she experienced weakness in her right lower leg one month after returning to work. Due to these symptoms, she visited our clinic. During her first visit, her chief complaints included leg weakness, headaches, difficulty concentrating, apathy, and fatigue, which had hindered her ability to work. Additionally, she reported forgetfulness and recurrent abdominal pain, with no objective neurological symptoms observed apart from orthostatic dysregulation. We hypothesized that all these symptoms were related to migraine, although it was possible that her return to work exacerbated the underlying pathophysiology. MRI and MRA findings were unremarkable, but ASL-MRI revealed hypoperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1–12). Attempts to manage her migraines with conventional preventive or abortive medications such as lomerizine, valproic acid, and triptans were unsuccessful; both lomerizine and valproic acid exacerbated her headaches, and amitriptyline aggravated her hypersomnia. However, daily treatment with 100 mg of topiramate improved her symptoms within one month.

Discussion

In this case series, patients with migraine exhibited persistent cognitive symptoms lasting over a month, including difficulties in thought processes, concentration, calculation, forgetfulness, repeated mistakes, and verbal disturbances. This paper is the first to highlight that reduced cerebral blood flow on ASL-MRI may be associated with the persistent cognitive dysfunction in patients with migraine. Cognitive symptoms were the primary complaint for 11 out of 12 patients during their initial visit to our clinic. Migraine-preventive medications tended to be more effective for headaches than for cognitive symptoms. In case 1, cognitive symptoms intensified during treatment for headaches with migraine-preventive medications. Notably, cognitive symptoms persisted beyond migraine attacks and throughout several interictal phases. Three patients (cases 2, 5, and 6) experienced recurrent cognitive symptoms. In the second episode of case 5, the cognitive symptom occurred only during the ictal phase.

Cognitive symptoms are known to occur during migraine attacks, including prodromic, aura, headache, and postdromic phases [2, 4, 12]. Karsan and Goadsby [15] proposed that cognitive, fatigue, and mood symptoms may exist along a continuum during migraine attacks, rather than occurring in discrete and defined phases. A complete migraine attack can last up to seven days [2]. Gil-Gouveia et al. [3] reported that attack-related cognitive symptoms correlate with the intensity and subjective disability attributed to the migraine attack. However, apart from cases 1, 4, and 12, our patients did not present cognitive symptoms as a continuum of the headache phase. The cognitive symptoms in our patients typically did not correlate with the severity or frequency of headache attacks, suggesting that cognitive symptoms in migraine could occur independently of the headache phase. As shown in case 5, cognitive symptoms may occur both during the ictal and interictal phases, possibly with a common underlying pathophysiology.

Symptoms of migraine typically present in an episodic manner during migraine attacks. However, the cognitive symptoms experienced by our patients persisted continuously for months or even years beyond individual migraine attacks and throughout multiple interictal phases. This duration appears to be much longer than those previously reported [2, 16]. The cognitive symptoms maintained a consistent intensity, except in cases 7 and 10, who exhibited diurnal or daily fluctuations. The prolonged nature of cognitive symptoms in these cases suggests that they may not be strictly confined to migraine attacks. Various symptoms associated with migraine, such as allodynia, photophobia, visual snow, Alice in Wonderland syndrome, vertigo, dizziness, paresthesia, hemiplegia, ophthalmoplegia, and somnolence, may occur in a continuous manner [12, 17, 18]. Additionally, the susceptibility of patients with migraine to motion sickness and postural tachycardia may contribute to lasting changes in sensitivity, potentially mediated by central sensitization [19]. This persistent central sensitization or long-lasting regional hypoperfusion in the cerebral hemisphere may reflect the continuous manifestation of cognitive symptoms. Recently, the interictal burden of migraine has garnered attention among clinicians [20]. Such long-lasting symptoms may be part of this interictal burden.

Brain ASL-MRI was conducted during the presentation of cognitive symptoms, revealing regional cerebral hypoperfusion in all evaluated patients, although the reduction was minimal in cases 7 and 8. Conventional MRI and MRA findings in these patients did not reveal any abnormalities. Therefore, our findings suggest that cognitive symptoms in patients with migraine may be associated with regional cerebral hypoperfusion related to migraine. The beneficial effects of migraine-preventive medications on cognitive symptoms support this hypothesis. Such hypoperfusion on ASL-MRI may be associated with migraine aura [7]. Migraine aura typically precedes the headache phase and lasts for no more than 60 min [2]. However, recent advancements in neuroimaging suggest that the onset of aura or CSD is not a distinct phase of the migraine but can occur during or even after the headache phase [21]. Our findings align with their assertion that cerebral hypoperfusion can occur at any point during a migraine attack and, indeed, in the absence of headache.

In the majority of patients, the left hemisphere appears to be more affected. La Pegna et al. [22] reported that 62.8% of their right-handed patients with migraine exhibited unilateral headache on the right side, and 63.5% of their left-handed patients presented with a headache on the left side. In the present study, only case 11 was left-handed. It is possible that regional cerebral hypoperfusion in patients with migraine may occur preferentially on the dominant side of the brain. The cognitive symptoms associated with migraine showed improvement with migraine-preventive treatments. Discontinuation of preventive therapy was correlated with a relapse of cognitive symptoms, as observed in two patients (cases 2 and 5). Notably, the recurrence of cognitive symptoms in case 5 occurred only during a single migraine attack. The efficacy of preventive medications, such as lomerizine, valproic acid, and anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies (mABs), in alleviating cognitive symptoms supports the notion that these symptoms may stem from migraine pathophysiology.

In three patients (cases 1, 9, and 11), anti-CGRP mABs effectively improved cognitive symptoms. These mABs may function within the trigeminal ganglion to abort and/or prevent the amplification of pain signals that induce migraine headaches [23]. Additionally, anti-CGRP mABs may exert effects on the central nervous system (CNS), potentially reversing the migraine pathophysiology and improving cognitive symptoms.

Psychiatric disorders such as depression, dysthymia, adjustment reactions, or developmental disorders could be considered in the differential diagnosis for the cognitive symptoms in certain patients. For instance, cases 3, 7, 11, and 12 reported a history of consulting a psychiatrist following the onset of cognitive symptoms, ultimately leading to a diagnosis of depression. Case 3 experienced psychosocial distress prior to the emergence of cognitive and mental symptoms. However, conventional antidepressant therapy administered by a psychiatrist over three months did not alleviate the mental symptoms, except for lamotrigine, which improved depressive mood but not other mental issues such as anxiety, hyperventilation, and apathy, as well as cognitive symptoms, sleep disorders, dizziness, and headaches. In contrast, valproic acid led to improvements in all these symptoms within two months. Case 7 also sought psychiatric help at the onset of cognitive symptoms. Her concentration difficulties began shortly after childbirth and were accompanied by anxiety and insomnia. Postpartum depression could be a potential diagnosis in her case; however, her cognitive symptoms persisted for over a year and did not respond to antidepressant therapy but improved with migraine-preventive treatment. Childbirth poses a risk for developing not only depression but also migraine [24]. The cognitive symptoms experienced by these patients were associated with regional cerebral hypoperfusion and improved following treatment with migraine-preventive medications. Therefore, their cognitive symptoms may not be linked to psychiatric disorders but rather to migraine. Panic attacks are well-known to occur frequently in patients with migraine [25, 26]. Terlevic et al. [27] suggested that these migraine-related panic attacks are associated with hypothalamic dysfunction. Stankewitz et al. [28] reported the predominant role of the hypothalamus in generating migraine attacks. They suggested a loss of hypothalamic control over limbic structures during the headache phase [28]. In a previous report, we presented two cases with migraine who experienced panic attacks in association with regional cerebral hypoperfusion on ASL-MRI [12, 29]. Migraine may induce or accelerate the emergence of not only cognitive symptoms but also psychiatric symptoms. Additionally, persistent psychiatric, behavioral, and sleep-related issues, such as depressive mood, anxiety, apathy, hyperventilation syndrome, and hypersomnia, may have stemmed from migraine-related dysfunction of the CNS.

The results of this study indicate that cognitive symptoms may not necessarily occur in conjunction with migraine attacks and can persist for months or even years beyond an attack and/or throughout several interictal phases. Migraine should be included in the differential diagnosis of cognitive dysfunction, encompassing difficulties with concentration, attention, calculation, verbal function, and memory, even in the absence of obvious concomitant headaches. To diagnose migraine in patients presenting with persistent cognitive dysfunction, it is essential to obtain a history of recurrent headaches as well as associated symptoms such as visual aura, shoulder stiffness, dizziness, abdominal pain, photophobia, phonophobia, somnolence, and a family history of migraine, which are frequent manifestations of migraine [2]. It seems that psychiatric symptoms can be considered in a similar way to cognitive symptoms. ASL-MRI may serve as a valuable tool to confirm the cerebral hypoperfusion associated with migraine-related CNS symptoms including neurological, cognitive, and psychiatric symptoms.

Limitations

Cognitive dysfunction in the patients was evaluated based on self-reported cognitive complaints but not using standard cognitive assessments. Follow-up ASL-MRI scans conducted after improvements in cognitive symptoms were not available. Furthermore, brain ASL-MRI results for patients with cognitive or psychiatric disorders were not available.

Conclusions

Cognitive symptoms may occur in patients with migraine and can persist for prolonged periods, ranging from months to years. Notably, these symptoms may develop independently of overt headache attacks, and ASL-MRI may serve as a valuable tool for detecting regional cerebral hypoperfusion that could reflect migraine-related CNS manifestations.
