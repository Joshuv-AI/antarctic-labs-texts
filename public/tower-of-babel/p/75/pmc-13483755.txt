If you build it, will they come? A mixed-method formative evaluation of the start-up of a post-discharge clinic to improve care transitions
Authors: Seung-Yup Lee, Reid M. Eagleson, Larry R. Hearld, Madeline J. Gibson, Kristine R. Hearld, Allyson G. Hall, Greer A. Burkholder, Jacob McMahon, Shoaib Y. Mahmood, Corey T. Spraberry, Thalia J. Baker, Alison R. Garretson, Heather M. Bradley, Michael J. Mugavero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe transition from hospital care to post-discharge follow-up is pivotal for patient health and healthcare system efficiency. While Post Discharge Clinics (PDCs) are designed to facilitate this transition, these new ventures can face challenges, such as patient adherence to appointments. Formative evaluations can provide guidelines for their effective implementation.MethodsA mixed-method formative evaluation was conducted at the University of Alabama at Birmingham Health System (UABHS)‘s PDC to evaluate the first 12 months of operation. The study combined quantitative analysis of patient characteristics, appointment status, and comorbidities with qualitative interviews of PDC staff to assess operational performance and identify areas for improvement.ResultsThe quantitative analysis reported results on 2,168 PDC appointments during the study period. About half of the referred patients attended their appointment (52.1%), while the rest either cancelled (24.0%) or did not show up (23.3%). Statistically significant associations were found between patient demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors (e.g., marital status, insurance type, comorbidities) and adherence to appointments. The qualitative findings with PDC staff highlighted perceived benefits of the clinic, challenges in patient engagement, and the need for dedicated resources, such as social workers and specialists to optimize potential benefits.ConclusionsThe initial operation of the UAB PDC indicates the potential effectiveness of such clinics in improving transitional care. However, addressing barriers to patient adherence, particularly for socially vulnerable groups, and enhancing resource allocation based on stakeholder engagement are critical for the success and scalability of PDCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14712-x.

Introduction

Transitioning from hospital care to post-discharge follow-up is crucial for patient outcomes and healthcare system utilization [1, 2]. Inadequate follow-up, especially for patients with continuous medical needs and limited access to primary care, can lead to increased hospital readmissions and emergency department (ED) visits, burdening hospital operations and finances [3–5]. Post discharge clinics (PDC) - medical facilities that are designed to provide follow-up care to patients after they are discharged from the hospital [6–8] have been proposed as a solution to this transition gap, reducing readmission rates and enhancing medication adherence [9–12]. The promise of PDCs in improving healthcare utilization and outcomes has attracted the attention of many U.S. hospitals, especially in the wake of (dis)incentive programs such as the Hospital Readmission Reduction Program [13, 14].

Many new business ventures (such as the PDC) fail, a phenomenon labeled the “liability of newness” in the business and organization literature [15] that is attributed to: 1) dependence on new roles and tasks that have to be learned at some cost; 2) the amount of time and effort required to learn and coordinate organizational roles; 3) reliance on new social relationships that may lack a common normative basis or informal information structure for doing so; and 4) the absence of stable linkages to stakeholders when they begin operating. These challenges can lead to resource waste and skepticism among organizational members, thereby undermining program implementation efforts [16, 17]. Thus, the start-up period of new ventures like the PDC is a critical juncture for healthcare organizations.

This paper presents a mixed method formative evaluation of the initial 12 months of a PDC established within the University of Alabama at Birmingham Health System (UABHS), a leading academic medical center located in Southern United States. At the time of PDC implementation, our analysis of high-risk ED patients likely to meet PDC referral criteria had a 3-month ED revisit rate of 16.8%, highlighting the acute care burden and underscoring the need for targeted post-discharge follow-up mechanisms. The evaluation’s objectives were to identify facilitators and barriers to PDC operations, assess service improvement needs, and evaluate the feasibility of expanding it to a larger patient base [18]. This enabled a forward-looking evaluation of the PDC’s potential in reducing admissions and ED visits. Integrating both data and stakeholder engagement into the continuous improvement of PDC implementation can allow us to proactively address the risks, such as “liability of newness”, minimizing resistance to change and unintended consequences.

Methods

Study setting and design

UABHS, a tertiary care academic medical center in Birmingham, Alabama with more than 1,200 inpatient beds, 135,000 ED visits and 409,000 inpatient days annually, established the UABHS PDC in September 2022 to provide follow-up care within 7 to 14 days post-discharge from any UAB Medicine ED or Hospital. The PDC accepts referrals for any medically related conditions, initiated at the discretion of ED providers. Once a referral is made, ED patients may schedule a PDC appointment directly at discharge using tablets provided in the ED. If not scheduled at that time, they are contacted within 48–72 hours by the Access Pool—UAB’s centralized scheduling service—which coordinates ambulatory scheduling across the health system. Additionally, all patients discharged from UAB Hospital are contacted by the Access Pool within 48–72 hours and offered a follow-up appointment at the PDC, regardless of diagnosis.

It is staffed with a Physician/Medical Director, an Advanced Practice Practitioner, and a Registered Nurse, dedicating 50, 90, and 90% of their full-time equivalents to clinical activities, respectively. Support staff, including reception and social workers, are shared with other clinics in a co-located ambulatory care building. Core services at the PDC include medication reconciliation and prescribing for patients requiring multiple medications post-discharge. Providers also order any necessary laboratory tests or imaging based on the patient’s condition. For patients needing specialist care, referrals are made through the Access Pool, which contacts patients to schedule appointments. For those without a primary care provider (PCP), the PDC facilitates connection to a PCP to ensure long-term continuity of care. In this study, we employed a concurrent, mixed methods formative evaluation of the PDC. Quantitatively, we analyzed data from UAB’s administrative scheduling database to assess PDC patient characteristics, referrals, and appointment status (i.e., arrivals, cancellations, and no-shows) through univariate and bivariate analyses, using ANOVA and Chi-square tests, both in aggregate and monthly.

Qualitatively, we conducted interviews with clinicians and staff of the clinic. Interview topics were developed collaboratively with the PDC Medical Director and UAB health services researchers, specifically for this study. For more details on the interview topics, see Supplement 1. Focusing on the PDC’s perceived value and implementation challenges, we applied emergent thematic analysis [19, 20] to identify themes around early successes and challenges. This involved manual analysis without a pre-existing code book, suitable for exploratory research in understudied areas. [21] To reduce bias, two independent coders conducted the analysis, with a third researcher adjudicating discrepancies. This study, encompassing both quantitative and qualitative aspects, received approval from the UAB’s Institutional Review Board.

Data

Quantitative data

Our dataset encompasses 2,168 PDC appointment records from 1,716 patients in the PDC’s first year, including two data categories: appointment details and demographic data. Anonymized appointment data includes the scheduling and appointment dates and times, the last point of care before each PDC appointment, comorbidities, and PDC appointment status (arrived, no show, canceled, or bumped) with reasons for cancellations and rescheduling. Demographic and socioeconomic data captures city, state, Zip code, age, gender, ethnicity, race, marital status, language preference, and primary insurer, essential for understanding healthcare access, post-discharge utilization, and potential care disparities [14, 22]. Given the low expected rate of missing values across variables, the extent of missingness was assessed and is reported in the results narrative, rather than analyzed in detail.

Qualitative data

Semi-structured interviews with PDC staff were organized into three sections: background questions to understand the participant’s professional background, role, and daily practices in the PDC; the perceived value of the PDC, its challenges, and responses from patients and colleagues; and sustainability challenges and suggestions for improvement. The questions were open-ended, allowing participants to expand on their responses and not invoke a directional answer. The interviews were conducted and recorded via HIPAA-compliant Zoom and transcribed verbatim.

Three individuals were interviewed: a registered nurse with less than one year of experience at UAB, a nurse practitioner with 14 years of experience, and a physician with four years at the institution. These interviews represented the entire clinical team at the PDC during its early operation. Two analysts independently reviewed and coded the transcripts using Microsoft Excel. A codebook was initially developed from the interview guide and iteratively refined based on patterns emerging during transcript review. After independent coding, the analysts met to reconcile codes, merge related categories, and agree on overarching themes. Discrepancies were discussed collaboratively, and a third team member was available to adjudicate if needed; however, no adjudication was required. An inductive thematic analysis approach guided the development of final themes.

Univariate: Appointment Characteristics. In its first month, the PDC saw 90 patients, with numbers growing to over 230 monthly in the final five months of its first year (Table 2). The average time from referral to appointment was 5.5 days, remaining stable in the first half-year but varying between 4.1 and 7.0 days in the latter half (F = 2.99, p < 0.001). About half of the referred patients attended their PDC appointment (52.1%), while the rest either cancelled (24.0%) or did not show up (23.3%). Table 2 reports that the majority of patients (85.7%) received ED care as their last touch point with UAB Health System prior to their PDC appointments, with a growing proportion of patients whose last point of care was hospitalization before PDC appointments, reflective of initial roll out in the ED and subsequently, Hospital Medicine inpatient service. There was significant variation in the distribution in where the last point of care occurred across the 12 months (χ2 = 86.9, p < 0.001). Bivariate: Our bivariate analyses (Table 1, columns 3–6) show significant differences in patient characteristics by appointment status. Among PDC attendees, 52.2% were Black and 40.4% White. A higher proportion of no-show cases involved Black individuals (58.9%) compared to White individuals (31.0%) (χ2 = 30.1, p < 0.001). Females comprised 58% of cancellations, 53.3% of attendees, and 50.6% of no-shows (χ2 = 6.4, p = 0.04). Divorced/separated or widowed individuals were more likely to cancel their appointments. 13.1% of cancellations were from divorced/separated individuals while 9.1% of attendees were divorced/separated individuals (5.4% vs. 4.5% for widowed). Patients who did not show up for their appointments were more likely to be single and less likely to be married, compared to patients who arrived for their appointments (57.3% and 23.7% vs. 52.1% and 32.2%; χ2 = 20.5, p = 0.009). Insurance coverage distribution also varied depending on appointment status. Compared to attendees, patients who did not show up were less likely to have commercial insurance or Medicare and more likely to have Medicaid or be uninsured/self-insured (48.3% vs. 34.4% commercially insured, 20.1% vs. 17.7% with Medicare, 13.4% vs. 21.8 with Medicaid, and 18.2% vs. 26.1% uninsured/self-insured) (χ2 = 48.9, p < 0.001).

Chi-square analysis reveals significant behavioral differences linked to specific comorbidities like chronic kidney disease, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, venous thromboembolism, anxiety, autoimmune disorders, depression, dyslipidemia, and heart failure (Table 1). For instance, 49.9% of attending patients had hypertension compared to 38.4% of no-show patients, indicating their better adherence to appointments. In contrast, anxiety and depression were more common among no-show patients, suggesting barriers to accessing PDC for these groups.

Qualitative results

Below we describe key findings from each theme, supported by illustrative quotes.

Discussion

This paper presents early results from the implementation of a PDC for transitional care post-hospital or ED discharge. Our quantitative analysis showed a diverse patient population, predominantly comprised of Black, single, commercially-insured patients, utilized the PDC in its first year. Referrals to the PDC increased from 760 referrals (127 per month) in the first six months to 1408 referrals (235 per month) in the last six months, reflecting increased awareness of this transition clinic and partly resulting from collaboration with CTCC, highlighting its growing role in transitional care from hospital to community settings.

Marital status was association with appointment adherence; divorced, separated, or widowed individuals had higher cancellation and no-show rates, while single patients were more likely to not show up, possibly reflecting varying levels of social support affecting healthcare access and adherence [23, 24]. Patients with commercial insurance or Medicare attended appointments more consistently than those with Medicaid or no insurance, highlighting the healthcare accessibility and affordability issues, perhaps as a proxy for socioeconomic status [25, 26]. Addressing these barriers, e.g., reducing insurance complexities and out-of-pocket costs, and providing community health workers or patient navigators to those lacking endogenous social support, could improve appointment adherence. Racial disparities in appointment adherence were also found.

Similar to findings a large health system in Chicago, we observed a high no-show rate among PDC-referred patients [8]. The persistent low attendance despite increased appointments has strained the clinic with patient engagement barriers. Both our quantitative and qualitative analyses suggest the high no-show rate issue is multifactorial, possibly rooted in socioeconomic barriers and a potential gap in patients’ understanding of the importance of post-discharge care. This gap, combined with limited awareness of the PDC’s role, creates a confluence of factors affecting adherence as found in other post-discharge care studies [27, 28]. This underscores an opportunity to track an objective indicator readily measured at the point-of-care, to enhance the recognition of the perceived role and value of the PDC among health system leaders.

Behavioral differences associated with comorbidities were notable. Patients with hypertension showed greater adherence to follow-up care, likely due to increased health awareness or perceived healthcare need. In contrast, patients with mental health conditions like anxiety and depression had higher no-show rates, indicating barriers like stigma and motivational and organizational challenges for attending appointments [29]. This calls for personalized approaches to improve attendance among persons with mental health conditions [30, 31].

The PDC’s integration within a large academic medical center is an important step towards improving resource access and bridging care gaps. Staff interviews also suggested a perceived reduction in potentially avoidable or “soft” admissions, as ED providers expressed greater comfort discharging patients knowing that timely PDC follow-up was available. Staff narratives also underscored the need for dedicated social workers and specialists to address complex needs, particularly those related to social determinants of health. Initiatives could include targeted education campaigns, integrated care pathways, a tracking system for identifying no-show reasons, and community partnerships, to broaden the PDC’s reach.

The PDC’s broader strategy needs to emphasize the leadership’s role in navigating its operational challenges and meeting the clinic’s goal of managing approximately 6,000 patients’ post-discharge care in the next two years. The initial attention of the clinical and administrative leadership was largely operational with far less systematic attention on marketing the PDC within the medical center. Engaging stakeholders is vital for raising PDC awareness and achieving patient volume and revenue goals. This engagement can address the “liability of newness”, transforming initial challenges into learning experiences, enhancing the PDC’s likelihood of success and contributing to the medical center’s culture and reputation.

This study has limitations. First, its findings, based on one institution, may not be broadly generalizable. Future research could consider comparing different PDCs, despite challenges due to the relative novelty of PDCs and their unique implementations across different healthcare systems. Second, as an early formative evaluation, our study focused on provider perceptions and key process measures (e.g., appointment volume, scheduling, and adherence), rather than clinical outcomes such as readmission rates, ED visits, or patient experience. Future research is planned to assess the PDC’s impact on care quality and system-level efficiency. Third, our qualitative analysis did not include the patient voice. Although we interviewed frontline staff involved in PDC operations, we acknowledge that patients—especially those who do not attend scheduled appointments—could offer critical insights into barriers to engagement and care continuity. Incorporating patient perspectives will be a key priority in future research. These follow-up studies will be crucial for scaling up this care model and guiding decisions for health system leaders in optimizing the portfolio of ambulatory care delivery options.

Similar clinics are being established in other health systems [2, 8, 32]. The collective evidence seems to suggest their potential in post-discharge patient engagement. Our findings offer valuable insights for healthcare institutions contemplating PDC development, contributing to the growing body of knowledge on transitional care models and the iterative process of healthcare innovation. Promising early returns are balanced with challenges and opportunities for enhancement that will inform the evolution of the PDC at UAB and could help guide health systems in implementing such clinics.
