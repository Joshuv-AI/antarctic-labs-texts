Genome-wide identification of the MYB gene family and identification of candidate genes that involved in anthocyanin synthesis in cowpea (Vigna unguiculata [L.] Walp)
Authors: Lei Xia, Kai Wang, Feng Guan, Bo Shi, Xuetong Yang, Yuanyuan Xie, Xinjian Wan, Jingyun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MYB transcription factors are important for plant growth development and abiotic stress response, especially in anthocyanin synthesis. However, the comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the MYB gene family in cowpea is unclear. In this study, 227 VuMYB genes were identified in cowpea, including 62 1R-VuMYB, 161 2R-VuMYB, and 4 3R-VuMYB. Phylogenetic analysis showed that 2R-VuMYB genes could be divided into 26 groups, where members of the same subfamily exhibited relatively conserved motifs. The co-linearity analysis showed that 66 segmental duplication events were found in VuMYB genes, and 130 orthologous groups were identified among MYB synteny genes in legume plants. By comparative transcriptome and protein interaction analysis, 2 genes (VuMYB84 and VuMYB132) were identified as potentially involved in cowpea pod anthocyanin synthesis. Subcellular localization and transcriptional activation experiments showed that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 located in the nucleus, which had transcriptional activation activity. Sequence analysis and yeast two-hybridization showed that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 contained WER motifs (DEEDLIIRLHKLLGNRWSLIAGR), which could interact with VubHLH3. In conclusion, our results provide a systematic analysis of the VuMYB gene family and ideas for the VuMYB gene family’s involvement in the regulation of cowpea anthocyanin biosynthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08929-5.

Background

Transcription factors are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences, which could activate or inhibit the transcription of specific genes [1, 2]. As the regulators of gene expression, transcription factors are widely involved in various biological processes such as plant growth development, hormone signaling and abiotic stress response [3, 4]. Currently, several transcription factors have been identified in plants, including MYB, bZIP and bHLH [5]. Among them, as one of the largest transcription factors in plants, MYB transcription factors have been identified in several species such as rice [6], tobacco [7], red clover [8] and medicinal tea tree [9]. The MYB transcription factors were found to contain highly conserved repetitive sequences in the N-terminal DNA-binding region (DBD) [10], which are composed of 50–54 conserved amino acids and spacer sequences, where amino acid residues are involved in the DNA-binding process in the form of helix-turn-helix (HTH). Based on the different number of MYB repeat sequences, MYB transcription factors were divided into 4 types: 1R-MYB, 2R-MYB, 3R-MYB, and 4R-MYB [11]. Previous studies found that MYB transcription factors have played important roles in plant growth development [12]. For example, SlMYB102 enhanced salt tolerance by regulating Na-K homeostasis and ROS homeostasis in transgenic tomato plants [13]. Overexpression of MdMYB23 enhanced cold tolerance in transgenic apple callus [14], whereas BhMYB79 overexpression reduced salt tolerance in wax gourd [15].

As an important component of plant flavonoid compounds, anthocyanins accumulate in plant organs, which are one of the important phytochromes for different coloration in plants [16]. The MYB transcription factors were found to be involved in the process of plant anthocyanin accumulation and play an important role in the formation of color in different organs, such as pericarp, leaves and floral organs [17]. For example, MYB6 overexpression caused a remarkable increase in anthocyanin accumulation in populus tomentosa [18], and PcMYB113 expression in pistacia chinensis promoted anthocyanin biosynthesis during leaf coloration [19]. A further study revealed that MYB, bHLH and WD40 could form an MYB-bHLH-WD (MBW) repeat protein to regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis in plants. For example, PyMYB10, PybHLH and PyWD40 transcription factors interacted to form the MBW complex [20], which regulated the biosynthesis and accumulation of anthocyanins in Yunnan red pear. In persimmon, DkMYB4, DkMYC1, and DkWDR1 could form the MBW complex, which participated in the regulation of fruit anthocyanin accumulation [21].

As one of the top ten vegetable crops in Asia [22], cowpea has high nutritional and economic value. The MYB genes play important roles in plant growth and development and response to abiotic stresses, but there have been few reports on MYB gene-related studies in cowpea. Among these, VuMYB90-1 [23]and VuMYB114 [24] have been reported to regulate cowpea pod anthocyanin synthesis, but the comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the MYB gene family in cowpea is unclear. Here, the genome-wide identification and bioinformatics analysis of the VuMYB gene were performed based on the previously published cowpea ‘A147’ reference genome [25]. Moreover, the potential functions of the VuMYB gene were explored by analyzing the structural domains and expression patterns in different tissues and various stresses. In addition, comparative transcriptomic analysis, protein interaction prediction, protein function analysis, subcellular localization and yeast two-hybridization experiments were used to identify candidate genes involved in anthocyanin synthesis in cowpea pods. These results could deepen the understanding of the function of MYB proteins in cowpea plants, particularly in the process of anthocyanin synthesis in cowpea pods, which also would provide new directions for breeding high anthocyanin cowpea.

Materials and methods

Identification and characterization analysis of the VuMYB genes

To identify MYB genes in cowpea, the genome sequence and annotation files of cowpea ‘A147’ were downloaded from the database website (http://tree-bio.hzau.edu.cn/download/A147-yard-long-bean). The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) file of the MYB structural domain (PF00249) was downloaded from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/). To identify the MYB gene in cowpea, a preliminary screening was conducted using a hmmsearch tool with an E-value (e < 1e-5) in the TBtools software [26]. Subsequently, homology alignment with the Arabidopsis ATMYB protein [6]was utilized to identify candidate VuMYB genes using BLASTP with an E-value (e < 1e-5). Finally, the structural domains of the candidate VuMYB genes were validated by the NCBI conserved structural domain database (NCBI-CDD, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/) and the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/).

In addition, the ExPasy website (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was utilized to resolve the basic features of the VuMYB genes, including coding sequence length, isoelectric point (pI) and relative molecular weight (MW). Moreover, the subcellular localization of the VuMYB protein was predicted by the WoLF PSORT platform (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Chromosome distribution, conserved structure and motifs analysis

The chromosomal distribution of VuMYB genes was plotted based on the annotation information of the cowpea ‘A147’ genome by the MG2C [27]. Structural domain analysis was performed by the NCBI conserved structural domain database (CDD) to determine the number of MYB binding sites in all VuMYB genes. Conserved motifs of VuMYB genes were identified using the MEME online tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), setting the parameters: maximum motif number of 10 and maximum motif width of 100. The final gene structure and conserved motifs were visualized using TBtools software.

Phylogenetic analysis

The previous reported AtMYB proteins [6] were downloaded from the Arabidopsis genome database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Multiple sequence comparisons of 1R-VuMYB and 2R-VuMYB proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana and cowpea were performed by muscle software [28], respectively. The phylogenetic tree was constructed by the maximum likelihood (ML) method in MEGA11 software [29] and the boostrap was set to 1000. The phylogenetic tree was visualized by Evolview v3 [30].

Gene duplication and synteny analysis

Homologous gene pairs and synteny analysis of the VuMYB genes in cowpea were identified using the Multiple Collinearity Scan toolkit (MCScanX) software [31] and visualized by the advanced circos plugin of TBtools software [26]. The Ka/Ks ratios of the segmental duplicate gene pairs in VuMYB genes were calculated by using the Simple Ka/Ks Calculator function of TBtools software [26] for selection pressure analysis. The co-linearity analysis of MYB genes in cowpea and other 3 plant species (soybean, common bean and Arabidopsis)was identified by MCScanX software [31] and visualized by the advanced circos plugin of TBtools software [26]. OrthoFinder software [32] was used to find orthologous genes on MYB synteny genes in legume plants (cowpea, soybean and common bean).

Analysis of the Cis-elements and protein-protein interaction analysis

The upstream 2000 bp promoter sequences of VuMYB gene family members were extracted using TBtools based on genome annotation information. The promoter sequences were submitted to the PlantCARE online tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) for cis-acting element analysis. To detect protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks in VuMYB protein, Using Arabidopsis thaliana (NCBI Taxonomy ID: 3702) as a reference, the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was employed to analyze.

Expression analysis of the VuMYB genes and qRT-PCR

The expression pattern of VuMYB genes was analyzed by using the RNA-Seq data (Table S1) in NCBI under different tissues and conditions (including various biotic and abiotic stresses). Among them, transcriptome data of different tissues were obtained from developing seeds, roots, seedling pods, leaves, and young flower buds in ZN016 materials. The expression of VuMYB genes were calculated as the average fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values. In addition, the expression differences of VuMYB genes in anthocyanin synthesis were further analyzed by NCBI RNA-Seq data of pods in light green cowpea and red cowpea (Table S1). Differential expression genes were identified by DESeq2 [33] with parameters (p < 0.05 and |log2Fold-Change|≥1). Finally, heatmaps were drawn by the Heatmap plugin in TBtools software [26].

To validate transcriptomic results, commercial pods (15 days after pollination) from red and green pod samples were selected. These pods were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C refrigerator. Total RNA was extracted using Tirzol and first-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript™ IV 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Mix (TaKaRa, Japan). The primers were designed using the Primer-BLAST program in NCBI, which were listed in Table S2. qRT-PCR was performed in a 96-well plate using a BIO-RAD CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (BIO-RAD, USA) with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan). Gene expression level was calculated by 2–ΔΔCt [34] with 3 biological and 3 technical replicates. Data analysis was performed by Two-tailed Student’s t-test of SPSS. P value < 0.05 (*) and P value < 0.01 (**) are regarded as significant.

Subcellular localization

Based on the vector sequences, subcellular localization primers were designed (Table S2). The full-length coding sequences of VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 were cloned and ligated into the empty 35 S-GFP. The leaves of healthy Arabidopsis plants with about 4 weeks of growth were selected, and protoplasts were obtained by enzymatic digestion method [35]. Subsequently, plasmids (35 S-VuMYB84-GFP and 35 S-VuMYB132-GFP) were transformed into Arabidopsis protoplasts, respectively. Fluorescence signals were observed by laser confocal microscope Nikon C2-ER. Among them, the GFP green fluorescent protein excitation wavelength was 488 nm, and the chloroplast red fluorescent protein excitation wavelength was 640 nm.

Transcriptional activation activity assay and yeast two-hybrid

For transcriptional activation activity assays of VuMYB4 and VuMYB132, the full-length CDS of VuMYB4 and VuMYB132 were cloned into the PGBKT7 vector. The relevant primer information can be found in Table S2. The pGBKT7-VP16 was used as a positve control and empty pGBKT7 was used as a negative control. The plasmids were transformed into yeast strain AH109 and cultured on SD/-Trp, SD/-Trp-His + X-α-Gal and SD/-Trp-His-Ade + X-α-Gal plates medium, respectively. Moreover, AD-VubHLH3, BD-WER vector were constructed for yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) experiments. The plasmids were transformed into yeast strain AH109 and cultured on SD/-Trp-Leu, SD/-Trp-Leu-His + X-α-Gal and SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade + X-α-Gal media, respectively. All plates were incubated inverted at 30 °C for 3–5 d until yeast colonies grew. The 3-AT (3-amino-1,2,4-triazole) was used in Y2H experiment to avoid the autoactivation effect of VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 protein.

Results

Identification, characterization and chromosome distribution of VuMYB genes in the cowpea

In this study, a total of 227 VuMYB family genes were obtained in the ‘A147’ cowpea genome by HMM search and BLASTP. Based on their chromosomal locations, the 227 MYB genes were named VuMYB1 to VuMYB227 (Table 1). As shown in Fig. 1, the 227 identified VuMYB genes were distributed on 11 chromosomes of cowpea, of which Chr05, Chr03, and Chr09 contained a higher number of MYB members. The physical and chemical characterization of 227 VuMYB genes were revealed in Table S1. Among them, their protein lengths ranged from 81 aa (VuMYB212) to 1652 aa (VuMYB131) with an average length of 358.69 aa. VuMYB212 had the lowest molecular weight (MW) of 9.66 kDa, while VuMYB131 had the highest MW of 179.64 kDa. The Protein isoelectric points (pI) of the 227 VuMYB genes ranged from 4.43 (VuMYB13) and 10.75 (VuMYB106) with a mean of 7.32. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that 215 VuMYB genes were located in nucleus, 6 in chloroplast, 3 in cytoplast, 1 in mitochondria, 1 in peroxisome, and 1 in cytoplast_nucleus (Table 1).

Conserved structure, motifs and phylogenetic analysis of the VuMYB genes

To explore the function of the VuMYB protein, the conserved structural domains and motifs of the VuMYB family genes were analyzed. The VuMYB genes were divided into 3 classes based on the differences in the number of MYB binding sites (Fig. S1), which were 1R, 2R, and 3R, respectively. Among them, 62 VuMYB genes were divided into the 1R-VuMYB members, because they contained 1 MYB binding site. The 161 VuMYB genes were divided into the 2R-VuMYB members, which contained 2 MYB binding sites. The 4 VuMYB genes were assigned to 3R-VuMYB members, which contained 3 MYB binding sites. In addition, a total of 10 conserved motifs were found in 227 VuMYB genes (Fig. 2). The motif analysis showed that all VuMYB sequences in the cowpea contained motif 2 and motif 4, indicating that 2 motifs (motif 2 and motif 4) are highly conserved and essential in the VuMYB gene family (Fig. S2A). Moreover, we found that motif 6, motif 7, motif 8 and motif 10 were specifically present in 1R-VuMYB and 2R-VuMYB (Fig. S2B).

To investigate the functions of VuMYB protein, the phylogenetic trees of 1R-VuMYB and 2R-VuMYB were analyzed respectively. In the 1R-VuMYB proteins, they could be classified into 10 groups with AtMYB proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana. Among them, the S6 and S8 subgroups only contained VuMYB genes (Fig. S3). In the 2R-VuMYB proteins, they could be divided into 26 groups (Fig. 3). Based on the known functions of MYB proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana, the 26 subgroups can be divided into 4 types, abiotic stress-related subgroups (S4, S5, S7, S8, S12, S17, S19, S23 and S25), anthocyanin synthesis-related subgroup (S6, S14, S16 and S20), plant growth and development-related subgroups (S3, S9, S11, S13, S15, S18, S21 and S26) and metabolism-related subgroups (S2, S10 and S24). Gene duplication and co-linearity analysis of the VuMYB genes

Gene duplication plays an important role in plant evolution, which is significant for the expansion of plant gene families. In this study, we found that there were 66 pairs of segmental duplication genes on 11 chromosomes from 227 VuMYB family genes (Fig. 4A). The Chr03 and Chr09 respectively contained 17 and 16 pairs of segmental duplication genes, which were the most numerous. Further analysis revealed that the 66 pairs of segmental duplication genes, such as VuMYB224/VuMYB108 and VuMYB224/VuMYB209 (Table S3). Also, the 12 tandem duplication events in 8 chromosomes (Chr01, Chr02, Chr04, Chr05, Chr06, Chr07, Chr10 and Chr11) were identified (Table S3). For example, 3 tandem duplication genes (VuMYB20, VuMYB20 and VuMYB20) were found in Chr02. These results suggest that some MYB family genes in the cowpea genome could be caused by tandem duplications and segmental duplication, and these genes might have similar functions. We further analyzed the selection pressure and calculated Ka/Ks values for 66 pairs of segmental duplication genes. The results revealed that the Ka/Ks ratios ranged from 0.074346697 to 0.515903433 (Table S4), which were all less than 1, suggesting that the segmental duplication genes in VuMYB family genes could have undergone purifying selection during the evolutionary process.

To investigate the evolutionary relationship of MYB among different species, the co-linearity analysis was performed in cowpea and other 3 plant species, including soybean (Glycine max), common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris) and Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana). The results showed that there were more syntenic relationships between cowpea and other legume crops, including 733 (soybean) and 368 (common bean) MYB syntenic gene pairs (Fig. 4B, Table S5). Among them, the 732 MYB homolog pairs consisted of 183 VuMYB genes and 387 soybean genes, whereas the 368 MYB homolog pairs consisted of 188 VuMYB genes and 201 common bean genes. Moreover, 212 syntenic relationships between cowpea and Arabidopsis thaliana were found (Fig. 4B), which were composed of 117 VuMYB genes and 130 Arabidopsis genes. Some VuMYB genes showed one-to-many or many-to-one homology with MYB genes in cowpea and other species (Table S5). Moreover, a total of 130 orthologous groups were identified among MYB synteny genes in legume plants in Supplementary Table S6. Most VuMYB synteny genes in cowpea exhibited simple one-to-one orthologous relationship with common bean synteny genes, such as VuMYB120 and Phvul.006G105200.1.v2.1 (Table S6). However, compared to soybean synteny genes, VuMYB genes showed one-to-many and many-to-many orthologous relationships, such as VuMYB120, Glyma.03G250600.1.Wm82.a2.v1 and Glyma.19G248100.1.Wm82.a2.v1 (Table S6), which could be attributed to soybean as a polyploid plant with multiple copy genes.

Expression analysis and Cis-acting elements of the VuMYB genes

To better understand the potential functions of VuMYB genes in growth development, we analyzed their expression patterns in different tissues of cowpea (roots, leaves, young pod, young flower bud, and developing seeds) using published transcriptomic data. As shown in Fig. 5, most 1R-VuMYB genes showed low expression in root but high expression in young pod and young flower bud. Among them, 4 1R-VuMYB genes (VuMYB94, VuMYB114, VuMYB115 and VuMYB218) showed unexpressed characteristics in all tissues (Fig. 5A). For 2R-VuMYB genes, they had tissue expression specificity and could be divided into 6 categories, which were highly expressed in roots, leaves, young pod, young flower bud, and developing seeds, respectively (Fig. 5B). Among 2R-VuMYB genes, 15 genes (VuMYB219, VuMYB20, VuMYB21, VuMYB35, VuMYB47, VuMYB56, VuMYB79, VuMYB80, VuMYB82, VuMYB84, VuMYB116, VuMYB150, VuMYB162, VuMYB195 and VuMYB215) were unexpressed in all tissues. Among the 4 3R-VuMYB genes, all were expressed basically at low levels in roots, and VuMYB93 was not expressed in all tissues (Fig. 5C).

The promoter is important for gene expression in plants, the promoters of 227 VuMYB genes were analyzed for cis-acting regulatory elements. The results showed that 227 VuMYB genes all contained light-responsive elements, such as G box (CACGTG) and Box 4 (ATTAAT) (Table S6). Furthermore, 3 response elements were detected in the VuMYB genes, including phytohormone-related response elements (elements-auxin response element (TGA, AuxRR and AuxRE), gibberellin response element (GARE, TATC-box and P-box), abscisic acid response element (ABRE), methyl jasmonate response element (MeJA), and salicylic acid response element (TCA and SARE)), stress-related response elements (low-temperature response element (LTR), mixed stress response element (TC-rich repeats) and anaerobic-related response elements (ARE)), and endosperm-related elements (GCN4_motif and AACA_motif) (Table S7). The abscisic acid response element (ABRE) was found in most VuMYB genes, including 53 1R-VuMYB genes, 130 2R-VuMYB genes, and 4 3R-VuMYB genes (Table S6). A few cis-acting elements on Meristem (CAT-box, GCCACT), Seed (RY, CATGCATG) and Wound (WUN-motif, AAATTTCCT) were identified in 1R-VuMYB and 2R-VuMYB genes (Table S7). These results suggested that the VuMYB family genes could be involved in the regulation of cowpea phytohormones and response to stresses.

Differential expression analysis of the VuMYB genes

To resolve the potential role of VuMYB family genes in anthocyanin synthesis, a comparative transcriptome analysis was carried out by public transcriptomic data. a cutoff of Fold Change ≥ 2 and p value < 0.05 was used to identify differentially expressed genes in the transcriptomic analysis. We found that there were 41 differential expression genes in red cowpea compared to light green cowpea, including 3 1R-VuMYB genes and 38 2R-VuMYB genes (Table S8). The analysis of 41 differentially expressed genes revealed that 2 up-regulated and 1 down-regulated expression were found in the 3 1R-VuMYB genes, whereas 23 up-regulated and 15 down-regulated expression were found in the 38 2R-VuMYB genes (Table S8). Functional annotation analysis of the 41 genes revealed that VuMYB84, VuMYB132, and VuMYB166 of the 2R-VuMYB genes could have the function of promoting anthocyanin synthesis (Table S9). To verify the accuracy of transcriptomic results, qRT-PCR was used to evaluate the expression of 3 genes (VuMYB39, VuMYB132 and VuMYB174) in red-pod and green-pod materials. The qRT-PCR results revealed that VuMYB39 and VuMYB132 were significantly higher in red-pod material than in green-pod material (Fig. S4), which was consistent with the transcriptomic findings.

Interaction network identification of VuMYB genes

To mine more information about the function of VuMYB family genes, the protein interaction networks by STRING online software were perfomed. Protein interaction mapping showed that there were 3 groups of interaction network maps in 38 VuMYB genes from 227 VuMYB family genes, including 34 2R-VuMYB genes and 4 1R-VuMYB genes (Table S10). Based on the interaction network map differences, we found that the first group was composed of AtMYB114, AtMYB123, AtMYB4, and AtMYB7 and so on (Fig. 6A), which could be potentially involved in flavonoids and anthocyanins synthesis-related pathways. In the second group of interactions network, it was composed of AtMYB77, AtMYB15, AtMYB70, AtMYB73, and AtMYB96 (Fig. 6B). Among them, the key core factor was AtMYB77, which could be potentially involved in plant auxin response and auxin signaling. The third group of the interactions network was composed of AtMYB153, AtMYB20, AtMYB85 and AtMYB103 (Fig. 6C). Among them, the key core factor was AtMYB52, which was related to abscisic acid synthesis and involved in plant salt stress and drought stress responses. Interestingly, we found VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 that were expressed up-regulated in red cowpea, belonging to the first group, could be involved in the regulation of anthocyanin synthesis in cowpea.

Subcellular localization and transcriptional activity of VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 proteins

To verify the function of VuMYB84 and VuMYB132, the subcellular localization and transcriptional activity experiments were carried out. VuMYB84-GFP, VuMYB132-GFP and empty vector 35 S-GFP were transfected into Arabidopsis protoplasts by the Agrobacterium injection-mediated method, and GFP fluorescent staining was observed using laser confocal microscopy. It was observed that the green fluorescent signal of empty vector 35 S-GFP was present in the entire cell (Fig. 7A), while the green fluorescent signal of VuMYB84-GFP and VuMYB132-GFP was present in the nucleus (Fig. 7A).

VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 interact with VubHLH3

Based on the above results, an interaction network with the Arabidopsis as a reference for functional analysis of the VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 protein were performed. The prediction results of protein interactions revealed that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 protein could interact with bHLH3 protein (Fig. 8A). Structural analysis of the VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 revealed that they all have WER motifs (DEEDLIIRLHKLLGNRWSLIAGR) that could bind to bHLH (Fig. 8B), which could form MYB-bHLH complex protein to promote anthocyanin formation. Therefore, the Y2H experiment was conducted to explore the relationship between VuMYB84, VuMYB132 and VuBHLH3. To avoid the autoactivation effect of VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 protein, 3-AT (3-amino-1,2,4-triazole) was used in Y2H experiment. The experimental results on different concentrations of 3-AT showed that the optimal inhibitory concentrations for VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 protein were 80 mM and 40 mM, respectively (Fig. S5). The Y2H results showed that BD-VuMYB84, BD-VuMYB132 and positive control pGBKT7-VP16 yeast strains grew normally on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade + X-α-gal medium and turned blue (Fig. 8C), while the other strains didn’t grow, which proved that there was an interaction between VuMYB84, VuMYB132 and VuBHLH3 protein (Fig. 8C). Further, Y2H experiments on the WER motifs present in VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 revealed that the WER motifs interacted with VuBHLH3 protein (Fig. 8D). These results indicated that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 could be involved in anthocyanin regulation through the interaction of WER motifs with VuBHLH3.

Discussion

The MYB gene family is one of the largest gene families and is important for many physiological processes in plants [11, 12]. Significant progress has been made in elucidating the role of MYB proteins in the secondary metabolite metabolism and stress response pathways in various species, such as tomato [13], apple [14] and wax gourd [15]. However, the function of the MYB gene family in cowpea is still poorly understood. In this study, a total of 227 VuMYB genes were first identified from the cowpea ‘A147’ genome and a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis was performed. It was found that there were 161 2R-VuMYB genes, which were dominant in the cowpea VuMYB family genes, accounting for 70.93%. The result is similar with findings from Arabidopsis thaliana [6], Brachypodium distachyon [36] and Hedychium coronarium [37]. Motifs are locally conserved regions in gene sequences, which play important roles in specific biological functions [38]. It was found that 1R-VuMYB and 2R-VuMYB genes have more conserved motifs such as motif 10 in the VuMYB family genes, suggesting that they could be potentially important in the function of MYB transcription factors. Chromosome distribution results showed that MYB genes were unequally distributed in cowpea, where Chr03 and Chr09 contained more MYB members. This uneven chromosome distribution pattern could be originated from gene segmental duplication, which have been reported in species such as Casuarina equisetifolia [39].

Gene duplication is a major driver of the evolutionary expansion of gene families [40], which is important for species diversity and adaptation. In this study, we found that segmental gene duplication was the main driver of the expansion of the VuMYB gene family, in which Chr03 and Chr09 contained 17 and 16 pairs of segmental duplication genes, respectively. All homologous gene pairs exhibited Ka/Ks values of less than 1.0, which suggested that segmental duplication genes of VuMYB genes are subject to purifying selection pressure. This phenomenon has been documented in the results of MYB studies in Wax gourd [15], Casuarina equisetifolia [39] and Prunus amygdalus [41]. Co-linearity analysis revealed that there were more syntenic gene pairs between cowpea and legume crops than Arabidopsis thaliana. In contrast to soybean, cowpea had more homologous genes, which indicated that the closest genetic distance between the common bean. This result was consistent with previous evolution studies of cowpea genome [22].

To better understand the evolutionary relationships among the VuMYB genes, the phylogenetic relationships of the MYB family genes in cowpea and Arabidopsis thaliana were investigated. Phylogenetic trees showed a high degree of conservation of 1R-VuMYB proteins between cowpea and Arabidopsis thalian, but 2 specific subgroups (S6 and S8) were observed to be found only in cowpea, which might have been acquired during the evolution of cowpea. Previous studies found that AtMYB114 [42] and AtMYB112 [43] were involved in the regulation of anthocyanin and flavonoid metabolism. In this study, AtMYB114 and AtMYB112 genes were clustered into the S16 and S20 subgroups, respectively, which implied that VuMYB genes in the S16 and S20 subgroups could be involved in the synthesis of anthocyanins and flavonoids. Comparative transcriptomic analysis further indicated that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 in the S16 subgroup were highly expressed in high anthocyanin cowpea pods, which could be involved in anthocyanin regulation. Interestingly, VuMYB84 was not expressed in green pods and ZN016 pods (green pods) [44], which might be expressed specifically in red pod materials. In addition, protein interaction prediction and functional annotation results indicated that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 have the function of regulating anthocyanin, which could interact with VubHLH3. Subcellular localization experiments showed that the VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 proteins were localized in the nucleus, which could form a complex with VubHLH3 protein in the nucleus.

Many MYB transcription factors have been found to be involved in the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis through the MYB-bHLH-WD repeat protein (MBW) ternary complex in plants [45]. For example, 2 MBW complexes (RcMYB1-RcBHLH42-RcTTG1; RcMYB1-RcEGL1-RcTTG1) were proved to be involved in the regulation of anthocyanin accumulation in rosa [46]. In Pericallis hybrida, the PhMYB2-PhbHLH17 complex could activate the promoter of the anthocyanin-related gene PhDFR3 and regulate anthocyanin synthesis [47]. In this study, the Y2H results showed that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 interacted with the VubHLH3 protein, which could form a complex to regulate anthocyanin synthesis. Moreover, VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 were found to have WER binding motifs (DEEDLIIRLHKLLGNRWSLIAGR). The WER motif has been shown in several plants to be a conserved binding site involved in binding bHLH transcription factors [48, 49]. Our Y2H results revealed that WER in VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 protein could interact with the VubHLH3 protein. These results further indicated that anthocyanin synthesis in red cowpea could be regulated by the MBW complex, which could provide new insights into the molecular regulatory mechanisms of anthocyanin biosynthesis in cowpea pods.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the VuMYB family genes were comprehensively identified and characterized in the cowpea genome, including 62 1R-VuMYB, 161 2R-VuMYB, and 4 3R-VuMYB. Phylogenetic and co-linearity analyses indicated that 2R-VuMYB genes could be divided into 26 groups, segmental duplication contributed to the expansion of the VuMYB gene family, and 130 orthologous groups were identified among MYB synteny genes. Cis-acting element analysis revealed that VuMYB family genes promoters were enriched in response to phytohormones and response to stresses element. The analysis of comparative transcriptomics, protein-protein interactions, and protein function revealed that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 could be involved in regulating anthocyanin synthesis of cowpea pod. Moreover, VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 were found to be located in the nucleus, which have autoactivation functions and bHLH-binding motifs (DEEDLIIRLHKLLGNRWSLIAGR). Y2H results showed that VuMYB84 and VuMYB132 could interact with VubHLH3. Our results would provide new ideas for molecular breeding techniques for pod color formation and high anthocyanin content in cowpea.
