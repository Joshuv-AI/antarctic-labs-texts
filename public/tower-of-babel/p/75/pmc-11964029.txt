Prognostic Implications of Resistive Reserve Ratio in Patients With Nonobstructive Coronary Artery Disease With Myocardial Bridging
Authors: Negin Mahmoudi Hamidabad, Yoshihisa Kanaji, Ilke Ozcan, Jaskanwal Deep Singh Sara, Ali Ahmad, Lilach O Lerman, Amir Lerman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial bridging (MB) is accompanied by the dynamic extravascular compression of epicardial coronary arteries, leading to intracoronary hemodynamic disturbance with abnormal coronary flow profiles. We aimed to evaluate the prognostic implications of resistive reserve ratio (RRR), a composite measure of flow and pressure parameters that represents the vasodilatory capacity of the coronary arteries, in patients with angina with nonobstructive coronary artery disease (ANOCA) and MB, in comparison with coronary flow reserve (CFR).Methods and ResultsIn this retrospective cohort study, we included patients with ANOCA who underwent coronary reactivity testing, where MB was identified by transient constriction in coronary artery segments between systole and diastole. Abnormal CFR and RRR were defined as <2.5 and <2.62, respectively. Major adverse cardiac events, including cardiovascular death, stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and late revascularization, served as outcomes. Among 1251 patients with ANOCA, 191 (15.3%) had MB. The prevalence of abnormal CFR or RRR was not significantly different between patients with and without MB (P=0.144 and P=0.398, respectively). Over a median follow‐up time of 6.9 years, abnormal RRR predicted major adverse cardiac events in patients with MB (hazard ratio [HR], 4.38 [95% CI, 1.71–11.21]; P=0.002) and without MB (HR, 1.91 [95% CI, 1.38–2.64]; P<0.001). Abnormal CFR predicted major adverse cardiac events in patients without MB (HR, 2.15 [95% CI, 1.54–3.00]; P<0.001), whereas it was not predictive of major adverse cardiac events in patients with MB (HR, 2.29 [95% CI, 0.93–5.65]; P=0.073).ConclusionsIn patients with ANOCA and MB, impaired RRR was superior to impaired CFR in distinguishing patients at a higher risk of future adverse events, suggesting that RRR may serve as a risk stratification tool in patients with MB and ANOCA.Keywords: coronary flow reserve, microvascular dysfunction, myocardial bridging, nonobstructive coronary artery disease, resistive reserve ratioSubject Categories: Diagnostic Testing, Imaging

Myocardial bridging (MB) has gained importance as one of the mechanisms of ischemia in the subset of angina with nonobstructive coronary artery disease (ANOCA). 1 MB is a congenital anomaly of the coronary arteries in which a coronary artery segment takes an intramyocardial course, resulting in systolic and early diastolic compression of the bridged segment. With growing knowledge of the mechanisms of ANOCA, coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) has become an increasingly recognized cause of chest pain and ischemia, and its prognostic importance has been intensively investigated in patients with ANOCA. 2 , 3 , 4 We have previously reported a high prevalence of endothelium‐dependent CMD in MB. 5 However, the link between MB and endothelial‐independent CMD and its prognostic value in patients with ANOCA remains to be elucidated. Coronary flow reserve (CFR) is the ratio of hyperemic to resting coronary blood flow and represents the contribution of epicardial and microvascular coronary arteries to myocardial perfusion, evaluating coronary microvascular function in the absence of obstructive epicardial stenoses. 6 However, CFR might have limited efficiency in assessing CMD in the presence of MB due to the dynamic nature of epicardial extravascular compression and hemodynamic disturbances that persist into systole and early diastole. Resistive reserve ratio (RRR) integrates indices of flow and pressure parameters measured at rest and during hyperemia and represents the vasodilatory capacity of the coronary microcirculation. 7 We hypothesized that RRR might yield an incremental prognostic value in patients with ANOCA with MB by reflecting both the status of resting and maximum hyperemia, with the goal of identifying patients with MB who are at higher risk of adverse outcomes regardless of their hemodynamic disturbance. This study aimed to assess the prognostic implications of CMD evaluation using RRR in comparison with CFR among patients with ANOCA, both with and without MB.

Methods

Study Population

In this retrospective observational cohort study, we included consecutive patients presenting with symptoms of stable ischemic heart disease who underwent clinically indicated coronary angiography and invasive physiological coronary reactivity testing (CRT) between 1997 and 2021 at the Mayo Clinic to assess coronary microvascular function and were found to have nonobstructive coronary artery disease (<40% stenosis in all 3 vessels).

From this cohort, we identified patients with MB determined visually at coronary angiography and propensity score–matched patients without MB. The exclusion criteria comprised a history of myocardial infarction (MI), cerebrovascular event, or percutaneous coronary intervention in the past 6 months; acute coronary syndrome presentation; coronary artery bypass surgery; valvular heart disease; advanced kidney disease; significant endocrine disorders; primary cardiomyopathy; reduced left ventricular systolic function (<50%); and inflammatory diseases. 8 The study protocol was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (No. 05‐004191). The present study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki for investigation in human beings, and all patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. First and corresponding authors have full access to all the data in the study and take responsibility for its integrity and the data analysis. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Coronary Angiography and CRT

All patients underwent diagnostic coronary angiography using standard clinical protocols. MB was determined visually at coronary angiography by identifying segments of the coronary artery that were characterized by transient constriction or a “milking effect” between systole and diastole. 9 CRT was performed to identify the origin of chest pain in all participants without obstructive coronary artery disease (<40% stenosis) as described in detail previously. 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 In brief, an intracoronary Doppler guidewire (FloWire, Philips/Volcano Corp., San Diego, CA) was advanced and positioned proximal to the MB site or in the mid–left anterior descending coronary artery after diagnostic coronary angiography. Intracoronary bolus injections of incremental doses (18–72 μg) of adenosine were administered through the guiding catheter until maximal hyperemia was achieved and hemodynamic parameters were recorded at baseline and during maximal hyperemia. CFR was calculated by dividing the average peak velocity (APV) during maximal hyperemia by the baseline APV, 13 and abnormal CFR was defined as CFR<2.5. 14 Baseline microvascular resistance (BMR) and hyperemic microvascular resistance (HMR) were calculated as the ratio of the mean aortic pressure (MAP) and APV at baseline and during maximal hyperemia, respectively. RRR was then calculated as the ratio of BMR to HMR, and abnormal RRR was defined as <2.62, based on the cutoff value calculated for patients with ANOCA as previously reported. 12 Vasoactive cardiovascular medications such as long‐acting nitrates, calcium channel blockers, and ß blockers were discontinued for at least 48 hours before the procedure.

Clinical Assessment and Follow‐Up

Clinical evaluation, medical history, laboratory data, and medications at admission were collected from a detailed review of the medical chart in a blinded fashion by investigators. Patients were followed up for the occurrence of major adverse cardiac events (MACEs), defined as cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, heart failure, stroke, and late revascularization. Heart failure was diagnosed from any cardinal symptoms and abnormal findings of echocardiography, brain natriuretic peptide/NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) level, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, or right heart catheterization according to current guidelines for heart failure. 15 Individual events were ascertained by a combination of institutional databases, death certificates, and detailed chart review and were independently adjudicated by investigators.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 28.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL) and R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) software. Categorical data are expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages and were compared by the χ2 or Fisher's exact test. Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD for normally distributed variables or as median (25th–75th percentile) for nonnormally distributed variables and were compared using Student's t test and the Mann–Whitney U test, respectively. Survival curves were plotted using Kaplan–Meier estimates and were compared using log‐rank tests on the basis of the presence of microvascular dysfunction defined by abnormal CFR or RRR. Time to event was calculated as the period between the date of undergoing coronary angiography and the first occurrence of a MACE. Patients without a MACE were censored at the time of the last follow‐up. Cox proportional hazard analysis was used to determine the predictive value of impaired RRR and CFR on MACE occurrence in patients with and without MB. To ensure the optimal selection of variables for the multivariable Cox model, covariates were added in a stepwise manner. Variance inflation factor was calculated for each model to check for multicollinearity. After adding each variable, the variance inflation factor was calculated for the model to check for multicollinearity, and models with variance inflation factor ≥10 were excluded due to high multicollinearity. Then the Akaike information criterion (AIC) was calculated for each model, and the model with the lowest Akaike information criterion including the covariates age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia was selected as the final multivariable model. We used propensity score matching to adjust for baseline covariates between the cohort with and without MB. Propensity scores were computed by modeling a logistic regression, with the dependent variable as the odds of the presence of MB and the independent variables as age, sex, race, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes using 2:1 nearest‐neighbor matching with 0.01 calipers. A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. We tested the predictiveness on RRR and CFR on MACE incidence among patients with and without MB in the propensity score–matched population once with the original 5‐component MACE and then repeated the test with cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, and heart failure to ensure the consistency of our findings across different definitions of MACE.

Results

Characteristics and Adverse Outcomes

A total of 2175 patients underwent coronary angiography and comprehensive invasive physiological CRT at the Mayo Clinic between 1997 and 2021. Of these, 1251 patients with ANOCA fulfilled the inclusion criteria for this study (Figure 1). Overall, the median age of the study population was 52 (43–60) years, and 68.0% were women. MB was angiographically detected in 191 patients (15.3%), all within the left anterior descending coronary artery. Baseline characteristics, divided into 2 groups on the basis of the presence of MB, are described in Table 1. Patients with MB were significantly younger and more likely to be men than patients without MB (P=0.001 and P<0.001, respectively).

Propensity‐Matched Analysis

After 1:2 propensity score matching, a total of 358 patients without MB were matched to 184 patients with MB. The median age of the matched groups was 51 [41–59] years, and 59.8% were women. Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the groups with and without MB (Table S3). After matching, there were no significant differences in MACEs among patients with and without MB (MB, 11.4% versus non‐MB, 12.8%; P=0.631). In this cohort, 39.7% of patients with MB and 42.2% of those without MB had impaired RRR (P=0.575), and 20.1% of patients with MB and 20.1% of patients without MB had impaired CFR (P=0.999). There was no significant difference in MAP at rest, MAP during maximal hyperemia, APV at rest, APV during maximal hyperemia, BMR, HMR, CFR, and RRR, as well as impaired CFR and RRR between patients with and without MB (all P>0.05). Patients with MB had a significantly higher diastolic–systolic velocity ratio compared with patients without MB (P<0.001). Physiological data of the propensity matched groups are summarized in Table S4. Impaired RRR continued to have increased rates of MACE in groups with and without MB (HR, 3.87 [95% CI, 1.50–9.98]; P=0.005; and HR, 2.45 [95% CI, 1.34–4.46]; P=0.003, respectively). Meanwhile, impaired CFR was not significantly associated with MACEs in patients with MB but was associated with MACEs in patients without MB (HR, 2.36 [95% CI, 0.94–5.88]; P=0.066; and HR, 2.26 [95% CI, 1.23–4.15]; P=0.009, respectively). Among 147 patients with MB and normal CFR, 39 (26.5%) had impaired RRR, and they had significantly worse outcomes than those with normal RRR (HR, 4.83 [95% CI, 1.61–14.46]; P=0.005). In the 286 patients without MB and with normal CFR, impaired RRR was present in 87 (30.4%); it was not a significant predictor of worse outcomes (HR, 1.85 [95% CI, 0.90–3.81]; P=0.096). When the analysis was restricted to the MACE subgroup with cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, and heart failure, impaired RRR was still found to be a significant factor for worse outcomes in patients with and without MB (HR, 4.51 [95% CI, 1.45–14.04]; P=0.009, and HR, 2.26 [95% CI, 1.01–4.79]; P=0.033, respectively), while impaired CFR was a significant predictor of events in patients without MB but not in those with MB (HR, 2.45 [95% CI, 1.15–5.22]; P=0.021; and HR, 2.06 [95% CI, 0.71–5.97]; P=0.182, respectively).

Discussion

The current study compared 2 adenosine‐dependent physiological indices reflecting microvascular function and for the first time demonstrated the prognostic efficacy of RRR and its difference from CFR in patients with ANOCA with MB. The major findings of this study are as follows:

1. There was no significant difference in CFR, BMR, HMR, and RRR between patients with and patients without MB.

2. CFR and RRR were independent predictors of MACE after adjustment for confounders in the overall patients with ANOCA.

3. In patients with ANOCA with MB, impaired RRR was associated with an increased risk of MACEs, whereas impaired CFR was not.

4. When patients with ANOCA with MB and normal CFR were stratified by RRR, patients with impaired RRR had significantly worse outcomes than those with normal RRR.

MB is characterized by dynamic epicardial coronary stenosis caused by systolic compression of the intramyocardial arterial segment, known as the “milking effect,” with delayed early diastolic arterial relaxation, leading to an intracoronary hemodynamic disturbance with abnormal coronary flow profiles. 16 These dynamic abnormalities of the coronary flow profiles potentially interfere with the physiological assessment of CMD in patients with ANOCA and MB and hinders our understanding of the growing importance of CMD in the pathomechanisms of ANOCA. CMD is an important prognostic factor in patients with ANOCA; however, to our knowledge, no study has evaluated CMD and its prognostic implication in patients with concomitant MB. Therefore, the results of our study might provide novel insights into the physiological underpinning of CMD in patients with ANOCA and MB.

MB and Microvascular Dysfunction in ANOCA

It has been reported that up to half of all patients who underwent invasive coronary angiography have ANOCA. 17 With the development of the physiological coronary circulation assessment, ANOCA has been shown to have occult coronary abnormalities, and it encompasses a wide range of pathophysiological diagnoses including CMD and MB. 1

CMD is defined as the inability of the coronary microvasculature to augment blood flow in response to increased myocardial demand secondary to architectural changes or vasomotor dysfunction. CMD can contribute to myocardial ischemia and adverse outcomes 2 , 3 , 4 and has attracted great interest as a potential pathomechanism in multiple cardiovascular diseases, including ANOCA. Various studies have demonstrated that 2 distinct entities, endothelial‐dependent or ‐independent CMD can manifest in isolation or simultaneously in patients with ANOCA. 18 , 19 A significant relationship between MB and endothelial‐dependent CMD has been revealed in previous studies. 18 , 20 Endothelial‐dependent microvascular dysfunction is identified as an imbalance between endothelial‐derived vasodilators and vasoconstrictors, resulting in an abnormal vasoconstriction in microvasculature. In patients with MB, systolic compression of the bridged segment causes retrograde flow, resulting in abrupt breakage in the antegrade systolic wave, leading to low wall shear stress proximal to the MB. Low wall shear stress is related to endothelial dysfunction and reduced expression of provasoactive agents such as nitric oxide synthase. 21 , 22 , 23 We have previously reported higher mortality rates in patients with ANOCA and endothelial‐independent CMD. 12 Endothelial‐independent microvascular function depends on myocyte tone in response to various stimuli and changes in vascular smooth muscle cells in the vessel wall. Adenosine induces microvascular dilatation by stimulating the adenosine A2 receptor on vascular smooth muscle cells in microvasculature, which can be detected by different indices. In the present study, adenosine‐dependent physiological indices, including CFR, BMR, HMR, and RRR, as well as the presence of CMD defined by impaired CFR or RRR, were similar in patients with and without MB. These results are consistent with previous studies 24 and suggest that the presence of endothelial‐independent CMD is similar in patients with ANOCA with and without MB. Pargaonkar et al demonstrated the complication of endothelial‐independent CMD defined by higher index of microcirculatory resistance with MB. 25 Our results support this relationship and suggest that endothelial‐independent CMD may represent a possible mechanism and therapeutic target of ischemia in patients with ANOCA and MB in addition to other proposed mechanisms.

Potential Clinical Implications

Various hyperemic and nonhyperemic physiological indices measured by using intracoronary Doppler, including diastolic‐fractional flow reserve and instantaneous wave‐free pressure ratio, have been proposed to assess the hemodynamic significance of MB instead of fractional flow reserve, which is generally considered the gold standard for the physiological assessment of epicardial stenosis. 26 , 27 , 28 Furthermore, inotropic stimulation with dobutamine has been reported to potentially improve the diagnostic sensitivity of these indices, as MB is a dynamic stenosis deeply influenced by the degree of extravascular compression and intramyocardial tension. 29 , 30 However, the discussion of these indices focuses almost exclusively on hemodynamic abnormalities caused by MB in the epicardial coronary artery and not in the microvasculature. Moreover, the prognostic implication of these indices in patients with MB remains elusive. The present study demonstrated that impaired RRR and CFR were significantly associated with worse outcomes in patients with ANOCA, whereas in the presence of MB impaired RRR but not CFR significantly predicted worse outcomes. We found the same results in the analysis using the matched groups. Furthermore, stratification by RRR could discriminate against patients with ANOCA with MB who had a normal CFR at high risk for worse outcomes. These results suggest that the use of RRR rather than CFR should be considered for the assessment of CMD in patients with ANOCA and MB.

Although CFR is a well‐established physiological index derived from flow velocity to assess circulation in both epicardial coronary arteries and microvasculature, the retrograde flow due to the Venturi effect and the abrupt breakage in the antegrade systolic wave caused by dynamic compression in the tunneled segment might result in higher variability of CFR and limit its clinical relevance in the presence of MB. Considering this hemodynamic disturbance caused by MB, an integrated physiological index of both pressure and flow components might provide a better identification of patients with microvascular dysfunction and an enhanced discrimination of patients at higher risk of future clinical events. Notably, in patients with MB, impaired RRR was significantly associated with MACE, whereas impaired CFR nor MAP at rest/maximum hyperemia were not significant in univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard analysis. Moreover, as the dynamic nature of MB is influenced by the degree of extravascular compression and intramyocardial tension, the assessment by RRR reflecting both the status of resting and maximum hyperemia might be rational to identify a proportion of hemodynamically relevant MB that are not detected by BMR or HMR alone. Nevertheless, these are merely hypothesis‐generating explanations. Therefore, further large prospective studies are required to test our hypothesis for elucidating the mechanisms of the prognostic impact and association of MB and ANOCA with worse outcomes. Furthermore, given the growing body of evidence supporting the noninvasive evaluation of coronary microvascular function, noninvasive modalities may enable a more multifaceted identification of CMD, exploring the mechanisms of CMD in a broader range of patients with MB, while also providing benefits such as cost effectiveness and reduced invasiveness for patients.

Study Limitations

We need to acknowledge that our study comes with multiple limitations. First, it included a relatively small number of patients with MB from a single center, which precluded extensive subgroup or powered multivariable analyses. Given the limited number of events and unadjusted confounders, our results should not be taken as a decisive finding and should be tested in future studies. Furthermore, inherent selection bias is introduced. To try and ameliorate the bias, propensity matching was performed. Second, MB was detected by visual assessment using coronary angiography imaging and was not evaluated hemodynamically significant as epicardial stenosis, which may lead to an underestimation of MB and possibly affect the assessment of prognosis in patients with MB. Third, we alternatively used aortic pressure to calculate RRR. Although the differences between aortic pressure and coronary pressure are negligible and the calculation of RRR using mean aortic pressure is validated, 12 it might affect the RRR value in the presence of MB. Furthermore, the cutoff value of RRR has not been fully clarified. Future large studies are needed to validate the present results and to identify the best cutoff values for CFR and RRR in the subset of ANOCA with MB. Fourth, this study has limitations inherent to the period when the protocol was established, resulting in a partially different procedure and definition from the current consensus. 31 We cannot rule out the possibility that there are some differences from the current consensus document, nor can we completely exclude patients with noncardiac causes of chest pain. Fifth, we do not have data on distal coronary pressure in all cases, and it is possible that we could not rule out concomitant epicardial stenosis, including diffuse narrowing and coronary artery compression caused by MB, due to the lack of physiological assessment for epicardial coronary artery stenosis. This limitation could potentially result in an inaccurate evaluation of microvascular dysfunction by CFR, although it should be noted that under the same conditions, RRR, an integrated physiological index calculated from both aortic pressure and coronary flow components, provided a more accurate assessment of coronary microvascular function, and impaired RRR provided a better discrimination of higher‐risk patients than impaired CFR in the presence of MB. In addition, it must be taken into consideration that the population enrolled in this study had ANOCA, while the prevalence of MB in the general population is ≈25%, with the majority of patients being asymptomatic. Therefore, the results of this study should be validated in further studies to extend the subset with the asymptomatic MB.

In conclusion, impaired RRR and CFR were significantly associated with worse outcomes in all patients with ANOCA. Meanwhile, in the presence of MB, impaired RRR provided superior discrimination of patients at higher risk of future clinical events in patients with ANOCA compared with impaired CFR. The use of RRR rather than CFR might provide a more accurate assessment of CMD in patients with ANOCA with MB and insight into the effect of MB on CMD, independent of the hemodynamic disturbance caused by MB.
