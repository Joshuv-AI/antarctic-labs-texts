Effects of controlled, acute exposure to air pollutants on heart rate variability in humans: a meta-regression and methodological recommendations
Authors: Cameron K. Stopforth, Rachel J. Keith, Emily Converse, A. Tai Simpson, Lee Stoner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe consequences of air pollution on cardiovascular disease (CVD) are heavily supported. One proposed mechanism that underpins the development of many CVD-related pathologies is autonomic imbalance, which can be measured through the high frequency (HF) component of heart rate variability (HRV). Despite evidence suggesting that air pollution reduces HRV, findings remain inconsistent across varying exposure durations and study designs.ObjectiveThis meta-regression addressed the question: Is there a dose-response relationship between controlled pollutant exposure duration (in hours), compared to filtered air, and autonomic imbalance (HF component of HRV) in adult humans?MethodsWe searched PubMed, Scopus, and Embase for cross-over or randomized controlled trials involving adults exposed to both a filtered air and air pollutant condition in controlled environments. These studies needed to report HF before and after exposure. Quality, bias, and certainty of evidence were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist, the Cochrane risk-of-bias 2 tool, and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework, respectively. We used a restricted cubic spline (RCS) model to estimate the standardized mean difference (SMD) of exposure duration (in hours) on the HF component of HRV.ResultsThis meta-regression included u=27 studies, from which we extracted k=55 effect sizes, representing n=747 participants. The RCS modelling indicated that study-level exposure duration was negatively associated with HRV, with studies using longer exposure protocols reporting a minor pooled decrease in HRV relative to filtered air controls (SMD=-0.221 [-0.365, -0.077], p=0.003). Only study bias significantly moderated the effect of exposure hour on HRV (p=0.084).DiscussionThese findings suggest a duration-dependent association at the study level, with greater pooled decreases in HRV (HF) observed in studies employing exposure protocols beyond 3 hours. This work also assessed critical study design considerations including exposure duration, pollutant types, disease states, publication quality/bias, and exercise protocols to provide methodological suggestions. These recommendations are essential for reducing variation in future cross-over studies and refining CVD-related air quality policies.This meta-regression was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023470139) and was supported in part by the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences of the National Institutes of Health [T32ES011564; R01ES029846; P42ES023716].Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28459-x.

Background

At least 27 meta-analyses report a link between air pollution and incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1, 2]. This association is a particular problem for people exposed to air pollution during daily activities like work [3], commuting [4], or outdoor hobbies [5]. There is acute risk with exposure; just one to seven days of elevated exposure can increase the relative risk of cardiovascular events by 1–3% [6]. However, a critical knowledge gap remains: we do not know the acute temporal association. Specifically, does CVD risk increase with each hour of air pollution exposure? Answering this question is crucial for refining public health policies. A meta-regression can fill this gap, but first, we must identify three key elements: (i) an appropriate intermediate outcome, (ii) the correct study designs, and (iii) key moderators.

The value of a meta-analysis depends on selecting a suitable intermediate outcome; an outcome on the causal pathway between air pollution and CVD [7]. Because this pathway is complex, the ideal intermediate outcome should serve as a proxy for generalized cardiovascular dysregulation. Autonomic imbalance, measured via heart rate variability (HRV), is a common underlying mechanism in many CVDs and serves as an excellent intermediate outcome for this purpose [8].

The high-frequency (HF) component of HRV is a particularly useful metric. This measure reflects short-term parasympathetic nervous system (PSNS) activity and respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), the natural fluctuation of heart rate with breathing [9–11]. Because HF represents short-term changes, the metric is well-suited for quantifying autonomic responses to acute pollutant exposure. Furthermore, previous research shows that the largest air-pollution-induced reductions often occur in the HF domain [2]. For these reasons, we selected HF as our proxy for autonomic imbalance.

Previous meta-analyses of observational studies suggest that short-term increases in air pollution are inversely related to several HRV parameters [12–17]. However, confidence in these findings is weakened by methodological inconsistencies across studies [15]. In fact, a recent systematic review of more controlled cross-over exposure studies found the evidence between air pollutants and HRV to be inconclusive [18]. A new meta-analysis focused exclusively on controlled exposure protocols is therefore needed to quantify the effects of acute exposure on HRV and resolve these discrepancies.

Several factors in exposure studies could influence these findings. First, identifying and quantifying air pollutants is often ambiguous because ambient air is a complex mixture of particles and gases [19]. Second, different study populations (e.g., people with chronic diseases) may have altered HRV independent of air pollution exposure [8, 20]. Third, HRV metrics are often reported at different times following exposure, complicating interpretation [9]. Fourth, inconsistent use of exercise can alter the inhaled pollutant dose and independently suppress HRV. These differences in exposure, population, and study design can all affect the results. To address these issues, we focused on studies with controlled exposures that compare polluted and filtered air, allowing for precise monitoring of exposure. By restricting our analysis to randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cross-over designs, we could use within-subject comparisons. This approach enhances internal validity and provides a more consistent basis for evaluating exposure effects.

Objective

This meta-regression addressed the question: Is there a dose-response relationship between controlled pollutant exposure duration (in hours), compared to filtered air, and autonomic imbalance (HF component of HRV) in adult humans? RCTs or cross-over trials study designs were eligible for inclusion. The following a priori determined moderators were used to explain variability in the pooled effect and were assessed for methodological implications: Exposure (i.e., pollutant type), population (i.e., healthy or disease state), and study design (i.e., exercise implementation, quality, and bias). Together, the findings of this meta-regression clarify the impact of acute, controlled pollution exposure on HRV (HF) and provide insight for future methodological recommendations on exposure-based autonomic function studies.

Methods

This meta-regression was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023470139) and adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines [21].

Data sources and searches

Two authors (C.S. and A.S.) searched the electronic databases PubMed, Scopus, and Embase. The search terms were based on the PECOS (population, exposure, comparison, outcome, and study type) criteria [21], targeting a wide range of terms for air pollution and HRV in human studies (Additional Table 1). The search was limited to English language articles published between inception and December 2023. We also examined the reference lists of all identified articles and relevant reviews. Full details of the search strategy are presented in the supplemental materials (Additional Table 2).

Article selection

Two researchers (C.S. and A.S.) completed the study selection independently, with a third author (L.S.) resolving any disagreements. We use the term “article” synonymously with “study,” and “effect size” (ES) as the unit for the meta-regression. An article could yield multiple ES if it used separate independent samples (e.g., males and females) or multiple experimental conditions (e.g., two different concentrations or types of a pollutant). In such cases, we extracted data for each estimate separately. After excluding duplicates, we screened titles and abstracts for relevance. The original data source for each u and any additional requested information from authors can be found in Additional Table 3.

An article’s full text was included if it met these criteria: (i) subjects were adult humans (> 18 years); (ii) the study measured quantifiable air pollutant exposure in a controlled environment; (iii) pollutant exposure was compared to a filtered air control; (iv) HF was reported as an outcome; and (v) the design was a cross-over trial or RCT. We did not exclude articles based on the exposure delivery system, exposure duration, co-exposure to exercise, or pre-existing health conditions.

Study methodological quality and bias assessments

Two researchers (C.S. and A.S.) assessed study quality using the 13-item Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist for RCTs [22]. Items were scored as 1 (yes), 0 (unclear), or -1 (no), with summed scores ranging from 13 (highest quality) to -13 (lowest quality). A full description of the CASP instructions can be found in the supplemental material (Appendix 1).

Separately, two researchers (C.S. and E.C.) assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane risk-of-bias 2 (ROB-2) tool [23]. This tool evaluates randomization, deviations from interventions, carryover effects, outcome measurement, and reporting. Each study was assigned an aggregate score of ‘low risk of bias’, ‘some concerns’, or ‘high risk of bias’. A full description of the ROB-2 instructions can be found in the supplemental material (Appendix 2).

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for the HRV outcome (HF) was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach [24]. The evidence was evaluated across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Evaluation was determined for (i) risk of bias using the ROB-2 tool; for (ii) inconsistency using the Higgins I² as a measure of heterogeneity; for (iii) indirectness based on judgement of how closely the population, intervention, comparator, and outcome measures in the included studies matched the research question; for (iv) imprecision based on the width of the 95% confidence interval around the pooled effect estimate at the median study duration (2 h); and for (v) publication bias using visual assessment of funnel plots for asymmetry and regression tests. Given that the included studies were RCTs or cross-over trials, the certainty of evidence initially started at “high confidence.” Final certainty was adjudicated as high, moderate, low, or very low based on the presence of downgrading or upgrading factors.

Data extraction

Data extraction was completed independently by two researchers (C.S. and E.C.) and checked for agreement. If a data point differed by more than 5%, both authors re-checked their extraction. The final dataset used values averaged across authors. We extracted: (i) bibliographic information; (ii) sample characteristics (e.g., age, sex, body mass index (BMI)); (iii) exposure details; and (iv) baseline and post-exposure HF data. Since HF was reported using different units, we prioritized extraction in the following order: (1) normalized units; (2) milliseconds/milliseconds squared; and (3) natural log of milliseconds/milliseconds squared.

Missing data

We addressed missing data in the following order: (i) requested via corresponding email from the authors; (ii) extracted from a figure; (iii) imputed using Cochrane Handbook methods; [25] or (iv) excluded. As a quality control step, we imputed data before contacting authors to compare our estimate with the data they provided. We made three contact attempts, two weeks apart. If authors were unresponsive and data could not be extracted or imputed, it was excluded. Data was extracted from figures using PlotDigitizer. If standard deviation (SD) was missing, we estimated it from the: (i) standard error (SE): SE x √n; (ii) 95% confidence interval (CI): (upper confidence interval (UCI) – lower confidence interval (LCI))/3.92; (iii) 90% CI: (UCI -LCI)/3.29; (iv) baseline SD if the post-exposure SD was missing; or (v) SD from closest study if both the pre-exposure and post-exposure SDs were missing [25].

Sample size inflation

To limit bias from sample size inflation, we took the following steps: (i) for multiple time points, we used the measurement closest to the start of exposure (‘pre’) and the one closest to the end of exposure (‘post’); (ii) for studies with multiple treatment arms and one control, the control group’s sample size was divided by the number of treatment groups; and (iii) for cross-over trials, the sample size was divided by the number of treatments [25].

For the control (CON) and experimental (EXP) conditions within each ES, we calculated the change from baseline and its associated SD. Data analysis

A single author (L.S.) conducted the data analysis in RStudio (v. 2024.04.01) using R (v. 4.3.1) [26]. We specify the R argument and package used as (argument[package]). The α level was set at 0.05 for the pooled effect and 0.10 for moderator analysis and the Q test. Meta-regression was used to estimate the pooled effect (SMD) of exposure duration (in hours) on HRV (HF). The magnitude of the pooled SMD was adjudicated as minor (< 0.2), small (0.2), moderate (0.5), or large (0.8) [27]. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were calculated using the Knapp and Hartung method [28].

We used a 3-level multivariate meta-regression model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation to test the study objective [29]. This model accounts for dependency when a study (u) contributes more than one ES (k) [29]. To ensure full transparency, we first fit the data to univariate fixed- and random-effects models [30, 31]. We calculated variance (tau2) for the between-study (Level 3), within-study (Level 2), and sampling (Level 1) levels. We compared model fit using Akaike’s information criterion, corrected for small samples (AICc), and the log-likelihood ratio test (LRT) (anova[metafor]). After running the final model, we tested: (i) meta-analytical assumptions; (ii) robustness; (iii) heterogeneity; and (iv) a priori moderators.(i). Assumptions: We tested linearity by specifying polynomials and RCS ([rcs]). Formal rejection of the linear assumption was based on a significant improvement in model fit (LRT) when non-linear terms were added. Model selection among non-linear candidates (quadratic, cubic, RCS) used AICc, with models <2 AICc units considered statistically equivalent per established convention [32]. When models were statistically equivalent, selection prioritized biological interpretability and extrapolation behavior beyond the observed data range. We checked normality of errors with quantile-quantile (QQ) plots (qqnorm[metafor]) and homoscedasticity of errors by plotting standardized deleted residuals against fitted values. We tested for multicollinearity by creating a correlation matrix table (corrMatrix[jmv]) and chart (Data.Corr[PerformanceAnalytics]) to identify r values exceeding 0.80. We also tested multicollinearity during moderation analysis using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) (vif.ram[metafor]).(ii). Robustness: We confirmed the robustness of the pooled SMD and 95%CI using permutation tests (permutest[metafor]) [33]. We calculated Pearson’s residuals, hat values, and Cook’s distances to identify potential outliers at both the ES (k) and study (u) levels [34]. A Pearson’s residual greater than 3.84 (the χ2 quantile at α=0.05) indicated a potential outlier. A hat or Cook’s distance value greater than 3*(1/[k or u]) or 0.5, respectively, indicated potentially influential k or u. We used Leverage (x=hat, y=Pearson’s, size=Cook’s) and Baujat (x=Pearson’s2, y=Cook’s) plots to visually identify data points requiring closer inspection (ggplot[ggplot2]). We assessed publication bias qualitatively with Begg’s funnel plot [35] and quantitatively using a regression intercept test, with the standard error of the observed outcomes specified as a moderator in the 3-level model.(iii). Heterogeneity: We assessed heterogeneity using Cochrane’s Q-test, [36] Higgin’s I² statistic, [37] and pseudo R². We categorized I² as low (<25%), moderate (25%-75%), or high (>75%). Greater than low-risk heterogeneity (≥25%) indicated that the pooled SMD could not be treated as one common effect.(iv). The multicollinearity assumption was tested using VIF (vif.ram[metafor]), where 1=not correlated, 1-5=moderate, and >5=high.

Results

Literature search and trial selection

Our literature search is outlined in Fig. 1. The initial database search identified 1850 potentially eligible articles. After removing 863 duplicates, we screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles, which led to the exclusion of 939 articles. A subsequent full-text screening excluded another 21 articles (Additional Table 4). Ultimately, we included u = 27 studies in the meta-regression, from which we extracted k = 55 ES, representing a total sample of n = 747 participants.

Description of the included trials

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Additional Table 5. The studies were conducted in the United States of America (USA) (63%) [38–54], Sweden (18%) [55–59], Canada (7%) [60, 61], Denmark (4%) [62], Italy (4%) [63], and the United Kingdom (UK) (4%) [64], with all studies being published between 2003 and 2023. The number of participants per effect size ranged from 5 [60] to 87 [48]. Participant age ranged from a mean of 23 years [47] to 68 years [64]. Two ES included only female participants [56] and seven included only males [55, 58, 61, 63]. Only six studies reported ethnicity [42, 45, 49, 50, 52, 60], which was predominantly Caucasian. Nine studies involved participants with prior health conditions, which we grouped into three categories: cardiometabolic (15%) [46, 54, 58, 64], respiratory (7%) [47, 49], or a mix of healthy and clinical populations (11%) [38, 50, 60]. All studies with clinical populations appropriately controlled for vasoactive medications. Two studies used fish or olive oil supplementation [42, 52].

An overview of pollutant exposures is provided in Additional Table 5. We grouped exposure types into seven categories: ozone (O3) (21%) [38, 39, 42, 45, 48, 55, 60]; nitrogen dioxide (NO2) (4%) [57, 64]; ultrafine particulate (UFP) (17%) [46, 47, 51, 54, 56]; particulate matter < 2.5 μm (PM2.5) (47%) [43, 44, 49, 50, 52, 53, 57–62]; particulate matter < 10 μm (PM10) (3%) [40, 41, 63]; PM2.5 + O3 (6%) [39, 60]; and PM2.5 + NO2 (2%) [57]. Each scenario was compared to a filtered air control. Exposure duration ranged from 0.5 h [61] to 5 h [62], with 34 of our 55 ES using a 2-hour duration (62% of ES). 17 ES utilized intermittent exercise to increase inhaled load [38, 42, 45, 47–49, 51, 55, 58] and three used continuous exercise [53, 61]. For the remaining 35 ES, participants rested quietly. Most studies implemented a washout period of 7 to 30 days [40, 41, 53, 59, 61, 64], though two did not report washout duration [56, 57]. Notably, the two studies using oil supplementation exposed all participants to filtered air first, forgoing randomization of the exposure order [42, 52].

Study methodological quality and bias

The assessment of methodological quality is summarized in Additional Table 6. CASP scores ranged from 5 to 13 [42, 44, 59], with a median score of 11, indicating generally high quality. The eight studies [41, 44, 46, 47, 51, 57, 60, 64] deemed “average quality” (score 8–9) or lower all had poor reporting of results, and three had incomplete blinding during analysis [44, 51, 60]. Of the seven studies [39, 42, 43, 48, 56, 59, 62] rated as “excellent quality” (score 12–13), only one implemented an exercise protocol [48]. Notably, studies scored an average of 1 (out of 3) on Section C of the CASP assessment, which assesses three questions focused on the reporting of results, sample variance, and benefit/harm analysis (Additional Table 6).

The potential for within-study bias is summarized in Additional Fig. 1. The ROB-2 assessment identified two studies with ‘high concerns’ [56, 57] due to the judgement of ‘some concerns’ in section DS, which focuses on bias from period and carryover effects. The remaining studies scored ‘some concerns,’ primarily due to the judgement of ‘some concerns’ in section D5, which assesses the selection of the reported results. Together, the study quality and bias assessments highlight the reporting of the results as an area for improvement.

Synthesis of the results

Observed SMDs ranged from − 0.76 to 0.95, with 56% of estimates being positive (i.e., pollutant exposure increased HRV (HF)). The pooled estimates for each model are summarized in Table 1. The univariate random-effects estimate was comparable to our final multivariate model (SMD = -0.019, [-0.050, 0.013]). The assumptions of normality, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity were not violated; however, the linearity assumption was. Consequently, we fitted polynomials and spline models (Fig. 2). AICc values for the non-linear models were: quadratic (67.8), RCS (68.0), and cubic (70.0) (Table 1; Fig. 2). The quadratic and RCS models were statistically indistinguishable (ΔAICc = 0.2, within the established 2-unit equivalence threshold), and both achieved complete residual heterogeneity absorption (QEp = 1.0). The RCS model was selected as the primary model over the quadratic model on scientific grounds: (i) RCS constrains the fitted curve to be linear beyond the outer knots, preventing unrealistic extrapolation in the data-sparse 4–5 h range where only a small number of effect sizes are available and (ii) the quadratic model imposes a symmetric parabolic shape that may not reflect the asymmetric plateau-then-decline pattern observed in the data.

Certainty of evidence (GRADE)

The GRADE assessment for the effect of pollutant exposure on the HF component of HRV is summarized in Additional Table 7. The overall certainty of the evidence was rated as “low”. The evidence was downgraded by one level for risk of bias, as 25 of 27 studies were judged to have “some concerns” regarding the selection of reported results (Domain 5 of ROB-2) and two studies had “high concerns” regarding carryover effects (Domain S of ROB-2). The evidence was further downgraded by one level for imprecision because the confidence bands at the 2-hour timepoint encompass the null (Fig. 3). No downgrades were applied for inconsistency (I2 = 0; p = 1.0), indirectness, or publication bias (regression test p = 0.184).

Discussion

A summary overview is presented in Fig. 4. When summarizing findings from a meta-analysis, it is important to consider: (i) the magnitude and direction of the pooled effect; (ii) the precision of the pooled effect; (iii) the quality of the evidence; and (iv) the clinical significance of the findings.

The aim of this meta-regression was to determine the effect of controlled pollutant exposure duration (in hours), compared to filtered air, on HRV (HF) in adult humans. Initially, we observed no detrimental decrease in HRV (HF) from air pollution exposure compared to the filtered air control (SMD = -0.019 [-0.050, 0.013], p = 0.239, Table 1). However, the association was non-linear; HRV (HF) appeared stable during the first three hours of exposure but then sloped downward (Fig. 3). A non-linear RCS meta-regression revealed a significant negative association between study-level exposure duration and pooled HRV (HF) effect sizes (SMD = -0.221 [-0.365, -0.07], p = 0.003, Table 1), suggesting that studies using longer exposure protocols tended to report greater decreases in HRV.

The first half of this discussion elucidates potential factors that may have influenced the pooled effect. Notably, despite the significant association observed in the RCS model, the overall certainty of this evidence was rated as ‘low’ based on the GRADE approach. Accordingly, the second half of this discussion explores the clinical implications of our findings, critiques the individual studies, and provides specific recommendations for future research design and bias control.

Limitations and strengths

Several limitations should be borne in mind. First, we included cross-over studies and RCTs which should reduce inter-subject variability and increase internal validity to ensure an optimal pooled estimate. However, many studies showed unexpected HRV (HF) changes in the control group. These unexpected changes in control groups may reflect natural short-term variability in autonomic tone, differences in breathing patterns, or participant stress such as habituation to the laboratory environment. Because HF is sensitive to respiratory rhythm and parasympathetic modulation, even small differences in resting state, posture, or breathing can produce apparent changes in control conditions that may not be attributable to exposure. It is unclear if these changes are due to this natural variation or issues with the study quality, but they diminished the estimated pooled effect. Second, while we identified 27 studies and 55 ES, most studies had small sample sizes (median n = 18). This contributed to high level 1 (sampling) variance and limited our ability to test moderators that might explain level 3 (between-study) variance. Third, the reporting of exposure composition and concentration lacked standardization. This creates uncertainty about whether we were truly investigating a single pollutant model, as we had to treat pollutant exposure as a singular effect when it may have been a mixture. Fourth, our focus on laboratory-based studies maximized internal validity at the expense of external validity (generalizability). Lastly, seven studies [65–71] that met inclusion criteria were not included because authors could not provide the requested data. While we did not statistically observe it, the exclusion of these studies may have led to publication bias.

This meta-regression also had several strengths. First, this analysis is the first to calculate a pooled estimate of the effect of controlled air pollutant exposure on autonomic balance (i.e., HRV (HF)) in humans. Second, we utilized cross-over designs and RCTs, which theoretically provide robust effect size estimates. Third, we focused on laboratory-based studies to maximize internal validity and obtain more precise estimates. Lastly, we had a sufficient number of ES to conduct moderator analysis and generate insights for designing future studies.

Interpretation and comparison with other studies

In this section, we discuss: (i) the magnitude and direction of the effect compared with other studies; (ii) unexpected findings; and (iii) moderation analysis.

Comparison with other studies

This work expands on a prior systematic review that noted inconsistent HRV outcomes in acute exposure studies [18]. Eight studies [40, 41, 44, 46, 51, 54, 60, 63] from that review were included in our current analysis of 27 studies. The addition of 19 studies and an in-depth analysis of exposure duration led us to believe temporality is a key factor in the acute autonomic response (Table 2). Using an RCS approach, we found that studies employing exposure protocols up to 3 h reported negligible pooled HRV changes (SMD = -0.026). However, exposure beyond 3 h is associated with a larger negative HRV response, regardless of concentration (Fig. 3). The predicted effect at 5 h suggests a small, negative change in HRV (SMD = -0.367) compared to a negligible change at 3 h (SMD = -0.026). One biologically plausible explanation for this time-dependent pattern is that controlled pollutant exposure may require time to trigger a physiological response including pulmonary and systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and autonomic reflex changes. These pathways can reduce parasympathetic activity and alter vagal control, which would be expected to appear more clearly during longer exposure windows than during brief exposures. It is important to note that this pattern reflects variation in study-level design characteristics, specifically, the duration of the experimental exposure protocol, and does not constitute direct evidence of an individual-level dose-response relationship.

Two concepts, cumulative exposure and lag effects, are crucial for interpreting these findings. Our results could suggest a greater reduction in parasympathetic activity with higher cumulative exposure. However, they could also support the concept of “lag effects,” where adverse health effects are not immediately noticeable with the onset of exposure [72]. Most research on lag has focused on 24- to 72-hour exposures. For example, data shows greater decreases in HF power with higher 24-hour PM2.5 levels [73], and another study demonstrated a greater magnitude of detrimental change as the moving average for exposure increased from 4-hour to 24-hour to 48-hour [74]. Importantly, 41 of our 55 ES had measurement durations below our identified 3-hour threshold. More research on acute exposures between 3 and 24 h is needed to better understand these acute exposure responses.

Unexpected findings

The random variance (tau²) for the meta-regression was zero, implying one underlying effect. This is unlikely given the differences in exposure, population, and protocols across studies. The tau² value is likely underestimated, possibly due to sampling variance. A closer inspection of the confidence intervals supports this; while the pooled effect CI is relatively narrow (-0.365, -0.077), the individual effect CIs are wide, indicating limited precision, likely due to small sample sizes across k (median of 18).

Moderation analysis

We applied moderation analysis to study quality and bias, pollutant type, disease state, and exercise. First, only study bias was a significant moderator. It is worth noting that only two [56, 57] of the 27 included studies were deemed to have “high concerns”. We acknowledge that stratifying our meta-regression by study quality or risk of bias may introduce collider stratification bias, as studies with larger effects or lower precision tend to receive lower quality ratings, creating a spurious association within quality strata [75]. While this analysis provides exploratory insights into potential methodological influences, these moderation analysis findings should be interpreted cautiously with this limitation in mind. Second, our finding of a minor HRV decrease after 3 h agrees with previous meta-analyses of observational studies, which reported significant decreases in HRV following a median 24-hour (range: 6–72 h) PM2.5 (1.87% to -2.44%) [12–14] or O3 (− 3.01% ) [17] exposure. Prior literature showed no change following NO2 exposure [15], inconsistent with our overall findings. Third, although it is thought that air pollution is more detrimental to vulnerable populations, we did not observe worse outcomes in studies with participants who had prior health conditions. Fourth, we could not distinguish different effects of pollution on HRV between exercising and non-exercising protocols. Last, there appears to be no meta-level literature on other CVD-related outcomes in controlled, acute exposures, limiting our ability to compare these findings.

Clinical significance, implications, and future recommendations

Clinical significance and implications

Treatment for air pollution-related disease typically occurs after major events like a heart attack. Most improvements in air pollution-related disease are tied to regulation, policy, and public health campaigns. The recommendations needed to guide policy and public health campaigns requires specific data on chemical health impacts, mixtures, dosing, and thresholds. This requires high-quality, controlled experiments and an understanding of environmentally relevant chemicals. The research used in this study helps inform those recommendations by providing specific data on chemical exposure duration and the association with a clinically relevant intermediate outcome related to CVD. Notably, in this analysis we also identified variation in studies that could affect data quality, challenge data harmonization, and potentially limit the impact for data-driven policy change. To address these concerns, we provide suggestions for future cross-over studies aimed at reducing variation (Table 3).

Recommendations for future pollutant exposure related HRV research

Future recommendations for exposure assessment

Three main considerations for pollutant measurements are: (i) the lack of comprehensive reporting of pollution mixtures, (ii) variations in inhaled dose, and (iii) washout periods. Variance in pollutant composition and concentration was an issue across studies. Many projects focused on one pollutant despite using ambient pollution, which is a complex mixture of chemicals including UFP, PM2.5, PM10, and/or gaseous pollutants [2]. Additionally, fluctuations in individual pollutant concentrations across exposure sessions may have led to differences in inhaled load, equating to different doses per visit, and varying HRV responses between participants. The inability to purify to a single pollutant and keep concentrations consistent across visits may have added noise to the dataset. Furthermore, two studies [56, 57] had high concerns of bias due to incomplete reporting of washout periods, raising concerns about carry-over effects. Finally, without biological markers to quantify exposure, investigators could not statistically account for co-exposures or adjust for baseline exposure levels, which can improve the rigor of the analysis.

Future recommendations for HRV measurement

Three main considerations for HRV assessment are: (i) the proportion of unanalyzable electrocardiograms (ECGs), (ii) variance within frequency domain metrics, and (iii) the influence of exercise. Eight studies [39–41, 43, 46, 56, 58, 60] had noticeable differences between the number of participants tested and the number of usable ECGs, with one study losing 76% of its data [60]. Differences in lead placement, the number of leads, participant position, and quality of equipment may contribute to low quality ECG recordings and impact the derivation of HRV metrics. Additionally, there was high internal variation (standard deviation value > mean value) for HF measurements in this sample [40, 41, 45, 47, 48, 51, 54, 59]. This measure is parasympathetically mediated and primarily represents heart rate variation as a result of respiratory changes and RSA [10, 76, 80]. Therefore variation in HF may be, in part, due to fluctuations in breathing rate. HF is derived from a frequency band (0.15–0.40 Hz) [11] and as breathing rate decreases and depth increases, the HF band can become less representative of vagal tone. The use of exercise may have further influenced this variance. Most included studies only approximated minute ventilation (respiratory rate x tidal volume) measurements, making it difficult to distinguish whether changes seen in HF measures were from pollutant effects or changes in breathing patterns. Standardization of study protocols is needed to increase the quality and reliability of HF measures in future studies.

Future recommendations for study design and population selection

Intermittent exercise was often implemented to increase the inhaled pollutant load [81]. However, exercise itself acutely decreases HRV [82], suggesting it may be a confounding variable. For example, two studies that used continuous exercise (moderate-to-vigorous intensity) showed expected decreases in HF outcomes for both exposure and control conditions [53, 61]. Although studies approximated minute ventilation, this measure can vary depending on training status [83] and disease state [84, 85], complicating its use as an appropriate way of standardizing the impact of exercise on HRV across a group of participants. Therefore, the implementation of exercise to increase inhaled load should be used with caution when HF is the primary outcome variable.

Moderator analysis showed no difference between healthy and clinical cohorts, contradicting previous research which suggests prior health conditions may make participants more susceptible to the health consequences of air pollution exposure [86]. However, multiple studies analyzed a “mixed” population, which may have obscured the potential effects of a disease state. Future studies should analyze participants with each health condition independently to increase the rigor of the design.

Conclusions

Evidence indicates that autonomic imbalance is a driving force for CVD. This meta-regression was conducted to clarify the effect of controlled pollutant exposure on HRV in adult humans. Overall results suggest a minor decrease in HRV (HF) following air pollution exposure over time. At the study level, greater autonomic imbalance was observed in studies employing exposure protocols longer than 3 h, suggesting that study-level exposure duration is a meaningful design characteristic associated with the magnitude of the pooled HRV effect. Future research is needed to reduce intra-study variation and to further investigate acute exposure durations.
