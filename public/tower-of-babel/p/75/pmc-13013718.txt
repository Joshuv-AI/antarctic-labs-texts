Pathological Insights into the Limitations of Clinical and Dermoscopic Evaluations in Monitoring Sonidegib Treatment Response for Locally Advanced Basal Cell Carcinoma: A Real-World Study
Authors: Xurong Liu, Jipang Zhan, Jingwen Zou, Rongyi Chen, Jing Zhong, Renliang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSurgical excision is the standard treatment for basal cell carcinoma (BCC). For locally advanced BCC (laBCC) not suitable for surgery or radiotherapy, Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (HHIs) such as sonidegib are important options. Clinical observations have shown that sonidegib may lead to pigmentation and scarring, which can affect treatment evaluation. We evaluated the efficacy and safety of sonidegib in Chinese patients with laBCC and examined discrepancies between clinical/dermoscopic assessments and pathological findings, including posttreatment pathological changes.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 54 patients with laBCC treated with sonidegib 200 mg/day for ≥ 3 months (October 2022–July 2025). Response assessment integrated VISIA-based planimetric lesion-area regression, standardized dermoscopy, and dermoscopy-guided multi-site biopsy as the pathological gold standard. The primary endpoint was objective response rate (ORR); secondary endpoints included disease control rate (DCR) and safety.ResultsAt 3 months, ORR was 87% (complete response [CR] 48%; partial response [PR] 39%), and DCR was 100%. Pathology showed complete clearance in 48.1% and residual tumor in 51.9%, with six cases showing apparent histologic subtype shifts. Dermoscopy in patients with complete remission still demonstrated a high false-positive rate (branching blood vessels 53.8%, blue-gray dots 61.5%), leading to decreased diagnostic specificity. Adverse events occurred in 81.5% of patients; 70.4% reported multiple events, most commonly muscle cramps (66.7%), dysgeusia (59.3%), and alopecia (55.6%). All events were grade 1–3, and no patient discontinued treatment as a result of toxicity.ConclusionIn this real-world Chinese laBCC cohort, sonidegib produced a clinically meaningful response with a favorable safety profile. However, clinical and dermoscopic assessments showed substantial false positives due to posttreatment changes; pathological biopsy remains essential to confirm tumor clearance. Advanced noninvasive imaging (e.g., reflectance confocal microscopy) may further improve monitoring. Prospective studies with longer follow-up are warranted.

Introduction

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common skin malignancy in middle-aged and older adults, accounting for approximately 80% of non-melanoma skin cancers [1]. Despite low mortality, its high incidence imposes a substantial healthcare burden [2]. Surgical excision remains first-line therapy, with Mohs micrographic surgery the gold standard for high-risk or recurrent disease [3]. However, in locally advanced or metastatic BCC, extensive invasion and patient-related factors may preclude curative surgery or radiotherapy, and procedures can cause major disfigurement or functional impairment, highlighting the need for effective nonsurgical options [4, 5]. Major BCC risk factors include older age, male sex, fair skin, UV exposure, prior or family history, immunosuppression, and genetic syndromes such as basal cell nevus (Gorlin–Goltz) syndrome driven by PTCH1 (or less commonly PTCH2) mutations in the Hedgehog signaling pathway [6].

Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (HHIs) have therefore emerged as an important therapeutic option for advanced BCC [7]. BCC development is strongly driven by Hedgehog pathway dysregulation: binding of Hedgehog ligands to PTCH1 relieves its inhibition of Smoothened (SMO), activating downstream signaling that promotes tumor proliferation [8]. More than 90% of BCCs show aberrant Hedgehog activation [9]. While this pathway regulates growth and differentiation during embryogenesis, it is largely quiescent in adults, with limited roles such as hair follicle regeneration and taste function [10].

Sonidegib is a Smoothened (SMO) inhibitor and the second approved Hedgehog pathway inhibitor for locally advanced BCC (laBCC) after vismodegib, with efficacy and safety supported by trials and real-world evidence [11, 12]. Although approved in China in July 2021, data in Chinese populations remain limited [13]. In 2024, Zhang and Ran described misleading posttreatment pigment retention during sonidegib therapy that was mistaken for residual tumor, highlighting potential discordance between clinical assessment and pathology [14].

To further investigate the discrepancies between clinical evaluation, dermoscopic imaging, and pathological gold standard results of skin lesions following sonidegib treatment, this study retrospectively analyzed cases of BCC treated with sonidegib at the Department of Dermatology, Southern Medical University, from October 2022 to July 2025. The study focuses on analyzing the histopathological changes following treatment, evaluating the efficacy and safety of sonidegib in Chinese patients, comparing the consistency of clinical evaluation, dermoscopy, and pathological gold standard in efficacy assessment, and exploring the clinical application value of different evaluation methods in treatment monitoring.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

A retrospective study included patients with laBCC who received sonidegib treatment at the Department of Dermatology, Southern Medical University, from October 2022 to July 2025. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosis of laBCC confirmed by clinical and pathological gold standard prior to treatment, with no eligibility for traditional treatment modalities; (2) Patients who received the standard dose of 200 mg daily of sonidegib for ≥ 3 months; (3) Patients who underwent standardized dermoscopic examination and dermoscopy-guided multi-site (multiple-point) tissue biopsy at 3 months of treatment (see biopsy protocol below); (4) Patients with complete clinical and imaging data, including baseline, 1-month, and 3-month tumor lesion photographs. Exclusion criteria: (1) Use of sonidegib as adjuvant treatment for postsurgical positive margins; (2) Concurrent use of other BCC treatments, such as cryotherapy, laser therapy, or photodynamic therapy, during sonidegib treatment; (3) Patients who have previously received treatment with vismodegib. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dermatology Hospital of Southern Medical University (Approval No. IIT-2025-065) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and ICH-GCP guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The patients in this manuscript provided written informed consent for publication of their case details.

This study retrospectively collected the following data: (1) Demographic characteristics (age, gender, and underlying diseases); (2) Tumor characteristics (primary location, maximum diameter, and histopathological subtypes, including superficial, nodular, infiltrative, adenoid, basosquamous cell carcinoma, morpheaform, and micronodular types); (3) Treatment history (pre-treatment BCC therapies before HHI); (4) Treatment parameters (start and end dates of HHI); (5) Efficacy assessment components (VISIA-based clinical imaging, standardized dermoscopic assessment, and dermoscopy-guided multi-site biopsy results at 3 months); (6) Laboratory test indicators (pre- and posttreatment blood routine tests, muscle enzyme spectrum, liver and kidney function, and lipid profile); (7) Safety data (recording all treatment-related adverse events and grading according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 by the National Cancer Institute).

Efficacy was assessed using a multidimensional approach: After 3 months of treatment, the following criteria were used: (1) VISIA-based clinical imaging: standardized VISIA photographs were obtained using the VISIA imaging system (Canfield Scientific, USA) at baseline and 3 months. Lesion regression was quantified by planimetric measurement of lesion area (cm2) using VISIA analysis software; % regression = (baseline area − follow-up area)/baseline area × 100. Two senior clinicians independently assessed tumor presence/absence and lesion area while blinded to dermoscopic and pathological findings; Disagreements were resolved by joint review; unresolved cases were adjudicated by a third senior clinician. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s κ (binary) and ICC (continuous). (2) Dermoscopy: three representative dermoscopic images per lesion (center and margins) were captured using a digital dermatoscope (JY-2980; Jinyuan, China). Two senior dermatologists independently and blindly evaluated images using a predefined checklist of dermoscopic criteria. Major features included arborizing vessels, short fine telangiectasias, blue-gray ovoid nests, multiple blue-gray globules/dots, leaf-like areas, spoke-wheel/concentric structures, and ulceration/erosion (including multiple small erosions). Supportive features included shiny white structures (white shiny lines/blotches and strands). Lesions were classified as “tumor-positive” if ≥ 1 major feature suggestive of residual disease was present; supportive features alone were not considered sufficient for tumor positivity. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, and Cohen’s κ was calculated. (3) Pathology (gold standard): dermoscopy-guided multi-site biopsy was performed at week 12 with ≥ 3 samples per lesion using 3-mm punch biopsies, targeting the most suspicious dermoscopic foci plus at least one peripheral/marginal site (clock-face mapping) to reduce sampling bias. All specimens underwent step-sectioning at three levels, and two senior pathologists reviewed samples in a double-blind manner for residual tumor, with consensus resolution of discrepancies. (4) After data collection was completed, the information from the data collection forms was entered into an electronic database. Two individuals independently performed double data entry for the same dataset. The results were then compared, and any discrepancies identified were resolved by referring back to the original source documents to ensure data accuracy.

Efficacy criteria were determined according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST 1.1), integrating clinical, dermoscopic and pathological results into the following categories: complete response (CR, confirmed by biopsy with no tumor residue), partial response (PR, ≥ 30% tumor regression but with pathological residue or complete ulcer resolution if present at baseline), stable disease (SD, change < 30%), and progressive disease (PD, ≥ 20% increase in lesions or new ulcerations/lesions). The primary endpoint was the objective response rate (ORR = CR + PR), with secondary endpoints including disease control rate (DCR = CR + PR + SD), time to first tumor response, and safety indicators (AE and laboratory results, etc.).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed using SPSS version 26.0 software. Continuous variables with normal distribution were described using mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed continuous variables were described using median values. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages (%). Primary endpoint analysis: The ORR was estimated using the two-sided 95% confidence interval (95% CI) calculated with the Clopper–Pearson method. Safety analysis: the occurrence of adverse events (AEs) was listed by frequency and further categorized by system organ class and severity. Patients who experienced at least one specific AE were described in terms of both count and percentage.

Results

General Information

This study included 54 patients with laBCC who received 200 mg/day sonidegib treatment. Among them, 29 were male (53.7%) and 25 were female (46.3%), with a median age of 67 years (range 20–84 years). Tumors were predominantly located on the face, with specific distribution as follows: 3 cases (5.6%) in the scalp region, 9 cases (16.7%) in the mid-face, 8 cases (14.8%) around the eyes, 17 cases (31.5%) in the nasal region, 4 cases (7.4%) in the ear region, and 4 cases (7.4%) around the lips; an additional 4 cases (7.4%) involved multiple lesions. 32 cases (59.3%) were associated with ulceration, while 22 cases (40.7%) had no ulceration. Previous treatments were administered to 64.8% (35/54) of the patients, including 12 cases (22.2%) of surgical treatment, 21 cases (38.9%) of nonsurgical treatment, and 2 cases (3.7%) of superficial X-ray radiotherapy. Lesion Improvement

After 3 months of treatment, imaging analysis showed complete lesion disappearance in 12 patients (22.2%), partial regression (≥ 30% reduction from baseline) in 35 patients (64.8%), and no significant regression in 7 patients (13.0%), who showed stable lesions. No tumor progression was observed in any patient. Among them, 23 patients (42.6%) developed depressed scars, 15 patients (27.8%) developed superficial scars, and 42 patients (77.8%) still had pigmented lesions, including 16/26 (61.5%) of those with complete pathological clearance. Scarring and pigmented lesions may affect the accurate assessment of tumor area. Posttreatment Histopathological Findings

After 12 weeks of treatment, all patients underwent multiple-point biopsy of suspicious lesions under dermoscopic guidance. Pathological assessment revealed that 26 patients (48.1%) achieved complete pathological remission with full tumor cell clearance, while 28 patients (51.9%) had tumor residue. Baseline subtype (noninvasive vs invasive) showed clearance in 19/33 (57.6%) versus 7/21 (33.3%), respectively, with no significant difference (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.101). The odds of clearance were lower in invasive versus noninvasive lesions (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.12–1.15). Among patients with residual tumor, 6 patients (21.4%) experienced pathological subtype transformation (Figs. 2, 3), including changes from morpheaform to micronodular, micronodular to infiltrative, micronodular to nodular, morpheaform to superficial, infiltrative to adenoid, and nodular to funnel-shaped cystic type. Among the 28 patients with residual BCC, 10 (35.7%) ultimately underwent Mohs micrographic surgery. Posttreatment Dermoscopic Findings

After 3 months of treatment, dermoscopic examination revealed that 31 patients (57.4%) exhibited branched vessels, 22 patients (40.7%) displayed blue-gray ovoid nests, 40 patients (74.1%) showed blue-gray dots and globules, 16 patients (29.6%) demonstrated maple-leaf-like structures, 19 patients (35.2%) presented radial structures, and 6 patients (11.1%) showed ulceration/erosion. Efficacy

The ORR was 87% (47/54), with 48% achieving complete response (CR) (26/54), 39% showing partial response (PR) (21/54), and 13% showing stable disease (SD) (7/54), resulting in a disease control rate (DCR) of 100%.

Adverse Events

Among all 54 patients, 44 (81.5%) experienced adverse events (AEs), with 38 (70.4%) reporting multiple AEs. The most common adverse events included muscle cramps (36 cases, 66.7%), taste disturbance (32 cases, 59.3%), and hair loss (30 cases, 55.6%), with 4 cases presenting as diffuse non-scarring alopecia. Additionally, fatigue (13 cases, 24.1%) and loss of appetite (14 cases, 25.9%) were also relatively common, with other types and specific grading detailed in Fig. 6. Laboratory abnormalities were most commonly observed in elevated myocardial enzymes, lipid levels, and liver enzymes, with most abnormalities being mild and transient. The majority of adverse events were graded as level 1 or 2, with grade 3 events occurring at a lower rate, primarily including muscle cramps, loss of appetite, diarrhea, weight loss, elevated creatine kinase (CK), and hyperlipidemia. No grade 4 or 5 adverse events were reported. None of the adverse events led to treatment discontinuation. Discussion

This study observed a clinically meaningful response to sonidegib in a real-world Chinese laBCC cohort. A multicenter randomized controlled trial reported that the overall response rate of sonidegib in the treatment of locally advanced BCC was 56.1%, with a median time to onset of 2.5 to 4 months [11]. Another study showed that the median time to onset of sonidegib (200 mg) in BCC treatment was 3.9 months, with an objective response rate of 36.0% at 6 months of treatment [15]. In our cohort, many patients showed visible improvement within 1 month, and ORR reached 87.0% at 3 months, with pathological complete clearance in 48.1%. However, direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, because our study is retrospective with short follow-up and differences in baseline tumor characteristics, endpoints, and assessment methods may influence apparent response rates and time-to-response.

The main histological subtypes of BCC include noninvasive (nodular, superficial) and invasive (morpheaform, infiltrative, micronodular, and basosquamous cell carcinoma) types [16]. Invasive subtypes typically exhibit stronger local invasive behavior, resulting in a higher recurrence risk and extensive local tissue destruction [17]. Pathological analysis revealed important tumor characteristics in 26 patients with confirmed tumor clearance: 18 cases of nodular type, 3 cases of superficial type, 2 cases of morpheaform type, 2 cases of infiltrative type, and 1 case of adenoid type. Interestingly, among the 28 patients with confirmed residual BCC tumor cells, 6 cases showed a shift in pathological subtypes. Previous studies have reported a case in which, after sonidegib treatment, the histological type transitioned from the invasive infiltrative type to the noninvasive superficial and nodular types, indicating a shift towards a more indolent subtype [18]. The six transitions observed in this study included changes from invasive to noninvasive subtypes, as well as conversions among invasive subtypes, but no transition from noninvasive to invasive subtypes was observed. This phenomenon may reflect intratumoral heterogeneity: BCC is unlikely to be a single clonal population, and multiple histologic components can coexist within the same tumor, such that different biopsy sites or timepoints may capture different predominant patterns. Under treatment pressure and microenvironmental changes, selective clearance of more drug-sensitive components could further shift the apparent subtype on follow-up biopsy. Although subtype-specific sensitivity to sonidegib is a plausible explanation for differences in response depth, this has not been confirmed, and available evidence suggests that invasive and noninvasive BCC subtypes respond similarly to sonidegib [16].

Recent studies have identified significant challenges in evaluating the efficacy of sonidegib treatment for BCC. Zhang and Ran reported a case of deceptive pigment retention after sonidegib treatment for BCC, which was misinterpreted as tumor residue. Postsurgical pathology confirmed complete tumor regression, but the tissue contained numerous pigment granules and tissue cells [14]. This study systematically analyzed 26 cases with pathological confirmation of complete remission, revealing significant limitations in clinical evaluation and dermoscopic examination for efficacy assessment. Histopathological examination showed characteristic posttreatment changes in all cases, primarily including lymphocytic infiltration, pigment granule deposition/macrophage infiltration, scar formation, basal cell liquefaction degeneration, inflammatory response, and granuloma formation. These posttreatment changes hindered identification of true residual disease. Among pathologically cleared cases (n = 26), the false-positive rate was 53.8% (14/26) for clinical assessment and 61.5% (16/26) for dermoscopy, with correct identification of clearance in 46.2% (12/26) and 38.5% (10/26), respectively (Table 4). The important findings of this study pose new challenges to the current diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines for BCC. Dermoscopy is a widely used, highly sensitive, and specific tool for primary BCC diagnosis, with reported pooled sensitivity and specificity of 91.2% and 95.0%, respectively [20]. The 2023 European guidelines recommend dermoscopy as a grade A tool for BCC diagnosis, with level 1 evidence [21]. Our observations suggest that dermoscopy may have certain limitations for assessing response to sonidegib. Certain typical dermoscopic features may show false positive results after sonidegib treatment. For instance, branched vessels may actually result from superficial dermal vascular dilation, while pigmented structures such as blue-gray ovoid nests, dots, maple-leaf-like, and radial structures may originate from pigment granules retained in macrophages, rather than viable tumor cells. These factors directly lead to a decrease in the specificity of dermoscopy for efficacy assessment. Histologically, tumor-free posttreatment biopsies frequently showed pigment granules and pigment-laden macrophages (melanophages), which can manifest as persistent blue-gray structures on dermoscopy and explain “tumor-positive” readings despite pathological clearance. These deposits show size-dependent correlates—fine granules as blue-gray dots, clustered granules as globules, and confluent aggregates as blue-gray structureless or ovoid nest–like areas—mimicking pigmented BCC features. Together, these findings support a tumor–pigment regression desynchrony, in which tumor eradication precedes pigment clearance and creates a distinct diagnostic window early after treatment.

The possible mechanism is that, in addition to tumor cells, the tumor tissue contains a large number of dendritic melanocytes and pigment-laden macrophages [22, 23]. Sonidegib primarily exerts its anti-tumor effect by inhibiting the Hedgehog signaling pathway in cancer cells, but has lower efficacy in clearing pigment-laden or phagocytosed cells. Even after cancer cells have died, existing pigment granules may remain locally and be phagocytosed by macrophages. The metabolism and clearance of these pigment granules is a slow biological process, and the degradation mechanism remains unclear, often requiring months or even years [24].

This finding identifies key drivers of false positives in clinical and dermoscopic assessment and supports pathological confirmation to distinguish true residual disease after sonidegib. Reliance on noninvasive evaluation alone may overestimate residual tumor, leading to overtreatment and unnecessary interventions. Future response-assessment frameworks should incorporate objective validation, and emerging modalities such as LC-OCT, reflectance confocal microscopy, dynamic OCT, and 18F-FDG PET/CT may further improve noninvasive monitoring by providing real-time histology-like or metabolic information [25–28].

Vismodegib, the first HHI approved for BCC, has raised safety concerns, with some studies suggesting an increased risk of secondary squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), particularly in older patients or sun-exposed sites [29]. Mohan et al. [30] reported a higher SCC risk (HR 8.12, 95% CI 2.15–30.72), possibly related to activation of alternative pathways (e.g., RAS/MAPK) after HH inhibition, although other studies found no such association [30–32]. To date, no reports have linked sonidegib to secondary SCC, likely due to limited long-term data. In our cohort (n = 54; 12 weeks), we observed squamous epithelial dysplasia (3.7%) (Fig. 7), squamous hyperplasia (5.6%), and a keratotic neoplasm (1.9%), but no SCC. After sonidegib, lesion regression may simplify subsequent surgery, enabling greater tissue preservation and potentially improving excision success and quality of life. However, delayed pigment clearance, scarring, and difficulty defining tumor margins may necessitate pathological biopsy and/or emerging imaging modalities for accurate assessment [34].

Our study has some limitations: it was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size, no control group, and short follow-up, precluding assessment of long-term efficacy, recurrence, and secondary SCC risk. Given differences in baseline histology and outcome assessment, cross-study comparisons should be made cautiously. Prospective studies are warranted to optimize individualized dosing and clarify the timing of sonidegib in combination with surgery.

Conclusion

In this single-center real-world cohort of 54 patients with locally advanced BCC treated with sonidegib (200 mg/day), the 3-month ORR was 87% (CR 48.1%, PR 38.9%), while pathology confirmed complete tumor clearance in 48.1% of cases. Notably, dermoscopic features suggestive of BCC frequently persisted even after pathological clearance, indicating that clinical and dermoscopic assessment alone may overestimate residual disease in the early posttreatment setting. Sonidegib was generally well tolerated, with most adverse events being mild and manageable, and no discontinuations due to toxicity. Therefore, dermoscopy-guided biopsy remains essential to confirm tumor clearance when posttreatment pigmentation or scarring confounds noninvasive assessment, and longer prospective studies are warranted to refine monitoring strategies and evaluate long-term outcomes.
