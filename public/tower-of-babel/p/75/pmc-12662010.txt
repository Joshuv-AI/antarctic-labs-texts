A comprehensive dataset on stakeholder participation in international accounting standards board’s due process (2001–2024): Insights into legitimacy, engagement, and influence in international accounting standard-setting
Authors: Horacio Molina-Sánchez, Mar Ortiz-Gómez, Francisco Fernández-Navarro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This article presents an extensive dataset capturing stakeholder participation in the due process of the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) from 2001 to 2024. It systematically records and analyzes 23,925 instances of participation from 5046 unique entities across 202 standard-setting projects, providing valuable insights into stakeholder engagement patterns, geographic and institutional influences, and legitimacy strategies employed by the IASB. The dataset distinguishes between individual and joint submissions, facilitating nuanced analyses of stakeholder motivations and engagement intensity. Demographic variables such as geographic origin, legal tradition, organizational type, and historical participation rates are included, enabling researchers to investigate institutional drivers and barriers affecting stakeholder involvement. By shedding light on variations in participation over critical periods—before and after International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) adoption, during the global financial crisis, and following increased procedural emphasis as a legitimate strategy—the dataset supports further examination of IASB’s legitimacy strategies, stakeholder influence, and international accounting standard-setting dynamics.

Background

The IASB is the authoritative body responsible for developing International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), which are currently employed by publicly listed entities across 147 jurisdictions to prepare consolidated financial statements. The IASB functions under the governance of the IFRS Foundation, a private organization that implements various strategies to ensure the legitimacy of its standards [[1], [2], [3]].

The accounting literature on stakeholder participation has significantly expanded over the last three decades. Most studies have primarily examined responses to individual consultation documents, termed the single-issue approach, whereas relatively fewer have adopted a multi-issue approach, addressing multiple consultations simultaneously [[4], [5], [6],2,7]. Larson (2007) [4], for instance, applied a methodology similar to ours, analyzing comment letters submitted in response to interpretations issued by the International Financial Reporting Interpretations Committee (IFRIC). Similarly, our dataset distinguishes between individual and joint submissions, a crucial differentiation for accurately capturing patterns of stakeholder engagement [8].

In contrast, several authors adopt a longitudinal and multi-issue perspective. Jorissen et al. (2012, 2013, 2014) [6,2,7] and Larson and Herz (2013) [8] conducted extensive analyses covering various periods and regulatory topics, typically using stakeholder groups as their primary unit of analysis.

Data Description

This study utilizes a meticulously constructed panel dataset that captures stakeholder engagement in the due process of the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) and the governance activities of the IFRS Foundation from 2001 to 2024. The dataset is designed to facilitate empirical analyses of participation patterns, stakeholder characteristics, and the evolution of international accounting standard-setting processes.

Dataset composition

The dataset comprises two interrelated components:•IASB Due Process Participants Database: This database records all identifiable participants who submitted to IASB projects during the specified period. It includes details such as the constituent’s country or region of origin, type of jurisdiction, stakeholder group classification, and the year of first participation.•IASB Comment Letter Submissions Database: This component focuses on individual comment letter submissions, enabling a granular analysis of stakeholder influence through formal feedback mechanisms. It documents the number of comment letters submitted by each participant, distinguishing between individual and joint submissions.

Temporal coverage and project inclusion

The dataset encompasses 202 IASB projects for which information was available between 2001 and 2024. Notably, from 2004 onwards, the dataset aims to include all projects issued for public consultation, barring any inadvertent omissions. Each project is represented with a unique identifier, and the dataset specifies the involvement of each participant in these projects. The projects included address financial reporting (183 projects), governance (18 projects), and the first consultation on sustainability issues, held before the launch in 2021 of the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), which assumes a role similar to that of the IASB for sustainability standards.

Data coding and classification

Stakeholders are categorized based on their organizational characteristics and roles in the standard-setting process. The classification includes, but is not limited to, audit firms, standard setters, preparers, users, regulators, and academics. Geographical and legal tradition classifications are assigned according to established frameworks, with coding schemes documented in accompanying codebooks.

In instances where entities have undergone name changes or mergers, efforts have been made to maintain continuity in the dataset. Historical records are preserved, and, where applicable, previous and subsequent entity names are identified. For mergers involving previously non-participating entities, the history of the active entity is maintained; if both entities had prior participation, a new participant entry is created.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data sources and collection procedures

Data were collected through systematic archival research of publicly available documents published by the IASB and the IFRS Foundation. Primary sources include comment letters submitted in response to IASB consultations, project pages detailing due process steps, and official documentation related to the IFRS Foundation’s governance structure. For the Participants Database, information on each identifiable participant to IASB due process consultations from 2001 to 2024 was manually compiled. For the Submissions Database, data were assembled focusing on each individual submission, treating each comment letter as a separate observation.

Data processing and organization

All data underwent manual review and coding to ensure accuracy and consistency. Data cleaning and structuring were performed using Microsoft Excel and Python, facilitating further analysis and export to standard research formats. The methodology is transparent and replicable using the same sources.

These findings affirm that the IASB’s due process functions not only as a mechanism of democratic legitimacy [6,8], but also as an avenue for gathering high-quality technical input, reinforcing a broader legitimacy strategy based on expertise [3]. Within this framework, the dataset identifies 5046 unique participants, each contributing an average of 4.74 times. When accounting for individual and collective submissions, this figure corresponds to 4716 entities, with a slightly higher mean participation rate of 4.82.

A closer examination of regional trends reveals that international organizations—including global auditing firms and regulatory bodies—exhibit the highest levels of engagement, averaging 19.09 submissions per entity. Among regional contributors, Europe and Asia & Oceania display the strongest and most consistent participation, with mean submission rates of 6.09 and 5.98, respectively. This high engagement likely reflects their early and sustained adoption of IFRS, as well as their institutional maturity and economic development.

Conversely, entities based in the United States demonstrate lower participation rates, which is consistent with the country’s non-mandatory IFRS regime. Nevertheless, Canada exhibits notably higher engagement (averaging 3.98 letters per entity), suggesting regional variation within North America. U.S. stakeholder activity appears to have been concentrated during specific collaborative efforts between the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) and IASB, particularly around the 2006 Memorandum of Understanding, which spurred robust input on high-profile projects such as Leases and Revenue Recognition.

African and Latin American participation, while present, tends to be more sporadic and less sustained over time. Despite formal adoption of IFRS in these regions, participation rates—averaging 5.02 and 4.11 submissions per entity, respectively—remain comparatively low. These patterns likely reflect underlying economic and institutional constraints and underscore the ongoing challenge of achieving balanced global representation in international accounting standard-setting.

Beyond multinational audit firms, regional and national standard setters, as well as audit associations, also demonstrate significant levels of engagement in the IASB’s due process. Regional and domestic standard-setting bodies participated in an average of 42.32 projects (20.95 % of the total), submitting comment letters either individually or in collaboration in 43.11 cases (21.31 % of the total). Audit associations—some of which function as delegated standard setters in specific jurisdictions, such as the Accounting Practice Board of SAICA in South Africa—have also shown a sustained commitment, averaging 20.34 submissions per entity.

Corporate entities, while accounting for a large volume of total comment letters—5978 in total—display a more selective engagement profile. Although 6748 companies participated over the period, the average number of letters per entity is relatively low (3.20), indicating that participation is often project-specific and likely driven by issues of particular strategic or operational relevance. These patterns are consistent with previous findings by Jorissen et al. (2012) [6] and Dobler and Knosper (2016) [9], who also observed intense but selective corporate participation. Moreover, Orens et al. (2011) [10] have shown that the jurisdictional context influences the likelihood and nature of corporate involvement, a line of inquiry that this dataset now makes possible to explore more comprehensively by identifying key actors within this stakeholder group and analyzing their participation trajectories in greater detail.

By contrast, investor participation—despite investors being a primary user group of financial reporting—is markedly limited. The dataset records a total of 1073 submissions from investor-related entities, either individually or jointly, with an average of only three comment letters per participant. In response to this imbalance, the IASB has initiated various outreach efforts aimed at encouraging greater investor involvement. The richness of the dataset now allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of whether those initiatives have been effective—particularly by comparing participation patterns across regulatory projects with varying levels of promotional activity and stakeholder targeting.

In addition to investors, financial institutions and insurance entities also represent unique stakeholder profiles in terms of their motivations for participation. These entities often occupy a dual role: as users of financial statements—particularly in their capacity as providers of capital—and as preparers of financial information subject to IFRS standards.

These data shed light not only on the breadth but also on the depth of stakeholder involvement over time.

The dataset shows that only a small fraction—approximately 0.99 %—of all participating entities have engaged in >50 % of the projects throughout the period under analysis. Temporal analysis of stakeholder participation

Stakeholder participation in the standard-setting process has evolved over time, reflecting different sources of legitimacy attributed to the IASB and its predecessor, the International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC). During the IASC phase, legitimacy was primarily derived from participation and representation, aiming to reinforce the democratic nature of the process. In a subsequent stage, following the establishment of the IASB, legitimacy increasingly relied on technical expertise, whereby high-quality stakeholder input was expected to contribute to the development of technically sound standards. Finally, in the aftermath of the global financial crisis, the IASB placed greater emphasis on procedural legitimacy, positioning due process as a key mechanism to compensate for the institution’s limited democratic foundation [3].

To analyze how stakeholder engagement evolved over time, participation was segmented into four distinct periods: (1) the initial stage from 2001 to 2004, preceding the adoption of IFRS in the European Union; (2) the mid-period from 2005 to 2015, spanning pre- and post-crisis years characterized by intense regulatory activity; (3) the most recent phase from 2016 to 2024, during which legitimacy has increasingly been associated with the technical quality of contributions, and (4) the whole period from 2001 to 2024. Analytical applications and methodological potential

The datasets presented here, comprising metadata on stakeholder characteristics, dates, and frequencies of participations, lend themselves to a range of quantitative and comparative research methods suitable for a robust scientific manuscript. These approaches can generate valuable insights into patterns of engagement, stakeholder typologies, and temporal dynamics, without requiring access to the full text of comment letters.

A foundational strategy involves the use of descriptive statistical techniques. By examining central tendencies, measures of dispersion, and proportional distributions of participation, researchers can identify dominant jurisdictions, stakeholder groups, or years of heightened involvement. This descriptive layer offers clarity on the general landscape of engagement and serves as a preliminary step in formulating more nuanced research questions. A complementary line of inquiry relies on time-series analysis to uncover temporal shifts in the intensity or diversity of participation, focusing on potential inflection points that align with external developments, such as IFRS adoption milestones or significant amendments to the governance structure. Time-series approaches highlight dynamic aspects of stakeholder behavior, revealing whether and how global economic or regulatory events trigger measurable changes in the data.

For investigations that track individual stakeholders across multiple time periods, panel data or longitudinal methods enhance explanatory power by controlling unobserved heterogeneity. These methods allow researchers to assess both time-invariant characteristics—such as stakeholder type or jurisdiction—and time-varying factors, such as evolving regulatory environments. Panel analyses can clarify whether certain attributes, for instance the legal origin or economic status of a jurisdiction, systematically predict the extent or consistency of participation over the study period.

Event history or survival analysis provides an additional dimension, particularly for questions about the timing of initial engagement and the probability of repeated participation. By defining events such as the first submission of a comment letter or the cessation of ongoing involvement, event history models reveal how catalysts, including the publication of new standards or the commencement of pivotal projects, correlate with the hazard rate of participation. This approach also facilitates inferences regarding how governance reforms or macroeconomic shocks prompt stakeholders to enter—or exit—the standard-setting arena.

In instances where researchers seek to identify specific drivers of engagement, logistic regression or other discrete choice models can be employed to determine the likelihood of participation based on known entity characteristics. By treating submission decisions or project-level involvement as the outcome variable, these methods enable testing of hypotheses related to organizational incentives, such as whether preparers and regulators diverge systematically in their propensity to engage with certain types of consultations.

Comparative or cross-sectional analyses may further illuminate structural variations by focusing on aggregated snapshots of participation over defined intervals. Such approaches are valuable for contrasting, for example, participation rates of professional bodies versus those of large multinational preparers, or exploring differences in engagement between developed and emerging markets. The resulting findings enrich discussions on inclusiveness, global representation, and the extent to which diverse constituencies contribute to the standard-setting discourse.

The metadata on co-authored submissions, if sufficiently detailed, also permits limited application of network analysis. By mapping relationships between submitters who jointly sign comment letters, it becomes possible to visualize alliances and measure structural properties of these connections. Although this approach is inherently restricted by the level of detail available, it can offer insights into whether particular clusters of stakeholders or geographic blocks coalesce repeatedly around specific types of proposals.

Overall, these methods leverage the strengths of the dataset’s metadata, which captures essential information on who participates, when they do so, and in which projects. Researchers should note that the absence of full text comment letters in the database precludes direct textual or thematic analyses, including sentiment or argumentative structure; however, many of these letters remain accessible on the IASB website at the time of writing, referenced by their letter numbers. Nevertheless, employing the aforementioned quantitative, event-based, and comparative strategies enables a rigorous examination of global stakeholder involvement and provides evidence-based perspectives on the governance efficacy of the IASB’s due process and the IFRS Foundation’s broader regulatory framework.

Limitations

As with any manually constructed dataset, the present work is subject to several limitations that stem both from the nature of the source material and from the procedures used in data processing and variable construction. These limitations are classified into two main categories: (1) issues related to the source data and (2) issues arising from the development of derived variables.

Limitations related to source data

The data collection process was entirely manual and highly labor-intensive, involving the entry and verification of nearly 24,000 records. This introduces several potential sources of error:•Duplicate entities: One risk lies in the possible duplication of entities across different records. To mitigate this, country-level filtering was employed, which allowed for the identification and correction of duplicate entries within smaller, more manageable subsets.•Name changes and entity continuity: A second limitation relates to undetected changes in the names of participating entities. In some cases, an entity may appear twice—once under its former name and again under a new one—without being recognized as the same participant. Although such cases were reviewed and appear to be infrequent, they cannot be entirely ruled out.•Attribution errors in joint letters: Comment letters sometimes list multiple participating entities. There is a risk that such submissions were mistakenly attributed to only one participant, potentially underestimating the true extent of joint engagement.•Incomplete project coverage: Although significant effort was made to include all IASB projects open to public consultation between 2001 and 2024, it is possible that some were unintentionally omitted. However, the number of such omissions is expected to be minimal and unlikely to affect the overall representativeness of the dataset.

Limitations related to derived variables

In addition to raw participation data, the dataset incorporates a range of derived variables, which introduce a second layer of potential error:•Manual classification: Variables such as geographic region, legal tradition, and stakeholder group were manually assigned. Despite systematic cross-checking—such as validating consistency within country- based groupings—these classifications may still contain occasional inaccuracies, particularly in cases where entities fulfill multiple roles (e.g., both a standard-setter and a professional association).•Index calculation: Several indexes and summary statistics were computed using spreadsheet formulas. While standard techniques were employed to minimize error, the possibility of formula-related miscalculations cannot be fully excluded.
