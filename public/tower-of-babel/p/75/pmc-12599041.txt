The role of gut microbiota tyrosine decarboxylases in levodopa pharmacokinetics: insights from a levodopa challenge test
Authors: Yukun Feng, Ronggui Zhang, Guangxin Wen, Ling Xie, Tao Chen, Weiguo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiota is known to influence levodopa metabolism in the intestinal tract, primarily through the action of tyrosine decarboxylase, an enzyme encoded by the tyrosine decarboxylase gene (tyrDC). However, the effect of the abundance of the tyrDC gene on levodopa pharmacokinetics remains unclear.MethodsThe aim of this study was to investigate this relationship in Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients undergoing a levodopa challenge test. Our study enrolled 12 PD patients with a good response to levodopa. Plasma levodopa pharmacokinetics were determined via liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry, while tyrDC gene abundance in faecal samples was assessed via metagenomic shotgun sequencing.ResultsA total of 12 PD patients (age: 58.00 ± 8.80 years) with an Hoehn and Yahr stage of 2.25 (2.0–3.0) and a disease duration of 8.46 ± 4.94 years were enrolled. After levodopa administration, the MDS-UPDRS-III score decreased 71.28%±17.09%. Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, and levodopa is the most effective medication for alleviating motor symptoms and improving the quality of life of PD patients [1]. However, the bioavailability of levodopa varies among patients, resulting in significant interindividual differences in responsiveness [2, 3]. In some patients, resistance to levodopa contributes to increased dosing frequency and a greater risk of side effects such as dyskinesia [4]. Thus, identifying biomarkers that predict levodopa responsiveness is crucial for personalized treatment strategies.

While factors such as disease duration, motor phenotype, and genetic polymorphisms partially account for the variability in response to levodopa, emerging evidence highlights the role of the gut microbiota in modulating its metabolism [5, 6]. Balskus and colleagues first identified that tyrosine decarboxylase (tyrDC), expressed in human gut microbes such as Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, and Proteobacteria, promotes levodopa decarboxylation to dopamine within the gastrointestinal tract, thereby restricting its availability to the brain [7, 8]. Notably, while both Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis) and Enterococcus faecium (E. faecium) retained decarboxylase activity, complete levodopa conversion was restricted to E. faecalis. Consequently, E. faecalis may be a principal microbial modulator of levodopa pharmacokinetics. Moreover, some research has shown that the abundance of the tyrDC gene in the faeces of patients with PD is positively correlated with daily levodopa dosage and serves as a biomarker of levodopa responsiveness [9, 10]. These good responders presented elevated plasma levodopa levels at peak clinical improvement combined with reduced tyrDC gene abundance. Nevertheless, the study design prioritized clinical outcome tracking over pharmacokinetic assessments, making it impossible to determine whether reduced tyrDC gene abundance directly improves drug bioavailability. Additionally, the focus on optimal responders may minimize confounding effects of other variables, such as genetic polymorphisms in dopaminergic drug metabolism pathways.

In this study, we investigated the correlation between the microbial tyrDC gene and PD patients who are responsive to levodopa treatment via both the levodopa challenge test and plasma levodopa pharmacokinetic analysis and provide a mechanistic basis for optimization of personalized therapy.

Methods

Participant recruitment and data collection

Between November 2020 and December 2021, a total of 12 patients diagnosed with PD were recruited for a cross-sectional study by the Movement Disorders Clinic at the Department of Neurology and Institute of Neurology, Affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. The mean age at diagnosis was 49.58 ± 12.52 years (range: 15–64). The diagnosis of PD was confirmed by two movement disorder specialists via the UK Parkinson Disease Society Brain Bank criteria. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) atypical or secondary parkinsonism; (2) intolerance to levodopa; (3) a history of deep brain stimulation and gastrointestinal surgery; (4) chronic illnesses (e.g., abnormal liver enzymes, renal dysfunction, inflammatory bowel disease, malignancy, endocrine diseases, haematologic diseases, or heart failure); and (5) the use of antibiotics or probiotics within 3 months prior to sample collection. Demographic information, including age and sex, was recorded for each participant. The clinical characteristics of PD patients were ascertained through face‒to-face interviews, including the Hoehn and Yahr stage (H&Y stage), Movement Disorder Society-Sponsored Revision of the Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) score, medication usage and duration of PD. The motor phenotype was divided into tremor dominant (TD), indeterminate, and postural instability/gait difficulty (PIGD), as described by Stebbins et al. [11]. The levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was calculated via the method reported in a previous study [12].

Levodopa challenge test

Levodopa Challenge Test was performed after a 72-hour cessation of dopaminergic agonist intake and a 12-hour withdrawal of levodopa [13]. Levodopa/carbidopa (250 mg/25 mg) was administered at 08:00 under fasting conditions and was followed 30 min later by administration of standardized steamed bread to minimize food-induced pharmacokinetic variability. Motor symptoms were assessed via the MDS-UPDRS-III in the “OFF” state, which was repeated after levodopa/carbidopa administration for 10 min, 20 min, 40 min, 60 min, 90 min, 120 min, 180 min, 240 min and 360 min. The therapeutic response to levodopa was measured by a reduction (%) in the MDS-UPDRS-III score from the “OFF” condition to the maximal responsiveness (“best ON” condition) [13]. Clinical parameters reflecting the effect of the levodopa challenge test were defined: the “ON” condition was defined as a 20% reduction in the baseline MDS-UPDRS-III score [14], and the time from the “OFF” to the “ON” condition was recorded as the “time to ON” (Ton).

Sample collection

Blood samples were collected under “OFF” conditions and at 10 min, 20 min, 40 min, 60 min, 90 min, 120 min, 180 min, 240 min and 360 min after levodopa/carbidopa administration. Blood samples were centrifuged to obtain plasma, which was stored at − 80 °C until analysis. All participants’ faecal samples were immediately frozen in professional stool DNA preservation solution (2 ml, Dongyuan Yikang Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing China) in containers and stored at − 80 °C.

Shotgun sequencing

Faecal samples were utilized for DNA extraction via the PowerSoil® DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio, San Diego, CA, USA). The extracted microbial DNA was processed to construct metagenome shotgun sequencing libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq600 platform at Nanjing Qingke Biotechnology Corporation, generating a comprehensive dataset for further analysis. Since previous studies identified the levodopa metabolism-associated tyrDC gene and submitted the corresponding protein coding sequences (Supplementary Table 1) [7], we searched for accession numbers in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The results were subsequently compared with our sequencing library, and we calculated the total tyrDC gene abundance. In addition, the tyrDC genes from E. faecalis (Accession NO. NZ_KB944666.1) and E. faecium (Accession NO. NZ_CP038996.1:351431–353308; NO. NZ_CP038996.1:292533–294368) in NCBI were compared with our library sequence (Supplementary Table 1), and we calculated the tyrDC gene abundance of E. faecalis.

Liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry analysis

Liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry was conducted to measure plasma levodopa concentrations via the ACQUITY UPLC I-Class/Xevo TQD IVD system (Waters, Milford, USA). A 50 µL volume of each plasma sample was analysed alongside an internal standard solution (20 µL, containing 100 ng·mL−1 L-DOPA-D3; China National Institute for the Control of Pharmaceutical and Biological Products, China). Protein precipitation was induced through the addition of methyl alcohol, and the mixture was homogenized on a vortex mixer for 5 min and subsequently centrifuged at 1.4 × 104 r·min−1 for 5 min. The supernatant was injected into an Agilent Pursuit PFP (2.0 × 150 mm, 3.0 μm) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL·min−1 at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and phase B (acetonitrile). Mass spectrometry analysis was performed in positive ionization mode using selective reaction monitoring. The optimized conditions used for analysis were a fragmentor voltage of 75 V and collision voltages of 29 V and 9 V. The data, including the area under the curve (AUC), maximum concentration (Cmax), and time to maximum concentration (Tmax), were recorded and analysed via the DAS 2.0 pharmacokinetic program.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed via SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data with a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± SEM, whereas data without a normal distribution are shown as the median (interquartile range). Comparisons between groups for quantitative variables were conducted via the independent t test for normally distributed data or the Mann‒Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed data, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared via Fisher’s exact test. Correlations between potential factors and the tyrDC gene were evaluated via Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses. The pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated via the Pharsight Phoenix 6.1 software NCA. The effect size was also computed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with PD and results of the levodopa challenge test

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are detailed in Table 1. A total of 12 PD patients (age: 58.00 ± 8.80 years; 6 male patients and 6 female patients) were evaluated. Patients with H&Y stages of 2.25 (2.0–3.0) and long disease durations (8.46 ± 4.94 years) were included in the analyses. Patients with longer disease durations have better short-term levodopa responses [15]. All patients received long-term dopaminergic therapy (levodopa and/or dopamine agonists) with consistently good efficacy. For the motor phenotype, 5 patients had TD, 3 patients had indeterminate TD, and 4 patients had PIGD. All the patients were receiving treatment with either levodopa/carbidopa alone or in combination with dopamine receptor agonists. The LEDD was 362.50 (309.38, 431.25) mg. As illustrated in Table 1, all patients underwent a levodopa challenge test and exhibited a positive response to levodopa (≥ 24% reduction in the MDS-UPDRS-III score) [14]. After levodopa administration, the MDS-UPDRS-III score decreased by 71.28%±17.09%, and the “time to ON” (Ton) score (10.00 [10.00, 10.00]) was reduced compared to the score reported in previous studies [14].

Relationship of gut bacterial tyrDC gene abundance with the clinical characteristics in PD patients

Faecal samples were collected from PD patients and analysed for the total abundance of the tyrDC gene (TTDC) [9.91E-8 (3.20E-8, 1.32E-6)], as depicted in Table 1. We explored whether tyrDC gene abundance was influenced by factors in our cohort, including the use of PD medications, duration of disease, relative abundance of E. faecalis, and relative abundance of E. faecium in PD patients. We found no significant associations between bacterial tyrDC gene abundance and levodopa/carbidopa treatment dose (r=−0.23, p = 0.27), dopamine agonist treatment dose (r=−0.34, p = 0.20), LEDD (r=−0.39, p = 0.19), or duration of disease (r=−0.04, p = 0.84) via Spearman correlation analyses (Table 2). Furthermore, tyrDC gene abundance was strongly related to the relative abundance of E. faecalis (r = 0.93, p < 0.0001) but not E. faecium (r = 0.15, p = 0.64) (Table 3).

Role of gut microbiota-derived tyrDC gene abundance in the pharmacokinetics of levodopa in the blood

To further clarify whether tyrDC gene abundance influences levodopa metabolism in PD patients who respond well to levodopa, we analyzed the relationships between tyrDC gene abundance and the pharmacokinetic parameters of levodopa. Our analysis revealed no significant correlation between the AUC (r = 0.11, p = 0.74), Cmax (r = 0.44, p = 0.15) or Tmax (r = 0.0, p = 0.87) of levodopa and tyrDC gene abundance (Table 3). Given the strong association between tyrDC gene abundance and the relative abundance of E. faecalis (Table 3), we conducted subgroup analysis to investigate the specific influence of the tyrDC gene from E. faecalis (ETDC) on the pharmacokinetics of levodopa in the blood. No significant differences in PD disease duration, MDS-UPDRS-III score, H&Y stage, LEDD, levodopa/carbidopa treatment dose, dopamine agonist treatment dose, motor phenotype, Ton, or MDS-UPDRS-III score were found between the ETDC group and the TyrDC gene from E. faecalis (NETDC) group (Table 4). Additionally, the plasma levodopa pharmacokinetics (including the AUC, Cmax, and Tmax) did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 4) or between the different motor phenotypes (Supplementary Table 2). Similarly, the MDS-UPDRS-III score was not significantly different between the motor phenotypes (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

We found no significant association between the abundance of the microbial tyrDC gene and plasma levodopa pharmacokinetics in PD patients who exhibited a good response to levodopa. These findings suggest that the influence on levodopa metabolism by intestinal bacteria harbouring this gene is likely minimal in this patient subgroup.

When coadministered with carbidopa, 40% of the oral levodopa is still metabolized within the gastrointestinal tract [16]. This observation underscores the potential influence of the gut microbiota on the absorption of levodopa. Preclinical investigations have indicated that bacterial strains expressing tyrDC can prematurely decarboxylate levodopa to dopamine in the small intestine prior to its absorption, thereby diminishing the bioavailability of levodopa [7, 8]. Importantly, this microbiota-dependent conversion remains unaffected by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC) inhibitors, such as carbidopa [5, 8]. Recent studies have shown a positive association between the relative abundance of the bacterial tyrDC gene in the stool samples of PD patients and the dosage of dopaminergic medication (entacapone, rasagiline, pramipexole, and levodopa) as well as the disease duration [10]. Notably, our study, which was restricted to levodopa and/or pramipexole users, revealed no such associations. This discrepancy might be attributed to differences in study design. Previous evidence that established a link between the tyrDC gene and medication effects was derived from 2-year longitudinal cohorts, whereas our cross-sectional observational approach lacks longitudinal dimensions, potentially limiting causal interpretations.

Recent in vitro studies have confirmed that microbial tyrDC can decarboxylate levodopa to dopamine, but whether a similar process occurs in vivo is less clear [7, 8]. Although prior investigations have demonstrated a correlation between plasma levodopa concentrations at peak clinical improvement phases and tyrDC gene abundance during levodopa response testing, these findings are insufficient to elucidate the dynamic relationship between the tyrDC gene and levodopa metabolism in vivo [9]. Accordingly, our study established a novel method to investigate the impact of the microbial tyrDC gene on levodopa metabolism in vivo. Specifically, we conducted a levodopa plasma pharmacokinetic analysis by testing the plasma levodopa concentration at ten distinct time points. Intriguingly, our study revealed no association between the presence of tyrDC-producing bacteria and levodopa plasma pharmacokinetics. Nevertheless, the abundance of the microbial tyrDC gene has been suggested as a predictive factor for levodopa responsiveness, especially in distinguishing between moderate responders and good responders [9]. Thus, we speculate that the role of the microbial tyrDC gene in PD patients with a good response to levodopa may be ignored, which is in line with the findings of Masahiro Naga et al. [17]. It is conceivable that tyrDC gene expression may not be sufficient to alter the pharmacokinetics of levodopa immediately during a levodopa challenge test. Nevertheless, an average effect size was observed for the differences in Cmax and Tmax between the ETDC and NETDC groups, as shown in Table 4. Thus, additional studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm our speculation.

Previous studies have revealed that levodopa is decarboxylated to dopamine by tyrDC, which is encoded by specific gut bacteria, such as E. faecalis, E. faecium and Lactobacillus brevis [7, 8]. Among these bacteria, E. faecalis completely metabolizes levodopa [7]. However, we observed no correlation between the presence of the tyrDC gene in E. faecalis and the plasma pharmacokinetics of levodopa. This discrepancy may be attributed to the transient presence of the drug in the intestinal tract during the levodopa challenge test, which might limit the impact of bacteria on levodopa metabolism. Motor phenotype also influences levodopa responsiveness. Bradykinesia and rigidity typically respond best, whereas tremor suppression is variable, ranging from significant relief to minimal improvement. In contrast, postural instability shows little to no response. However, in this study, the motor phenotype did not differ between the ETDC and NETDC groups and levodopa responses among the different subtypes also showed no differences. Moreover, we considered the influence of Helicobacter pylori and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) on levodopa absorption [18]. Previous studies have indicated that Helicobacter pylori-positive PD patients frequently require prolonged proton pump inhibitor treatment and exhibit impaired gut motility, ultimately reducing levodopa availability [19, 20]. In our study, PD patients did not suffer from stomach ulcers or receive a long-term proton pump inhibitor treatment. In addition, SIBO has also been associated with a suboptimal response to levodopa, resulting in worse motor function [21]. While SIBO eradication with antibiotics such as rifaximin may improve motor performance in PD patients [22], several studies have shown no changes in levodopa pharmacokinetics posteradication, suggesting a complex relationship that requires further investigation [18, 22]. In addition to microbial factors, genetic factors contribute to interindividual heterogeneity in levodopa responsiveness, including DOPA decarboxylase (encoded by DDC), dopamine transporters (encoded by SLC6A3), dopamine receptors (encoded by DRD2), monoamine oxidase (encoded by MAO-B), and catechol-O-methyl transferase (encoded by COMT) [23–26]. Thus, future studies should consider the potential influence of these multiple factors on peripheral levodopa metabolism.

Our study also has several limitations. Enrolling PD patients was challenging due to the requirement for plasma collection at ten distinct time points. Consequently, the small sample size limits the reliability of our findings, necessitating validation in multicentre studies. Furthermore, faecal samples collected from the colon may not accurately reflect the bacterial conditions within the small intestine, where levodopa decarboxylation primarily occurs. Although a previous small study reported no association between levodopa and the tyrDC gene in small intestinal samples, future studies using standardized sampling methods are warranted. Additionally, the presence of the tyrDC gene does not necessarily correlate with tyrDC enzyme activity. Therefore, further research is needed to establish robust links between gene presence and functional enzymatic activity.

Conclusion

Our study indicates that the effect of the intestinal microbiota on levodopa efficacy may be negligible, particularly in patients with a good response to levodopa treatment and the levodopa challenge test. However, larger cohort studies are still needed to validate this observation. Collectively, these findings refine our understanding of the role that the microbial tyrDC gene plays in levodopa metabolism in PD and provide new insight into levodopa therapy.
