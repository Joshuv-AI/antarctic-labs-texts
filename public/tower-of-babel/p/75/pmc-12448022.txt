Transferrin receptor 1-targeted polymersomes therapy for colorectal cancer
Authors: Ariana Pina, Elisa Mastrantuono, Marta Silva, Valentino Barbieri, José Muñoz-López, Giuseppe Battaglia, Luís Graça, Diana Matias
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most common cancers and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths. The high mortality associated with CRC is attributed mainly to difficulties in early detection and lack of effective targeted therapies. The Transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) is particularly attractive as a therapy target given its notable overexpression in tumor cells, particularly in CRC. This study explored the potential of a polymeric nanoparticle (PSomes)-based drug delivery system targeting TfR1 to improve the precision and efficacy of CRC treatment. For this study, we used two human CRC cell lines (HT-29, and HCT116), a healthy human intestinal epithelial cell line (hIECs), and a murine CRC cell line (MC38). We engineered PSomes composed of poly (ethylene glycol) (PEG) and poly (lactic acid) (PLA), functionalized with the T7 peptide to enhance their specificity for TfR1-expressing cells. Targeting efficiency of these PSomes was assessed across all cell lines by evaluating the cellular uptake using flow cytometry. Upon establishing the optimal formulation for these NPs for TfR1-targeting, we encapsulated doxorubicin (DOX) to assess their therapeutic potential. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that DOX loaded TfR1-targeted PSomes delivered DOX to CRC cells, leading to efficient induction of CRC cell death, reducing tumor growth and improving survival rates, compared to the control groups. These results highlight the promise of TfR1-targeted PSomes as a precise strategy for CRC therapy, offering enhanced treatment efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity. This novel approach could lead to more targeted and less harmful cancer treatments.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a global health challenge, ranking as the third most prevalent cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. CRC is highly heterogeneous within individual tumors and across patients, with factors such as tumor location and interactions with immune cells contributing to its complex pathogenesis [2,3]. Consequently, treatment outcomes are often unpredictable, posing a challenge for conventional therapies. Standard CRC treatment typically involves surgical resection followed by chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy [4,5]. However, effectiveness depends largely on early detection, which is only achieved in approximately 40 % of cases, and even when successful, recurrence rates are high, making chemotherapy a primary treatment option, yet it is often associated with suboptimal response rates and treatment resistance [4,6]. This resistance, along with the lack of specificity of chemotherapeutic agents, contributes to significant collateral damage to healthy tissues, with toxicity in critical organs including the central nervous system, heart, liver, and kidneys. Such side effects can exacerbate patient morbidity, reduce quality of life, and, in some cases, lead to secondary malignancies [4,7].

Doxorubicin (DOX) is among the most widely used chemotherapeutic agents, acting as topoisomerase II inhibitor effective against multiple malignancies, including but carcinomas, sarcomas, and breast cancers [8,9]. It is also recognized for its cost-effectiveness and broad-spectrum activity [8,9]. However, in CRC, the therapeutic efficacy of DOX is significantly limited due to several mechanisms of innate and acquired resistance, restricting its clinical utility [10].

Acquired resistance remains a major challenge in CRC treatment. A key mechanism relevant to this study is enhanced drug efflux, which prevents sufficient intracellular drug accumulation. P-glycoprotein (P-gp), encoded by the multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR1) gene, is one of the best-characterized transporters, and its expression is frequently upregulated in CRC and other cancers [11]. By actively exporting chemotherapeutic agents, P-gp lowers their intracellular concentrations and reducing drug efficacy [11,12]. Another important mechanism involves the dysregulation of apoptotic pathways, often mediated by aberrant expression of apoptosis-regulating proteins. For example, mutant p53, commonly overexpressed in CRC, including HT-29 and MC38 cell lines, can inhibit apoptosis by disrupting caspase-dependent signaling. This alteration has been linked to resistance against a wide range of anticancer agents, including conventional chemotherapeutics such as 5-fluorouracil and DOX [[13], [14], [15]]. In addition, the tumor microenvironment (TME) contributes significantly to therapy resistance. Hypoxic and acidic conditions, characteristic of CRC tumors, impair drug delivery and cellular uptake. Notably, tumor-associated acidosis (low extracellular pH) reduces the uptake of weakly basic drugs such as DOX, thereby further decreasing their cytotoxic potential [11,16].

In light of these limitations, novel strategies are needed to enhance the specificity and efficacy of CRC treatments. One effective strategy is to exploit the overexpression of receptors on tumor cells, which are often present at higher levels compared to healthy tissues, and to leverage nanotechnology for targeted drug delivery. A highly promising target in CRC is Transferrin Receptor 1 (TfR1), a type II transmembrane glycoprotein that plays a crucial role in cellular iron uptake. While TfR1 is typically expressed at low levels in healthy tissues, it is often overexpressed in cancer cells, including CRC [17]. This overexpression is strongly associated with increased cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, making TfR1 an attractive target for drug delivery systems, such as nanoparticles [17]. Among various polymer-based nanoparticles, specifically polymersomes (PSomes) stand out due to their ability to be surface-functionalized to target specific receptors, such as TfR1, without compromising their structural integrity, making them ideal for precise and efficient drug delivery [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]].

The incorporation of FDA-approved poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) and poly(lactic acid) (PLA) in PSomes enhances their biocompatibility and in vivo stability, further supporting their safety profile [18,19]. Additionally, this unique composition allows for size, shape, and membrane properties customization, optimizing drug loading and targeting efficiency for improved therapeutic outcomes [18,19]. Although TfR1 has been widely explored as a therapeutic target in various cancers, its application in PSomes-based therapies for CRC remains largely unexplored [20]. One of the key challenges in targeting TfR1 is the competition between targeting peptides and endogenous transferrin, which significantly reduces treatment efficacy [17,20]. Overcoming this limitation is crucial to unlocking the full therapeutic potential of TfR1-targeted PSome therapies in CRC.

In this study, we developed TfR1-targeted PEG-PLA PSomes functionalized with the T7 peptide (HAIYPRH) as a novel targeting strategy. The T7 peptide is a ligand known for its high specificity and affinity for TfR1, while also bypassing competition against endogenous transferrin [[21], [22], [23]]. These T7-functionalized PSomes were designed to encapsulate and deliver DOX to CRC cells efficiently. Although DOX is not a first-line treatment for CRC, it remains a potent chemotherapeutic agent across multiple cancer types with diverse mechanisms of action [10]. However, resistance mechanisms such as efflux pumps and systemic toxicity often limit its clinical application in CRC [11]. Through targeted delivery, our goal is to enhance DOX efficacy, minimize off-target effects, bypass resistance mechanisms, and ultimately provide a promising therapeutic alternative for CRC patients.

Materials and methods

Synthesis and purification of empty PSomes

The PEG45-PLA106 copolymers were purchased from Creative PEGWorks. The T7 conjugated copolymers (HAIYPRH-PEG20-PLA112) were synthesized and functionalized in-house. For polymer synthesis, N3-PEG-PLA was prepared using N3-dPEG24-OH (#PEG3770, Iris Biotech) as the initiator and polymerizing LA by using ring-opening polymerization with 1,8-Diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene (#139009, Sigma-Aldrich) as the catalyst. Afterwards, to synthesize T7 peptide (HAIYPRH)-PEG20-PLA112, copper(I)-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) click reactions were used, with {Propargylamine}T7 peptide (GenScript) and N3-PEG24-PLA112 reacting in the presence of copper(II) sulfate (#209198, Sigma-Aldrich) as the catalyst and sodium ascorbate (#A7631, Sigma-Aldrich) as the reducing agent. PEG-PLA PSomes with different % molar amounts of T7 peptide were obtained using the solvent displacement method (Supp. Table 1). To achieve this, PEG45-PLA106 polymer (20 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of dimethylformamide (DMF) and vortexed to ensure dissolution. Different formulations were obtained by varying the T7 conjugated copolymers (T7-PEG20-PLA112) ratio to the PEG45-PLA106. The solution was transferred to a syringe, and 600 μL were injected into a glass vial placed on a stirring plate at 500 rpm, containing a magnet and 1600 μL of MiliQ Water. The polymer solution was injected into the MiliQ water at a steady rate of 100 μL/min. After the injection, the solutions were transferred to dialysis membranes (#Z726176, Merck), put inside a beaker full of MiliQ water, and stirred for an hour. Then the MiliQ water was substituted by PBS and left to stir overnight. In these PSomes were also included a fluorophore: Cy7-PEG20-PLA112. After dialysis, samples were collected and transferred to 15 mL tubes and centrifuged at 1000 rcf for 10 min. These PSomes will be referred to as X%T7pep-PSomes from here onwards.

Characterization of PSomes by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity (PDI) were evaluated by DLS using a Zetasizer Ultra. 10 3.4.4 (Malvern Instruments) with a 10 mW He-Ne Laser at 632.8 nm at a scattering angle of 173°. The PSomes samples were prepared by diluting 50 μL of PSomes into 850 μL of MiliQ water and transferred into polystyrene disposable cuvettes at 25 °C. Data was processed using ZS XPLORER software (Malvern Instruments).

For TEM, Grids composed of 100 mesh formvar and carbon coat were glow discharged before the experiment, and samples were diluted in a 1:5 proportion in water and sonicated for 5 min. Grids were deposited on top of 5 μL of diluted sample for adsorption for 2 min and washed in distilled H2O 10 times. Posteriorly, the grids containing the sample were stained in 2 % Uranyl Acetate in dH2O for 2 min and left to dry on Whatman filter paper. Imaging was done using a Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN Transmission Electron Microscope from FEI, and pictures were acquired using an Olympus-SIS Veleta CCD Camera. The electron microscopy facility performed stainings and acquisition at the Gulbenkian Institute for Molecular Medicine (GIMM).

Quantification of PSomes

The concentration of PEG-PLA in each formulation was quantified using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (uHPLC) (Agilent 1260 Infinity II) with a Jupiter C18 column (Phenomenex). For PEG-PLA sample analysis, a gradient method was employed with 50 % (v/v) acetonitrile (Phase A) and 50 % (v/v) ultra-pure water (Phase B). The gradient progressively increased linearly from 50 % A to 100 % A over 20 min. This concentration was maintained for 25 min before returning to 50 % A over 1 min and holding for 6 min. The flow rate was set at a constant 1 mL/min. Before the UHPLC analysis, PSomes samples were diluted in acetonitrile to facilitate polymer disassembly. PSomes’ absorbance was detected at 220 nm using a UV–Vis detector. Absorbance curves were analyzed using Chromaleon Chromatography Data System (CDS) software.

Synthesis and purification of PSomes with encapsulated DOX

The same protocol was used to create PSomes with encapsulated DOX (#D2975000, Merck); however, before injecting the solution into the glass vial on the stirring plate, 333 μL of DOX at a concentration of 2 mg/mL was added. Furthermore, no fluorophores were added to these POS as DOX already presents fluorescence. PSomes were first purified using a dialysis membrane, followed by a crossflow filtration, and finally by size exclusion chromatography (SEC), to ensure the complete removal of free DOX from the Psomes solution, as described in literature [24,25]. A size exclusion column was used specifically for DOX encapsulating PSomes to separate them from the remaining free drug in the samples. The samples were run through a 53 ml glass liquid chromatography column containing Sepharose 4B (#4B200-500 ML, Sigma-Aldrich) as the stationary phase and PBS-endotoxin free (#TMS-012-A, Merck) as the eluent. After running through the column, PSomes were collected in a 96-well plate (around 200 μL per well) until PBS was clear and analyzed by DLS.

DOX loading efficacy and quantification in PSomes

To calculate the amount of encapsulated DOX in the PSomes, samples were diluted in a 1:5 ratio of acetonitrile to water and plated in a black flat-bottom 96-well plate. A standard curve of free DOX in acetonitrile/water was also prepared, starting at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, and subjected to serial dilutions in a 1:2 ratio. Fluorescence was measured using a TECAN Spark microplate reader, with an excitation wavelength of 470 nm and an emission wavelength of 560 nm. The concentration of DOX was determined by referencing the standard curve.

In vitro CRC and human intestinal epithelial cultures

Colon carcinoma cell lines from human (HCT116 (#CCL-247, ATCC), HT-29 (#HT29, Creative Biogene) and mouse (MC38) gently provided by (Centro de Inmunologia Molecular (CIM), Cuba) were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle (MEME) (#M4655, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10 % Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (#10500-064, Life Technologies) and 1 % Penicillin Streptomycin (P/S) (#15140-122, Life Technologies). Human Intestinal Epithelial cells (HIEC-6 cell line) (#CRL-3266, ATCC) were cultured in DMEM/F12 (#D0697-500 ML, Merck) supplemented with 5 % FBS, 1 % Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (#41400045, Thermo Scientific), 0.5 ng/mL of Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF) (#315-09-100 μg, PeproTech France), and 1 % P/S. All cell lines were maintained in an incubator at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, and constant humidity.

Evaluation of TfR1 levels in CRC and HIEC cells by immunofluorescence and flow cytometry

Monolayers of HCT116, HT-29, HIEC-6, and MC38 cells were cultured in ibidi μ-Slide 8-well plates (#80807, Enzifarma) at a density of 20,000 cells per well, with 200 μL of their respective media. After 48 h of incubation, the cells were washed with 1x PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) (#P6148-1 KG, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 solution (#93443, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. Afterwards, the cells were blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) (#071M1561V, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS with 0.1 % Tween-20 (#P1379, Sigma-Aldrich) (PBS-T) for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibody anti-TfR1 (#ab84036, Abcam, 1:200) diluted in 1 % BSA in PBS-T at 4 °C. Following the primary incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with the secondary antibody anti-rabbit IgG DyLight 488 (#406404, BioLegend, 1:200) diluted in PBS. Cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT) in a humidified, dark chamber. The cells were then washed with PBS, stained with DAPI at 0.1 μg/mL for 1 min, washed again with PBS, and visualized using the Zeiss LSM 880 with airyscan point scanning confocal microscope (LSM 880 with Airyscan, ZEISS Microscopy) at the bioimaging platform of the GIMM. Images obtained were analyzed and processed using Fiji/ImageJ version 1.53t software.

For flow cytometry analysis, 2 × 105 cells from each cell line were collected, washed twice with PBS +2 % FBS for 3 min at 1300 rpm, and incubated with LIVE/DEAD® Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit, for 405 nm excitation (#L34957, Life Technologies, 1:500) and APC anti-human CD71 Antibody (#334107, BioLegend, 1:75) for human cell lines and alternatively APC anti-mouse CD71 Antibody (#113820, BioLegend, 1:75) for the murine cell line, for 30 min at RT in the dark. The cells were washed with PBS and fixed for 30 min in the dark at 4 °C with 2 % PFA. After fixation, cells were washed twice with PBS +2 % FBS and resuspended in 200 μL of FACS buffer. The mixed cell suspensions were then analyzed by flow cytometry using the BD LSRFortessa cytometer with appropriate excitation and detection channels: LIVE/DEAD (405 nm excitation laser) and for CD71 (640 nm excitation laser). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo version 10.6.1.

PSomes uptake studies by flow cytometry

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 20 × 104 cells per well and left to grow for 48h. They were then incubated with empty PSomes labeled with Cy7 dyes at a density of 2.8 × 1010 PSomes per milliliter for 30 min and 2h timepoints. After the incubation, cells were washed with PBS and detached using trypsin. Cells were washed with PBS+2 % FBS for 3 min at 1300 rpm and incubated with LIVE/DEAD® Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit, for 405 nm excitation (#L34957, Life Technologies, 1:500) to discern live and dead cells. This staining lasted for 30 min at RT and in the dark. Afterwards, the cells were washed with 100 μL of PBS and centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 3 min at RT, and the supernatant was discarded. Next, the cells were fixed and incubated for 30 min in the dark and at 4 °C with PFA 2 %. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS containing 2 % FBS and resuspended in 200 μL of FACS buffer. Finally, the mixed cell suspensions were analyzed by flow cytometry using the BD LSRFortessa cytometer, at the flow cytometry platform of GIMM, with appropriate excitation and detection channels: 405 nm excitation laser with a 475 LP filter for LIVE/DEAD and 640 nm excitation laser with a 750 LP filter for PSomes detection. Flow cytometry analysis was done using FlowJo version 10.6.1.

XTT cell viability studies

MC38 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 104 cells per well. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh media containing either free DOX or DOX/0.25 %-T7pep PSomes, with increasing concentrations of DOX (0–60 μM). Cells were treated for 24 h, after which cell viability was assessed using the CyQUANT XTT Cell Viability assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #X12223), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Mitochondrial viability, used as an indirect measure of cell viability, was quantified at both time points. Cytotoxic effects were evaluated by measuring absorbance at 450 nm, with a secondary measurement at 660 nm to account for autofluorescence, using a TECAN Spark microplate reader.

Annexin V PI cell viability studies

CRC and hIEC cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing either free DOX or DOX-loaded polymeric nanoparticles functionalized with 0.25 % T7 peptide (DOX/0.25 %-T7pep PSomes), achieving a final DOX concentration of 4.6 μM. Control wells containing only culture medium were included. After 24 h of treatment, according to the manufacturer's instructions, cell viability and apoptosis were assessed using the eBioscience™ Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit APC (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #88-8007-72). Cytotoxic effects were evaluated by flow cytometry using the Cytek Aurora spectral cytometer. Excitation lasers were set to 640 nm for Annexin V (APC), 561 nm for propidium iodide (PI), and 488 nm for detection of DOX. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (version 10.6.1).

In vivo studies

C57BL/6J mice were bred, maintained, and used at the rodent facility of GIMM under pathogen-free conditions. Animal experimentation was approved by the ORBEA-GiMM (GiMM's Animal Welfare Body) and DGAV (the Portuguese National Authority for Animal Health) by portuguese regulations. To assess the anti-tumor efficacy of DOX-PSomes in vivo, 7.5 × 104 MC38 cells were injected into the flank of each mouse (N = 21). The animals were randomly assigned to four treatment groups: PBS (vehicle), free-DOX, DOX/0 % T7pep-PSomes, and DOX/0,25 %T7pep-PSomes. After 12 days post-tumor inoculation, mice received intravenous injections of DOX at a maximum dose of 2.5 mg/kg, administered every two days.

Tumor growth and size were monitored using a caliper (#PTR006777, PTROBOTICS). Tumor volume was obtained according to the following formula:Vt=π6xL×W×(L+W)/2

Animals were sacrificed when tumors reached 400 mm3 or when tumors became ulcerated. Euthanasia was performed via transcardiac perfusion, using 20 mL of PBS followed by 20 mL of 4 % formaldehyde (#F8775, Sigma-Aldrich). Blood was collected before the perfusion procedure. The following tissues were harvested: heart, liver, kidneys, spleen, lymph nodes, and tumor. They were washed in PBS, preserved in 4 % formaldehyde overnight, and kept in 30 % sucrose for subsequent studies on tissue cytotoxicity.

H&E and immunohistochemistry analysis of treated tissues

All fixed organs and tumors collected from the mice were sent to the histology department for processing. Tissues were processed using an overnight protocol in a Tissue HistoCore Pearl (Leica) and embedded in paraffin (#39602012, Leica). Sections of 3 μm thickness were obtained using a microtome (Minot Microtome Leica RM2245). Tumor and organ sections were stained with hematoxylin (#0506004E, bio-optica) and eosin (#110132-1L, Sigma-Aldrich) (H&E) for morphological examination. For immunohistochemistry, tumor sections were subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval, followed by inactivation of endogenous peroxidase using 3 % hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in methanol for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were then blocked with Dako Protein Block for 40 min at room temperature, incubated with Caspase 3 primary antibody (#9662S, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:300) (diluted in Dako diluent) for 1h, and washed. Following that, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody EnVisionTM Flex conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (α-rabbit) (#K4010, Dako). After further washing, the sections were incubated with diaminobenzidine (DAB) for 3 min, counterstained with Harris Hematoxylin for 10 min, and washed again. Both stainings were performed by the Histopathology Facility at GIMM. Finally, the sections were mounted, and images were captured using the Zeiss Axioscan 7 microscope at the bioimaging platform of GIMM.

Statistical analysis

Graphs and statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.4.2. Statistical significance is reported as follows: p ≤ 0.05 (∗), p ≤ 0.01 (∗∗), p ≤ 0.001(∗∗∗) and p ≤ 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗). A One-way ANOVA paired with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used to compare the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of cellular uptake of PSomes with different molar concentrations of T7 peptide between two CRC cell lines. Two-way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used to compare cellular uptake of PSomes with different molar concentrations of T7 peptide in each cell line, and for the cell viability assays. One-way ANOVA and was applied to compare linear regressions of tumor growth. The Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test was used to compare survival between the tested mouse groups.

Results

Design and characterization of empty T7pep-PSomes targeting TfR1

Considering our aim to use PSomes as drug delivery systems targeting specific receptors, such as TfR1, it is crucial to analyze their size [26]. Using the solvent displacement method, we first designed and prepared various formulations of PSomes targeting TfR1 by decorating their surface with different molar ratios of T7 peptide (HAIYPRH). DLS then characterized the PSomes to evaluate their size (nm), homogeneity, and quality. Light scattering analysis revealed that functionalization with different molar ratios of T7pep on the surface of PEG-PLA PSomes did not alter their size (Fig. 1A and B), with an average hydrodynamic diameter (Dh) of 88.8 nm (Table S1). TEM analysis of T7pep-PSomes confirmed the formation of spherical vesicles, with Dh diameter consistent with the DLS measurements (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1). All PSome formulations exhibited a narrow size distribution, as indicated by a PDI of less than 0.3, suggesting good homogeneity and an average zeta potential of −0.4 mV, indicating an inert surface charge. Uptake of empty PSomes in healthy and CRC cell lines

To characterize TfR1 expression in our cell models, we first assessed its cellular distribution through immunofluorescence (Fig. 2A, Fig. S2A), confirming the presence of TfR1 on the cell surface in all cell lines tested. To quantitatively evaluate TfR1 levels, we performed flow cytometry (Fig. S2B), revealing a significantly higher percentage of TfR1-positive cells in CRC cell lines compared to hIECs (p < 0.0001). Additionally, analysis of the MFI (Fig. 2B) demonstrated that the murine CRC cell line expressed notably higher levels of TfR1 than the human CRC and healthy cells (p < 0.0001). Therefore, to assess the specificity of our PSomes for targeting TfR1 in our cell models, we first optimized the incubation conditions to avoid saturation. HCT116 cells were incubated for 2 h with varying numbers of 0 % and 0.25 %-T7pep-PSomes, and cellular uptake was assessed using flow cytometry (Fig. 2C & Fig. S3B). 0.25 %-T7pep-PSomes were chosen as preliminary data (Fig. S3A), prior to this assay, suggested high uptake of this formulation, making it a suitable control. The results identified an optimal number of 2.8 × 1010 PSomes, which minimized nonspecific binding of pristine PSomes while still allowing the binding of 0.25 %-T7pep-PSomes. Next, we repeated the cellular uptake assay with all PSome formulations in HCT116, HT29 (Fig. 2D and E), left and right, respectively), MC38 and hIECs (Fig. S3C). In the HCT116 cells, only the 0.25 % and 1 %T7pep-PSomes exhibited nearly 100 % uptake at 2 h, with both formulations showing significant differences compared to 0 %T7pep-PSomes (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2D). A similar trend was observed in the HT29 cell line (Fig. 2E). In the MC38 and hIEC cell lines, after 2 h, the 0.25 % and 1 %T7pep-PSomes also displayed higher specific uptake compared to the other formulations (Fig. S3C). Notably, cellular uptake was lower in the healthy hIECs cells compared to the CRC cells (Fig. S3C). These findings highlight the differential expression of TfR1 across cell types and confirm the specificity of T7pep-PSomes for CRC cells, with significantly higher uptake in CRC cell lines compared to healthy hIECs. Effect of DOX-encapsulated TfR1-targeted PSomes on CRC cells in vitro

Based on the results of the cellular uptake assay, we selected the 0.25 %T7pep-PSomes for encapsulating DOX, as it exhibited the highest cellular uptake in CRC cells (Fig. 2D and E). The DOX-loaded PSomes were characterized by DLS to assess their size, homogeneity, and quality. Our data showed that the size of 0.25 %T7pep-PSomes with DOX was 74.83 nm (Fig. 3A and B & C and Table S2), indicating that encapsulation did not significantly affect the size compared to non-encapsulated PSomes (Fig. 3C). However, the DOX/0 %T7pep-PSomes were similar (78.24 nm) as the empty PSomes (Fig. 3C). TEM analysis confirmed the formation of spherical vesicles for DOX/T7pep-PSomes, with sizes consistent with the DLS measurements (Fig. 3D). Additionally, the encapsulation amount of DOX in 0 %T7pep-PSomes was 42.86 μg/mL, while in the 0.25 %T7pep-PSomes was 46.9 μg/mL, corresponding to encapsulation efficiencies of 2.14 and 2.35 %, respectively (Table S2). Following successful DOX encapsulation, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes in CRC cells and compared their efficacy to free DOX in vitro (Fig. 3E). MC38 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of either free DOX (yellow) or DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes (red) for 24 h. Dose-response curves were generated using a variable slope nonlinear regression model (Fig. 3E). Effect of DOX-encapsulated TfR1-targeted PSomes on CRC model

To test the effectiveness of the DOX-loaded T7pep-PSomes in a murine CRC model, we conducted in vivo experiments. MC38 cells were injected into C57BL/6J male mice, and after 12 days post-injection when tumor was already visible (on average, 22.1 mm3), we started the treatments. Mice were assigned to four treatment groups: PBS (control), free DOX, DOX/0 %T7pep-PSomes and DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes (Fig. 5A). Survival analysis (Fig. 5B) demonstrated that DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes significantly prolonged survival compared to both free DOX treatment group (p = 0.0082) and PBS control group (p = 0.0216). Unexpectedly, mice receiving free DOX exhibited the lowest survival rate among all groups. Tumor growth was monitored throughout the study, and linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate growth trends (Fig. 5C). Both DOX/T7-PSomes formulations reduced tumor progression, with DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes showing the most pronounced effect. This formulation significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to PBS (p = 0.0081 and free DOX (p = 0.0016), suggesting its enhanced therapeutic efficacy whereas animals treated with free DOX did not present differences from the PBS group (p = 0.3988). Weight analysis revealed no major changes during the experimental period (Fig. S5A). Discussion

CRC is a highly heterogenous malignancy characterized by significant innate and acquired resistance to treatments, making it challenging to cure and affecting millions of patients worldwide [1,2]. Standard therapeutic approaches, primarily based on chemotherapeutic agents, can extend a patient survival, but often induce severe side effects, including neuro-, cardio-, hepato- and nephrotoxicity due to their lack of specificity [4,7]. This highlights the urgent need for more targeted treatments that are not only effectively eliminate cancer cells but also minimize harm to healthy tissues, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and quality of life. One promising target for such therapies is TfR1, overexpressed in tumor cells, particularly in CRC [17]. This makes TfR1 an ideal target for testing NP-based therapies, such as PSomes which can work as drug delivery systems [18,19,27]. In our study, we demonstrated that TfR1-targeted PSomes delivered DOX intracellularly and induced cell death of tumor cells. We first generated different TfR1-targeting PSomes by functionalizing them with the T7 peptide, a highly specific peptide against TfR1 that does not compete with endogenous transferrin in the body [[21], [22], [23]]. We then confirmed TfR1 expression in our cell models before performing cellular uptake studies. Since at 0.1 mg/mL concentration of PSomes, we observed a saturation of the receptors in our system, prompted us to refine our approach by testing various PO concentrations in the HCT116 cell line at 2 h. This included both the pristine PSomes and the more specific 0.25 %T7pep-PSomes, which we inferred to be among the most specific formulations in the prior assay. Upon optimizing the assay conditions, we observed notable differences in uptake. Notably, cells treated with 0.25 % and 1 %T7pep-PSomes exhibited significantly higher uptake, indicating enhanced specificity. These results surpassed previously reported NP formulations used in CRC research, even at lower NP concentrations. For instance, both formulations achieved greater cellular uptake than the T7-liposomes described by Wang & Sun, even when tested at reduced concentrations [28]. Regarding healthy cells, there are, to our knowledge, no published studies on TfR1-mediated uptake. However, it is well-established that healthy cells express lower TfR1 levels than CRC cells, which likely explains the lower affinity and reduced PO uptake observed in these cells [17,29]. After identifying the optimal T7pep-PSome candidate with superior affinity and barrier-crossing properties, we successfully encapsulated DOX into both pristine and 0.25 %T7pep-PSomes, removing any leftover free DOX by employing several filtration techniques (i.e. dialysis, crossflow filtration and SEC) as recommended by literature [24,25]. The encapsulation efficiency in our system was lower than that reported in similar studies, likely due to the limited internal volume of the small-sized nanoparticles, which restricts drug-loading capacity. Additionally, the tendency of DOX to form large aggregates may have hindered its incorporation during PSome self-assembly. It is also possible that the system reached its maximum loading capacity under the given experimental conditions. Optimization of DOX loading is ongoing in future work [30].

The obtained IC50 values for free DOX were aligned with available literature which vary from 2 μM to 9 μM in the MC38 cell line [31,32]. Subsequent cell viability studies yielded promising results, particularly considering the relatively low DOX concentration used (4.6 μM/2.67 μg/mL). Wei et al. showed that PEG-poly (trimethylene carbonate-co-dithiolane trimethylene carbonate) PSomes functionalized with the TfR binding peptide (CGGGHKYLRW) and encapsulating DOX reduced cell viability by 45 % after 72 h at a concentration of 5 μg DOX/mL in HCT116 cells. In contrast, our T7pep-PSomes at 24h were already much more effective, resulting in only 20–30 % viable cells [33]. Similarly, when compared to T7-liposomes, our PSomes exhibited higher cytotoxic against CRC cell lines at 24 h, despite using different therapeutic agents. At equivalent therapeutic concentrations, our PSomes achieved lower cell viability than the liposomes, which maintained 75 % cellular viability [28]. Size discrepancies may be the underlying cause of these differences as our PSomes have an average hydrodynamic diameter of 90 nm whereas the T7-liposomes have a diameter of 130 nm. Furthermore, the difference in viabilities might be related to a difference in the mechanisms of action between DOX and other therapeutic agents, as well as the superior stability of PSomes compared to liposomes. Unlike liposomes, PSomes can evade the immune system and maintain structural integrity under physiological conditions [18,19,28,34]. In in vivo assays, DOX/0.25 % T7pep-PSomes effectively suppressed tumor growth, reducing the average tumor size to 300 mm3 by day 18 post-treatment, consistent with findings by Alibolandi et al. However, survival outcomes differed significantly. Mice treated with DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes in our study survived for 21 days after treatment initiation, while Alibolandi et al., reported survival extending up to 40 days [35]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in euthanasia criteria and targeting, as their study used PSomes to target the integrins (αvβ3). In our study, mice were euthanized when tumors reached 400 mm3 or ulcerated in the case of free DOX and PBS groups. In contrast, they euthanized mice only when tumors reached 2000 mm3 or impaired feeding [35]. Additionally, the use of different cell lines, sexes, and animal models, C26 cells (BALB/c, female) and MC38 cells (C57BL/6, male) in our study, may have contributed to the observed variations. [35]. Histopathological analysis revealed minimal necrosis in tumor from the PBS and free DOX-treated groups compared to the DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes group. The limited necrotic effect of free DOX likely reflects its strong dependence on both dose and administration frequency [[36], [37], [38]]. For instance, Wei et al., demonstrated that higher DOX doses (e.g. 4 mg/kg) induce necrosis in both tumor and healthy tissue [33]. Hosseini et al. used a 4 mg/kg dose administered more frequently than in our study, which resulted in noticeable weight loss and a slightly greater reduction in tumor growth [39]. Similarly, Momin et al. employed a 5 mg/kg DOX dose in their control group and observed damage to healthy tissues such as the heart, confirmed by H&E staining [40]. In contrast, our study used a lower DOX dose (2.5 mg/kg), which lies at the lower end of the range reported in the literature (2–40 mg/kg) [36]. This likely explains why necrosis was observed in the tumors but not in healthy tissues. The absence of histological evidence of organ damage following free DOX administration further supports the notion that higher dosages are required to elicit such adverse effects [33,36]. Importantly, the central necrosis observed in tumors treated with DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes appears to be treatment-induced, as these tumors, although slightly larger, did not present ulceration and displayed slower growth. By contrast, necrosis in PBS and free DOX-treated groups was likely driven by hypoxia associated with rapid tumor growth and surface ulceration. The enhanced necrotic effect of our formulation can be attributed to its optimized nanoparticle design, which improved tumor penetration and active uptake, thereby enabling deeper intratumoral delivery and stronger cytotoxic activity [[41], [42], [43]]. Despite the relatively low DOX concentration, the DOX/0.25 %T7pep-PSomes demonstrated significant anti-tumoral activity while sparing healthy tissues. These findings underscore the potential of T7pep-functionalized PSomes as a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC treatment, offering an effective and less toxic alternative to conventional chemotherapy.

Conclusions

Although TfR1 has been a key focus in cancer therapy research for decades, particularly in brain-related malignancies, its exploration in the context of CRC remains relatively limited. Our findings strongly reinforce the potential of TfR1 as a viable target for drug delivery systems in CRC, highlighting the promise of our TfR1-targeting PSomes in advancing more precise and effective therapeutic strategies. The observed variations in outcomes between studies can be attributed to differences in euthanasia criteria, cell lines, animal models, and DOX dosing regimens. In this study, the lower DOX concentration (2.5 mg/kg) likely explains the reduced necrotic effect on tumors and the absence of healthy tissue damage compared to higher doses reported in the literature. Despite the lower dose, DOX/0.25 % T7pep-PSomes demonstrated significant anti-tumoral activity while sparing healthy tissues. These findings highlight the potential of T7pep-functionalized PSomes as an effective and less toxic therapeutic strategy for CRC treatment, offering a safer alternative to conventional chemotherapy. Notably, the 0.25 % T7pep-PSomes exhibited remarkable specificity in targeting CRC cells through T7 peptide functionalization, further emphasizing its promise as an innovative treatment approach for this challenging malignancy.
