Comparison of total treatment duration with hybrid pendulum and buccal interradicular miniscrew supported maxillary molar distalization mechanics: a retrospective study
Authors: Berrak Çakmak, Selin Kale Varlık, Tuba Tortop
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe aim of this retrospective cohort study was to compare the total duration of Class II malocclusion treatment with hybrid pendulum vs. buccal interradicular miniscrew supported distalization mechanics followed by fixed appliances. Molar distalization duration and end-of-the-treatment changes were also compared.Materials and methodsThe sample of this retrospective cohort study consisted of clinical records, pre-treatment (T1) and post-treatment (T2) dental models, and panoramic and lateral cephalometric radiographs of 80 skeletal Class I patients with bilateral Class II molar relation. Molar distalization was achieved with a hybrid pendulum in Group P (15 males, 25 females; mean age: 15.19 ± 0.32 years) and with buccal interradicular miniscrew-supported compressed sentalloy coil springs in Group B (15 males, 25 females; initial mean age: 15.65 ± 0.32 years). Following molar distalization, all patients in both groups received comprehensive fixed orthodontic treatment. Molar distalization and total treatment durations and changes in Peer Assessment Rating scores and 14 cephalometric measurements were evaluated.ResultsStatistically significant molar distalization and distal tipping, mandibular incisor protrusion, and PAR reduction were achieved in both groups, with no difference between groups. There were no significant differences between groups for total treatment (24.58 ± 5.19 months in Group B and 24.81 ± 5.33 months in Group P) and molar distalization durations (7.01 ± 2.17 months in Group B and 7.03 ± 1.42 months in Group P) (p > 0.0167).ConclusionsThe total length of orthodontic treatment for Class II malocclusion which involved molar distalization by miniscrew supported appliances was not affected by whether the appliance was located palatally or buccally.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06714-8.

Introduction

Maxillary molar distalization is the most frequently used nonextraction treatment in the correction of Class II malocclusion to establish a Class I molar and canine relationship [1]. For molar distalization many appliances such as magnets [2], distal jets [3], pendulums [4], and nickel-titanium open coil springs [5] are used. However, these appliances’ common undesirable side effects are forward movement of incisors and premolars and the resulting anchorage loss [5]. Support can be provided from surrounding skeletal structures with the help of temporary anchorage devices (TADs) such as miniscrews to prevent anchorage loss [6].

Recently, many studies have reported the effectiveness of using buccally or palatally placed TADs for maxillary arch distalization [7–11]. TADs placed in interradicular spaces for this purpose are increasingly used due to their simple and less invasive placement procedures, but they are not always easy to place buccally in narrow interradicular spaces, especially in adolescents [8]. Moreover, reinsertion of miniscrews may be required when a large amount of distalization is required [12, 13]. Alternatively, miniscrew placement in the palatal region is recommended due to sufficient high-quality bone [14] and not preventing tooth movement [15] during distalization.

One of the most important factors affecting success in orthodontic clinical practice is the duration of treatment. According to the American Board of Orthodontics (ABO) standards, the average duration of single-phase orthodontic treatment is 24.9 months [16]. Treatments that last longer than normal can reduce patients’ quality of life and cause discomfort. Long-term treatment has been associated with worse or unacceptable occlusal results. In addition, longer than expected treatment duration increases the risk of root resorption, white lesions, caries and gingivitis [17]. Therefore, longer than expected treatment duration is seen as one of the main reasons for patient dissatisfaction.

Although many studies [1, 6–9] have evaluated the treatment efficacy of buccally or palatally placed TADs during distalization, the number of studies on treatment duration is quite limited. Moreover, studies mostly compare the treatment duration of distalization mechanics using miniscrews and other distalization appliances [18, 19]. A meta-analysis by Bellini-Pereira et al. [20] reported the mean treatment duration for non-compliance intraoral distalization devices as 8.34 months, but miniscrew anchors were not evaluated. In another systematic review conducted by Ragris et al. [8], while the effectiveness of the palatal and buccal-positioned miniscrews was investigated, the effect of placement localizations on treatment duration was not compared.

This retrospective cohort study aimed to compare the total treatment duration of two groups of patients whose treatment involved molar distalization either with hybrid pendulum appliance or buccal positioned mechanics composed of stainless-steel sections with sentalloy coil springs and inter radicular mini-screws. The primary outcome was the difference between the total treatment duration of the two groups. The secondary outcome was the comparison of distalization duration. The null hypothesis was that there would not be a significant difference between the two groups for total treatment time.

Materials and methods

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.0.10 (Franz Faul, Universitat Kiel, Kiel, Germany). for t-test. For an effect size of 0.8, to achieve a power of 90% at a significance level of 5%, a minimum of 34 cases for each group was required.

The sample included clinical patient records, pretreatment (T1) and post-treatment (T2) dental models, as well as panoramic and lateral cephalometric radiographs of 80 patients who completed treatment between January 2013 and February 2020. These patients were non-randomly selected from the treated patient files of the Orthodontic Department at Gazi University Faculty of Dentistry. Patient selection criteria were as follows: 1)No prior orthodontic treatment 2) Skeletal Class I, bilateral half cusp or ¼ cusp Class II molar relationship with mild crowding (< 4 mm) in mandibular arch 3) permanent dentition including second molars 4)Nonextraction treatment with 0.018-in slot Roth prescription conventional brackets including molar distalization either with hybrid pendulum appliance or open coil springs with buccally placed inter radicular mini-screws for anchorage 5) incisor retraction with closing loop mechanics 6) Third molars (if present) extracted or surgically removed before the onset of distalization 7) Acceptable occlusal results at T2 defined by maximum anterior contact point displacement of 1 mm, good posterior interdigitation with no crossbite or open bite, 2 to 3 mm overbite and overjet.

For patients who were treated by more than one resident, en masse retraction or segmental mechanics were used, had transversal discrepancy, poor quality records, notes on her/his clinical records about being non-compliant, having multiple missed appointments, failed mini-screws, appliance breakage, and poor oral hygiene were excluded from the study.

In 40 patients who comprised Group P, molar distalization was achieved by a hybrid pendulum (Fig. 1). The hybrid pendulum consisted of two paramedian mini-screws (Ancor Orthodontics, Ankara, Turkey; length: 10 mm; diameter: 2 mm), occlusal rests on first premolars, two 0.032 inch TMA springs, and an acrylic button. Springs were activated 90 degrees (bent parallel to the palatal midline) to produce a force of approximately 230 g per side. The acrylic button was cemented with cold-curing, methyl methacrylate-free acrylic resin (Ufi Gel hard; Voco GmbH, Cuxhaven, Germany), and springs were inserted into the lingual sheats on the first molar bands. Springs were reactivated intraorally if needed. When the Class I molar relationship was achieved, TMA springs were deactivated intraorally, and the appliance was left passively as a Nance button.

Miniscrew-supported pendulum application progress in Group P

In the second group Group B, which consisted of 40 patients, first molars were banded and a mini screw (Ancor Orthodontics, Ankara, Turkey; length: 8 mm; diameter: 1.6 mm) was placed in the interradicular space between the maxillary canines and first premolars. A system consisting of a sectional 0.017 × 0.025 SS wire with a distal step extending between the buccal tube and the mini screw and a compressed sentalloy coil exerting 250 gr was used for distalization (Fig. 2). Force magnitude was monitored at 4-week intervals, and coil springs were reactivated if necessary. When Class II molar relation was corrected to Class I, coil springs were removed, but the SS segments were kept in place to stabilize molars.

Application of miniscrew-assisted distalization mechanics in Group B

In both groups, leveling and aligning of the mandibular arch was initiated concomitantly with molar distalization. In the maxillary arch, brackets were placed after the cessation of distalization and stabilization of the molars. Second premolars were not engaged in the archwire for three to four months to allow their spontaneous distal drift. When leveling and aligning were achieved, maxillary premolars and canines were sequentially retracted using elastomeric chains (Ormco, Glendora, Calif) and NiTi coil springs (ORTHO organizers, ABD). In group P, the Nance button and palatal TADs were removed before incisor retraction, and all posterior teeth were ligated for anchorage control. In group B, before the retraction of the first premolars, mini-screws were relocated to the mesial of the first molars (Fig. 2); sectional 0.017 × 0.025 ss wires extending from the molar tubes to the TADs were used for anchorage control. In both groups, continuous archwires with T loops were used for incisor retraction. During this stage, Class II elastics were used to enhance anchorage. Fixed appliance therapy was finalized with 0.017 × 0.022 in TMA or SS ideal archwires.

Patients’ gender and age at T1, duration of distalization and total treatment, duration of class II elastic use, and information about the treatment mechanics were determined from the clinical records. All cephalograms were hand-traced. A horizontal reference (TW) line (line passed through tuberculum sella and wings points) and a vertical reference (Tver) line (perpendicular lowered to the TW line from the tuberculum sella point) were constructed. A total of 14 measurements were evaluated (Fig. 3).

For the calculations of the Peer Assessment Rating (PAR) Index, standardized orthodontic dental models made from orthodontic plaster and mounted on bases with hinges were used. Wax bite registrations were utilized to ensure maximum intercuspation. PAR Index on T1 and T2 dental models with United Kingdom weighting were calculated [21]. The improvement in malocclusion was calculated as the difference between T1 PAR and T2 PAR.

To exclude inter examiner differences, all PAR calculations and cephalometric measurements were performed by one investigator (BÇ) who was blinded for the distalization mechanics throughout the evaluation process.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The normal distribution of data was evaluated by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics for measurements were expressed as mean ± SD. A comparison of pretreatment and post-treatment measurements within groups was assessed using a paired t-test with Bonferroni correction. The significance level was p < 0.025. The Chi-square test was used to compare gender distribution. For intergroup comparison of treatment changes (T2-T1), the Student t-test with Bonferroni correction was used to reduce the probability of type I error and p < 0.0167 was considered statistically significant.

Three weeks after the initial measurements, the same examiner (BÇ) remeasured dental models and lateral cephalograms of 40 randomly selected patients. To assess intra-examiner reliability, intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using a single-measurement, absolute-agreement, two-way mixed-effects model.

Intraclass correlation coefficients of the variables varied from 0.90 to 0.98 with 95% confidence interval indicating excellent intra-rater agreement.

Comparisons of treatment changes (T2-T1) and treatment durations between the two groups are shown in Table 3. No significant differences between groups were found for total treatment, distalization, and Class II elastic use durations. Total treatment lasted 24.58 ± 5.19 months in Group B and 24.81 ± 5.33 months in Group P. Molar distalization was achieved in 7.01 ± 2.17 months in Group B, and 7.03 ± 1.42 months in Group P. Mean duration of Class II elastic use was 4.93 ± 2.20 months in group B and 4.42 ± 2.31 months in Group P. Increases observed in SNB (0.18 ± 0.43 in Group B, 0.66 ± 0.95 in Group P), SNGo/Gn (0.55 ± 1.04° in Group B, 0.66 ± 1.04° in Group P), mandibular incisor proclination (2.17 ± 2.96° in Group B, 0.66 ± 1.12 in group P) and protrusion (0.73 ± 0.91 in Group B, 0.82 ± 1.37 in Group P) were similar in both groups. No significant differences between the groups were found for first and second molar distalization and distal tipping. In Group B, the first and second molars were distalized 2.38 ± 1.31 mm and 2.18 ± 1.02 mm and tipped 1.72 ± 4.56 and 1.45 ± 4.24 degrees, respectively. The first and second molars were distalized 2.61 ± 1.55 mm and 2.66 ± 1.61 mm and distally tipped 0.86 ± 10.16 and 0.17 ± 11.03 degrees in Group P. Overjet and overbite reduction was 2.23 ± 1.67 mm and 1.57 ± 1.30 mm in Group B, 3.04 ± 2.16 mm, and 1.28 ± 1.77 in Group P. Improvement in the PAR score was 18.75 ± 5.33 in Group B and 19.90 ± 5.74 in Group P. Treatment-related changes in these measurements were similar in both groups. T2-T1 changes in SNA, 1-NA (mm), and 1-NA (°) were significantly different between the two groups (p = 0.003, p = 0.005 and p = 0.009 respectively). The increase in SNA in Group P (0.26 ± 0.78°) was significantly higher than the change in Group B (0.00 ± 0.40°). The position of maxillary incisors slightly changed in Group B (0.45 ± 5.08°, 0.1 ± 1.56 mm). On the other hand, significant retrusion was observed in Group P (−2.77 ± 5.67°, −1.00 ± 1.84 mm).

Discussion

Many studies have examined the effects of distalization appliances with skeletal anchorage for correcting Class II molar relation and reported on the advantages of different skeletal anchorage locations and/or appliance designs [1, 3, 7–13, 19]. Still, to date, no studies have compared the effect of appliance location on total treatment duration. In this study, we compared the orthodontic treatment duration of patients with Class II malocclusion whose treatment involved bilateral molar distalization either with a hybrid pendulum appliance (Group P) or a buccally placed distalization mechanics consisting of sentalloy coil springs applied via SS segments and buccal inter radicular miniscrews for anchorage (Group B).

In former studies, several factors were found to be associated with longer treatment durations. To minimize the impacts of some of these factors and compare the effect of distalization mechanics on treatment time as exclusively as possible, patients who were treated by more than one orthodontist, who had several missed appointments, frequent appliance breakage, poor oral hygiene or maxillary constriction were not included in the study [22]. Moreover, patients with full cusp Class II molar relation were also excluded as miniscrews would need to be removed and reinserted for the correction of full cusp Class II relationship. Although miniscrews have high survival rates, ranging from 81 to 93%, with palatal miniscrews demonstrating higher rates than intraradicular miniscrews, failure of miniscrews during orthodontic treatment can jeopardize the initial treatment plan [6, 23–25].If a miniscrew fails during active treatment, the recommended course of action is to either immediately replace it in adjacent interradicular space or to remove the failed miniscrew, suspend active orthodontic treatment for 4 to 6 weeks to allow the site to heal and place a new miniscrew at the same location [26, 27]. These options necessitate a change in the original treatment mechanics and lead to an extension of the treatment duration. Therefore, in an effort to eliminate this predictable and confounding factor, patients with failed miniscrews were excluded.

Regarding initial age, sex, the severity of Class II malocclusion, Par index values, and vertical and sagittal skeletal patterns, the groups were compatible at T1 (Table 1). The only pretreatment difference between the two groups was that in Group P, maxillary incisors were labially tipped and protruded (Table 1). Since retrusive rather than protrusive incisors were reported to affect treatment duration, this difference was assumed not to affect the study’s primary outcome [28]. However, due to this difference, maxillary incisors were significantly retracted in Group P during treatment and in contrast, their position did not change significantly at T2 in Group B (Table 2). Consequently, this resulted in significantly different treatment-related changes in U1-NA(mm) and U1-NA(°) measurements between the two groups (Table 3).

In this study, Class I molar relationship was achieved in a mean duration of 7 months in both groups. In a meta-analysis by Costa Grec et al. [29], distalization time ranged from 5.0 to 7.8 months when skeletal anchorage was used. In a systematic review on maxillary molar distalization with mini-screw supported appliances, regardless of the appliance and mini-screw location, the duration for distalization varied from 4.6 months to 11.27 months [29]. In another meta-analysis, the mean distalization duration with non-compliance intraoral distalizing appliances was estimated as 8.2 with buccal intra-radicular miniscrews and 5.3 months with palatal miniscrews [11]. Overall, the mean duration of molar distalization in the present study was comparable to these studies. When mini screw-supported pendulum appliance group was considered separately, similar distalization durations were reported in previous studies [30–33]. Buccal interradicular miniscres were used mostly for total arc distalization, and no other studies have used distalization mechanics similar to the one in Group B. Therefore, based on the conclusion of a meta-analysis stating that distalization duration might not be influenced by the type of anchorage used, we discussed the distalization duration of group B in comparison with conventional distalization appliances with compressed coil springs on the buccal side [20]. Seven months of distalization time in this study was longer than 9 to 10 months reported for Jones Jig in several studies [29–36]. And longer than 4.1 and 4.4 months reported by Papadopoulos et al. [37] and Mavropoulas et al. [38], respectively, for modified jones-jig appliance. Over-correction to super Class I in previous studies, differences in the severity of Class II molar relation, eruption stages or presences of second or third molars, or patient-related factors might have contributed to the differences in the reported distalization periods.

In this study, total treatment lasted an average of 25 months in both groups, and a similar reduction in PAR scores was achieved in this period. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in total treatment duration. Therefore, the null hypothesis that there would not be a significant difference between the two groups for total treatment time was not rejected. A systematic review reported a significantly shorter duration of 19.9 months for comprehensive orthodontic treatment [39]. In most studies included in the review, various factors that might have contributed to differences in treatment duration, such as information about the classification, features, and severity of malocclusion, treatment protocol, and finishing criteria, were not provided [40, 41]. Additionally, based on the results of previous studies in which Class II malocclusion was associated with prolonged treatment, the longer treatment time in the present study could be best explained by the fact that all patients had Class II molar relation [42–44].In previous studies on Class II treatment involving non-compliance intraoral distalization appliances, the total treatment time was longer (ranging from 27 to 31 months) than the present study [45–47]. This could be explained by the fact that skeletal anchorage reduced some of the side effects of conventional distalization mechanics and allowed spontaneous distalization of premolars shortening the total treatment time [29].

At the end of the treatment, statistically non-significant differences were observed between the two groups in the amount of first and second molar distal movement and tipping. The mean first molar distalization of 2.38 mm in Group B was similar to the 2.75 mm reported for appliances with buccal inter-radicular TADs in a meta-analysis by Bayome et al. [11] but less than the value in another meta-analysis by Ceratti et al. [48]. Distalization of 2.61 mm in Group P was less than the values reported for palatal TADs (4.07 mm and 3.74 mm, respectively) in Bayome et al.’s [11] and Ceratti et al.’s [48] studies. Distal tipping of molars observed in the present study was remarkably low (Group B:1.72°, Group P: 0.86°) compared to the values previously reported for distalization appliances with skeletal anchorage [1, 11, 22]. Although these differences between the studies could be explained by several factors, such as the variations between the severity of the initial Class II molar relationship and the choice of cephalometric landmarks [49], the presence or eruption stages of second or third molars, one critical factor that should not be overlooked is that the present study evaluated end-of-treatment measurements. Consequently, distal tipping of molars, which added to distalization values in the abovementioned studies, was considerably corrected during comprehensive treatment in this study, leading to seemingly less distal movement and tipping. A similar finding was reported by Caprioglio et al. [50]. In earlier studies, palatal acting distal force was associated with less distal tipping. In another study, no significant difference was found between appliances when compared by the position of TADs; on the other hand, non-rigid appliances such as pendulum were claimed to cause more tipping [46]. In the present study, whether or not different molar tipping had occurred in two groups after distalization was not evaluated, but at the end of the treatment, no difference was found between groups. Therefore, even if one of the appliances had caused more tipping, this did not affect the total treatment duration.

Limitations and strengths

One limitation of this study was that the possible effect of growth changes on treatment was not evaluated. The mean age of patients at T1 was approximately 15. Although onset, duration, and magnitude of growth cannot be accurately foreseen, clinically insignificant changes in SNB and SN-Go/Gn angels indicated relatively less growth, as anticipated at this age [51]. Additionally, T1-T2 changes in these measurements were similar in both groups. SNA did not change in Group B, whereas a statistically significant but clinically subtle increase was observed in Group P. This increase was most likely due to the retroclination of maxillary incisors [52].

In order to minimize the confounding factors that might affect the treatment duration and to ensure that the groups were similar in baseline factors, this study used strict inclusion criteria. This might have limited the generalizability of the results and could be considered as another limitation.

Because this was a retrospective study, patients were not randomly selected or randomly assigned to groups. The lack of randomization can result in confounding factors and selection bias. In this study, it was impossible to determine why one of the two distalization methods was initially preferred. While there might have been a reason for choosing one method over the other, it was not feasible to identify that reason in hindsight. However, the patient characteristics were mostly balanced at T1, which may have helped mitigate selection bias to some extent.

The Bonferroni correction was used to lower the probability of a Type I error (false positive), but this also raised the risk of a Type II error (the probability of not rejecting the null hypothesis; false negative)) meaning that, this study might not have had sufficient statistical power to detect an effect of a certain size This limitation is particularly significant, especially given that the null hypothesis was not rejected in the current study.

Conclusion

Both distalization mechanics were equally effective in achieving a Class I molar relationship and the amount and duration of molar distalization was similar in the 2 groups.

Total orthodontic treatment lasted an average of 25 months in both groups, and a similar reduction in PAR scores was achieved in this period.

There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in total treatment duration. Therefore, the null hypothesis was not rejected.

Within the limitations of the present study, we can conclude that when choosing between distalization mechanics, treatment duration should not be the primary concern. Instead, factors such as patients’ as alveolar bone quality, age, root proximity, and the type of tooth movement required to correct the malocclusion should be taken into account.
