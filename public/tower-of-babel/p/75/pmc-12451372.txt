Advanced bifunctional nanotherapeutics display multi-tiered defense against Magnaporthe oryzae through targeted fungal inhibition and rice immunity enhancement
Authors: Mohammad Shafiqul Islam, Muhammad Noman, Zhen Zhang, Temoor Ahmed, Yingying Cai, Jing Wang, Md Arshad Ali, Rahila Hafeez, Haiping Qiu, Zhongna Hao, Rongyao Chai, Munazza Ijaz, Yanli Wang, Bin Li, Jiaoyu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice blast disease, caused by Magnaporthe oryzae (Mo), severely threatens global rice production. In this study, biogenic copper nanoparticles (bio-CuNPs) were synthesized extracellularly using Bacillus amyloliquefaciens Q1 and characterized for antifungal activity and plant defense induction. Bio-CuNPs (16–62 nm, average 37 nm) exhibited potent antifungal effects by significantly inhibiting Mo mycelial growth, conidial germination, and appressorium formation in a dose-dependent manner. Microscopic observations revealed that bio-CuNPs disrupted Mo hyphal integrity, caused intracellular leakage, and induced DNA damage. Transcriptomic profiling identified key regulatory genes in Mo upon CuNP exposure, with key disruptions in cell wall biosynthesis (MoCHS-A, MoCHS-B, MoCHS-C, and MoCHS-D) and membrane transport pathways (MoMFS-1 MoMFS-2, and MoMSC-2). Deletion mutants for these genes demonstrated heightened sensitivity to bio-CuNPs, indicating that these genes are critical for helping Mo to withstand the antifungal effects of bio-CuNPs. However, bio-CuNPs disrupted their functions in Mo, confirming these genes as one of the molecular targets to suppress Mo growth and virulence. In planta assays revealed that bio-CuNP foliar application reduced disease severity, improved plant growth, and activated antioxidant enzymes, while suppressing oxidative stressors (i.e., super oxide radicle and hygrogen peroxide). Metabolomic analysis revealed significant alterations in defense-related pathways, including phenylpropanoid and amino acid metabolism. Additionally, CuNPs enhanced salicylic acid and methyl jasmonate levels, which subsequently upregulated defense gene expression. Cytotoxicity assays revealed that bio-CuNPs were non-toxic to AML12 cells at effective concentrations, highlighting their potential as an eco-friendly strategy for sustainable rice blast management.Keywords: Bio-CuNPs, M. oryzae, Metabolome, Rice blast disease, Rice immunity, Transcriptome

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) serves as a staple food for approximately 50 % of the global population, particularly in Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, and South America. Globally, 480 million metric tons of rice are produced annually, with China and India accounting for ∼50 % of the global production [1]. During recent years, rice production is threatened by fungal diseases, especially rice blast disease caused by a filamentous ascomycete fungus, Magnaporthe oryzae (Mo) [2]. Rice blast disease causes an estimated 40–50 % yield loss in global rice production under severe infection conditions [3]. Mo infects rice by germinating spores on the leaf surface, sensing host signals to form appressoria, and acquiring nutrients through invasive growth, ultimately producing characteristic leaf lesions and posing a significant threat to global rice production [4].

Traditional rice blast management relies on chemical fungicides, such as tricyclazole and hexaconazole, which can reduce disease severity by 8.15 % and 10.23 % [5]. However, the conventional methods and strategies utilized to manage Mo infection in rice have become inefficient due to their off-target effects and eco-safety concerns [6]. Integrating beneficial microbes with chemical fungicides has proven effective for managing crop diseases, including rice blast. For example, Bacillus cereus OG2L and Bacillus subtilis OG2A (2 g/L), when combined with Nativo 75 WG (trifloxystrobin + tebuconazole; 0.5 g/L), reduced rice blast incidence by 50 %. This combined treatment significantly improved rice yield compared with both individual treatments and the untreated controls [7]. Similarly, the combined application of Trichoderma harzianum, Trichoderma viride, and fungicides (tricyclazole and tebuconazole 50 % + trifloxystrobin 25 %) significantly reduced rice blast severity by 83.09 % and 77.37 % during Kharif 2018 and 2019, respectively, under field conditions [8]. However, the low efficacy, poor survival, and time-consuming nature of biocontrol agents under field conditions limit their large-scale application [9].

Copper (Cu) has long been used in plant protection due to its antimicrobial properties [10]. Cu oxychloride, the active ingredient in Kocide® 3000, is an effective fungicide against several fungal diseases, including potato late blight [11]. However, like other agrochemicals, Cu-based pesticides have limitations, including ecotoxicity, persistence, biocompatibility concerns, and off-target effects [12]. Recent studies indicate that chemically-synthesized Cu nanoparticles (NPs) offer a sustainable and eco-friendly alternative to conventional Cu-based pesticides for pathogen management [13]. A recent study demonstrated that CuNPs (65 μg/mL) reduced bacterial blight in rice by 23.01 % compared to untreated controls [14]. Similarly, Cu–Cu2O NPs in an alginate complex (17.8 mg/L) exhibited significant antifungal activity against Candida albicans, inhibiting its growth and reproduction [15]. Moreover, Cu–Cu2O NPs in an alginate complex reduced rice blast severity up to 61 % compared with untreated controls [16]. Similarly, Cu-sulfide NPs (50 mg/L) inhibited the growth of Gibberella fujikuroi, the causative agent of bakanae disease in rice, by 35.7 % [13]. While these findings highlight the potential of Cu-based NPs for controlling rice pathogens, chemically-synthesized CuNPs have also been reported to cause phytotoxic effects on the morpho-physiological traits of several plant species, including Glycine max [17], Brassica rapa [18], Cucurbita pepo [19], Mentha spicata [20], and Coriandum sativum [21]. Cu-based NPs show promise for disease management but require careful consideration of potential phytotoxicity.

In recent years, microbe-mediated NP synthesis, particularly using bacteria, has gained attention for its higher biocompatibility, lower toxicity, and favorable properties such as smaller size and higher surface area-to-volume ratio compared with chemically-synthesized NPs [22]. For example, biogenic (bio)-CuNPs (100 μg/mL) improved watermelon (Citrullus lanatus L.) growth by 34 % by enhancing Cu distribution, antioxidative activity, and photosystem II efficiency, thereby increasing resilience against the bacterial fruit blotch pathogen Acidovorax citrulli [23]. Although extensive literature documents the disease-controlling and pathogen-inhibiting potential of biogenic NPs, the precise molecular mechanisms by which they enhance plant defense and affect pathogen toxicity remain unclear.

In this study, we synthesized antimicrobial and phytobeneficial CuNPs using a well-established antagonistic bacterial strain, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens strain Q1 [24]. Further, we explored targeted suppression mechanisms of bio-CuNPs against the rice blast fungus Mo using transcriptomic and metabolomic approaches. Overall, our study results proposed CuNP-based management of rice blast disease as a potent and sustainable method in rice fields, ensuring increased crop production and food security.

Materials and methods

Biosynthesis and characterization of bio-CuNPs

Bio-CuNPs are synthesized by the extracellular approach utilizing the culture enrichment procedure as previously described [23]. Briefly, Q1 bacterial strain was cultivated in an Erlenmeyer flask containing 100 mL of nutrient broth medium. The medium cultured was at a temperature of 28 ± 2 °C and a shaking speed of 200 rpm in dark conditions in an incubator for 48 h. To biosynthesize CuNPs, an equivalent amount of copper chloride (CuCl2.2H2O; 5 mM) was added as a precursor salt to the supernatant culture of Q1 strain. The reaction mixture was incubated repeatedly at 28 ± 2 °C and 200 rpm for 72 h. Formation of CuNPs was indicated by a color change from light blue to dark blue. Afterwards, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min, and the CuNP pellet was washed several times with deionized water to remove residues from the media or bacterial culture. The purified bio-CuNPs were vacuum-dried for 6–8 h and stored in a tightly sealed container until further use. Supernatant without CuCl2·2H2O and media without bacterial supernatant were used as controls to assess and standardize potential contaminants in the recovered bio-CuNPs.

The biosynthesis of CuNPs was confirmed using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Model 2600, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) over a wavelength range of 200–700 nm. Surface characteristics, including biomolecule capping agents, were analyzed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Thermo-Fisher Scientific NICOLET iS50FT-IR, Waltham, MA, USA) across 4000–500 cm−1. The crystallographic structure of bio-CuNPs was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D8) at 2θ angles from 10° to 80°, with a working voltage of 45 kV and current of 40 mA. Morphology and particle size were assessed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; ZEISS GeminiSEM 300) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi HT-7820) following previously published methods [23]. Samples were prepared by mounting on a carbon-coated Cu grid for TEM and an aluminum stub for SEM. The elemental composition of bio-CuNPs was analyzed using Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS; Oxford, High Wycombe, Buckinghamshire, UK) at 20 kV.

In vitro antifungal efficiency of bio-CuNPs against Mo

The antifungal activity against Mo was evaluated using a modified assessment approach, as reported previously [25]. Briefly, bio-CuNPs at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL were added to a complete medium (CM) and potato dextrose agar (PDA). The mixture was then properly mixed and placed into petri plates. A positive control was prepared by adding bulk-Cu (Copper(II) oxide, CuO, ≥99.9 % purity, 1000–2000 nm particle size, CAS 1317-38-0, black powder, density 6.9 g/cm3, MSE Supplies LLC) at 100 μg/mL to CM and PDA. Mycelial discs (∼5 mm) from 7-day-old cultures were incubated on media plates at 28 ± 2 °C under a 12-h light–dark cycle for 8 days. To determine the percentage inhibition rate, the treatment and control plates were compared [25].

The variables Mt and Mc represent colony diameter in treated and control plates, respectively.

Conidiation, conidial germination, and appressorial formation assays

Mo was grown on CM plates containing bio-CuNPs at 0, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL at 28 °C under a 12-h light–dark cycle for 8 days. Conidia were harvested with 5 mL sterile water, filtered, and counted using a hemocytometer. Antifungal activity of bio-CuNPs was evaluated by assessing Mo conidial germination and appressorium formation, using a modified standard protocol [26]. A spore suspension (5 × 104 spores/mL) was prepared from 8-day-old Mo cultures grown on CM. Aliquots (20 μL) were mixed with bio-CuNPs at 0, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL on hydrophobic slides. Controls included spore suspensions with ddH2O and bulk-Cu (100 μg/mL). Conidial germination and appressorium formation were observed under a compound microscope (ZEISS-AX10) at 8, 24, and 48 h, incubated at 22 ± 2 °C in dark, humid conditions. DNA damage analysis

The effect of bio-CuNPs on Mo damage was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis [27]. Precisely, 1 mL of Mo conidia (5 × 104 spores/mL) was centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 10 min and treated with bio-CuNPs (0, 25, 50, 100 μg/mL) or bulk-Cu (100 μg/mL) at 28 °C and 150 rpm for 6 h, with untreated conidia as control. Genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial Fungal DNA kit (Qiagen, UK). DNA damage was assessed qualitatively on 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis and quantitatively using NanoDrop-1000.

Mycelial morphology and cellular integrity assay

Fungal mycelium is grown in CM media with 100 μg/mL or without-bio-CuNPs to detect the fungal morphology under the SEM and TEM. Fungal disks from treated (100 μg/mL, 10-day-old) and untreated plates were incubated overnight at 4 °C in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde solution. The samples were rinsed three times with 0.1 M PBS and fixed at room temperature with 1 % osmium oxide. For dehydration and post-fixation, a graded series of ethanol or acetone (30–100 %) was applied. The prepared samples were then examined by SEM and TEM [28].

Transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from Mo cultures treated with 100 μg/mL bio-CuNPs for 12 h, with untreated cultures as controls. RNA was isolated using the TRIzol® Reagent kit (Invitrogen, USA), and genomic DNA was removed with DNase I (Takara). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase, followed by second-strand synthesis. Libraries were constructed, enriched by PCR amplification, and finalized with an average fragment size of 450 bp.

Library quality and concentration were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries with different index sequences were pooled proportionally based on the desired data volume. Paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq2500 platform (Shanghai PersonalBio Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and data analysis was conducted by Novogene (Beijing, China). Clean reads were obtained by removing PCR duplicates, contaminants, low-quality reads, and adaptors, then aligned to the reference genome (GCF_000002495.2) using TopHat 2 (v2.0.12) [29].

The RSEM program was used to obtain gene expression levels by estimating the FPKM (Fragments per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) values [30]. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq R package, with genes exhibiting log2FC ≥ or ≤2, p ≤ 0.05 considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [31]. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encylcopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using the GOseq R package and the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), respectively [31].

Construction of targeted disruption mutants

Chitin and transporter-related gene deletion strains were generated based on the premise of homologous recombination. The upstream and downstream portions of the specified gene were initially amplified from a wild-type genomic DNA template (about 1.0 kb each) and subsequently cloned into the plasmid pKO3A between the restriction enzyme cleavage sites HindIII and XbaI [32]. Following the acquisition of the plasmids pKO3A-CHS, pKO3A-MFS and pKO3A-MSC, and subsequent verification of their accuracy via sequencing, they were employed for the Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation (AtMT) into the wild-type Mo strain (G-11) [33]. Gene deletion mutants were acquired via CM plate screening, utilizing 200 μg/mL hygromycin B, and validated through PCR,. The primers used for PCR validation are included in (Table S3).

Cell wall and cell membrane sensitivity assays

To assess cell wall integrity, mycelial plugs of wild-type G-11 and deletion mutants for chitin/transporter genes were inoculated onto CM plates containing 100 μg/mL Congo red (CR) with or without 100 μg/mL bio-CuNPs. For cell membrane integrity assays, mycelial plugs from wild-type G-11 and deletion mutant strains were placed on CM plates supplemented with 0.02 % SDS, with or without 100 μg/mL bio-CuNPs [34]. After being cultured for 8 days, the growth diameter was measured perpendicularly, and the inhibition ratio was calculated by comparing growth diameter of controls and treated plates using the abovementioned formula in Equation (1) [35].

Plant material and disease assays

Rice blast–susceptible seeds (var. CO-39) were surface-sterilized with 5 % sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, rinsed with distilled water, and soaked overnight (≥12 h) before sowing. Rice was grown in peat moss amended with 7 g/L iron sulphate and 1.4 g/L nitrogen-phosphorous-postassium fertilizer and in field soil collected for rice fields near Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Hangzhou, China. Plastic pots (12 cm diameter × 16 cm height) were filled with 0.6 kg sieved soil per pot. Plants were maintained in a greenhouse at 24 ± 2 °C under a 14:10 h light–dark cycle with 65–70 % humidity and irrigated every 2 days. Three treatments, each with three replicates, were arranged in a completely randomized design.

7-day-old seedlings were subjected to foliar treatments of bulk-Cu (100 μg/mL) and bio-CuNPs (25, 50, and 100 μg/mL) every 2 days for 2 weeks prior to inoculation with Mo strain G-11 conidia (5 × 104 mL−1), following established NP foliar application protocols [36]. Healthy and inoculated seedlings sprayed with sterile ddH2O served as controls. Infected rice seedlings were incubated in the dark at 22 °C with 80–85 % humidity for 2 days, then transferred to a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle for 5 days. Lesion number and area were recorded on leaves at 7 days post-inoculation (dpi) [37]. The infection assays were repeated three times, with significantly consistent results.

Leaf ultrastructure observation

Bio-CuNPs-treated and control rice leaf samples were examined by Cryo-SEM and TEM, as described previously [38]. For EDS analysis, samples were attached to carbon type by an SPI Module ™ sputter coater (SPI supplies, USA). For SEM and TEM obervations, samples were fixed and prepared following established protocols as described previously [39]. Stomatal morphology, cell wall, and intracellular structure of bio-CuNP-treated and untreated healthy and infected plants were observed in each treatment triplicate sample.

Estimation of the plant growth, antioxidant enzymes, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

Leaf tissues (200 mg), collected at 7 days post-treatment, were homogenized in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Enzyme activities were determined spectrophotometrically by measuring absorbance at 560 nm for superoxide dismutase (SOD), 520 nm for catalase (CAT) [40,41], and 290 nm for ascorbate peroxidase (APX) [42]. An absorbance change of 0.01 units/min corresponded to one unit of enzyme activity.

To assess malondialdehyde (MDA), fresh rice leaves (100 mg) were homogenized in 0.1 % trichloroacetic acid and centrifuge at 12,000×g, 4 °C. Then, mixed the supernatant with 0.5 % thiobarbituric acid, incubated at 95 °C, followed by measuring the absorbance at 532 nm and 600 nm for MDA quantification [43]. The total amount of superoxide anion (O2•‒) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was quantified by centrifuging homogenized leaf tissue in 0.1 % trichloroacetic acid at 12,000×g for 12 min. The supernatant was heated for 30 min at 95 °C with 0.5 % thiobarbituric acid in a water bath and then cooled to room temperature. H2O2 [44] and O2•‒ [45] concentrations were measured spectrophotometrically at 430 and 530 nm, respectively. O2•‒ and H2O2 were detected in rice leaves using nitro-blue tetrazolium (NBT) and 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining methods, respectively [39].

Quantification of salicylic acid and methyl jasmonate

Salicylic acid (SA) content was determined as described previously [46]. In brief, leaf sample (0.1 g) was ground and extracted with 1 mL 90 % methanol at 4 °C, then centrifuged at 8000×g. The residue was re-extracted with 0.5 mL methanol, and supernatants were combined, evaporated, treated with TCA, and extracted using ethyl acetate. Samples were analyzed by high performance liquid chromatography with fluorescence detection at 294/426 nm.

We quantified the methyl jasmonate (MeJA) concentration as described previously [47]. Briefly, leaf tissue (0.1 g) was extracted with 10 mL 80 % acetonitrile at 4 °C overnight and centrifuged at 8000×g. The supernatant was passed through a C18 column and eluted using 1 % formic acid, methanol, and 0.5 % ammonia in methanol. The eluate was evaporated, re-dissolved in methanol, filtered, and analyzed by HPLC-UV at 210 nm.

Metabolomic analysis

Metabolomic analysis of rice plants was performed to evaluate host responses to 100 μg/mL bio-CuNPs with or without G-11 inoculation. For each group, 200 mg of leaves were harvested, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, lyophilized, and stored at −80 °C. Analysis was conducted by PersonalBio Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry, following previously described methods [48]. Lyophilized leaf tissue was weighed and processed using a high-throughput tissue grinder. A pooled QC sample (10–20 μL from each filtrate) was prepared to assess instrument stability and data reliability. Samples were analyzed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 Å, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) at 0.4 mL/min.

Raw data were processed with Compound Discoverer 3.1 for peak alignment, feature extraction, and quantification. Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified using hierarchical clustering and Pearson correlation analysis based on Log2FC values. Metabolite annotation was performed via MetaboAnalyst, PCA, KEGG, and Venn diagram tools (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst). Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using Metabolite Set Enrichment Analysis, with significance assessed by hypergeometric test p-values.

Expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted using a commercial kit and treated with DNase to remove residual DNA. RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop-1000 spectrophotometer. cDNA synthesis was performed using the ReverTra Ace RT-qPCR kit (TOYOBO Co., Ltd). The RT-qPCR reaction contained 10 μL 2X SYBR Premix Ex-Taq, 10 μM gene-specific primers, and 1 μg cDNA. Reactions were run on a CFX96 real-time PCR system, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primers used for expression validation and rice defense genes are listed in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Cytotoxicity assay of bio-CuNPs

The toxicity test was accomplished following the (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) (MTT) based assay protocol with slight modification [49]. Briefly, the AML12 cells were seeded at 5 × 104 at 96 well plates, and incubated overnight at 37 oC [HEPA Class 100, IncuSafe MCO-15 (Panasonic)]. Cells were treated with CuCl2·2H2O, bulk-Cu, or bio-CuNPs at 25, 50, 100, 500, and 1000 μg/mL and incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 24 and 72 h ddH2O-treated cells served as controls. Cells were then incubated with 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution for 3 h at 37 °C. The formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO with gentle shaking for 15 min. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 570 nm using a BioTek Cytation 3 microplate reader to assess cell viability.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was conducted with three biological replicates, and the results are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v8) and Statistics software (v9.0). One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by LSD test was used to assess significant variability within the experimental data. For transcriptomic and metabolomic data analysis, R was used to determine the statistical significance of the treatments; an all-pairwise comparisons test was employed with a probability level of p ≤ 0.05.

Results and discussion

Biosynthesis and characterization of the bio-CuNPs

For this study, bio-CuNPs were extracellularly-synthesized using the antagonistic bacterium B. amyloliquefaciens Q1, as previously reported [24]. CuNP formation was initially confirmed by a visible color change from light blue to dark blue. The presence of bio-CuNPs in the culture supernatant was further verified by a characteristic absorption peak at 217 nm (Fig. 1a), indicating that B. amyloliquefaciens Q1-secreted bioactive compounds capable of reducing Cu2+ ions from CuCl2·2H2O into CuNPs. No such peak was observed in control samples lacking either the substrate or the bacterial supernatant. These findings are consistent with previous reports of B. amyloliquefaciens-mediated CuNP synthesis, characterized by a surface plasmon resonance peak at 386 nm and particle sizes of 31–98 nm. Additionally, these findings are consistent with previous studies reporting bio-CuNP synthesis using cell-free culture filtrates from Bacillus altitudinis [23] and Shigella flexneri SNT22 [50]. Collectively, our results together with previous studies underscore the potential of Bacillus species, including B. amyloliquefaciens Q1, as effective biocatalysts for the green synthesis of CuNPs, offering an eco-friendly alternative to conventional chemical methods.

The FTIR spectra of bio-CuNPs confirmed the presence of a range of bioactive components on the surface that are involved in their long-term stability [51]. To characterize the surface chemistry of bio-CuNPs, FTIR analysis was performed. The spectra showed absorption peaks at 3386, 2959, 1648, 1405, 1065, and 582 cm−1, corresponding to O–H, C–H, C=C, O–H bending, C–N, C–O, and C–I stretching, indicating the presence of alcoholic, carboxylic, alkene, aromatic amine, and halo functional groups (Fig. 1b), These findings align with previous studies, suggesting that these functional groups contribute to the stability and functionalization of bio-CuNPs [52]. XRD analysis of bio-CuNPs showed distinct peaks at 2θ values of 35.65°, 38.84°, 48.80°, 61.61°, and 68.09°, corresponding to the (110), (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of face-centered cubic Cu crystals (Fig. 1c). Similar diffraction angles and facet positions were observed for bio-CuNPs synthesized by the B. altitudinis [23].

The SEM and TEM images revealed variable morphology of bio-CuNPs (Fig. 1d–f). Most particles were semi-spherical and agglomerated, ranging from 16 to 62 nm (with average particle size of 37 nm) (Fig. 1f). The EDS spectra showed that bio-CuNPs had carbon (36.89 %), oxygen (23.03 %), Cu (21.39 %) as the key components (Fig. 1g). Overall, characterization confirmed that the biosynthesized CuNPs were solid, semi-spherical, stable, and well-structured NPs.

In vitro antifungal effect against Mo

In vitro assays demonstrated that bio-CuNPs significantly inhibited Mo radial growth in a dose-dependent manner. Bio-CuNPs effectively inhibited Mo mycelial growth in a concentration-dependent manner on both CM and PDA media, while showing exceptional results at 100 μg/mL concentration. At 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL, inhibition rates on CM were 28.16 %, 37.95 %, and 62.85 %, and on PDA were 40.40 %, 58.08 %, and 73.23 %, respectively, compared to 100 μg/mL bulk-Cu, which showed minimal inhibition (4.08 % CM; 6.56 % PDA) (Fig. 2a–c). Bio-CuNPs at 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL significantly reduced fungal biomass, with fresh weights decreasing by 28.06 %, 38.81 %, and 48.99 %, and dry weights by 25.33 %, 48.62 %, and 70.64 %, respectively, compared to the untreated controls (Fig. 2d and e). These results showed that bio-CuNPs directly inhibited hyphal growth of Mo, which could be one of the potential mechanisms of CuNP-based disease suppression in rice.

Conidiation assays showed that bio-CuNPs at 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL significantly inhibited conidiation by 30.53 %, 45.28 %, and 78.94 %, respectively, compared to untreated controls, whereas bulk-Cu showed non-significant inhibition (1.75 %) compared to controls (Fig. 2f). The appressorium is the primary infection structure of Mo and is closely linked to virulence [53]; therefore, we further evaluated conidial germination and appressorium formation. Bio-CuNPs (25, 50, and 100 μg/mL) significantly suppressed conidial germination and appressorium formation over 48 h. At 48 h, 100 μg/mL bio-CuNPs reduced conidial germination by 19.31 % (s (Fig. 2g) and appressorium formation by 49.65 % compared to untreated controls (Fig. 3a and b). Bio-CuNPs (25, 50, and 100 μg/mL) caused 1.7–2.68-fold and 0.43–1.55-fold increases in DNA (OD620) and protein (OD280) leakage in Mo compared to controls (Fig. 3c). DNA integrity assays showed that bio-CuNPs (25, 50, and 100 μg/mL) significantly decreased DNA concentration and band intensity compared to bulk-Cu and untreated controls (Fig. 3d and e). Previous studies support our findings, showing that Cu-chitosan NPs (0.1 mg/L) inhibited Pyricularia grisea mycelial growth by 80 % [54]. Similarly, bio-silver NPs (50 μg/mL) reduced Mo radial growth, conidial germination, and appresorium formation. Collectively, these results show that bio-CuNPs effectively disrupted key physiological processes of Mo required for successful infection and virulence, including conidiation, conidial germination, and appressorium formation.

Effects of bio-CuNPs on the mycelium morphology and ultrastructure

To examine the impact of bio-CuNPs on Mo mycelium and cell structure, SEM and TEM imaging were performed. Bio-CuNPs (100 μg/mL) disrupted the structural integrity of Mo hyphae and cells, whereas no noticeable changes were observed in bulk-Cu-treated or untreated controls (Fig. 3f). Precisely, bio-CuNPs-treated hyphae were constricted and twisted (Fig. 3f). Further, TEM images revealed structural alterations in cellular components, including the cell wall, cytoplasm, vacuole, and nuclear membrane, ultimately leading to leakage of intracellular contents including DNA and protein (Fig. 3c–f).

Previous studies have shown that NPs disrupt microbial cell walls and hyphal structure, including CuNPs (0.30–0.35 M) damaging Fusarium solani hyphae [55]. A similar finding was reported by Hafeez et al. [56], demonstrating that bio-chitosan NPs (200 mg/L) induced structural damages to Mo hyphae and cells to suppress its infection in rice plants. Consistently, our results demonstrate that bio-CuNPs disintegrated Mo hyphal and cellular integrity to disrupt growth, conidiation, and infection-related structures, highlighting a direct pathogen-inhibiting mechanism of bio-CuNPs against Mo.

Bio-CuNPs induced transcriptional changes in Mo

Bio-CuNPs induced a range of physiological responses in Mo, including growth inhibition, hyphal damage, and cell death, as observed in our experiments and reported in a recent study [56]. To explore the molecular basis of these effects, RNA-seq analysis was performed on Mo treated with bio-CuNPs (100 μg/mL). High-quality data were obtained for both control (98.49 %) and treated samples (98.51 %). Differential expression analysis identified 1309 upregulated and 1319 downregulated DEGs (log2FC ≥ or ≤ 2, respectively, p ≤ 0.05), as confirmed by hierarchical clustering and bar plots (Fig. 4a and b; Tables S4 and S5). These findings indicate that bio-CuNPs disrupt multiple cellular processes in Mo, contributing to growth inhibition and cell death.

KEGG pathway analysis identified 180 pathways in bio-CuNP-treated Mo cells, with 101 upregulated and 79 downregulated. Notably, pathways related to chitin biosynthesis and transporter genes were significantly affected, indicating key targets of the antifungal action of bio-CuNPs. Precisely, upregulated and downregulated pathways were enriched in starch and sucrose metabolism (19 upregulated DEGs and 4 downregulated DEGs), glycerophospholipid metabolism (4 upregulated DEGs and 3 downregulated DEGs), steroid biosynthesis (9 upregulated DEGs and 1 downregulated DEGs), various types of N-Glycan biosynthesis (9 upregulated DEGs and 4 downregulated DEGs), cysteine and methionine metabolism (9 upregulated DEGs and 4 downregulated DEGs) (Fig. 4d and e). Chitin is an essential component of the Mo cell wall, and disruption of its synthesis leads to structural abnormalities [57]. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that chitin biosynthesis, associated with citral-linked metabolic pathways [58], was significantly dysregulated in Mo after bio-CuNPs treatment, likely contributing to impaired cell wall integrity and antifungal activity.

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed 402 genes classified into molecular functions, biological processes, and cellular components. Specifically, molecular functions were enriched in catalytic activity, DNA binding, and transition metal binding, while biological processes and cellular components were enriched in signal transduction, DNA recombination regulation, and nuclear transcription factor complex (Fig. 4f and g). Both upregulated and downregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in ion and organic cyclic compound binding, membrane-associated components, and organic substance metabolic processes across molecular, cellular, and biological levels. Previous studies reported that NP treatments disrupted fungal processes, including membrane biosynthesis, calcium ion transport, steroid metabolism, and transcriptional regulation [57,59]. Consistently, our transcriptomic analysis shows that bio-CuNPs directly targeted and impaired multiple essential pathways in Mo, suppressing its growth and leading to cell death, highlighting a direct antifungal mechanism.

Chitin and transporter-related genes are involved in the sensitivity response to bio-CuNPs in Mo

To investigate the biochemical and molecular mechanisms underlying Mo sensitivity to bio-CuNPs, we focused on their effects on cell wall and membrane integrity. For this purpose, deletion mutants were generated for chitin synthesis genes (MoCHS-A, MoCHS-B), chitin deacetylase genes (MoCHS-C, MoCHS-D), and three transporter genes (MoMFS-1, MoMFS-2, MoMSC-2) (Fig. S1a–e). We assessed the effect of bio-CuNPs (100 μg/mL) on radial growth of deletion mutants ΔMoCHS-A, ΔMoCHS-B, ΔMoCHS-C, ΔMoCHS-D, MoMFS-1, MoMFS-2, and MoMSC-2. All mutants exhibited significantly reduced growth on bio-CuNP-amended CM plates, indicating increased sensitivity compared to bulk-Cu-treated and untreated controls (Fig. 5a and b). Specifically, ΔMoCHS-A, ΔMoCHS-B, ΔMoCHS-C, ΔMoCHS-D, ΔMoMFS-1, ΔMoMFS-2, and ΔMoMSC-2 mutants exhibited reduced radial growth by 21.50 %, 20.47 %, 10.75 %, 20.43 %, 19.35 %, 21.50 %, and 55.91 %, respectively, on bio-CuNP-amended plates compared to the untreated wild-type G-11 strain, indicating increased sensitivity to bio-CuNP treatment. (Fig. 5a and b). These results indicate that MoCHS-A, MoCHS-B, MoCHS-C, MoCHS-D, MoMFS-1, MoMFS-2, and MoMSC-2 play a key role in Mo sensitivity to bio-CuNPs, highlighting their involvement in the antifungal mechanism against rice blast fungus. Additionally, conidial germination and appressorium formation assays (Fig. S2a–c) showed that bio-CuNPs exerted stronger inhibitory effects on the deletion mutants compared to the wild-type G-11 strain (Fig. S4a–c). A recent study showed that ΔFonPEX2, ΔFonPEX8, and ΔFonPEX10 mutants were more sensitive to 100 μg/mL bio-MgNPs than wild-type Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. niveum (Fon) [60], indicating the potential molecular targets for managing fungal infection in crop plants. Our results show Mo deletion mutants are more sensitive to bio-CuNPs, indicating specific target genes for fine-tuning of nano-enabled disease management strategies in rice as well as in other crops.

In the presence of CR (100 μg/mL), all deletion mutants (except ΔMoCH-A) (Fig. 5a–c). The heightened growth inhibition in deletion mutant by bio-CuNPs combined with cell wall and cell membrance disrupting agents suggests functional synergism among bio-CuNPs and cell wall/membrane disrupting agents. The heightened sensitivity of these mutants to bio-CuNPs confirms that cell wall disruption is linked to the dysregulation of these genes.

Rice blast disease suppressing effect of bio-CuNPs

In recent years, nano-enabled crop protection approaches have been widely reported to protect plants against devastating pathogens, including the rice blast fungus. Among these, spray application of NPs has emerged as an effective strategy to enhance crop disease resistance [12,13,57,63,64]. In this study, the efficacy of foliar-applied bio-CuNPs in controlling rice blast disease was evaluated by quantifying lesion number and area on infected rice and barley leaves (commonly used model host for Mo infection assays). Pretreatment with bio-CuNPs at 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL significantly reduced both lesion number and infected leaf area in infected rice and barley leaves compared to untreated controls, demonstrating a dose-dependent protective effect (Fig. 6a and Fig. S3a,b,d). Specifically, bio-CuNPs at 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL significantly reduced lesion numbers in infected rice leaves by 50.53 %, 61.09 %, and 79.63 %, respectively, compared to untreated controls (Fig. 6b,c and Fig. S3c and e). These findings align with previous studies showing that foliar application of silicon oxide NPs (100 mg/L) decreased Mo infection by 70 % [65]. Similarly, 14-day foliar treatment with chitosan NPs (200 mg/L) reduced blast disease by 77.7 % [65], while 1000 mg/L CuNPs lowered bacterial panicle blight severity caused by B. glumae by 21.23 %. A recent study by Hafeez et al. [57] demonstrated that foliar application of bio-CuNPs (25–100 μg/mL) markedly suppressed rice blast disease by reducing lesion development and Mo infection in rice, highlighting their potential applications in managing crop diseases [66].

Effects of bio-CuNPs on the rice leaf morphology and cellular structures

Next, the interaction of bio-CuNPs (100 μg/mL) and Mo on the rice leaf surface and within cells was analyzed using EDS-coupled SEM and TEM (Fig. 6d, e and Fig. S4a–f). Cryo-SEM images showed that bio-CuNP-, bulk-Cu-treated, and untreated healthy rice leaves maintained normal surface morphology (Fig. 6d). In contrast, Mo-infected leaves exhibited severe surface damage, which was effectively restored by bio-CuNP treatment. Such pathogen-induced damage remained unmitigated in bulk-Cu-treated and untreated infected controls (Fig. 6d). EDS elemental mapping confirmed the absorption and dispersion of bio-CuNPs and bulk Cu on healthy and Mo-infected rice leaf surfaces (Fig. S4a–f).

Further, ultrastructural imaging showed intact membranes, with high proportion of normal closed stomatal aperture in bulk-Cu-treated and untreated leaves. Notably, partially open stomata in bio-CuNP-treated leaves suggest NP entry through these openings, consistent with previous reports of bio-chitosan NP uptake, subcellular distribution, and inhibition of Mo infection in rice [57]. TEM analysis confirmed the localization of bio-CuNPs within subcellular spaces of rice leaves. These results indicate that bio-CuNPs inhibit Mo penetration and proliferation, enhancing leaf resistance to fungal infection (Fig. 6e). Without bio-CuNPs, Mo penetrated rice leaves through stomata and the cuticle, forming penetration pegs and spreading within extracellular spaces. The fungus damaged the cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, and vacuoles. In contrast, bio-CuNP treatment limited Mo invasion and associated damage, while maintaining typical cellular structures with intact membranes and organelles, thereby suppressing rice blast disease (Fig. 6e).

A previous study reported that titanium dioxide NPs extensively covered rice root epidermis and penetrated roots, resulting in higher accumulation in above-ground tissues compared to bulk particles, as shown by SEM and TEM analysis [2]. Similarly, TEM analysis in rice revealed that foliar-applied silica NPs (40 nm) were absorbed through stomata and distributed in extracellular air spaces without entering cell walls, while root-applied silica NPs penetrated multiple tissues, including epidermis, exodermis, endodermis, and xylem, facilitating systemic uptake and translocation [65]. A recent study investigated the efficacy of bio-MnNPs against Fon in watermelon, revealing NP distribution within root tissues [67], which reduced disease symptoms and progression. Similarly, our results demonstrate that bio-CuNPs effectively inhibited rice blast disease by restricting Mo growth and spread within rice tissues, preserving normal leaf cell structure and integrity, highlighting the potential of biogenic NPs for plant disease management.

Effects of bio-CuNPs on morpho-physiological parameters of rice

To further investigate the impact of bio-CuNPs on rice growth and physiology, we assessed their effects on plant morpho-physiological traits following foliar application. One-week-old rice plants were treated with 100 μg/mL bio-CuNPs at 2-day intervals for two weeks. The treatment enhanced plant growth, improved physio-morphological traits, reduced ROS accumulation, and increased antioxidant enzyme activity in healthy and Mo-infected plants. Bio-CuNP-treated healthy plants exhibited significant improvements in all measured growth parameters compared to untreated controls. Shoot height was increased by 13.23 % and 20.79 % in bulk Cu- and bio-CuNPs-treated plants, respectively, whereas root length was improved by 16.15 % and 25.76 %, in bluk Cu- and bio-CuNPs-treated plants, respectively, compared to untreated healthy plants. Further, shoot fresh and dry weights in bulk Cu/bio-CuNPs-treated plants were increased by 27.26 %/38.37 % and 37.63 %/56.58 %, respectively, while root fresh and dry weights were increased by 45.41 %/53.47 % and 28.13 %/32.61 % in bulk Cu/bio-CuNPs-treated plants, respectively, compared to untreated controls (Fig. 7a–c and Fig. S4g, h). These findings are consistent with Choudhary et al. [68], who showed that Cu and CuNPs enhanced plant height, root length, root number, and chlorophyll content in greenhouse and field trials against Curvularia leaf spot. Similarly, Noman et al. [67] reported that bio-MnNPs treatment improved all growth parameters in watermelon plants under Fon infection.

Enhanced plant growth is closely associated with the activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms, as antioxidant enzymes play a pivotal role in mitigating oxidative stress and strengthening plant immunity [69]. Treatment with 100 μg/mL bio-CuNPs significantly enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities in healthy and Mo-infected rice plants. In bio-CuNP-treated healthy plants, SOD, APX, and CAT were increased by 18.16 %, 24.40 %, and 15.57 %, respectively, while in infected plants, the increases were 26.91 %, 22.95 %, and 30.0 % compared to respective untreated controls. (Fig. 7e–g). These results align with previous studies showing that bio-CuNPs (100 μg/mL) activate antioxidant defenses, reducing oxidative damage and mitigating bacterial fruit blotch in watermelon [23].

Upon pathogen or herbivore attack, plants often trigger a burst of ROS, which can harm invading organisms but may also induce cellular oxidative damage if not adequately regulated [[67], [70]]. Treatment with 100 μg/mL bio-CuNPs effectively reduced O2•‒ (41.15 %), H2O2 (43.97 %), and MDA (31.34 %) in healthy plants compared to untreated healthy controls. Similarly, bio-CuNP treatment decreased O2•‒ (43.57 %), H2O2 (41.02 %), and MDA (33.96 %) in infected plants compared to untreated diseased controls (Fig. 7d–h-j). Although bio-CuNPs restricted the hyper accumulation of ROS by activating enzymatic antioxidants in healthy and diseased, they maintained a basal level of ROS that is essential to initiate immune response against the pathogen. A recent study demonstrated that bio-chitosan NPs (200 mg L−1) activated innate immune responses in rice, enhancing antioxidative defenses and providing protection against Mo [57]. Similarly, Choudhary et al. [68] reported that Cu-chitosan NPs enhanced maize defense against Curvularia lunata, reducing ROS-induced damage. Ahmed et al. [71] demonstrated that biogenic iron nanocomposites mitigated bacterial leaf blight in rice by stimulating antioxidant enzymes and suppressing ROS accumulation.

Consistently, histochemical staining confirmed that bio-CuNP treatment reduced endogenous ROS levels in both healthy and infected rice plants. Untreated healthy rice leaves showed prominent brown and blue spots, indicating H2O2 and O2•‒ accumulation, respectively, which were decreased upon exposure to bio-CuNPs. In contrast, bio-CuNP-treated diseased plants exhibited reduced brown and blue blots on leaf surfaces compared to untreated infected controls (Fig. 7d), indicating that bio-CuNPs effectively suppress Mo infection-induced ROS accumulation, which is consistent with previous reports on bioformulated chitosan NPs [57].

At the molecular level, bio-CuNPs markedly upregulated antioxidant enzyme-encoding genes (OsSOD, OsAPX, and OsCAT) in healthy rice plants compared to untreated healthy controls (Fig. 7m). Mo infection significantly reduced OsSOD and OsAPX transcripts compared to healthy controls, whereas bio-CuNP treatment significantly elevated expression of OsCAT in infected plants relative to untreated infected controls (Fig. 7m), indicating bio-CuNP-mediated defense adaptation in rice plants against Mo infection. Contrarily, bio-CuNP treatment significantly downregulated RbohA and RbohB, genes encoding NADPH oxidases responsible for ROS generation, in diseased plants compared to untreated infected controls [72] (Fig. 7n). This observation correlates with the reduced ROS levels in bio-CuNP-treated diseased plants, highlighting their capacity to regulate ROS production and mitigate pathogen-induced oxidative stress. Similarly, recent studies show that bio-chitosan-iron nanocomposites triggered guard cell morphological changes, promoting stomatal closure to limit Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae entry, while activating immune responses through upregulation of defense genes (OsNPR1, OsPR1, OsPR5, OsPR9, and OsEDS1) to suppress bacterial leaf blight in rice [71]. Similarly, bio-CuNPs suppressed watermelon bacterial fruit blotch by inducing stomatal closure genes (ClLOX1, ClSLAC1, ClOST1), activating innate immunity genes (ClNPR1, ClPR1, ClPR2), limiting pathogen entry, and downregulating ROS-related genes (ClRbohF, ClRbohD) [23]. Overall, these results demonstrate that bio-CuNPs suppressed rice blast disease by strengthening antioxidant defenses, activating innate immunity, promoting plant growth, and mitigating pathogen-induced oxidative stress.

Bio-CuNPs triggered defense-related phytohormones

Building on the observed bio-CuNP–induced enhancement of antioxidant defenses and immune responses, we next evaluated the involvement of key defense-related phytohormones, SA and MeJA, in rice defense against Mo infection [73,74]. Bio-CuNP application improved the SA levels in healthy and infected plants compared to untreated controls. Specifically, bio-CuNP treatment significantly increased SA content in healthy and Mo-infected plants by 13.96% and 66.15 %, respectively, compared to untreated corresponding controls (Fig. 7k). Similarly, bio-CuNP treatment elevated MeJA levels in leaves of both healthy and Mo-infected plants, with increases of 29.72 % and 54.44 %, respectively, compared to respective untreated controls (Fig. 7l). These results indicate that bio-CuNPs activated key SA- and MeJA-responsive defense signaling pathways, thereby strengthening the plant immune response against Mo infection.

To further confirm the involvement of SA and MeJA signaling in bio-CuNP-mediated defense in rice, we analyzed the expression of key phytohormone-related genes, OsLox1 and OsPR1a, in untreated and bio-CuNP-treated healthy and Mo-infected rice plants. All defense-related genes were significantly upregulated in bio-CuNP-treated healthy plants compared to untreated controls (Fig. 7o). While Mo infection alone increased their expression relative to healthy controls but not strong enough to initiate a defense response. Importantly, bio-CuNP-treated infected plants exhibited the highest induction of SA and MeJA biosynthesis and signaling genes, than that in untreated infected plants (Fig. 7o), thus inducing strong defense response against Mo infection.

These results are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that chemically-synthesized sulfur NPs induced systemic acquired resistance in tomato against F. oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici by upregulating defense-related genes such as PR1, PR-P2, PR5, STH2, GluB, and CH9 [75]. Similarly, Noman et al. [67] reported that bio-MnNPs modulate SA biosynthesis and signaling genes in watermelon against Fon. These findings strongly support that bio-CuNPs enhanced rice genetic defense responses against Mo by activating phytohormone-mediated signaling pathways.

Bio-CuNPs modulated metabolites in rice plants

To further dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying bio-CuNP-induced defense responses, metabolomic approaches were employed to evaluate their impact on rice physiology, antioxidant activity, and phytohormone-mediated defense signaling [76,77]. These techniques can detect subtle yet significant alterations in plants exposed to NPs that might otherwise remain undetected [78]. In this study, differential analysis using MetaboAnalyst revealed significantly upregulated and downregulated metabolites across the experimental conditions. A total of 11 distinct metabolite classes were detected in rice tissues, with the following proportional distribution: lipids and lipid-like molecules (29.39 %), organic acids and derivatives (19.59 %), organoheterocyclic compounds (16.23 %), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (12.43 %), organic oxygen compounds (8.19 %), benzenoids (7.89 %), nucleosides, nucleotides and analogs (1.9 %), organic nitrogen compounds (1.9 %), alkaloids and derivatives (1.2 %), lignans and neolignans (1.2 %), and other compounds (0.4 %) (Fig. 8a).

PCA revealed distinct clustering of CK, CK + Mo, CuNPs, and CuNPs + Mo groups along PC1, accounting for 38.7 % of the total variance. This separation indicates that both pathogen infection and bio-CuNP treatment induced significant metabolic shifts. The distinct separation between CK + Mo and CuNPs + Mo along PC1 and PC2 indicates that bio-CuNP treatment markedly reshapes the metabolic profile of rice plants under Mo infection (Fig. 8b). Complementing the PCA results, Venn diagram analysis revealed that 171 metabolites were differentially expressed across all experimental groups. Additionally, 41, 51, 32, and 47 metabolites were uniquely expressed in the CK_vs_CK + Mo, CK_vs_CuNPs, CK + Mo_vs_CuNPs + Mo, and CuNPs_vs_CuNPs + Mo comparisons, respectively (Fig. 8c and d).

This study identified a total of 2855 DAMs in rice plants following bio-CuNPs treatment and/or inoculation with Mo, comprising 2001 upregulated and 854 downregulated metabolites. Specific pairwise comparisons revealed the following patterns of metabolite regulation: CK_vs_CK + Mo (720 upregulated DAMs, 191 downregulated DAMs), CK_vs_CuNPs (339 upregulated DAMs, 142 downregulated DAMs), CK + Mo_vs_CuNPs + Mo (280 upregulated DAMs, 410 downregulated DAMs), and CuNPs_vs_CuNPs + Mo (662 upregulated DAMs, 111 downregulated DAMs) (Fig. S5).

Hierarchical clustering analysis and heatmap visualization further corroborated the differential abundance of key metabolites. Several compounds, including cyanidin, succinyladenosine, and 5-(hydroxymethyl)-4-methoxy-2, showed elevated levels in the CuNPs + Mo group compared to other treatment conditions (Fig. 8e), suggesting their potential involvement in bio-CuNP-mediated defense responses against rice blast disease.

The KEGG database provides comprehensive information on diverse metabolic pathways [79]. We expanded our investigation of DAMs through KEGG pathway enrichment analysis and heatmap visualization. Our analysis revealed that bio-CuNP treatment potentially upregulated several key metabolic pathways, including purine metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis (Fig. 8f). All of these pathways are integral components of plant defense mechanisms and stress signaling networks [80].

The observed alterations in metabolites and metabolic pathways offer key insights into how rice plants respond to bio-CuNP treatment. Similarly, previous study has shown that nano zero-valent iron modulated the rice grain metabolome, enhancing antioxidant and defense systems and increasing the production of growth hormones, defense-related compounds, and antioxidants [78]. Further, it has been previously observed that foliar application of zinc oxide NPs (20 and 100 mg/L) upregulated the expression of genes associated with antioxidant enzymes, transporters, secondary metabolism, and carbon/nitrogen metabolism in tomato plants [81]. These results highlight a conserved mechanism by which NPs modulate plant metabolism to enhance defense responses across diverse plant species against broad range of pathogens including Mo.

Toxicity effect of bio-CuNPs on the survival rate of mice cell line AML12

Although bio-NPs are effective in crop disease management, concerns about off-target and cytotoxic effects remain unaddressed. To address this, mouse hepatocyte AML12 cells were exposed to bio-CuNPs, bulk-Cu, and CuCl2·2H2O at various concentrations for 24 and 72 h. Cell viability assays showed that bio-CuNPs and bulk-Cu exhibited no significant cytotoxicity, even at the highest tested concentration (1000 μg/mL). In contrast, freshly prepared CuCl2.2H2O exhibited significant cytotoxicity across all tested concentration ranges at both time points (Fig. S6a–c). Interestingly, a slight increase in cell proliferation was observed in some groups after 24 h exposure to bio-CuNPs and bulk-Cu. However, this effect was not statistically significant after 72 h, indicating a transient hormetic response, which is occasionally seen with metal NPs and warrants further investigation.

The cytotoxicity results indicate that the concentrations of bio-CuNPs and bulk-Cu used in this study for rice blast management are likely non-toxic for other organisms. These safety tests of effective doses at cellular levels suggest minimal risk to plant beneficial microflora and other higher organisms. Overall, these findings highlight bio-CuNPs as a safe and environmentally compatible alternative to conventional copper-based agrochemicals for sustainable rice blast control in rice fields.

Conclusions

Rice blast disease, caused by Mo, significantly threatens global rice production and food security. Conventional control methods, such as chemical fungicides and biocontrol agents, are constrained by environmental toxicity, off-target effects, and limited field efficacy. This study establishes that bio-CuNPs, synthesized using B. amyloliquefaciens Q1, provide an effective and environmentally sustainable solution for rice blast management. Our findings reveal that bio-CuNPs exerted potent antifungal effects by inhibiting Mo growth, disrupting cell wall biosynthesis and membrane transport pathways, and inducing DNA damage, as confirmed by transcriptomic profiling and gene knockout studies demonstrating heightened fungal sensitivity. Moreover, bio-CuNP treatment enhanced rice defense mechanisms by upregulating antioxidant enzymes, suppressing ROS, and modulating SA and MeJA signaling pathways, with metabolomic analysis confirming shifts in defense-related metabolites (Fig. 9). These combined effects highlight a synergistic mechanism whereby bio-CuNPs directly target fungal viability while bolstering host plant resistance, offering a dual approach to disease control. Importantly, the biosafety of bio-CuNPs is supported by their non-toxicity to mammalian cells at effective antifungal concentrations. While these results are promising, the study was conducted under controlled greenhouse conditions, and future research must evaluate bio-CuNP efficacy in diverse field settings, alongside their soil persistence, potential crop accumulation, and effects on non-target organisms to ensure their safe and sustainable integration into crop protection strategies.
