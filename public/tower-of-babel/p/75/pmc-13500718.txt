Root-inspired hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface enables ultrasensitive sensing
Authors: Jie Tang, Yuan He, Ruiqi Guo, Qiao Hu, Yuanji Yao, Zicai Zhu, Dayu Zhang, Hongbo Wei, Xinyu Li, Masuki Kawamoto, Sunghoon Lee, Takao Someya, Jian Lv, Peng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltrasensitive detection of low-frequency and weak-pressure stimuli is crucial for non-invasive wearable physiological signal monitoring, human-machine interfaces, and sonar technologies. Hydrogels, with their low elastic modulus, high conformability, and tunable functionality, have been explored as suitable candidate materials for transduction of such signals. However, conventional hydrogel-based sensors lacking effective interfacial integration or surface engineering are often constrained by weak interfacial adhesion and microstructural fragility, resulting in compromised signal stability. Therefore, overcoming these limitations requires a robust interfacial design that can simultaneously enhance mechanical robustness and stabilize signal transmission. Here, we report a root-inspired hydrogel-electrode interface that integrates dendritic metallic nano-roots interfacial locking and monolithically fabricated microarray architectures. This nano/micro-hierarchical interface synergistically enhances sensing performance through amplified electrical double-layer capacitance, providing an ultralow detection limit of ~0.38 Pa, a gauge factor of 6.0 in the low-pressure region, reliable responses across 50-900 Hz, and stable performance. This strategy offers a versatile approach for realizing robust and ultrasensitive hydrogel-based sensors operating under weak and low-frequency stimuli.Subject terms: Gels and hydrogels, Gels and hydrogels, Sensors and biosensors

Introduction

Accurate and reliable detection of low-frequency, weak-pressure stimuli is paramount for diverse applications, including biomedical monitoring1,2, human-machine interfaces3–5, and sonar technologies6. However, it remains highly challenging due to the small amplitudes and slow temporal dynamics of these signals. Such weak inputs necessitate an ultrasensitive sensing interface capable of transducing subtle mechanical changes into stable and high-fidelity electrical signals. Hydrogels, owing to their intrinsic adaptability and geometric conformability with soft tissues and fluidic environments7,8, emerge as promising candidates for ultralow-frequency and weak-pressure sensing9,10. Crucially, conventionally engineered hydrogels often suffer from severe interfacial mismatch with high-modulus metallic electrodes, which commonly results in poor bonding and unstable signal transduction11,12. Therefore, achieving robust and stable interfacial bonding while simultaneously preserving the hydrogel’s intrinsic softness and mechanical compliance is the key challenge for developing high-performance, hydrogel-based ultrasensitive sensors13.

To facilitate the transduction of subtle pressure fluctuations into stable electrical signals, the prerequisite conductivity often provided by mobile ions or conductive material networks14, is utilized to form an electrical double layer (EDL) at the hydrogel-electrode interface15,16. Given that EDL-based signal generation is highly sensitive to interfacial contact quality and charge-transfer continuity, the mechanical and physical integrity of the hydrogel-electrode interface plays a decisive role in ensuring reliable signal output, especially under low-frequency and weak-pressure conditions. Consequently, substantial efforts have focused on enhancing interfacial stability through strategies such as introducing hydrogen bonding17, covalent anchoring18, and supramolecular interactions19, to reinforce hydrogel-electrode adhesion and suppress delamination. These chemical or physical interactions help compensate for the intrinsic softness of hydrogels and mitigate the mechanical mismatch with high-modulus electrodes, thereby improving signal stability under deformation. Nevertheless, the inherent mechanical disparity between rigid electrode materials and the compliant, hydrated nature of hydrogels imposes an intrinsic limit on interfacial durability, making long-term stability under repeated deformation still highly challenging.

Instead of interfacial integrity issues, EDL-based signal generation is also strongly influenced by the hydrogel-electrode topology, which is capable of amplifying the interfacial capacitance, enabling enhanced sensitivity and an expanded detection range. Micro-patterns2, gradient surfaces20, and other stress-focusing geometries15, have been explored to strengthen the deformation-induced variations in contact area and local electrical fields. Nevertheless, these delicate microstructures are inherently vulnerable to distortion or damage during fabrication and operation, making it difficult to maintain structural fidelity and stable signal transduction. Overall, the combined effects of vulnerable interfacial bonding and structural fragility lead to compromised device reliability, highlighting the need for strategies that can simultaneously reinforce interfacial mechanics while preserving microstructural integrity, motivating the exploration of sophisticated hierarchical interface architectures to reconcile these competing requirements.

Here, inspired by the robust anchoring of plant roots, we propose a root-inspired strategy that simultaneously constructs a hierarchical structure including metallic nano-roots anchored hydrogel-electrode interface with a monolithically formed microarray architecture for ultrasensitive sensing. This integrated approach strengthens interfacial anchoring while preserving the integrity of delicate microstructures, effectively addressing the dual challenges of weak bonding and structural instability. Through electrochemically mediated interfacial evolution, dendritic silver nano-roots interlocking structures are generated, enabling concurrent enhancement of mechanical anchoring and charge-transfer efficiency. Subsequently, the integral hydrogel-electrode casting framework enables dissolution-assisted demolding rather than mechanical peeling, allowing the soft hydrogel microarrays to be preserved in a complete and intact form, thereby sustaining EDL variation amplification under ultralow deformation. Upon external loading, stress concentration within the hydrogel microarray amplifies surface EDL-induced capacitance variation, while nano-roots anchoring enables efficient deformation transfer to the internal dendritic nano-roots, resulting in coupled surface-to-internal EDL modulation and enhanced sensitivity under ultralow pressure. The obtained hydrogel array with hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface delivers an ultrahigh sensitivity (6.0 kPa−1 at 0–2.5 kPa) with ultralow detection limits (~0.38 Pa), and stable responsiveness in the low-frequency range (50–900 Hz). When coupled with artificial intelligence-assisted signal analysis, accurate recognition and classification of low-frequency acoustic patterns are enabled, supporting intelligent sound recognition. Overall, the strategy offers a versatile platform for ultrasensitive hydrogel-based transducers and provides an applicable design principle for soft-matter sensing systems operating under weak and low-frequency stimuli.

Results

Design and mechanism of root-inspired dual-microstructural hydrogel-electrode interface

Inspired by the anchoring mechanism of plant roots, which penetrate and interlock with soil while sustaining nutrient exchange at the soil-air interface, we propose a root-inspired biomimetic strategy to achieve ultrasensitive and stable acoustic sensing. By introducing hierarchical coupling between the hydrogel’s microscale surface architectures and nanoscale metallic “roots” (Ag0 dendrites) at the hydrogel-electrode interface, a hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface is formed. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the fabrication process combines a 3D-print wax mold templating with in situ electrochemical reduction. The wax mold defines the microscale surface array of the hydrogel for gelation, while the subsequent electroreduction process induces the growth of dendritic nano-roots at the hydrogel-electrode interface. These dual-scale structures work synergistically: (i) the microscale array enhances surface deformability and stress concentration under weak pressure, and the co-molding of the hydrogel precursor with the wax-templated electrode ensures accurate monolithic micro-structuring and stable interfacial contact; (ii) the nanoscale roots provide mechanical anchoring and enhanced interfacial bonding, preventing delamination and enabling efficient charge transfer through EDL expansion. Compared with conventional assembly-based sensors or sole molding routes, this design preserves microarray integrity while maintaining effective nano-roots anchoring (Supplementary Fig. 1). This root-inspired design reconciles soft mechanical compliance with strong interfacial coupling, forming a continuous conductive network that stabilizes both mechanical and electrical responses under dynamic loading.

The dual-microstructural hydrogel array, constructed based on the root-inspired biomimetic concept, effectively overcomes the weak interfacial adhesion and poor signal transmission that limit conventional assembled hydrogel sensors (Fig. 1b). In this interlocked configuration, microarray architectures are coupled with nanoscale dendritic silver nano-roots at the hydrogel-electrode interface to form a robust hierarchical structure. The microscale pillars derived from wax templating amplify local deformation and concentrate stress under subtle stimuli, including tactile pressure, pulse, airflow, and acoustic waves, while the nanoscale silver nano-roots deeply anchor into the hydrogel matrix, expanding the electroactive area and reinforcing the interface through mechanical interlocking. Upon loading, stress focusing at the microarray surface amplifies EDL-driven capacitance responses, while nano-roots mediated anchoring facilitates deformation propagation into the internal dendritic structures, giving rise to synergistic surface-internal EDL modulation and markedly enhanced sensitivity at ultralow pressures. As a result, the sensor constructed with such a hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface achieves highly sensitive and stable signal outputs across an ultrawide pressure range from sub-Pascal to kilopascal levels. When combined with AI-assisted recognition algorithms, the sensor array can realize classification of complex acoustic patterns, demonstrating its capability for intelligent and multifunctional low-frequency sensing. Such a dual-microstructural hydrogel-electrode interface could exhibit markedly enhanced gauge factor and adhesion strength compared with both conventionally assembled and integrated counterparts, underscoring the critical role of dual-microstructural interface design (Fig. 1c).

Fabrication and structural characterizations

The dual-microstructural hydrogel-electrode interface was constructed through an intentionally sequential electrochemical process and monolithic demolding (Fig. 2a). First, a precursor hydrogel solution (AA, APS, and AgNO3) was cast into a 3D-print wax mold patterned with cubic micropillars, defining the microscale surface topography with high fidelity. Electrodes (several silver wires in this case) were laminated onto the patterned mold to maintain intimate contact during polymerization, ensuring continuous coupling between the hydrogel network and the electrode surface. After gelation under room temperature (self-triggered polymerization), an electrochemical reduction step was applied to induce the growth of dendritic nano-roots (Ag0 clusters) along the hydrogel-electrode contact regions. Finally, by fully dissolving the mold in solvent, an integrated hydrogel microarray with embedded electrodes is obtained, completing the fabrication of the dual-microstructural hydrogel-electrode interface.

Since the characteristic dimension of the hydrogel microstructure is closely associated with the sensing performance, we evaluated molds with different microhole sizes ranging from 300–700 μm (Fig. 2b, c). We found that the mold with 300 μm microholes exhibited relatively large dimensional deviations, whereas the molds with 500–700 μm microholes showed smaller fabrication errors. Therefore, a side length of 500 μm was selected to ensure reliable fabrication while maintaining an effective microstructured geometry. Quantitative measurements of three independently fabricated wax molds further showed average cavity length, width, and spacing values of 499.778 ± 2.117 μm, 497.444 ± 1.710 μm, and 1491.889 ± 3.289 μm, respectively, with low batch-to-batch coefficients of variation of 0.424%, 0.344%, and 0.220%, confirming the high printing fidelity and dimensional reproducibility of the 3D-printed wax molds for hydrogel microarray fabrication (Fig. 2d, e). The resulting microscale hydrogel micropillars exhibited excellent dimensional uniformity, providing a reliable structural basis for reproducible pressure-sensing performance (Supplementary Fig. 2).

As shown in Fig. 2f, macroscopic photographs illustrate the complete construction process of an ultrasensitive hydrogel sensor constituted by a hydrogel array with a hierarchical interface. Firstly, the hydrogel precursor solution was injected into a wax mold featuring a cubic micropillar array, while aligned metal wire electrodes were laminated onto the mold surface to maintain conformal contact before gelation (inset picture). After self-initiated gelation, electrochemical reduction, and subsequent mold dissolution, the final structure was obtained, showing an ordered hydrogel surface array where microscale geometries and nanoscale dendritic metallic features coexisted at the hydrogel-electrode interface.

To confirm and characterize that, the morphology and distribution of the nano-roots were further examined by confocal laser scanning microscopy (LSCM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Macroscopically, the integrated dual-microstructural hydrogel exhibits a well-defined cubic array composed of microscale cylindrical pillars. On each pillar, densely branched Ag dendritic clusters were formed via in situ electrochemical reduction, producing a gray-black appearance that reflected the localized deposition of metallic silver atop the patterned microarray (Fig. 2g). LSCM showed that the distribution of surface patterned micropillars of the hydrogel was spatially uniform and tunable defined by the 3D morphology of the wax mold, indicating and processibility and controllability of the wax scarifying template for generating surface microarray (Fig. 2h). Further, SEM images revealed clear dendritic nanostructures interpenetrating the patterned micropillars, while EDS confirmed their composition as metallic silver (Fig. 2i).

The efficiency of wax mold removal was evaluated by monitoring the dissolution process in the demolding agent. The residual mold area remained nearly unchanged initially, then rapidly declined, and reached complete disappearance within 180 s, confirming efficient and residue-free template removal (Supplementary Fig. 4). This rapid and high-precision wax sacrificial templating method maximizes the preservation of monolithic material integrity within the mold, which is particularly critical for fabricating complex surface microstructures (Supplementary Movie 1). In contrast, conventional mechanical demolding approaches often damage delicate features during mold separation, severely compromising structural fidelity.

Beyond planar micropatterns, the monolithic demold sacrificial strategy also demonstrates considerable potential for fabricating large-scale three-dimensional hydrogel architectures. As a proof-of-concept, we fabricated a hydrogel lampshade with a cubic framework (~1 cm) and patterned surfaces (Supplementary Fig. 5). After complete demolding, the construct retained its designed geometry and exhibited sufficient robustness to house a yellow LED bulb, which emitted consistent warm light (Supplementary Movie 2). This demonstration highlights the adaptability of such a mold-templating strategy to produce complex, freeform 3D hydrogel structures.

Interfacial adhesion and mechanical performance

The interfacial adhesion between the hydrogel and the electrode is a key determinant of sensor stability and reliability21,22. To investigate it, both 90° peeling and lap-shear tests were performed on three representative hydrogel-electrode interfacial configurations (Fig. 3a). They are denoted as (i) physically attached interface, where the hydrogel matrix is simply laminated onto the electrode surface; (ii) monolithic wax-assisted integrated interface, where the interface is formed via wax sacrificial templating of this work to ensure better conformity rather than simply physical attachment; and (iii) nano-roots anchoring interface formed by in situ electrochemical growth of dendritic metallic nano-roots.

Data are presented as mean values ± SD for n = 3 replicates. Adhesion strengths of physically attached (gray bars), integrated interfaces (light beige-gray bars), and nano-roots anchoring interfaces (light pink bars) were compared using gelatin hydrogel, EMIMTFSI-based gel, PAA hydrogel, and PVA/PAA double-network hydrogel. f Cyclic peeling test over 2000 loading-unloading cycles, demonstrating stable adhesion. g Crack extension rate (dc/dN) as a function of applied energy release rate (G = F/W) for nano-roots anchoring interface (light blue line) compared with physically attached control (black line). Linear extrapolation to the G-axis yields the fatigue threshold Γ₀. h Adhesion strength of the dual-microstructural hydrogel-electrode interface over prolonged time (gray bars), confirming interfacial stability. Quantitative analysis revealed average peeling strengths of 173.6 N m−1 for nano-roots anchoring interface, 73.7 N m−1 for wax-assisted integrated interface, and 17.9 N m−1 for physically attached counterpart. The adhesion strength of the nano-roots anchoring interface was 2.4-fold higher than that of the wax-assisted sample and 9.7-fold higher than that of the physically attached group, demonstrating the substantial improvement in interfacial bonding and toughness (Fig. 3b). The nano-roots anchoring interface also displayed optimal peeling resistance with stable load fluctuations, indicating that dendritic nano-roots provide strong mechanical anchoring and dissipate energy effectively during separation. A similar trend was observed in the shear test, during which the nano-roots anchoring interface could sustain an external load of 200 g without detachment (Fig. 3c). The corresponding force-displacement curves revealed that the nano-roots anchoring interface exhibited the highest lap-shear strength (200.8 N m−1), compared with 116.6 N m−1 for the wax-assisted integrated interface and 16.4 N m−1 for the solely physically attached counterpart (Fig. 3d). To provide a systematic comparison, the adhesion values from both peeling and shear tests are summarized (Supplementary Fig. 6). The physically attached samples exhibited the weakest adhesion due to interfacial voids during lamination. The assisted integrated design minimized these defects caused by direct mold templating, yielding improved adhesion to a certain extent. In contrast, the nano-roots anchoring interfacial architecture achieved the highest value attributed to monolithic demolding that eliminates interfacial water/air and increases intimacy23, as well as dendritic silver nano-roots that mechanically interlock, strengthening interfacial bonding. As a result, the adhesion performance was dramatically enhanced. To examine the hydrogel-dependent interfacial behavior, we further tested gelatin hydrogel, EMIMTFSI-based gel, and PVA/PAA double-network hydrogel. In all systems, the in situ integrated gel–electrode interface showed higher adhesion strength than the physically attached interface, while the enhancement magnitude varied with gel chemistry and intrinsic mechanical strength (Fig. 3e). For gelatin hydrogel, the limited difference in adhesion strength may be attributed to its relatively low intrinsic strength, as detached Ag nanowires remained covered with gelatin after separation, indicating partial cohesive failure within the gelatin matrix (Supplementary Fig. 7). For the EMIMTFSI-based gel, the absence of an obvious nano-roots structure led to limited adhesion enhancement. In contrast, the PVA/PAA double-network hydrogel showed an adhesion trend similar to that of the PAA hydrogel, likely due to its improved mechanical strength. These results suggest that electrode nanostructuring can enhance hydrogel–electrode adhesion by promoting interfacial anchoring, but the extent of improvement is sensitive to hydrogel chemistry, intrinsic gel strength, and nano-roots formation at the interface.

The durability of such a hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface was also validated by cyclic fatigue tests. Here, 2000 consecutive peeling-release cycles were applied (Fig. 3f), the nano-roots anchoring interface exhibited nearly identical force-displacement curves from the first to the last cycle, with no measurable decline in adhesion. This outstanding cyclic stability confirms that the nano-roots reinforced interface can withstand extensive mechanical loading without degradation. To further assess interfacial fracture toughness, the fatigue threshold Γ₀ was quantified by plotting crack extension rate (dc/dN) versus applied energy release rate (G) according to established protocols24. The nano-roots anchoring interface exhibited a Γ₀ of 53.9 J m−2, compared with only 1.2 J m−2 for physically attached samples, a > 40-fold enhancement (Fig. 3g). This remarkable improvement indicates that nano-roots anchoring not only increases static adhesion but also suppresses crack initiation and propagation under cyclic loading. 14-day stability was also examined by monitoring adhesion strength over prolonged time (Fig. 3h). The nano-roots anchoring interface maintained stable adhesive performance for up to 14 days without significant decline, confirming its robustness against environmental degradation. Overall, the dual-microstructural strategy comprehensively reinforces hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interfaces with strong adhesion and durability, laying the foundation for reliable ultrasensitive sensing.

Ultrasensitive sensing performance

The hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface not only enhances the interfacial interaction but also provides distinct advantages for ultrasensitive sensing. To directly compare the capacitance response of different interfacial configurations, capacitance measurements were conducted using an LCR meter at 1 kHz and 20 mV in parallel mode. As summarized in Supplementary Table 1, the plain surfaced gel without microstructures and nano-roots showed a limited ΔC of 13.76 under 2.4 kPa pressure. The physically attached interface with microstructures increased ΔC to 42.66, while the wax-assisted integrated interface further increased ΔC to 84.90. Notably, the nano-roots anchoring interface exhibited a markedly enhanced ΔC of 805.32, which was 58.53-, 18.88-, and 9.49-fold higher than those of the plate, physically attached, and wax-assisted integrated interfaces, respectively. These results directly demonstrate that nano-roots substantially amplify the loading-induced capacitance variation. Since the micropillar-patterned microarray enlarges the effective contact area, promoting deformation-induced EDL capacitance changes, the dendritic nano-roots could further enable efficient charge redistribution and EDL capacitance under loading. Moreover, simulations comparing the flat root structure and high-aspect-ratio microstructured root structure show that the microstructured architecture promotes greater internal root-spacing separation under deformation, supporting stronger surface-to-internal EDL modulation (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Movie 3). Together with the added capacitance measurements and plain-surface gel sensing data, these simulation results clarify that the enhanced capacitance sensitivity of the hierarchical sensor originates from the synergistic effect of microscale structural deformation and nano-root-assisted EDL modulation (Supplementary Fig. 9).

To be specific, upon external loading, the microarray architecture induces pronounced stress concentration at the hydrogel surface, allowing even ultralow pressures to trigger appreciable local deformation. This stress localization effectively amplifies deformation-induced variations in the interfacial contact area, thereby enhancing modulation of the surface electrical double layer (EDL) capacitance. As further described by the EDL-based transduction framework in Supplementary Note 1, the capacitance response is governed by the pressure-induced relative change in the effective ion-accessible EDL-active area and the possible local ion-concentration variation near the electrode/hydrogel interface. Moreover, the micropillar array amplifies the local mechanical input through its low projected area fraction, thereby coupling microscale stress concentration with nano-root-assisted EDL modulation. In parallel, the nano-roots anchored hydrogel-electrode interface provides robust mechanical interlocking, which facilitates efficient transmission of the applied deformation from the surface microstructures into the internal dendritic nano-roots network. As a result, deformation is not confined to the surface but propagates into the bulk interfacial region, activating additional EDL modulation pathways at the embedded nano-roots interfaces. The cooperative engagement of surface microarray-driven stress amplification and internal nano-root-mediated deformation transfer gives rise to a coupled surface-to-internal EDL response, substantially enhancing signal output and sensitivity in the ultralow-pressure regime. This multiscale synergy greatly amplifies the electromechanical transduction efficiency, laying the foundation for superior pressure responsiveness across a wide dynamic range.

To systematically evaluate that, we employed a capacitance meter coupled with a universal testing machine to record real-time capacitance variations under applied pressures. The gauge factor (GF), defined as GF = (ΔC/C0)/P, was used to quantify sensitivity. As shown in Fig. 4a, the dual-microstructural sensor exhibited the highest sensitivity across a broad pressure range (0–20 kPa), with three distinct linear regimes (R2 ≥ 0.98): GF = 6.0 kPa−1 (0–2.5 kPa), 3.9 kPa−1 (2.5–7.5 kPa), and 1.3 kPa−1 (7.5–20 kPa). In the ultralow-pressure region (0–2.5 kPa), its GF was nearly four times higher than the only wax-assisted sensor and over 200 times greater than the traditionally attached sensors. This outstanding performance arises from a synergistic mechanism. When the microarray deforms under load, it amplifies local contact deformation and promotes surface EDL compression, thereby enhancing the pressure-induced capacitance variation. Meanwhile, dendritic nano-roots embedded at the hydrogel-electrode interface provide multiple internal EDL-active sites, where pressure-induced ion redistribution around the nano-root branches further contributes to capacitance modulation (Fig. 4b, i). As a result, the hierarchical interface enables coupled surface/internal EDL modulation, leading to markedly amplified sensing responses under weak pressure. In contrast, the interface without nano-roots mainly relies on surface EDL modulation at the hydrogel-electrode contact region, with limited internal EDL-active sites and weaker ion-redistribution-induced capacitance changes under pressure (Fig. 4b, ii). To quantify that, the sensor of hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface exhibited stable capacitance responses under varying pressures (Fig. 4c) and showed ultrafast response and recovery times within 100 ms at 10 Pa (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 10).

Remarkably, such a sensor was capable of detecting subtle weak pressures. Based on the actual pressure-response data, the limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 0.38 Pa using the conventional 3σ criterion (Supplementary Note 2). To further demonstrate its capability for acoustic-signal perception, when a loudspeaker generated vibrations across 50–900 Hz, the assembled sensor produced distinct capacitance fluctuations under acoustic stimulation (Supplementary Movie 4), as further validated by real-time dB meter tracking (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 11). It should be noted that the capacitance signals in the acoustic sensing experiments were recorded with a sampling interval of 10 ms per point, corresponding to a sampling frequency of 100 Hz under the stable LCR measurement conditions used in this work. Therefore, these measurements demonstrate detectable capacitance responses under acoustic excitation from 50 to 900 Hz, but the current acquisition setup is limited for cycle-by-cycle or high-fidelity reconstruction of the original acoustic waveforms, especially at higher acoustic frequencies. Future implementation with higher-bandwidth capacitance acquisition will be needed for faithful acoustic waveform reconstruction. Beyond acoustic waves, the device also responded to ultra-low pressures in the form of weak airflow and landing of a lightweight leaf (0.02 g), as well as maintaining reliable sensing capability under large deformation,, such as finger pressing (Supplementary Figs. 12–14). These results demonstrated that the ultrasensitive hydrogel sensor has a broad detection range spanning from sub-Pascal-level stimuli to kilopascal-level mechanical loading.

For ultrasensitive sensors, maintaining stable and drift-free signals over extended use is particularly critical, as even minor interfacial degradation or mechanical fatigue can result in pronounced fluctuations in output, especially in applications such as wearable monitoring, biomedical diagnostics, and environmental sensing. Here, the stability and durability of the dual-microstructural sensor were validated by cyclic tests. Under 1000 continuous loading-unloading cycles at 1 kPa, the capacitance signals remained highly consistent without noticeable degradation, confirming their excellent repeatability and stability (Fig. 4f). During sensor testing, a thin layer of silicone oil was applied onto the hydrogel surface to reduce direct contact with ambient air, thereby suppressing water evaporation without significantly affecting the mechanical deformability of the sensor. After testing, the sensor was sealed in a plastic storage bag and stored away from light to further minimize water loss and environmental disturbance.

Comprehensively, the ultrasensitive hydrogel sensor achieves a rare combination of high sensitivity, ultrafast response, broad detection range, and outstanding stability attributed to the hierarchically engineered hydrogel-electrode interface, surpassing the performance of previously reported hydrogel-based pressure sensors. To contextualize these advances, we compared our device with state-of-the-art hydrogel sensors reported in the literature (Supplementary Table 2). When benchmarked against reported gel-based pressure sensors, our hierarchical hydrogel-electrode sensor exhibits a well-balanced overall performance, combining high sensitivity, an ultralow detection limit, strong interfacial adhesion, a high fatigue threshold, and excellent cycling stability, thereby highlighting its potential as a robust and versatile platform for soft mechano-sensing applications (Fig. 4g). These results demonstrate that the ultrasensitive sensor with hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface holds significant potential for diverse practical applications, ranging from wearable health monitoring and human-machine interfaces to environmental surveillance, and even extending to acoustic and underwater sonar sensing.

AI-assisted acoustic recognition and interpretation

Building on the demonstrated capability of the ultrasensitive hydrogel sensor, we further advanced its application toward intelligent sound recognition to exploit its potential in information acquisition and human-machine interaction. To clarify whether the acoustic information used for intelligent recognition was enabled by the hierarchical interfacial design, we first compared the sound-induced responses of sensors with different interfacial configurations under the same acoustic source (Supplementary Fig. 15). A plain-surface gel was additionally tested as a control to evaluate the role of the microstructured interface. This control sample showed almost no detectable response under acoustic excitation, with the signal remaining nearly identical to the baseline. The physically attached interface produced only a slight response, and the wax-assisted integrated interface showed a weak but distinguishable response. In contrast, the nano-roots anchoring interface exhibited the strongest acoustic response, with the largest signal deviation from the baseline, confirming that the hierarchical interface substantially enhanced acoustic signal transduction and generated more distinguishable signal features for subsequent ML analysis.

As illustrated in Fig. 5a, acoustic signals emitted from a loudspeaker were transduced into capacitance signals by the hydrogel sensor, subsequently processed by machine learning (ML) algorithms, and ultimately classified into distinct categories. Representative time-domain capacitance traces of six musical tracks (Hai Kuo Tian Kong, Take me home, Aloha heja he, Hélène Rollès, España Cañí, and Lemon) revealed distinct temporal capacitance signatures over each 35 s sampling trial (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Movie 5). In total, 294 trials (49 per song) were collected.

To enable model training compatible with the ~6 s temporal resolution commonly used in acoustic-recognition systems, the raw capacitance recordings were segmented using a sliding-window approach. Each 35 s trace was trimmed to 30 s and divided into 6 s windows with a 1 s stride, yielding 25 sliding windows per recording. These windows were subsequently converted into low-dimensional feature vectors through a combined extraction pipeline that integrates custom time-frequency descriptors with advanced audio features (e.g., Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients and chroma components) from the librosa library. This preprocessing framework provides a compact yet information-rich dataset suitable for robust machine-learning analysis.

To visualize the separability of the collected signals, dimensionality reduction was performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), which projected the high-dimensional capacitance data into two dimensions (Fig. 5c). Distinct clustering of the six categories was observed, confirming the discriminability of the hydrogel-derived signals. Consistently, the unsupervised K-Means clustering algorithm achieved an average classification accuracy of 93.5%, as reflected in the confusion matrix (Fig. 5d). This result highlights the intrinsic quality of the raw signals, which are sufficiently distinctive for robust recognition even with a simple unsupervised model.

To further assess whether the recognition accuracy is contingent on the size of the training set, we performed a learning-curve analysis under a strict temporal-isolation cross-validation protocol in which training and testing windows of every recording were drawn from non-overlapping time segments separated by a buffer of one window length, so that no training and testing windows ever shared a single time sample (Supplementary Note 3). Five common supervised classifiers were evaluated under this protocol: artificial neural networks (ANN), support vector machines (SVM), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), random forest (RF), and extra-trees (ET). Across a nine-fold range of training-set sizes spanning 294 to 2,646 samples, all five classifiers maintained a macro-F1 above 0.993 and exhibited a monotonically increasing learning (Supplementary Fig. 16). Notably, all supervised models achieved recognition accuracy above 99.89% (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 17), demonstrating that the hydrogel sensor outputs are of such high fidelity that classification accuracy is minimally dependent on the specific ML architecture employed.

Given that all five classifiers achieved comparably high macro‑F1 scores (>0.998) at the sufficient training set, model choice should be guided by cost rather than accuracy. On the same commodity CPU workstation (Supplementary Note 3), the five architectures showed markedly distinct computational profiles spanning over three orders of magnitude in per‑sample latency (Supplementary Fig. 18). The fully‑connected neural network delivered the fastest inference (5.9 µs per sample) but the highest training cost (~1.1 ms per sample). In contrast, k‑nearest‑neighbor required negligible training time (~2.9 µs per sample) yet was among the slowest at inference (~127 µs per sample). Tree‑based ensembles (RF and ET) displayed decreasing per‑sample cost as the training set grew, offering a balanced train‑once–deploy‑many‑times trade‑off. The support‑vector machine was cheap to train, though its latency increased modestly with dataset size. Therefore, for real‑time, high‑throughput inference (e.g., on‑device acoustic recognition), the fully‑connected network is most favorable, while for rapid retraining on new sound categories, k‑nearest‑neighbor or SVM are more economical.

These results demonstrate that the ultrasensitive hydrogel sensor, when coupled with ML algorithms, enables high-precision and low-latency sound recognition. Beyond traditional pressure and acoustic detection, this intelligent sensing framework is potentially applicable to voice and music recognition, adaptive human-machine interfaces, and underwater acoustic communication.

Although the Ag-based nano-roots interface effectively enhances interfacial anchoring and EDL modulation in the present proof-of-concept devices, Ag usage, material cost, and scalability should be considered for large-area, high-density, or disposable sensing applications. In the current device, dendritic Ag nano-roots are generated through localized redox/electrochemical reduction of AgNO3 and are mainly confined to the wire electrodes and hydrogel-electrode interfaces, so the Ag consumption is relatively limited at the small-device scale. For future scale-up, reducing Ag loading through patterned electrochemical growth, Ag-coated low-cost conductive substrates, lower AgNO3 usage, or alternative electrode/ion-precursor chemistries may improve practical manufacturability while preserving controlled nano-roots formation. These directions will be important for translating the current hierarchical interface strategy toward broader practical applications.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a root-inspired hydrogel-electrode interface that integrates metallic nano-roots and microarray architectures within a unified fabrication framework, enabling a robust and ultrasensitive hydrogel sensor. This hierarchical hydrogel-electrode interface exhibited strong interfacial adhesion, high fatigue tolerance, and superior EDL-based transduction, collectively supporting an ultralow detection limit (~0.38 Pa), high sensitivity in the low-pressure regime, and broadband acoustic responsiveness (50–900 Hz). Leveraging these capabilities, the system could support reliable detection of subtle pressure fluctuations and low-frequency acoustic signals, as well as accurate, high-fidelity acoustic recognition through machine-learning-assisted analysis.

This strategy offered a conceptually facile yet powerful approach for constructing mechanically reinforced and structurally precise hydrogel-electrode interfaces. By integrating electrochemical-generated nano-roots anchoring with template-assisted monolithic structuring, this strategy overcame long-standing challenges in hydrogel sensor engineering, including weak interfacial bonding, microstructure fragility, and signal drift under cyclic loading. Compared with conventional approaches that typically rely on either post-assembly lamination or single-scale patterning, this work provided simultaneous interfacial strengthening and structural definition. This strategy would offer a versatile approach for engineering reliable, ultrasensitive hydrogel-based sensors.

Methods

Materials

Unless otherwise noted, all chemicals and solvents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. Acrylic acid (AA, 99%) and ammonium persulfate (APS, ≥98%) were obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Silver nitrate (AgNO3, ≥99%) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Wax models (CAST:M2) and demolding agent (DE:M2) were obtained from Jinan GalaxyBio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China). Deionized water was used throughout all experiments.

Preparation of microarrayed hydrogel-electrode integration

A precursor solution was prepared by dissolving AA (2.00 g), APS (0.012 g), and AgNO3 (0.034 g) in deionized water (8.00 g) under stirring. The solution was poured into 3D-printed wax molds embedded with electrodes and allowed to form hydrogel at room temperature through the APS/AgNO3 redox process.

Formation of nano-roots at the hydrogel-electrode interface

Electrochemical reduction was carried out by applying a DC bias (3.0 V, 30 s) across the electrode-hydrogel interface using a regulated DC power supply. Ag+ ions were reduced in situ to dendritic Ag0 nanostructures, forming nano-roots architectures at the hydrogel-electrode interface.

Demolding

The whole mold containing the assembled hierarchical hydrogel was immersed in a 65 °C demolding bath containing DE:M2. The wax template was completely dissolved, yielding an integrated hydrogel sensor with dendritic silver nano-roots and preserved microarray architectures.

Characterizations

The morphologies of hydrogels and interfacial nanostructures were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4800, Japan) and laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM, Carl Zeiss LSM 800, Germany). Elemental composition was analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, attached to SEM). Optical microscopy (Olympus BX53, Japan) was used to image hydrogel micropillars.

Measurement of interfacial adhesion performance

Interfacial adhesion was characterized using a universal tensile testing machine (SanSi, China; 500 N load cell). For 90° peeling tests, the interfacial toughness (Γ) was calculated as Γ = Fs/W, where Fs is the steady-state peeling force, and W is the effective width (half perimeter of Ag wire, 0.2 mm diameter). Lap-shear strength was measured under shear loading until failure. Cyclic 90° peeling tests were performed under force-control mode to evaluate fatigue resistance. A cyclic peeling force Fa (Fa < Fs) was applied for over N cycles, and crack extension (c) was recorded. The crack growth rate (dc/dN) versus applied energy release rate (G = Fa/W) was plotted, and linear extrapolation to the x-axis gave the fatigue threshold (Γ0).

Ultrasensitive sensing measurements

The capacitance measurements in this work were performed using an LCR meter (Keysight E4980A, USA) under fixed conditions of 1 kHz and 20 mV in parallel mode, following the testing configuration reported for hydrogel-based capacitive systems10. Capacitance changes (ΔC/C0) were recorded using the LCR meter coupled with a universal testing machine (Instron 5565, USA) and exported to a LabVIEW program for synchronized pressure application. The gauge factor (GF) was defined as GF = (ΔC/C0)/P. Pressure-dependent sensing tests were conducted over the range of 0–20 kPa. Response and recovery times were evaluated by applying and releasing 10 Pa step pressures. Durability was tested under 1000 cyclic loading–unloading cycles at 1 kPa.

Acoustic sensing tests

Acoustic responsiveness was evaluated using a loudspeaker (AIRS 4 Ohm Super Bass, AIRS Loudspeaker Co., China) generating sound waves in the frequency range of 50–900 Hz. The loudspeaker was placed at a fixed distance of 30 cm from the hydrogel sensor, while the sound pressure level was monitored with a digital sound level meter. Capacitance signals were simultaneously recorded with the LCR meter. Additional low-pressure responsiveness was examined using weak airflow and the landing of lightweight objects (leaf, 0.02 g).

AI-assisted analysis for acoustic recognition

A total of 294 acoustic datasets were collected from six representative songs. Data were preprocessed in Python 3.8 using scikit-learn 1.2, and dimensionality reduction was performed using t-SNE. Unsupervised K-Means clustering algorithm achieved ~93.5% accuracy, while supervised models, including ANN, SVM, KNN, RF, and ET, were trained on a 50/50 split of the dataset, all yielding accuracies above 99.7% with high precision, recall, and F1 scores.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard deviation. Exact sample sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends and Source Data file.
