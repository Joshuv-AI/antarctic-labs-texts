Covalent Organic Frameworks with 2‑Fold Functional Pores Produced from a Monofunctional Monomer as Predicted by Computational Methods
Authors: Michael J. Wenzel, Alathea E. Davies, Alexander K. Goroncy, Laura de Sousa Oliveira, John O. Hoberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Manipulating pore groups in covalent organic frameworks (COFs) changes the function of the material while also modifying pore size. These groups are often assumed based on monomer structure; however, common characterization techniques can be inadequate in the verification of these pore groups. Computational modeling has the ability to predict and illustrate the potential errors that may arise within these COF structures. We report herein a side reaction in two synthesized COFs that was identified through simulation data and confirmed through a variety of techniques. The synthetic conditions and mechanistic rationale leading to this side reaction can be rationalized with the use of computational insight, which proves to be a powerful tool in providing insight into these polymeric materials. As a result, a COF with two different functional groups within the pore has been constructed from a monofunctionalized monomer.

Introduction

Predicting the outcomes of organic reactions has been a long-standing goal within the computational chemistry community, and fundamental problems are still being addressed. The vast amount of effort has been directed toward small molecule synthesis in which the resulting products are often below 1000 Da. Fortunately, these efforts have been aided by the ease of characterization of the products. The characterization of small organic molecules has become an exact science in which the use of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), mass spectroscopy (MS), Infrared spectroscopy (IR), and X-ray crystallography can definitively assign the structure, even establishing relative stereochemistry with ease. However, in the case of polymers, and specifically two-dimensional (2D) polymers, these same instruments are of limited use for characterization, as characterization typically focuses on material crystallinity, morphology, and architectural determination. A common characterization tool used in 2D polymers is powder X-ray diffraction (pXRD), which provides information on bulk materials but not specifically bonded atoms. Furthermore, predicting the structure of side reactions in 2D polymer formation is fraught with difficulty and often overlooked. , This difficulty is tied to the above characterization of the polymer in which the synthetic chemist is unable to detect minor reaction pathways. Therefore, predicting side reactions via computational methods would greatly enhance the final understanding and predict new polymeric compositions. We report herein such a case in which, guided by computational results, the synthesis and characterization of two new structures of 2D materials were obtained.

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are porous networks composed of organic building blocks linked together through covalent bonds, resulting in extended periodic structures. − They have emerged as a versatile class of porous materials with tunable structures and functionalities, making them attractive candidates for various applications. Their unique feature lies in their modularity, enabling precise tailoring of properties through rational design and synthesis. One area where COFs have shown promising potential is in the development of advanced separation membranes. − Traditional separation membrane technologies, such as polymer-based membranes, often face limitations in terms of selectivity, permeability, and stability. COFs as separation membranes offer several advantages compared to these traditional materials. Their interconnected pore networks allow for the separation of molecules based on size, shape, and chemical affinity, leading to enhanced selectivity. Additionally, their crystalline nature and covalent linkages ensure long-term stability and resistance to harsh chemical environments. An example that we previously reported is PzPy-pa (Figure (left structure)), which involves the condensation of the amines with ketones, i.e., a Schiff base reaction. Thus, reaction of the Phenazene 1, where R = propiolic acids (pa), with the Pyrene tetraone 2 produced highly ordered materials. This COF was used in a variety of separations that demonstrated its utility as a size and ion-selective membrane. − Furthermore, the permeance exhibited by this COF far exceeded most reported materials. We therefore targeted a modification of this material in which a decrease in the size of the pore could be accomplished using hexaketotriphenylene 3 to produce PzTp-pa. At the start of these studies, it was predicted by computational methods that a decarboxylation would likely occur and report herein the realization of this prediction.

Results and Discussion

A typical strategy to manipulate pore size is to modify the symmetry of the reactants. In the case of PzPy-pa, the 3-fold symmetric monomer 1 was reacted with the 2-fold symmetric monomer 2. Alternatively, to decrease pore sizes, the use of two 3-fold symmetric reactants is usually performed, which was employed in this study. To this end, amine 1 was reacted with hexaketotriphenylene 3 to produce PzTp-pa as illustrated in Figure . Hexaketo 3 has been previously used in COF formation by Coskun and co-workers, along with decorated variants of 3; thus, precedence existed for successful reaction. While these reports used extremely high solvothermal temperatures of 400 °C, we found that the same microwave conditions previously employed for the synthesis of PzPy were also successful for the formation of PzTp. Thus, microwave heating of 1 and 3 at 80 °C for 3 h in a mesitylene/acetic acid mixture produced the expected black solids. A variety of conditions were used that involved acid-catalyzed reactions with acetic acid solutions and base-catalyzed reactions using KOH solutions. Typically, acid-catalyzed conditions are used to form these pyrazine-type COFs; however, a recent report illustrated that basic conditions noticeably improved crystallinity. Thus, these basic conditions were also investigated. Temperatures up to 200 °C were tolerated and produced good yields, but 80 °C was again found to be optimal.

Characterization of the new material was initially performed using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Notably, the disappearance of the carbonyl stretch (1671 cm–1) in 3 with the appearance of the imine stretch (1607 cm–1) in the COF indicated the formation of PzTp (Figures S3 and S4a). Additionally, a broad OH stretch from 3100 to 2500 cm–1 confirmed the expected carboxyl H-bonded OH. Weak 2970 cm–1 CH stretches for the aromatic bands were also observable.

Next, powder X-ray diffraction (pXRD) was performed to evaluate the crystallinity of the materials (Figure a). Most often, COF-forming reactions involve only a single synthetic step (i.e., imine formation from an aldehyde with an amine); however, the formation of PzTp involves two Schiff base reactions that lead to the formation of an aromatic pyrazine moiety, and consequently, reversibility is extremely unlikely to occur. Thus, narrow signals are difficult to acquire in these systems, ,, and “broad 001 suggest the particles are highly ordered in-plane but disordered along the stacking dimension implying the potential for multiple or disordered stacking order.” The resulting pXRD of PzTp is illustrated in blue and, as expected, produced sharp 100 and broad 001 peaks. Crystal structural simulations for a periodic system (orange) using a slipped AA-stacking unit cell confirmed that order in the system was localized and polycrystalline. Pore size distributions for the acid-catalyzed reaction (Figure b,c) were obtained and indicated a 15 Å diameter, which was within the expected size. PzTp, with its nonuniform pore, produced predictable experimental distribution and nitrogen isotherms that matched other very similar types of COFs. , High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) illustrated hexagonal flakes (Figure e,f) with noticeable lattice fringes displaying excellent long-range order in Figure d and a measured angle of 124.5° between edges as highlighted in Figure e. Thus, given the similarity of PzTp to our previous PzPy and other similar pyrazine COFs, ,− formation was deemed conclusive.

COFs PzPy and PzTp were simulated with self-consistent charge density functional tight-binding (SCC-DFTB) as implemented in the DFTB+ software package. The 3OB-3–1 parameter set − was utilized along with van der Waals and hydrogen bonding corrections. A (protonated) monolayer of each COF was initially fully optimized. A 4 × 4 × 1 k-point mesh was used to calculate the self-consistent charges to a tolerance of 1 × 10–5 eV, and the atomic forces were minimized to a tolerance of 1 × 10–4 eV Å–1. The lattice constants for each monolayer are included in Table S1. After the monolayers were fully optimized, they were placed in water to model the acid-catalyzed (system 1) and base-catalyzed (system 2) COFs. Using PACKMOL, a 20 Å box of water, with a density equal to that of H2O at 298 K, was placed above each monolayer. In the base-catalyzed systems, the propiolic acid groups were all deprotonated, and an equivalent number of Na+ ions was added to balance the charges. A Γ-centered minimization was performed on each system. An electronic (Fermi) temperature of 300 K was used to smear the electron occupations. The optimal geometries predicted were of partially decarboxylated frameworks.

Within the modeled cells of PzPy and PzTp, there are two and one explicitly modeled pores, respectively (see Figure S5 for diagram and explanation). In the acid-catalyzed system (1) for both COFs, all carboxylic acid functional groups within the pores were kept in the protonated form (Figures S6a and S7a). In the base-catalyzed system (2), all functional groups were deprotonated into carboxylates (Figures S6c and S7c). Decarboxylation was observed in both PzPy and PzTp for the carboxylate systems, i.e., when a COF-forming synthesis was performed in basic conditions. COF formation modeled in acidic conditions, i.e., system 1, did not result in decarboxylations. In these calculations, we observed two decarboxylations per unit cell. In subsequent structural relaxation calculations, the presence of water and the Fermi temperature were found to influence the number of decarboxylation events. Additional detail is offered in the Supporting Information. We attribute the degradation of the functional groups to the steric and repulsive electrostatic interactions that result from the proximity of pairs of propiolic carboxylates on the same node. This is supported by the observation that in both COF geometries modeled, there was no instance in which both functional groups on the same node decarboxylated, as illustrated in Figure , in which the node is represented in yellow. Subsequently, an additional system similar to PzTp, in which the alkyne linking the CO2H group to the COF backbone was removed, was modeled to benchmark the SCC-DFTB model in predicting the decarboxylation side reaction. In this COF, labeled as PzTp-M in Figure S8a, the CO2H groups within the pore were deprotonated and, as expected, no decarboxylation was observed.

Structures of (a) PzTp and (b) PzPy formed using basic conditions as predicted via simulations, illustrating the loss of CO2 groups to produce dual-function pores.

Very brief semiempirical molecular dynamics simulations, done with SCC-DFTB, were conducted on the decarboxylated PzTp system to see how it evolved after decarboxylation. In Figure S10, screenshots show proton exchange occurring between H2O atoms and the terminal carbanion atom on the functional group. This is expected in the evolution of this specific system after decarboxylation, further suggesting that the SCC-DFTB model is reliable in predicting the results of this side reaction.

With computational results indicating a decarboxylation would occur, acid–base titration of PzTp was performed to quantify the level of carboxylic acid groups in the pores. Titrations were performed on both the acid- (Figure , top) and base-catalyzed (Figure , middle) COF-forming reactions using our previous protocol. A sample of PzTp was sonicated with NaOH to produce the sodium carboxylate and to disperse the individual sheets, followed by back-titration with HCl. The titration consists of two steps in which HCl first reacts with excess NaOH (Step 1, blue zone), and subsequently, HCl reacts with the COF-COONa (Step 2, green zone). The quantity of carboxylate groups is calculated by the consumption of the standardized HCl in Step 2, and in the case of the acid-catalyzed COF-forming reactions, it was found to be in excellent agreement (6.0) with the theoretical structure of six carboxylic acid groups (Table S2 and Figure S12). However, in the case of the base-catalyzed reactions, a value of a maximum of four carboxyl groups was observed for any given titration (Table S2 and Figure S13). These results confirm the simulations in which base-catalyzed reactions result in the loss of carboxylic acid moieties. Visually, Figure illustrates the noticeable difference in the green zones between the middle and top experiments, showing the loss of carboxylic groups in the base-catalyzed reactions that result in decarboxylation. It should be noted that protonation of the imine moieties in the COF does not occur due to the very low pK a’s of pyrazine nitrogens (pK a = 1.2).

Next, PzPy was investigated to verify the computational predictions. The initial report on the formation of PzPy used only acidic conditions, and in that report, titration confirmed that all carboxyl groups remained in place as predicted in this study. For this analysis, formation of PzPy was performed using the same basic conditions as PzTp. Upon the formation of PzPy, titration curves were obtained (Table S2 and Figure S14) and, as predicted from the above calculations, also resulted in decarboxylations to produce a pore with only six of the 12 carboxylic acid groups intact (titration results shown in Figure bottom with the proposed structure in Figure ).

Additional evidence for decarboxylation in PzTp was obtained from thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Figures S15 and 16), in which noticeable discrepancies were observed. First, the initial weight loss of water from PzTp indicated a different percentage of solvent from the 6 CO2H pore compared to the 3–4 CO2H pore. This is likely due to the increased hygroscopic nature of the additional carboxylate groups within the pore, increasing hydrophobicity over the 3–4 CO2H pore of PzTp fabricated under basic conditions with its terminal alkyne moieties. The second divergence was seen at approximately 325 °C, in which a significant loss was observed with the 6 CO2H PzTp but absent in the 3–4 CO2H COF. This can be attributed to the onset of material degradation, occurring at functional moieties prior to breakdown of the more rigid and robust framework backbone. Finally, a solid-state 1H NMR was obtained of PzTp synthesized under basic conditions to confirm the presence of terminal alkyne protons. Multiple signals were observed in the alkyne region between 2.2 and 3.4, with the 2.2 signal being the most intense (Figure S17). Given that four neighboring carboxyl groups in the pore would greatly increase shielding, an upfield shift of the alkyne proton signal was not surprising.

Mechanistically, it appears that decarboxylation occurs to produce alkyne anions, which upon aqueous workup are protonated to produce terminal alkynes. Not surprisingly, the highly negative nature of a pore in these basic conditions is promoting decarboxylation to reduce electronic repulsion in the COF pore. This also appears to be distinctive to the COF, as monomer 1 was subjected to the same basic conditions as used in the COF-forming reactions, and no decarboxylation was observed.

Conclusions

In almost all reports of COF synthesis, the structure of the bulk sample is often reported with excellent details of the framework; however, side reactions in the 2D polymer formation can be overlooked due to the difficulty in characterization of these finite details. For example, the pore distribution difference between the six CO2H (15.1 Å) and three CO2H (15.9 Å) PzTp is only marginally different (Figure S18), which can be easily overlooked. Thus, predicting side reactions via computational methods, as illustrated herein, has made possible a complete characterization of the pore structure of these COFs. As is known, one of the challenges with ab initio methods is the limitation on system size. Solving the Hamiltonian becomes exponentially more computationally expensive as the system size grows. This has resulted in a wide use of classical molecular dynamics for investigating the interactions between frameworks and water. − Many of these studies also assume framework rigidity, allowing for faster simulations. However, studies indicate that framework flexibility can significantly impact water interactions with the framework. ,, Self-consistent charge density functional tight-binding (SCC-DFTB) is a faster alternative to traditional density functional theory for modeling larger systems (semiempirically) and incorporating framework flexibility. This enables researchers to achieve better confidence in pore structure. Finally, using different reaction conditions, the pore structure has been varied between all carboxyl pore vs mixed terminal alkyne/carboxyl pores in this new COF.

Materials and Methods

Polymerization Reactions

PzTp-pa, 6 CO2H groups per pore: Hexamine 1 (124 mg, 0.106 mmol) was dissolved in a 25% acetic acid solution (2 mL) in a 10 mL reaction vessel. Hexaketotriphenylene 3 (120 mg, 0.377 mmol) was dissolved in mesitylene (2 mL) in a separate flask and then pipetted onto the AcOH solution. The vessel was purged with argon for 5 min, capped, and heated in the microwave at 80 °C for 3 h. The resulting black solid was Soxhlet extracted with water (24 h), then EtOH (24 h), and dried using supercritical CO2 to give a solid (93 mg, 75% yield).

PzTp-pa, Decarboxylation Route: A 10 mL microwave reaction vessel was purged with argon and loaded with hexamine 1 (146 mg, 0.124 mmol), hexaketonetriphenylene 3 (37.5 mg, 0.118 mmol), KOH (0.5 mL, 4 M), and deionized water (5 mL). The vessel was capped and heated in a microwave oven at 80 °C for 2h. The solution was filtered, Soxhlet extracted with deionized water (24 h), then EtOH (24 h), and dried using supercritical CO2 to yield a black solid (83 mg, 57% yield).

PzPy-pa, Decarboxylation Route: The above conditions in the decarboxylation of PzTp were used with hexamine 1 (99.4 mg, 0.084 mmol), 4,5,9,10-pyrenetetrone (22.6 mg, 0.0862 mmol), KOH (0.33 mL, 4 M), and deionized water (3.25 mL).

Computational Methods

All COFs were modeled using the self-consistent charge density functional tight-binding (SCC-DFTB) approach implemented through the package DFTB+. The minimized lattice constants, angles, and total number of water molecules in each simulation are included in Table S1. For the lattice constants, a and b within the protonated and deprotonated systems are equal, and the lattice constant in c is indicated for the protonated (deprotonated) systems. Semiempirical molecular dynamics were performed with DFTB+ and included the same corrections described in the main paper. An NPT ensemble was employed at 300 K and 1 atm. The previously described simulations were conducted with a minimized monolayer of the protonated COFs. A minimization of the deprotonated COF PzTp was conducted to investigate the necessity for the simulation to include water and the effect of its presence on the decarboxylation. Additional groups decarboxylated (4 per pore) at the same Fermi temperature (300 K) without the presence of water. When the Fermi temperature is reduced to 10 K, 2 groups per pore were decarboxylated. While a molecular dynamics calculation of the solvated framework is more representative of the synthetic conditionsand shows proton exchange between neighboring water and the terminal negatively charged C (Figure S10)the fact that the decarboxylation readily occurs during a structural minimization highlights the likelihood of this side reaction. Total energies for individual decarboxylation events are given in Table S1. Associated images are given in Figure S11. The simulated powder diffraction pattern was calculated with the solid-state software VESTA.
