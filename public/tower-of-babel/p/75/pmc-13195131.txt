Short video addiction is associated with students’ academic disengagement: a moderated mediation analysis
Authors: Yantao Shi, Xueli Hui, Guanghai Li, Yuanchang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mobile short-video addiction has been linked to students’ academic disengagement, yet the mediating and moderating mechanisms underlying this association remain insufficiently understood. This study examined whether self-control mediates the relationship between mobile short-video addiction and academic disengagement and whether psychological resilience moderates both the direct and indirect pathways (i.e., a moderated mediation model). Using a convenience sample of 2,687 vocational college students enrolled in preschool teacher-education programs in Guangxi, China (Mage = 19.280, SD = 1.206), we assessed mobile short-video addiction, academic disengagement, self-control, and psychological resilience and tested the conditional process model. Results showed that mobile short-video addiction was positively associated with academic disengagement, and this association was partially mediated by reduced self-control. Psychological resilience further exhibited dual-path moderation: it strengthened the negative association between addiction symptoms and self-control and amplified the protective association between self-control and academic disengagement. Correspondingly, both pathway effects were weaker in the low-resilience group than in the high-resilience group. By demonstrating a context-sensitive pattern of resilience-related resources in a vocational preschool teacher-education cohort, this study extends existing evidence in vocational education settings and offers theoretical and practical implications for interventions aimed at reducing short-video addiction and enhancing self-regulation and academic engagement.

Introduction

Smartphones have profoundly reshaped everyday lifestyles. As of June 2024, the number of Chinese internet users accessing the internet via smartphones reached 1.09967 billion (CNNIC, 2024), and the associated problem of mobile short-video addiction has become increasingly salient1. Prior research defines short-video addiction as excessive involvement in short-video applications2,3. Such involvement may substantially disrupt daily functioning and contribute to psychological and behavioral problems, manifesting as compulsive overuse of short-video content, impairments in daily life and social interaction, and elevated mental health risks such as anxiety and depression4–7. In the present study, mobile short-video addiction is conceptualized as an excessive and difficult-to-control pattern of use accompanied by addiction-like symptoms and functional impairment, including four symptom dimensions assessed by the MSVAS: withdrawal reactions, escapist use, loss of control, and reduced efficiency. Importantly, short-video addiction should be distinguished from non-addictive high-frequency use: the latter refers to relatively frequent or prolonged viewing that remains voluntary and controllable and is not accompanied by core addiction symptoms. Accordingly, this study operationalizes short-video addiction propensity using symptom-based indicators rather than using duration or frequency of use as diagnostic criteria.

Among users of short-video applications, vocational college students majoring in preschool education may be particularly susceptible to the influence of mobile short-video use. Compared with other vocational student groups, preschool-education majors often exhibit relatively homogeneous demographic profiles across training contexts (e.g., a higher proportion of females)8–10, and they are in a sensitive period of emotional and psychological development11. Their professional identity is not yet fully consolidated12, making them more vulnerable to platform-driven influences on self-concept and sense of vocational goal direction13–15. Meanwhile, the entertainment-oriented and idealized nature of short-video content may disrupt their professional values and self-perceptions, thereby eliciting cognitive dissonance and anxiety16,17. In addition, preschool education training typically requires students to complete a large volume of contextualized practicum tasks within relatively short time frames. These tasks demand sustained attention, deep processing, and ongoing self-monitoring. During practicum and interpersonal interactions, students also need to frequently regulate emotions and manage emotional expression, thus bearing higher levels of emotional labor. Moreover, short-video use may function as an avoidant coping strategy, exacerbating conflicts between academic demands and entertainment needs18–20, undermining academic motivation and self-regulation21, and ultimately posing risks to academic performance and professional development. Accordingly, examining how mobile short-video addiction relates to academic disengagement among vocational preschool-education students is of particular importance.

Mobile short-video addiction may exert a detrimental influence on academic disengagement among vocational college students majoring in preschool education. According to Cognitive Load Theory22, excessive immersion in mobile short videos can substantially deplete individuals’ limited cognitive resources7,23,24, thereby reducing the psychological resources available for other goal-directed activities such as learning. For preschool-education majors in particular, practicum training and skill integration often require continuous switching among classroom instruction, skills rehearsal, and situated reflection while maintaining high levels of sustained attention25,26,). The highly stimulating nature and rapid information switching characteristic of short-video content can occupy working memory and attentional resources, making it more difficult for students to sustain concentration and self-monitoring during practicum tasks and coursework. This, in turn, may heighten cognitive fatigue and boredom experiences and ultimately reduce learning engagement and increase avoidance behaviors27–29. Prior research further indicates that learning engagement is shaped not only by the allocation of cognitive resources but also by key psychological resources. In particular, academic self-efficacy and academic/psychological resilience are positively associated with learning engagement and may operate through a sequential pathway in which self-efficacy fosters resilience, thereby enhancing learning engagement30,31. Such reallocation and depletion of cognitive resources undermine sustained task focus, rendering individuals more prone to fatigue and burnout-like experiences (32–34). Empirical evidence also supports the proposition that mobile short-video addiction is positively associated with academic disengagement. For example, meta-analytic findings indicate a positive correlation between mobile short-video addiction and academic disengagement35,36, suggesting that prolonged immersion in fast-paced and highly arousing short-video content may increase boredom and loss of interest when students confront academic tasks3,36. Consequently, students’ learning interest and academic enthusiasm may decline, and avoidance tendencies may emerge or intensify.

Mobile short-video addiction substantially shapes the learning routines of vocational preschool-education students and may exacerbate academic disengagement. After becoming immersed in short-video content, students may perceive conventional learning materials as uninteresting3, experience attentional diversion, and show reduced willingness to participate in class discussions and learning activities, thereby undermining learning effectiveness36,37. They may also exhibit diminished self-control and difficulties in planning study time and managing academic tasks19,20,36,38,39. Furthermore, mobile short-video addiction may trigger or aggravate mental health problems, including anxiety and depressive symptoms40,41. In addition, students who rely excessively on short-video platforms for social interaction may be more likely to experience feelings of social isolation and being misunderstood in offline settings38,39,42.

Although prior studies have begun to examine the association between short-video addiction and academic disengagement among vocational college students and have yielded initial evidence, much of this work remains at the level of descriptive correlations and has not sufficiently elucidated the underlying psychological and behavioral mechanisms (e.g., mediating and moderating processes) through which short-video addiction contributes to disengagement. More importantly, existing research has rarely situated this association within the preschool-education training context—characterized by intensive practicum demands and comparatively high emotional labor—and has seldom integrated cognitive-load considerations with self-regulatory resource depletion to account for the pathways linking short-video addiction to academic disengagement. This limits the contextual fit and explanatory power of current theoretical accounts. To address these gaps, the present study proposes a moderated mediation model to test (a) whether self-control mediates the association between mobile short-video addiction and academic disengagement and (b) whether psychological resilience moderates this association. In the learning engagement literature, psychological resilience is commonly conceptualized as a key resource that enables individuals to maintain adaptive functioning and sustained engagement under academic stress and challenge. Prior evidence suggests that resilience is not only positively associated with learning engagement but may also play an important role in linking external support or individual beliefs to engagement outcomes30,31. Accordingly, incorporating psychological resilience into a moderation framework helps to delineate the boundary conditions and identify subgroups at differential risk in the association between mobile short-video addiction and academic disengagement. Beyond its theoretical contribution, this model is intended to clarify how mobile short-video addiction translates into academic disengagement and to identify the conditions under which this effect becomes more pronounced.

The mediating role of self-control

Self-control refers to an individual’s capacity to regulate behavior, emotions, and desires in the service of long-term goals and to resist immediate temptations43,44. Self-Determination Theory posits that self-regulatory capacity and intrinsic motivation largely depend on the extent to which basic psychological needs—autonomy, relatedness, and competence—are satisfied45–47. In the context of short-video platforms, mechanisms of instant gratification and reward reinforcement readily promote continued use and may strengthen users’ dependence through repeated mood regulation and short-term rewards48–50.

Self-control is negatively associated with academic disengagement. According to the strength model of self-control and resource-depletion accounts51, self-control constitutes a limited psychological resource,prolonged exposure to multiple academic and life stressors may gradually consume this resource, thereby fostering learning fatigue and feelings of weariness45,52–54. As self-control resources are continuously depleted, students facing academic demands—particularly when encountering setbacks or challenges—are more likely to experience negative affective fluctuations such as frustration, anxiety, and disappointment. These adverse emotional reactions may further intensify tendencies toward academic disengagement, creating a maladaptive cycle of “stress–depletion–withdrawal.”

Empirical research also indicates a robust negative association between self-control and academic burnout55,56, such that students with lower self-control tend to report stronger fatigue and helplessness under academic pressure and consequently invest less effort in academic activities. Similar evidence has been reported in samples of secondary-school students, where self-control is negatively related to academic burnout57. However, existing studies have predominantly focused on middle- or high-school populations and have mainly examined academic burnout,comparatively little is known about the association between self-control and academic disengagement among vocational college students majoring in preschool education.

In sum, the documented association between mobile short-video addiction and self-control, alongside the negative association between self-control and academic disengagement, suggests that self-control may function as a mediating mechanism linking mobile short-video addiction to academic disengagement. Although the specific mediation chain of “mobile short-video addiction → self-control → academic disengagement” has not been directly and sufficiently tested, prior work in adolescent samples suggests that self-control can mediate the associations between academic burnout and its antecedents (e.g., social media use, academic motivation), providing indirect support for the present inference58.

Based on the foregoing theoretical rationale and empirical evidence, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1

Among vocational college students majoring in preschool education, mobile short-video addiction will be negatively associated with self-control; self-control will be negatively associated with academic disengagement; and mobile short-video addiction will be positively associated with academic disengagement.

Hypothesis 2

Self-control will mediate the association between mobile short-video addiction and academic disengagement, such that mobile short-video addiction will indirectly predict higher academic disengagement via reduced self-control.

The moderating role of psychological resilience

Firstly, psychological resilience refers to an individual’s capacity to maintain or regain healthy psychological functioning and achieve adaptive adjustment under stress and adversity, as reflected in effective emotion regulation, active coping, and rapid recovery59–62 ). Protective Factor Theory posits that when exposed to stressors or risks, individuals may draw on positive internal and external resources to sustain psychological health and adaptive functioning6364,65). As a key psychological resource, resilience is typically associated with higher levels of emotion regulation, self-monitoring, and executive control, thereby supporting self-regulatory processes19,20,60,61. Importantly, the effects of protective factors are context-dependent and do not always manifest as a classic buffering effect. Evidence suggests that when risk exposure is intense or chronic, resilience may prompt individuals to engage in greater effortful regulatory investment to maintain goal consistency and behavioral standards16,66,67. In addictive patterns of short-video use—characterized by high frequency, persistence, and difficulty disengaging—repeated inhibitory control and self-monitoring can accumulate regulatory load, accelerating the depletion of self-regulatory resources. As a result, self-control may be more likely to decline as addiction severity increases (68–71). Accordingly, we further infer that psychological resilience may amplify the negative association between addiction symptoms and self-control. Specifically, compared with individuals low in resilience, those high in resilience may exhibit a stronger negative effect of mobile short-video addiction on self-control.

Secondly, Conservation of Resources (COR) theory posits that individuals strive to protect existing resources and continuously acquire new resources in order to cope with stress72. Psychological resilience enhances individuals’ capacity to adapt to and recover from stressful and adverse circumstances62,73 and, through more effective emotion regulation and active coping, facilitates resource maintenance and replenishment, thereby slowing resource depletion74 (Ong et al., 2013)105. Accordingly, among students with higher resilience, stress-induced emotional exhaustion and attentional resource loss may be less likely to occur, allowing self-control to operate more stably and, in turn, more effectively inhibit academic disengagement. This implies a stronger negative association between self-control and academic disengagement at higher levels of resilience.

Prior studies provide support for this inference. Resilience has been linked to lower emotional exhaustion, better emotion regulation, and greater self-protective functioning48,75–77, and it is further negatively associated with maladaptive learning outcomes and poorer academic functioning78–81.

Based on the above reasoning and the existing empirical evidence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3 (moderated mediation)

Psychological resilience moderates both the direct and indirect processes linking mobile short-video addiction to academic disengagement. Specifically:

Psychological resilience moderates the mobile short-video addiction → self-control path, such that this negative effect is stronger in the high-resilience group than in the low-resilience group; andPsychological resilience moderates the self-control → academic disengagement path, such that the protective (inhibitory) effect of self-control on academic disengagement is stronger in the high-resilience group than in the low-resilience group.

The purpose of this study

In summary, this study investigates the mediating role of self-control in the relationship between mobile short video addiction and academic disengagement behaviors among preschool teacher education students in vocational colleges, as well as the moderating role of psychological resilience in both the direct and indirect relationships between mobile short video addiction and academic disengagement. This research helps to answer the psychological mechanisms underlying the close relationship between mobile short video addiction and academic disengagement behaviors among preschool teacher education students in vocational colleges under the condition of psychological resilience.

Methods

Participants

A total of 2,687 vocational college students were recruited from China through a convenience sampling method. Participants were invited to complete a questionnaire survey via the Questionnaire Star platform (www.wjx.cn). Of the total sample, 1,986 (73.9%) were female, while 701 (26.1%) were male. The mean age of the participants was 19.28 years (SD = 1.21), with ages ranging from 16 to 29 years. In terms of geographical background, 347 participants (12.9%) were from urban areas, while 2,340 participants (87.1%) were from rural areas. Regarding their academic level, 37.4% of the participants were in their first year of vocational education, 47.3% were in their second year, and 15.2% were in their third year of study.

Measures

Mobile short video addiction

The Mobile Short Video Addiction Scale (MSVAS;82 ) was utilized to assess the propensity for mobile short video addiction among vocational college students. The scale includes 14 items in 4 dimensions: 5 items about withdrawal (e.g., “If you haven’t been using short videos for a while, you may experience anxiety.”) , 3 items about avoidance (e.g., “When experiencing feelings of loneliness, individuals may turn to short videos for engagement.”), 4 items about loss of control (e.g., You never feel that you’ve spent enough time watching short videos), and 2items about in efficiency (e.g., The time spent on watching short videos directly leads to a decrease in your work efficiency). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (completely consistent) to 5 (completely inconsistent). The second-order CFA model showed that the ELS had good construct validity, with χ2/df = 7.01, RMSEA = 0.05, NFI = 0.98, GFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.97 . In this study, the overall scale’s internal consistency reliability coefficient is 0.888..

Academic disengagement

The Academic Disengagement Scale (ADS), developed by Chen83, was employed to assess the academic disengagement of vocational college students. The scale comprises 21 items divided into two dimensions: behavioral disengagement (11 items, e.g., “I spend minimal time and effort on studying.”) and emotional disengagement (10 items, e.g., “I feel exhausted when I have to start studying in the morning after waking up”). Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater academic disengagement. Consistent with the original conceptualization of the ADS, academic disengagement in the present study was operationalized as behavioral withdrawal/low effort and negative emotional experiences toward learning. We therefore did not include additional items tapping cognitive appraisals (e.g., perceiving learning as meaningless), as adding such items would alter the established factor structure of the ADS and reduce comparability with prior studies. Moreover, cognitive statements about the value or meaning of learning may overlap with adjacent constructs (e.g., perceived value, learning meaning, or amotivation) and would require separate scale development and validation procedures beyond the scope of the present study. A second-order confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) demonstrated that the scale exhibited satisfactory construct validity, as evidenced by the following fit indices: χ2/df = 7.48, RMSEA = 0.05, NFI = 0.97, GFI = 0.98, TFI = 0.96, and AGFI = 0.99. In this study, the reliability of the scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.894.

Psychological resilience

The Brief Psychological Resilience Scale (PRS;84) was used to measure psychological resilience. The Chinese version was developed via a standard forward–backward translation procedure: two bilingual researchers with graduate-level training in psychology and prior experience in scale localization independently produced the forward translation. An independent bilingual translator, blinded to the original English items, then conducted the back-translation. A bilingual review panel compared the back-translated and original versions and resolved discrepancies through consensus to ensure semantic equivalence and clarity of item wording. We did not conduct a separate pilot test of the translated PRS prior to the formal survey,this may limit the rigor of the cross-cultural adaptation and is acknowledged as a limitation to be addressed in future research. No substantive cultural adaptation was undertaken; this is acknowledged as a limitation and will be addressed in future research. The PRS contains six items, including positively worded statements (e.g., “After facing challenges, I am typically able to quickly return to my normal state.”) and negatively worded statements (e.g., “In the face of adversities in life, I require an extended recovery period to manage and overcome these challenges.”). Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Negatively worded items were reverse-coded, and higher mean scores reflected greater resilience. A second-order confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported the scale’s construct validity (χ2/df = 5.665, RMSEA = 0.042, GFI = 0.997, NFI = 0.994, TLI = 0.988, CFI = 0.995). Internal consistency in the present sample was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.740).

Self-Control

The Self-Control Scale (SCS;85) was administered to assess self-control among vocational college students. The Chinese version was developed via a standard forward–backward translation procedure: two bilingual researchers with graduate-level training in psychology and prior experience in scale localization independently produced the forward translation. An independent bilingual translator, blinded to the original English items, then conducted the back-translation. A bilingual review panel compared the back-translated and original versions and resolved discrepancies through consensus to ensure semantic equivalence and clarity of item wording. We did not conduct a separate pilot test of the translated SCS prior to the formal survey; this may limit the rigor of the cross-cultural adaptation and is acknowledged as a limitation to be addressed in future research. No substantive cultural adaptation was undertaken; this is acknowledged as a limitation and will be addressed in future research. The SCS comprises 13 items across two subscales: General Self-Discipline (9 items; e.g., “I am good at resisting temptation.”) and Impulse Control (4 items; e.g., “I have a hard time breaking bad habits.”). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), and an overall score was computed by averaging responses, with higher scores indicating greater self-control. A second-order confirmatory factor analysis supported satisfactory construct validity (χ2/df = 11.13, RMSEA = 0.06, GFI = 0.97, NFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, CFI = 0.99), and internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.972).

Procedure

This study received ethics approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the authors’ university, and all procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical standards of the Research Ethics and Scientific Safety Committee of Guangxi Minzu University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data were collected in September 2024 at a full-time higher vocational (junior-college level) institution in Guangxi, China, selected for feasibility and proximity to the research team. The study context was the institution’s Preschool Education program (vocational preschool teacher-education), which emphasizes competency-based preparation and intensive practice-oriented training (e.g., child-behavior observation and analysis, skills training, and kindergarten practicum/internship placements). Given the close temporal proximity to the CNNIC statistics released in June 2024 and the absence of indications of major structural shifts in the short-video ecosystem during this interval, the CNNIC data were used as a macro-level contextual reference for interpreting the present sample. To enhance within-site representativeness, stratified random sampling was implemented after site confirmation: students were stratified by grade cohort (Year 1–Year 3), major, and class, and randomly selected within each stratum. Participants completed an anonymous online questionnaire (www.wjx.cn) assessing mobile short-video addiction, academic disengagement, psychological resilience, and self-control. Participation was voluntary, responses were confidential, and participants could withdraw at any time without penalty.

Results

Bivariate correlation analysis

Results of the bivariate correlation analyses are presented in Table 1. Mobile short-video addiction was positively associated with academic disengagement (r = 0.444, p < 0.01) and negatively associated with psychological resilience (r = − 0.188, p < 0.01) and self-control (r = − 0.181, p < 0.01). Academic disengagement was negatively correlated with psychological resilience (r = − 0.400, p < 0.01) and self-control (r = − 0.411, p < 0.01). Psychological resilience was positively correlated with self-control (r = 0.385, p < 0.01). In addition, gender showed small but significant correlations with mobile short-video addiction (r = − 0.180, p < 0.01), academic disengagement (r = − 0.046, p < 0.05), psychological resilience (r = 0.113, p < 0.01), and self-control (r = 0.056, p < 0.01), whereas student origin was not significantly related to the focal psychological variables (|r|≤ 0.031). Regarding distributional properties, the continuous variables (mobile short-video addiction, academic disengagement, psychological resilience, and self-control) exhibited acceptable univariate normality, with skewness ranging from − 0.409 to 0.138 and kurtosis ranging from − 0.546 to 1.293, all within commonly used thresholds (e.g., |skewness|< 2 and |kurtosis|< 7). These results support the use of regression- and conditional process–based analyses. Therefore, the positive association between mobile short-video addiction and academic disengagement support Hypothesis 1.

Mediating effect of Self-Control

We used Model 4 of the PROCESS macro86 to examine the mediating effect of self-control in the relationship between mobile short video addiction and academic disengagement (controlling for gender and student origin). As shown in Table 2, mobile short video addiction was negatively associated with self-control (β = -0.177, p < 0.001) (see Model 2):academic disengagement as the dependent variable, including self-control), and self-control was negatively associated with academic disengagement (β = -0.343, p < 0.001) (see Model 3;total effect model, with academic disengagement as the dependent variable). By bootstrapping 5000 samples, the indirect effect of mobile short video addiction on vocational college students’ academic disengagement through self-control was significant (ab = 0.060, Boot SE = 0.010, 95% CI = [0.042, 0.082]), and the direct effect was also significant (c′ = 0.389, SE = 0.016, 95% CI = [0.357, 0.421]). Thus, self-control acts as a partial mediating role in the relationship between mobile short video addiction and academic disengagement, which accounted for 13.49% of the total effect.

Discussion

Drawing on prior research on mobile short-video addiction and maladaptive academic adjustment, the present study extends this literature in two ways: first, by focusing on vocational preschool teacher-education students, a population embedded in distinctive contextual demands; and second, by proposing a more context-sensitive, process-oriented explanatory account. These students typically learn within an ecology characterized by intensive practicum requirements, frequent skills-based assessments, and elevated expectations for emotional and behavioral regulation as part of professional preparation. Core training activities—including child-behavior observation and documentation, micro-teaching and lesson demonstrations, and extended kindergarten practicum placements—require sustained attention, rapid inhibitory control, and ongoing emotion regulation in authentic interactions with children, mentors, and parents. In such a high-practicum, high–emotional-labor context, the immediate rewards and pervasive cues embedded in short-video platforms are especially likely to compete with learning demands that depend on sustained attention and impulse inhibition, making self-regulatory resource depletion a plausible pathway to academic disengagement. For example, during observation training, students are expected to maintain continuous vigilance, accurately code children’s behaviors, and produce structured records and reflective notes; frequent short-video checking may fragment attention and undermine inhibitory control, thereby compromising the sustained self-control needed to complete high-quality observation documentation and reflection. Likewise, during kindergarten practicum placements, students face continuous emotional demands and rapidly changing classroom contingencies, which elevate baseline self-regulatory load; cue-driven short-video use during breaks or after work may impede recovery and further erode self-control, increasing the risk of disengagement from practice logs, lesson planning, and post-lesson reflection. From the learning engagement perspective, academic disengagement can be conceptualized as the inverse manifestation of learning engagement. Prior research indicates that key psychological resources—such as academic self-efficacy and academic resilience—are positively associated with learning engagement and may operate via a sequential mechanism in which self-efficacy fosters resilience, thereby enhancing engagement30. Accordingly, by situating the “digital cue competition–self-regulatory resource change–academic disengagement” process within a high-practicum learning ecology, the present study offers a complementary lens for understanding how learning engagement may be undermined in highly rewarding digital environments.

From a methodological standpoint, the present study extends prior work by specifying and testing a conditional process (moderated mediation) model, thereby clarifying both the putative mechanism and its boundary conditions. The results indicate that self-control partially accounts for the positive association between mobile short-video addiction and academic disengagement. Psychological resilience further moderates both the addiction → self-control link and the self-control → disengagement link. Notably, resilience does not operate as a uniform buffer; rather, its function appears phase-dependent. Under sustained exposure to repetitive digital cues—particularly in training activities that require prolonged vigilance and rapid inhibition (e.g., observation coding) or continuous emotion regulation (e.g., practicum interactions)—resilience may prompt greater effortful regulatory investment to “push through,” accumulating regulatory load and strengthening the association between addiction symptoms and diminished self-control. At the same time, resilience amplifies the protective role of self-control for academic adjustment by supporting persistence and recovery-oriented coping, thereby strengthening the beneficial effect of self-control in reducing academic disengagement. Taken together, these findings suggest that the same resource may function differently across pathways, consistent with the learning engagement literature emphasizing resource synergy and contextual contingency. For example, prior research shows that external support (e.g., teacher support) can promote learning engagement by enhancing self-efficacy and psychological resilience31, implying that resilience may be more beneficial during the resource-protection and engagement-maintenance phase, whereas under conditions of high cue exposure and sustained effortful inhibition it may not serve as a stable, invariant buffer.

In sum, these findings enhance the ecological validity of digital addiction models in vocational education contexts and yield practical implications for more targeted interventions—particularly the development of integrated approaches that combine addiction-risk reduction, self-regulation skills training, and resilience-oriented support. Drawing on evidence from the learning engagement and academic resilience literature30,31, we further argue that interventions should not focus solely on strengthening individual resources per se,rather, they should institutionalize a “cue reduction–load optimization–recovery facilitation” framework at the system level. Concretely, during observation-training periods, programs may implement time-windowed cue management and structured attention-recovery routines; during kindergarten practicum placements, interventions may prioritize post-shift recovery, sleep hygiene, and reflective scaffolds to protect inhibitory-control resources and sustain engagement in practice-based assignments. Such designs may more effectively minimize the sustained capture and depletion of attentional and inhibitory-control resources by highly rewarding digital cues, thereby sustaining learning engagement and reducing the risk of academic disengagement.

The mediating role of self-control

This study found a significant negative correlation between mobile short video addiction and self-control, as well as a significant negative correlation between self-control and academic disengagement behaviors among preschool teacher education students in vocational colleges. In other words, self-control plays a mediating role in the relationship between mobile short video addiction and academic disengagement behaviors. The results support the hypothesis of the mediating role of self-control proposed in this study. These findings indicate that mobile short video addiction is directly related to academic disengagement behaviors, and also indirectly related through the effect of self-control. The findings of this study help to explain how mobile short video addiction influences academic disengagement behaviors among preschool teacher education students in vocational colleges through self-control. The present study helps to clarify that mobile short-video addiction may promote academic disengagement among vocational preschool teacher-education students by undermining self-control. As a core self-regulatory resource that supports sustained engagement and inhibits distraction, self-control is more likely to be depleted under sustained capture by addiction-related cues, which may in turn reduce learning engagement and increase avoidance tendencies. This account is consistent with evidence showing a positive association between academic resilience and learning engagement30.

To further explore the mediating role of self-control in the relationship between mobile short video addiction and academic disengagement behaviors among preschool teacher education students in vocational colleges, we will discuss the two indirect pathways involved separately.

Firstly, the present study found a significant negative association between mobile short-video addiction and self-control among preschool teacher-education students, indicating that greater addiction severity is linked to lower self-control. This finding converges with prior evidence87–89. From the perspective of delay of gratification theory90, effective self-regulation requires resisting immediately rewarding temptations in order to pursue longer-term goals. Short-video platforms offer rapid, salient rewards that may incentivize students to cope with academic demands by shifting attention away from learning and toward short-term entertainment. In addition, prolonged academic engagement can tax self-regulatory capacity, producing self-control fatigue and further diminishing students’ ability to prioritize long-term objectives over immediate gratification. These findings offer targeted practical implications for educational institutions and administrators. Building on evidence that academic self-efficacy and academic resilience jointly promote learning engagement30, the present study further suggests that, in learning contexts characterized by frequent exposure to highly rewarding short-video cues, interventions should not focus solely on strengthening individual resources. Rather, they should also reduce the attentional capture imposed by short-video cues and incorporate recovery-oriented arrangements to replenish self-regulatory resources, thereby more stably sustaining learning engagement and attenuating tendencies toward academic disengagement. First, schools may implement structured programs to strengthen self-control and delay-of-gratification skills, helping preschool-education students allocate time more effectively and establish goal-consistent study routines. We recommend a 12-week stratified intervention: Weeks 1–2 involve baseline assessment and stratified grouping based on addiction × resilience × self-control; Weeks 3–10 provide one group session per week (e.g., If–Then implementation intentions, task decomposition, delay-of-gratification training, and learning-log feedback); Weeks 11–12 focus on consolidation and transfer, with particular emphasis on high-load periods such as practicum training and internships. For students characterized by high resilience and high addiction risk, the emphasis should shift from willpower-based approaches to a combined “cue reduction + recovery facilitation” strategy (e.g., app locking during critical periods, recovery training, and sleep management) to mitigate the risk of resource depletion triggered by sustained high-effort self-monitoring. Second, schools should optimize the learning environment to support sustained attention and minimize interference from highly rewarding digital cues. Academic affairs offices can establish guidelines for smartphone and short-video use during practicum training, implemented by course instructors, while counselors periodically collect behavioral indicators (e.g., screen-time reports) and provide feedback. For students with high resilience and high addiction risk, cue management should be strengthened during internship weeks, and contextual restructuring (e.g., separating phones from learning settings and implementing environmental control during critical task periods) should be used to replace reliance on effortful willpower alone, thereby improving the sustainability of the intervention. Third, schools may encourage healthier leisure substitutes for short-video use (e.g., physical exercise, reading, or mindfulness-based practice) to facilitate recovery after academic strain and promote more adaptive stress-management strategies. A 10-week recovery package (exercise relaxation peer study) can be implemented with counselor-led check-ins and process monitoring. For evaluation, we recommend assessing addiction, self-control, and academic disengagement at T0/T1 (Week 8)/T2 (end of term), supplemented by process indicators such as screen time, attendance, and the quality of assignments/practicum logs. Where feasible, a control class can be included to compare intervention effects and differential benefits across resilience strata.

Secondly, this study further found a significant negative association between self-control and academic disengagement, suggesting that students with higher self-control are less likely to experience disengagement-related emotions and avoidance tendencies during learning. From the perspective of resource depletion theory , self-control is a limited capacity that can be progressively consumed under sustained academic and daily-life demands (91–93). Students with stronger self-control may better protect and allocate self-regulatory resources through effective planning, time management, and emotion regulation, thereby reducing stress-related emotional interference and sustaining academic engagement. In addition, self-efficacy theory94 posits that students with greater self-control often hold stronger confidence in managing academic challenges, which helps maintain motivation and task involvement and decreases the likelihood of avoidant responses. In contrast, students with lower self-control may be more vulnerable to emotional dysregulation under stress and may lack confidence in coping effectively, which in turn increases the risk of academic disengagement. These findings align with prior research linking self-control to more adaptive academic functioning43,95–97 and extend this association to vocational preschool teacher-education students. These findings provide clear practical implications for digital-addiction governance and learning support in vocational education contexts. Building on evidence from the learning engagement literature that academic self-efficacy and academic resilience can jointly promote learning engagement30, the present study further indicates that interventions should not focus solely on strengthening individual capabilities. Rather, they should embed cue-exposure reduction, task-load optimization, and recovery facilitation within an institutional support framework to minimize the risk of sustained capture of attentional and inhibitory-control resources. In practice, an integrated approach that combines risk reduction, self-regulation training, and resilience-oriented support can be adopted, with stratified implementation based on addiction × resilience × self-control profiles. For students characterized by high resilience and high addiction risk, “cue reduction + recovery facilitation” should be prioritized (e.g., app restrictions during critical periods, separating smartphones from learning settings, and sleep/recovery management) to prevent resource depletion driven by sustained high-effort self-monitoring. For students with low self-control or low resilience, skill training should be complemented by contextual supports and stress-reduction measures to interrupt chronic resource loss. In terms of evaluation, longitudinal assessment at T0/T1/T2 combined with process indicators (e.g., screen time, attendance, and the quality of practicum-related tasks) is recommended to enhance the verifiability and scalability of the findings.

The moderating role of psychological resilience

Firstly, the present study indicates that psychological resilience moderates the association between mobile short-video addiction and self-control. Specifically, among vocational preschool teacher-education students, the negative effect of mobile short-video addiction on self-control was weaker at low levels of resilience but stronger at high levels of resilience. In other words, as resilience increased, the adverse association between addiction symptoms and self-control became more pronounced. This pattern suggests that higher psychological resilience does not necessarily offset the detrimental influence of addictive short-video use on self-control; rather, when addiction severity increases, declines in self-control may be more evident among highly resilient students. This finding can be interpreted through Protective Factor Theory, which posits that positive individual resources and environmental supports facilitate adaptation under adversity, yet the protective function of such factors is highly context-dependent and does not invariably produce a buffering effect63 (Hamby et al., 2017)106. Importantly, Protective Factor Theory and Conservation of Resources (COR) theory both conceptualize resilience as a personal resource that promotes adaptation. However, they diverge in their explanatory emphasis: Protective Factor Theory underscores that the benefits of resilience are context contingent and may weaken—or even reverse—under certain high-risk conditions, whereas COR theory clarifies the resource-based mechanisms that can generate such conditional effects, including sustained resource investment, subsequent depletion, and gain–loss spirals over time. Under conditions of frequent, persistent, and dependence-like short-video use, students with higher resilience may engage in greater effortful regulatory investment. Importantly, given the contextual characteristics of vocational preschool teacher education, such elevated regulatory investment may be especially likely and more readily accumulate into regulatory burden. This group often confronts substantial emotional labor and interpersonal demands during practicum experiences, requiring repeated mobilization of executive-control resources. Moreover, students are commonly situated in a sensitive phase of professional identity formation and value internalization, which may heighten their tendency to respond to role and learning expectations through stronger self-discipline and restraint. When addictive digital cues are present, they may therefore adopt intensive strategies of inhibition and self-monitoring. Over time, sustained regulatory investment can increase regulatory load and accelerate resource depletion, thereby amplifying the negative effect of short-video addiction on self-control. From a COR perspective, this “amplified” pattern may reflect a high-effort adaptation pathway. When confronted with salient addictive cues, highly resilient students may be more inclined to sustain intensive regulatory investment; however, under chronic cue exposure, such continued investment is more likely to accelerate resource depletion. Consequently, resilience may not buffer the addiction → self-control association in this context but may instead coincide with a stronger negative linkage. This explanation is consistent with the view that self-regulation is effortful and may culminate in self-regulatory fatigue under prolonged demands69,70. It also aligns with emerging evidence linking short-video use to impaired inhibitory control and self-control failures68,71. Prior research has already documented that mobile short-video addiction, as a risk behavior, undermines students’ self-control87,98. Extending this work, the present findings demonstrate that this detrimental association is not uniform across individuals but is significantly contingent on resilience. Thus, the moderating process in the first pathway appears to be best characterized as effort-and-depletion oriented. Resilience may foster greater persistence in self-regulation when individuals face temptation—an adaptive tendency in many contexts. However, under dependence-like short-video exposure, sustained regulatory effort may paradoxically accelerate resource depletion, thereby exacerbating declines in self-control. This finding also suggests that discussions of “resilience” should distinguish its typically facilitative role in learning engagement from a potential “high-effort–high-depletion” side effect under high-risk cue conditions. Although the learning engagement literature consistently reports that resilience is associated with higher engagement30,31, the present study further indicates that when external cues persistently elicit inhibitory effort, resilience may accelerate resource consumption by promoting stronger, sustained regulatory investment, thereby producing an amplified effect in the addiction → self-control pathway.

In summary, these results challenge a simplistic “resilience-as-buffer” assumption and instead highlight a context-sensitive high-effort adaptation risk. Although resilience may exert a positive main effect on self-control, under intensified addictive use, highly resilient students may be more inclined to persistently increase regulatory effort. In contexts characterized by high emotional-labor pressure, heightened sensitivity in professional identity development, and frequent digital cue exposure, sustained self-control investment may hasten resource depletion and precipitate self-control decline—manifesting as an “amplified” addiction → self-control pathway. Therefore, resilience-related resources may not invariably attenuate risk effects; when regulatory demands accumulate, they may paradoxically intensify vulnerability. From an applied perspective, these findings suggest that interventions should not focus solely on enhancing resilience. Instead, resilience-building should be integrated with strategies that reduce cue exposure and regulatory load, such as digital cue management, context restructuring, and recovery-oriented training, to prevent persistent regulatory investment from translating into accelerated depletion.

Secondly, the present study indicates that psychological resilience significantly moderates the association between self-control and academic disengagement. Regression analyses showed that self-control negatively predicted academic disengagement (β = − 0.269, p < 0.001), and the self-control × psychological resilience interaction was also significant (β = − 0.032, p < 0.001). Simple slope analyses further revealed that the negative self-control–disengagement association was relatively weaker among students with low resilience (βsimple = − 0.237, p < 0.001) but stronger among those with high resilience (βsimple = − 0.301, p < 0.001). Thus, psychological resilience is not only associated with lower academic disengagement, but also strengthens the inhibitory effect of self-control on academic disengagement. These findings are in line with Conservation of Resources (COR) theory. Hobfoll argues that individuals under stress strive to protect existing resources and acquire new ones to prevent the escalation of resource loss99. From this perspective, self-control can be conceptualized as a regulatory resource that is readily depleted under sustained academic pressure and self-regulatory demands, yet can be maintained through recovery and replenishment. Psychological resilience, as a higher-order psychological resource, may facilitate resource protection and restoration, thereby reducing the likelihood of self-control depletion under stress. Extensions of COR theory by Halbesleben and colleagues likewise underscore the central role of resource acquisition and maintenance in successful stress adaptation100. Crucially, the moderating mechanism in the second path is theoretically “resource-protection and efficiency” oriented: resilience supports coping, recovery, and resource replenishment, thereby stabilizing the availability of self-control and increasing the efficiency with which self-control translates into sustained engagement (i.e., stronger self-control → lower disengagement). Moreover, the findings also support the notion of synergistic interactions among personal resources. Psychological resilience may enhance emotion regulation and coping efficiency, allowing self-control to operate more consistently and thereby more effectively suppress academic disengagement101–104. In contrast, students low in resilience may experience more rapid resource loss, making it more difficult for self-control to translate into behavioral persistence and learning engagement under high academic demands, thus weakening its protective effect against disengagement. Collectively, these dual-path results clarify why the same moderator can operate differently across pathways: in the addiction → self-control path, resilience may promote sustained regulatory investment under tempting cues, whereas in the self-control → disengagement path, resilience primarily protects and replenishes resources, enabling self-control to function more effectively in sustaining academic engagement. This distinction provides a deeper, theory-linked integration of Protective Factor Theory with COR theory .Consistent with prior work in the learning engagement literature, resilience may be more likely to promote resource protection and replenishment by enhancing coping and recovery efficiency, thereby strengthening the efficiency with which self-control is translated into sustained learning engagement and lower academic disengagement30,31. This resource-efficiency perspective provides a plausible explanation for the pattern observed in the second pathway, namely that higher resilience amplified the protective effect of self-control on academic disengagement.

The present findings suggest the need for a more targeted “dual-pathway” intervention approach, focusing on (a) strengthening self-control skills and (b) cultivating resilience-related resources. To enhance feasibility and implementation fidelity, we recommend a 12-week, stratified closed-loop program coordinated by student affairs, supported by the counseling/psychological services center, and jointly delivered by counselors, course instructors, and practicum supervisors. During Weeks 1–2, students should be screened and stratified into groups based on mobile short-video addiction × resilience × self-control profiles. During Weeks 3–10, a 60–90-min group session should be conducted weekly, covering If–Then implementation intentions, time structuring, task decomposition, micro-skills for emotion regulation, and learning-log feedback. During Weeks 11–12, the program should emphasize consolidation and transfer, with a focus on practicum training and other high-load learning periods. Importantly, the intervention should be differentiated by resilience profiles. For students characterized by high resilience and high addiction risk, a combined “cue reduction + recovery facilitation” package (e.g., app locking during critical periods, smartphone management in learning settings, post-internship recovery training, and sleep management) is recommended to mitigate resource depletion associated with sustained high-effort self-monitoring. For students with low resilience, self-control training should be complemented with stress reduction and contextual supports (e.g., scaffolded task scheduling, peer-support groups, and stress-management training) to reduce chronic resource loss, prevent excessive depletion, and ultimately lower the risk of academic disengagement. Evaluation is recommended at baseline (T0), Week 8, and Week 12, supplemented with process indicators such as screen-time use; where feasible, a control class can be included, and intervention fidelity can be monitored via attendance and task-completion rates.

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations . Firstly, the present study used a cross-sectional design; the observed associations should not be interpreted causally, and the temporal ordering among mobile short-video addiction, self-control, psychological resilience, and academic disengagement cannot be established. Future research should employ two-wave or multi-wave longitudinal designs and, where feasible, quasi-experimental or experimental approaches to better test directionality and potential mechanisms.

Secondly, the study relied on retrospective self-report measures, which may be susceptible to common method bias and reporting inaccuracies, threatening internal validity. Future studies should incorporate multi-method and multi-informant assessments and/or time-separated measurement to obtain a more robust understanding of the proposed processes.

Thirdly, the representativeness and measurement considerations warrant caution. Because participants were drawn from a specific region and training context, external validity may be constrained; future work should recruit samples across multiple regions, institutions, and diverse student populations. In addition, several measures were originally developed in other languages. Although we adopted a conservative cross-cultural strategy, systematic localization/adaptation and formal measurement invariance testing were not conducted. Specifically, although the Chinese versions were developed via a standard forward–backward translation procedure, we did not conduct an independent pilot test of the translated scales (SCS and PRS) prior to the formal survey, which may weaken the rigor of cross-cultural adaptation. Moreover, no substantive cultural contextualization/adaptation was undertaken; this should be addressed in future research. Future research should combine cognitive interviews, item analysis, and multi-group invariance tests to strengthen instrument fit for Chinese vocational-education contexts. Moreover, academic disengagement was assessed using behavioral and emotional dimensions of the ADS, whereas cognitive disengagement was not directly measured; future studies may incorporate cognitive disengagement or broader multidimensional disengagement/engagement instruments to test incremental explanatory power.

Despite these limitations, the study offers meaningful theoretical and practical contributions. Theoretically, the findings extend prior work by clarifying a conditional process in which self-control partly accounts for the association between mobile short-video addiction and academic disengagement, and psychological resilience serves as a boundary condition, suggesting context-dependent roles of resilience-related resources. Practically, the results support a differentiated prevention and intervention approach that integrates digital literacy education with self-control–supportive strategies and resilience training, alongside family involvement and tiered or personalized support for students at higher risk of academic disengagement.
