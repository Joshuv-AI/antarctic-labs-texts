The effect of storage temperature and time before centrifugation on coagulation parameters in neonatal Japanese Black calves
Authors: Riko ITO, Keinosuke SANADA, Satoshi INOUE, Toko MAEHARA, Hiroshi SATOH
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to determine the effects of precentrifugation storage time and temperature on coagulation and fibrinolysis parameters in Japanese Black calves. Blood samples were collected from calves under 6 weeks of age into plastic tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate. Whole blood was stored at either room temperature or under refrigeration prior to centrifugation. Prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen level, and antithrombin level were measured. The results indicated that plasma parameters in samples centrifuged within 9 hr of collection showed no more than ± 10% variation compared with those in samples centrifuged within 1 hr of collection.Keywords: activated partial thromboplastin time, antithrombin, calf, fibrinogen, prothrombin time

In neonatal calves, the activity of the coagulation factors VII, VIII, IX, X, and XI as well as the level of fibrinogen (Fib) fall within the normal ranges for adult cattle. However, the levels of the anticoagulant proteins α2-macroglobulin and antithrombin (AT) are lower in neonatal calves than in adults [3]. In contrast, the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and prothrombin time (PT) in neonatal calves fall within the normal range observed in adult cattle, suggesting that their intrinsic and extrinsic coagulation pathways are comparable to those of adults [3]. Inherited coagulation abnormalities, including factor XI deficiency [4, 10, 13] and hemophilia A [10], have been reported in breeds common in Japan, such as Japanese Black [13], Japanese Brown [12], and Holstein-Friesian cattle [4, 10]. More recently, reports have suggested the occurrence of thromboembolism secondary to diarrhea [1, 5, 16], systemic bacterial infections [2], and disseminated intravascular coagulation in calves [7, 15]. These findings underscore the growing need for more detailed data on coagulation parameters in neonatal calves. Furthermore, we encountered a case of aortic thromboembolism secondary to diarrhea in a Japanese Black calf, and reports of thromboembolism in this breed are on the rise [1, 2]. This experience has reinforced the critical need to establish reference data for these parameters. Key preanalytical factors that influence coagulation test results include the blood collection method, order of draw, anticoagulant type and concentration, hematocrit value, collection tube fill volume, transport conditions, centrifugation parameters, temperature, time interval between collection and analysis, and storage conditions [6]. In Japan, while a consensus on handling coagulation test samples was established in human medicine in 2016 to address interfacility inconsistencies [9], such standards are lacking in veterinary medicine. Consequently, reference ranges for coagulation parameters often vary among textbooks and other sources. In Japan’s bovine healthcare system, which is based on veterinary farm visits, the most variable preanalytical factors are sample transport and storage conditions, specifically temperature and the time elapsed before testing. However, data on the impact of whole blood sample transport and storage on coagulation test results under field conditions are scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) establish coagulation parameters for Japanese Black calves during the nursing period; (2) investigate fluctuations in these parameters in whole blood samples under various time and temperature storage conditions that mimic those encountered in clinical practice; and (3) determine appropriate sample storage conditions.

This study involved 11 clinically healthy Japanese Black calves under 6 weeks of age sourced from various farms in Akita Prefecture. The study was conducted as part of a routine clinical examination and performed in accordance with the “Guidelines for Industrial Animal Medicine” of the Japan Veterinary Medical Association. A 45-mL blood sample was collected from the jugular vein of each calf using an 18-gauge needle and a 50-mL syringe (Nipro Corp., Osaka, Japan). The blood was immediately aliquoted into ten 4.5-mL vacuum collection tubes (Terumo Corp., Tokyo), which were then divided into two groups of five tubes each. One group was stored at room temperature (20–25°C), and the other was refrigerated (4–8°C). To obtain platelet-poor plasma (PPP), the blood samples were centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 20 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully aspirated, leaving at least 10 mm above the upper layer of the buffy coat to prevent contamination of the plasma with blood cells, such as platelets [14]. PPP obtained following 1-hr centrifugation served as the control values. The remaining whole blood samples were centrifuged under identical conditions at 2, 3, 6, and 9 hr, and PPP was collected. Each PPP sample was refrigerated at 4–8°C and transported to Koto Biken (Tokyo, Japan) for analysis within 24 hr. PT, APTT, and Fib were measured using the transmitted light detection method, whereas AT was measured using the chromogenic detection method. All analyzes were performed on a CA-5100 coagulation analyzer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). Statistical analysis: PT, APTT, and Fib values were expressed as mean ± SE. The rate of change from baseline was calculated using the formula: (value at each time point − 1-hr value)/ 1-hr value. Changes of ± 10% or more from baseline were considered clinically significant [11, 14]. Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess differences between the two temperature storage conditions. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study included 11 calves (6 males and 5 females) with a mean age of 22 days (range: 10–36). For each parameter, centrifugation was performed at 1, 2, 3, 6, and 9 hr under both room temperature and refrigerated storage conditions (Table 1). The 1-hr control measurements were as follows: PT, 26.6 ± 1.1 sec; APTT, 47.4 ± 5.5 sec; Fib, 129.3 ± 14.1 mg/dL; and AT activity, 85.2% ± 2.9%. Compared with the 1-hr control values, PT was significantly prolonged at 6 and 9 hr at room temperature, whereas no significant changes were observed under refrigerated conditions. A statistically significant difference was observed between the two storage conditions for PT values at the 6- and 9-hr time points. Similarly, aPTT was significantly prolonged after 9 hr at room temperature but not under refrigeration, resulting in a significant difference between the two conditions at this time point. Fib increased significantly after 9 hr at room temperature and after just 2 hr under refrigeration. Consequently, significant differences in Fib levels were observed between the room temperature and refrigerated samples at 2 and 9 hr post collection. AT activity did not change significantly from baseline under either storage condition; however, a statistically significant difference in AT levels was found between the room temperature and refrigerated samples at 3 hr. Despite these statistical variations, the magnitude of change for all parameters remained within 5% of the baseline value for up to 9 hr under both conditions, and none of the observed changes were considered clinically significant.

Coagulation parameters in neonatal Japanese Black calves were consistent with those reported in previous studies for PT and APTT [3, 7, 15]. However, AT values were found to be low [7, 15], and Fib values were also marginally reduced [3, 8, 15]. These lower AT and Fib may be attributable to factors such as the interval between sample collection and analysis, physiological traits unique to Japanese Black calves, or differences in coagulation assay methodology. Our findings suggest that when blood is collected from calves under 6 weeks of age in plastic tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate and stored at either room temperature (20–25°C) or under refrigeration (4–8°C), no significant deviation exceeding ± 10% from baseline values occurs if centrifugation is performed within 9 hr. This finding suggests that coagulation assays in bovine samples, such as PT, APTT, and Fib, exhibit greater stability over extended periods than recommended by guidelines for human samples. Furthermore, AT activity demonstrated lower sensitivity to the duration of whole blood storage. The results of this study can provide foundational data for standardizing coagulation sample handling in bovine veterinary practice, which relies heavily on farm calls. Study limitations include the small number of measurement time points (n=5) and the nonstandardized plasma storage time post separation. In this study, all samples were analyzed on the day of collection, and the resulting plasma was stored under refrigeration per laboratory guidelines. However, for coagulation parameters, it is standard practice to freeze plasma samples if immediate analysis post separation is not feasible. Therefore, further validation of these results using plasma samples frozen post centrifugation may be warranted.
