Comparative analysis of soil microbial diversity and chemical properties in different Phallus dongsun habitats
Authors: Li Cao, Qunying Xiao, Yuan Yao, Rongxian Zhang, Jie Ma, Juncheng Hu, Wei Zhang, Xiaohui Xue, Lijian Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhallus dongsun is a rare edible mushroom, and soil covering cultivation is its main cultivation mode. Soil microbial community and chemical properties play crucial roles in the growth, development and yield formation of P. dongsun. To clarify differences in microbial community composition, structure, and soil chemical properties across soils associated with the growth of P. dongsun, four types of soil samples were collected from its habitats, namely wild soil (YS), no-yield soil (JC), medium-yield soil (ZC), and high-yield soil (GC). Systematic analyses were conducted on soil nutrient contents, pH values, and microbial communities. The results showed that compared with JC and ZC soils, YS and GC soils had significantly higher abundances of beneficial microbial phyla and genera. Specifically, they include the phyla Pseudomonadota, Gemmatimonadota, and Myxococcota, as well as the genera Bradyrhizobium, Sphingomonas, Candidatus Angelobacter, Pseudolabrys, and Rhodoplanes. The enrichment of these beneficial microorganisms may provide a favorable soil environment for the growth of P. dongsun. In addition, the results of microbial α-diversity analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) further indicated that the YS soil and GC soil had higher microbial richness and evenness. Moreover, these two soil types showed relatively small differences and high similarity in the composition and structure of the microbial community. Analysis of soil chemical properties indicated that YS and GC had higher pH values, and higher contents of total phosphorus (TP), available phosphorus (AP), total potassium (TK), and available potassium (AK) than JC and ZC. Meanwhile, a strong correlation was observed between multiple soil chemical properties and microbial communities in YS and GC. This study clarifies the microecological characteristics of different soils supporting P. dongsun growth and provides a theoretical basis for the high-yield simulated wild cultivation of P. dongsun.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45668-x.Keywords: Phallus dongsun, microbial community, soil chemical properties, high-throughput sequencingSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Phallus dongsun (common name: Dongsun) is classified under the phylum Basidiomycota, subclass Phallomycetidae, order Phallales, family Phallaceae, and genus Phallus. The derivation of its common name “Dongsun” lies in its biological trait—growing and developing fruiting bodies specifically during the cooler autumn and winter periods1. Before 2020-year, Phallus impudicus was often misidentified as “Dongsun”. In fact, P. impudicus is a species complex well-known for the presence of indusium, and it actually represents at least four phylogenetic species. It was not until the new species P. dongsun was proposed in 2020 that the samples from China previously named P. impudicus were identified as P. dongsun—whereas P. impudicus itself is a species native to Europe2. Fruiting bodies of Phallus are rich in bioactive substances such as polysaccharides, proteins, amino acids, terpenoids, and phenols, and exhibit significant biological activities including immunomodulation, antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and anticoagulant activities3–8. Furthermore, the polysaccharides of edible fungi have also shown promising development and application potential in the fields of food, health products, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics9,10. And also have the functions of soil improvement and bioremediation. Field cultivation of edible fungi not only helps improve the bioavailability of nutrients, but also promotes the degradation of pollutants and the formation of soil aggregates11. For example, P. impudicus possesses the ability to decompose substrates and has been proven to be responsible for nutrient distribution, absorption, and storage. Rotating the cultivation of P. impudicus with Gastrodia elata can change the diversity, complexity, and stability of the soil microbiome structure, reshape the composition of the soil microbial community, thereby reversing the decrease in soil pH value and nutrient levels caused by G. elata cultivation, increasing the degree of soil humification, and optimizing the composition of humus12,13. Therefore, Phallus is widely popular worldwide due to its unique and attractive appearance, distinctive flavor, rich nutrition, as well as its high medicinal value and ecological functions.

Currently, among all cultivated Phallus species in China, the most widespread is P. dongsun—a product with “National Geographical Indication” status. It has been commercially cultivated throughout Southwest China, with Guizhou Province as a key production area14. The growth and development of P. dongsun follows a similar pattern with P. impudicus, primarily encompassing four stages: primordium, spherical, peach-shaped, and mature. During the peach-shaped stage, the fruiting bodies of P. dongsun are fully differentiated and reach an edible state. Under optimal conditions, these fruiting bodies can elongate to their full length within a few hours and mature. A mature fruiting body of P. dongsun mainly consists of three components: the pileus, volva, and stipe15,16. China is the earliest country in the world to achieve the artificial domestication of edible and medicinal fungus Phallus. Among them, Dictyophora rubrovolvata, Dictyophora echinovolvata and Dictyophora indusiate have realized large-scale cultivation. At present, bag-removing followed by soil-covering is one of the dominant practices in its cultivation17,18. During the soil-covering cultivation of edible fungi, the soil microbial community plays a crucial role in soil nutrient cycling and growth. During the entire growth cycle of D. rubrovolvata, there exists a dynamic coupling relationship between soil microbial communities, soil nutrient contents, and soil pH value19. Cultivation of Phallus rubrovolvatuson reduces the diversity of bacteria and fungi in soil, and the dynamic change of soil bacterial diversity more drastically. Among them, dominant bacteria (Actinobacteria, Sphingomonas, Xanthobacteraceae, Gemmatimonadaceae) and dominant fungi (Mortierellales, Eurotiales) are core components of the soil microbial community network20. Therefore, analyzing the microbial community in the soil for edible fungi cultivation can provide a theoretical basis for improving the soil environment after edible fungi planting and realizing the green, precise and high-yield cultivation of edible fungi.

Continuous monoculture and irregular cultivation practices are more likely to cause the occurrence of diseases, pests and continuous cropping obstacles, and reconstructing the rhizosphere microbiome of cultivated crops has become a promising strategy to promote crop growth. Wild habitats usually harbor a wealth of potentially beneficial microorganisms, which have higher abundance compared to cultivated soils and can be used to enhance the ecological integrity of agricultural systems21. The functional mechanisms of plant-beneficial microbiomes are complexly regulated by evolutionary and ecological factors. Modern agriculture integrates the eco-evolutionary principles of native, wild, and extreme environments, which can clarify plant-microbiome interaction mechanisms, help mitigate biodiversity loss, and ensure the sustainability of agricultural ecosystems22. Compared with the rhizosphere microorganisms of cultivated Coptis chinensis, those of wild C. chinensis contain more diverse ecological groups and exhibit greater functional diversity. Moreover, the rhizosphere of wild C. chinensis has a higher proportion of beneficial fungi, while the cultivated C. chinensis has a larger number of pathogenic fungi. Meanwhile, the study also identified a widely reported plant-growth-promoting rhizobacterial genus—Paraburkholderia, this bacterium possesses multiple growth-promoting properties and exhibits antagonistic activity against the known pathogens that cause C. chinensis root rot23.Therefore, a comprehensive comparison of the structural characteristics of rhizosphere microbial communities between wild and artificially cultivated crops helps identify microbial groups that are beneficial to crop growth, and points out directions for the future reconstruction of the rhizosphere microbiome of cultivated crops and wild-simulated cultivation.

The purpose of this study is to reveal the differences in the composition and structure of rhizosphere microbial communities between wild and cultivated P. dongsun, screen out beneficial microbial groups that promote the growth of P. dongsun, provide key references for the cultivation practice of P. dongsun and the reconstruction of rhizosphere microbial communities, and ultimately achieve high-yield cultivation of P. dongsun—all through comparative analysis of the rhizosphere microbial communities and soil chemical properties of wild and cultivated P. dongsun.

Results

Soil chemical properties of different habitats for P. dongsun growth

To evaluate the effects of soil quality on the growth of P. dongsun, we measured key soil chemical properties, including soil pH, organic matter (OM), total nitrogen (TN), available nitrogen (AN), total phosphorus, available phosphorus, total potassium, and available potassium. As shown in Fig. 1A, all four soils were acidic with pH values lower than 6. Pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences in pH among all soil samples, with the order as follows: YS > GC > ZC > JC. Soil OM content analysis revealed significant differences among all four soils in pairwise comparisons, with the order as follows: JC > ZC > GC > YS (Fig. 1B). Consistently, analysis of TN and AN contents revealed significant pairwise differences among the four soils, and their ranking was consistent with that of OM (Fig. 1C, D). Analysis of TP and AP contents revealed significant pairwise differences among the four soils. In contrast, the order of TP content among the four soils was GC > JC > YS > ZC, whereas that of AP content was GC > YS > JC > ZC (Fig. 2A, B). The TK and AK contents indicated that GC soil had the highest TK and AK contents, followed by YS soil. Moreover, the TK and AK contents of GC and YS soils were significantly higher than those of JC and ZC soils (Fig. 2C, D).

In conclusion, Compared with JC and ZC soils, the YC and GC soils exhibited significantly higher pH, as well as higher contents of TP, AP, TK, and AK, but lower contents of OM, TN and AN.

Microbial composition of Different Habitats for P. dongsun Growth

To clarify the microbial community composition of different soils, the community composition was analyzed at various taxonomic levels. At the phylum level, showed that the top 10 most abundant microbial phyla were Pseudomonadota, Acidobacteriota, Actinomycetota, Chloroflexota, Gemmatimonadota, Myxococcota, Candidatus Rokubacteria, Bacteroidota, Candidatus Binatota, and Verrucomicrobiota. Among these, Pseudomonadota was the most abundant microbial phylum in YS and GC soils, accounting for 37.24% and 34.28% respectively. In contrast, Acidobacteriota was the most abundant microbial phylum in ZC and JC soils, representing 34.49% and 31.34% respectively (Fig. 3A). At the class level, the major microorganisms in the four different soils were Alphaproteobacteria, Actinomycetes, Betaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, Terriglobia, Myxococcia, Thermoleophilia, Candidatus Binatia, Gemmatimonadetes, and Chitinophagia, and all of which were distributed under the major dominant bacterial phylum (Figure S2). Furthermore, analysis at the species level provided additional evidence for the phyla that the major dominant microorganisms are affiliated with (Figure S3). A comparative analysis of the differences among several high-abundance microbial phyla revealed that the abundances of Pseudomonadota, Gemmatimonadota, and Myxococcota in YS soil and GC soil were significantly higher than those in JC soil and ZC. On the other hand, the abundances of Actinomycetota and Chloroflexota in JC soil and ZC soil were significantly higher than those in YS and GC soil ((Fig. 3C). At the genus level showed that the top 10 most abundant microbial were Bradyrhizobium, Trebonia, Sphingomonas, Candidatus Angelo, Lysobacter, Pseudolabrys, Candidatus Binatus, Nocardioides, Cellvibrio, and Rhodoplanes (Fig. 3B). A comparative and differential analysis of the high-abundance microbial genera revealed the following results: In YS soils and GC soils, Bradyrhizobium had the highest abundance, and its abundance in YS soils was significantly higher than that in the other three soil types. In JC soils and ZC soils, Trebonia had the highest abundance, which was significantly higher than that in YS soils and GC soils, and the abundance of this genus was the lowest in YS soils. Furthermore, the abundances of Sphingomonas, Candidatus Angelobacter, and Rhodoplanes in YS soils were significantly higher than those in the other three soil types. There was no significant difference in the abundance of Pseudolabrys between YS soil and GC soil, accounting for 1.58% and 1.30%, respectively. Similarly, the abundance of this genus showed no significant difference between JC soil and ZC soil, representing 0.67% and 0.55%, respectively (Fig. 3D). In conclusion, there were no significant differences in the microbial community composition among the four soils, but there were significant differences in their abundances. Specifically, the relative abundances of Pseudomonadota, Gemmatimonadota, Myxococcota, Bradyrhizobium, Sphingomonas, Candidatus Angelobacter, and Rhodoplanes in GC and YS soils were significantly higher than those in JC and ZC soils.

LEfSe analysis was performed on the microbial communities in different P. dongsun growing soils to identify differential microorganisms with statistical significance and biological effect size among different groups, thereby searching for biomarkers in different soils. The results showed that there were only three dominant microbial phyla in JC soils, and all of them were uncultured microbial. Five microbial phyla were significantly enriched in GC soils, namely Nitrososphaerota, Fusobacteriota, Planctomycetota, Candidatus_Eisenbacteria, and Candidatus_Edwardsbacteria. Eight microbial phyla were significantly enriched in ZC, including Actinomycetota, Chloroflexota, Candidatus_Binatota, Candidatus_Wirthbacteria, Dictyoglomota, Candidatus_Verstraetearchaeota, Candidatus_Bathyarchaeota, and Candidatus_Desantisbacteria. In YS soils, 10 significantly enriched microbial phyla were identified, which were Pseudomonadota, Myxococcota, Gemmatimonadota, Bacteroidota, Nitrospirota, Hofneiviricota, Lenarviricota, Candidatus_Cerribacteria, Candidatus_Kuenenbacteria, and Candidatus_Latescibacterota (Fig. 4). Importantly, when analyzed at higher taxonomic levels, compared with the other three soils, there were no biomarkers in the JC soil (Figure S4). Overall, the number of dominant species in the microbial community of soils unfavorable for P. dongsun growth is lower than that of the other three soil suitable for its growth. Additionally, the number of dominant species in the microbial community of soils where wild P. dongsun grows is higher than that of the other two soil types suitable for its growth.

Microbial diversity of different habitats for P. dongsun growth

To assess the α-diversity of microorganisms in different soil samples, this study conducted a Shannon index analysis on the microbial communities of each soil sample. The results showed that: compared with YS soil, the Shannon indices of JC soil and ZC soil were lower, and there was a significant difference in the Shannon index between ZC soil and YS soil; in addition, the Shannon index of GC soil was higher than that of YS soil, but there was no significant difference between the two. Meanwhile, the Shannon index of GC soil is significantly higher than that of ZC soil and JC soil (Fig. 5A). It can be seen from this that the Shannon index analysis further confirms that the microorganisms in YS soil and GC soil have higher richness and more uniform distribution.

Diversity and of Microbial Communities in Different Soils. (A) Shannon index of α-diversity. (B) PCA analysis of β-diversity. Error bars indicated the standard errors of results from three biological replicates. The significance level for statistical testing was set at p < 0.05.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a standard method for identifying differences between datasets, which transforms raw data into a series of linearly independent components through linear transformation. To analyze the differences among samples from different groups, PCA analysis was conducted for each group. The results showed that at the genus level: the contribution rate of PC1 was 31.38%, the contribution rate of PC2 was 15.35%, and the cumulative explanatory rate of the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) was 46.73%, indicating that the model had good representativeness. Among them, the scattered points of the YS group and GC group were distributed at the same end of PC1, while the scattered points of the JC group and ZC group were distributed at the other ends of PC1. This indicated that the scattered points of the YS group were relatively scattered from those of the JC group and ZC group, with significant differences among samples, and the differences between the soil samples of the YS group and GC group were small. In addition, the scattered points of the JC group and ZC group showed a tendency of overlap and were distributed at the same end of PC1, indicating no difference between the two types of soil samples. In summary: there was no significant difference between the soil samples of YS group and GC group; there was no difference between the soil samples of JC group and ZC group; the soil samples of YS soil and GC soil had significant differences from those of JC soil and ZC soil, showing a distribution at two ends (Fig. 5B).

Correlation analysis between chemical properties and soil microbial communities of YS soil and GC soil

To further investigate the complex relationships between different soil chemical properties and the soil microbial communities that favor P. dongsun growth, a correlation heatmap was constructed to visually depict the associations between soil factors and microbial communities. As shown in Fig. 6, a series of soil chemical properties exhibited strong correlations with microbial communities. In YS and GC soils, Lysobacter, Pseudolabrys, and Rhodoplanes had a positive correlation with AN, TP, and AP; Bradyrhizobium had a positive correlation with TP, AP, and AK; Trebonia showed a positive correlation with pH, TK, and AK; Sphingomonas showed a negative correlation with pH, TK, and AK; Candidatus-Angelobacter had a strong positive correlation with TK, while Candidatus Binatus had a strong negative correlation with TP and AP. In addition, in GC soil, Bradyrhizobium, Trebonia, Lysobacter, Pseudolabrys, and Rhodoplanes exhibited a strong negative correlation with OM content. Among them, Trebonia had a significant positive correlation with pH and AK content (correlation coefficients were 0.99 and 0.82, respectively) and a significant negative correlation with AN in YS soil. Candidatus Angelo had a significant positive correlation with the OM (correlation coefficient was 0.99) in YS soils; Pseudolabrys had a significant positive correlation with TN, TP, and AP (all correlation coefficients reached 0.99) in YS soils. In GC soil, Bradyrhizobium and Lysobacter showed a significant positive correlation with AN and AP, respectively. To summarize, the associations between the microbial communities and soil chemical factors in YS and GC exhibit commonalities.

Discussion

Rhizosphere microorganisms are closely related to the growth and development, nutrient utilization, environmental adaptation, and rhizosphere microecological balance of edible fungi. In this study, we analyzed the microbial community composition across four types of Phallus dongsun cultivation soils. The results demonstrated that the relative abundances of Pseudomonadota, Gemmatimonadota, Myxococcota, as well as the genera Bradyrhizobium, Sphingomonas, Candidatus Angelobacter, and Rhodoplanes were significantly higher in GC and YS soils than in JC and ZC soils. Pseudomonadota, as the dominant phylum of rhizosphere soil microorganisms, can decompose phosphate, organic matter, nitrogen, and ammonium salts, produce siderophores and IAA (indole-3-acetic acid) biotin, and promote plant growth24. When Inoculation with Pseudomonas putida promoted the growth of Agaricus bisporus, shortened its growth cycle, and linearly increased the relative abundance of key bacterial genera associated with its growth25. Among the putative roles of the most abundant bacteria in morel substrates, members of the genus Pseudomonas are presumed to be involved in the fructification of morels26. Apart from Pseudomonadota, the abundances of Gemmatimonadota and Myxococcota in the wild soils and high-yield soils were significantly higher than those in no-yield and medium-yield soils in this study. Gemmatimonadota and Myxococcota are extremely abundant in soil and can degrade organic matter27,28. In the rice-mushroom rotation system, Gemmatimonadota has a significant positive impact on soil properties and enzyme activities, and is closely related to the accumulation of soil nutrients29. The analysis at the genus level showed that Bradyrhizobium had the highest abundance, followed by Trebonia in this study—a result consistent with the main microbial groups in the loquat-tea intercropping system. Moreover, in the loquat-tea intercropping system, Bradyrhizobium and Trebonia exhibited strong correlations with the contents of soil organic matter (OM), available nitrogen (AN), available phosphorus (AP), and available potassium (AK)30. In addition, the relative abundance of Sphingomonas in wild soil was significantly higher than that in the other three soil types in this study. Notably, Sphingomonas can produce plant growth hormones such as gibberellins and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and thus exhibits a prominent role in promoting plant growth31. Similarly, a few related studies analysis also confirmed that the tested plant growth‑promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) have the functional potential for nitrogen fixation, phosphate solubilization, and the synthesis of IAA and siderophores32,33. In this study, LEfSe analysis further revealed that the biomarker with significant differences in high-yield soil was Nitrososphaerota phylum. Most microorganisms of this phylum are chemoautotrophic, capable of performing ammonia oxidation, synergistically participating in the soil nitrogen cycle, and increasing soil nitrogen supply28. The wild soil contained the largest number of microbial taxa with significant differences, and its main biomarkers were Pseudomonadota, Gemmatimonadota, and Myxococcota. These phyla belong to beneficial microbial groups and are closely related to the degradation of soil organic matter, the cycling and utilization of nitrogen, and soil enzyme activity24,29. Therefore, as common beneficial microorganisms, Pseudomonadota, Gemmatimonadota, Myxococcota, Bradyrhizobium, Trebonia, and Sphingomonas have the potential to provide a healthy soil environment for the growth of P. dongsun.

Shannon index is an indicator for measuring species diversity. It quantifies diversity by comprehensively considering the species richness and evenness in a community, with higher values indicating greater diversity34. The analysis of microbial α-diversity in the four soil types in this study showed that wild soil and high-yield soil had higher Shannon indices, indicating that the microorganisms in these two soil types had greater richness and a more uniform distribution. A similar study analyzed the microbial diversity in the rhizosphere soils of 11 wild celery plants and 1 rhizosphere soil sample of artificially cultivated celery (CK). The results showed that except for three wild soil samples whose Shannon indices were lower than that of the artificially cultivated soil sample, the Shannon indices of the remaining wild soil samples were all higher than that of the artificially cultivated soil35. Therefore, wild soil exhibits higher microbial diversity compared to artificially cultivated soil. Principal component analysis (PCA) is one of the indicators for β-diversity assessment. The distance in the PCA scatter plot reflects the similarity of community structures: the closer the distance, the more similar the microbial community composition between samples; conversely, the greater the distance, the greater the difference36. In the present study, PCA analysis indicated no significant distinction between wild soil samples and those derived from high-yield soils. Notably, wild soils and high-yield soils differed markedly from soils corresponding to no-yield and medium-yield P. dongsun cultivation, with this variation manifesting as a bimodal distribution. Therefore, microbial diversity analysis further indicated that the soil supporting wild growth of P. dongsun and the soil conducive to high yield of P. dongsun were highly similar in terms of microbial community composition and diversity characteristics.

The contents of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in soil are closely associated with its microbial communities. Soil organic matter (OM) characteristics serve as the primary driver shaping soil microbial communities across different land use types; notably, soil bacterial communities are more sensitive to changes in soil OM and geochemical properties than fungal communities37. This also is reflected in the fact that bacteria are significantly more efficient than fungi in the process of organic matter degradation38. In this study, the abundances of Pseudomonadota, Gemmatimonadota, and Myxococcota in wild soil and high-yield soil were higher than those in no-yield and medium-yield soils. It is inferred that the relatively low contents of soil OM, TN, and AN in these two soil types may be attributed to the presence of these three phyla, which promote the degradation of OM and nitrogen. A few Studies have shown that pH value and TK are the main factors influencing the community structures of bacteria and fungi39,40. In this study, the pH values and TK contents in wild soil and high-yield soil were significantly higher than those in no-yield and medium-yield soils, which may also explain why these two soil types have higher abundances of dominant microbial species and greater microbial diversity. Correlation analysis can be used to further investigate the complex relationships between different soil chemical factors and various soil microbial communities. A study employed correlation analysis and revealed that during the growth of D. rubrovolvata, there exists a dynamic coupling relationship among soil microbial communities, soil nutrient contents, and pH19. Studies have found that TN, AP, AK, and nitrate nitrogen are significantly positively correlated with Chloroflexota, Bacillus, and Bacillaceae family, while significantly negatively correlated with Pseudomonas and Bacteroides genus39. In this study, the main dominant microbial groups in wild soil also exhibited strong correlations with various soil chemical properties. Specifically, Trebonia showed a significantly positive correlation with the pH value and AK, while a significantly negative correlation with AN; Candidatus Angelobacter displayed a significantly positive correlation with the OM content ; and Pseudolabrys had significantly positive correlations with the TN, TP, and AP. Therefore, based on the series of correlations between the chemical properties and microbial communities of wild soil, the high-yield and high-quality cultivation of P. dongsun may be achieved by adding relevant substances to improve the microbial community structure of its cultivation soil.

Based on the high similarity in a series of chemical properties, microbial community composition, and diversity between wild soil and high-yield soil, it is inferred that wild-simulated ecological cultivation of P. dongsun may be an effective strategy to achieve high P. dongsun yields. Furthermore, during the wild-simulated cultivation process, the composition of the microbial community in P. dongsun cultivation soil may be regulated by changing the soil chemical properties, thereby realizing high-yield cultivation of P. dongsun.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the soil chemical properties, microbial community composition, diversity, and their correlation in four types of soils for P. dongsun growth, aiming to provide theoretical basis and practical guidance for the high-yield cultivation of P. dongsun. The results showed significant differences in soil chemical properties among the four tested soils: compared with JC and ZC soils, YS and GC soils had significantly higher pH values, as well as higher contents of TP, AP, TK and K, but lower contents of OM, TN and AN. At the microbial community level, there was no significant difference in community composition among the four soils, but significant differences in the relative abundance of dominant taxa were observed. Specifically, the relative abundances of Pseudomonadota, Gemmatimonadota, Myxococcota, Bradyrhizobium, Sphingomonas, Candidatus Angelobacter, and Rhodoplanes in GC and YS soils were significantly higher than those in JC and ZC soils. LEfSe analysis revealed that the number of dominant microbial species in soils unfavorable for P. dongsun growth (JC soil) was lower than that in suitable soils. And the PCA results further demonstrated that there was no significant difference in soil samples between the YS group and the GC group. However, the soil samples of the YS and GC groups differed significantly from those of the JC and ZC groups. Correlation analysis showed that the associations between microbial communities and soil chemical factors in YS and GC soils exhibited both commonalities and specificities: key microbial genera such as Lysobacter, Pseudolabrys, Rhodoplanes, and Bradyrhizobium were positively correlated with TP, AP, and AK, which were relatively high in YS and GC soils, while Trebonia and Sphingomonas had obvious correlations with pH, TK, and AK; several genera also showed significant correlations with OM and AN, which were relatively low in YS and GC soils. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the soils supporting wild P. dongsun growth and those conducive to its high yield are highly similar in microbial community composition, diversity, and chemical properties, thus providing important theoretical support for the development and promotion of wild-simulated high-yield cultivation of P. dongsun.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The soil samples in this study were collected from the P. dongsun cultivation bases and surrounding wild P. dongsun growth areas in Dafang County, Bijie City, Guizhou Province. The sampling time was October 3rd, 2024, which corresponds to the egg stage of P. dongsun. Based on the growth performance of P. dongsun, the samples were categorized into four types: wild soil (YS, yield: 81.47 g/m2), high-yield soil (GC, yield: 353.65 g/m2), medium-yield soil (ZC, yield: 134.87 g/m2), and no-yield soil (JC), as shown Figure S1 and Table 1. Among them, the sampling site for YS is Heishadi, Lütang Township, Dafang County, Bijie City, Guizhou Province, at an altitude of 1946 m; the sampling site for GC is Wufengyan, Wufeng Community, Liulong Town, Dafang County, Bijie City, Guizhou Province, at an altitude of 1650 m; the sampling sites for ZC and JC are Qingliangzi, Duijiang Town, Dafang County, Bijie City, Guizhou Province, at an altitude of 1690 m. Five duplicate sampling points were set for each soil type, resulting in a total of 20 samples. Five-point sampling method was adopted for sample collection: 10 m×10 m quadrat was demarcated at each sampling point, and five positions (including the diagonal points and the center) were selected. After removing the surface litter, the 0–20 cm topsoil was collected using a sterile soil auger. Approximately 500 g of soil was collected from each position; the soil samples from the same quadrat were mixed, sealed in sterile polyethylene bags, and labeled with the sampling time, location, and soil type. All samples were transported back to the laboratory within 24 h. After removing impurities such as stones and plant roots, each sample was divided into two parts: one part was stored in a 80 °C refrigerator for subsequent microbial sequencing analysis, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate, while the other part was air-dried naturally and passed through a 2 mm sieve for the determination of soil chemical properties.

Determination of soil chemical properties

All soil chemical properties were determined using standardized methods, as detailed below: Soil pH was measured in accordance with the standard for soil pH determination specified in NY/T 1121.2–200641. TN was determined via the Kjeldahl nitrogen method42; TP was determined using the NaOH alkali fusion-molybdenum antimony resistance spectrophotometric method43; TK was determined using the sodium hydroxide alkali fusion-flame photometry method44; AN was determined through the alkali hydrolysis diffusion method; available soil phosphorus was determined using the spectrophotometric method45; AK was determined via the ammonium acetate extraction method46; and OM was determined by the potassium dichromate volumetric method47.

High-throughput sequencing analysis

Three grams of fresh soil was weighed, and total soil DNA was extracted using the Ezup Column-Based Soil DNA Extraction Kit from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd., following the kit’s instructions. The extracted DNA was then purified using a gel extraction kit buffer. After confirming the DNA concentration and integrity, Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. was entrusted to perform metagenomic high-throughput sequencing using the Illumina sequencing platform. The raw Illumina sequencing data have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), with the BioProject accession numbers PRJNA1396612.

After obtaining the metagenomic data, analyses such as community bar charts, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), LEfSe analysis, and diversity analysis were performed using the cloud analysis platform of Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. Firstly,

Microbial community composition was evaluated across samples at various taxonomic levels. Systematic generation of species abundance tables was performed using QIIME 2, followed by visualization in R packages. Stacked bar plots were used to represent community structure at phylum, class, genus, and species levels for each sample19. Secondly, LEfSe analysis is employed to species that exhibit significant variation across two or more groups, potentially serving as biomarkers. The streamlined process is as follows: Initially, the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test is applied to assess species abundance among groups, thereby identifying species with notable differences. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon rank sum test is conducted on the subspecies of the identified species to ascertain if they consistently align with a specific taxonomic rank. Ultimately, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) is utilized to pinpoint the definitive differential species34. Finally, Mothur v. 1.30.2 was used to calculate the Shannon indices, and multiple comparisons for the Least Significant Difference (LSD) in a one-way ANOVA (SPSS 16.0) were used to analyze the differences in alpha-diversity between the groups. In addition, beta diversity was analyzed based on principal component analysis (PCA) using R software34,48.

Data analysis

Data statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0, GraphPad Prism 9.0, and OriginPro 2025 software. When analyzing the multi-group differences in soil chemical properties with SPSS 20.0, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was first conducted to test for overall differences among groups. Subsequently, the least significant difference (LSD) method and Duncan’s new multiple range test (Duncan method) were used for pairwise comparisons to accurately identify the specific groups with significant differences. The significance level for statistical testing was set at 95% (i.e., α = 0.05). Visualization of soil chemical properties was completed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. During the correlation analysis, the false discovery rate (FDR) approach was used to adjust the p-values for multiple comparisons, followed by the application of the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method to further refine the adjusted p-values. We filtered the corrected results to retain only correlation data with an absolute correlation coefficient |r| > 0.8 and a p-value < 0.0534. Visualization of these correlations was accomplished using OriginPro 2025 software, where red and blue colors represent positive and negative correlations between soil microorganisms and soil chemical properties, respectively.
