Does Disability Bias Exist in Pediatric Orthopaedic Surgery?
Authors: Taylor M Adams, Maria F Canizares, Shanika D Silva, Benjamin J Shore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImplicit bias among health care providers can influence clinical decision-making and patient outcomes. These unconscious attitudes may be subtly shaping health care interactions and priorities in orthopaedics for complex care delivery in neuromuscular patients. This study assessed implicit disability bias among pediatric orthopaedic providers and examined whether factors such as race, gender, education level, medical specialty, and prior experience working with individuals with disabilities influenced bias levels.MethodsMembers of a national pediatric orthopaedic organization were invited via email to complete a Qualtrics survey assessing implicit bias in orthopaedics. Implicit association test (IAT) scores (D-scores) were calculated based on respondent speed and accuracy. Statistical analyses included Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data and Wilcoxon rank sum or Kruskal–Wallis rank sum tests for continuous data. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure adjusted P values to control for false discovery rates.ResultsOf the 183 providers surveyed, 86 (47%) fully completed the IAT, while 97 (53%) partially completed it. Among full respondents, 71% were male and 29% female, with a mean age range of 35-52 years. IAT scores showed that 73% of providers had a strong preference for abled patients. No significant differences in levels of bias were found across’ demographic, educational, and occupational characteristics (P > .05). Providers working primarily with complex care patients also showed a strong preference for the physically abled (n = 80, 93%).ConclusionsPediatric orthopaedic providers exhibited a moderate-to-strong implicit preference for abled patients, regardless of background or experience. These biases persisted even among those frequently treating complex care patients, suggesting that exposure alone may not mitigate bias. Increased education and awareness are needed to address implicit attitudes and their potential impact on patient care. Further research is needed to understand its impact on the care delivered to complex care patients.Key Concepts(1)Disability bias refers to negative attitudes or beliefs about people with disabilities that operate either explicitly or implicitly.(2)Pediatric orthopaedic surgeons demonstrated a strong implicit preference for abled individuals, regardless of provider’s background or experience.(3)Implicit bias can undermine communication, skew clinical judgment, and contribute to inequities in access and quality of care for patients with disabilities.(4)Mitigating disability bias requires coordinated strategies that combine individual awareness, disability-focused education, and system-level policies that prioritize accessibility and inclusion.Level of EvidenceLevel IVKeywords: Disability, Implicit bias, Neuromuscular, Orthopaedic surgery, Implicit association test (IAT)

Introduction

Patients with disabilities (PWD) remain among the most underserved in the United States, experiencing shorter life expectancy, higher chronic disease burden, and poorer health outcomes [[1], [2], [3]]. PWD also receive unequal treatment [4] and face persistent barriers to health care access compared to those without disabilities [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. An underrecognized driver of this inequality is disability bias, defined as negative attitudes or beliefs about individuals with disabilities that may be either explicit (conscious) or implicit (unconscious) [10]. While explicit bias is more easily identified, implicit bias persists even among health care professionals, often influencing clinical judgment in subtle but impactful ways [11]. For instance, caregivers of medically complex children report clinician apathy, assumptions, and insufficient knowledge as key drivers of health care discrimination [12]. These problems persist into adulthood as evidenced by limited accessibility in medical and surgical practices [13], lower rates of preventive breast cancer screenings [14], and differential cancer treatment recommendations for disabled patients [15,16]. Additional manifestations of disability-based discrimination include lack of accessible medical equipment, ineffective communication, and provider assumptions about a person’s cognitive or functional abilities [17,18], which contribute to increased psychological distress, higher rates of depression, and decreased overall quality of life among PWD [19,20].

Across health care disciplines, implicit ableism is well documented: physician assistants demonstrate a preference for younger, nondisabled patients [21], applied behavior analysts show negative implicit attitudes toward autistic children [22], and occupational therapy students displayed a strong implicit preference for abled patients [10,23]. Among mental health clinicians, higher levels of implicit bias were associated with overdiagnosis of psychiatric disability [24]. When such biases involve pediatric populations, they are linked to less attentive care, poorer communication, and unfounded assumptions about a child’s capabilities [12], contributing to caregiving burden, diminished trust, delayed diagnoses, and fragmented care [12,13]. Notably, even professionals whose work center on disability are not exempt from bias. In a recent study of 417 disability professionals, 77.2% explicitly preferred nondisabled individuals and more than 80% showed implicit bias [11]. These patterns not only adversely affect children’s immediate health outcomes but may also contribute to lasting psychological distress, lower quality of life, and reduced health care utilization into adulthood [19,20].

Despite the growing awareness of implicit bias in health care, research on its prevalence among pediatric orthopaedic providers is limited. This gap matters because these clinicians guide diagnosis and longitudinal management for children with neuromuscular conditions (eg, cerebral palsy, spinal muscular atrophy), often alongside intellectual and developmental comorbidities; where mobility aids such as ankle–foot orthoses, wheelchairs, and braces may also be perceived as markers of weakness and be underprescribed [25]. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to evaluate whether implicit disability bias exists among pediatric orthopaedic providers and to examine whether specific demographic, occupational, or experiential factors are associated with higher levels of bias. We hypothesize that implicit disability bias will be present across pediatric orthopaedic practitioners and that higher levels of bias will be observed in those who do not primarily work with PWD.

Methods

This study was a cross-sectional survey sent to members of the Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America (POSNA). An institutional review board approval was obtained for this study, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before survey completion.

Study cohort

Participants included practicing orthopaedic surgeons and active members of POSNA. Participants were asked to complete an electronic survey included in their organization’s quarterly newsletter, with a reminder email sent every 2 weeks over a 3-month interval. Inclusion criteria consisted of currently practicing orthopaedic surgeons and providers in U.S. and Canada with an active membership.

Study design

The survey consisted of 3 sections: (1) acknowledgment and consent (2) 10 demographic questions developed by the authors addressing age, gender, race, training level, type of practice, practice region, prior experiences of PWD, and prior bias training; and (3) the disability implicit association test (IAT) to assess implicit attitudes toward PWD which is an established validated tool [26,27]. The survey was informed by the first author’s prior experience designing and publishing pediatric implicit bias instruments [28]. The IAT was created using the iatgen tool (Thomas Carpenter, Department of Psychology, Seattle Pacific University, Seattle, WA, 98119) [29] (available at https://iatgen.wordpress.com/). Prior to distribution, the disability IAT was pilot tested with a small group of orthopaedic clinicians not included in the analytic sample to evaluate clarity, timing, and technical performance; minor wording and interface refinements were made based on feedback. The survey was distributed electronically via Qualtrics from 03/05/2024, to March 28, 2024. Participants completed the survey anonymously and no identifying information was collected unless participants requested to know their scores. In such cases, participants acknowledged the loss of anonymity and were prompted to provide their email address for score distribution.

Survey instrument

For this study, the disability IAT was used. The IAT begins with a series of training blocks to familiarize participants with the testing format. Each training block includes multiple trials in which respondents categorize pictures of abled and disabled people or words with positive or negative connotations, according to on-screen instructions. Following the training blocks, participants complete testing blocks in which images or words are randomly presented on the screen and must be categorized as quickly as possible using designated computer keys. For example, in one testing block, participants may be instructed to sort images of an abled person and words with good connotation to the left side of the screen using the ‘e’ key and images of a disabled person and words with negative connotation to the right side of the screen using the ‘i’ key. In a subsequent block, the key assignments are reversed—images of an abled person and words with negative connotation to the left using the ‘e’ key and images with a disabled person and words with positive connotation to the right using the ‘i’ key.

The IAT measures the relative strength of association by comparing the response times and accuracy across conditions [30]. The D-score represents the degree to which participants more rapidly associate disability status with positive and negative attributes. In IAT terminology, the term “preference” refers to an implicit associative preference, meaning faster categorization when one concept (eg, nondisabled individuals) is paired with positive attributes compared with when disability is paired with positive attributes. This terminology does not imply a conscious or deliberate choice but reflects automatic or subconscious associative patterns measured through response latency. D-scores range from −2.0 to 2.0, with scores between −0.14 and 0.14 indicating no preference; 0.15 to 0.34 indicating a slight preference for nondisabled individuals; 0.35 to 0.64 indicating a moderate preference; and scores >0.65 indicating a strong preference for nondisabled individuals. Negative scores of −0.14 and lower reflect a relative high preference for people with disabilities [10,23]. IAT scores are generated through a D-algorithm that uses measurements like time and speed to generate a score (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

The implicit bias, as measured by the IAT D-score, was summarized for the surveyed providers using medians and interquartile ranges. Additionally, the IAT D-scores were categorized into discrete levels and presented as frequencies and percentages of responders within each level. For the continuous IAT D scores, comparisons across these characteristics were performed using Wilcoxon rank sum tests or Kruskal–Wallis rank sum tests as appropriate. All P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. The categorical IAT D-scores were visualized through bar plots for key factors, while continuous IAT D-scores were graphically represented using boxplots. To evaluate nonresponse bias, the demographic, educational, and occupational characteristics of providers who completed the IAT were compared to those who did not complete the IAT using Fisher’s exact tests. The analysis was conducted using R version 4.3.2.

Results

Of the 183 providers who attempted the survey, 86 (47%) completed the IAT while 97 (53%) did not. No statistically significant differences were found in provider demographic, educational, and occupational responses between respondents and nonrespondents (all P > .05).

Overall characteristics of respondents

Most respondents were male (71%), between 43 and 51 years of age (33%), and identified as White (73%). The majority completed their residency training and currently practice in the northeast region (35%), are based at university-affiliated hospitals (76%), and work at major teaching hospitals (79%). Furthermore, nearly all providers (91%) reported working at institutions where 75% or more of the patient population comprises pediatric patients. Notably, 93% of respondents reported currently working with patients with physical disabilities (Table 1).

Implicit disability attitudes

The IAT scores ranged from −0.12 (no preference) to 1.53 (strongly prefer nondisabled people), with a median of 0.91 (interquartile range: 0.64–1.07). In this sample, 73% of the orthopaedic providers exhibited a strong implicit preference for nondisabled patients, 17% exhibited a moderate preference, and only a small fraction of the 86 respondents scored as having slight preference for nondisabled people (5%) or no preference (5%) (Fig. 2). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test indicated that IAT D-scores were significantly greater than 0 (P < .001), providing evidence of implicit disability bias among participants.

Implicit disability attitudes by demographic and educational categories

When analyzing the IAT D-score by demographic and educational categories, we observed that male providers were observed to have a slightly higher median score than female providers (0.94 vs 0.71; P = .18). Providers in the older age group (≥52 years) also exhibited a higher median D-score (1.02) than providers aged 18-34 years (0.92) and 35-51 years (0.80) (P = .27). When analyzing by race, White providers had a slightly higher median IAT D score (0.94) compared to providers of other racial/ethnic backgrounds (0.83) (P = .28), but both White and non-White respondents demonstrated a strong implicit preference for nondisabled patients. Providers with a self-reported diagnosis of a physical disability had a numerically lower median IAT D-score (0.71) than providers without such a diagnosis (0.91) (P = .52). Providers practicing in the Midwest and the South had slightly higher median D-scores (1.02 and 0.91, respectively) compared to providers practicing in the West (0.75). Discussion

Our study of 86 orthopaedic providers revealed a particularly strong implicit preference for nondisabled patients. While our results echo prior work showing that most health care providers and students unconsciously favor nondisabled individuals; the magnitude of bias among orthopaedic providers appears greater than what has been recorded in these other subspecialties [31,32].

Consistent with prior analyses [11,23,33], our results show that implicit disability bias is evident across all provider demographics, training levels, or practice region, with no subgroup demonstrating significantly higher or lower bias. This is important to highlight because it suggests that implicit disability bias is not simply “picked up” in orthopaedic surgery as part of the specialty training program but is woven into broader societal attitudes about disability [31,32]. The mere fact of working with—or having prior bias training on—patients with disabilities did not predict lower bias scores in our study or others [11]. This echoes that exposure alone often fails to reduce implicit prejudice unless the exposure is positive, structured, and perspective changing [31]. A plausible explanation is that in routine clinical care, contact is often episodic and occurs in contexts of acute illness, high complexity, and unequal power dynamics, which may reinforce disability as a marker of limitation rather than broadening views of capability and personhood [34]. Moreover, implicit associations are shaped by lifelong cultural messaging and may be relatively resistant to change from clinical exposure alone, particularly when providers rarely receive explicit feedback or structured reflection that challenges automatic assumptions [35].

While our study did not directly measure how implicit disability bias influences clinical decision-making or patient outcomes, a growing body of evidence indicates that these unconscious attitudes can meaningfully exacerbate health care disparities, undermine communication, and erode trust among patients with disabilities [12,36]. In pediatric orthopaedics, implicit bias may influence not only major treatment decisions but also the cumulative “micro-decisions” that shape family experience, including how prognosis is framed, expectations for function are communicated, and the degree of shared decision-making and advocacy offered. This is particularly relevant in neuromuscular care, where heterogeneous outcomes and visible use of mobility aids may inadvertently be interpreted through a deficit lens, potentially lowering expectations or narrowing perceived possibilities. At the same time, orthopaedic surgery is inherently oriented toward restoring function and mobility; disability may therefore be unconsciously framed as impairment or loss. It is possible that some of the measured associations reflect this clinical orientation rather than animus or diminished commitment to patients. Nonetheless, even well-intentioned evaluative asymmetries may subtly shape communication, assumptions about quality of life, and alignment of care plans with family priorities, thereby contributing to disparities described in prior work.

To address this challenge within orthopaedic surgery, the literature supports a multifaceted strategy. As noted earlier, exposure to PDW or simply raising awareness is not enough but feedback from IAT workshops have shown that this awareness can promote self-reflection and motivate clinicians to seek change [21]. Beyond IAT feedback, training programs can increase knowledge and improve attitudes among health care students toward PWD [37]. A study by Sinha et al. 2024 including content on disability etiquette, patient-centered and interprofessional learning sessions for individuals with physical disabilities and intellectual and developmental disabilities, and a debriefing session with physiatrists showed significantly better attitude toward PWD post intervention [38]. When such awareness is paired with structured, positive encounters—through standardized patient programs or clinical rotations involving PWD as teachers—students not only report greater empathy but also show modest reductions in implicit bias on follow-up testing [39,40]. Similarly, previous studies have suggested that increasing provider awareness of bias, fostering advocacy, and improving communication skills can contribute to more equitable care for PWD [17]. In addition to provider-level interventions, structural changes such as policies that prioritize accessibility and inclusive care models are essential to creating health care environments that support equitable treatment for all patients [41].

This study has several limitations. First, the use of the IAT as a measure of implicit bias, while well-established, may not fully capture the complexity of real-world clinical behaviors in the setting of pediatric orthopaedic disability. The IAT assesses unconscious associations but does not necessarily predict explicit actions or decision-making in clinical practice. Additionally, the IAT does not have an established minimal clinically important difference as it is a psychometric measure of relative associative bias rather than a patient-centered clinical outcome. As such, while established interpretive categories (slight, moderate, and strong) describe the magnitude of implicit association, the clinical significance of a given D-score—particularly in terms of its impact on patient care—remains uncertain. Further research is needed to explore how implicit disability bias manifests in orthopaedic care, including potential impacts on surgical recommendations, resource allocation, and patient-provider interactions. Second, technical constraints related to survey distribution via Qualtrics and the relatively low IAT completion rate limited the effective sample size. While no differences were observed between respondents and nonrespondents based on available demographic data, nonresponse and partial participation may have resulted in unmeasured selection effects and limited the generalizability of our findings to the broader orthopaedic community. Third, although our team sought expert review to ensure a respectful and inclusive approach, we acknowledge that no members of our research team had lived experience with neuromuscular conditions, medical complexity, or physical disabilities. Engaging researchers and clinicians with lived experience in future studies will be critical in ensuring that disability perspectives are authentically and meaningfully incorporated into research and education efforts.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes valuable insights into a relatively understudied area in pediatric orthopaedic surgery and highlights the importance of continued investigation into implicit bias and its impact on care for PWD. Our goal was to foster dialog and reflection rather than to provide definitive solutions, recognizing that change in this area will require ongoing commitment and collaboration.
