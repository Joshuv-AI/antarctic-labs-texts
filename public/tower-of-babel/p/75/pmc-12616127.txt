Microplastics primarily provide habitats for benthic microalgae in Fujian coastal waters of China
Authors: Kang Wang, Conghui Peng, Hui Lin, Huina Lin, Baohong Chen, Yahui Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMicroplastics (MPs) serve as habitats and vectors for microalgae—termed epimicroplastic microalgae (EMP-MA). This study analyzed the community structure and diversity of EMP-MA in three bays of Fujian Province, and compared them with planktonic microalgae (PM) communities. Results showed both EMP-MA and PM communities mainly comprised diatoms and dinoflagellates, but differed significantly. Specifically, the relative abundance of benthic microalgae in EMP-MA communities was significantly higher than that in PM communities, with all dominant species of EMP-MA communities being benthic species. These results meant that MPs primarily provide habitats for benthic microalgae and may expand their spatial distribution. In addition, environmental variables exerted weaker influences on EMP-MA than on PM, which might be attributed to the buffering effects of biofilm. This study holds significance for deepening the understanding of the vertical distribution dynamics of marine microalgae and the effects of MPs on marine biological processes.

Introduction

Microplastics (MPs) are commonly defined as small plastic particles with a diameter smaller than 5 mm, primarily originating from sources such as cleaning products, cosmetics, fertilizers, medical supplies, and the weathering of large plastics.1,2,3,4 MPs have been found to have significant negative impacts on human health and environments.1,5 MPs exhibit ubiquitous distribution across global marine environments, with confirmed presence in the Pacific,6 Atlantic,7 and even in polar glaciers.8,9,10 MPs have additionally been documented in nearly all inland and marginal marine waters of China, where their abundance in estuarine and coastal bay systems consistently displays significantly higher levels than those observed in open-sea regions.11,12,13,14,15,16,17,18 It is estimated that there were approximately 3,200 kilotons of buoyant plastics in the global ocean in 2020, with 500 kilotons of plastics entering the global ocean annually.19 Plastic pollution in the global ocean continues to rise relentlessly, posing substantial risks to marine ecosystems; however, effective mitigation measures remain conspicuously absent.

Abundant MPs serve as extensive habitats and vectors for microalgae,20,21,22 which are termed as epimicroplastic microalgae (EMP-MA).23,24 EMP-MA have some potential influences on marine environments and organisms. First, it may facilitate species invasions, particularly of harmful algae, thereby disrupting local microalgae communities and potentially triggering harmful algal blooms (HABs) under favorable conditions.20,22,25,26,27,28 Second, EMP-MA facilitates the entry of MPs into marine food webs, thereby posing a significant threat to the survival and well-being of aquatic organisms.29,30 Predators may accidentally ingest those MPs coated with microalgae.31,32 However, MPs are challenging for predators to digest, significantly impacting their growth and potentially causing mortality.32,33,34 Furthermore, they disrupt matter cycling and energy flow within marine food webs.33 Third, EMP-MA may play an important role in alleviating MPs pollution, as they may be involved in the biodegradation and biotransformation of MPs.20,25,35 Therefore, the microalgal biodegradation of plastics positioned as a sustainable approach exhibits considerable potential for mitigating plastic pollution while producing valuable by-products.36

Some studies identified those microalgae adhering to MPs by microscopic observation or gene testing; they were primarily composed of some diatom (Navicula, Nitzschia, and Amphora) and a small amount of dinoflagellates (Prorocentrum and Amphidinium).20,25,37,38,39 Notably, these microalgal species generally exhibit benthic ecological habits, leading us to hypothesize that MPs primarily serve as habitats for benthic microalgae. This is of significant importance for enhancing our understanding of the role of MPs in shaping marine ecosystems. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct in-depth research on the community structure and diversity of the EMP-MA community in the waters polluted by MPs. Dongshan Bay (DSB), Luoyuan Bay (LYB), and Quanzhou Bay (QZB) are prominent coastal bays in China, distinguished by their abundant aquatic resources, thriving shipping industries, and robust tourism sectors. The high density of human activities has made these bays critical hotspots for the accumulation of diverse pollutants. Studies have revealed that these bays are contaminated with MPs. Specifically, MPs concentrations in the surface water of Quanzhou Bay and Dongshan Bay reached 230 and 100 particles/m3, respectively. Meanwhile, MPs levels in sediments of Luoyuan Bay were measured at 382 particles/kg.40,41,42 Additionally, these bays share comparable environmental characteristics, both being characterized by semi-diurnal tides and a subtropical monsoon climate. Consequently, they not only serve as a natural laboratory for EMP-MA research but also enable findings from different bays to validate one another, thereby strengthening the robustness of the final conclusions. This study conducted field surveys across the three bays (Figure 1), with a primary focus on the species composition, taxonomic characteristics, and biodiversity of EMP-MA and planktonic microalgae (PM) communities. Additionally, it examined the correlations between environmental factors and microalgae communities. The findings not only provide theoretical foundations and datasets to advance future research on microalgal biodiversity and the ecological effects of MPs but also carry significant implications for the sustainable utilization and management of plastic products in coastal regions.Figure 1Study area and sampling stations

Results

Community structure and diversity

In PM communities, QZB and LYB exhibited the highest (172) and lowest (56) species richness, respectively (Figure 2). Similarly, in EMP-MA communities, DSB and LYB displayed the highest (135) and lowest (73) species richness, respectively. Diatom (Bacillariophyta) was the most diverse group, 45–156 and 69–129 diatom species were identified in PM and EMP-MA communities, respectively (Figure 2), with the average relative abundance (RA) of 88.07% and 94.06%, respectively (Figure 3B). Dinoflagellate (Pyrrophyta) constituted the second most diverse group, with a total of 8–10 and 0–8 species identified in the PM and EMP-MA communities, respectively. Their average RA was 8.80% and 1.65% in these two communities, respectively. In addition, Chlorophyta, Cyanophyta, and Xanthophyta were also found in the two communities; however, their species richness and RA was far less than those of diatom and dinoflagellate. Correlations between microalgae and environmental variables

The results of canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) showed that 35.54% of the total variance in PM community structure was explained by 6 environmental variables (Table S1), with the first two ordination axes collectively accounting for 23.32% of the variance (Figure 9A). Specifically, CCA 1 explained 12.31% of the total variance in community structure, mainly correlated with salinity, nitrate, nitrite, ammonium, and phosphate. CCA 2 explained an additional 11.01% of the variance, mainly correlated with temperature. Temperature and phosphate, as significant environmental variables (p < 0.01), were selected with the forward selection. Notably, certain species, including two dominant species (Navicula sp. and Pseudo-nitzschia pungens), exhibited significant positive correlations with temperature, phosphate, nitrate, nitrite, and ammonium, but displayed significant negative correlations with salinity (p < 0.05). Discussion

Differences in community structure between epimicroplastic microalgae and planktonic microalgae communities

There were significant differences in community structure between EMP-MA and PM. Notably, the RA of pennate diatoms in EMP-MA communities was significantly higher than that in PM communities. These observed discrepancies between EMP-MA and PM communities are closely associated with the ecological habits of diatoms. Diatoms are taxonomically categorized into two primary groups based on valve symmetry: centric (radially symmetrical) and pennate (bilaterally symmetrical) diatoms.43 A key morphological distinction lies in the presence of a raphe—a slit-like structure on the frustule (cell wall). While many pennate diatoms possess this raphe, which enables their remarkable ability to adhere to and glide across substrate surfaces,44,45 centric diatoms entirely lack a raphe in their frustules. This functional disparity underpins their divergent ecological niches: centric diatoms primarily inhabit the water column, whereas pennate diatoms are predominantly associated with substrate surfaces, where their raphe-mediated motility confers a selective advantage.46 Adhesion of diatoms is a complex process that is mediated by extracellular polymeric substances (EPSs) mainly consisting of polysaccharides and proteins.47,48,49 Within these matrix components, polysaccharides are the main source of stickiness of EPS and are essential for microalgal adhesion.50,51 Several studies have shown that Navicula and Nitzschia are common taxa and major producers of bound polysaccharides in epibiont microalgal communities.52,53,54 Glucose is one of the most predominant monosaccharides comprising the binding polysaccharides of Navicula,55,56 Nitzschia,57,58 and Amphora.59 Beyond glucose, the EPS of Navicula are also enriched with acidic monosaccharides, including glucuronic acid, along with sulfate and pyruvate moieties, which collectively facilitate the formation of acidic binding sites. These sites function as intercellular gels, promoting cell aggregation and thereby enhancing the organism’s adhesive capacity.60,61 These characteristics may be essential for the widespread distribution of N. perminuta in epibiotic microalgal communities worldwide, such as the nearshore waters of China, the Baltic Sea, Spain, Korea, and Antarctica.62 Protein is another important component of EPS, and studies have found that total protein makes up as much as 13% of the EPS of Amphora coffeaeformis.63 Proteins can also interact physicochemically with polysaccharides to enhance the stability and robustness of the attachment structure, possibly determining the attachment process of microalgae to the substrates.50 Thus, the abundant extracellular polysaccharides and proteins may enhance the adhesion of dominant species to MPs.64 In addition, EPS can also form diverse mucilaginous structures for sessile adhesion.65 Notably, the non-filamentous structures often exhibit greater tolerance to intense physical disturbances compared to their filamentous counterparts. Consequently, non-filamentous diatom taxa, including Navicula, Nitzschia, and Amphora, possess enhanced competitive advantages and a higher likelihood of becoming dominant species within EMP-MA communities in flowing water environments.66,67

Except for diatoms, dinoflagellates were also a common group in EMP-MA and PM communities. Generally, epibiotic dinoflagellates are mainly represented by the genera Gambierdiscus, Ostreopsis, Coolia, Amphidinium, and Prorocentrum,68,69,70 which could be found on various surfaces (macrophytes, rocks, corals, and detritus) and in the water column.71,72 Up to now, some species have been found on the surface of marine plastic debris, including Prorocentrum sp., Alexandrium sp., Ceratium sp., Coolia sp., Dinophysis sp., Pentapharsodinium tirrenicum, Heterocapsa sp., and Ostreopsis sp.20,25,73,74,75,76 In this study, Corythodinium reticulatum and Katodinium glaucum were identified on the surface of MPs, which have rarely been reported in existing research. Although dinoflagellates do not have a raphe such as diatoms, its adhesion on the surface of MPs is also correlated with cell shape. Studies have shown that flat cell shape is favorable to adhesion, which can increase the contact area between microalgae and MPs.77,78 Prorocentrum generally has a flat cell shape and possesses a mucus-secreting capability, which facilitates the attachment of algal cells to substrate surfaces.77,78 Other species, such as Coolia sp. and Gambierdiscus sp., adhere loosely to the substrate surface and are easily swept away by wave action, becoming resuspended in the water column.79,80,81 In addition, the substrate preference of dinoflagellates also plays a critical role in their adhesion. For example, Gambierdiscus tends to adhere to biotic substrates such as macroalgae and turf algae, exhibiting a higher growth rate.82,83,84 However, Ostreopsis prefers rocky surfaces over macroalgae as attachment sites.85 Except for diatoms and dinoflagellates, other microalgae did not display a significant difference between EMP-MA and PM communities.

In this study, dinoflagellates did not dominate EMP-MA communities; their RA and species richness were both lower than those of diatoms. However, dinoflagellate blooms frequently exhibit harmful and even toxic effects on marine life. Dinoflagellates identified in this study were primarily composed of species belonging to the genus Prorocentrum, specifically including P. donghaiense, P. mexicanum, P. micans, and P. triestinum; they occasionally contribute to the occurrence of HABs (Li et al., 2011). P. donghaiense has been the most frequently recorded causative species in Chinese coastal waters.86 P. donghaiense blooms have dominated the East China Sea, especially around the Changjiang Estuary, since 1998.87 P. donghaiense blooms have also frequently occurred in the coastal waters of Japan and Korea,88 in addition to being recorded in the coastal waters of India,89 the Mediterranean Sea of Italy,90 and the southern coast of Myanmar.91 Although P. donghaiense cannot produce toxin, it is a typical harmful species due to its negative impacts on phytoplankton biodiversity and zooplankton abundance.92,93 Actually, there were nine benthic species belonging to Prorocentrum with the ability to produce diarrhetic toxins, okadaic acid (OA), and methyl derivatives dinophysistoxins94,95. They may also adhere to MPs due to their characteristics being similar to P. donghaiense. Studies have also shown that polystyrene MPs promoted the OA production of P. lima96 and the paralytic shellfish toxin production of Alexandrium tamarense mediated by reactive oxygen species.97 Therefore, MPs, as vectors of harmful algae and the source of oxidative stress, may facilitate the invasion of harmful algae, stimulate the increase of HABs, and amplify the toxic effects of HABs on marine life.20,22,25,26,27,28,76,98

Microplastics primarily provide habitats for benthic microalgae

Benthic microalgae mainly live in bottom water by attaching to various substrates, including sediments, rocks, and bottom vegetation.23,24,99,100,101 However, this study found that many benthic species belonging to Navicula, Nitzschia, Amphora, and Cocconeis resided in surface waters through attachment to MPs. The RA of benthic microalgae in EMP-MA communities was significantly higher than those in PM communities, and all dominant species in EMP-MA communities were benthic species, which indicated that MPs mainly provide habitats for benthic microalgae and may expand the spatial distribution of those benthic species not inhabiting planktonic niches. Notably, the cell densities of EMP-MA were far lower than those of PM. Consequently, the spatial expansions of species inhabiting both epimicroplastic and planktonic niches—such as C. subtilis, N. perminuta, and Nitzschia fonticola—are likely insignificant under current MPs pollution scenarios. Conversely, dynamics may differ for other microalgae exclusively inhabiting epimicroplastic niches, such as N. climacospheniae, Rhoicosphenia genuflexa, and T. parva. Despite their similarly low cell densities, the MPs-driven spatial expansion of these species could hold greater ecological implications. Should marine MPs pollution remain unmitigated, the MPs-induced spatial expansion of benthic microalgae is expected to become progressively more pronounced. Similarly, previous studies also reported abundant benthic diatoms on plastic debris including MPs. The first study on EMP-MA had described those benthic microalgae, such as Mastogloia angulata, Mastogloia pusilla, Mastogloia hulburti, Cyclotella meneghiniana, and Pleurosigma sp., attached to the MPs from the Sargasso Sea.102 Some species belonging to Navicula, Nitzschia, and Amphora were also found on the plastic debris from the Arabian Gulf37 and the North Atlantic,20 which are commonly substrate-associated and known biofilm formers in aquatic environments.38 In the epiplastic microalgae community in the coastal waters of Australia, the total RA of Nitzschia, Amphora, Licmophora, and Navicula was as high as 76.4%.25 And benthic diatoms belonging to the genera Amphora, Navicula, and Nitzschia exhibited significant enrichment within the eukaryotic community associated with MPs collected from the Eastern Mediterranean Sea.39 Both benthic microalgae and MPs are ubiquitous in the global ocean.103,104,105,106 Although this study was conducted exclusively in Fujian coastal waters, it is reasonable to infer that this phenomenon may extend to the global marine environment.

MPs can provide stable habitats and good vectors for benthic microalgae. Some studies have also shown that plastics have excellent persistence and usually take decades to hundreds of years to decompose fully in natural environments.98,107,108 Although MPs could indeed be rapidly aged in the laboratory within a few days, and then produce more micro and nano plastics.109 In fact, the aging rates of MPs in natural waters are much slower than those in the laboratory, as they are less likely to experience high temperatures and strong oxidation.110 A study on MPs in sediments in the eastern equatorial Pacific Ocean showed that plastic debris did not show significant features of degradation after being buried for 20 years.111 In addition, due to the differences in density, 40% of plastic debris entering the marine system may sink initially, while the other 60% may float on the surface ocean.112 The densities of several common types of MPs, including polyethylene, polypropylene, expanded polystyrene, and polyvinyl chloride, are lower than that of seawater, thereby they generally float on the surface ocean and can be transported by subsurface currents from subtropical and subpolar regions to polar regions.113

Benthic diatoms play a crucial role in maintaining ecological balance, as they constitute a major component of primary production in aquatic ecosystems. They not only contribute to the stability of sediment surfaces, stabilize oxygen and nutrient exchange, but also serve as a critical food source for various benthic organisms. Additionally, benthic diatoms are used as ecological indicators due to their sensitivity to environmental fluctuations.114 Theoretically, MPs may exert non-negligible effects on benthic microalgae. On one hand, MPs can provide extensive habitats for benthic microalgae, potentially enhancing their biomass and triggering nutrient competition between benthic and planktonic microalgae.115,116,117 Notably, however, the results of this study reveal that the cell density of EMP-MA is even lower than that of PM in surface water, suggesting that EMP-MA may not currently pose a threat to phytoplankton through resource or niche competition. On the other hand, EMP-MA may provide predators with a new food source, potentially alleviating the grazing pressure on phytoplankton,22 and these predators may also die from ingesting MPs coated by microalgae.32,33 Studies further indicate that MPs with realistic concentrations (≤1.5 particles/L)significantly suppressed the abundance of zooplankton, thereby reducing grazing pressure on phytoplankton.118,119 Nevertheless, more studies have shown that those concentrations of MPs currently recorded in the environments have limited or negligible effects on the community structure and abundance of zooplankton and invertebrates.120,121,122,123,124 Moreover, some MPs' exposure experiments showed that MPs with concentrations far higher than reality have various effects on benthic microalgae, including inhibiting the growth and photosynthesis, stimulating the production of EPS, and causing physical damage to the cell membranes or cell walls.4,125,126,127,128 If MPs pollution cannot be effectively controlled, zooplankton grazing on MPs over the next century could disrupt marine carbon cycling, leading to an annual carbon export loss of 1 Gt yr−1, and reducing primary productivity and organic matter remineralization by up to 50% in heavily contaminated regions.129,130

Effects of environmental factors on epimicroplastic microalgae-MA communities

Environmental factors usually play important roles in the growth, motion, and adhesion of benthic microalgae.131 In this study, several dominant species showed significant negative correlations with temperature (Figures 8C and 8D). The only available study also indicated that temperature was one of the most important factors influencing the community structure of EMP-MA.39 Generally, the optimal temperatures of most diatom species range from 5°C to 20°C.132 In this study, the highest water temperature reached up to 21.8°C; higher temperatures might inhibit the growth of benthic diatoms. In the western Wadden Sea (Cadée & Hegeman, 1977), the Tagus estuary (Portugal) (Brito et al., 2013), and the Ems estuary (Germany),133 high temperatures additionally resulted in reduced biomass of benthic diatoms in summer compared to winter. Moreover, temperatures exceeding 20 °C may reduce the cell abundance of the motile diatoms and significantly change the community structure of benthic diatoms.134,135,136 These dominant species, such as N. perminuta, N. climacospheniae, and S. constricta, all fall within the motile diatom group, and thus may migrate to cooler microhabitats to avoid the high-temperature stress. A mesocosm experiment has also shown that an increase in temperature from 11°C to 16°C resulted in a decrease in the abundance of motile ,diatoms including N. perminuta, Nitzschia paleacea, and Rhoicosphenia abbreviata.137 In addition, temperature has been proven to have effects on the production and composition of EPS.63,131,138,139 Firstly, a change in temperature can disrupt the balance between energy supply and demand. Specifically, when temperatures drop while light conditions remain constant, energy supply may exceed demand.140,141 Therefore, the extra carbon fixed by photosynthesis can be released to extracellular environments in the form of EPS. Moreover, low temperatures can slow down bacterial activities,142 the degradation of EPS will decline, which is beneficial for maintaining the adhesion of benthic microalgae on substrates. EPS contributes to reinforcing cell adhesion on substrates by interconnecting the cells and promoting envelopment in the biofilm matrix23,47,48,49,143. Therefore, low temperature may intensify the adhesion of benthic microalgae on MPs. Actually, due to genetic diversity and different strategies in the secretion of EPS, it is difficult to describe a general mechanism of photosynthetic adjustment to temperature encompassing all phototrophic microalgae.141,144,145

Although salinity was also a significant environmental variable affecting the EMP-MA community, all dominant species showed no significant correlations with salinity. The reason may be that almost all the dominant species were euryhaline species and can live in marine, brackish, and even freshwater environments (Table S2); they have a strong ability to tolerate the changes in salinity. However, only a few non-dominant species showed significant negative correlations with salinity; they were primarily freshwater species (e.g., C. affinis, Spirulina sp., and S. dimorphus) or species (e.g., P. micans) typically inhabiting brackish waters such as estuaries.146,147 Similar to salinity, although ammonium and phosphorus were also significant environmental variables affecting the EMP-MA community, they were only positively correlated with several non-dominant benthic species, such as C. affinis and Mastogloia pumila. The species belonging to the genus Cymbella and Mastogloia have been associated with oligotrophic waters in many regions throughout the world, which may be related to their physiological requirements or is a consequence of poor competitive interactions with other taxa.148,149,150,151,152 However, all dominant species showed no significant correlations with nutrients in this study. Studies have shown that many species belonging to the genus Amphora, Navicula, Nitzschia, and Stauroneis typically thrive in nutrient-rich aquatic environments,153,154,155 which are consistent with the results of this study. The trophic level of these three bays was higher than the level of medium nutrition assessed by the PE3 eutrophication model,156 and most of the dominant species belonged to the genus of Navicula and Nitzschia. In addition, it is a well-established fact that the starvation of nutrients can result in an increase in EPS production secreted by some diatom species, including Thalassiosira pseudonana, Phaeodactylum tricornutum, Cylindrotheca closterium, Skeletonema costatum, and Cylindrotheca fusiformis.157,158,159,160,161,162 However, the trophic level of these three coastal bays did not lead to the starvation of nutrition. In contrast, other research has shown that high nutrients can promote benthic microalgae to produce more EPS.52,163,164,165 Although the effects of nutrients on the EPS production of benthic microalgae are species-specific,166 long-term starvation of nutrients is not conducive to their growth and can even lead to their death, because algal cells cannot produce essential metabolic components such as amino acids, nucleic acids, and proteins for cell replication.167,168

Implications for policy and management of microplastics

Internationally, the ongoing Intergovernmental Negotiating Committee on Plastic Pollution (INC-PP) has primarily focused on advancing plastic reduction, recycling, and waste management initiatives. In China, preceding the 2008 Beijing Olympics, the State Council General Office issued the Notice on Restricting the Production, Sale, and Use of Plastic Shopping Bags in 2007. This regulation mandated a nationwide prohibition—commonly known as the “Plastic Bag Restrictions”—on the production, sale, and use of plastic shopping bags thinner than 0.025 mm, taking effect on June 1, 2008.169 Owing to the significance of this policy measure, which was aimed at controlling the production and use of plastics, studies have shown that plastic-bag-reduction policies led to a 49% reduction in plastic-bag consumption.169,170 The 14th Five-Year Plan Action Plan for Plastic Pollution Control in 2020 has also achieved initial results in banning disposable plastic products and promoting degradable materials. However, these policies generally ignore the source control of MPs and the fragmentation process that generates secondary MPs, while receiving even less attention to their ecological consequences, such as facilitating the adhesion and proliferation of harmful algae. The fourth meeting of the ad hoc open-ended expert group on marine litter and microplastics (AHEG-4) in 2020 pointed out that MPs also have the effect of risk amplification, which can act as a floating vector for biological membranes and microbiological communities and provide a large surface for the adsorption or leaching of environmental pollutants. It urgently needs a quantitative risk assessment framework, supplemented by high-quality monitoring research and more environmental impact research, so as to comprehensively learn the risks of MPs.

This study shows that MPs mainly provide a vast habitat for benthic microalgae and may expand the spatial distributions of those benthic species not inhabiting planktonic niches; however, much of the current monitoring work is still focused on the abundance, size, and distribution of MPs. Therefore, this study recommends that future policies should break through the traditional notion that MPs are inert substances and pay attention to their effects on marine microorganisms. The monitoring of epimicroplastic microorganisms should be integrated into the conventional environmental monitoring system to track the impacts of MPs on marine ecosystems. Significantly, MPs also provide vectors for many harmful algae;20,23,25,76 therefore, it should also be considered to incorporate epimicroplastic harmful algae into routine monitoring efforts in the waters with a high frequency of HABs and restrict the use of plastic breeding facilities or promote more environmentally friendly alternative materials. Plastic pollution control needs to change from the traditional management mode to an ecological function-oriented mode. This means that microplastic-related ecological processes should be integrated into marine space planning and ecological protection management, and the professional advice of ecologists, toxicologists, and marine managers should be introduced into policy formulation.

Limitations of the study

Firstly, this study did not classify MPs samples according to their particle sizes, making it impossible to compare the composition and diversity of microalgae attached to MPs of different sizes, which may have resulted in the omission of some significant findings. In addition, this study only analyzed the effects of temperature, salinity, and nutrients on the community structure of microalgae, and did not include other environmental factors related to microalgae growth and adhesion, such as light intensity, waves, and water transparency, which would affect the interpretation of the results to some extent. Therefore, this study suggests that subsequent studies should devote more attention to these limitations.

Method details

Field sampling

All sampling processes were conducted in the spring. MPs samples from LYB, DSB, and QZB were collected in March 2021, April 2022, and May 2022 respectively. We collected 4, 8, and 7 MPs samples from LYB, DSB, and QZB respectively, with a total of 1186, 1022, and 433 MPs particles (500 μm-5 mm) respectively. It should be noted that our data on Luoyuan Bay have been published,23 and are cited for comparison with the other two bays in this study. Floating MPs were collected by a surface manta trawl with a mesh size of approximately 330 μm and a steel mouth of about 0.3 × 0.6 m of opening. Manta trawl was deployed to surface water (within 0.5 m) and kept 20 m away from the research vessel. The angle between towrope and shipping route was about 20°, which helped to reduce the ship’s interference to the distribution of MPs. The manta trawl was towed by research vessel to collect MPs at a speed of approximately 3 knots for 10 min; the manta trawl always floated on surface water and the lower part of the steel mouth was submerged by water. The volume of water filtered by the manta trawl was calculated using a flowmeter (HYDRO-BIOS D-FLOW) fixed at the steel mouth of the manta trawl. At the end of trawling, the manta trawl was dragged back to afterdeck and then rinsed with natural seawater from outside to drive all materials down to the bottom of the trawl. Debris with a diameter larger than 5 mm were filtered out using a stainless-steel sieve, and then the rest was transferred into a glass bottle (1 L). 5% formaldehyde solution (37.0%–40.0%, Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd) was used to the fixation of MPs samples. At the same time, 1 L of surface water was collected at each sampling site for the identification of phytoplankton and were fixed by 15 mL Lugol’s iodine solution (iodine ≥99.8%,Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.; potassium iodide ≥99.0%, Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd). The surface water (500 mL) for the analysis of nutrients was collected in a Niskin bottles and filtered immediately through precleaned cellulose acetate membrane filters with a pore size of 0.45 μm. Nutrients samples were first stored at low temperature (4 °C) and immediately analyzed upon returning to the laboratory.

Sample processing

MPs samples in glass bottles were poured through mesh sieves with mesh sizes of approximately 330 μm. And then these MPs with a diameter between 500 μm and 5 mm were selected with sterilized steel tweezers and rinsed gently with sterile artificial seawater (with a salinity of 30) for three times and then transferred into 10 mL glass vials containing 2 mL of sterile artificial seawater. EMP-MA were removed from MPs in ultrasound bath (300 W for 30 s) and washed four times with sterile artificial seawater, the washing solution was collected for the identification of microalgae.171 Phytoplankton samples were concentrated to 10 mL using sedimentation method and stored in the dark environments. In order to identify dominant species, a part of the samples was acidized with HCl (36%–38%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd) at 100 °C for 20 min to eliminate organic matter, and then rinsed with Milli-Q water eight to ten times.

Temperature, salinity, and pH were measured in situ by EXO2 Multiparameter Sonde (YSI Incorporated, Yellow Springs, USA). Dissolved inorganic nutrients of NO3-N, NO2-N, NH4-N and PO4-P were determined by colorimetric methods according to the Specification of Oceanographic Survey-Part 4: Survey of Chemical Parameters in Sea Water.

Species identification and diversity calculation

The ecological habits of microalgae were classified according to the book named after Marine Benthic Diatoms in China.172,173 Cells count and species identification were performed in a cell enumeration chamber (0.1 mL, 20 mm × 20 mm) under an inverted microscope (CKX41, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). A portion of the samples were acidified and then used for further species identification. The methods of acidification was detailed in the supporting materials.23 Acidized samples were daubed on clean glass sheets or cellulose acetate membrane filter, and then coated with 10–20 nm gold using the Leica EM ACE200 coating system (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) after samples have dried. Prepared samples were observed with scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JEOL JSM 6390LV, Japan; TESCAN MIRA, The Czech Republic) at 10–20 KV.

The dominant species are those with the dominance (Y)174 greater than 0.02. Shannon-Wiener Index (H′),175 Simpson Index (D)176 and Pielou Evenness Index (J)177 were used to assess diversity.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The software of Canoco 5.0 (Jan Leps and Petr Smilauer, Bohemia, Czech) was used for the normalization of data, Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), detrended correspondence analysis (DCA), and canonical correspondence analysis (CCA). First, these algal species with a number of occurrences ≥3 at all sampling sites and a proportion ≥10% of the total cell density of single sample were selected for the next analyses. Second, the data of microalgae and environmental variables were normalized using the formula lg (1 + x) so that the data is closer to normal distribution. Two-way ANOVA was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA) to analyze the significant differences between EMP-MA and PM communities. Origin 2024 (OriginLab, Massachusetts, USA) was used for linear correlation analysis between the cell density of microalgae and environmental variables. For environmental parameters, replicated samples were collected for quality control at stations that accounted for approximately 10% of the total stations. For the microalgal samples we did not conduct repeat sampling, but did conduct surveys in three different bays and got consistent conclusions, which indicated that the results are robust and well support the claims of this study.
