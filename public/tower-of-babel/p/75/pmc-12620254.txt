The Functional Connectome Mediates Associations Between Fitness and Cognition Across the Adult Lifespan
Authors: Junaid S Merchant, Jeremy J Purcell, Daniel D Callow, Junyeon Won, Yash Kommula, Samuel Rosenberg, Yi Wei, John L Woodard, Kristy A Nielson, Stephen M Rao, Sally Durgerian, Shuo Chen, J Carson Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is increasingly recognized as essential for improving neural and cognitive function, especially in older age. Here, we examined a cross‐sectional sample of the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Aging dataset (ages 36–100; N = 378) to test the hypothesis that functional connectivity profiles that are related to CRF are also associated with episodic memory and executive function across middle age and older adults. To test these hypotheses, we first used connectome‐based predictive modeling (CPM) to identify functional connections (FCs) that were separately predictive of CRF (2 Minute Walk Test), episodic memory (Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test), and executive function (Flanker task). Briefly, this involved using nine‐fold cross‐validation to identify the top 2% of FCs most correlated with the outcome measure (e.g., CRF), adjusting for age, education, and sex. We then examined the relationship between CRF (as the predictor), cognitive function (as outcome), and FCs (as mediator or moderator). First, there was a high degree of overlap between the CRF and the cognition measure functional connectivity profiles in both the ventral attentional and limbic networks. Second, although we did not observe moderation effects, we did observe that these functional connectivity profiles fully mediated the relationship between CRF and episodic memory and partially mediated the relationship between CRF and executive function. Together, these findings suggest that the connectivities within the ventral attention network (VAN), particularly with the right mid‐insula, right inferior frontal gyrus, and the limbic network, are neural mechanisms that underlie the associations between CRF and cognition across the lifespan from middle to older adulthood. These findings provide insight into the potential targets (CRF) and biomarkers (functional connectivity profiles) of brain health for future interventions to improve or maintain neurocognitive health in aging.Keywords: cognitive aging, episodic memory, executive function, exercise, fMRI, functional connectivity, physical activity

Introduction

Age‐related cognitive impairment is an imminent public health crisis (Rajan et al. 2021). Exercise and regular physical activity improve cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and are recommended lifestyle modifications for reducing the risk of age‐related cognitive decline and maintaining healthy brain function in older adulthood (Barnes and Yaffe 2011; Boa Sorte Silva et al. 2024; Sabbagh et al. 2022). CRF reflects the capacity of skeletal muscles to utilize oxygen supplied by the cardiovascular system during sustained physical activity (Caspersen et al. 1985). Different lines of research have linked CRF to age‐related changes in cognitive ability and variations in brain structure and function (Colcombe et al. 2004; España‐Irla et al. 2021; Voss, Erickson, et al. 2010; Voss, Prakash, et al. 2010; Won, Callow, Pena, Gogniat, et al. 2021; Won, Callow, Pena, Jordan, et al. 2021; Won, Faroqi‐Shah, Callow, Williams, et al. 2021). This line of research has led to proposals that CRF is associated with metabolic and epigenetic mechanisms that facilitate brain connectivity changes underlying the protective effects of physical activity on healthy cognitive aging (Anderson et al. 2010; Boa Sorte Silva et al. 2024; Lloyd et al. 2017; Won, Callow, Pena, Gogniat, et al. 2021; Won, Callow, Pena, Jordan, et al. 2021; Won, Faroqi‐Shah, Callow, Williams, et al. 2021). However, resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) studies have yielded inconsistent findings concerning the specific brain networks that are associated with CRF and cognition (e.g., hippocampal system, default mode network (DMN), etc.) and the direction of this relationship (e.g., positive versus negative relationship; between versus within network FC; Boa Sorte Silva et al. 2024; Won, Callow, Pena, Gogniat, et al. 2021; Won, Callow, Pena, Jordan, et al. 2021; Won, Faroqi‐Shah, Callow, Williams, et al. 2021). Thus, it remains unclear which functional brain systems mediate the relationship between CRF and cognition that spans middle age and older adulthood, and the nature of the relationship with functional brain measures.

Whereas there is evidence in animal models for an impact of increased physical activity (PA) on neural network function through metabolic pathways (Anderson et al. 2010; Lloyd et al. 2017), in humans these pathways are typically studied through CRF as an integrative marker of PA‐related adaptation, and evidence in older adults remains limited. The literature reports that CRF is associated with the strength of connectivity of various functional networks in adults. CRF has also been associated with a more extensive DMN and hippocampal FC in older adults (Voss, Erickson, et al. 2010; Voss et al. 2016), while in younger adults, hippocampal functional connectivity was shown to mediate the associations between better CRF and better cognitive function (Won et al. 2024). Furthermore, the local efficiency of brain networks has been shown to mediate the association between CRF and executive function performance in older adults as measured with structural whole‐brain diffusion‐weighted imaging (Callow and Smith 2023). This previous work supports the claim that functional connectivity in specific networks in older adults mediates the relationship between CRF and cognitive function. In this study, we build upon this previous work by using a data‐driven approach called CPM to empirically identify the edges and network‐level patterns most predictive of individual differences in CRF and cognitive performance. In doing so, we aim to identify functional connectivity profiles in middle‐aged and older adults that mediate the relationship between CRF and cognitive function. Although we anticipate replicating the findings from this previous work, we expect that our data‐driven approach may reveal novel functional connections (FCs) previously unreported.

CRF is responsive to increased physical activity and is partly heritable (Stefan et al. 2007; Thyfault and Morris 2017). Previous studies have indicated that the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Toolbox 2‐Minute Walk Test (2MWT) (Bohannon et al. 2015; Callow and Smith 2023) reasonably estimates an individual's CRF. Given that this measure is provided in the Human Connectome Project‐Aging (HCP‐A) along with resting‐state fMRI data and various cognitive measures, we utilized this extensive data set to explore the cross‐sectional relationships between CRF, brain functional connectivity, and cognition.

While multiple domains of cognitive function decrease with age, we focused on two specific cognitive domains, verbal learning and memory (assessed with the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Task (RAVLT) (Rey 1964; Woodard 2006) and the Flanker Task (Zelazo et al. 2014)), due to their vulnerability to decline with age and importance in daily functions of living (Cansino 2009; Fjell et al. 2017). Episodic memory pertains to remembering and retrieving an experience in space and time and is often assessed using a verbal learning paradigm. Performance in episodic memory tasks begins to show normal age‐related decline between 40 and 60 years old (Cansino 2009), and abnormal performance is a key indicator of progression to Alzheimer's disease (Albert et al. 2011). Executive function, which involves higher‐level cognitive functions that control and coordinate thoughts and behaviors, including task switching and inhibitory control, begins to decline in adulthood between the ages of 30 and 40 years and continues to decline with increased age (Ferguson et al. 2021). It has been well‐documented that exercise training, physical activity, and CRF are associated with better performance on episodic memory and executive function tasks in aging populations (Aghjayan et al. 2022; Chang et al. 2024; Colcombe and Kramer 2003; Dustman et al. 1990; Hayes et al. 2015; Oberlin et al. 2025). Our previous work utilizing HCP‐A data reported strong associations between CRF and cognitive performance (Callow and Smith 2023; Won et al. 2024). Despite this extant literature, there is little evidence for large‐scale functional neural network connections that modulate episodic memory and executive control performance and may underlie associations between CRF and cognitive function across the older adult lifespan.

In the current study, we endeavored to build upon previous work by addressing key limitations and gaps in the field. Although there has been previous work indicating that CRF in older adults is related to within‐ and between‐network functional network connectivity, such as the DMN (Voss, Erickson, et al. 2010; Voss et al. 2016; Won et al. 2023), the analysis of predefined networks has constrained past work and has not considered the whole‐brain functional connectome using a data‐driven approach (Bullmore and Sporns 2012). It is currently unknown whether whole‐brain functional connectome profiles mediate the relationship between CRF and cognition in older adults (Stillman et al. 2016). As noted, the HCP‐A has not only CRF and cognitive indices but also high‐quality resting‐state fMRI data. The HCP‐A dataset permits the application of innovative whole‐brain data‐driven approaches to investigate the FCs predictive of both CRF and cognitive function, thereby addressing a key limitation of previous research.

CPM is a data‐driven approach to creating a brain‐behavior model to predict individual variation more effectively (Finn et al. 2015). Unlike traditional methods that often rely on correlations between brain measures defined a priori and behavioral traits, CPM provides a more rigorous, cross‐validated framework to extract the most accurate predictive features from subjects' resting‐state functional connectivity matrices (rsFC) (Shen et al. 2017). Cross‐validation protects against “overfitting”—a common pitfall in modeling neuroimaging data—thereby allowing for better generalizability of results across different datasets and populations (Yarkoni and Westfall 2017).

Beyond attempting to identify functional connectivity profiles that are predictive of CRF, episodic memory, and executive function, we examined whether any overlapping FCs mediated the associations between CRF and cognition, thereby addressing gaps in the literature by exploring neurofunctional mechanisms of how CRF may impact cognitive function across both middle‐aged and older adults. We hypothesized that the prominent overlapping FCs that predict both CRF and episodic memory would, in turn, mediate the relationship between CRF and episodic memory. We also posed an analogous hypothesis regarding the FCs that predict CRF and executive function.

Methods

Participants and Measures

Our analytic sample was obtained from the HCP‐A of the “healthy aging” population, which consists of individuals over 36 years old who are not experiencing pathological cognitive decline. We used the HCP‐A rfMRI data from the 2.0 release, which included 725 adults who underwent imaging assessment sessions starting in 2019. Key exclusion criteria for HCP‐A included (1) Significant history of neurological or psychiatric disorders (including substance abuse); (2) Cognitive impairment (assessed by Montreal Cognitive Assessment using age‐specific thresholds); (3) Capacity to consent; and (4) Moderate to severe claustrophobia (Bookheimer et al. 2019). For the current study, we further excluded subjects who were missing any of the following: resting‐state fMRI scans, structural MRI scans, the 2MWT (CRF), the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT; episodic memory), or the Flanker task (response inhibition/attention). Applying these criteria excluded 121 participants; the reasons for missing data were unspecified. Finally, we excluded participants based on the rsFC quality control criteria described in the next section below, which excluded 226 participants. Our final sample consisted of 378 individuals aged 36–100 years (Table 1). Additional summary statistics and histograms for demographic and behavioral measures can be found in Figure S1.

We used the 2MWT as a proxy estimate of CRF. The 2MWT consists of a 50‐ft out‐and‐back course as part of the Motor Domain of the NIH Toolbox. The raw 2MWT score was considered to be the total distance (feet and inches) traveled, and then it was normalized to a scaled score with mean = 100 and standard deviation = 15. Research supports that the 2MWT has good test–retest reliability, strong external validity with the 6‐minute walk test, and moderate‐to‐strong correlation with gold standard measures of CRF (Callow and Smith 2023; Reuben et al. 2013). As shown in Table 1, participants' normalized 2MWT scores (M = 99.38, SD = 13.28) closely reflected the NIH Toolbox population norms (M = 100, SD = 15), which indicates average overall fitness levels in our sample. We also report the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) Total as Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET)‐minutes/week as an estimate of activity. “High” activity can be met by vigorous activity on ≥ 3 days totaling ≥ 1500 MET‐min/week or any combination ≥ 3000 MET‐min/week; “Moderate” and “Low” use lower thresholds (IPAQResearchCommittee 2005). Given positive skew, we also report median and IQR in Figure S1. Our sample mean of (2910 MET‐min/week) suggests moderate‐to‐high activity on average.

We used the RAVLT and the Flanker test for the cognitive measures. The RAVLT measures episodic memory by quantifying recall of word lists. Participants were presented with a list of 15 unrelated words across five trials, followed by a list of distractors, and then asked to recall the original list immediately. Memory was estimated using the variable RAVLT Short Delay Total Correct (RAVLT_tot) provided by HCP‐A. Given the numerous trials that inform it, the total recall score provides a reliable measure of verbal episodic learning and memory. The NIH Toolbox Flanker task measures inhibitory control and attention by requiring participants to indicate the direction of a central target arrow while ignoring flanking arrows that are either congruent or incongruent. Performance is evaluated based on accuracy and reaction time differences between congruent and incongruent trials.

fMRI Acquisition, Preprocessing, and Parcellation

In HCP‐A, the MRI scans were performed on a Siemens Prisma 3‐Tesla with a Siemens 32‐channel Prisma head coil. rfMRI data were obtained during a 6 min 41 s‐long session (488 timepoints, repetition time = 800 ms, echo time = 37 ms, multi‐band acceleration 8) with a 2.0 mm isotropic resolution and 72 slices (protocols available at https://www.humanconnectome.org/study/hcp‐lifespan‐aging/project‐protocol/imaging‐protocols‐hcp‐aging). Raw rfMRI data from HCP‐A underwent volumetric preprocessing (including gradient distortion correction, motion correction (using FLIRT), slice timing correction, spatial normalization, and spatial smoothing) and surface‐based preprocessing (including surface reconstruction, registration to the structural T1w image, and smoothing (Glasser et al. 2013; Smith et al. 2013)). Additionally, multi‐run FIX (MR‐FIX) was applied to separate signal and noise components more effectively. This approach precluded the need for calculating motion parameters, like frame‐wise displacement, to include as regressors (Glasser et al. 2013). We did not apply additional temporal filtering to the time series beyond MR‐FIX denoising as recommended by the HCP preprocessing protocol given the short TR and ICA‐based removal of low‐frequency/motion components; furthermore high‐pass filtering can alter temporal structure, reduce effective degrees of freedom, and remove meaningful amounts of information from the time series (Glasser et al. 2013). We then aligned the cleaned, FIX‐denoised rfMRI data across subjects using MSMAll multimodal surface registration, registered the data to a 2 mm MNI152‐template using FSL (Jenkinson et al. 2012), and extracted time series from 246 ROIs from The Human Brainnetome Atlas (Fan et al. 2016) using AFNI (Cox 1996; Cox and Hyde 1997). The Brainnetome Atlas was used because it uniquely offers a high‐resolution parcellation of both cortical and subcortical regions, including the hippocampus, and is informed by functional and structural connectivity networks (Fan et al. 2016). Thus, it provides a fine‐grained, anatomically comprehensive framework well‐suited for whole‐brain, edge‐level analyses. All HCP‐A participant data were subject to quality control measures at various stages, starting with real‐time oversight during data acquisition and followed by manual and automated image reviews after acquisition. After downloading the cleaned data, quality control included removing participants with excessive missing data and/or unexpected signal drops that resulted in extremely high temporal correlations and minimal resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) variations. Thus, the following participants were excluded: (1) those whose rsFC data had a missing rate exceeding 5% (of missing FCs that could not be calculated after denoising); (2) those who had 10% or more rsFC edges (i.e., connections) with a value over 0.9; and (3) those whose rsFC edges had a standard deviation of 0.1 or less (see (Pan et al. 2024) for additional details).

The Human Brainnetome Atlas (Fan et al. 2016) was used to further reduce the preprocessed rsFC of the included participants into 246 cortical and subcortical parcels (i.e., nodes or regions of interest; ROI). The Brainnetome Atlas was developed through in vivo multimodal imaging of a subset of the HCP dataset to provide a data‐driven parcellation of brain regions rather than traditional neuroanatomical regions defined through histology (Fan et al. 2016). Blood oxygenation‐level dependent (BOLD) measures within a parcel were averaged by time point (i.e., TR) to create a parcel‐averaged time series. The Pearson correlation coefficient between each pair of parcel‐level time series was used to construct our rsFC matrices for each participant. Thus, each FC ranged from −1 to 1, representing the strength and direction of the functional relationship between the two brain regions. Larger positive values indicate strong temporal correlations between areas, while FC values closer to and below zero indicate temporal decoupling between regions. We further categorized each parcel into the Yeo‐7 and Yeo‐17 atlases to provide a broader look at the fundamental functional networks involved in CRF and cognition (Yeo et al. 2011). Table S1 provides additional details about the Brainnetome Atlas parcels used in the current analyses, which include each node's label, the broader neuroanatomical division it belongs to (e.g., gyri, sulci), Brodmann area, MNI coordinates, and placement in the Yeo‐7 and Yeo‐17 atlases. We chose the Yeo 7 network atlas to report the data for the main manuscript primarily because it facilitates easier comparison with prior studies and aligns well with much of the existing literature, especially in developmental and clinical contexts. While the Yeo 17 atlas offers greater regional specificity, our data are already presented at a high node‐level granularity, which allows others to map onto more fine‐grained parcellations if needed. Using Yeo 7 as a framework helps organize and interpret broader network‐level patterns while keeping the findings accessible and interpretable across studies, and provides a more readable and digestible visual representation of the results.

Connectome‐Based Predictive Modeling (CPM)

We used CPM as the starting point for identifying FCs related to the three aforementioned measures of CRF and cognition. CPM is a robust approach for establishing relationships between individual differences in FC and behavior that leverages methodological advancements popularized by machine learning (Finn et al. 2015; Shen et al. 2017). This approach goes beyond previous approaches that utilized measures of association sensitive to the biases of the specific dataset examined, further reducing generalizability to out‐of‐sample data (also known as overfitting; Kriegeskorte et al. 2009). CPM utilizes cross‐validation—splitting datasets into independent partitions for model training and testing—to protect against FC selection bias and overfitting, thereby increasing the likelihood of replication in new datasets and strengthening the translational utility of the findings (Shen et al. 2017). Thus, CPM aligns with the broader paradigm shift in the psychological sciences from an explanatory, theory‐testing analytical framework toward data‐driven predictive modeling techniques (Yarkoni and Westfall 2017).

The current study implemented CPM using nine‐fold cross‐validation repeated 100 times, which follows the recommended practices of leaving out 10%–20% data in each fold (Varoquaux et al. 2017). Our CPM procedure for each measure involved splitting the participants into nine independent partitions so that on each cross‐validation “fold,” data from eight of the nine partitions (8/9 = 336 participants) were used to “train” the model, and data from the remaining partition (1/9 = 42 participants) was used to “test” the trained model (Figure 1A). Within each cross‐validation fold, model training first involved a FC selection step wherein we calculated the Spearman correlation between the outcome measure and each FC across participants. We then selected the top 2% of FCs (i.e., ~600) most positively related to the outcome and the bottom 2% of negatively related FCs (Figure 1B). For each participant, the FC values (i.e., Pearson correlation coefficients) of the top and bottom 2% FCs were then algebraically summed to create two different independent variables for linear regression models predicting the outcome, which also included participant age, education, and sex as covariates of no interest (Figure 1C, left and middle). The algebraic sum of the FCs provides a measure that better represents the overall strength of the connectivity profile for each measure, in that it incorporates both magnitude and number of edges. Regression coefficients for these independent variables were identified using the training set, and these parameters were then applied to estimate the outcome scores for individuals in the test set (Figure 1C, right). We repeated this process across the nine folds to obtain predicted values for each participant (Figure 1C, right). To further ensure the reliability of our results was robust against the biases of the random splits, we repeated the nine‐fold cross‐validation CPM procedure 100 times. CPM model performance was evaluated by calculating the Pearson correlation between the observed and predicted values (averaged across the 100 iterations) for each outcome as our measure of predictive performance, which was tested for significance using 10 K permutations (Figure 1D, left). The positively and negatively related FCs selected across all folds of the 100 iterations of the CPM were used as the representative FCs predictive of a given outcome. Moreover, the overlap of representative FCs between CRF and each of the two cognition measures was used in follow‐up mediation analyses (Figure 1D, middle).

Overview of connectome‐based predictive modeling (CPM) methods, functional connectivity (FC) overlap, and mediation models. (A) We split the full sample (n = 378) into nine partitions (n = 42) and used nine‐fold cross‐validation. This approach involves selecting FCs and calculating a linear model using 8/9 s (n = 336) of the sample as “train” data, applying the model on the left‐out “test” data, and repeating this process over the nine folds. (B) FC selection for each CPM involves calculating the Spearman correlation between each node‐to‐node FC value (n = 30,135) and the outcome measure and selecting the top 600 positively related and bottom 600 negatively related FCs (approximately 2% each). (C) Top and bottom FCs were summed separately and used as independent variables in linear models predicting the outcome, along with participant age, education level, and gender as covariates of no interest. The estimated parameters from this model were used to predict the outcome values of the test sample, and this process (steps B, C) was repeated across all nine folds, resulting in predicted values for each of the 378 participants. Steps A–C were repeated 100 times to ensure the reliability of the CPM results of each random nine‐fold split. (D) CPM model performance was evaluated using Pearson's correlation between the observed and averaged predicted values for each participant across the 100 iterations and tested for significance using the 10 K permutation test. The top and bottom FCs consistently selected across all 100 iterations of the CPM were considered the representative FCs for each outcome. The overlapping FCs between CRF and each cognition outcome were used in follow‐up mediation analyses.

It is important to note that prior studies using the CPM approaches have used r‐value or p‐value thresholds for selecting meaningful FCs in the training step of each cross‐validation fold (Anderson and Barbey 2023; Finn et al. 2015; Shen et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2021). This approach can lead to varying numbers of selected FCs depending on the outcome measure being predicted. It can also exclude weak or negatively related FCs with meaningful individual‐level variation. Because the motivations for the current study included examining the overlap between FCs predictive of CRF and cognition and disambiguating findings from prior work showing positive and negative relationships between CRF and FC measures, we decided to use the top and bottom 2% FCs. This approach ensures that a common number of FCs is selected in earlier steps of each CPM to facilitate finding the overlap between outcomes and capturing weak or negatively related FCs that are meaningful but may not surpass effect‐size‐based thresholds. Figure S2 includes histograms of the distribution of outcomes by FC Spearman correlation values for each outcome and values of the percent‐based thresholds from 1% to 10%. Importantly, these histograms show that the 2% threshold balances the capturing of meaningful, positively related FCs in the range of 0.158–0.196 and enough negatively related FCs to investigate how functional decoupling may contribute to individual variation in the relationship between CRF and cognition.

FC Patterns, Overlap, and Mediation

We characterized the functional connectivity associated with each of the three measures by focusing on both the positive and negative FCs selected in all folds of the CPM and visualizing the sample‐averaged FC values (i.e., raw node‐to‐node) organized by the Yeo‐7 functional brain atlas (Yeo et al. 2011). We additionally visualized the gyri/area‐averaged FC values and counts to understand the functional coupling of anatomical divisions, as well as Yeo‐7 and Yeo‐17 atlas' parcel‐averaged FC counts to understand the functional coupling within and between large‐scale functional networks (Table S1). We used a similar approach to visualize the overlapping FCs between CRF (i.e., 2MWT) and cognitive measures (i.e., RAVLT, Flanker). That is, common positively and negatively related FCs were selected across all nine folds of the 2MWT, and each of the cognitive measures (i.e., RAVLT, Flanker) was visualized. The summed FC values of these sets of overlapping positively‐ and negatively‐related FCs were used as separate mediators in models predicting cognition from CRF, including age, gender, and education as covariates of no interest. For example, the common positively related FCs selected in the 2‐minute walk and Flanker tests were summed and included as one mediator. Common negatively related FCs were chosen in both the 2MWT and the Flanker test and were also summed and included as a second mediator in models with the 2MWT as an independent variable and the Flanker test as a dependent variable. Standard error estimates for mediation models were calculated using 10 K bootstraps. We defined partial mediation as when the direct, indirect, and total effects are significant. Full mediation was identified when the total and indirect effects are significant, but the direct effect is not (indicating that the indirect effect accounts for the relationship).

We additionally calculated supplementary stepwise linear regression models to assess whether significant mediation effects could be attributable to FC by CRF interactions (i.e., FC moderating the relationship between CRF and cognition). In the first step, main effects for cognition (i.e., Flanker or RAVLT), summed positively related FCs, and summed negatively related FCs were included as the primary independent variables of interest, and age, gender, and education were included as covariates of no interest. Separate interaction terms for positive CRF FCs and negative CRF FCs were included in the second step. We characterized significant interaction effects as when either the inclusion of the interaction terms resulted in a significant R 2 change from the first to second step and/or when there was a statistically significant coefficient for the interaction term. Code for implementing these analyses is shared in an open science framework: https://osf.io/yrp98/.

Results

Connectome‐Based Predictive Modeling

We found that CPM performed well for all three measures, with prediction accuracy (quantified as Pearson correlation values between the CPM‐predicted and actual outcome) ranging from 0.45 to 0.58 (ps < 0.0005; Figure 2), but there was some variation across measures in the top 2% of the positively and negatively related FCs selected. Specifically, CPM for the 2MWT had the best model performance (r = 0.58, p = 0.002) and selected the most FCs, with 80 positively related FCs across 51 nodes and 110 negatively related FCs across 103 nodes (Figure 2A). CPM performance for Flanker and RAVLT was comparable (r's = 0.45–0.46, p = 0.002) but had different numbers of positively and negatively related FCs. The Flanker CPM selected 50 positively related FCs between 50 nodes and 67 negatively related FCs between 51 nodes (Figure 2B). The RAVLT CPM selected 35 positively related FCs across 36 nodes and 40 negatively related FCs across 40 (Figure 2C).

The right panels of Figure 2 visualize the group‐averaged ROI‐to‐ROI FC values organized by Yeo‐7 functional networks for all three outcomes, with the positively related FCs in red and the negatively related FCs in blue. A more detailed version of these heatmaps with node labels can be found in Figure S3, and heatmaps of the number of FCs (i.e., counts) within and between neuroanatomical areas, Yeo‐7 networks, and Yeo‐17 networks can be found in Figure S4. We see a similar general pattern across all three outcomes, such that a large concentration of positively related FCs was within the ventral attention network (VAN) (Yeo‐7 Network 4), while many negatively related FCs were between network connections with limbic network nodes (Yeo‐7 Network 5; see Figure S4 for within and between network FC counts). This result is consistent with recent brain‐wide functional connectome analysis identifying a connectivity profile in older adults associated with age‐related declines in FC within sensorimotor and dorsal/VANs, including the insular cortex, as well as the basal ganglia (Pan et al. 2024).

In contrast to age‐related declines in FC (Pan et al. 2024), better performance on the 2MWT was characterized by positively related FCs within and between sensorimotor and VANs and, to a lesser degree, DMN, frontoparietal, and dorsal attention networks. FCs within the limbic network and between the limbic network and all other Yeo‐7 networks were negatively associated with CRF. Similarly, the positively related FC profiles for each cognition measure were dominated by within and between VAN connections. However, they differed because they had fewer positively related FCs within and between the sensorimotor network, and the negatively related FCs were less concentrated in limbic system nodes. The Flanker task was positively associated with numerous DMN FCs and negatively related to numerous FCs between the DMN and other networks. Positively related FCs to the RAVLT were more distributed. They had more FCs within and between subcortical nodes that could not be categorized into the Yeo‐7 atlas. In contrast, the negatively related FCs were more concentrated in nodes within and between the DMN.

Figure S5 visualizes the positively and negatively related FCs as unweighted edges in a circle plot color‐coded by the Yeo‐7 network and highlights the common nodes and FCs across the outcomes. This view reveals that the right dorsal mid‐insula (node 174) of the VAN is the most connected ROI among the positively related FCs for all three outcomes and that the orbital gyrus node of the limbic system (node 48) is among the most connected negatively related FCs. Moreover, the figure visualizes the different FCs selected across all three CPMs, supporting the hypothesis that a fundamental set of FCs may mediate the relationship between CRF and cognition measures.

Mediation Models

Common, positively related FCs that were selected both in the 2‐min walk and Flanker test CPMs were summed and used as a mediator in models predicting the Flanker from the 2MWT, while common, overlapping negatively related FCs were summed to create a second mediator of the same model (Figure 3A). Similarly, the overlap between FCs selected in the 2MWT and RAVLT CPMs was summed and used in mediation models predicting RAVLT from the 2MWT (Figure 3B). Participant age, sex, and education were included in the mediation models as covariates of no interest. Mediation models were calculated using the entire sample of 378 participants using a 10 K bootstrap approach to estimate standard errors for significance testing. Mediation models examining the relationship between CRF (as predictor) and cognition (as outcome) demonstrated that these overlapping FCs partially mediated the relationship with Flanker (i.e., direct, indirect, and total effects were significant), with indirect paths accounting for 22.3% of the total effect. Overlapping FCs between CRF and RAVLT fully mediated the relationship with RAVLT total, such that the total effect was significant. However, the direct effect was not, with indirect paths accounting for 24.9% of the total effect (see Table S3 for additional details). We further used these summed FCs to assess if the mediation effects could be accounted for by the moderating effects (i.e., FC by CRF interactions). Table S4 indicates that the mediation effects between CRF and RAVLT could partially be accounted for by moderation, such that including the interaction terms led to a marginally significant R 2 change (= 0.013, p = 0.04), but neither interaction term had statistically significant coefficients.

Discussion

In this study, we identified widespread overlap between FCs that predict CRF, measured by the 2MWT, and those that predict episodic memory and executive function. Higher CRF was positively associated with FCs within and between the sensorimotor, ventral attention, DMN, frontoparietal, and dorsal attention networks. At the same time, negative associations were observed primarily within and between the limbic network and all other networks. Notably, positively predictive FCs were more distributed, while negatively predictive FCs were concentrated within the limbic network. Key connectivity edges included the right dorsal mid‐insula within the VAN (positively predictive) and the orbital gyrus nodes of the limbic system (negatively predictive). A similar connectivity profile emerged for both cognitive outcomes, with intersecting predictive FCs fully mediating the relationship between CRF and episodic memory and partially mediating the relationship between CRF and executive function, independent of moderation effects. These findings suggest that connectivity profiles involving the insula, VAN, and limbic networks contribute to the neurofunctional mechanisms linking greater CRF to better cognitive function in middle‐aged and older adults.

Interestingly, the strongest negatively related FCs across outcomes exhibited group‐averaged FC values near or below zero. While these were nominally classified as negatively predictive, the findings suggest that higher CRF may inhibit or decouple connectivity within these nodes rather than simply reflecting an inverse relationship. This decoupling may enable more efficient network specialization, as evidenced by its significant contribution to mediation models, highlighting individual differences in the degree of network decoupling as a potential mechanism underlying these associations.

Insular Cortex—A Key Hub Responsive to CRF

The dorsal insular cortex was found to serve as a predictive hub of CRF, episodic memory, and executive function. This finding aligns with previous evidence highlighting the insula's responsiveness to exercise (Williamson et al. 1999) and CRF (Hayes et al. 2013; Yogev‐Seligmann et al. 2021). Two prominent functions relevant to physical activity and associated with the insula are interoceptive and emotional awareness, modulated by exercise (Wallman‐Jones et al. 2021). For instance, a single session of moderate‐intensity exercise reduces insular cortex functional connectivity with the hippocampus, mediating improvements in mood after exercise (Alfini et al. 2020). In a longer‐term, 12‐week moderate‐intensity walking intervention, there was a report of increased connectivity between the bilateral anterior insular cortex and the precuneus (a hub of the DMN), which in turn was related to improved subjective well‐being after 12 weeks of exercise training (Won et al. 2022). Further work is required to determine if there is an association with emotional cognition in these data.

Another possible functional role is associated with cognitive health in older age. For example, increased CRF after a 12‐week exercise intervention was associated with increased cortical thickness in the insular cortex in healthy older adults and those diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (Reiter et al. 2015). In another paper that examined the structural integrity of gray matter using diffusion‐weighted imaging in older adults, Callow et al. reported that increased mean diffusivity in the left insula after exercise training in MCI was associated with improved verbal fluency performance (Callow et al. 2021). These findings are also consistent with reduced insular cortex cerebral blood flow (CBF) after exercise training in individuals diagnosed with MCI, which was also associated with improved verbal fluency (Alfini et al. 2019). Both increased mean diffusivity and decreased CBF have been hypothesized to reflect reduced inflammation in gray matter (Callow et al. 2021; Wierenga et al. 2014). Thus, our findings provide insight into the functional underpinnings of these structural, CBF, and diffusivity differences in healthy and cognitively impaired older adults.

Finally, one characteristic of the predictive dorsal insular cortex connections reported here was that it was predominantly associated with positive FCs, thus suggesting that CRF and cognitive function are associated with a more functionally synchronous insular hub. This result aligns with previous work indicating that local functional connectivity was particularly synchronous (i.e., homogeneous) in the anterior insular cortex after a single session of acute exercise (Purcell et al. 2023). These findings collectively suggest that single and repeated bouts of exercise that lead to increased CRF may stimulate adaptation within the insular cortex, resulting in enhanced functional connectivity between the insula and other brain regions. These more extensive network connections may contribute to better cognitive performance.

Ventral Attention Network

More generally, the VAN—the insular cortex is a key hub—was positively associated with CRF and cognitive function. The VAN has notably been characterized by its role in reorienting attention to salient stimuli (Ghosh et al. 2021). Thus, improved function of such a key hub of the VAN may lead to broad improvements in multiple cognitive tasks for which attentional allocation to external stimuli would be necessary. Notably, performance on both the RAVLT (Baños et al. 2005; Vakil et al. 2012) and the Flanker task (McDermott et al. 2017) has been associated with healthy attentional processing. Thus, one aspect of the predictive connections observed in the VAN in both CRF and RAVLT and the Flanker task may be related to the attentional aspects of these tasks, which contribute to performance levels. The association between higher CRF and better attentional performance is supported by ERP work showing that acute exercise modulates attentional networks, including the VAN (Chang et al. 2015). An acute exercise study with fMRI also showed improved executive function performance and concurrent brain activation on the incongruent trials during the Flanker task in VAN regions such as the inferior frontal and inferior parietal cortices (Won et al. 2019). Furthermore, longer‐term 10‐week aerobic exercise interventions improved VAN function in children with developmental coordination disorders (Tsai et al. 2012). The current findings are consistent with these previous studies and provide evidence that increased connectivity with nodes within the VAN is strongly associated with greater CRF and may contribute to better attention and higher performance on verbal episodic memory and executive function tasks.

Limbic Network

In contrast to the connections with the insular cortex and VAN, we observed that limbic network connectivity was negatively associated with CRF and cognitive performance. One interpretation of these findings is that greater CRF may promote increased differentiation or decoupling between limbic and cognitive neural networks related to better cognitive performance. The negative associations with limbic regions suggest that decoupling within this network and other brain regions may play a critical role in CRF's emotional and cognitive benefits. This specific profile aligns with a previously mentioned study reporting that reduced connectivity between the hippocampus and insular cortex was related to mood improvement immediately after exercise in older adults (Alfini et al. 2020). This finding is consistent with the current results, which revealed that connectivity between a parahippocampal region (node 109) and a ventral insula region (node 166) was negatively related to CRF and Flanker performance (Figure 3A). Furthermore, more recent work reported that acute exercise resulted in greater segregation between the salience network and the DMN, as well as between the salience network and the affect‐reward network (Kommula et al. 2024). These findings also align with task fMRI results, which reported that acute exercise, compared to seated rest, resulted in less activation in the precuneus region during the viewing of pleasant emotional pictures and greater activation in the dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex during the viewing of unpleasant pictures (Kommula et al. 2023). These effects are consistent with mood improvement after exercise and underscore the limbic network's sensitivity to exercise‐induced changes.

CRF and Cognitive Performance

As expected, the positive associations between higher CRF (measured using the 2MWT) and better performance in episodic memory and executive function (assessed via the RAVLT and Flanker task, respectively) align with previous research in older adults (Chang et al. 2024; Colcombe and Kramer 2003; Dustman et al. 1990; Hayes et al. 2015). Previous studies have primarily focused on older adults, often emphasizing that CRF is particularly beneficial for age‐sensitive cognitive processes such as executive function and episodic memory. However, some research has suggested that these associations may be more robust in older populations due to more extensive variability in cognitive and CRF levels (Gajewski et al. 2023). However, we did not observe interactions between CRF and age on cognitive performance in this sample (p > 0.05), indicating that the associations between CRF and cognition, and CRF and network connectivity were consistent across middle‐aged to older adults. The present study extends the prior literature by including an adult sample across the lifespan. It provides additional evidence that the relationship between enhanced CRF and better cognitive function, including episodic memory and executive function, may persist throughout adulthood (Stillman et al. 2020).

Alignment With Evidence From Exercise Interventions

The findings reported here align with a growing literature documenting the effects of exercise training on functional connectivity and cognition in older adults (Won, Callow, Pena, Gogniat, et al. 2021). For example, a 12‐week walking program improved hippocampal and cerebellar connectivity and inter‐network connections in older adults with normal cognition and mild cognitive impairment (Won, Callow, Pena, Jordan, et al. 2021). A randomized controlled trial in older adults reported improvements in DMN after a 1‐year exercise and stretching intervention (Voss, Prakash, et al. 2010). Notably, several studies have reported that improved brain neural network functional connectivity was associated with improvements in memory and executive function task performance (e.g., RAVLT and verbal fluency) (Won, Callow, Pena, Jordan, et al. 2021; Won, Faroqi‐Shah, Callow, Williams, et al. 2021) suggesting the beneficial effects on cognitive performance induced by exercise training may be related to alterations in neural network connectivity. In an analysis of large‐scale networks in older adults after an exercise intervention, it has also been shown that increased connectivity within the salience network, as well as increased connectivity between the salience network and frontal–parietal network, were related to improved episodic memory performance (Won et al. 2023). Yet another intervention study reported that increased VO2peak was associated with increased functional connectivity between the DMN and prefrontal cortex and decreased functional connectivity between the thalamus and sensorimotor region (Flodin et al. 2017). These latter findings are consistent with the negative associations between CRF and connectivity with several subcortical areas, including the thalamus. These parallels strengthen the argument that CRF‐related connectivity associations could support CRF‐related cognitive benefits.

Mechanistic Interpretations

Our findings point to distinct patterns of functional connectivity associated with greater CRF, with positive associations involving integrative networks such as ventral attention, frontoparietal, and DMNs, and negative associations concentrated mainly within the limbic system. These patterns suggest multiple underlying neurofunctional mechanisms linking CRF to cognition. One possibility is cholinergic neuromodulation: recent work by Won et al. (2023) demonstrated that basal forebrain functional connectivity mediates the relationship between CRF and cognition in healthy older women, implicating the cholinergic system as a key contributor to enhanced cortical communication in support of cognitive performance. Additionally, the dopaminergic system may contribute to these effects. Dopamine plays a central role in cognitive flexibility, working memory, and goal‐directed attention, and is known to influence large‐scale network dynamics, including frontoparietal and DMN interactions (Dang et al. 2012; Shine 2019). Recent work suggests that the dopaminergic system may play a role in CRF's impact on improved cognition by use of spontaneous eye blink rate as a proxy, noninvasive marker of neural dopaminergic activity (Kuwamizu et al. 2021). CRF has been linked to preserved dopamine receptor availability in aging, suggesting a potential mechanism that supports flexible and efficient brain function.

Notably, the negatively predictive FCs we observed may reflect CRF‐related decoupling or functional segregation of brain networks, particularly in limbic regions exhibiting near‐zero or below‐zero average FC. This observation is consistent with the neural differentiation hypothesis, wherein greater fitness supports more selective, task‐relevant communication by suppressing interference from internally driven or emotionally responsive systems. Such decoupling may be driven by increased GABAergic inhibitory tone, which has been associated with enhanced network segregation and efficiency. Recent work suggests that the recovery of inhibitory functions via the GABAergic system may play an essential role in exercise‐induced improvements in brain health (Novak et al. 2024). Together, these findings suggest that CRF may enhance cognition by amplifying integrative signaling via neuromodulatory systems and promoting efficient network specialization through targeted network segregation.

Limitations and Future Directions

There are some limitations to this work that need to be taken into consideration. First, the NIH ToolBox provides limited information regarding CRF. Although the 2MWT is a relatively reliable measure of the 6‐minute walk test, which is correlated with VO2 max (Bohannon et al. 2015; Selman et al. 2014), it is not as precise as the gold standard of CRF—direct measurement of VO2MAX using indirect calorimetry during a graded exercise test. Nevertheless, our findings replicate previous reports for significant associations between 2MWT performance and cognitive performance in younger (Won et al. 2024) and older adults (Callow and Smith 2023). Regarding our analytic strategy, CPM may not capture highly complex, non‐linear relationships as effectively as support vector regression (SVR) and other machine‐learning methods. Nevertheless, it has the advantage of clear interpretation through predictive values of features rather than assuming features' weights reflect feature importance (Shen et al. 2017). This consideration is crucial for comparison to previous literature and to inform future hypotheses on brain network structure and function more effectively (Shen et al. 2017). The balance of simplicity, predictive power, and generalizability makes CPM a robust tool for investigating brain‐behavior relationships. Future studies should examine the longitudinal trajectory of change in these associations and whether genetic risk for Alzheimer's disease (APOE genotype) moderates these effects and consider the additional role of structural indices (i.e., cortical thickness) in protecting cognition in later life.

Conclusion

Together, these findings suggest that the magnitude of positive connectivity within the VAN, particularly with the insula, and negative connectivity (or decoupling) between the limbic and other functional networks may be the mechanism by which CRF is related to better cognition across both middle and older adulthood. This work provides insights into functional profiles that can be targeted for interventional studies that involve exercise to improve CRF, or possibly even neural stimulation, or a combination thereof. Finally, future work is anticipated to utilize and build upon these findings to determine if these relationships are also reported longitudinally.
