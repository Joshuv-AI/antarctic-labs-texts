Liposomal bupivacaine versus ropivacaine perianal infiltration for postoperative analgesia of haemorrhoidectomy: a multicentre, randomised controlled trial
Authors: Huageng Huang, Shanshan Wei, Yingnian Lan, Yanjuan Huang, Shucong Liang, Ning Wei, Xiantao Wei, Xiaojuan Wei, Xinmei Tan, Ningkang Liu, Xiangquan Qin, Kejian Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate whether liposomal bupivacaine provides superior postoperative analgesia compared with ropivacaine following haemorrhoidectomy, reduces the need for rescue analgesics in patients with mixed haemorrhoids and maintains a comparable safety profile.DesignProspective, randomised controlled multicentre trial.SettingThree grade 3A general hospitals in Guangxi, China, conducted between September 2023 and July 2024.ParticipantsEligible patients were aged 18–75 years undergoing elective external dissection and internal ligation for mixed haemorrhoids (grade III and IV mixed haemorrhoids). Key exclusion criteria were severe systemic diseases, history of substance abuse or known allergies to local anaesthetics. A total of 264 patients (male, n=162; mean age, 43.1±12.2 years) were enrolled and completed the study, with 132 randomised to each group.InterventionsPatients received either ropivacaine (100 mg in 20 mL) or liposomal bupivacaine (226 mg in 20 mL) through local infiltration at the end of surgery.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were the resting numerical rating scale (NRS) pain scores (0–10) at postoperative 1, 5, 7, 9, 11, 24, 36, 48 and 72 hours. The secondary outcomes included the following: (1) exercise NRS pain scores at the same time points; (2) time to first ambulation, ambulation NRS pain scores on postoperative days 1, 2 and 3 and the proportion of patients having ambulation NRS pain scores ≥4; (3) defecation NRS pain scores and the proportion of of patients having score ≥4; (4) the time to the first administration of rescue analgesics and proportion of patients requiring rescue analgesics; (5) proportion of pain-free patients (NRS pain scores ≤1) 0–24, 24–72 and 0–72 hour after surgery; (6) incision assessments on postoperative days 1, 3 and 7 and postoperative adverse events (AEs); and (7) patients’ and researchers’ satisfaction with pain management.ResultsResting NRS pain scores were significantly lower in the liposomal bupivacaine group at all time points (median, 0–2.0 vs 2.0–4.0 for ropivacaine, p<0.001). Exercise NRS pain scores were also significantly lower in the liposomal bupivacaine group (median, 0–2.0 vs 2.0–5.0, p<0.001). Additionally, median ambulation NRS pain scores were reduced in the liposomal bupivacaine group on postoperative days 1–3 (median, 1.0–2.0 vs 3.0–5.0, p<0.001). By 72 hours after surgery, 78.8% of the patients in the liposomal bupivacaine group had defecated, reporting lower defecation NRS pain scores (median, 3.0 vs 5.0, p<0.001). Fewer patients in this group required rescue analgesics (43.9% vs 83.3%, p<0.001). Incision assessment scores were comparable between groups, with no significant difference in AE rates (3.8% vs 1.5%, p=0.444).ConclusionsPerianal infiltration of liposomal bupivacaine significantly alleviates postoperative resting and exercise pain in patients undergoing surgery for mixed haemorrhoids, enhances comfort during ambulation and defecation, reduces the need for additional analgesics and demonstrates a safety profile comparable to that of ropivacaine.Trial registrationchictr.org.cn # ChiCTR2300075276.

Introduction

Haemorrhoidectomy is one of the most common anorectal surgical procedures, with postoperative pain being a major source of patient discomfort.1 This pain not only leads to high blood pressure, heart rate and stress responses, which can impair wound healing, but also contributes to sleep disturbances and anxiety and, in severe cases, may prolong hospital stays and recovery periods.2 Traditional postoperative pain management includes systemic analgesics (eg, opioids), epidural anaesthesia and regional nerve blocks.3 However, each method has limitations. Although opioids are effective, their side effects, such as nausea, vomiting, constipation and potential for addiction, restrict their clinical use.4 Therefore, exploring safer and more effective analgesic methods is clinically significant for enhancing recovery and reducing healthcare burdens.

Local infiltration anaesthesia is commonly used for perioperative pain management in haemorrhoidectomy. By directly injecting anaesthetics around the surgical site, it effectively avoids the risks and complications associated with general anaesthesia. Additionally, local anaesthesia significantly reduces the need for opioids, thereby minimising the occurrence of opioid-related adverse effects.5 Ropivacaine, a long-acting local anaesthetic, is favoured in clinical local infiltration anaesthesia owing to its low cardiac toxicity and high safety profile.6 However, the analgesic duration of a single perianal injection of ropivacaine is limited, typically failing to meet the prolonged pain management needs after haemorrhoidectomy, which presents a challenge in pain control.7

Liposomal bupivacaine, a novel long-acting local anaesthetic, offers extended pain relief for up to 72 hours through a slow-release mechanism facilitated by its lipid encapsulated structure.8 The prolonged analgesia of liposomal bupivacaine (up to 72 hours) is attributed to its DepoFoam multivesicular delivery system, which encapsulates the drug in discrete aqueous chambers, enabling biphasic release.9 10 Drug release occurs in two phases: 15%–20% is released in the first 24 hours for immediate pain relief, and the remainder is slowly released over 48–72 hour via passive diffusion and lipid degradation.11 Clinical studies have confirmed its extended analgesic effect and reduced opioid use postoperatively.12 13 Pharmacokinetic data show that over 90% of the drug remains at the injection site, maintaining effective levels while minimising systemic exposure.14 15

Local infiltration of anaesthetics has been shown to effectively alleviate postoperative pain following haemorrhoidectomy; however, the potential advantages of liposomal bupivacaine in the context of external dissection and internal ligation for mixed haemorrhoids remain insufficiently established. This study was therefore designed to directly compare liposomal bupivacaine with ropivacaine, with the aim of determining whether the former offers prolonged analgesia that better addresses the postoperative pain management needs of patients undergoing surgery for mixed haemorrhoids.

Methods

This multicentre randomised controlled trial was conducted from September 2023 to July 2024 at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (The Second Nanning People’s Hospital), Hechi Third People’s Hospital and Hechi People’s Hospital, and the first patient participated on 21 September 2023. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethical Committees of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, Hechi Third People’s Hospital and Hechi People’s Hospital.

The trial protocol was registered at the China Clinical Trial Registry (31 August 2023, # ChiCTR2300075276) and conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. The study design adhered to the 2010 Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials statement. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before enrolment.

Patients

Patients aged 18–75 years scheduled for haemorrhoidectomy for grade III and IV mixed haemorrhoids (Goligher classification)16 were eligible. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status IV or higher; (2) body mass index (BMI) <18 or >30 kg/m²; (3) contraindications to intraspinal nerve block; (4) expected difficult airways (Mallampati class 3 or 4), acute respiratory infection or asthma attack; (5) unstable angina, severe arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension (systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥160 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥100 mmHg despite medical treatment), hypotension (SBP≤90 mmHg or DBP≤60 mmHg), bradycardia (heart rate<50 beats/min), atrioventricular block or sinoatrial block; (6) abnormal liver function (aspartate aminotransferase and/or alanine aminotransferase ≥ 3× the upper limit of normal range (ULN) and/or total bilirubin (TBIL) ≥ 2× ULN) or abnormal renal function (creatinine >2 × ULN); (7) history of drug abuse and/or alcoholism within 2 years before screening; (8) history of psychiatry, stroke within 6 months or stroke sequelae; (9) long-term use of hypnotics, sedatives or opioids; (10) known allergy or contraindication to ropivacaine, liposomal bupivacaine or their components; and (11) participated in other drug trials within 3 months before screening.

Randomisation, masking and blinding

Randomisation (1:1 ratio) to receive either ropivacaine or liposomal bupivacaine and concealment were conducted using a web service (www.medresman.org.cn). Both the patients, outcome assessors and statistician were blinded to the group assignment. The attending anaesthesiologists and surgeon were aware of the group assignment.

Intervention

Spinal anaesthesia was induced. The puncture site was at the L2–L3 or L3–L4 intervertebral space, with the patient positioned in the left lateral decubitus position. On successful epidural puncture, a lumbar puncture needle was inserted through the epidural needle to access the subarachnoid space, delivering 2 mL (15 mg) of 0.75% ropivacaine hydrochloride injection intrathecally. The level of blockade was controlled below T10. The surgical position was the jack-knife position. Common intraoperative infusion solutions included a mixture of 1.5 g cefuroxime sodium in 100 mL of 0.9% NaCl, lactated Ringer’s solution and hydroxyethyl starch. All patients underwent haemorrhoidectomy using a standardised external dissection and internal ligation (EDIL) technique or plus injection sclerotherapy. This involved excision of prolapsed external haemorrhoidal tissue using conventional instruments (either electrocautery or scalpel) to minimise mucosal disruption and preserve anorectal function, followed by ligation of internal haemorrhoidal vascular pedicles at their base using absorbable 2–0 Vicryl sutures, without mucosal excision.17 When applicable, adjunctive sclerotherapy was performed by injecting 3% polidocanol into the residual internal haemorrhoidal tissue to induce fibrosis and reduce recurrence risk.

Immediately following haemorrhoidectomy, the surgeon prepared 20 mL of the study drug using two 10 mL syringes. Using a 2-gauge needle, perianal local infiltration anaesthesia was administered at six evenly distributed points around the anus, corresponding to clock-face positions (12, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 o’clock) around the anal sphincter. Infiltration volumes were divided into six equal portions, with 3.3 mL injected at each of the first five points and 3.5 mL at the final point (figure 1). For perianal local infiltration anaesthesia, the needle was inserted approximately 2 cm below the dentate line into the anal canal at an angle of 33–40° to the canal wall. An incremental infiltration technique was used to confirm the accuracy of the injection depth; swelling in the anal mucosal area indicated an incorrect angle or inadequate needle depth, warranting repositioning of the needle.

Study drug infiltration technique. The study drug was administered via infiltration in a fan-like fashion outside of the anal sphincter (centre circle); using a 25-gauge needle, perianal local infiltration anaesthesia was administered at six evenly distributed points around the anus, corresponding to clock-face positions (12, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 o‘clock) around the anal sphincter. Infiltration volumes were divided into six equal portions, with 3.3 mL injected at each of the first five points and 3.5 mL at the final point.

Frequent aspiration was performed during injection to minimise the risk of inadvertent intravascular injection. If blood was aspirated, the needle was repositioned, and aspiration was repeated until no blood was visible. The study drug consisted of 20 mL of liposomal bupivacaine (20 mL: 266 mg, 230 215BL, Hengrui Medicine, China) for the treatment group and 0.5% ropivacaine hydrochloride solution (20 mL: 100 mg, 230603CA, Hengrui Medicine) for the control group. Patients were observed for at least 72 hours after surgery. If patients reported pain with a numerical rating scale (NRS) score of ≥4 (assessed with patients in a supine position using an 11-point scale ranging from 0 to 10, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 represents the most severe pain), rescue analgesics were administered on evaluation by the investigator. The category and dosage of rescue analgesics at each centre adhered to local standards of clinical practice. All administrations were meticulously documented, including the analgesic agent used, dosage, route and timing in relation to pain assessments.

Trial outcomes

The primary outcome was resting NRS pain scores 1, 5, 7, 9, 11, 24, 36, 48 and 72 hours after surgery. Secondary outcomes included (1) exercise NRS pain scores at the same time points (exercise defined as the patient lying supine and lifting the hips); (2) time to first ambulation, ambulation NRS pain scores on postoperative days 1, 2 and 3 and the proportion of patients having ambulation NRS pain scores ≥4 at these time points; (3) defecation NRS pain scores (no score if defecation did not occur) and the proportion of patients having scores ≥4; (4) the time to the first receipt of rescue analgesics and proportion of patients requiring rescue analgesics; (5) proportion of pain-free patients (NRS pain score ≤1) 0–24, 24–72 and 0–72 hours after surgery; (6) incision assessments on postoperative days 1, 3 and 7, evaluating erythema, exudate, oedema and induration. Each item was scored from 0 (least severe) to 4 (most severe), with a total score calculated as the sum of all items. Adverse events (AEs), including drowsiness, dizziness, nausea/vomiting and constipation within 72 hours after surgery, were recorded; (7) patients’ and researchers’ satisfaction with pain management assessed using a 5-point scale (0, very dissatisfied; 4, most satisfied) 72 hours after surgery. The study period extended to postoperative day 7, with phone follow-up conducted by a study nurse for early discharges.

Safety

AEs were evaluated using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0 and included but not limited to dizziness, fatigue, fever and incision infection. Anaesthesia-related AEs that occurred during the perioperative period were managed according to the medical practice of each centre.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Sample size and statistical analysis

Based on the pre-experiment data from our centre, the postoperative 24-hour resting NRS pain scores between the two groups exhibited the smallest variability; therefore, the time point was selected for sample size estimation. This approach maximises the likelihood of detecting a significant difference in the primary outcome. In the ropivacaine group (n=25), the mean 24-hour NRS pain score was 4.12±1.52 (mean±SD), whereas in the liposomal bupivacaine group (n=25), it had a mean score of 3.39. Using a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1 - β) of 0.90 and assuming equal allocation between groups, a minimum of 93 participants per arm was required to detect a statistically significant difference. To account for an anticipated drop-out rate of 30%, the final target enrolment was set at 133 patients per group, for a total sample size of 266 participants.

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean (SD) and analysed using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (IQR) and analysed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were assessed using the χ² test. Primary outcome analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat (ITT) basis. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p<0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The primary endpoint was assessed in the ITT population (all randomised subjects) as well as in the per-protocol population (those who actually received the intended intervention).

As shown in table 4, fewer patients in the liposomal bupivacaine group required rescue analgesia within 72 hours (43.9% vs 83.3%, p<0.001), and the median time to the first use of analgesics was later (10.0 vs 7.0 hours, p<0.001). At 72 hours postoperatively, both patients and researchers reported higher satisfaction with pain management in the liposomal bupivacaine group than in the ropivacaine group (3.0 vs 1.0 for both; all p<0.001).

Discussion

This prospective, randomised, multicentre study demonstrated that perianal infiltration with liposomal bupivacaine significantly improved pain control on postoperative day 3 in patients with mixed haemorrhoids, reduced the need for rescue analgesics and exhibited a safety profile comparable with that of ropivacaine. Compared with previous studies,18 the present study introduced several methodological innovations to enhance clinical relevance. The infiltration protocol was refined by adopting a 20 mL dose to minimise tissue distension and improve precision in anatomically constrained perianal regions. We applied updated surgical techniques, including EDIL and, when appropriate, adjunctive sclerotherapy, which reflect contemporary, tissue-sparing practices. Furthermore, our outcome assessment framework extended beyond conventional pain scores to include functional recovery indicators, standardised incision assessment, patient- and researcher-reported outcomes and the use of multimodal analgesia. Collectively, these improvements align with the principles of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) and strengthen the translational value of our findingative 72 hous in the context of modern anorectal procedures.

Liposomal bupivacaine demonstrated superior pain control compared with ropivacaine, significantly reducing resting NRS pain scores at all assessed postoperative time points from 5 to 72 hours (eg, 5 hours, 0 vs 2.0; 72 hours, 1.0 vs 3.0; all p<0.001). This efficacy is attributed to its biphasic lipid-layered structure, enabling sustained drug release over 72 hours.15 The results align with the findings of previous studies across surgical contexts, substantiating the capacity of liposomal bupivacaine for prolonged analgesia.19 20 In this study, assessment time points of 5, 7, 9 and 11 hours after surgery were strategically selected based on the pharmacokinetics of the anaesthetics used. Spinal anaesthesia typically lasts 4–6 hours, whereas ropivacaine provides local analgesia for 2–6 hours. In contrast, liposomal bupivacaine offers prolonged effects of up to 72 hours. These intervals were chosen to capture the critical transition from waning spinal and ropivacaine analgesia to the onset of liposomal bupivacaine’s extended action, enabling precise evaluation of analgesic duration and informing postoperative pain management.

In total knee arthroplasty (TKA), liposomal bupivacaine demonstrated comparable reductions in visual analogue scale (VAS) pain score, with a 78% decrease in opioid demand within 48 hours.21 Similarly, patients who had undergone breast reconstruction experienced lower pain scores 24 hours after surgery (3.5 vs 5.0; p<0.01) and delayed opioid initiation.22 These findings underscore its clinical advantage in reducing opioid consumption while maintaining effective pain control across diverse surgical procedures.

Mechanistically, by attenuating early postoperative pain, liposomal bupivacaine may mitigate the physiological stress response, characterised by heightened sympathetic activity.23 This extended analgesic profile fosters a smoother recovery trajectory and potentially reduces pain-induced complications, such as impaired incision healing and haemodynamic instability.24 25

Exercise pain scores, often more predictive of functional recovery, showed significant improvement with liposomal bupivacaine (eg, 5 hours, 0 vs 3.0; 72 hours, 1.0 vs 2.0; all p<0.001). These findings are particularly relevant in surgeries prioritising early ambulation, such as joint replacements or abdominal procedures.26 27 Exercise pain control enhances patient compliance with rehabilitation protocols, reducing immobility-associated complications such as venous thromboembolism.28

Additionally, by suppressing local inflammatory mediators, liposomal bupivacaine minimises nociceptive sensitisation, facilitating superior motion-tolerant analgesia.29 This capability cements its role in multimodal analgesia, further supported by studies demonstrating similar benefits in major orthopaedic and oncological surgeries.27 30

Although the time to first ambulation showed no significant difference between the groups (median, 535 vs 647.5 min, p=0.359), liposomal bupivacaine significantly reduced the NRS pain scores during ambulation on postoperative days 1–3 (eg, postoperative day 1, 2.0 vs 5.0, p<0.001). The proportion of patients reporting severe pain (NRS ≥4) during exercise also markedly decreased. Effective pain relief during activity is paramount in postoperative recovery frameworks such as ERAS, which emphasises early mobilisation.31 Studies in total shoulder arthroplasty further highlight the role of liposomal bupivacaine in improving ambulation-related outcomes, reporting lower opioid consumption and better functional recovery.19

Effective control of pain during bowel movements is an underexplored but critical parameter of recovery. Liposomal bupivacaine did not increase the proportion of patients who defecated within 72 hours (78.8% vs 84.1%, p=0.342) but reduced the NRS pain scores during defecation (3.0 vs 5.0, p<0.001). Given the substantial effect of opioids on gastrointestinal function, these results suggest that minimising systemic opioid exposure via local analgesia can enhance bowel recovery.32

Liposomal bupivacaine significantly reduced the need for rescue analgesics (43.9% vs 83.3%, p<0.001), with longer intervals to the first rescue dose (10 vs 7 hours, p<0.001). Furthermore, a higher proportion of patients experienced pain-free periods (NRS ≤1) within 72 hours (9.8% vs 0%, p<0.001). These results mirror findings from TKA studies, where opioid demand was reduced by nearly 80%, reflecting a clear opioid-sparing advantage.21 Compared with some clinical trials, such as the HYR-PB21 study by Cui et al, which reported a 62%–92% opioid-free rate, the analgesic efficacy observed in this study was slightly lower. Several factors may account for this discrepancy: challenges in selecting the injection site and performing the technique (eg, difficulties with internal sphincter injections), drug leakage due to the extensive incision area in haemorrhoidectomy, localised oedema and discomfort following injections in internal haemorrhoids, increased pain from early postoperative activities or bowel movements.

Increased patient satisfaction scores (3.0 vs 1.0; p<0.001) further support the clinical value of liposomal bupivacaine. Enhanced patient-reported outcomes not only validate its analgesic efficacy but also underline its contribution to psychological comfort during recovery.33

Importantly, liposomal bupivacaine exhibited no detrimental effects on incision assessment, as reflected by comparable incision assessment scores on postoperative days 1, 3 and 7. AEs such as infection and delayed incision closure were also similar across the groups, underscoring its safety profile.34 35

By reducing opioid reliance while maintaining effective analgesia, liposomal bupivacaine aligns with contemporary pain management paradigms advocating multimodal analgesia.36 Its integration into ERAS protocols may further enhance recovery timelines and decrease hospitalisation costs.37 Further investigations are warranted to optimise dosage regimens, refine patient selection criteria and elucidate molecular mechanisms underlying its prolonged efficacy. Comparative studies assessing cost-effectiveness in broader surgical populations may also aid its adoption in clinical practice.38 39

This trial has several limitations. First, generalisability was constrained by the exclusion of patients with Mallampati class 3 or 4; thus, future studies incorporating advanced airway management protocols are warranted to evaluate safety and efficacy in this population. Second, the absence of subclassification within grade III and IV haemorrhoids limited our ability to perform detailed subgroup analyses of pain trajectories and recovery outcomes across varying severity levels, highlighting an important gap that future prospective trials should address using standardised grading frameworks. Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up data limits insights into delayed perioperative complications, such as anal sphincter hypotonia or altered bowel habits associated with prolonged nerve blocks and perianal infiltration techniques. These endpoints should be prioritised in longitudinal extensions of this work.

Conclusions

In summary, perianal infiltration of liposomal bupivacaine significantly alleviates postoperative resting and exercise pain in patients undergoing surgery for mixed haemorrhoids, enhances comfort during ambulation and defecation, reduces the need for additional analgesics and demonstrates a safety profile comparable with that of ropivacaine.
