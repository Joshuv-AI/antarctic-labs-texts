Doxorubicin Impairs Skeletal Muscle Regeneration by Dysregulating the Early Inflammatory Response
Authors: Shinya Kondo, Takahiro Ishizaka, Mao Yamada, Shugo Yonehara, Haruo Sasaki, Masashi Isaji, Takahiro Nakagawa, Keisuke Horiuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFor patients with certain types of malignant tumors, chemotherapy is administered before and/or after surgery. However, the potential impact of chemotherapeutic agents on tissue regeneration and wound healing after surgery is not fully understood. In this study, we examined the possible effects of doxorubicin (DOX), one of the most widely used chemotherapeutic agents, on muscle regeneration using a mouse muscle injury model. Histologic analysis revealed that DOX significantly impairs muscle regeneration, resulting in reduced muscle mass and fibrosis when administered during the early phase of the inflammatory response following injury. Contrary to our initial assumption, DOX administration did not suppress the proliferation of satellite cells or the expression of myogenic transcripts. Rather, DOX delayed the infiltration of immune cells to the injury site, which most likely resulted in insufficient clearance of necrotic tissues and prolonged immune cell infiltration. Taken together, our findings reveal an unrecognized effect of DOX on muscle regeneration and underscore the critical role of the early inflammatory response in initiating proper muscle regeneration after injury.

Introduction

Chemotherapy is often administered to patients with certain types of malignancies before and/or after surgery (neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy, respectively) to improve clinical outcomes. These malignancies include breast cancer [1], non‐small cell lung cancer [2], colon cancer [3], and gastric cancer [4]. In the field of bone and soft tissue tumors, neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy combined with definitive surgery remain the standard treatment for osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma [5, 6]. Although there is limited clinical data regarding the optimal interval between chemotherapy and surgery, several studies suggest that shorter intervals may be associated with improved outcomes, at least for certain types of malignancies [7, 8, 9]. A retrospective study involving 703 patients with extremity osteosarcoma showed that increased time from the definitive surgery to adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with poorer survival [10]. These findings suggest the potential clinical benefit of shortening the time between surgery and chemotherapy. However, there is a lack of data on the potential adverse effects of chemotherapeutic agents on wound healing and tissue regeneration following surgery. Possibly for this reason, adjuvant chemotherapy is typically administered only after wound healing is complete.

Skeletal muscle is one of the tissues commonly damaged during surgery, especially in patients with tumors arising in soft tissue or bone. Within several hours after injury, immune cells, such as neutrophils and macrophages, infiltrate the injured site and clear necrotic myofibers. Subsequently, fibro‐adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) proliferate and produce extracellular matrix that helps form a niche for regenerating myofibers. Around the time the early inflammatory response is resolved, satellite cells (SCs) proliferate and ultimately differentiate to form myofibers. In mice, these sequential events typically occur within 1–2 weeks, with full recovery of muscle tissue by 4 weeks post‐injury (refer to [11, 12, 13, 14] for a detailed description of muscle regeneration processes). Although skeletal muscle has a robust regenerative capacity, this can be compromised under various pathological conditions, often resulting in irreversible changes such as intramuscular fibrosis and fatty infiltration [15, 16, 17].

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the potential adverse effects of doxorubicin (DOX) [18], one of the most widely used chemotherapeutic agents, on muscle regeneration using a mouse model. In this model, muscle injury is induced by intramuscular administration of cardiotoxin (CTX), a snake venom used to model muscle injury in animals [19]. We found that DOX administration significantly impairs muscle regeneration; however, the effect of DOX was most prominent when administered at the time of injury. Of note, our data suggest that DOX administration does not directly impede muscle regeneration by inhibiting SC proliferation, but appears to suppress immune cell infiltration into the injured muscle during the early inflammatory phase. Taken together, our findings reveal a previously unrecognized effect of DOX on muscle regeneration and may also provide insight into the optimal timing of perioperative chemotherapy.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Ten‐week‐old male C57BL/6N mice were purchased from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan) and housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions. Mice were maintained at a constant temperature under a 12:12‐h light–dark cycle and had ad libitum access to feed and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the National Defense Medical College (approval number: 23073‐1).

Muscle Injury and DOX Administration

For general anesthesia, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of a combination of medetomidine (0.3 mg/kg), midazolam (4 mg/kg), and butorphanol (5 mg/kg). Muscle injury was induced by intramuscular injection of CTX as previously described [19]. Specifically, 50 μL of CTX (10 μM in PBS; Latoxan Laboratory, Portes‐lès‐Valence, France) was injected into the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle of the hindlimb. The contralateral limb remained uninjured and was used as a control. A single intravenous injection of either 10 mg/kg DOX (Nippon Kayaku, Tokyo, Japan; DOX‐treated group) or an equivalent volume of PBS (Ctrl group) was administered. TA muscles were harvested at various time points post‐injury to evaluate muscle regeneration.

Histological and Immunohistochemical Analyses

For histological analysis, TA muscles were collected at days 3, 7, 14, and 28, and 12 weeks post‐injury. The samples were mounted on tragacanth gum (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) and snap‐frozen in 2‐methylbutane (FUJIFILM Wako) cooled with liquid nitrogen. Cryosections (10 μm thick) were prepared from the mid‐belly of the TA muscle using a cryostat and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), van Gieson, or Alizarin Red according to standard protocols.

For immunohistochemistry, fresh‐frozen TA sections were fixed with acetone (FUJIFILM Wako) at −30°C for 20 min, blocked with Blocking One Histo (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti‐laminin rabbit polyclonal (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO; L9393, 1:1000), anti‐laminin rat monoclonal (Sigma‐Aldrich; L0663, 1:500), and anti‐PAX7 rabbit polyclonal (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab187339, 1:100). After washing, sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with Alexa Fluor Plus 555‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA; A32732, 1:500) and CF 488‐conjugated anti‐rat IgG (Biotium, Fremont, CA; 20023, 1:500). Sections were mounted using VECTASHIELD Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA; H‐1800).

The cross‐sectional areas (CSAs) of regenerating myofibers were measured from images of laminin‐immunostained sections. Myofibers with CSA < 50 μm2 were excluded from the analysis. The number of SCs was determined from laminin‐ and PAX7‐immunostained sections by counting cells with nuclei that stained positively for PAX7. Microscopic analysis was performed using a BZ‐X810 fluorescence microscope, and quantification was conducted using BZ‐X Analyzer software (KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan).

Gene Expression Analysis by RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from whole TA muscles collected on days 1, 3, and 7 post‐injury using Isogen II (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan). Reverse transcription was carried out using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) to synthesize cDNA. Gene expression was quantified using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio) on a Fast 7900HT Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA). Expression levels were normalized to Gapdh transcript levels and analyzed relative to the expression level on day 1 post‐injury in the Ctrl group (set to 1). Data analysis was performed using SDS 2.4 software (Applied Biosystems). All experiments were conducted in triplicate. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Flow Cytometry for Immune Cell Quantification

TA muscles collected on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post‐injury and whole blood collected on day 1 post‐injury were processed for flow cytometry as previously described with some modifications [20]. The TA muscles were finely minced and digested with 0.2% collagenase type II (Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, NJ) at 37°C for 90 min. The digested tissues were filtered through cell strainers to remove debris, and red blood cells were lysed with a hypotonic solution (Sigma‐Aldrich) prior to antibody staining. Isolated cells were blocked with anti‐mouse CD16/32 antibody (BioLegend, San Diego, CA; 101319, 1:200), and stained at 4°C for 30 min in the dark with the following antibodies: FITC‐conjugated anti‐mouse CD45 (BioLegend; 103107, 1:200), PE‐conjugated anti‐mouse F4/80 (BioLegend; 123109, 1:100), and APC/Cyanine7‐conjugated anti‐mouse Ly‐6G (BioLegend; 127623, 1:100). Macrophages were identified as CD45+ F4/80+ cells, and neutrophils as CD45+ F4/80− Ly‐6G+ cells. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed with BD FACSDiva and FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA).

Statistical Analysis

One‐way analysis of variance followed by Turkey's multiple comparison test was performed to evaluate time‐course changes within a group. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired t‐test was used. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Administration of DOX at the Early Inflammatory Phase Impedes Muscle Regeneration

To examine whether DOX affects muscle regeneration following injury, we administered DOX at different time points and analyzed the muscle tissues histologically 14 days post‐injury (Figure 1A). As shown in Figure 1B, muscle regeneration was severely compromised when DOX was administered at the time of injury (day 0), as indicated by the presence of immature muscle fibers and infiltrating immune cells. Administration of DOX 3 days post‐injury also impaired muscle regeneration; however, the effect was significantly diminished. In contrast, there were no apparent defects in regeneration when DOX was administered 7 and 3 days before, or 7 days after injury. We also quantified myofiber CSA of TA muscles collected on day 14 and found that the myofibers were significantly thinner when DOX was administered at the time of injury (day 0) compared to the other specimens (Figure 1C,D). These observations suggest that DOX disrupts muscle regeneration when administered during the early phase of the inflammatory response, whereas muscle regeneration appears insensitive to DOX outside this window.

(C, D) Frequency distribution (C) and median (D) of CSA of TA muscle 2 weeks post‐injury. N = 3–4 for each group.

Regenerated Muscle Is Irreversibly Damaged by DOX

Since the effect of DOX on muscle regeneration was most pronounced when administered at the time of injury (day 0), we used this protocol for subsequent experiments. Ctrl mice received an intravenous injection of 100 μL PBS. Following muscle injury, both Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice exhibited a significant decrease in muscle volume relative to body weight (Figure 2A). In Ctrl mice, muscle volume gradually increased after day 7 and fully recovered by day 14. In contrast, recovery was slower in DOX‐treated mice, and muscle volume did not return to baseline even at day 28. There was no significant difference in the relative muscle volume of uninjured TA muscles in the contralateral limb between Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice. Although DOX‐treated mice exhibited some body weight loss, they recovered to baseline levels by day 21 after injury (Figure 2B). The relative volume of regenerated muscle to body weight was significantly lower in DOX‐treated mice than in Ctrl mice even at 12 weeks post‐injury (Ctrl mice: 2.58 ± 0.18 mg/g; DOX‐treated mice: 1.65 ± 0.18 mg/g; p = 0.0034; unpaired t‐test). Consistent with the reduced muscle volume, the cross section of TA muscles collected from CTX‐injured limbs was markedly smaller in DOX‐treated mice compared to Ctrl mice (Figure 2C). Furthermore, the histogram of myofiber CSA and median myofiber CSA showed that TA muscles collected from CTX‐injured limbs in DOX‐treated mice predominantly consist of much thinner myofibers than those in Ctrl mice (Figure 2D, upper panels, Figure 2E). The median myofiber CSA of TA muscles collected from uninjured contralateral limbs did not differ significantly between Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice (Figure 2E). However, histogram analysis showed a leftward shift in myofiber size distribution in DOX‐treated mice (Figure 2D, lower panels), consistent with the proportional decrease in muscle volume relative to body weight loss (Figure 2A, right panel).

To further investigate the effects of DOX on muscle regeneration, we histologically analyzed muscle tissues from Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice on days 3, 7, 14, and 28 post‐injury. As shown in Figure 3A and Figure S1, necrotic muscle fibers and massive immune cell infiltration were observed on day 3 in Ctrl mice. By day 14, the infiltration of immune cells was significantly decreased, and the necrotic myofibers had completely disappeared, indicating the resolution of the inflammation induced by muscle damage. By day 28, we were able to histologically confirm that muscle tissue had fully recovered in Ctrl mice. In DOX‐treated mice, immune cell infiltration on day 3 appeared comparable to that in Ctrl mice; however, immune cells persisted through day 14, indicating a markedly prolonged inflammatory response. Of interest, despite comparable immune cell infiltration on day 3, necrotic myofibers remained even on days 7 and 14. While the necrotic myofibers were cleared by day 28, there were hematoxylin‐stained deposits in the regenerating muscle tissue. These depositions were positively stained with Alizarin Red, indicating that these were calcified depositions, which are often observed in mice with defective muscle regeneration (Figure 3B) [21, 22, 23]. Furthermore, the interstitial space of regenerated muscle tissue in DOX mice exhibited extensive fibrosis compared to Ctrl mice at 12 weeks post‐injury, as evidenced by robust Van Gieson's staining (Figure 3C).

Dysregulated SC Differentiation in DOX‐Treated Mice

Since DOX suppresses cell proliferation by inducing cell death through various mechanisms [18], we first examined whether the defects in muscle regeneration were derived from impaired SC proliferation. SCs are muscle‐specific stem cells that differentiate into myofibers and are indispensable to muscle regeneration [24, 25]. Muscle tissues from Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice were collected on day 7 and stained for laminin (a basal membrane constituent) and PAX7 (an SC‐specific transcription factor), and with DAPI (which stains nuclei). PAX7‐positive SCs were located on the inner side of the basal membrane in both Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice; however, contrary to our initial hypothesis, the number of SCs in regenerating muscle was significantly higher in DOX‐treated mice than in Ctrl mice, indicating that the proliferation of SCs was not suppressed by DOX administration (Figure 4A,B).

Bar, 100 μm. (B) Quantification of PAX7‐positive cells per mm2. N = 3 for each group. Unpaired t‐test. (C) Relative expression levels of SC‐related (Pax7, Myod, Myf5, and Myh3) and FAP‐related (Pdgfra, Col1a1, Col3a1, and Pparg) transcripts in muscle tissues collected from Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice on days 1, 3, and 7 post‐injury. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. N = 6–8 for each group and each time point.

To gain further insight into how DOX affects muscle regeneration, we analyzed the expression of key muscle‐regeneration‐related factors in muscle tissues collected on days 1, 3, and 7. In both Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice, transcriptional levels of Pax7, Myod, and Myf5, peaked around day 3 (Figure 4C). These transcripts returned to near‐baseline levels by day 7 in Ctrl mice, but remained persistently elevated in DOX‐treated mice. The expression of Myh3, which encodes embryonic myosin and is transiently induced after injury, was highly dysregulated in DOX‐treated mice, exhibiting significantly elevated expression on day 7 (p < 0.001; unpaired t‐test) In addition to SC‐specific transcripts, we analyzed the expression levels of transcripts that are associated with FAPs. In Ctrl mice, Pdgfra (a FAP cell‐surface marker) [26], Col1a1, Col3a1, and Pparg were transiently upregulated following injury and all returned to baseline levels by day 7. However, in DOX‐treated mice, the expression of these transcripts remained persistently elevated even on day 7. These findings suggest that activation of SCs and FAPs following injury was not compromised in DOX‐treated mice and that defective muscle regeneration was unlikely due to impaired SC proliferation or transcriptional activation.

Prolonged Infiltration of Immune Cells in DOX‐Treated Mice

Given that the proliferation and differentiation of SCs were not impaired by DOX administration, we investigated whether the inflammatory response following muscle injury was affected. To address this, we prepared single‐cell suspensions using muscle tissues collected from Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post‐injury and performed flow cytometric analysis (Figure 5A–D). In Ctrl mice, neutrophil (CD45+ Lys6G+) levels peaked on day 1, followed by a peak in macrophages (CD45+ F4/80+) on day 3, with both populations subsequently decreasing. In DOX‐treated mice, the increase in neutrophils on day 1 appeared to be reduced compared to Ctrl mice, but persisted through day 3, subsequently decreasing to basal levels by day 7. Similarly, the number of macrophages tended to be lower in DOX‐treated mice than in Ctrl mice on day 1. However, it increased on day 3 and remained elevated through days 5–7, consistent with our histological results showing robust immune cell infiltration even on day 7 (Figure 3A). To determine whether the reduced and delayed cell infiltration in muscle tissues following injury resulted from decreased circulating immune cells due to DOX administration, we analyzed peripheral blood samples collected on day 1 by flow cytometry. Contrary to this possibility, DOX‐treated mice had a higher proportion of neutrophils than Ctrl mice, while macrophage levels were comparable (Figure 5E).

To further investigate the dysregulated inflammatory response in DOX‐treated mice, we analyzed the transcription levels of several inflammatory markers. In both Ctrl and DOX‐treated mice, transcripts for F4/80 (encoded by Adgre1), a marker for macrophages, peaked on day 3 (Figure 6). However, while Adgre1 transcripts returned to baseline levels by day 7 in Ctrl mice, they remained elevated on day 7 in DOX‐treated mice, consistent with prolonged cell infiltration in DOX‐treated mice observed in histologic and flow cytometric analyses (Figures 3A and 5D). CCL2, also known as MIP‐1, is a chemokine involved in directing monocytes and T cells to the site of inflammation, and loss of this chemokine signaling has been shown to significantly impair muscle regeneration after injury [22, 27, 28]. However, we found that transcript expression for this chemokine was not reduced, but rather increased in DOX‐treated mice. In Ctrl mice, transcripts for CD86, a marker for M1 macrophages [29], elevated on days 1–3 and returned to baseline by day 7 (note that the difference between expression levels between day 3 and 7 did not reach statistical significance, most likely due to the relatively large variance on day 3). Transcripts for TNFα, a proinflammatory M1‐associated cytokine, showed a similar temporal expression pattern. In contrast, in DOX‐treated mice, CD86 transcript levels remained elevated through day 7, ultimately exceeding levels in Ctrl mice (p = 0.0011; unpaired t‐test). TNFα expression followed a similar delayed profile, peaking on day 3 instead of day 1 and exceeding Ctrl levels on days 3 and 7 (p = 0.0038 and p < 0.001, respectively; unpaired t‐test). Expression of transcripts for CD163, a marker of M2 macrophages, peaked on day 1 and declined to baseline by day 3 in Ctrl mice. However, in DOX‐treated mice, CD163 expression remained almost unchanged during the experiment. The temporal expression pattern of transcripts for IL‐10, an anti‐inflammatory cytokine associated with M2 macrophages, was similar in both groups. However, contrary to the nearly unchanged expression of CD163 transcripts in DOX‐treated mice, IL‐10 levels were significantly higher in DOX‐treated mice compared to Ctrl mice on day 3 (p < 0.001; unpaired t‐test).

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. N = 6–8 for each group and each time point. ns, not significant.

Taken together, these observations suggest that the inflammatory response following injury is dysregulated in DOX‐treated mice, and that defective clearance of necrotic myofibers, likely resulting from the aberrant inflammatory response, led to defects in muscle regeneration.

Discussion

In this study, we showed that systemic administration of DOX during the early phase of the inflammatory response significantly impairs the regenerative process. This impairment was most evident when DOX was administered at the time of injury, indicating a relatively narrow but critical window during which DOX interferes with muscle regeneration. Our data suggest that DOX impedes immune cell infiltration into injured muscle, thereby impairing the sequential regenerative processes, particularly the clearance of necrotic tissues and resolution of the inflammation. While there are studies reporting that DOX induces muscle atrophy [30, 31, 32, 33], to our knowledge, this study is the first to report the negative impact of DOX on muscle regeneration following injury.

One of the critical findings in this study is that the administration of DOX at the time of injury leads to muscle regeneration defects characterized by reduced muscle mass, smaller myofiber CAS, fibrosis, and calcified depositions (Figure 3). Importantly, the defects were not reversed even weeks after injury, suggesting that damage to the muscle caused by DOX is irreversible. Chronic administration of DOX has previously been shown to induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in skeletal muscle, leading to apoptosis of skeletal muscle cells, increased proteolysis, and ultimately muscle atrophy [30, 31, 32, 33]. A similar mechanism involving oxidative stress to cardiomyocytes is thought to be involved in DOX‐induced cardiotoxicity [34]. Based on these observations, we initially hypothesized that DOX would reduce the number of SCs through apoptosis. However, contrary to our assumption, the number of PAX7‐positive SCs was even higher in DOX‐treated mice compared to controls. In accordance, the transcript levels for myogenic transcription factors, including Pax7, Myod, and Myf5, were not reduced, but remained elevated at higher levels in DOX‐treated mice (Figure 4). These findings suggest that DOX does not inhibit SC activation or proliferation per se, but hampers their terminal differentiation and the formation of mature myofibers, possibly due to the persistent necrotic debris and the unresolved inflammation. The aberrant and prolonged expression of Myh3 transcripts, which are expressed in newly formed myofibers, may also support this hypothesis.

We also observed that FAP‐associated transcripts, including Pdgfra, Col1a1, Col3a1, and Pparg, remained elevated in DOX‐treated mice. FAPs typically expand transiently following muscle injury to support regeneration, peaking within 3–4 days and subsequently returning to baseline levels through apoptosis. While FAPs are essential for muscle regeneration, previous studies have shown that dysregulated expansion can result in fibrosis and fat deposition [12, 16, 17, 24]. Our findings suggest that DOX indirectly dysregulates FAP proliferation or suppresses apoptosis, leading to excessive collagen deposition and ultimately contributing to muscle fibrosis.

The altered immune response following DOX administration observed in this study was unexpected. Flow cytometric analysis revealed delayed infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages during the early post‐injury period, but prolonged presence of these cells thereafter (Figure 5). Histologic analysis confirmed persistent immune cell infiltration and delayed clearance of necrotic tissues (Figure 3). Importantly, these effects were not attributable to systemic immune suppression, as DOX‐treated mice exhibited normal or even elevated levels of circulating neutrophils. This suggests a specific defect in immune cell recruitment into injured muscle, possibly due to impaired chemotaxis or phagocytosis. Of note, the defective muscle regeneration observed in DOX‐treated mice closely resembles the mouse models with impaired immune cell infiltration or macrophage depletion. CXCL7 deficiency [35], which impairs neutrophil recruitment, and macrophage depletion via CCR2 or CCL2 knockout [22, 27, 28] or clodronate administration [36], all lead to delayed clearance of necrotic tissue and defective muscle regeneration. These observations support the notion that DOX impairs muscle regeneration following injury by dysregulating the early inflammatory response. Furthermore, it has recently been reported that 5‐fluorouracil, an antimetabolite, causes impaired muscle regeneration due to delayed infiltration of inflammatory cells. This finding is similar to what was observed in this study [37] and suggests that impaired muscle regeneration associated with inflammatory responses may occur with some clinically used chemotherapeutic agents.

The gene expression analysis of inflammatory markers provided further insight into the altered inflammatory response (Figure 6). Macrophage polarization from a pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype is considered critical for proper muscle regeneration, and disruption of this transition can result in fibrosis and fatty infiltration [38]. In DOX‐treated mice, we observed sustained expression of Adgre1 and Cd86 transcripts, indicative of prolonged pro‐inflammatory activity. In contrast, induction of the transcripts for Cd163, a marker of M2 macrophages associated with tissue repair and resolution of the inflammatory response, was essentially absent. These findings may suggest that the transition from M1 to M2 phenotype is disrupted in DOX‐treated mice, preventing proper resolution of the inflammatory response. Interestingly, transcripts for IL‐10, a cytokine typically associated with M2 macrophages, were significantly elevated in DOX‐treated mice. This paradoxical upregulation of IL‐10 transcription may reflect a compensatory response to unresolved inflammation and impaired regenerative progression.

The present study has several limitations to be noted. First, this study focused exclusively on the potential effects of DOX. To assess the broader clinical relevance of our findings, it will be important to assess whether other commonly used cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents affect muscle regeneration. In a similar vein, it is also important to investigate whether DOX impairs the wound healing and regeneration of other tissues, including skin and viscera, following injury. Second, while our data suggest that the administration of DOX at the time of injury leads to delayed and prolonged immune cell infiltration, the underlying mechanism remains to be clarified. Third, the present model has an inherent issue in standardizing the severity of muscle injury among individual animals. This variability may have limited the statistical robustness of some flow cytometric and gene expression analyses (Figures 4, 5, 6). In addition, it is not clear to what extent the present model replicates surgical injuries in humans. Last, although it is likely that a defective inflammatory response triggered the cascade of events that ultimately resulted in defective muscle regeneration in DOX‐treated mice, this hypothesis requires further validation. Nevertheless, given that the negative impact of DOX on muscle regeneration was observed only when it was administered during the early inflammatory response in our model (Figure 1B), and that DOX‐treated mice exhibited phenotypes resembling those of other mouse models with impaired immune function [21, 35, 36], it is reasonable to assume that the altered inflammatory response is, at least in part, responsible for the observed effects on muscle regeneration.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal that DOX significantly disrupts skeletal muscle regeneration when administered during the early phase of the inflammatory response. While the resulting defects appear irreversible, the window during which DOX interferes with regeneration is relatively brief. Notably, DOX had little effect when administered outside this window (after 3 days after muscle injury in our model). Conventionally, post‐operative chemotherapy is administered after wound healing is complete (2–3 weeks after surgery); however, our data may suggest that this time period could potentially be shortened without compromising muscle regeneration. Furthermore, our mouse model may serve as a valuable tool for investigating muscle regeneration following injury, particularly the role of the inflammatory response in triggering the regenerative process.
