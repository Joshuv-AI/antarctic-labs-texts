L-type calcium channel-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming in gastric adenocarcinoma progression
Authors: Miaomiao Cui, Shuqiang Cheng, Xianhui Wen, Hanlin Yang, Yun Huang, Li Pan, Sixi Wei, Hai Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CACNA1D and CACNA1F are specifically upregulated in GAC and associated with poor prognosis.•LTCCs inhibition suppresses gastric cancer malignancy through lipid metabolic reprogramming.•Exogenous palmitic acid partially reverses the anti-tumor effects of LTCCs antagonism, confirming lipid metabolism as a key downstream mechanism.

Introduction

GAC is one of the most common malignant tumors of the digestive system, accounting for >95 % of gastric malignant tumors, and ranking fifth among new cancer cases and deaths with an incidence rate of 4.9 % and a mortality rate of 6.8 % [1,2]. Despite significant progress in surgical techniques, chemotherapy regimens, and targeted therapies in recent years, most patients are in the progressive stage of gastric cancer when diagnosed due to atypical early symptoms, resulting in an overall prognosis that is still poor, with a 5-year survival rate of <30 % [3]. In addition, gastric cancer is highly heterogeneous, with significant differences in response to therapy across molecular subtypes, which further increases the challenge of clinical treatment [4]. Therefore, in-depth study of the molecular pathogenesis of GAC, identification of new therapeutic targets, and development of more effective therapeutic strategies are of great clinical significance for improving patient prognosis. For clarity, it is noted that in this study, GAC is synonymous with Stomach Adenocarcinoma (STAD), the designation used in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database.

Calcium ions (Ca2+), as ubiquitous second messengers, play a central regulatory role in cellular physiology and pathology. Precise regulation of intracellular calcium homeostasis is essential for the maintenance of normal cellular function, and its dysregulation is closely associated with the development of many diseases [5] In tumor cells, aberrant activation of calcium signaling pathways is involved in regulating malignant phenotypes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis resistance [6]. Voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) are key membrane proteins that regulate cellular calcium homeostasis, controlling the transmembrane transport of Ca2+ by responding to changes in membrane potential. Among them, l-type calcium channels (LTCCs) are characterized by long opening duration and are encoded by four genes: CACNA1S, CACNA1C, CACNA1D, and CACNA1F [7,8]. While traditionally associated with excitable cells, emerging evidence indicates that LTCCs are also expressed and functional in various cancer cells, influencing tumor progression through diverse mechanisms [9,10] However, the expression profile and functional significance of LTCCs in GAC, as well as their potential as therapeutic targets, remain poorly studied.

Metabolic reprogramming is one of the core characteristics of cancer, and in order to adapt to rapid proliferation and harsh microenvironment, tumor cells gain growth advantage by altering their metabolic patterns [11]. In particular, aberrant lipid metabolism is recognized as a key driver of GAC progression, supplying both biosynthetic precursors and signaling molecules [12,13]. Despite this, the upstream regulatory mechanisms orchestrating lipid metabolic reprogramming in GAC remain unclear. Some studies have shown that certain calcium channel modulators can influence lipid metabolism in non-cancerous environments [14,15], suggesting that calcium channels may be key upstream regulatory factors in lipid metabolism.

In summary, although the role of LTCCs has been reported in a variety of tumors, their expression pattern, biological function and therapeutic potential in GAC remain unclear. Moreover, the potential link between LTCCs activity and the dysregulated lipid metabolism characteristic of GAC remains unexplored. Based on this, the present study aimed to (1) assess the expression characteristics of LTCCs family members in GAC, and clarify their diagnostic and prognostic value; (2) evaluate the effects of LTCCs inhibitors on the malignant phenotypes of gastric cancer cells; (3) reveal the molecular mechanisms of LTCCs regulation of gastric cancer progression; (4) explore therapeutic strategies that jointly target calcium channels and lipid metabolism. This study will provide new insights into the role of calcium signaling in gastric cancer and lay a theoretical and experimental foundation for the development of new calcium channel-based therapeutic strategies for gastric cancer.

Methods

Data sources and pre-processing

The gene expression data used in this study were obtained from the TCGA PanCanAtlas project (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/pancanatlas), specifically using the batch effect corrected RNA-seq V2 gene expression data file EBPlusPlusAdjustPANCAN_IlluminaHiSeq_RNASeqV2.geneExp.tsv (https://api.gdc.cancer.gov/data/3586c0da-64d0–4b74-a449–5ff4d9136611).

To eliminate batch effects between different tumour types, the raw expression data were Z-Score normalised by tumour type by transforming the gene expression values into unitless Z-Score scores using the formula Z = (x - μ)/σ. Data points with absolute Z-Score values greater than 3 were defined as outliers and excluded. Only tumour types with a number of normal tissue samples of not fewer than 3 were included for subsequent analysis.

Calcium channel gene expression profile analysis

The expression patterns of three major types of calcium channel families in digestive system tumors were systematically analyzed: the LTCCs family (Cav1.x: CACNA1S, CACNA1C, CACNA1D, CACNA1F), the high-voltage-activated calcium channel family (Cav2.x: P/Q-type, N-type, R-type), and the T-type calcium channel family (Cav3.x). The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare the gene expression differences between tumour tissues and normal tissues, focusing on specific expression patterns in GAC.

Paired-sample validation analysis

To exclude the influence of individual differences, paired samples of GAC tissues and normal tissues adjacent to the cancer from the same patient were analysed. The Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test, a non-parametric statistical method, was used to compare the median gene expression differences between the paired samples to further validate the expression changes of CACNA1D and CACNA1F in GAC.

Gene expression and risk assessment of GAC development

Logistic regression analysis was performed using the R language glm function, in which the family parameter was selected binomial to specify the binomial distribution family. The association between CACNA1D and CACNA1F gene expression levels and tumour status (tumor group vs. normal group) was assessed by calculating the ratio (OR) and its 95 % confidence interval to quantify the changes in the risk of GAC with each one-unit increase in gene expression.

Diagnostic value assessment and prognostic analysis

To assess the potential value of CACNA1D and CACNA1F in the diagnosis of GAC, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used. Two datasets were used for the analyses, respectively: (1) the TCGA dataset, which contains GAC samples and normal control samples; and (2) the TCGA-GTEx combined dataset, which integrates tumor samples from TCGA and normal tissue samples from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project to expand the normal control sample size. The diagnostic efficacy of gene expression levels for GAC was evaluated by calculating the area under the curve (AUC), and the AUC value closer to 1 indicated a higher diagnostic value.

To comprehensively assess the prognostic value of CACNA1D and CACNA1F, this study analyzed their prognostic significance in GAC using the publicly available Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (http://kmplot.com/analysis/).

Cell culture and processing

The cells used in this study were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai). GES-1, HGC-27 and AGS cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Biological Industries, Israel) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% humidified carbon dioxide.

IC50 determination

Cells were added to 96-well plates at 3000 cells/100 μl. 12 h later, different concentrations of (+)-Bay-K-8644 were added for treatment. 72 h later, 10 μl of CCK8 reagent (MCE, Shanghai, China) was added to each well. The samples were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in a carbon dioxide incubator, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme marker (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA).

Cell viability assay

Cells were added to 96-well plates at 3000 cells/100 μl. 12 h later, the cells were treated with (+)-Bay-K-8644 at concentrations of 0 μM, 20 μM, and 40 μM. After incubation for 0 h, 24 h,

48 h and 72 h, respectively, 10 μl of CCK8 reagent was added to each well, and the absorbance of the samples at 450 nm was measured by using an enzyme marker after 2 h of incubation.

Clone formation experiment

Cells were inoculated in 6-well plates at 1000 cells/well. After incubation for 48 h, (+)-Bay-K-8644 was added at concentrations of 0 μM, 20 μM, and 40 μM, respectively, and the medium with the corresponding drug concentration was changed every 3 days. After a 12-day incubation period, the medium was discarded, washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, washed twice with PBS and photographed.

Scratch experiment

Take cells in logarithmic growth phase and inoculate them in six-well plates. The inoculum density was about 90%. Culture for 12 h to make the cells adherent to the wall. Use the tip of a 200 μl pipette to make a “+” scratch, remove the floating cells by PBS rinse, and carry out a 0 h photo. Six-well plates were added with 1% FBS complete medium containing (+)-BAY-K-8644 at concentrations of 0 μM, 20 μM, and 40 μM, and incubation was continued for 24 h and 48 h for photographs. The scratch area was statistically analysed by Image J software.

Trans-well migration assay

Cells in logarithmic growth phase (5 × 10^4) were taken and added into 200 μl serum-free medium and inoculated into the upper chamber. At the same time, 650 μl of medium with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. The upper and lower chambers of the medium contained corresponding 0 μM, 20 μM, and 40 μM of (+)-BAY-K-8644. After 48 h of incubation with HGC-27 and 24 h of incubation with AGS, the chambers were washed once using PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, photographed, and the number of migrated cells was statistically analysed using Image J software.

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR reaction

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method (Invitrogen, CA, USA), and RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Takara PrimeScriptTM kit (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). The reaction system was prepared using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). GAPDH was selected as the internal reference, and 2-△△CT was used to indicate the relative expression of the target gene. Metabolomics analysis

HGC-27 cells were treated with (+)-BAY-K-8644 at a concentration of 0 μM, 40 μM for 48 h. 1 × 10^7 cells were harvested for untargeted metabolomics analysis. Cells were gently washed twice with PBS. Add pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile/water solution (2:2:1, v/v), vortex to mix, sonicate at low temperature for 30 min, let stand at -20 °C for 10 min, centrifuge at 14000 g, 4 °C for 20 min, take the supernatant for vacuum drying, and for mass spectrometry analysis, add 100 μl acetonitrile/water solution (acetonitrile: water = 1:1, v/v) to re-dissolve, vortex, and centrifuge at 14000 g, 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred into the sample for analysis. The samples were separated using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC ultra - high performance liquid chromatography system, and subsequently analyzed by mass spectrometry on a Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX), with electrospray ionization in positive and negative ion modes separately. The raw data were transformed into the .mzXML format by ProteoWizard. Subsequently, XCMS software was employed for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. The data extracted by XCMS were initially subjected to structural identification and data pre - processing, followed by an evaluation of the experimental data quality, and finally, data analysis was carried out.

FASN content assay

Intracellular FASN content was detected by sandwich enzyme immunoassay using FASN ELISA kit (biorbyt, Cambridge, United Kingdom). Cells were treated with (+)-BAY-K-8644 at concentrations of 0 μM, 20 μM and 40 μM for 48 h. 1 × 10^7 cells were harvested and added to the lysis buffer. After sonication, the cells were centrifuged at 4 °C, 1500 g for 10 min and the supernatant was taken for assay. 100 μl of standards or samples were added to the corresponding microtiter plate and incubated for 80 min at 37 °C. The liquid in the plate was discarded, and 200 μl of washing buffer was added to each well and washed three times. Add 100 μl of biotin-labelled antibody working solution to each well and incubate for 50 min at 37 °C. Wash the plate 3 times, add 100 μl of streptavidin-HRP working solution to each well and incubate for 50 min at 37 °C. Wash the plate 5 times, add 90 μl of TMB substrate solution to each well and incubate for 20 min at 37 °C, protected from light. Add 50 μl of termination solution to each well, incubate for 20 min, and then measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

BODIPY staining

Cells were cultured on sterile glass coverslips and allowed to grow to 50%-70% density. Wash the cells twice with PBS, fix the cells with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Wash the cells twice with PBS, cover the cells with 1 μg/ml BODIPY staining solution, incubate the cells for 10 min at room temperature and under the condition of light avoidance. Rinse the cells twice with PBS, cover the cells with 1 μg/ml DAPI staining solution, incubate the cells for 5 min at room temperature and under the condition of light avoidance. Rinse the cells twice with PBS, and then observe the cells with a fluorescence microscope. The cells were incubated at room temperature and protected from light for 5 min, rinsed with PBS for 2 times, and photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

Oil red O staining

Incubate the cells on sterile glass coverslips and wait until the cells grow to 50 %−70 % density. Wash the cells twice with PBS, fix the cells with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Wash the cells twice with PBS, add the staining washing solution to cover the cells for 20 s and then remove it, add the oil red O staining working solution, and then remove it after 30 min of staining. Add appropriate amount of staining washing solution, remove it after 30 s of standing, and wash with PBS for 2 times. The nuclei were restained with hematoxylin for 1 min, washed with PBS for 2 times, and then photographed by microscopic observation.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between two groups were analysed using unpaired t-test, with P < 0.05 indicating statistically significant differences.

Results

Characterization of differential expression of LTCCs in GAC

Bioinformatics analysis based on the TCGA database revealed that members of the LTCCs family showed a unique expression pattern in GAC. Wilcoxon rank-sum test showed that while CACNA1D and CACNA1F were significantly down-regulated in most tumor types, it was up-regulated in GAC , and its expression level was significantly higher than that of normal gastric tissue (Fig. 1A). This expression profile contrasts with other calcium channel subtypes - P/Q, N and R-type (Cav2.x) (Fig. 1B), and T-type (Cav3.x) calcium channel families (Fig. 1C) that are generally downregulated in GAC. Pairwise analysis of GAC tissues and paired paracancerous normal tissues from the same patient further confirmed that CACNA1D and CACNA1F were indeed significantly up-regulated in cancerous tissues (Fig. 1D). This pairwise analysis excluded the interference of individual difference factors and enhanced the reliability of the findings. In GAC, the risk of developing GAC increased approximately 2.01-fold for each unit increase in CACNA1D expression , while the risk of developing the disease increased approximately 1.82-fold for each unit increase in CACNA1F expression (Fig. 1E). Notably, GAC and cholangiocarcinoma were the only two types of cancer that exhibited dual upregulation of both CACNA1D and CACNA1F.

Diagnostic value and prognostic significance of LTCCs in GAC

ROC curve analysis showed that both LTCCs isoforms had significant diagnostic value for GAC. CACNA1D demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.811 for the TCGA dataset and an AUC of 0.849 for the combined TCGA-GTEx dataset (Fig. 2A, left panel). CACNA1F also showed diagnostic potential with an AUC of 0.634 for the TCGA dataset and 0.6 for the TCGA-GTEx dataset (Fig. 2A, right panel). To evaluate the clinical significance of CACNA1D and CACNA1F upregulation, we used the Kaplan-Meier Plotter, an online survival analysis platform. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with elevated expression of CACNA1D and CACNA1F exhibited significantly poorer prognoses in terms of overall survival (OS), relapse-free survival (RFS), and post-progression survival (PPS). This association between CACNA1D and CACNA1F upregulation and poor clinical outcomes, combined with the overexpression of LTCCs in GAC, provided compelling rationale for exploring LTCCs antagonism as a therapeutic strategy.

The results demonstrated that CACNA1D expression was significantly elevated in both HGC-27 and AGS cells compared with GES-1 cells (Fig. 3A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed the significant effects of (+)-Bay-K-8644 treatment on cellular metabolism, especially lipid metabolism. Among the top 20 significantly enriched pathways, several pathways closely related to lipid metabolism were significantly enriched, including Choline metabolism in cancer, Sphingolipid signaling pathway, Glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (associated with phospholipid synthesis), among others. Notably, choline metabolism, a key pathway for phosphatidylcholine synthesis, plays an important role in cancer metabolic reprogramming, and its significant enrichment suggests that LTCCs may influence gastric cancer cell function by regulating membrane lipid composition (Fig. 6B).

Heatmap analysis demonstrated in detail the changing patterns of metabolites related to lipid metabolism. Choline and its metabolites were significantly altered after (+)-Bay-K-8644 treatment, and the altered levels of choline, an important precursor for phosphatidylcholine synthesis, directly affected cell membrane lipid composition. Metabolites related to energy metabolism and membrane stability, such as creatine and sarcosine, also showed significant changes. In addition, a variety of amino acid precursors related to sphingolipid metabolism, such as serine and threonine, were abnormally expressed in the treated group, suggesting that the sphingolipid metabolic pathway was significantly affected. In particular, changes in the levels of S-adenosylmethionine, an important methyl donor involved in phosphatidylcholine synthesis, further confirmed the reprogramming of lipid metabolism (Fig. 6C).

LTCCs inhibitor significantly inhibits lipid metabolism and lipid droplet formation in gastric cancer cells

In order to further verify the regulatory effect of LTCCs inhibition on lipid metabolism, we examined the changes in the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes in gastric cancer cells after treatment with (+)-Bay-K-8644. RT-qPCR results showed that the mRNA expression of several key genes for lipid metabolism was significantly down-regulated in HGC-27 cells treated with 40 μM (+)-Bay-K-8644 for 24 h.

Among them, the expression of fatty acid synthase (FASN), the rate-limiting enzyme for fatty acid synthesis, was most significantly decreased. In order to verify whether the gene expression changes translated into altered protein levels, we quantified the protein expression of FASN using ELISA. The results showed that (+)-Bay-K-8644 inhibited the protein expression of FASN in a concentration-dependent manner in both HGC-27 and AGS (Fig. 7B).

Altered lipid metabolism inevitably affects the formation of intracellular lipid droplets. We used the lipid droplet-specific fluorescent probe BODIPY staining to observe the effect of (+)-Bay-K-8644 on lipid droplet formation in gastric cancer cells. Confocal microscopy observation showed that a large number of green fluorescently labeled lipid droplets were visible in control HGC-27 and AGS cells, which were distributed in the cytoplasm in the form of dots or granules. After treatment with 20 μM (+)-Bay-K-8644, the number of lipid droplets and the fluorescence intensity in both cell lines were significantly reduced; the inhibition effect was even more significant in the 40 μM treatment group, and the presence of lipid droplets was almost undetectable in the cells (Fig. 7C).

To further confirm the above results, we used the classical oil red O staining method to detect neutral lipids. The results were consistent with BODIPY staining, and a large number of red-stained lipid droplets were visible in the cytoplasm of control cells, while the number of lipid droplets decreased in a concentration-dependent manner after (+)-Bay-K-8644 treatment. In the 40 μM-treated group, lipid droplets in HGC-27 and AGS cells almost completely disappeared, and only a very small number of scattered red particles were visible (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

This study presents two important findings regarding the role of LTCCs in GAC. First, it reveals that LTCCs (particularly CACNA1D) are upregulated in GAC and are associated with poor prognosis, challenging the conventional view that LTCCs function primarily in excitable cells. Second, it demonstrates that LTCCs inhibitors exert anti-tumor effects primarily through reprogramming lipid metabolism, thereby suggesting that the “LTCCs–lipid metabolism” axis as an important element in GAC progression.

We observed that the expressions of CACNA1D and CACNA1F were upregulated in GAC, which was consistent with the findings reported in the literature that CACNA1D was highly expressed in prostate cancer, breast cancer and esophageal cancer [10]. Among them, CACNA1D has better diagnostic and prognostic value, suggesting that it may play a more prominent role in the occurrence and development of GAC. However, the upstream driving factors for CACNA1D overexpression remain unclear. Based on previous studies, we propose the following potential mechanisms: (1) Transcription factors, such as CREB and MEF2 [17], may be abnormally activated in GAC, thereby leading to the upregulation of CACNA1D transcription [18,19]. (2) Histone modifications, such as H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, may promote chromatin remodeling and enhance the expression of the CACNA1D gene [20]. Clarifying the upstream regulatory mechanism of CACNA1D is crucial for developing its targeted therapeutic strategy against GAC, and it is also the core of our future research.

Multiple studies have confirmed that LTCCs can promote the occurrence and development of various tumors through different mechanisms of action. Chen et al. found that CACNA1D promotes tumor growth by enhancing androgen receptor transcriptional activity in prostate cancer [21]. Jaclyn et al. reported that LTCCs promote invasive metastasis of breast cancer cells by regulating filament pseudopod formation [22]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that CACNA1C promotes colorectal cancer stem cell properties by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [23]. Notably, a significant finding of this study is that calcium channel blockers primarily inhibit fatty acid synthesis, thereby blocking the malignant progression of GAC. This discovery opens a new direction for the mechanistic research of GAC. However, the specific molecular mechanism by which Ca2+ regulates the lipid metabolic reprogramming of gastric cancer cells remains to be further elucidated. Based on previous relevant research reports, the following several hypotheses are proposed. Firstly, the influx of Ca2+ will activate calcium-dependent protein kinases (CaMKs). Subsequently, CaMKs can phosphorylate mTORC1 complex, which is a key upstream regulatory factor for the expression and activity of FASN [24]. Secondly, the disruption of calcium homeostasis can trigger endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [25], which in turn interferes with lipid metabolism [26].

In the backfill assay, exogenous PA could reverse the anti-tumor effect of calcium channel blockers, and its recovery effect on the migration ability of gastric cancer cells was significantly stronger than that on the proliferation ability. This difference indicates that processes such as membrane dynamics and cytoskeleton remodeling, which are dependent on migration, are highly sensitive to lipid availability [27]; in contrast, proliferation may involve more complex biosynthetic networks or have stricter threshold requirements for lipid precursors [28].

In addition to the lipid metabolism pathway, the KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that after treatment with (+)-Bay-K-8644, the “ABC transporters” pathway also showed significant enrichment. The ABC transporter protein is a key factor that contributes to the occurrence of multidrug resistance in tumors. It can reduce the efficacy of chemotherapy drugs by expelling them from the cells [29,30]. This enrichment result suggests that calcium channel blockers may enhance the chemosensitivity of gastric cancer cells by inhibiting the function of ABC transporters and reducing the efflux of chemotherapeutic drugs.

In recent years, drug repositioning strategies have shown great potential in anticancer drug development, and the antitumor effects of calcium channel blockers have attracted widespread attention. Epidemiological studies have disputed the relationship between calcium channel blockers and cancer risk: early small-scale studies suggested a possible increase in cancer risk, but subsequent large-scale cohort studies and meta-analyses did not find a significant correlation [31]. Experimental studies, on the other hand, provide evidence for the antitumor activity of calcium channel blockers: verapamil inhibits hepatocellular carcinoma growth by inducing autophagy [32]; amlodipine inhibits angiogenesis by blocking VEGF signaling [33]; and nimodipine enhances glioma sensitivity to chemotherapy with temozolomide [34]. These studies suggest that calcium channel blockers may exert their anticancer effects through a variety of mechanisms, but their roles and mechanisms in gastric cancer have not yet been clarified. Our study contributes to this growing field by providing the first evidence that LTCCs antagonism exerts antitumor effects through lipid metabolic reprogramming. This mechanism not only expands the potential therapeutic applications of calcium channel blockers but also suggests that combining LTCCs inhibitors with lipid metabolism-targeted agents may offer synergistic benefits. However, it is important to note that different calcium channel blockers may act through different mechanisms, as in the case of lymphedema studies showing that calcium channel blockers use increases the risk of lymphedema in breast cancer patients [35], suggesting the need for careful evaluation when selecting a specific drug.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, we conducted metabolomics analysis only in the HGC-27 cell line. Whether the lipid metabolism changes exist in the same way in other gastric cancer cell lines still needs further verification. Secondly, the inhibitory effect of calcium channel blockers on GAC and their influence on lipid metabolism still lack validation through in vivo experiments. Finally, at the mechanism level, we did not elaborate on the specific molecular mechanism by which calcium ion channel blockers regulate FASN. In the future, we will reduce the expression of the CACNA1D gene in gastric cancer cells, conduct in vivo experiments to verify its impact on the malignant progression of GAC, and further explore its molecular mechanism in lipid metabolism regulation.

Conclusions

This study reveals that LTCCs members, particularly CACNA1D and CACNA1F, are up-regulated in GAC and correlate with poor prognosis. We have confirmed that (+)-Bay-K-8644 can inhibit the proliferation and migration of gastric cancer cells by reducing lipid synthesis, while exogenous PA partially reverses these antitumor effects. The results of this study confirm that the “LTCCs-lipid metabolism” axis is an important driving factor for the progression of GAC, providing a theoretical basis for the application of calcium channel blockers (alone or in combination with FASN inhibitors) in the treatment of GAC.
