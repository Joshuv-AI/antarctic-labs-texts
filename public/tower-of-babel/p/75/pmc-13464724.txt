Endoscopic reduction of true gastrogastric intussusception in a dog
Authors: Takumi Ogawana, Sho Fukui, Yuta Daimon, Hideyuki Kanemoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 9-year-old neutered male Toy Poodle was diagnosed with true gastrogastric intussusception based on clinical signs, imaging studies, and endoscopic findings. The dog presented with vomiting, hematochezia, and decreased activity. Abdominal ultrasonography identified edematous thickening of the gastric wall and invagination of the gastric fundus into the gastric body. The diagnosis was confirmed by computed tomography (CT). Endoscopic examination clearly identified the intussuscepted portion of the gastric fundus, which was successfully reduced without surgery using insufflation. Histopathologic analysis of gastrointestinal biopsy samples identified chronic gastritis, enteritis, and colitis. After the procedure, the dog remained free of recurrence through day 216. This case emphasizes the value of early ultrasonographic diagnosis and successful endoscopic reduction in the management of true gastrogastric intussusception.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal intussusception is defined as the telescoping of one segment of the gastrointestinal tract into an adjacent segment. Although most cases occur in the direction of normal peristalsis and commonly involve the ileocecal junction or jejunum, gastric intussusceptions are relatively rare and typically involve the esophagus or pylorus. Among these, so-called “true” gastrogastric intussusception, in which one portion of the stomach invaginates into another gastric region, is extremely rare in dogs. To date, only a few cases of true gastrogastric intussusception have been reported in dogs, including cases involving the pyloric antrum and gastric body or the fundus and body, most of which were managed surgically or resulted in euthanasia.1–5 Reported causes include foreign bodies and underlying pathologic conditions, but in some cases no definitive cause has been identified.1–4 In contrast, cases in humans are most commonly associated with gastric neoplasia.6,7 Rare non-neoplastic cases also have been reported in humans.6

Although surgical reduction traditionally has been the primary treatment modality for gastric intussusceptions, no established treatment protocols exist for true gastrogastric intussusception, and to our knowledge, no reports have described successful endoscopic reduction in dogs.

We report a case of true gastrogastric intussusception in a small breed dog, which was diagnosed early by abdominal ultrasonography, confirmed by computed tomography (CT) and endoscopy, and successfully managed by endoscopic reduction without surgery.

Case description

A 9-year-old neutered male Toy Poodle weighing 4.1 kg was referred to our hospital for evaluation of vomiting, diarrhea, hematochezia, and decreased activity. Twenty-five days before referral, the referring veterinarian suspected pancreatitis based on clinical signs and increased pancreatic lipase activity. Symptomatic treatment improved the vomiting and diarrhea, but hyporexia and lethargy persisted. Six days before presentation, the dog developed ataxia and right pelvic limb lameness, and firocoxib (Previcox®, Boehringer Ingelheim Animal Health Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was administered at 7.1 mg/kg PO q24h for 1 day followed by 3.5 mg/kg PO q24h for 4 days for suspected intervertebral disc disease, but without clinical improvement. Three days before referral, periorbital swelling developed, and prednisolone (0.6 mg PO as needed) was initiated immediately after discontinuation of firocoxib, with no washout period. Hematochezia developed after the administration of prednisolone, prompting referral.

Although ataxia and right pelvic limb lameness had been noted before referral, these clinical signs had improved before presentation, and no neurologic or orthopedic abnormalities were detected at presentation. Hematology identified mild thrombocytopenia (14.1 × 104/μL; reference interval [RI], 20.0-50.0), and serum biochemistry identified increased ALT activity (198 U/L; RI, 17-78), hypoalbuminemia (1.7 g/dL; RI, 2.6-4.0), hypocholesterolemia (85 mg/dL; RI, 115-337), and increased C-reactive protein concentration (CRP; 6.4 mg/dL; RI, 0-0.7). Thoracic and abdominal radiographs disclosed no clinically relevant abnormalities. Fecal flotation performed at the referring hospital before referral was negative. The dog also had been receiving routine antiparasitic prophylaxis including sarolaner, moxidectin, and pyrantel before presentation.

Abdominal ultrasonography (FUJIFILM LISENDO 880) identified focal edematous thickening of the gastric wall in the fundic region and concentric invagination of the gastric fundus into the body of the stomach. The wall layering was preserved within the invaginated segment (Figure 1A). Contrast-enhanced CT (FUJIFILM SCENARIA View, 64-slice) was performed with the dog in sternal recumbency using 120 kVp and 140 mAs, and images were acquired before and after intravenous [IV] administration of iopamidol (600 mg I/kg; Iopamidol 300; FUJIFILM). The CT confirmed intussusception of the fundus into the body of the stomach, with hypodense submucosal thickening and no signs of necrosis or perforation (Figure 1B). No ultrasonographic or CT findings consistent with pancreatitis were identified. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of true gastrogastric intussusception was made.

The wall layering is preserved within the intussuscepted segment. White arrows indicate the thickened wall of the fundus. Upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy (GIF-FUJIFILM ELUXEO V) was performed with minimal insufflation to confirm the intussusception. The gastric fundus was observed to have invaginated into the body, showing congestion and mild edema. Mucosal hemorrhage was noted at the site of intussusception, and patchy hemorrhagic areas were also present in the fundus. Upon further insufflation, the intussusception was spontaneously and easily reduced, restoring normal gastric anatomy (Figure 2A, B; Video 1). Biopsy samples were taken from the stomach, duodenum, terminal ileum, and colon. After completion of the endoscopic procedure, abdominal ultrasonography and non-contrast CT were performed. These confirmed anatomical resolution of the intussusception: the gastric fundus had returned to its normal position on ultrasonography (Figure 3A), and CT imaging showed no evidence of recurrence or structural abnormality, although mild thickening of the gastric wall persisted (Figure 3B). The clinical course after endoscopic reduction was uneventful. The dog resumed oral intake the next day and was discharged on day 4 post-procedure.

Histopathologic examination of the biopsy specimens identified mild lymphoplasmacytic infiltration and submucosal hemorrhage in the intussuscepted segment. Mild chronic inflammation also was observed in the non-invaginated gastric mucosa, as well as in the duodenum, ileum, and colon.

After discharge, dietary management was initiated using a hydrolyzed protein diet, along with administration of a proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole, 1.25 mg/kg PO q12h), an appetite stimulant (capromorelin, 3 mg/kg PO q24h), and a probiotic and digestive enzyme preparation (Bioim Buster®, containing lactic acid bacteria and pancreatin, 1 tablet PO q12h). These medications were gradually tapered over 34 days before discontinuation. No recurrence of gastrointestinal signs was observed, and the dog remained clinically normal through day 216 after the initial presentation.

Discussion

This case represents a rare report of true gastrogastric intussusception in a dog in which early diagnosis and non-surgical treatment were successful. In previous reports, gastric intussusception in dogs has been described most commonly in association with the pylorus or the esophagus, whereas true gastrogastric intussusception, in which one part of the stomach invaginates into another gastric region, is extremely rare.1–5 Recent reports have described gastrogastric intussusception in dogs associated with inflammatory gastric disease, suggesting that mucosal inflammation may contribute to the development of this condition.4 In the present case, chronic inflammation also was identified in the gastric and intestinal mucosa, which may have contributed to the development of intussusception. Inflammatory chronic enteropathy, including food-responsive enteropathy, was considered a possible underlying condition in this case, given the histopathologic findings of chronic inflammation and the favorable clinical response to dietary management. In this dog, abdominal ultrasonography clearly identified edematous thickening of the gastric wall and invagination of the gastric fundus into the body of the stomach, with preserved wall layering. The preserved layered structure indicated an early lesion without necrosis or perforation, which was a key factor in selecting a treatment approach. Subsequent contrast-enhanced CT showed thickening of the submucosa with a hypodense appearance and maintained enhancement of the mucosa, indicating preserved blood flow in the intussuscepted segment. The combination of ultrasonography and CT enabled non-invasive assessment of both structural abnormalities and perfusion dynamics, and provided a strong rationale for choosing endoscopic rather than surgical reduction. Similar diagnostic approaches have been described in human medicine, where CT is considered an important modality for confirming gastrointestinal intussusception and evaluating associated complications.6,7 In previously reported cases of true gastrogastric intussusception in dogs, diagnostic evaluation of tissue viability has been limited, because earlier reports relied primarily on ultrasonography or necropsy imaging.1,2,3 These observations emphasize the importance of early diagnosis and careful assessment of lesion viability. In the present case, preserved wall layering on ultrasonography and maintained mucosal enhancement on CT indicated viable tissue, supporting the decision to attempt endoscopic reduction. Therefore, contrast-enhanced CT may be particularly valuable for assessing the viability and reversibility of the intussuscepted segment when considering treatment options.

Surgical treatment conventionally has been the first-line approach for gastrointestinal intussusceptions in dogs, especially when necrosis or perforation is suspected. However, previous reports also have described non-surgical reductions using saline infusion or endoscopic insufflation when ischemic changes are absent.8,9 More recently, spontaneous resolution of true gastrogastric intussusception also has been documented, suggesting that this condition may not always require immediate surgical intervention in clinically stable cases.5 In the present case, no signs of necrosis or perforation were observed, and early diagnosis and prompt treatment enabled successful endoscopic reduction with minimal insufflation. The invagination was reversed without mechanical manipulation, which suggests the lesion was at an early stage and not yet anatomically fixed. These findings raise the possibility that some cases diagnosed as gastric ulcer or chronic gastritis potentially may represent unrecognized true gastrogastric intussusception. Although only a limited number of cases have been reported to date, most previously described cases in dogs have been managed surgically or resulted in euthanasia. To our knowledge, successful endoscopic reduction of true gastrogastric intussusception has not been previously reported in a dog. This case supports reconsideration of current treatment strategies and suggests that endoscopic reduction may be considered as an initial treatment option in carefully selected early-stage cases.

This dog had a history of vomiting and diarrhea 25 days before presentation and was suspected to have pancreatitis by the referring veterinarian based on clinical signs and increased pancreatic lipase activity. Vomiting was noted again after administration of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs 6 days before referral, and hematochezia developed after administration of prednisolone 3 days earlier. These events raise the possibility that vomiting itself and drug-induced gastric mucosal injury contributed to the development of intussusception. Endoscopic examination identified hemorrhagic lesions at the intussusception site, as well as patchy hemorrhagic changes throughout the gastric mucosa, particularly in the fundus, supporting this hypothesis. After the procedure, the dog was treated with a proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole), a probiotic, and dietary management.10,11 After tapering and discontinuation of medications, no recurrence of clinical signs was observed, and the dog maintained long-term remission with diet alone.

In conclusion, this case describes successful endoscopic reduction of true gastrogastric intussusception in a dog, in which early diagnostic imaging and assessment of blood flow contributed substantially to the decision for non-surgical treatment.

In early-stage lesions without evidence of necrosis or perforation, endoscopic reduction is a minimally invasive and potentially effective treatment option.

Because gastric mucosal injury and recurrent vomiting may have contributed to the development of intussusception in this case, management of underlying conditions is considered important for prevention of recurrence.

In future cases, evaluation of lesion reversibility based on imaging findings may serve as an important indicator when considering endoscopic reduction.
