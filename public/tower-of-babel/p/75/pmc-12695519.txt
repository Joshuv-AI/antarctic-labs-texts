Low Alanine Aminotransferase Levels in Alcohol Consuming Male Subjects, Carriers of the Common ALDH2 Deficient Variant, ALDH2*2
Authors: Takuya Seike, Che‐Hong Chen, Eishiro Mizukoshi, Daria Mochly‐Rosen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlanine aminotransferase (ALT) is a commonly used indicator of hepatocellular injury in clinical liver disease practice. While a variety of factors influence serum ALT levels, we focus here on the effect of alcohol consumption on ALT levels in the context of the common East Asian aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) dysfunctional polymorphism, ALDH2*2 (or rs671 E504K missense variant). ALDH2 plays a key role in the detoxification of alcohol‐derived metabolite, acetaldehyde. Four of the five studies identified from a literature search showed that individuals with the ALDH2*1/*2 heterozygous genotypes had ALT levels below the normal range if they consume alcoholic beverages despite having only 10%–38% of the normal ALDH2 activity. This effect is consistent and more pronounced in men in studies from different East Asian populations. It is noteworthy that one large study showed that low ALT levels of ALDH2*1/*2 were also observed with excessive alcohol consumption, which is a common risk factor for alcohol‐related liver disease (42 g ethanol per day in men). It is unclear if these lower‐than‐normal ALT levels indicate a hepatoprotective effect or if it is a missed clinical indicator of hepatic injury. As ALT levels in the general practice are used to diagnose potential liver disease, it is therefore necessary to determine whether alcohol‐induced decline in ALT may mask the diagnosis of liver disease in an estimated 540 million carriers of the ALDH2*1/*2 genotypes in the East Asian ancestry group in clinical practice.

Introduction

The level of the serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is a well‐established indicator of hepatocellular injury [1]. ALT is used widely not only in routine health screening, but also in various aspects of liver disease management, including liver disease surveillance [1], criteria for initiation of viral hepatitis treatment, and evaluation of treatment efficacy [2, 3]. However, it should be noted that ALT levels are influenced by genetic factors [4, 5, 6] in addition to sex, age, body mass index (BMI) [4, 7], and analytical methods, such as the inclusion or absence of vitamin B6 (Figure 1A) [8, 9].

Factors affecting ALT levels and general information about ALDH2. (A) Factors affecting ALT levels are shown. (B) Gene sequences of ALDH2 variants and differences in activity are shown.

In this review, we focused on the contribution of aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) genotype as a factor that may influence ALT levels. ALDH2, an enzyme present in the mitochondrial matrix, is the rate limiting step in ethanol metabolism [10]. An estimate 540 million East Asians, or nearly 8% of the world population, carry a dominant negative Glu504Lys missense mutation in the ALDH2 gene, most of which are of East Asian ancestry (ALDH2*2 or E504K missense variant, SNP ID: rs671, G > A) [11]. The ALDH2 polymorphism has designated ALDH2*1 for the wild‐type Glu504 or G allele and ALDH2*2 for the Lys504, or A variant allele. The genotypes for wild‐type homozygous ALDH2*1/*1 (GG), heterozygous ALDH2*1/*2 (GA), and variant homozygous ALDH2*2/*2 carriers (AA), have ALDH2 enzyme activity of 100%, 10%–38%, and 1%–5%, respectively (Figure 1B) [12, 13].

ALDH2 was not included in the construction of polygenic risk scores for Alcohol‐associated cirrhosis [14] nor for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [15] risk prediction for non‐East Asian, and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [16]. On the other hand, it has been demonstrated that ALDH2 was associated with the number of days of alcohol consumption [17, 18], increased risk of alcohol‐related cancers [19, 20] and overall alcohol‐related mortality [19], non‐alcohol‐related cancers [19, 21], and gout [22] for mainly East Asian. Furthermore, the less active ALDH2 variant (rs671) has been shown to be significantly associated with ALT levels [5]. When lifestyle factors are combined with the ALDH2 variant, the influence of ALDH2 genetic status on ALT may be more apparent. Many reviews on ALDH2 have indicated that alcohol consumption is a strong and common lifestyle risk factor for liver disease [23, 24, 25]. Since serum ALT level has been widely used as an indicator of liver pathology, the interaction between alcohol consumption and ALDH2 genotype on ALT levels and liver disease should be examined; Especially in the case of MASLD which includes fatty liver disease that is associated with social drinking (30 g/day for men, 20 g/day for women) [26], the effect of alcohol consumption on ALT with ALDH2*2 genotypes may influence liver disease screening.

The ALDH2*2 genotype is extremely common in the East Asian ancestry group, ranging from 30% to 50% in the different regions of East Asia [27]. East Asia also has the rapidest rise of alcohol consumption in the world with an increase of alcohol drinkers from 48% in 1990 to 67% in 2017 [28]. The proportion of alcohol‐dependence (heavy alcohol drinkers) carrying with the deficiency ALDH2*2 has also risen significantly from 2.5% in 1979 [29] to 15.4% in 2006–2010 [30] in Japan. Therefore, the amount of daily alcohol consumption in people with different ALDH2 genotypes [19, 31] has raised an important clinical question on the relationship between serum ALT level and state of hepatic function.

Using published studies, we analyzed the correlation between serum ALT levels, ALDH2 genotype and alcohol consumption from data of East Asian populations. We also summarized studies of alcohol administration and its effect on serum ALT levels in different ALDH2 transgenic animal models and discussed possible molecular mechanisms that may influence the interaction of alcohol, ALDH2 genotype and serum ALT level.

The Effect of Alcoholic Beverages Consumption on Serum ALT Levels in Subject Carriers of the Dysfunctional ALDH2*2 E504K Genetic Variant

A search for articles was conducted on Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science using the search terms “Liver and ALDH2”. 1887 articles were identified, 977 articles were screened, and 11 articles were picked up for analysis. Literature published by January 17, 2025 was included in the search. (Supporting Information S1 and Figure S1). The articles analyzed in this study may be subject to bias related to confounding factors (age, BMI, sex, exclusion of liver disease including viral hepatitis, alcohol consumption, medication history, and nutritional status). However, the studies summarized in Tables 1 and 2 were conducted on healthy individuals, and the possibility of confounding factors such as unadjusted medication history and nutritional status is considered to be low (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). First, we summarized the data from three articles for which changes in ALT were observed but mean or median serum ALT levels were not provided (Table 1) [32, 33, 34]. In a study which included regular health‐check data from 264 Japanese males, serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT) levels increased significantly in subjects who drank more than 20 g (1.4 drinks) of alcohol per day than non‐drinkers regardless of ALDH2 genotype. However, the ALT level showed no significant difference [32]. In this study, ALDH2 enzyme deficiency genotype was classified based on the ethanol patch test (not by DNA genotyping) [32]. A second study of 1741 Japanese subjects showed that in a population adjusted for alcohol consumption, the ALDH2*1/*2 drinkers had a significantly lower serum ALT level as compared with the ALDH2*1/*1 drinkers (regression analysis showed β = −3.14, ALDH2*1/*2 vs. ALDH2*1/*1) [33]. Similarly, a third study using a large cohort of 7856 Japanese subjects found genotype‐specific and significant ALT reductions in ALDH2*1/*2 carriers consuming an average of 14–28 g (1–2 drinks) or more of alcohol per day compared to ALDH2*1/*2 carriers who consumed 1.4 g (0.1 drink) or less of ethanol per day [34]. Surprisingly, ALT levels (below 20 IU/L) in ALDH2*1/*2 heavy drinkers who consumed 42 g (3 drinks) alcohol per day or more were even lower than those in ALDH2*1/*2 who consumed less than 1.4 g (0.1 drink) of alcohol per day (The difference in ALT is about 5 IU/L) [34]. In this large study, ALT levels did not change in ALDH2*1/*1 drinkers who drank 1.4–42 g (0.1–3 drinks) alcohol per day compared to those drinking less than 1.4 g (0.1 drink) alcohol per day. However, ALT level was significantly elevated in the group of subjects who drank more than 42 g (3 drinks) alcohol per day [34]. We also noted that the low ALT levels in ALDH2*1/*2 drinkers were more pronounced in males than in females [33, 34].

We also summarized two additional studies where mean or median serum ALT values were clearly reported (Table 2) [35, 36]. In one study, ALT levels were significant lower in ALDH2*1/*2 (17.8 ± 8.8 IU/L) group than the ALDH2*1/*1 (29.8 ± 12.7 IU/L) group (mean ± SD) with alcohol intake greater than 40 mL per day (32 g ethanol, 2.3 drink) [35]. In the other study, ALT level was also significantly lower in the ALDH2*1/*2 (17.1 ± 1.0 IU/L) group than in ALDH2*1/*1 (26.5 ± 1.5 IU/L) group with alcohol consumption greater than 31.5 mL per day (25 g ethanol, 1.8 drink) were statistically significant (mean ± SE) [36]. Importantly, there was no significant difference in alcohol intake between ALDH2*1/*1 (active variant) and ALDH2*1/*2 (less active variant) in both studies [35, 36]. These studies consistently showed that heavy alcohol consumption in individuals with ALDH2 enzyme deficiency presented noticeable decreased ALT levels relative to individuals with normal ALDH2 activity.

Effect of Alcohol Consumption on Serum ALT Levels in Alcohol‐Related Diseases of Subjects Carrying Different ALDH2 Genotypes

Next, we analyzed six studies on the effect of the ALDH2 genotype on alcohol‐related diseases in which data of ALT levels were available (Supporting Information S1 and Figure S1) (Table 3) [37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. In an analysis of 32 Japanese alcohol‐dependent patients [37], 147 Taiwanese with alcohol‐dependence [42], 281 Chinese patients with alcoholic cirrhosis [40], and 80 Taiwanese trauma center patients with high blood alcohol concentration (≥ 30 mg/dL) [39], there was a trend of lower ALT levels in ALDH2*1/*2 patients than in ALDH2*1/*1 patient although the difference was not statistically significant. In another very small study of Japanese patients with alcoholic liver disease, ALDH2*1/*1 (n = 12) and ALDH2*1/*2 (n = 4) had ALT levels of 84 ± 38 IU/L and 40 ± 8 IU/L, respectively, showing a significant lower value in the ALDH2*1/*2 group (mean ± SD). It should be noted, that the ALDH2*1/*2 patients in that study had lower amount of alcohol consumption compared to the ALDH2*1/*1 patients [38]. Finally, in a study of alcoholic cirrhotic patients in the Hakka population of southern China, ALDH2*1/*1 (n = 254) and ALDH2*1/*2 (n = 36) subjects showed no significant difference in ALT levels; (61.5 ± 76.1 IU/L vs. 68.36 ± 93.8 IU/L); [41]. In summary, although the levels of ALT were not significantly lower, all the studies, except for one, showed the same trend: lower ALT levels in subjects who carry ALDH2*2 compared to those who carry ALDH2*1 genotype.

ALT Levels and Possible Molecular Mechanism of ALT Regulation in Transgenic Animal Models With Reduced or Lack of ALDH2 Activity

In animal models with available serum ALT data, the findings related to the effect of ethanol exposure and ALDH2 genotype on ALT levels were inconsistent; some studies report an increase in ALT level [43, 44, 45, 46] whereas others report a decrease, or no change in ALT [47, 48, 49, 50, 51] after ethanol treatment. These conflicting reports in mice and rats may be due to the difference in ethanol metabolism by species [52], the amount of ethanol administered, the duration of administration, and the route of ethanol administration (Table 4).

ALT increase may be related to ethanol‐induced impairment of the intestinal epithelial tight junction and barrier dysfunction in ALDH2−/− mice [45]. The absence of ALDH2 activity in ALDH2 knockout mice (ALDH2−/−) has been shown to increase the sensitivity of intestinal cells to oxidative and nitric acid stress induced by ethanol administration, leading to apoptosis of intestinal cells and nitration of the gut adherent junction and tight junction proteins. This impaired intestinal barrier function leads to elevated serum endotoxin and bacterial migration, thus causing liver injury [46] (Figure 2). This mechanism may be related to alcoholic liver disease involving increased intestinal permeability and may be different from a damaging effect induced by alcohol directly on the liver [53].

On the other hand, there are several possible mechanisms which may explain the observed decrease of serum ALT levels, or lack of ethanol‐induced elevation of serum ALT with less or no ALDH2 activity. The absence of ALDH2 activity results in the accumulation of ethanol‐derived acetaldehyde in the body, which may have acute effect on either impair T‐cell glucose metabolism or result in elevated corticosteroid hormones, and then reduce the cytokine‐producing capacity of T‐cells [51]. In addition, ethanol consumption in mice with absent ALDH2 activity could lead to interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) production by activation of Kupffer cells as a result of malondialdehyde‐acetaldehyde accumulation in the liver. IL‐6 may prevent hepatocyte damage by upregulating the expression of antioxidant stress genes [49]. Therefore, ethanol administration to animals with reduced ALDH2 activity may result in decreased oxidative stress and suppression of tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) production [47] (Figure 2), resulting in hepato‐protective effect. However, it is unclear if low ALT levels may have a masking effect on liver disease in these ALDH2 models.

Mice With Reduced ALDH2 Activity Appear to Be More Susceptible to Alcohol‐Induced Liver Fibrosis and Carcinogenesis Relative to Wild Type (WT) Mice

In WT mice, ALT levels were significantly increased after ethanal administration [49, 50]. However, in mice with reduced or no ALDH2 activity (ALDH2−/− mice [49, 50] or ALDH2*1/*2 knocked‐in mice [50]) that were treated with ethanol, an increased susceptibility to liver fibrosis and HCC was observed despite that their ALT levels were only slightly increased [49, 50]. Importantly, liver inflammation and fibrosis resulting from the toxicity of aldehyde from alcohol consumption developed independently of hepatocellular injury [49]. Mitochondria dysfunction and the oxidized mitochondrial DNA damage incurred by alcohol‐derived acetaldehyde produced in mice may synergistically lead to carcinogenesis by activating multiple oncogenic pathways (JNK, STAT3, BCL‐2, and TAZ) [50]. Thus, ALDH2*2 deficiency does not appear to be hepato‐protective, especially in the context of ethanol exposure.

Discussion

Serum ALT level in humans is a well‐established marker to screen for liver disease in general practice. It is therefore important to determine the impact of alcohol consumption and common ALDH2 variant genotypes, two known contributors to liver disease, on this diagnostic marker. This is particularly important, since reduced activity of the detoxifying enzyme, ALDH2, and a key enzyme in ethanol metabolism is associated with liver disease and liver cancer [23, 24, 25, 50, 54].

Human data showed that carriers of the less active ALDH2 variant, ALDH2*1/*2 who consume alcoholic beverages have a higher concentration and longer exposure time to elevated acetaldehyde in the body relative to carriers of the active variant ALDH2*1/*1 [55, 56]. The risk of HCC for subjects with ALDH2*1/*2 genotype relative to ALDH2*1/*1 among drinkers who consume less than 60 g ethanol per day was increased based on large outpatient data [57]. Furthermore, heavy alcohol consumption (> 40 g ethanol/day in men, > 20 g ethanol/day in women for more than 5 years [50]; > 80 g ethanol/day [58]) in ALDH2*1/*2 subjects with hepatitis B virus‐related cirrhosis and have significantly increased risk of HCC and mortality compared to heavy drinkers with ALDH2*1/*1 and with hepatitis B virus‐related cirrhosis. Because acetaldehyde, a Group I carcinogen, reaches a much higher level in carriers of the inactive ALDH2 variant, and because of the increased risk of liver disease in these subjects, the levels of serum ALT as a marker of hepatocellular injury is expected be high as well. However, in contrast to the above expectation, several studies consistently showed that subjects with low ALDH2 activity together who consume alcohol exhibit significantly “lower” ALT levels relative to the general population (Tables 1 and 2).

A population‐based study reported a significantly lower ALT levels (< 20 IU/L) even in the ALDH2*1/*2 genotype groups even with high alcohol consumption (> 42 g ethanol/day) [34]. Importantly, an ALT of < 20 is a value considered as completely normal liver histology [59]. Yet, it is well known that alcohol intake exceeding 42 g per day of ethanol is a level of serious harmful alcohol consumption, and a proven risk factor for liver damage and alcohol‐associated liver disease [60, 61]. This raises the concern if low ALT levels in ALDH 2*1/*2, less active ALDH2, variant subjects is a true reflection of a normal liver in individuals who consume a high level of ethanol.

Many studies recommend an upper reference limit of 29–33 IU/L for ALT levels in men [59, 62]. However, a study of Korean male subjects reported a relative risk of 3.4 (2.8–4.3) for death from HCC and a relative risk of 2.0 (1.4–2.8) for death from non‐neoplastic liver disease in a lower ALT range of 20–29 IU/L compared with ALT levels below 20 IU/L was reported [63]. Since known liver diseases were excluded from that study at the time of enrollment, the possibility that unrecognized liver diseases may have existed [63]. The reason for the overlooked liver disease may be explained by the prevalence of ALDH2*2 carriers (30.4%–33.5% in male and 26.7%–29.9% in female [64, 65]) and alcohol consumption (41.1%, current drinkers represent [66] and 9.2% moderate‐to‐heavy drinks consuming > 20 g ethanol/day [64]) in Korea; If these patients were ALDH2*2 drinkers, the lower ALT levels due to these two factors may have masked their chronic liver disease.

The mechanisms of low ALT activity in individuals who carry ALDH2*2 and consuming alcoholic beverages is currently unknown. Could reduced levels indicate a decrease in protein content, a decrease in ALT blood half‐life, or a decrease in enzyme activity? We did not find reports examining the first two possibilities. However, it was reported that pseudo low ALT levels may be caused by low vitamin B6 (Figure 3A–E, supplementary materials). The activation of ALT requires pyridoxal‐5′‐phosphate (P5P), an activation form of vitamin B6 as cofactor (Figure 3A). ALT released into the blood is composed of a fully active form saturated with P5P (holoenzyme) and an inactive form without P5P (apoenzyme) (Figure 3B). In clinical diagnosis using automated chemical analyzers and spectrophotometry, the assay quantifies only the catalytic activity of the P5P‐bound ALT (holoenzyme) [67] and therefore may reflect a lower level of P5P. Inactive ALT to which P5P is not bound may leak into the blood from damaged liver cells, and lead to an underestimation of ALT activity by the clinical assay (Figure 3C,D). Since alcohol use reduces vitamin B6 availability [68, 69], in the context of ALDH2 enzyme deficiency and alcohol consumption, the possibility of increased inactive ALT to active ALT released into the blood as a cause of observed decrease in total ALT activity in the serum should be considered (Figure 3E).

This hypothesis also involves the long‐debated issue of whether the addition of P5P should be required for the assay of transaminase. It should be understood that, in contrast to the recommendations of the International Union of Clinical Sciences, many laboratories use reagents that do not contain P5P for commercial reasons [70]. The addition of P5P has been reported to increase ALT levels by 12% in those with low vitamin B6 in published results [71]. Therefore, it is important to consider adding P5P in samples of ALDH2*2 carriers who consume ethanol and have low vitamin B6, and to analyze whether the addition of vitamin B6 will influence the ALT levels. This is a question that requires more research and should be clarified in the future. If vitamin B6 was not involved, quantification of alcohol consumption by blood markers may be considered for the adjustment for ALT level. Biomarkers for estimating alcohol consumption are available include phosphatidylethanol (PEth) [72, 73], carbohydrate‐deficient transferrin (CDT) [74, 75], ethyl glucuronide (EtG), and ethyl sulfate (EtS) [76]. PEth and CDT are detected in blood for 2–4 weeks [72, 77] after ethanol exposure, EtG and Ets are detected in blood for about 1 day, and in urine for about 3–5 days after the last drink [78, 79]. Since ALT has a half‐life of about 48 h [80], combining these sensitive alcohol consumption biomarkers with different half‐lives will enable more accurate quantification of alcohol exposure and correction of ALT values.

In the context of alcohol consumption, the involvement of alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) which is responsible for the degradation of ethanol to acetaldehyde must also be a factor to be considered. Among people of East Asian descents, in particular, a ADH1B*2 (rs1229984) allele which encodes a highly active ADH enzyme is reported to be carried by over 80% of the populations [81]. However, a combination of low activity of ADH1B*1 together with a normal activity ALDH2*1 genotype confers a higher rate of alcoholic liver disease [82]. In a study based on trauma center patients with elevated blood alcohol concentration (BAC) of > 30 mg/dL, the group with the lowest acetaldehyde accumulating genotype (ADH1B*1, low activity + ALDH2*1, normal activity; Group A) had ALT of 40 ± 69 IU/L and malondialdehyde (MDA) of 7.3 ± 8.5 μmol/L, as compared with the group with the highest acetaldehyde accumulating genotype (ADH1B*2, high activity + ALDH2*2, low activity; Group B) with much reduced ALT level at 21 ± 24 IU/L and MDA at 19.6 ± 25.5 μmol/L [39] (albeit no significant difference in ALT values, but a significant difference in MDA values between the groups) (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). On the other hand, in a group of 66 Taiwanese non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) subjects, a significantly higher ALT level was reported in subjects with the Group B genotype compared to Group A (β = 0.273, p = 0.04) [83]. However, both studies were conducted in a small number of subjects, and further large‐scale studies are needed.

More recent studies have indicated that there is no amount of alcohol consumption that is safe. Many studies showed that even light and moderate alcohol consumption increases the risk of stroke [84], cancer [85], and early death [86]. Both WHO and U.S. Dietary Guidelines also stated that non‐drinkers should not start drinking alcohol for any reason [87]. Since alcohol consumption can elicit a variety of responses in the human body, it would be premature and misleading to conclude that the observed decreased ALT associated with alcohol consumption is “hepatoprotective”. Further studies are needed to determine the mechanism of reduced serum ALT and whether alcohol‐induced changes in serum ALT levels in ALDH2*2 carriers represent a true hepatoprotective state, or a misleading indication of normal hepatic function and if that would affect the diagnosis of alcoholic liver disease and long‐term prognosis.

The interpretation of ALT levels in the context of alcohol‐associated liver disease is complex. This is also because that alcoholic liver disease is manifested in diverse pathologies with different phases including steatosis, steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC [88, 89, 90], and ALT levels may vary depending on the progression of the disease [91]. It was also reported that many patients with alcoholic cirrhosis have normal ALT levels [92]. However, since hepatologists regularly evaluate the pathology of liver disease based on various blood parameters and imaging tests, ALDH2 variants may have little impact on determining disease progression and/or the benefits of therapeutic interventions. On the other hand, the screening or evaluation of liver disease in medical checkups or general practice must rely solely on ALT. Since ALDH2*2 drinkers may have lower ALT levels as our review indicates, we suggest that ALDH2 genotype should also be considered when screening for chronic liver disease using ALT values, especially in those who consume alcohol. We therefore would like to suggest a new screening approach for chronic liver disease in consideration of habitual alcohol consumption, ALDH2 genotype and ALT level as summarized in Figure 4. A large study of healthy Japanese males has shown that ALT is on average 5 U/L lower in ALDH2*1/*2 subjects who consume alcohol as compared to non‐drinkers [34]. Therefore, positive diagnosis of individuals with a history of drinking and ALDH2*2 genotypes who exhibit up to 5 U/L lower ALT values from cut off values probably should be tested carefully to minimize under‐diagnosis of patients due to underestimation of ALT. ALDH2 genotypes can be accurately and easily determined by different available methods. In the era of precision medicine, such single SNP genotyping is rather affordable and perhaps should be considered by health policy makers. Instead of direct genotyping, skin alcohol patch test which relies on visual inspection of erythema reaction may also be considered as an alternative (sensitivity and specificity are 0%–100% and 69.6%–100%, respectively) [93, 94].

Hypothetical screening approach for chronic liver disease. We assumed a cut off value of 30 U/L for screening for chronic liver disease. In ALDH2*2 drinkers, ALT is reduced by approximately 5 U/L compared to non‐drinkers. Therefore, patients with ALT of 25–30 U/L should be asked if they have been drinking. Consider evaluation for ALDH2 polymorphism only in drinkers; if ALDH2*2, a clinic visit is recommended.

Our review has several limitations: (1) some of the analyzed studies did not adequately control for potential confounding factors such as age, gender, BMI, concomitant liver disease, and drug use that may affect ALT levels (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). (2) Due to the small number of articles and cases included in the analysis, a meta‐analysis could not be performed. (3) A direct causal relationship between ALDH2 variants, alcohol consumption, and ALT decrease cannot be established due to the lack of clear mechanistic data. (4) We were unable to investigate the role of ALDH2 on ALT in patients with cirrhosis, whose ALT levels were often within the normal range. (5) Human and rodent data should be considered separately because of metabolic differences between human and rodent [95].

Conclusions

Low serum ALT levels in subjects with the ALDH2*2 deficiency genotypes who consume alcohol have been consistently observed in many clinical reports. It raises the possibility that relying on ALT index for hepatocellular injury evaluation may not be completely accurate the group of ALDH2*2 carriers. Clarifying the mechanism of this unexpected observation will provide new insights into how serum ALT levels should be better utilized in clinical practice as a diagnostic indicator for liver disease. Such knowledge and practice may be particularly important for the care of 540 million ALDH2*2 carriers of the East Asian genetic ancestry group in the era of precision healthcare.
