Pregnancy rates among users of combined oral contraceptives in Aotearoa New Zealand in 2019: a nationwide retrospective cohort study
Authors: Caryn Thomas, Nicholas Bowden, Rhiannon Braund, Jesse Kokaua, Matthew Hobbs, Helen Paterson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy rates amongst users of combined oral contraceptives (COCs) in Aotearoa New Zealand (Aotearoa/NZ) are unknown. Filling this gap aims to help guide future research and initiatives for achieving equitable contraceptive services in Aotearoa/NZ.ObjectiveTo estimate the rate of pregnancy among users of COCs in Aotearoa/NZ in 2019, and explore correlates of pregnancy rate across sociodemographic and geographic factors.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used whole-of-population data from the Integrated Data Infrastructure, a research database containing deidentified routinely collected government data. Users of COCs were identified using pharmaceutical dispensing data, and pregnancy events were identified in maternity and hospital discharge datasets. Pregnancy events were included if they were estimated to have been conceived during a period of COC use. Observed pregnancy rates were calculated for the entire study population, new and established users, and sociodemographic and geographic subgroups. Rates were analysed using Poisson regression models to produce comparative risk ratios and 95% CIs that were adjusted for covariates.ResultsA pregnancy rate of 3.50 per 100 person-years of COC use was identified from a cohort of 208 197 individuals using COCs in Aotearoa/NZ in 2019. There was significant variation in pregnancy rates by several key sociodemographic and geographic factors including by whether a user was new/restarting or established (reference) on COCs (adjusted risk ratio 1.28, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.37), or lived in a rural or urban (reference) area (adjusted risk ratio 1.32, 95% CI 1.21 to 1.44). There were also significant differences in pregnancy rate by age group, ethnicity, parity, highest qualification, household income, and neighbourhood deprivation.ConclusionsThe estimated pregnancy rate among users of COCs in Aotearoa/NZ is comparable to international findings. Sociodemographic and geographic variation in pregnancy rates may be explained by inconsistent pill use, access barriers, contraceptive failure, and early discontinuation of COCs with intended pregnancy conception. An opportunity remains for further research to fully understand the drivers of such variation. Nevertheless, these findings will inform family planning services, clinical practice, and public health policy. Furthermore, they have implications for whether healthcare services are providing equitable contraceptive care and meeting the needs of contraceptive users.Keywords: Pharmacoepidemiology, Sexual Health, Pharmacovigilance

Introduction

Combined oral contraceptives (COCs) are widely used globally,1 2 including by an estimated 14.3% of reproductive-aged females in Aotearoa New Zealand (Aotearoa/NZ).3 This makes these medications the most commonly used hormonal contraceptive method in Aotearoa/NZ.4 Contraceptive failure is when pregnancy occurs while using a method of contraception. Contraceptive failure rates among users of COCs are estimated to be 0.3% per year with perfect use, where all pills are taken as directed.5 However, in real-world ‘typical’ use, individuals may struggle to maintain consistent pill use and pills may be missed, resulting in increased pregnancy risk.6,11 Perhaps contributing to this, a stronger desire to avoid pregnancy is associated with more consistent contraception use compared with people with a weak pregnancy avoidance attitude, although pregnancy avoidance attitude can change rapidly over time.12 With ‘typical’ use, the failure rate may be as high as 7%–9% in the first year of using COCs, based on survey data.2 13 Other studies, using prospective data collection, have found contraceptive failure rates of between 0.5 and 2.2 per 100 woman-years of ‘typical’ COC use in Europe and the USA.14 15 A retrospective study using a French administrative health database found a pregnancy rate of 3.5%–4.8% among COC users in 2012, where a pregnancy was counted if it was conceived during the theoretical duration of COC use.16 Official resources for both clinicians and consumers in Aotearoa/NZ quote COC failure rates with ‘typical’ use as being 8%–10% per year17 18 despite there not being any Aotearoa/NZ-specific data.

Significant differences in the probability of oral contraceptive failure by age group, ethnicity/race, parity and household income have been described in the USA13; nulliparous (no children), White and higher income individuals had lower oral contraceptive failure rates than parous (one or more children), Black and lower income individuals. There are no data on pregnancy rates among users of COCs in Aotearoa/NZ and whether similar disparities in contraceptive failure rates between demographic groups exist, as well as limited studies on contraceptive utilisation in Aotearoa/NZ.

Understanding the pregnancy rate among users of COCs in Aotearoa/NZ, and whether there is variation of this rate by sociodemographic or geographic subgroup, will provide further insights into contraceptive services and utilisation in Aotearoa/NZ. This study therefore aimed to:

Estimate the pregnancy rate among users of COCs in Aotearoa/NZ in 2019.

Explore correlates of pregnancy rate by age, ethnicity, area-level deprivation, rurality, distance to pharmacy, parity, highest qualification, household income and new and established COC use.

Methods

This nationwide retrospective cohort study used data from the integrated data infrastructure (IDI). The IDI is a research database containing probabilistically linked, deidentified, person-level microdata from government departments including health, education and internal affairs. It is managed by Statistics New Zealand (StatsNZ).19 The false positive linkage rate for Health data in the IDI refresh used in this study was 0.9%.20

Study population

Data on females dispensed a government-subsidised COC between 1 April 2017 and 30 September 2020 were extracted from the Pharmaceutical Collection, a dataset containing dispensing data for all government-subsidised medications. Individuals eligible for publicly funded healthcare include all citizens, permanent residents, refugees and those on long-term work visas. The partners of people belonging to any of these groups are also eligible for publicly funded maternity care.21 During the time period of this study, most dispensations of government-subsidised COCs were associated with a NZ$5 user co-payment.

Government-subsidised COCs were defined as Therapeutic Group Level 3 ‘Antiandrogen Oral Contraceptives’ and ‘Combined Oral Contraceptives’ in the Pharmaceutical Collection. Data on the medication recipient, date dispensed, COC brand, formulation and quantity dispensed were extracted for each dispensation.

Individuals’ dates of birth are deidentified, with date of birth recorded as the middle day (14th–16th) of the month of birth). Individuals were only included in the analytical sample if they were aged between 16 and 50 years on 31 December 2019.

Medications data

Dispensation data described in Study Population were removed from the dataset if they occurred during or after the individual’s month of death, or if the individual was aged under 16 or over 50 years on 31 December in the year of that dispensation.

Dispensation quantities were converted into a standardised 21 days of active medication followed by a 7-day hormone-free interval (21/7 cycling). For subsequent dispensations, any remaining COC supplies, defined as dispensing quantity minus days between dispensations, were carried over into the next dispensation period. Discontinuation was defined as >28 days with no supply. The 28 day grace period was to allow for new users waiting to start COCs to align with menstrual cycles and individuals already using COCs to use remaining COC supplies from dispensations prior to the study period.22 See online supplemental Materials S.1 for further details.

Individuals were excluded if: they discontinued COCs prior to 4 December 2018 with no further COCs dispensed in the 2019 calendar year; their first COC dispensation in the study period was in 2020; they had dispensations of non-equivalent COC formulations on the same day; they had an average of more than one dispensation per 3 weeks over the period 1 April 2017 to 30 September 2020 or four or more dispensations in a calendar month as these were considered unusual dispensing patterns with potentially inaccurate dispensing data.

Individuals were considered to be either new or restarting users if they were not already using COCs on 1 January 2019, with those not new/restarting being classed as established users.

The number of days an individual was considered to be ‘at risk’ of pregnancy was defined as the number of days covered by COC use in the time period of interest, with individuals not considered to be using COCs after pregnancy conception. See online supplemental Materials S.1 for further details.

Pregnancy events

Pregnancy events identification was based on methods used by Foster et al.23 Pregnancies were identified from the nationwide Maternity Dataset, which holds data on all pregnancies that persist beyond 20 weeks and received publicly funded maternity services,24 and the National Minimum Dataset, which contains data on publicly funded hospital discharges. Estimated conception dates were generated for each pregnancy, and the pregnancy was counted if it was conceived during a period of COC use or within 28 days of discontinuation of COCs, with this grace period allowing for individuals not necessarily starting to take the COC on the day of dispensation.11 Only the first pregnancy conceived per dispensation was included if an individual had further pregnancies between dispensations. See online supplemental Materials S.1 for further details.

Sociodemographic and geographic covariates

Age (16–20, 21–30, 31–40 and 41–50 years), total ethnicity by major ethnic group using StatsNZ classifications (European/Other, Māori, Pacific Peoples, Asian, Middle Eastern/Latin American/African)25 and month and year of death if deceased were extracted for the study population from the personal details tables in the IDI. A proxy deceased date was set on the 15th of the month of death because the exact day of death is not available in the data.

The meshblock, a small neighbourhood-level area unit, was extracted based on where participants were living as at 31 December 2019. These were used to link to NZDep2018, a measure of neighbourhood-level deprivation based on the 2018 census,26 distance to nearest pharmacy using the national roading network and Geographic Classification for Health urban/rural variable.27 NZDep2018 was collapsed into quintiles, with 1 being areas of least deprivation and 5 being areas of most deprivation. The distance to the nearest pharmacy variable was generated using methods described in Hobbs et al28 and collapsed into a binary of <10 km or ≥10 km.

The number of children to members of the study population prior to 2019 was extracted from the Department of Internal Affairs births dataset. A binary variable was generated for study participants, with 0—no children born in Aotearoa/NZ, and 1—one or more children born in Aotearoa/NZ.

Highest qualification and total household income were extracted from the 2018 national census. Highest qualifications were collapsed into a four-category variable: National Certificate of Educational Achievement Level 1 or below, Upper secondary (National Certificate of Educational Achievement Level 2 or 3) or overseas secondary, Diploma, Bachelor’s degree or higher. Total household income was collapsed into a five-category variable (≤NZ$25 000, NZ$25 001–NZ$50 000, NZ$50 001–NZ$70 000, NZ$70 001–NZ$100 000, ≥NZ$100 001).

Procedure

Data were sourced from the October 2023 refresh of the IDI, extracted using SAS Enterprise Guide V.8.329 and analysed using Stata/MP V.18.0 for Windows30 within the secure IDI environment by an approved researcher. All StatsNZ confidentiality requirements were adhered to.

Statistical analysis

Reporting of analyses was informed by the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) guidelines.31 Pregnancies were reported as counts and rates per number of days exposed to COC use. The random rounding of counts to base 3, one of Stats NZ’s confidentiality requirements,32 means counts may not add to exactly 100%. Poisson regression was used to generate adjusted risk ratios and 95% CIs, adjusted for sociodemographic and geographic covariates and whether a user was new/restarting or established, for the outcome of number of pregnancies per participant, offset by the natural log of the number of days each participant was exposed to COC use.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore the effects of changing the grace period length, using equivalised household income and including data for privately-funded hospital discharges. See online supplemental Materials S. 2 for more details.

Patient and public involvement

The use of routinely collected health data precluded patient involvement in study recruitment. Previous research in Aotearoa/NZ has shown that the majority of the public are comfortable with their health data being used to make decisions to improve health services, particularly when the data are stored securely and confidentiality is maintained, among other factors.33 Data security and confidentiality are ensured by IDI protocols, as is the requirement for published findings being publicly available as open access. A separate qualitative study was conducted to hear COC users’ perspectives on contraceptive services and their experiences of COC shortages.

Results

There were 208 197 individuals included in the analysis of pregnancy events conceived in 2019, as shown in table 1. The mean age of participants in 2019 was 27.3 years (SD 8.1). Approximately five out of six participants were European/Other ethnicity, with one in six identifying as Māori and less than 1 in 20 identifying with a Pacific ethnic group. A quarter of participants had one or more children born in Aotearoa/NZ. Just over one-third of participants were new or restarting COC users in 2019. Approximately one-third of participants’ highest educational qualification was upper secondary, and just over a quarter had a bachelor’s degree or higher. Nearly half of the participants lived in a household earning more than NZ$100 000 annually. There were 16 827 fewer participants living in areas of most deprivation compared with areas of least deprivation. Over four in five participants lived in urban areas, and over nine in ten lived less than 10 km from a pharmacy.

Counts and rates of pregnancy per 100 person-years of COC use in the calendar year of 2019 are shown in table 2. There were 4617 pregnancies identified during periods of COC use in 2019, representing 3.50 pregnancy events per 100 person-years of COC use. Pregnancy rates varied significantly by age group, with the highest rates among those aged 31–40 followed by 21–30 years old. There was significant variation by ethnicity, with European/Other and Asian users having lower rates of pregnancy than non-European/Other and non-Asian users, respectively. Users with children had significantly higher pregnancy rates than users without children born in Aotearoa/NZ. New or restarting users had significantly higher pregnancy rates than established (not new or restarting) COC users. With increasing area-level deprivation, there was a gradual but significant increase in pregnancy rates. COC users living in rural areas had significantly higher pregnancy rates than those living in urban areas.

Sensitivity analyses

When the grace period for pregnancy events being counted was changed to 0 or 90 days from COC running out, 3780 and 6360 pregnancies were identified, respectively, in 2019. These gave respective pregnancy rates of 3.00 and 4.42 pregnancies per 100 person-years of COC use. An additional 18 pregnancies were identified in 2019 when private discharges data were used in addition to public discharges. There was little difference seen in other sensitivity analyses; see online supplemental Materials S.3 for details.

Discussion

This whole-of-population retrospective cohort study was the first to assess pregnancy rates among COC users in Aotearoa/NZ. It found the rate of pregnancy in 2019 was similar to that of a comparable French study where pregnancy rates of 4.8% for first and second generation COCs, and 3.5% for third generation COCs, were identified using administrative health data.16 There was significant variation in pregnancy rate by various sociodemographic and geographic factors, including age, ethnicity, highest qualification, nulliparity, whether a user was new to or restarting use of COCs, household income, area-level deprivation and rurality. This has implications for whether current contraception services in Aotearoa/NZ are providing equitable care.

The pregnancy rate identified in this study is significantly lower than the 8%–10% annual failure rate quoted in official contraceptive guidelines in Aotearoa/NZ.17 18 This indicates an update may be required to avoid misleading information being used in contraceptive decision-making.

While oral contraceptive failure rates are not directly comparable to the pregnancy rate identified in this study, significant variation in failure rates by similar sociodemographic subgroups has been described in the USA for 2006–2010.13 The difference by ethnic/racial group was of similar magnitude, with the 12-month failure rate among White users being less than half that of Black users in the USA (6% vs 13%). However, the differences in 12-month failure rate in the USA for nulliparous (no children) vs parous (one or more children) users (3.2% vs 11.5%), and lower vs higher household income (4.7% vs 13.2%), were more extreme than the variations in pregnancy rate seen in Aotearoa/NZ.

The variation in pregnancy rate by age group among COC users in 2019 was likely due to underlying population-level fertility trends varying by age with women aged 25–34 having the highest fertility rates in 2018.34 To some degree, this variation likely represents intended pregnancies. However, the significant variation in pregnancy rate among users by ethnicity, highest education level, household income, area-level deprivation and rurality after adjusting for confounding has a less clear cause. This could represent a combination of intended and unintended pregnancies. Intended pregnancies must not be seen as a contraceptive failure, and further research is needed to identify what is driving this variation in pregnancy rate and whether users feel contraceptive services are meeting their needs. Contraceptive failure resulting in unintended pregnancy due to inconsistent method use and missed pills6,10 is likely contributed to by systemic access barriers to contraception and primary healthcare in Aotearoa/NZ such as cost, distance to primary healthcare services and appointment wait times.35,37 Not having a new pill pack when the previous supply runs out has been associated with increased risk of pregnancy.9 Previous collaborative research with family planning service consumers in Aotearoa/NZ has identified strengthening the reach, inclusivity, scope, care and cultural responsiveness of services as key areas for improvement,35 and addressing these such as with mobile services and drop-in clinics may reduce access barriers and thus make it easier for COC users to maintain consistent pill use.

This study used real-world whole-of-population user data, which has strengths over previous estimates of pregnancy rates among COC users that relied on retrospective or real-time self-reporting, or used data from a subset of the population.213,16 Use of the IDI provided robust ethnicity data and allowed linkage to geographic and Census data.19 38 Not relying on retrospective reports of pregnancy and COC use eliminated any recall bias.

These findings must also be considered in light of the limitations of administrative health data, particularly regarding contraception and pregnancy. These limitations are consistent with those identified by Agostini et al and Foster et al.16 23 The estimated discontinuation date may overestimate time exposed to COC use and thus underestimate the pregnancy rate, as early discontinuation is not captured. The data cannot distinguish between intended pregnancy following early discontinuation of COC and unintended pregnancy that occurred during a period of COC use. Therefore, some of the pregnancies identified in this study may have been intended pregnancies rather than contraceptive failure. The 28-day grace period used in this study means some intended pregnancies conceived within 28 days following COC discontinuation when COC supplies ran out may have been counted, resulting in a possible overestimation of pregnancy rate. In-clinic dispensing of COCs is not captured in the Pharmaceutical Collection dataset, meaning some periods of COC use were not captured in this study. However, this represents a small proportion of COCs supplied in Aotearoa/NZ with over 94% of all funded COC formulations being dispensed to individuals at a community pharmacy,39 and this is unlikely to have affected the findings of this study, but the findings may not be applicable to individuals receiving COC supplies in-clinic. Pregnancy incidence may have been underestimated as early pregnancy loss or termination that did not result in hospital admission was not detected. The use of cross-sectional routinely collected health data that were not collected for the purposes of this study means causality cannot be determined.

This study provides the first Aotearoa/NZ-specific data on pregnancy rates among COC users. It showed estimated pregnancy rates among users in Aotearoa/NZ were similar to those seen internationally and were much lower than the figures used in national guidelines. The data revealed significant variation between sociodemographic and geographic subgroups, likely due to a currently unquantified combination of intended and unintended pregnancies among COC users. This variation warrants further investigation to better understand the driving factors and to determine whether current reproductive health services need to be reviewed or tailored to address the needs of specific populations. Initiatives to reduce systemic access barriers are also necessary to facilitate reducing unintended pregnancy rates among COC users and making contraceptive services more equitable.
