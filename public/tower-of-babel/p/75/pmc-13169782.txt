Impact of concomitant intra-aortic balloon pump support on outcomes in patients with acute myocardial infarction-related cardiogenic shock undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention with veno-arterial ECMO: a propensity-matched analysis
Authors: Mykhailo Todurov, Borys Todurov, Ihor Mokryk, Serhii Sudakevych, Sofia Chaikovska, Oleh Zelenchuk, Yurii Hutsuliak, Stepan Maruniak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myocardial infarction-related cardiogenic shock (AMI-CS) remains associated with extremely high mortality in patients with acute myocardial infarction despite timely revascularization and advances in mechanical circulatory support (MCS). Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) is increasingly used as rescue therapy during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI); however, ECMO-related left ventricular overload may compromise myocardial recovery. Whether concomitant intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support improves outcomes in this setting remains uncertain.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study including adult patients with cardiogenic shock undergoing PCI supported with V-A ECMO between January 2020 and December 2025. Patients were stratified according to the use of adjunctive IABP (ECMO-IABP vs. ECMO alone). Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed using age, sex, lactate and VIS before ECMO. The primary endpoint was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included ECMO weaning success, complications, length of intensive care unit stay and 30-day survival. Survival was assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis.ResultsAmong 57 eligible patients, 22 received concomitant IABP support and 35 were treated with V-A ECMO alone. After propensity score matching, 44 patients were included in the final analysis. Successful weaning from V-A ECMO was significantly more frequent in the ECMO-IABP group compared with ECMO alone (86.4% vs. 59.1%, p = 0.027). No statistically significant difference in in-hospital mortality was observed between the groups. In the unmatched cohort, 30-day survival was significantly higher in patients treated with concomitant IABP support (log-rank p = 0.038); after propensity score matching, no significant survival difference was observed (log-rank p = 0.051). Rates of major bleeding (BARC ≥ 3), vascular complications, renal failure, and neurological events were comparable between groups.ConclusionsIn patients with AMI-CS undergoing PCI supported with veno-arterial ECMO, adjunctive intra-aortic balloon pump support was associated with a higher rate of successful ECMO weaning. No significant difference in in-hospital mortality was observed after propensity score matching. A non-significant trend toward improved short-term survival was noted.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction-related cardiogenic shock (AMI-CS) remains the leading cause of early mortality in patients with acute myocardial infarction despite major advances in reperfusion strategies and intensive care management [1]. Even in the era of early percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), in-hospital mortality associated with cardiogenic shock continues to exceed 40–50%, particularly among patients requiring advanced mechanical circulatory support (MCS) [2].

Although current guidelines increasingly favor early left ventricular unloading using percutaneous microaxial flow pumps such as Impella, their widespread implementation remains limited [3]. Device availability, high costs, and the need for specialized expertise significantly restrict their use, particularly in low- and middle-income countries especially during wartime conditions in Ukraine [4]. As a result, many centers continue to rely on veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) and intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) as the most accessible support strategies. In this context, optimizing the use of these modalities in myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock remains an important and unresolved clinical challenge.

Despite a strong physiological rationale, the clinical benefit of concomitant IABP use during V-A ECMO remains uncertain. Randomized trials of IABP in cardiogenic shock conducted in the pre-ECMO era did not demonstrate a mortality benefit [5]. Accordingly, current guidelines do not recommend routine IABP use in this setting. However, these trials largely excluded ECMO-supported patients, limiting their applicability to contemporary practice and to patients undergoing PCI. Observational studies evaluating combined ECMO-IABP support have reported conflicting results. Some suggest improved survival through enhanced left ventricular unloading and reduced pulmonary complications [6, 7], whereas others show no clear mortality benefit and raise concerns regarding vascular and bleeding complications associated with dual-device strategies [8]. Importantly, most available data derive from heterogeneous shock populations, including postcardiotomy shock, myocarditis, and mixed etiologies. In contrast, evidence specifically focused on patients with acute myocardial infarction undergoing high-risk PCI remains limited.

The present study aimed to evaluate whether concomitant intra-aortic balloon pump support is associated with improved outcomes in patients with AMI-CS undergoing PCI supported with V-A ECMO. Using a propensity score–matched analysis, we assessed its impact on ECMO weaning, complication rates, and short-term survival. To our knowledge, this is one of the first propensity-matched studies specifically focused on AMI-CS in the setting of PCI with V-A ECMO support.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a single-center retrospective observational cohort study. We analyzed consecutive adult patients with cardiogenic shock who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and required veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) for hemodynamic support. All patients were treated at a tertiary referral center. The study period extended from January 2020 to December 2025, according to the availability of complete clinical data in the institutional ECMO registry.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to its retrospective design and the use of de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the local ethics committee.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18 years or older, had documented cardiogenic shock, underwent PCI, and required V-A ECMO support during procedure. Patients supported with veno-venous ECMO only, those receiving V-A ECMO for indications unrelated to PCI, or patients with incomplete outcome data were excluded from the analysis.

Cardiogenic shock was defined as persistent hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg for ≥ 30 min or requirement for vasopressor support to maintain adequate arterial pressure) accompanied by signs of end-organ hypoperfusion, including oliguria, altered mental status, elevated serum lactate levels, or peripheral hypoperfusion, despite adequate volume resuscitation. The diagnosis was established based on documentation in the medical records and the treating physician’s clinical assessment.

Mechanical circulatory support

All patients received veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) as rescue mechanical circulatory support for refractory cardiogenic shock in the setting of percutaneous coronary intervention.

V-A ECMO cannulation strategy was selected according to institutional practice and patient anatomy. Peripheral cannulation was preferentially used, with venous drainage obtained from the femoral vein and arterial return through the femoral artery. Distal limb perfusion was applied in all cases. The initiation of V-A ECMO was performed before PCI in cases of persistent hemodynamic instability despite optimal medical therapy, including fluid resuscitation, vasoactive agents, and inotropic support.

The decision to use concomitant IABP was made at the discretion of the treating multidisciplinary team based on clinical judgment. IABP was utilized as a strategy for left ventricular unloading, aiming to reduce left ventricular afterload, improve coronary perfusion, and mitigate pulmonary congestion during V-A ECMO support. IABP insertion was performed either before, during, or shortly after V-A ECMO initiation. The IABP was applied using a standard methodology. Femoral artery access was obtained percutaneously. Under fluoroscopic or echocardiographic guidance, a balloon catheter was positioned in the descending thoracic aorta below the origin of the left subclavian artery. The balloon was connected to a console that was synchronized with the patient’s ECG.

Weaning from ECMO was considered when the patient remained hemodynamically stable with mean arterial pressure > 90 mmHg, ECMO flow < 1 L/min/m², and adequate blood gas and acid-base parameters. In our cohort, IABP weaning was performed after ECMO decannulation, once adequate hemodynamic stability had been achieved. Weaning from IABP began when the cardiac index (CI) exceeded 2.5 L/min/m², central venous pressure (CVP) was ≤ 12 mmHg, blood lactate was < 2.5 mmol/L, mean arterial pressure (MAP) was > 65 mmHg, and urine output of ≥ 1 mL/kg/h was maintained for at least 5 consecutive hours. Stepwise reduction of support was performed by decreasing the IABP ratio from 1:1 to 1:2 during the first 4 h and then from 1:2 to 1:3 in the fifth hour, followed by complete discontinuation.

Percutaneous coronary intervention

PCI was performed according to standard techniques preferentially via right radial artery access. Routine therapy during angioplasty included nitrates, clopidogrel (loading dose 600 mg together with aspirin), and heparin (bolus, 100 IU/kg, to achieve activated clotting time [ACT] > 250 s), followed by continuous heparin infusion at 10–20 IU/kg/h (to maintain ACT at 160–200 s). ACT was monitored every 30 min using the ACT Plus™ device (Medtronic). Technical procedural aspects, including stent and balloon selection, were individualized by the operator.

The balloon inflation duration and pressure were adjusted in accordance with the patient’s conditions. Hemodynamically significant occlusion was defined as > 50% arterial lumen obstruction. All patients routinely underwent 12-lead electrocardiography (ECG) before and after PCI to detect procedure-related ischemic changes or the appearance of new pathological Q waves.

Data collection

Patients were identified using the institutional ECMO registry and medical records. All consecutive patients treated with V-A ECMO for cardiogenic shock in the setting of PCI during the study period were screened for eligibility. Demographic data, clinical characteristics, procedural details, and outcome variables were retrospectively extracted from the institutional database and patient medical records. Collected variables included age, sex, body mass index, lactate and VIS before ECMO, comorbidities, V-A ECMO duration, transfusion requirements, ECMO-related complications, length of intensive care unit stay, and in-hospital outcomes. The VIS was calculated according to the following formula:

VIS = dopamine (µg/kg/min) + dobutamine (µg/kg/min) + 100 × epinephrine (µg/kg/min) + 100 × norepinephrine (µg/kg/min) + 10 × milrinone (µg/kg/min) + 10,000 × vasopressin (U/kg/min).

VIS values were recorded immediately before ECMO initiation and incorporated into the analysis as an indicator of baseline hemodynamic dependence on vasoactive therapy. Together with metabolic markers such as serum lactate, VIS was used to provide a more comprehensive assessment of circulatory failure severity.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was in-hospital mortality, defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization. Secondary outcomes included duration of V-A ECMO support, length of intensive care unit stay, total hospital length of stay, bleeding complications, renal and neurological complications, transfusion requirements, major ECMO-related complications and 30-day survival. Perioperative bleeding events were evaluated and classified according to the Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) criteria [9]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined in accordance with the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria [10].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as absolute numbers and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

To minimize potential confounding due to baseline differences between patients treated with V-A ECMO with and without intra-aortic balloon pump support, a propensity score matching analysis was performed. Given the limited sample size, the number of covariates included in the propensity score model was restricted to avoid model overfitting. The propensity score was estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model including clinically relevant pre-ECMO variables: age, sex, lactate and VIS before ECMO. Patients were matched using a nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement in a 1:1 ratio, with a caliper width of 0.05 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance between the matched groups was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.10 considered indicative of adequate balance. Following matching, comparisons between groups were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for continuous variables and McNemar’s test for categorical variables.

Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method to estimate 30-day in-hospital survival. Differences in survival between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Patients were censored at hospital discharge or at 30 days of follow-up, whichever occurred first. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Propensity score calculation and case matching were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Between 2020 and 2025, a total of 113 patients with AMI-CS who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention were screened. After exclusion of patients without V-A ECMO support and those with incomplete clinical data, 57 patients treated with V-A ECMO were included in the final analysis. Among them, 22 patients received adjunctive intra-aortic balloon pump support (ECMO-IABP group), whereas 35 patients were managed with V-A ECMO alone. To minimize baseline differences between the treatment strategies, propensity score matching was performed using age, sex, lactate and VIS before ECMO. After 1:1 matching, 22 patients in the ECMO-IABP group were successfully matched with 22 patients in the ECMO-alone group, resulting in a final matched cohort of 44 patients (Fig. 1).

After propensity score matching, procedural characteristics remained well balanced between the two groups. The proportion of left main PCI, the prevalence of impaired coronary flow before intervention (TIMI 0–1), and the achievement of final TIMI 3 flow and complete revascularization did not differ significantly. The incidence of no-reflow syndrome, use of thrombus aspiration, and rates of periprocedural complications, including coronary dissection, acute stent closure, and cardiac arrest during PCI, were comparable between groups.

Although the total number of implanted stents was higher in the ECMO-IABP group before matching, these differences were no longer statistically significant after propensity score matching, indicating adequate procedural balance between the matched cohorts.

The duration of V-A ECMO support was comparable between the groups both before and after matching (Table 3). Before matching, patients treated with adjunctive IABP demonstrated significantly higher rates of successful weaning from V-A ECMO compared with those supported with ECMO alone (86.4% vs. 51.4%, p = 0.011). This difference remained statistically significant after propensity score matching (86.4% vs. 59.1%, p = 0.027).

Although patients in the ECMO-IABP group had longer intensive care unit stay and total hospital length of stay before matching (p = 0.038 and p = 0.029, respectively), these differences were no longer observed after propensity score matching. Rates of vascular, bleeding, renal, neurological, and infectious complications did not differ significantly between the two groups in either the unmatched or matched cohorts. Importantly, the use of adjunctive IABP was not associated with an increased incidence of major bleeding events (BARC ≥ 3), acute kidney injury, or need for renal replacement therapy after matching.

In-hospital mortality was numerically lower in the ECMO-IABP group compared with ECMO alone both before and after propensity score matching; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance (22.7% vs. 45.7% before matching, p = 0.097; and 22.7% vs. 50.0% after matching, p = 0.116).

Kaplan-Meier survival curves before and after propensity score matching are shown in (Fig. 2). Before matching, patients treated with ECMO-IABP demonstrated significantly higher 30-day in-hospital survival compared with those receiving V-A ECMO alone (log-rank p = 0.038). After propensity score matching, no statistically significant difference in survival was observed between the groups; however, an early separation of the survival curves favoring the ECMO-IABP group was noted (log-rank p = 0.051).

Discussion

The present propensity score–matched analysis suggests that the addition of intra-aortic balloon pump support to veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation during percutaneous coronary intervention in patients with AMI-CS may be associated with improved ECMO weaning success, while no significant difference in in-hospital mortality was observed after matching. Baseline shock severity was assessed using complementary metabolic and hemodynamic markers, including serum lactate and the vasoactive–inotropic score. Incorporation of VIS into the propensity model allowed adjustment for both metabolic derangement and the degree of hemodynamic dependence on vasoactive support, thereby strengthening the robustness of the analysis.

Our findings are consistent with observational data suggesting a potential benefit of adjunctive IABP during V-A ECMO in AMI-CS. A nationwide Japanese registry analysis reported lower mortality with combined V-A ECMO and IABP compared with ECMO alone in patients with AMI-CS undergoing PCI, supporting the concept that indirect LV unloading may improve outcomes in selected patients [11]. Similarly, a meta-analysis in AMI-CS demonstrated lower short-term mortality with ECMO-IABP compared with ECMO alone, although the authors emphasized the hypothesis-generating nature of these findings [12]. These results are concordant with the mortality reduction observed in our propensity-matched cohort and reinforce the plausibility of benefit in AMI-related shock.

Beyond AMI-specific studies, registry data in mixed shock populations also suggest a possible benefit of IABP after ECMO initiation [6]. However, the heterogeneity of these populations limits direct applicability to AMI-related PCI settings. The timing of IABP initiation may also be relevant. A prospective cohort study suggested that early or concurrent IABP use may influence outcomes in AMI-CS patients receiving ECMO [13]. Although not assessed in our study, these findings support the physiological rationale for early LV unloading.

Notably, some studies have reported neutral findings regarding mortality benefit from unloading in AMI-CS or raised concerns about complications, which may reflect differences in patient selection, shock severity, cannulation strategies, anticoagulation protocols, or competing risks such as advanced multiorgan failure. A recent retrospective analysis also suggested that concomitant IABP may be neither clearly protective nor clearly harmful in relation to ECMO-related complications, underscoring the heterogeneity of published results [14]. Importantly, our study narrows the clinical phenotype to PCI-related AMI cardiogenic shock and demonstrates a survival signal without an observed increase in major bleeding (BARC ≥ 3) or vascular complications after matching, which strengthens the argument that patient selection and clinical context are critical determinants of benefit.

Mechanistically, V-A ECMO increases LV afterload and may promote LV distension and pulmonary congestion. Both indirect (IABP) and direct (microaxial pumps) unloading strategies may mitigate these effects; however, the optimal approach remains uncertain and likely depends on patient characteristics and resource availability [15]. Current international guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndromes and cardiogenic shock do not support the routine use of intra-aortic balloon pump, given the lack of demonstrated mortality benefit in randomized trials [3, 4]. However, these recommendations are largely based on non-ECMO populations and may not fully apply to patients undergoing PCI with V-A ECMO support.

In Ukraine, the use of mechanical circulatory support in patients with AMI-CS during PCI faces additional limitations, including restricted access to advanced technologies, insufficient funding for high-cost equipment, and limited institutional experience with certain support devices in the majority of medical centers [16]. Specifically, devices such as microaxial flow pumps and TandemHeart, which are widely used in Europe and the United States due to their minimally invasive design and ability to provide physiological cardiac output support, remain largely unavailable in routine clinical practice. This is primarily related to their high acquisition cost, the need for specialized staff training, and the absence of structured reimbursement systems.

According to data from the National Registry of Cardiogenic Shock in Ukraine, among 221 patients with cardiogenic shock undergoing PCI, only 19.9% received any form of mechanical circulatory support, which most commonly involved intra-aortic balloon pump support and, less frequently, V-A ECMO [17]. Consequently, in many centers, the choice of mechanical support is effectively limited to IABP or ECMO, significantly reducing flexibility in interventional strategies and complicating individualized adaptation to specific clinical scenarios.

In this context, the observed association between adjunctive IABP use and improved weaning success and short-term survival in our study may be particularly relevant. Our findings support the concept that, when advanced unloading devices are unavailable, the combination of V-A ECMO and IABP represents a pragmatic and potentially beneficial strategy in selected patients with myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock undergoing PCI. These results may help inform clinical decision-making in resource-limited environments and underscore the need for tailored mechanical circulatory support algorithms adapted to local infrastructure and availability. Importantly, our findings should be interpreted within the specific clinical context of AMI-related cardiogenic shock undergoing PCI, and should not be generalized to broader cardiogenic shock populations with different etiologies or treatment strategies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective and single-center design introduces inherent risks of selection bias and and potential residual confounding despite the use of propensity score matching, as unmeasured variables may have influenced treatment allocation and outcomes. Given the limited sample size, the number of covariates in the propensity model was intentionally restricted to avoid model overfitting, in accordance with the events-per-variable principle. Second, the sample size remains relatively small, limiting the ability to perform subgroup analyses and reducing the statistical power to detect differences in mortality and complication endpoints, thereby increasing the risk of type II error. This is particularly relevant in light of p-values approaching statistical significance (e.g., log-rank p = 0.051), which should be interpreted with caution. Third, precise timing of IABP implantation relative to ECMO initiation was not standardized and could not be analyzed as a separate variable. Finally, long-term outcomes beyond 30 days were not assessed, and therefore the impact of adjunctive IABP on mid- and long-term survival remains unknown.

Conclusion

In this single-center propensity score–matched analysis, the use of concomitant intra-aortic balloon pump support during veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation in patients with acute myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention was associated with improved ECMO weaning success, while no significant reduction in in-hospital mortality was observed after propensity score matching. A trend toward improved short-term survival was noted. Importantly, the addition of IABP was not accompanied by a higher incidence of major bleeding, vascular, renal, or neurological complications. While microaxial flow pumps may represent a more effective strategy for left ventricular unloading during V-A ECMO, their availability remains limited in many settings. In this context, IABP may serve as a pragmatic and accessible alternative in selected patients, particularly in resource-constrained environments.
