Introduction

Moebius syndrome is a rare congenital disorder characterized by underdevelopment of cranial nerves, particularly the sixth (VI) and seventh (VII), and by neurocognitive deficits. It is associated with various cranial nerve palsies, orofacial malformations, musculoskeletal abnormalities, and intellectual disability [1]. Due to frequent misdiagnosis, lack of registries, and limited expertise, epidemiological data on Moebius syndrome remain scarce. However, the estimated prevalence is approximately 0.3 per 100,000 live births [2], with most diagnoses occurring between 2 and 3 years of age [2]. There are no published data on the prevalence of Moebius syndrome in Colombia.

Bronchial stenosis is also a rare condition. In adults, it may result from trauma, prolonged intubation, prior airway surgery, lung transplantation, infection, or malignancy [3]. In children, congenital bronchial stenosis is a more common cause and, although not always life-threatening, it can lead to recurrent pulmonary infections [4]. An association between Down syndrome and subglottic or bronchial stenosis has been described in pediatric populations [5]. Treatment options include bronchoscopic balloon dilation and surgery, with slide bronchoplasty being the preferred surgical technique [4]. However, published outcomes of open surgical approaches in children remain limited [6].

To date, no direct association between Moebius syndrome and congenital bronchial stenosis has been reported. However, both are rare congenital conditions that often coexist with other anomalies, particularly involving craniofacial or respiratory structures. Moebius syndrome has been linked to orofacial hypoplasia, brainstem dysgenesis, and cranial nerve dysfunction—features that may reflect broader embryological disruptions potentially involving the lower airway. In this context, the present case raises the possibility of a syndromic or developmental link between Moebius syndrome and bronchial stenosis, though a coincidental overlap cannot be excluded [7] [8]. Given the absence of prior documentation, this case represents a contribution to the understanding and multidisciplinary management of complex pediatric airway anomalies. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [9].

Presentation of case

An 8-year-old male with a history of Moebius syndrome presented to the emergency department (ED) after three days of increased tracheal secretions, cyanosis, altered mental status, and fever. His medical history was notable for severe respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) since birth, requiring prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) hospitalization, mechanical ventilation, and tracheostomy. He also had epilepsy and recurrent airway infections, averaging three hospitalizations per year. Over time, he became increasingly dependent on nocturnal mechanical ventilation via tracheostomy. Additionally, he developed a suction-deglutition disorder requiring gastrostomy placement.

Upon arrival, his temperature was 38 °C, heart rate 132 bpm, respiratory rate 28 breaths per minute, blood pressure 108/65 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 97 % on room air. Physical examination revealed respiratory distress with hypoventilation at the left lung base and audible crackles. Laboratory studies showed leukocytosis and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP). Chest X-ray (CXR) demonstrated right apical consolidation and complete left lung atelectasis (Fig. 1). The initial management included ICU admission, ventilatory support via tracheostomy, and empiric intravenous (IV) antibiotics for community-acquired pneumonia and tracheitis with amikacin and cefepime.

Chest X-Ray on Day 1, left lower lobe atelectasis with left mediastinal shift.

On the third hospital day, bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage revealed severe endobronchitis and significant stenosis of the left main bronchus and right upper lobe bronchus. Mucous plugs were aspirated and sent for culture. Despite negative blood cultures, airway antigen testing on the sixth day was positive for Pseudomonas aeruginosa, methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA), Escherichia coli, and Candida tropicalis—findings confirmed in the tracheal cultures. The antimicrobial regimen was adjusted to include IV amphotericin B and caspofungin. On the ninth day, computed tomography (CT) of the chest confirmed complete left lung atelectasis, obliteration of the left mainstem bronchus and right upper lobe bronchus (Fig. 2a), and chronic thrombosis of the right brachiocephalic vein, for which subcutaneous enoxaparin was initiated.

A second bronchoscopy on day 13 showed no significant improvement despite broad medical management, with persistent 80 % stenosis of the left main bronchus. Bronchoscopic balloon dilation (BBD) was performed on day 18, and postprocedural CT angiography demonstrated temporary repermeabilization (Fig. 2b). The patient was transferred to the general ward two days later on Venturi mask oxygen. A second BBD was performed on day 31, successfully dilating the right upper lobe bronchus, but revealing complete restenosis of the left main bronchus. A multidisciplinary team concluded that surgical bronchoplasty with veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) support was indicated due to the need to collapse the right lung for carinal reconstruction.

A preoperative chest CT was performed (Fig. 2c). A right posterolateral thoracotomy was carried out through the fifth intercostal space, with dissection of the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and serratus anterior muscle, preserving the latissimus dorsi. The azygos vein was dissected and ligated. VV-ECMO was established using an arterial cannula inserted into the proximal stump of the azygos vein and a venous cannula placed in the femoral vein. After careful exposure of the trachea and both main bronchi, the left main bronchus was transected at its origin. An 8 mm stenotic segment extending from the carina was resected until healthy tissue was identified. End-to-end anastomosis was performed using interrupted 4–0 polypropylene sutures. The anastomosis was water-tested and found to be airtight, with full right lung re-expansion observed. No intraoperative complications occurred, and the patient was successfully weaned off ECMO and tolerated mechanical ventilation via tracheostomy postoperatively.

Postoperative CXR showed improved left lung aeration and resolution of atelectasis (Fig. 3). The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 4. Bronchoscopy on postoperative day 5 revealed a patent left main bronchial lumen. Cultures from bronchoalveolar lavage grew S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, while blood cultures were positive for C. tropicalis and S. maltophilia. The antimicrobial regimen was adjusted to IV cefepime, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and caspofungin for 10 days. By hospital day 57, the patient tolerated reduced daytime ventilation, and by day 61, had returned to his baseline of nocturnal-only support. He was transferred to the general ward for continued nutritional support, physical therapy, and respiratory rehabilitation. After 97 days of hospitalization, the patient was discharged home with ongoing home healthcare support. Table 1 presents a summary timeline of the patient's hospital course.

Discussion

Diagnostic challenges

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of congenital bronchial stenosis (CBS), particularly in a child with Moebius syndrome and overlapping respiratory symptoms. Despite a history of recurrent lower respiratory infections since birth, the diagnosis was delayed due to the rarity of CBS and its nonspecific clinical presentation. Additional barriers included limited access to specialized care due to the patient's socioeconomic context.

CBS is a rare condition, often caused by extrinsic compression from cardiovascular or pulmonary anomalies [4]. Prematurity and prolonged intubation are also recognized contributing factors [10]. Clinical manifestations—such as respiratory distress, stridor, and persistent cough—can mimic more common airway pathologies in children. Definitive diagnosis typically requires both bronchoscopy and CT imaging to assess airway anatomy and stenotic extent [11].

Treatment approaches

Initial management of CBS often involves endoscopic procedures such as bronchoscopic balloon dilation (BBD), which can offer temporary relief. In our patient, two BBDs were performed with partial success, including transient repermeabilization of the left main bronchus and improved ventilation of the right upper lobe. However, the stenosis rapidly recurred.

Köse et al. demonstrated the safety and efficacy of BBD in a pediatric series of seven cases, although mucosal lacerations were reported in some patients [10]. Stent placement remains a theoretical option, but its application in children is limited due to the absence of biodegradable stents and challenges in sizing and placement [10]. Given the failure of conservative endoscopic measures, surgical intervention was indicated.

Surgical considerations

Surgical repair of CBS—via bronchial resection, bronchoplasty, or slide bronchoplasty—is technically demanding, particularly in pediatric patients. Nonetheless, it offers definitive treatment and preservation of lung function when endoscopic techniques are insufficient [4,12]. Common postoperative risks include atelectasis, airway restenosis, pneumonia, and empyema.

In our case, the patient underwent a left main bronchoplasty via right posterolateral thoracotomy under veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO). We detailed the surgical technique, including cannulation strategy, dissection, and anastomosis under direct vision. No intraoperative complications occurred, and airway patency was confirmed postoperatively.

In a prospective cohort of 67 patients undergoing tracheal reconstruction for congenital stenosis, Tanh et al. reported a mortality rate of up to 44 % in emergency settings or in patients with complex lesions [13]. These findings emphasize the importance of early diagnosis, elective surgical planning, and preoperative stabilization—especially in patients with multiple comorbidities, such as in this case.

Postoperative outcomes

Postoperatively, the patient showed progressive improvement. Bronchoscopy confirmed a patent left bronchial lumen. Cultures guided antimicrobial adjustments, and the patient gradually transitioned to nocturnal-only mechanical ventilation by hospital day 57. After completing physical, nutritional, and respiratory rehabilitation, he was discharged on day 97 in stable condition with home healthcare support.

Although a direct association between Moebius syndrome and CBS has not been described in the literature, both conditions may arise from broader neuroectodermal or embryological disruptions. Moebius syndrome has been linked to brainstem hypoplasia, cranial nerve dysfunction, and orofacial abnormalities—findings that may reflect systemic embryogenic anomalies potentially involving the lower airway [1,2]. This case raises the possibility of a syndromic or developmental link, though a coincidental overlap cannot be excluded. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of surgically managed CBS in a pediatric patient with Moebius syndrome.

Conclusion

Bronchial stenosis in pediatric patients remains a clinical and surgical challenge associated with considerable morbidity. Optimal outcomes depend on early recognition and coordinated management by a multidisciplinary team including pediatricians, pulmonologists, radiologists, intensivists, and thoracic surgeons. In complex cases, timely surgical intervention may offer definitive treatment when endoscopic approaches fail.
