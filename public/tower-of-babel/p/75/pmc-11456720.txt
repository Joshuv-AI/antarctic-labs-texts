Atypical Periprosthetic Femoral Fracture Might be Considered a Distinct Subtype of Atypical Femoral Fracture: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Kai Huang, Yi Zeng, Qingyi Zhang, Jie Tan, Hexi Li, Jing Yang, Huiqi Xie, Bin Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe exact relationship among atypical periprosthetic femoral fractures (APFFs), typical periprosthetic femoral fractures (PFFs), and atypical femur fractures (AFFs) remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of APFFs among PFFs and to identify the clinical characteristics, management, and prognosis that distinguish APFFs from typical PFFs and AFFs to further determine the relationship among these three fracture types.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we reviewed the clinical data of 117 consecutive patients who had PFFs after hip arthroplasty between January 2012 and December 2022 and further classified them into an APFF group and a typical PFF group according to the revised ASBMR diagnostic criteria for AFF. Moreover, patients who had subtrochanteric or femoral shaft fractures in the same period and met the diagnostic criteria for AFF were recruited and classified into the AFF group. Demographic information, minor features of AFF, comorbidities, history of medication usage, management, and complications were collected and compared among patients with typical PFFs, APFFs, and AFFs.ResultsEleven PFFs were identified as APFFs, and the prevalence of APFFs among PFFs was 9.4%. Significant differences were found in generalized increase in cortical thickness (p = 0.019), prodromal symptoms (p < 0.001), and the incidence of bilateral fractures (p = 0.010) among the groups, where the incidences of these minor features in the APFF group and the AFF group were higher than those in the typical PFF group. Of note, the duration of fracture healing of APFFs was significantly longer than that of typical PFFs and AFFs (p < 0.001 and p = 0.004, respectively). In addition, the APFF group and the AFF group had higher proportions of patients with rheumatoid arthritis (p = 0.004 and p = 0.027, respectively), bisphosphonate (BP) usage (p = 0.026 and p < 0.001, respectively), and longer duration of BP usage (p = 0.003 and p = 0.007, respectively) than the typical PFF group. Furthermore, significant differences were found in management (p < 0.001) and complication rate (p = 0.020) among the groups, and the rate of complications in the APFF group and the AFF group was higher than that in the typical PFF group.ConclusionsAPFFs not only fulfilled the mandatory and major diagnostic criteria for AFF but also had many clinical characteristics, management and prognosis distinguishing them from typical PFFs but resembling AFFs; hence, the diagnostic criteria for AFF might be revised to incorporate APFF as a distinct subtype of the condition.

Introduction

Atypical femur fractures (AFFs) were first described in 2005 in patients who suffered from low‐energy injury and received alendronate. 1 Since then, in many studies, researchers have taken considerable interest in this new disease entity. Located in the subtrochanteric region or diaphysis of the femur, AFFs are considered a kind of stress or insufficiency fracture with a transverse or short oblique fracture line originating at the lateral cortex. 2 Notably, AFFs are reported to be tightly associated with long‐term usage of bisphosphonate (BP) 3 , 4 , 5 and are known to have a higher incidence of delayed union, nonunion, or implant failure when compared to ordinary femoral fractures. 6 , 7 To standardize the clinical management and research of AFFs, the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research (ASBMR) outlined the specific diagnostic criteria for AFFs in 2010, 8 and revised the criteria in 2013 2 to more clearly delineate the features that distinguish AFFs from ordinary femur fractures (Table 1). Nevertheless, both the original and revised ASBMR criteria for AFFs specifically excluded periprosthetic femoral fractures (PFFs). 2 , 8

PFFs are one of the most severe complications of hip arthroplasty because they may predispose patients to experience poor functional outcomes and high rates of revision and mortality. 9 , 10 Due to the longer life expectancy of patients, expansion of the indications for THA and increasingly wide application of uncemented stem, the incidence of PFFs is increasing with an estimated incidence ranging from 0.7% to 11.1% following primary THA and 1.19% to 18% following revision THA, 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 presenting an enormous challenge for orthopaedic surgeons. Of note, a few published studies have revealed that some patients with PFFs receiving BPs had clinical history and radiographic features closely resembling AFFs. 12 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 These atypical periprosthetic femoral fractures (APFFs) fulfill the mandatory and major diagnostic criteria for AFF except that they themselves are one of the exclusion criteria for AFF (Table 1). Furthermore, the risk of complications in patients with APFFs was higher than in those with typical PFFs, which was consistent with data on AFFs when compared with data on ordinary femoral fractures. 18 , 20 Although an increasing number of studies focusing on APFFs have been performed in recent years, most orthopaedic surgeons may have overlooked or missed these fractures in clinical practice. To our knowledge, the epidemiology, clinical characteristics, management, and prognosis of APFFs remain unclear. Furthermore, there is currently no study that compares APFFs with typical PFFs and AFFs simultaneously to determine the exact relationship among these three fracture types.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was (1) to investigate the prevalence of APFFs among patients with PFFs in a single center and (2) to identify the clinical characteristics, management, and prognosis that distinguish APFFs from typical PFFs and AFFs to further determine the relationship among these three fracture types.

Methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of West China Hospital (No. 2022[915]), and all included patients provided written informed consent before participation. We retrieved all resultant medical records and radiographs of consecutive patients who were treated for fractures of the femur between January 2012 and December 2022 from our institutional database to screen out the eligible patients who had PFFs or AFFs. Specifically, only patients with PFFs around a stem in hip arthroplasty were included in the study. Therefore, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) femur fractures around implants other than hip arthroplasty; (2) intraoperative PFFs; (3) any pathologic PFFs associated with primary or metastatic bone tumors; (4) incomplete data. The Vancouver classification system was applied for the classification of PFFs. 21 After the initial screening, we further excluded type A PFFs whose fracture location involving the trochanteric area was not at the level of AFFs. Vancouver type B3 PFFs, presenting a loose stem and poor bone stock that may contribute to the fractures, were also excluded. PFFs that were finally included and that met at least four out of the five major features according to revised ASBMR diagnostic criteria for AFF 2 were identified as APFFs and classified into the APFF group, while the remaining PFFs were then classified into the typical PFF group. We also reviewed the data of consecutive patients with subtrochanteric or femoral shaft fractures who were injured in the same period and treated at our facility, and patients who met the revised ASBMR diagnostic criteria for AFF 2 were further screened out and classified into the AFF group. Conversely, any patients with femoral fractures due to oncologic disease or those with incomplete data were excluded. To establish reliability, two authors evaluated all radiographs independently, and a third senior author helped to reach a consensus if there was any doubtful case.

Clinical Measurements

All data related to the included patients were carefully reviewed. First, we collected demographic information on the patients, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). To further determine the relationship among typical PFFs, APFFs, and AFFs, other data were extracted with consideration of the diagnostic criteria for AFF (Table 1). In view of the minor features of AFF, 2 generalized increase in cortical thickness, prodromal symptoms, incidence of bilateral fractures as well as the duration of fracture healing were collected and compared among groups. Additionally, comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis, thyroid disease, and neurologic disease, were collected from medical records. We also considered the patients' medication histories available from electronic prescriptions and discharge letters to identify the medication history of BP, glucocorticoid, and proton‐pump inhibitor (PPI) usage, and the duration of BP usage. Finally, we focused on fracture management and complications and compared them among typical PFFs, APFFs, and AFFs.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables, including age, BMI, duration of fracture healing, and duration of BP usage, are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One‐way analysis of variance was used to determine the differences among typical PFFs, APFFs, and AFFs with a post hoc Tukey HSD test for pairwise analysis. For the categorical variables, the data are presented as the number (percentage) and were analyzed using Fisher's exact test with the Bonferroni correction for post hoc multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3), and statistical significance was considered as a p value <0.05.

Results

A total of 117 patients who had PFFs after hip arthroplasty and 27 patients who had AFFs eventually met our criteria and were included in the study. Among these PFFs, 11 fractures were identified as APFFs, and the prevalence of APFFs among patients with PFFs was 9.4% (11/117). The detailed filtration process is depicted in Figure 1.

Demographic Information and Minor Features

There were no significant differences in age (p = 0.249), BMI (p = 0.432), or sex (p = 0.406) among patients with typical PFFs, APFFs, and AFFs. In view of the minor features, significant differences were observed in all four minor features among groups. Specifically, the incidence of generalized increase in cortical thickness in the APFF group (27.3%) and the AFF group (29.6%) was higher than that in the typical PFF group (10.4%). The incidence of prodromal symptoms in the APFF group (54.5%) and AFF group (51.9%) was significantly higher than that in the typical PFF group (21.7%, p = 0.018 and p = 0.010, respectively). In addition, the incidence of bilateral fractures in the typical PFF group (4.7%) was not only lower than that in the APFF group (18.2%) but also significantly lower than that in the AFF group (22.2%, p = 0.028). Of note, the duration of fracture healing in the APFF group (8.5 ± 1.9 months) was significantly longer than that in the typical PFF group (5.7 ± 1.5 months, p < 0.001) and the AFF group (6.3 ± 2.6 months, p = 0.004), while no significant difference was found between the typical PFF group and the AFF group (p = 0.296).

Comorbidities and History of Medication Usage

Regarding comorbidities, no significant differences were found among groups regarding diabetes (p = 0.606), hypertension (p = 0.667), thyroid disease (p = 0.284), or neurologic disease (p = 0.866), while the incidence of rheumatoid arthritis in the APFF group (36.4%) and the AFF group (18.5%) was significantly higher than that in the typical PFF group (2.8%, p = 0.004 and p = 0.027, respectively). Additionally, in terms of medication history, we found that patients in the APFF group (81.8%, 6.8 ± 2.5 years) and in the AFF group (81.5%, 5.8 ± 2.5 years) had a higher rate of BP usage (p = 0.026 and p < 0.001, respectively) and a longer duration of BP usage (p = 0.004 and p = 0.008, respectively) than patients in the typical PFF group (38.7%, 3.9 ± 2.3 years), but no significant differences were found in glucocorticoid usage (p = 0.125) and PPI usage (p = 0.662) among the groups.

Management and Complications

It was not surprising to find that the management in the AFF group was significantly different from that in the typical PFF group (p < 0.001) and APFF group (p < 0.001). AFFs were more likely to be treated with intramedullary nailing (Figure 2), while most typical PFFs and APFFs seemed to be treated with fracture fixation or revision THA. Besides, we found that patients with typical PFFs often received internal fixation or revision THA with simple cerclage (Figure 3), but most patients with APFFs need to further reinforce fixation using cortical strut allografts (Figure 4), and the management in the typical PFFs group was also significantly different from that in the APFFs group (p < 0.001). Moreover, a significant difference was found in the overall complication rate among the groups (p = 0.020), and the rate in the APFF group (18.2%) and the AFF group (18.5%) were higher than that in the typical PFF group (4.7%). Specifically, a significant difference was found in the complication rate of nonunion, while no significant difference was found considering refracture. Anyway, consistent with the overall complication rate, the complication rate of nonunion or refracture in the APFF group and the AFF group were both higher than that in the typical PFF group (Table 2).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the prevalence of APFFs among patients with PFFs and compared the clinical measurements among patients with typical PFFs, APFFs, and AFFs. There were 11 APFFs included in the 117 PFFs, and the prevalence of APFFs among patients with PFFs was 9.4%. Moreover, significant differences were found in all four minor features of AFF, rheumatoid arthritis, BP usage, duration of BP usage, management, and complication rates among the groups, where APFFs not only fulfilled the mandatory and major diagnostic criteria for AFF but also had many clinical characteristics, management, and prognosis distinguishing them from typical PFFs but resembling AFFs. To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study that simultaneously evaluated the exact relationship among these three fracture types, and our outcomes revealed that APFFs might be considered a distinct subtype of AFFs.

The Prevalence and Inclusion Criteria of APFFs among PFFs

Recently, the incidence of PFFs is increasing constantly, 12 thereby potentially increasing the incidence of APFFs indirectly. However, because PFFs have been excluded by the specific definition of AFF, 2 , 8 orthopaedic surgeons may have overlooked or missed these fractures in clinical practice. Apart from several case reports and case series, there were only seven retrospective studies concerning APFFs in the literature, 12 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 and the reported prevalence of APFFs was 5.1% to 12.2% among patients with PFFs 12 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 or 10.7% among those with atypical fractures 15 (Table 3). In our study, the prevalence of APFFs among patients with PFFs was 9.4%. As far as we are concerned, the difference in prevalence found in the literature is due, on the one hand, to variations in study design and limitations of small sample size. On the other hand, this is attributed to the lack of standard diagnostic criteria for APFF, which further aligns with the difference in the inclusion criteria.

The criteria for the inclusion of APFFs based on the Vancouver classification remain controversial (Table 3). Vancouver type A PFFs are located at the trochanteric area, which do not meet the mandatory criteria for AFF and should be excluded. Likewise, there is a consensus that Vancouver type B3 PFFs have a loose stem and poor bone stock that may contribute to fracture and should also be excluded. Vancouver type B1 PFFs occur around a well‐fixed stem, and there is general agreement among authors that atypical fracture patterns could be found in cases of type B1 PFFs. Nevertheless, whether to screen APFFs from Vancouver type B2 PFFs is still unclear. Mondanelli et al. 12 thought that type B2 PFFs have a loose stem that loosens before or at the time of fracture, and this kind of pattern should not be considered atypical. In contrast, several cases of APFFs were found in patients with type B2 PFFs, as reported by Leclerc et al., 17 Schaeffer et al. 22 and Moya‐Angeler et al. 23 In our study, we also found two patients with type B2 PPFs presenting with a clinical history and radiographic features resembling AFFs and classified them into the APFF group. Moreover, sometimes it is difficult to identify whether the stem of a PFF is loose before surgery. Therefore, it is not reasonable to ignore potential APFFs among patients with type B2 PPFs. Vancouver type C PFFs are fractures well below the stem. Although they have been excluded by Lee et al. 16 and Dózsai et al. 19 we believe that once type C PFFs meet the diagnostic criteria for AFF without any other reason to explain the pathogenesis of the fracture itself, they should be included to look for APFFs.

The Clinical Characteristics of APFFs

In our study, APFFs were defined as PFFs that met the mandatory and major diagnostic criteria for AFFs, while none of the minor features were needed. Nevertheless, significant differences were observed in all four minor features among the groups, and the incidences of generalized increase in cortical thickness, prodromal symptoms, and the incidence of bilateral fractures in the APFF group were similar to those in the AFF group but higher than those in the typical PFF group. Of note, it took a significantly longer period for APFFs to achieve fracture healing compared to typical PFFs, which was consistent with AFFs when compared with ordinary femoral fractures. 6 , 7 In addition, it was not surprising to find that the duration of fracture healing of the APFF group was also significantly longer than that of the AFF group, as the long‐term existence of the stem in APFFs might to some extent affect the intramedullary blood supply of the femur, leading to a longer duration of fracture healing. Furthermore, there were significant differences in management among patients with typical PFFs, APFFs, and AFFs. A significant difference was also found in the overall complication rate and the complication rate of nonunion among the groups, where the rates in the APFF group and AFF group were both higher than that in the typical PFF group.

Additionally, according to the original and revised ASBMR criteria for AFFs, some medication usage (e.g., BPs, glucocorticoids, PPIs) and comorbidities (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, thyroid disease) might be considered risk factors for AFFs. In the present study, we observed that patients in the APFF group and in the AFF group both had a significantly higher rate and longer duration of BP usage than patients in the typical PFF group, which is consistent with previous articles. 16 , 18 , 19 , 20 Although BPs have been proven to be beneficial in inhibiting bone resorption and reducing the risk of fragility fractures by inducing osteoclast apoptosis, 24 prolonged BP usage is more likely to suppress bone turnover, further increase the mineralization and brittleness of bone and facilitate the accumulation and propagation of microcracks, 25 , 26 which may predispose patients to the development of atypical fractures. Moreover, this was the first time that the incidence of rheumatoid arthritis in the APFF group and AFF group was significantly higher than that in the typical PFF group. Some previous studies have also revealed that there were significant differences between patients with APFFs and those with typical PFFs in terms of BMI, 18 sex, 20 fixation type of the femoral stem, 20 or interval between PFF and prior hip arthroplasty. 16 However, each of these results occurred in a single study and were not found in other studies concerning this field. More studies are warranted to determine the role of these factors in patients with APFFs.

Given the above findings, the present study demonstrated that APFFs could not only meet the mandatory and major diagnostic criteria for AFF but also have clinical characteristics, management, and prognosis distinguishing them from typical PFFs but resembling AFFs. In our opinion, atypical fracture patterns may occur in femurs with prostheses, and a revision of criteria for AFFs might be considered to include PFFs and accept the existence of APFFs.

The Recommended Management of APFFs

In line with previous studies, APFFs were more prone to delayed healing, nonunion, and refracture than typical PFFs in our study. 18 , 20 However, there is still no guideline for the standard diagnosis and clinical management of APFFs. As mentioned above, APFFs have many clinical characteristics and prognosis resembling AFFs, so we believe that the management strategy used for AFFs could, to some extent, be applied to APFFs.

Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for the treatment of APFFs. In addition to radiographs that display the distinctive radiological features of atypical fractures, it is imperative to thoroughly investigate the patients' medical history, focusing primarily on the mechanism of injury, potential occurrence of prodromal symptoms, medication usage, and existing comorbidities. Specifically, APFFs typically occur spontaneously or result from a low‐energy trauma and could be anticipated by prodromal symptoms such as mild thigh pain or groin pain. Moreover, most APFF patients receive long‐term bisphosphonate therapy and may have a higher susceptibility to rheumatoid arthritis.

Upon identification of an APFF, a comprehensive approach combining conservative treatment and surgery is recommended. It is of vital importance to prioritize non‐weight bearing and discontinue the use of bisphosphonates. Exploring alternative anti‐osteoporotic therapies, such as teriparatide, is advisable. Teriparatide, a synthetic polypeptide hormone, has demonstrated its ability to enhance bone remodeling and expedite healing of AFFs. 27 There are also reports indicating the potential benefits of teriparatide in cases of APFFs with nonunion. 28 Additionally, regular monitoring of calcium, vitamin D, bone alkaline phosphatase, and parathyroid hormone levels is necessary to ensure that they remain within normal ranges.

From a surgical point of view, we found that the management of AFFs was significantly different from that of typical PFFs and APFFs. Intramedullary nailing is the first‐line surgical treatment for AFFs, 29 but it is not suitable for PFFs due to the presence of the prosthetic stem occupying the femoral medullary cavity. As a result, internal fixation or revision THA is regarded as the appropriate surgical management for PFFs. Nevertheless, compared to internal fixation or revision THA with simple cerclage for typical PFFs, the reconstruction of APFFs must provide enough stability to resist axial and rotational forces from transverse or short oblique fracture patterns. Of note, although a long‐locked plate is the most common surgical treatment option for APFFs in the literature, 18 , 19 , 20 we preferred to perform internal fixation or revision THA using cortical strut allografts in our center. Satisfactory clinical outcomes using cortical strut allografts have been widely reported to reconstruct the stability and bone stock in revision THA. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 These allografts have been shown to undergo revascularization, integrate with host bone, and undergo adaptive bone remodeling. 34 Moreover, use of cortical strut allografts could decrease the severity of stress shielding from revised femoral stem and provide it with axial and rotational stability. 30 , 35 Consequently, when it comes to APFFs, we think that cortical strut allografts could not only achieve strong fixation but also be incorporated into the host femur to further accelerate fracture healing and augment the host bone structure, which even makes further revision more manageable if required. Along with the using of cortical strut allografts, it is also advisable to moderately extend the length of the prosthetic stem beyond the fracture site when performing revision THA for APFFs, which might provide supplementary axial and torsional stability. However, the operation technique for APFFs might be more challenging than that for typical PFFs, and there are currently limited data available to fully support our management recommendations. Further rigorous validation and research are necessary to establish a stronger evidence base for the management of APFFs.

Strengths and Limitations

The present study had several limitations. First, this was a single‐center retrospective study, and selection bias was inevitable. But APFFs are an extremely rare pathology, making it challenging to conduct prospective observational studies with significant epidemiological value. Second, similar to previous studies, the number of included patients with APFFs was relatively small, hence the limited statistical power for various comparisons should be taken into account when interpreting the lack of significance observed for many factors.

Despite these limitations, our institution is a leading medical center of West China for the diagnosis and treatment of complicated and severe cases and performs over 3000 joint surgeries per year. As a result, the majority of PFFs, if not all, in the surroundings would visit our outpatient or emergency departments, allowing us to assess a very large and complete population. Most importantly, our study presents the first study to evaluate the exact relationship among APFFs, typical PFFs and AFFs simultaneously. The result clearly showed that APFFs was different from typical PFFs and might be considered a distinct subtype of AFFs, which would be instructive to clinicians, and particularly to those focusing on hip arthroplasty and PFFs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study identified 11 APFFs among 117 patients with PFFs, with a prevalence of 9.4%. It was also demonstrated that APFFs not only fulfilled the mandatory and major diagnostic criteria for AFF but also had many clinical characteristics, ways of managing, and prognoses that distinguish them from typical PFFs but resemble AFFs; hence, the diagnostic criteria for AFF might need to be revised to incorporate APFF as a distinct subtype of the fracture. Considering the infrequent occurrence of APFFs, more well‐designed, larger, multicenter studies are warranted to corroborate our findings and to facilitate a better understanding of this field.
