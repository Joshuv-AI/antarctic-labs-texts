Introduction

Ovarian steroid cell tumors (SCTs) represent a rare type of sex cord-stromal tumor with potential malignancy, comprising under 0.1% of all ovarian tumors [1]. SCT includes three primary subtypes: leydig cell tumors, stromal luteomas, and steroid cell tumors-not otherwise specified (SCT-NOS). SCT-NOS is the most common type, accounting for approximately 60% of SCTs. These tumors predominantly occur in middle-aged patients and are uncommon in younger women [2].The majority of these tumors secrete steroids, primarily testosterone, leading to hyperandrogenism and symptoms such as hair loss, hirsutism, temporal balding, menstrual irregularities or amenorrhea [3]. Although most SCT-NOS have a good prognosis, malignancy has been reported in one-third of these tumors [4]. Five pathological characteristics are strongly linked to malignancy: ≥ 2 mitotic figures per 10 high-power fields, necrosis, hemorrhage, tumor diameter > 7 cm and grade 2–3 atypia [1]. The primary treatment for SCT-NOS is surgery, and patients with malignant tumors should receive adjuvant chemotherapy [5]. Nevertheless, treatment decisions still remain disputable as the lineage is uncertain and the pathophysiology is not fully understood.

To date, most of the studies on ovarian SCT-NOS were case reports. The relatively rare incidence of ovarian SCT-NOS has hindered an exhaustive investigation into its molecular underpinnings. A Chao et al. conducted the first comprehensive genetic analysis of ovarian SCT-NOS using high-throughput sequencing and revealed that dysregulation of the hypoxia signaling pathway was a key molecular feature of ovarian SCT-NOS [6]. Although bulk RNAseq has become one of the most valuable and commonly used tool in tumor classification and diagnosis, biomarker and gene fusion discoveries, predicting prognosis and optimizing therapeutic treatment, the true signals from a rare cell type driving the tumorigenesis could be concealed by an average gene expression profile from bulk RNASeq [7]. Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a groundbreaking technique that allows for the analysis of individual tumor cell expression profiles, offering an unbiased approach to better investigate immune cell states and phenotypes within the complex tumor microenvironment [8, 9]. ScRNA-seq has become one of the most popular genomic tools to distinguish cell types, states and lineages within the context of heterogeneous tumor tissues since 2017 [10]. Moreover, this approach may provide important information for the development of a sequential treatment option, with successful translation in patients [11]. ScRNA-seq provides a great opportunity to comprehensively decipher the treatment and prognosis of ovarian tumors. Xu et al. delineated an ecosystemic landscape of high-grade serous ovarian cancer at early or late stages by scRNA-seq, focusing on the heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment, and they found a set of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition-associated gene signatures was identified as a genetic panel to predict the outcomes of patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer [12].

Although ovarian SCT-NOS have a relatively good prognosis after surgery, a cell atlas of the tumor microenvironment in ovarian SCT-NOS has not been constructed. To a large extent, it remains unknown about the cellular composition, tumor microenvironment, and molecular mechanisms underlying SCT-NOS.

In this study, a rare case of SCT-NOS with hyperandrogenism was presented, and this exploratory single-cell transcriptome analysis of a tumor specimen was performed. We revealed distinct cell types and key genes within the tumor microenvironment. Aberrant gene expression across various cell types in ovarian SCT-NOS was detected and genes involved in ovarian steroidogenesis and steroid biosynthesis pathways were enriched, which was consistent with the pathobiology of hyperandrogenism and advance the understanding of ovarian SCT-NOS biology. This study contributed a comprehensive single-cell transcriptome atlas and contributed into novel insight of intratumor heterogeneity in ovarian SCT-NOS.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimen collection

Our study included one patient with hyperandrogenism. A fresh tumor specimen was obtained during laparoscopic left-sided salpingo-oophorectomy, which was supervised by a qualified gynecologist. The specimen was meticulously dissected under the microscope and confirmed by a qualified pathologist. The detailed clinical information of the case was summarized in Additional file 1.

Preparation and sequencing of a single-cell transcriptome library

Following resection, the specimen was promptly prepared for single-cell RNA sequencing. A single-cell suspension was prepared following the standard protocol for Chromium Single Cell 3’ GEM, Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (10 × Genomics, PN-1000268). The specimen was rinsed twice with cold 1 × phosphate-buffered saline. Cell viability was assessed using a hemocytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The concentration of the single-cell suspension was measured using a Countess (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and adjusted to 1000 cells/μl. Finally, the cells were loaded, and a library was created using a Chromium Single Cell Chip (Chromium Single Cell G Chip Kit, 10 × Genomics, PN-1000120), and then sequenced at a depth of 40,000 reads per cell on the Illumina NovaSeq platform.

Preprocessing and quality control of single-cell RNA sequencing data

Raw count matrices were analyzed in R (v4.3.0) with Seurat (v4.4.0). Cells were considered high quality if they satisfied the following criteria: total UMI counts exceeding 1,500, more than 600 detected genes, and a mitochondrial gene fraction less than 10%. After quality control, 14,688 cells were retained for downstream analysis. These thresholds were selected based on the distribution of QC metrics across all cells (shown as violin plots in the doublet detection method; Appendix Fig. 1A-C), balancing the removal of low-quality or dying cells while preserving biologically meaningful populations. Cell cycle scoring was performed using canonical S phase and G2/M phase gene signatures. However, no strong cell cycle–driven clustering pattern was observed (Appendix Fig. 1D).

Cell phenotype identification through clustering analysis

Gene expression was normalized using the “LogNormalize” method and the top 2,000 variable features were selected. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted, and the top 20 components were utilized for clustering and visualization using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). A clustering resolution of 0.5 was used to identify major cell types. For higher-resolution analysis, Keratin 19 (KRT19)⁺ steroidogenic cell cell was reclustered at a resolution of 0.5, while immune cells were reclustered at a resolution of 1.0. Cell type annotation was conducted using the SingleR package with reference transcriptomic datasets and subsequently verified manually. All cells were categorized into 5 lineages, namely, the T/NK lineage, B lineage, myeloid lineage, stromal cell, and epithelial cell, based on their expression profiles of canonical markers. Cell clusters coexpressing markers of different lineages were removed. Large-scale copy number variations were inferred from single-cell RNA sequencing data using the InferCNV [13].

DEG analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell cluster were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test via the FindAllMarkers function. Genes expressed in at least 50% of cells within a cluster (min.pct = 0.5) and exhibiting a minimum expression difference of 20% between the target cluster and all other cells (min.diff.pct = 0.2) were considered. Genes were retained if they exhibited an average log2 fold change greater than 0.25 and a Bonferroni-adjusted P value < 0.05, and were upregulated in the cluster. The top 10 upregulated genes per cluster were selected based on descending average log2 fold change.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes were subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis via the clusterProfiler package. Enriched KEGG pathways were ranked according to statistical significance (P values), and the top 20 pathways were selected for downstream visualization.

Pseudotime analysis

Cell state transitions and lineage relationships were reconstructed using Monocle3. The algorithm fits a principal graph onto the low-dimensional embedding of single-cell transcriptomes to approximate the underlying developmental manifold, thereby enabling trajectory inference and pseudotime assignment. In accordance with the pseudotime values, Monocle further models gene expression dynamics as smooth, nonlinear functions of pseudotime to capture transcriptional changes during cellular progression. The KRT19+ Ster_IGFBP2 cluster was selected as the candidate starting population based on its transcriptional features, which are consistent with a relatively early state. Within this population, cells were projected onto the UMAP principal graph, and the principal graph node most frequently associated with KRT19+ Ster_IGFBP2 cell was chosen as the root, providing a data-driven definition of the trajectory origin.

Single-cell pathway activity scoring

To assess pathway-level activity across cell populations, single-cell module scores were computed using Seurat. Gene sets corresponding to steroid biosynthesis and ovarian steroidogenesis were assembled based on known pathway annotations. The AddModuleScore function was employed to compute module scores for each cell.

Results

Case description

A 41-year-old woman with a 6-year history of secondary amenorrhea and elevated testosterone levels presented to our hospital. Hormonal tests (using chemiluminescence immunoassay) indicated a progressive increase in her total serum testosterone concentration to 12.07 ng/ml (normal range: 0.1–0.75 ng/ml) on August 20, 2021. Physical examination revealed typical signs of hyperandrogenism, such as hirsutism, acne, clitoromegaly, Tanner stage VI- pubic hair, frontal balding, acanthosis nigricans and abdominal obesity (Fig. 1A–B). The patient’s body mass index was 32.8 kg/m2 (height, 158 cm; weight, 82 kg) and the waist‒hip ratio was 1.04 (waist circumference, 104 cm; hip circumference, 100 cm). Whole-body MRI revealed no enlargement or tumor in the adrenal gland but the left ovary appeared larger than the right ovary. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) revealed a 2.1 cm × 1.5 cm nodular hyperactive lesion with reduced apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values within the left ovary (Fig. 1C–E). On 24th september 2021, ovarian venous sampling under fluoroscopy revealed that the total testosterone concentrations (using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry) were > 248 ng/mL from the left ovarian vein, 13.02 ng/ml from the peripheral vein, and 12.90 ng/ml from the inferior vena cava, suggesting a left-sided ovarian origin of hyperandrogenism. The detailed clinical information of the case is summarized in Appendix1–Table 1 and 2.

The cut surface of the specimen was solid, gray red and brittle (Fig. 2B). Histopathology confirmed an ovarian SCT-NOS (Fig. 2C) without the presence of Reinke crystals. Immunohistochemistry showed the following results: calretinin (+ +), inhibin-α (+ +), and Ki-67 (< 2% +) (Fig. 2D–F).

Single-cell profiling and unbiased clustering of collected specimen

We applied scRNA-seq approach to the selected specimen. Postresection tissue was quickly digested into a single-cell suspension, and the 3′-scRNA-seq protocol (Chromium Single-Cell 3′ v2 Libraries) was impartially applied to the sample. After quality control filtering to remove cells with low gene detection, high mitochondrial gene coverage, and doublet filtration, we constructed a unified cell-by-gene expression matrix of a total of 14,688 individual cells. LogNormalize and PCA were used for unsupervised dimensionality reduction. Cells were clustered using graph-based analysis and visualized with UMAP. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted to identify marker genes for each cell cluster. We conducted somatic large-scale chromosomal copy number variation (CNV) analysis and calculated CNV scores for each cluster to better identify KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell in ovarian SCT-NOS. Our analysis revealed that one cluster exhibited notably lower CNV scores than the others did (Appendix Fig. 2A). Additionally, the cluster expressed KRT19, but showed limited expression of canonical epithelial markers such as keratin 7 (KRT7), mucin 1 (MUC1), keratin 18 (KRT18), and keratin 8 (KRT8). In contrast, the cluster showed expression of genes associated with steroidogenic differentiation, including steroidogenic acute regulatory protein‌ (STAR), cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1), and cytochrome P450 family 17 subfamily A member 1 (CYP17A1), together with sex cord–stromal/steroidogenic markers such as inhibin alpha subunit (INHA) and calbindin 2 (CALB2) (Appendix Fig. 2B).

Figure 4A shows the identification of distinct cell clusters, which were annotated using the following conventional cell lineage gene markers: CD3D for T cell (TC), HLA-DPB1 and LYZ for dendritic cell (DC), VWF and GNG11 for endothelial cell, COL1A2 for fibroblast, MYLK and COL1A21 for muscle cell, and CD163 for tumor-associated macrophage (TAM). Our analysis revealed that the KRT19+ steroidogenic cell cluster was characterized by upregulation of transcription factor teashirt zinc finger homeobox 2 (TSHZ2). Based on the expression profiles of gene markers, we identified seven primary cell groups: TC, KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell, DC, TAM, fibroblast, muscle cell and endothelial cell. Moreover, the KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell group consisted of four distinct subgroups. The exact number and percentage of cells for each identified cluster are shown in Fig. 4B and Appendix Fig. 3. Dot plots were constructed to display the expression of representative marker genes and their percentages across clusters (Fig. 4C). A heatmap illustrating the expression levels of the top 10 marker genes for each identified cell phenotype is shown in Fig. 4D. We identified a distinct expression signal, TSHZ2, associated with the KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell cluster, features with carcinogenic potential in ovarian SCT-NOS tissues [14]. It has been demonstrated that the silence of the TSHZ2 gene may play a critical role in carcinogenesis and might function as a tumor-suppressor gene [15, 16]. A previous study revealed that down-regulation of TSHZ2 correlated with shorter survival of patients with breast cancer and TSHZ2 overexpression inhibited tumor growth and metastasis in mice [17]. Furthermore, a previous study revealed that increased expression of TSHZ2 inhibited cell proliferation and predicted a favorable prognosis for patients with lung adenocarcinoma [18]. Therefore, the upregulated TSHZ2 signal might be associated with the relatively good prognosis of such disease.

ScRNA-seq reveals four cell subgroups of KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell and pseudotime analysis

Notably, the size of the KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell cluster was relatively large. We also identified 4 distinct subtypes of KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell, namely, KRT19+ Ster_IGFBP2, KRT19+ Ster_XIST, KRT19+ Ster_PMEL and KRT19+ Ster_C1QA (Fig. 5A). Their markers are shown in a heatmap (Fig. 5B). Additionally, we performed pseudotime analysis on KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell subsets to identify dynamic cell transition patterns and order cells based on genes that differed between clusters. Pseudotime analysis revealed the transition of KRT19+ Ster_IGFBP2 cell into KRT19+ Ster_XIST, KRT19+ Ster_PMEL and KRT19+ Ster_C1QA cell (Fig. 5C). To explore whether dynamic gene expression programs contribute to the observed cellular plasticity, we profiled pseudotime-dependent transcriptional changes and identified ten representative genes that exhibit distinct expression trajectories along the inferred lineage. We observed the dynamic changes in the expression levels of CYP17A1, ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), ferritin light chain (FTL), glutathione S-transferase alpha 1 (GSTA1), metastasis associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1), nuclear enriched abundant transcript 1 (NEAT1), prostaglandin D2 synthase (PTGDS), renin (REN), ribosomal protein large P1 (RPLP1), and S100 calcium binding protein A6 (S100A6) over pseudotime (Fig. 5D). Insulin-like growth factor binding protein 7 (IGFBP7) and X-inactive-specific transcript (XIST) play dual roles in tumor development, progression, and prognosis. Accumulating studies have suggested that these markers can be upregulated as oncogenic factors in some cancers, whereas in others, they are downregulated and act as tumor suppressors [19–22]. Previous studies have reported that high levels of premelanosome protein (PMEL), complement C1q A chain (C1QA), FTL, MALAT1 and S100A6 are associated with a dismal prognosis in patients with different cancers [23–28]. IGFBP7, CYP17A1, GSTA1 and PTGDS are involved in steroidogenesis [28–31]. Furthermore, KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell marker genes were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, and the top 20 significantly enriched pathway analysis were selected for visualization based on P values. KEGG pathways revealed that the KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell was enriched in ovarian steroidogenesis and steroid biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 6A), which are clinically associated with the pathobiology of hyperandrogenism. The pathway scores of ovarian steroidogenesis and steroid biosynthesis across different KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell subpopulations are shown in Fig. 6B.

ScRNA-seq identifies distinct immune cell types within the tumor microenvironment

Our transcriptional profiles revealed 5 distinct subclusters, namely, TAM, DC, CD8 T cell, γδ T cell and NK T cell, as illustrated in the cell cluster distribution map (Fig. 7A). TAM and DC were the predominant immune cells, comprising more than 50% of the total immune cell population based on the cell cluster frequency distribution. The average expression levels and expression percentages of each gene across different cluster are shown in Fig. 7B, Representative genes shown include: NKG7 for NK T cell, NKG7 and TRDC for γδ T cell, CD8A and CD3D for CD8 T cell, HLA-DRA for DC, and CD163 for TAM. A heatmap illustrating the expression levels of the top 10 marker genes for each subcluster is shown in Fig. 7C.

Discussion

In this study, our index case presented with hyperandrogenism from an ovarian SCT-NOS. During our evaluation for hyperandrogenism, ovarian venous sampling helped to locate the ovarian source of androgens in this patient. Surgery is the most common treatment for this type of ovarian tumor Following laparoscopic left-sided salpingo-oophorectomy, the hyperandrogenism symptoms of the patient fully resolved.

This study presents a pilot single-cell transcriptome analysis characterizing rare ovarian SCT-NOS. This study identifies 7 cell types within the tumor microenvironment, and upregulated steroidogenesis signaling pathways in the oncogenesis of ovarian SCT-NOS. However, predicting biological behavior based solely on histological features is challenging. Although the patient described in this study was tumor-free after 3 years of follow-up, 25% (2/8) of patients experience recurrence after 5 years [6]. Thus, long-term follow-up with periodic measurements of testosterone levels should be pursued in our patient with ovarian SCT-NOS.

The primary sources of androgens are the adrenal glands and ovaries [32]. In females, hyperandrogenism manifests has diverse clinical symptoms such as menstrual irregularities, hirsutism, moderate-to-severe acne, virilization, and androgenic alopecia [33]. When a female presents with hyperandrogenic symptoms, the top differential diagnosis including polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), nonclassical congenital adrenal hyperplasia (NCAH), hyperthecosis, ovarian or adrenal tumors and iatrogenic hyperandrogenism should be taken into consideration. The rarity of ovarian SCT-NOS often leads to delayed diagnosis or misdiagnosis, especially in young women. For example, an 18-year old woman and a 20-year-old woman with ovarian SCT-NOS were mistakenly diagnosed with PCOS and treated with combined oral contraceptives [33, 34]. To prevent misdiagnosis, it is essential to conduct thorough laboratory and imaging tests and involve a multidisciplinary team comprising a gynecologist, endocrinologist, radiologist, and pathologist. Selective venous sampling is advantageous for the localization of endocrine tumors [35, 36]. Histopathology remains the definitive method for diagnosing ovarian SCT-NOS, yet specific immunohistochemistry markers to distinguish it from other sex cord-stromal tumors are still absent and warrant further investigation.

Compared with previous scRNA-seq studies from human ovary, Jones et al. identified three major cell types, which were stromal cell, endothelial cell and pericyte, and four immune cell subtypes, which were T cell, NK cell, macrophage and mast cell in reproductive-age women [37]. While Fan et al. distinguished five major cell types including granulosa cell, mixed theca/stromal cell, smooth muscle cell, endothelial cell, and immune cell [38]. Furthermore, Wagner et al. identified six major cell types, including a oocytes, granulosa cell, immune cell, endothelial cell, perivascular cell, and stromal cell cluster [39]. In this study, we presented a rare case of ovarian SCT-NOS and utilized scRNA-seq for its identification. This study presents a comprehensive inaugural single-cell transcriptome analysis of ovarian SCT-NOS. However, the dominant cell population, stromal cell, in the normal ovary was absent in this case Instead, we identified the largest number cell population, annotated as KRT19⁺ steroidogenic cell. Additionally, withthe tumor microenvironment, TAM contributes to protumorigenic outcomes in cancer by regulating angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis, suppressing the immune response, inducing hypoxia, and promoting tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [40]. Conversely, DC exhibits robust anticancer T-cell responses as the strongest antigen-presenting cells [41].

This study has several limitations. First, in consideration of the rarity of ovarian SCT-NOS, single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from only one patient were presented, and on the one hand, preliminary observations, offer some insight into ovarian SCT-NOS; on the other hand, a sample size of one restricted the generalizability of our findings, including the inability to assess interindividual heterogeneity, subtype variability, and potential effects of sex, age, or sample processing. Thus, more cases are needed to validate and broaden the applicability. Second, the lack of appropriate control samples in our study have restricted the result interpretation of our study, and owing to some objective factors (i.e., neither fresh tissue from this case nor a commercial cell line was available); moreover, the key genes have not been validated in wet-lab experiments, and should be validated in future work. In future studies, we will continue to focus on similar suspicious cases in the clinic. Third, a manual approach (doublet detection methodology) was used in our analysis, which was less rigorous than automated doublet detection was also a limitation of our study.
