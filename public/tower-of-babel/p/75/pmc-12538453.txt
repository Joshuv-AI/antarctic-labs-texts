Laparoscopic management of sealed gastric perforation caused by an ingested foreign body: A case report
Authors: Aniket Mishra, Suvendu Sekhar Jena, Amitabh Yadav, Samiran Nundy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importanceForeign body ingestion is common in prison, psychiatric, and pediatric populations. While most cases resolve without intervention, about 20 % require endoscopic or surgical removal. Perforation, occurring in 1 % of cases, is a serious complication that may spontaneously seal. Background

Ingestion of foreign bodies is usually seen in children and, psychiatric and prison populations, or patients using psychoactive drugs either accidentally or as an attempt to cause self-harm [1]. Eighty percent of ingested foreign bodies pass through the gastrointestinal tract (GI) without causing any symptoms. Rest 20 % of patients may require intervention either endoscopically or surgically [2]. The most dreaded complication following foreign body ingestion is hollow viscus perforation which occurs in about 1 % of the cases. Ingested foreign bodies are usually sharp objects, most commonly fish bones [3]. Sometimes the perforated viscus may get sealed spontaneously by the fibrinous adhesions, the liver capsule, or omentum. Thus foreign body may be left in the peritoneal space causing an abscess. In such cases, mere identification and removal of the foreign body may suffice without any attempt to identify or close the luminal wall defect [3]. Unlike large perforations, foreign body–related perforations are usually pinpoint and may seal rapidly by fibrin deposition and adjacent visceral adhesions [[1], [2], [3]]. This explains why many patients present later with localized abscess or chronic inflammation rather than generalized peritonitis [3]. Open surgical removal may be required owing to dense surrounding adhesions [4]. Here, we report a patient in line with the requirements of the SCARE criteria [5], who had a spontaneously sealed gastric perforation after swallowing a foreign body which was removed laparoscopically.

Case report

A 38-year-old male with no previous comorbidities, presented to medical gastroenterology department with complaints of gradually worsening abdominal pain radiating to the back, nausea, and abdominal distension, associated with high-grade fever since ingestion of his last meal with chicken 24 h earlier. He denied having any sort of addiction or receiving physical trauma. On physical examination, he was conscious, and oriented with stable hemodynamic parameters. Abdominal examination revealed localized tenderness in the epigastrium without any guarding. Blood reports did not show any evidence of sepsis or organ failure. Initial abdominal X ray showed sharp radio-opaque foreign body with no evidence of pneumoperitoneum. Further evaluation with non-contrast computed tomography of the abdomen, revealed a radio-opaque foreign body along the anterior aspect of the pyloric antrum, extending into the liver and a small hypodense air-containing collection with peritoneal fat stranding (Fig. 1). The gastroenterologist decided to perform a therapeutic upper GI endoscopy which showed deep ulceration along the anterior aspect of the pylorus close to the antrum without any evidence of a foreign body (Fig. 2). After the failure of endoscopy to retrieve the foreign body, a diagnostic laparoscopy was performed. It revealed dense adhesions between the anterior wall of the body of the stomach, omentum, and liver capsule along the lesser curvature. After releasing a few adhesions along the liver capsule, copious pus was encountered, suggesting a sealed perforation, and a pointed bone (approximately 0.5 × 1 cm) was retrieved (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). A drain was placed in the subhepatic space and removed on the day of discharge. Due to dense adhesions, the perforation site could not be identified or repaired and was presumed to have sealed spontaneously. Postoperatively, a check CT scan was done on POD 1 which confirmed removal of the previous foreign body with no residual collection. He was managed with a 3-day course of IV antibiotics and allowed to take food orally on postoperative day two and discharged from the hospital on postoperative day 3. On follow up after 3 weeks of surgery, patient was doing well symptomatically and physically.

Discussion

Patients who have ingested foreign bodies accidentally may have varied clinical presentations ranging from being asymptomatic (92 %) or having localized inflammation and abscess formation in the abdomen which occurs at varying times over a period ranging from weeks to months [3,4]. Generalized peritonitis may also occur if the foreign object is sharp with more rapid penetration of the viscera [2]. This happens most commonly at the anatomic angulations in the GI tract, which are most commonly seen in the terminal ileum followed by the duodenal loop, duodenojejunal junction, sigmoid colon, and the appendix [6]. Obstruction, hemorrhage, fistulae and mucosal ulcerations are other reported complications [1]. Making a diagnosis in these patients can be challenging as they may not always give a history of foreign body ingestion and often present with vague symptoms like generalized pain, fever, and loss of appetite mimicking acute appendicitis or diverticulitis [7]. Routine laboratory investigations may show raised serum transaminases, amylase, white cell counts or markers like CRP and ESR owing to perihepatic inflammation [1]. Plain radiography including Computed tomographic images, may not be always able to find the foreign body due to the varied radiolucencies and many times foreign body perforation is an intra operative finding [7]. Therefore, a high index of suspicion is often required.

Symptomatic patients should be further evaluated with radiological tests with a plain abdominal X-ray followed by cross-sectional imaging. Due to the small size and low radio-opacity of the ingested foreign bodies, abdominal X-rays may not be sufficient for a diagnosis. [8]. Thus a CT scan is usually required owing to its high sensitivity and specificity to both diagnose and localize the site of perforation [8].

For the removal of such foreign bodies many methods are available including endoscopy, laparoscopy, and conventional surgery. Based on the clinical condition of the patient and radiological findings, further steps are taken regarding the management. Urgent removal is required in cases with acute abdominal symptoms suggesting perforation or a sharp-edged foreign body longer than 3 cm (even in an asymptomatic patient) as it often carries a high risk for perforation. [7] Likewise, poisonous foreign bodies like pencil batteries or impacted foreign bodies with a high aspiration risk also need urgent removal [9,10].

In patients with foreign bodies lodged intraluminally and no clinical signs of generalized peritonitis, endoscopy is often recommended as the first line of management owing to its minimally invasive nature with a success rate of over 95 % and complication rate of less than 5 %. Various instruments like a Dormia type basket or snares are available to grasp the foreign body. An overtube is also suggested to facilitate the removal of multiple foreign bodies and also to protect the gastrointestinal mucosa from injury [9]. In this case also an endoscopy was tried initially but the foreign body could not be visualized owing to its intramural location.

However, migration of the foreign body into the gastric wall and associated generalized peritonitis, localized abscess, or a luminal wall defect following endoscopic removal may require a laparoscopic procedure. Some case reports recommend open surgery after an initial laparoscopy, due to dense adhesions encountered in these cases [1,2].

The foreign body can penetrate through the anterior wall of the stomach with the perforation site sealed by the fibrin adhesions, omentum, or liver capsule spontaneously [3]. This is also the mechanism being spontaneous closure of gastrostomy defect caused by inadvertent removal of Percutaneous Gastrostomy tubes. This implies that identified and closing localized or sealed perforation defect by laparotomy of laparoscopy may not be necessary [3]. Williams et al. treated a patient with foreign body-caused gastric antral wall perforation (sealed spontaneously) with endoscopic removal without localizing the site of perforation through a laparoscopy or laparotomy [3].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report describing a spontaneously sealed gastric perforation after the ingestion of a foreign body removed laparoscopically without an effort to localize the site of perforation.

Conclusion

Patients who have ingested a foreign body that has caused the perforation of a viscus which has sealed subsequently require careful assessment. A high index of suspicion is often needed for the diagnosis. Computed tomography of the abdomen remains the most sensitive and specific confirmatory test.

In our case, endoscopic retrieval was unsuccessful, and laparoscopic removal of the foreign body with thorough lavage and subhepatic drainage—without attempting to identify or repair the sealed perforation—resulted in complete recovery. This highlights that when a gastric perforation has sealed spontaneously and the patient is stable, minimally invasive intervention without defect repair may be adequate.
