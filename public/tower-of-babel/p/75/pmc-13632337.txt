Integrated bioinformatic multi-omics analysis and experimental verification reveal the oncogenic role of WDHD1 and its underlying mechanism in gastric cancer progression
Authors: Xunkang Wang, Jiahui Yu, Zhenyu Sun, Yi Sun, Kun Li, Kaihan Meng, Junnan Kong, Gan Li, Peiwen Duan, Xiaoqiang Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide. WD repeat and HMG-box DNA-binding protein 1 (WDHD1) has been identified as an oncogenic factor involved in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and several other cancers. However, its role in GC remains insufficiently explored in the existing literature.MethodsIn this study, TCGA, GTEx, and GEO datasets were used to evaluate differences in WDHD1 expression between GC tissues and normal gastric tissues, and to analyze its associations with patient prognosis and clinicopathological characteristics. The CCLE database was used to assess WDHD1 expression levels in GC cell lines. The GDSC and CTRP pharmacogenomic databases were used to investigate the potential relationship between WDHD1 expression and sensitivity to antitumor agents. In addition, the TISCH2 single-cell database was used to analyze the distribution of WDHD1 expression across different cell populations in the GC tumor microenvironment and to preliminarily explore its association with the immune microenvironment. Subsequently, EdU, CCK-8, and colony formation assays were performed to evaluate the effect of WDHD1 on the proliferative capacity of GC cells, and a nude mouse xenograft tumor model was used to validate the regulatory role of WDHD1 in tumor growth in vivo. To further explore the potential underlying mechanisms, comparative proteomic analysis and cell-cycle flow cytometry were performed in WDHD1 knockdown cells and control cells, thereby preliminarily revealing the possible molecular mechanisms by which WDHD1 contributes to GC progression.ResultsWDHD1 was significantly overexpressed in GC, and its high expression was associated with poor prognosis and showed favorable diagnostic value. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that high WDHD1 expression was mainly associated with enrichment of pathways related to the cell cycle, DNA replication, and cell proliferation. In GC, WDHD1 downregulation exerted tumor-suppressive effects, inhibited DNA synthesis, and induced cell-cycle redistribution.ConclusionWDHD1, as a key factor regulating DNA replication and cell cycle progression during GC progression, may serve as a potential molecular target for diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic intervention in GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16401-8.

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health challenge and is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a particularly high burden in East Asia and other high-incidence regions [1]. Although the overall incidence of GC has declined in certain areas, the increasing trend of young-onset GC has attracted growing attention, highlighting an evolving epidemiological pattern [2]. In addition to established risk factors, emerging evidence indicates that lifestyle-related factors may also influence the risk of digestive system cancers, further reflecting the multifactorial nature of GC development [3]. Owing to the lack of specific symptoms at the early stage, many patients are still diagnosed at a locally advanced or advanced stage, which limits the opportunity for curative intervention and contributes to poor clinical outcomes. At present, the diagnosis and clinical stratification of GC are still largely based on histopathological evaluation and conventional clinical indicators [4]. However, with the rapid progress in molecular classification, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, the profound molecular heterogeneity of GC has been increasingly recognized, and traditional diagnostic and stratification approaches are no longer sufficient to satisfy the needs of precision oncology. Therefore, the identification of robust molecular biomarkers with diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic relevance is of considerable importance for improving early detection and guiding clinical decision-making in GC. In parallel, advances in gene expression profiling, transcriptome sequencing, and multi-omics bioinformatics analyses have provided powerful tools for the systematic discovery of key molecular drivers and potential therapeutic targets in GC [5].

Among the molecules implicated in tumor progression, WD repeat and HMG-box DNA-binding protein 1 (WDHD1) has attracted increasing attention in recent years as a potential therapeutic target in cancer. The WDHD1 gene is located on chromosome 14 (14q22.2–q22.3) and encodes a multifunctional protein consisting of 1,129 amino acids, with a molecular weight of approximately 126 kDa. This protein contains an N-terminal WD40 repeat domain, a central SepB domain, and a C-terminal HMG-box domain. WDHD1 is an evolutionarily conserved natural chimera, and its multiple functional domains make it a key regulatory factor in DNA replication [6, 7]. WDHD1 also plays an important role in cell cycle regulation, and its functional deficiency can lead to G1/S-phase arrest [8]. In various tumors, aberrant WDHD1 expression may lead to dysregulated DNA replication and exert oncogenic effects. Previous studies have shown that high WDHD1 expression is closely associated with the tumorigenesis and progression of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, triple-negative breast cancer, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma [9–11].

Pan-cancer analyses of WDHD1 have provided important insights into its genomic characteristics and biological functions across multiple cancer types, suggesting that WDHD1 may play a significant oncogenic role [12]. However, the role of WDHD1 in GC remains largely unexplored. To address this knowledge gap, we systematically integrated multi-omics datasets from TCGA, GTEx, GEO, and other public resources to comprehensively analyze WDHD1 in GC. We investigated not only the expression pattern, prognostic significance, and diagnostic value of WDHD1, but also its associated pathways and potential regulators. These multi‑omics analyses provided consistent evidence that WDHD1 is significantly upregulated in GC and associated with key pathways related to cell cycle progression and DNA replication. We therefore proceeded to experimentally validate the biological functions of WDHD1 through in vitro and in vivo assays.

Specifically, we performed functional validation in GC cell lines and a mouse xenograft model. We further explored the potential mechanisms by which WDHD1 contributes to the occurrence and progression of GC through proteomic profiling. Materials and methods

Data sources and processing

We obtained immunofluorescence images of WDHD1 from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) to examine its subcellular localization [13]. Differences in WDHD1 protein expression between tumor and normal tissues across multiple cancer types were obtained from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) database (https://proteomics.cancer.gov/programs/cptac) [14]. RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for 33 cancer types from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) [15]. Because some cancer types lacked sufficient normal control samples, normal tissue expression data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database were also obtained through the University of California Santa Cruz Xena platform (UCSC Xena) (https://xena.ucsc.edu/) [16, 17]. TCGA and GTEx RNA-seq data were uniformly processed via the Toil pipeline to generate TPM values. For downstream analyses including group comparison, survival analysis, and correlation analysis, TPM values were log2-transformed. Raw count matrices were reserved for differential expression analysis using DESeq2, edgeR, and limma-voom. The TCGA-STAD cohort was used as the primary discovery cohort for differential expression analysis, survival analysis, diagnostic performance evaluation, functional enrichment analysis, and co-expression network analysis.

The GSE66229 and GSE13911 datasets from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database were used for external validation [18]. GEO expression matrices were extracted using the GEOquery package, and sample phenotype information was obtained using pData(). Probe IDs were converted to gene symbols according to the corresponding platform annotation files. For genes matched by multiple probes, the probe with the highest mean expression across all samples was selected to represent the gene expression level. GEO expression data were also log2-transformed prior to diagnostic validation. Single-cell data from the Tumor Immune Single-Cell Hub 2 (TISCH2) database (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/) were used to analyze WDHD1 expression across different cell types [19]. The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [20], Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) [21, 22], Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) [23], Profiling Relative Inhibition Simultaneously in Mixtures (PRISM) [24], and Biomarker Exploration of Solid Tumors (BEST) databases were used for analyses of cell line characteristics, drug sensitivity, and immunotherapy-related features [25]. All statistical analyses were performed using R programming language (version 4.2.1), and visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package (version 3.3.6). Because all data used in this study were obtained from publicly available databases, no additional ethical approval or informed consent was required.

Differential expression analysis

To evaluate differences in WDHD1 expression between tumor and normal tissues, only cancer types containing both tumor and normal samples were included in the pan-cancer analysis of the combined TCGA and GTEx dataset. Within each cancer type, WDHD1 expression values were standardized using z-score normalization, and samples with extreme outliers (|z-score| > 3) were removed. Differences in WDHD1 expression between tumor and normal tissues were then compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For patients in TCGA with both tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues, paired samples were matched according to unique patient identifiers, and paired expression differences were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. These pan-cancer comparisons were performed as exploratory analyses, and two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the GEO external validation cohorts, WDHD1 expression values were extracted, and samples were classified into Tumor and Normal groups according to phenotype information. Differences between the two groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the results were presented as box plots.

To further identify differentially expressed genes in STAD, samples were classified into tumor and normal groups according to the sample type codes at positions 14–15 of the TCGA barcode. Raw count matrices were applied for differential expression analysis using DESeq2, edgeR, and limma-voom. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. Genes with the false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (log₂FC) | > 1 were considered differentially expressed. Upregulated and downregulated genes commonly identified by all three methods were defined as candidate differentially expressed genes. Principal component analysis was used to evaluate the overall distribution of tumor and normal samples, volcano plots were used to visualize fold changes and statistical significance of differentially expressed genes, and Venn diagrams were used to show the overlap among results obtained from different methods.

Survival analysis and diagnostic model

Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups according to the median WDHD1 expression level for Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. The R packages “survival” and “survminer” were used to analyze the associations between WDHD1 expression and multiple prognostic endpoints, including overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-free interval (DFI). Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis combined with univariate Cox regression was performed to determine whether WDHD1 functions as a protective or risk factor. A comprehensive survival landscape of WDHD1 was generated, and the results of the Cox regression analyses were visualized using the “forestplot” package to improve interpretability. Because the survival analyses were focused on a predefined target gene, two-sided P values were reported, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, the “pROC” package was used to calculate the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to evaluate the diagnostic potential of WDHD1 in cancer [26]. KM and Cox analyses were performed using the R package “survival”, and survival curves were visualized using “survminer” [27]. For ROC validation in external datasets, GEO datasets were downloaded in MINiML format, including platform and sample information. When necessary, raw expression data were log2-transformed and normalized using the normalize.quantiles function in the “preprocessCore” R package. Probe IDs were annotated to gene symbols according to the corresponding platform files; probes ambiguously mapped to multiple genes were discarded, and the average expression value was used when multiple probes corresponded to the same gene. Batch effects within or across datasets/platforms were corrected using the removeBatchEffect function in the “limma” R package. Data preprocessing quality was assessed by boxplots and principal component analysis (PCA). After preprocessing, ROC curve analysis was performed in the GEO validation cohorts using the “pROC” package to further evaluate the diagnostic performance of WDHD1.

Functional enrichment analysis

To explore pathways associated with WDHD1, samples were dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups based on the median WDHD1 expression level in each dataset. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified using the R package “limma” [28]. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for multiple testing correction, and genes with an FDR < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 were defined as DEGs. All genes were ranked according to log₂ fold change values for subsequent enrichment analyses. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA function in the “clusterProfiler” package, based on Hallmark gene sets [29] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) metabolic pathways [30, 31]. To evaluate the relationship between WDHD1 and functional states of cancer cells, gene sets covering 14 cellular functional states were obtained from the CancerSEA database [32]. Enrichment scores for each functional state were calculated using the z-score algorithm in the “GSVA” package [33]. Pearson correlation analysis was then performed between WDHD1 expression and each functional state score. For multiple comparisons across different functional states, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was adopted to control the FDR. An FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Identification of potential transcription factors

To investigate the potential transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying WDHD1 expression, seven databases—HTFtarget, JASPAR, ChIP-Atlas, FIMO_JASPAR, GTRD, KnockTF, and ENCODE—were utilized to predict candidate transcription factors (TFs). Differentially expressed genes were filtered using a threshold of |log₂FC| ≥ 0.5. The predicted TFs from all seven platforms were then intersected to identify key transcription factors consistently predicted across datasets [33]. The final set of candidate TFs was visualized using Venn diagrams.

Identification of WDHD1-related genes and pathway enrichment analysis

The “limma” package [28] in R was used to identify DEGs between STAD and normal tissues, and to calculate the log2 fold change (log2FC) between the WDHD1 high- and low-expression groups. Multiple testing correction for DEG analysis was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and genes with FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. In the external validation datasets, normalized expression values were further transformed into Z-scores for cross-sample comparison and visualization. Heatmaps were generated using the “pheatmap” package in R to visualize high-frequency genes functionally associated with WDHD1. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was subsequently performed on TCGA samples to identify gene modules correlated with WDHD1 expression [34]. Consensus DEGs, key module genes identified by WGCNA, and differentially expressed proteins derived from proteomic analyses were then integrated, and overlapping genes were identified using Venn diagrams. Finally, functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses, were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package [30]. For GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

To analyze the distribution of WDHD1 expression across different cell types in the GC tumor microenvironment, single-cell transcriptomic analysis was performed using the TISCH2 database. The GC-related single-cell RNA-seq datasets STAD_GSE167297 and STAD_GSE134520 were selected for analysis, and cell populations were classified according to the fine cell type annotations provided by the TISCH2 platform. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for dimensionality reduction and visualization of the single-cell transcriptomic data, with different colors representing distinct cell types. WDHD1 expression data were then extracted for each cell population to show its distribution across different cell types, and the average expression level in each cell cluster was calculated. Differences in WDHD1 expression among different cell types were visualized using bar plots.

Analysis of drug sensitivity and immunotherapy response associated with WDHD1

Drug sensitivity data from the PRISM, CTRP, GDSC, and GDSC2 databases were integrated to evaluate the association between WDHD1 expression and responses to various anticancer agents using Spearman correlation analysis. For analyses involving multiple drugs, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate, and FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subsequently, the Connectivity Map (CMap) database was used to identify candidate small-molecule compounds capable of reversing WDHD1-related gene expression signatures. Drug sensitivity scores were further calculated based on STAD transcriptomic data to determine the most promising therapeutic compounds for GC. In addition, the IMvigor210 and Wolf immunotherapy cohorts were used to assess the relationship between WDHD1 expression and the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-L1 treatment, thereby evaluating the potential of WDHD1 as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response.

Cell culture

The human GC cell lines AGS and HGC-27 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). AGS cells are derived from gastric adenocarcinoma, whereas HGC-27 cells are derived from undifferentiated GC. These two cell lines differ in their differentiation status and biological background, thereby reflecting, to some extent, the heterogeneity of GC cell models. Public database analyses showed that both cell lines exhibited relatively high WDHD1 expression levels and dependency features. Therefore, AGS and HGC-27 were selected as in vitro models to validate the functional role of WDHD1 in GC.

Lentivirus transduction

To establish stable WDHD1 knockdown GC cell lines, shRNA sequences targeting WDHD1 and a negative control sequence were cloned into the PGMLV-SC5 vector. The shRNA sequence targeting WDHD1 was ACTTAGTCGAACTACAAATAA, and the negative control sequence was TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT. AGS and HGC-27 cells were transduced with lentivirus for 72 h, after which the efficiency of WDHD1 knockdown was assessed. Subsequent in vitro functional experiments used the sh-NC group as the control group and the sh-WDHD1 group as the experimental group.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a SYBR Green PCR Master Mix kit (Yeason, Shanghai, China) and the following primers: GAPDH, 5ʹ-ACCCACTCCTCCACCTTTGAC-3ʹ (forward), 5ʹ-TCCACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAG − 3ʹ (reverse); WDHD1, 5ʹ-AGGAGGAGGAAGATGGTCAAGAAG-3ʹ (forward), 5ʹ- GTGAGTGAGATGCAACGGTGTAG − 3ʹ (reverse).

Western blot

All cell samples were washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). Subsequently, 20 µg of protein from each sample was boiled in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer, separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred onto an immobilon polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). The membranes were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Beyotime, China) prepared in Tris-buffered saline with Tween (TBST).

Primary antibodies against β-actin, GAPDH and WDHD1 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) were used at a concentration of 1 µg/mL, followed by incubation with alkaline phosphatase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Protein bands were visualized and analyzed using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Western blot (WB) band intensities were quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software. First, the grayscale intensity values of the WDHD1 band and the corresponding internal reference protein band, β-actin or GAPDH, were measured for each sample after background subtraction. The WDHD1 band intensity was then divided by the intensity of the internal reference protein to obtain the normalized relative WDHD1 protein expression level. Finally, the relative expression level in the sh-WDHD1 group was compared with that in the sh-NC control group to evaluate WDHD1 knockdown efficiency.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was examined with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Dojindo, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. WDHD1 knockdown and control GC cells were seeded in 96‑well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well. After overnight attachment, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. Before measurement, cells were incubated with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% CCK-8 solution for 1 h. The effect of WDHD1 knockdown on GC cell viability was evaluated by comparing changes in absorbance between the sh-WDHD1 and sh-NC groups.

Colony formation

For the colony formation assay, 1 × 103 GC cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured continuously for 1 week. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with crystal violet for 30 min. Colonies were imaged and counted by ImageJ software to compare differences in colony-forming ability between the sh-WDHD1 and sh-NC groups.

EdU incorporation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using a 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay followed by flow cytometry. Proliferating cells were labeled with 50 µM EdU for 3 h using the Cell-Light EdU Apollo488 In vitro Flow Cytometry Kit (RiboBio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. After incubation, the cells were harvested, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. Subsequently, intracellular EdU was stained with Apollo fluorescent dye. Fluorescence signals at 488 nm were collected by flow cytometry, and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified with FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC). The effect of WDHD1 knockdown on DNA synthesis and cell proliferation was evaluated by comparing the proportion of EdU-positive cells between the sh-WDHD1 and sh-NC groups.

Xenograft model study

Animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Naval Medical University (China). To establish the xenograft model, ten 5-week-old female nude mice (SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 5 per group) using a computer-generated random number sequence. Mice injected with HGC-27 cells stably transfected with negative control shRNA served as the control group (sh-NC), whereas mice injected with HGC-27 cells stably transfected with WDHD1 shRNA served as the experimental group (sh-WDHD1). sh-NC or sh-WDHD1 HGC-27 cells (8 × 10⁶) were collected, resuspended in 0.1 mL PBS, and subcutaneously injected into the axillary region of mice. Tumor volumes were measured every other day using calipers and calculated as 0.5 × width2 × length. Tumor measurements were performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation, and data were unblinded only after completion of all measurements. Mice were euthanized at the experimental endpoint (day 28 post-inoculation). For euthanasia, mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in oxygen at a flow rate of 0.5 L/min until deep anesthesia was achieved, as confirmed by loss of the righting reflex, followed immediately by cervical dislocation in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Death was confirmed by respiratory arrest and absence of heartbeat. Xenograft tumors were excised immediately after euthanasia and processed for staining.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Proteins were extracted from AGS cells in the sh-NC control group and sh-WDHD1 experimental group and digested with trypsin. Cell lysates were reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol at 55 °C for 30 min, and cysteine residues were alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. After reduction and alkylation, trypsin was added at an enzyme-substrate ratio of 1:100 (w/w), and digestion was performed overnight at 37 °C. The resulting peptides were separated by high-pH reverse-phase liquid chromatography. Each sample was collected into 60 fractions, which were then combined into 10 fractions. Each fraction was dissolved in 0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile, and 98% H2O, and then analyzed by Orbitrap Astral (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using DIA-NN for protein identification and quantification. Significantly differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified based on a threshold of |fold change| > 2 and an FDR < 0.05. GO and KEGG pathway analysis were performed using R software.

Cell cycle analysis

To investigate the effect of WDHD1 on cell cycle progression in GC cells, cell cycle distribution was analyzed using a Cell Cycle Detection Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, AGS and HGC-27 cells stably transfected with sh-NC or sh-WDHD1 were first subjected to serum starvation for cell cycle synchronization. After synchronization, cells were treated under normal culture conditions for 24 h. Cells were then harvested at 24 h and 48 h after treatment for cell cycle analysis. Subsequently, cells were fixed, stained with propidium iodide using the Solarbio cell cycle detection kit, and analyzed by flow cytometry. The proportions of cells in the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases were calculated using the corresponding analysis software, and differences in cell cycle distribution between the sh-WDHD1 and sh-NC groups were compared. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0, USA) and R software (version 4.2.1), as appropriate. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical tests were two-sided. Quantitative data from in vitro experiments were obtained from at least three independent biological replicates and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. For two-group comparisons in in vitro functional assays, the CCK-8 assay was analyzed using two-way ANOVA to evaluate the effects of group and time on changes in cell viability; when appropriate, between-group comparisons at individual time points were further performed. Other two-group comparisons, including qRT-PCR, Western blot quantification, EdU-positive cell proportion, colony number, and the proportions of cells in each cell cycle phase, were analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. In the in vivo xenograft experiment, terminal tumor weight and terminal tumor volume were compared using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, whereas tumor growth curves were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was represented by P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), and P < 0.0001 (****).

Results

Expression and mutation landscape of WDHD1

Analysis of immunofluorescence images downloaded from the HPA database revealed that WDHD1 predominantly localizes in the nucleus and exhibits a consistent nuclear distribution pattern across multiple tumor cell lines (Fig. 2A). The expression profiles of WDHD1 across various organs are shown in Fig. 2B. Pan-cancer analyses based on TCGA and GTEx revealed that WDHD1 is broadly upregulated in diverse tumor tissues (Fig.S1A, B), and CPTAC data further confirmed significantly elevated WDHD1 protein levels in several solid tumor types (Fig. 2C). Pan-cancer genomic profiling using the TCGA cohort in cBioPortal further revealed that WDHD1 harbors gene mutations or amplifications in multiple cancer types, with missense mutations representing the most frequent class of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) (Fig. S1C–E).

In STAD, multi-omics profiling highlighted a pronounced dysregulation of WDHD1. Analysis of the GSE66229 and GSE13911 cohorts showed that WDHD1 expression was markedly increased in tumor tissues compared with normal controls (Fig. 2D). PCA analysis demonstrated a clear separation between tumor and normal samples, indicating strong group differences and high data reliability (Fig. 2E). Differential expression analyses using DESeq2, edgeR, and limma-voom identified a substantial number of dysregulated genes. Among these, 1747 upregulated genes and 1991 downregulated genes were consistently identified across all three methods (Fig. 2F). The volcano plots further revealed that WDHD1 was significantly upregulated, suggesting its potential role in promoting STAD progression. (Fig. 2G).

In conclusion, WDHD1 demonstrates elevated expression levels and significant genomic alterations in STAD as well as in various other malignancies. These findings suggest that WDHD1 plays a crucial role in STAD tumorigenesis.

Prognostic significance and diagnostic value of WDHD1

Comprehensive clinical analyses indicated that WDHD1 was significantly associated with both prognosis and diagnostic performance across multiple human cancers. Pan-cancer survival analyses integrating OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI consistently demonstrated that elevated WDHD1 expression was associated with poor clinical outcomes in the majority of tumors (Fig. 3A). Forest plot analyses further quantified this association, revealing that elevated WDHD1 expression significantly increased mortality risk in multiple cancers (Fig. 3B). In terms of diagnostic performance, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis based on TCGA and TCGA-GTEX datasets demonstrated that WDHD1 effectively distinguishes tumor tissues from normal tissues across various cancer types (Fig. 3C). Notably, several cancer types achieved area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.90, indicating excellent diagnostic accuracy (Fig. S2A–X). Notably, this diagnostic model was constructed using tissue transcriptomic data and cannot be extrapolated to serum or liquid biopsy scenarios.

Focusing on STAD, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses revealed that elevated WDHD1 was significantly associated with poorer clinical outcomes. Specifically, high WDHD1 expression correlated with worse OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI, suggesting that WDHD1 overexpression is consistently associated with a poor prognosis in STAD (Fig. 3D). Further clinical correlation heatmap analyses demonstrated a significant association between WDHD1 expression and patient age (Fig. 3E).

In addition, WDHD1 demonstrated outstanding diagnostic performance in STAD, highlighting its potential clinical significance for tumor detection (Fig. 3F). To further assess its prognostic utility, a nomogram integrating WDHD1 expression and key clinicopathological variables was constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5- years overall survival in patients with STAD. WDHD1 contributed significantly to the overall risk score, indicating its strong and independent prognostic impact. Calibration curve analyses revealed a high degree of concordance between nomogram-predicted and observed survival probabilities at all evaluated time points, thereby supporting the robustness and reliability of the WDHD1-based prognostic model (Fig. 3G–H).

Drug sensitivity and immunotherapy response associated with WDHD1 in STAD

Radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy are major treatment modalities for cancer. We evaluated the associations between WDHD1 expression and treatment-related response indicators in STAD. Drug sensitivity analyses based on multiple pharmacogenomic databases indicated that high WDHD1 expression was negatively correlated with sensitivity to various anticancer drugs, suggesting that the overexpression of WDHD1 might reduce the therapeutic response to specific drugs (Fig. 4A). In addition, the Connectivity Map (CMap) reverse perturbation analysis identified several compounds, such as X4.15.Dianilinophthalimide, arachidonyltrifluoromethane and W.13, that showed a strong negative enrichment score relative to the WDHD1-associated transcriptional signature. These findings suggest that these compounds may potentially reverse WDHD1-associated transcriptional signatures and therefore represent candidate agents for further validation (Fig. 4B). The therapeutic potential of these candidate compounds was further supported by drug sensitivity scores calculated from STAD transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 4C).

Taken together, these results suggest that GATA1 may be potentially associated with changes in WDHD1 expression in STAD.

Stable WDHD1 knockdown cell models were successfully constructed via lentiviral transduction. Both qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed markedly decreased WDHD1 expression in sh-WDHD1 groups compared with sh-NC controls, verifying satisfactory knockdown efficiency (Fig. 9C, D).

WDHD1 knockdown suppresses HGC-27–derived tumor growth in vivo

To further verify the oncogenic role of WDHD1 in GC tumorigenesis, a subcutaneous xenograft mouse model was established using HGC-27 cells stably transfected with sh-NC or sh-WDHD1. Consistent with the in vitro findings, xenograft tumors derived from sh-WDHD1 cells were visibly smaller than those from the sh-NC group (Fig. 11A). Tumor growth curves confirmed that WDHD1 knockdown significantly retarded in vivo tumor growth throughout the experimental period (Fig. 11B). At the experimental endpoint, both tumor weight and tumor volume were significantly reduced in the sh-WDHD1 group compared with the sh-NC group (Fig. 11 C, D). Immunohistochemical staining further validated the knockdown efficiency and proliferative status of xenograft tumors. The average optical density (AOD) of WDHD1 staining was significantly lower in sh-WDHD1 tumors, confirming successful WDHD1 silencing in vivo (Fig. 11E). Moreover, the proportion of Ki-67-positive proliferative cells was markedly decreased in the WDHD1 knockdown group, indicating suppressed tumor proliferative activity (Fig. 11 F). Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that WDHD1 facilitates GC tumor growth, and WDHD1 depletion significantly inhibits xenograft tumor progression.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEPs was subsequently performed. Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP) enrichment showed that DEPs were primarily enriched in cell division-associated processes, such as sister chromatid cohesion. Notably, multiple immune-suppressive biological processes were also enriched, including negative regulation of monocyte differentiation and negative regulation of myeloid leukocyte differentiation. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed the core regulatory networks mediated by WDHD1. WDHD1 knockdown significantly altered classic cancer-related pathways governing cell proliferation and apoptosis, as well as tumor microenvironment remodeling pathways, including focal adhesion and TGF-β signaling. Importantly, metabolic reprogramming pathways (glycerophospholipid and sphingolipid metabolism), the ferroptosis cell death pathway, and homologous recombination-mediated genomic stability pathways were significantly enriched. Furthermore, complement and coagulation cascades and mannose-type O-glycan biosynthesis pathways were differentially enriched, indicating potential roles of WDHD1 in immune regulation and protein glycosylation modification in GC (Fig. 12E).

WDHD1 knockdown disrupted cell cycle progression in GC cells

Combined with multi-omics bioinformatics and proteomic findings, we further investigated whether the antiproliferative effect of WDHD1 knockdown was mediated by cell cycle dysregulation. Cell cycle distribution was detected by flow cytometry after serum starvation-induced cell cycle synchronization. In AGS cells, WDHD1 knockdown induced significant cell cycle redistribution at both 24 h and 48 h post-treatment, characterized by a decreased proportion of G0/G1-phase cells and an increased proportion of S-phase and G2/M-phase cells (Fig. 13A–D). Consistent alterations were observed in HGC-27 cells. At 24 h after synchronization, the proportions of S-phase and G2/M-phase cells were significantly elevated in the sh-WDHD1 group, while the G0/G1-phase proportion showed no significant difference (Fig. 13E, F). At 48 h, the G0/G1-phase population was further reduced, accompanied by sustained accumulation of cells in the S and G2/M phases (Fig. 13G, H). These results indicate that WDHD1 depletion induces cell cycle arrest and disrupts orderly cell cycle progression in GC cells, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the expression pattern, biological function, and potential molecular mechanisms of WDHD1 in GC by integrating multi-database bioinformatics analyses, single-cell transcriptomic analysis, proteomic profiling, and both in vitro and in vivo functional experiments. The results showed that WDHD1 was significantly upregulated in GC tissues and was closely associated with unfavorable prognosis. Further functional experiments demonstrated that WDHD1 knockdown markedly inhibited the proliferation, colony-forming ability, and DNA replication capacity of GC cells, and significantly suppressed tumor growth in a nude mouse xenograft model. Notably, flow cytometric analysis performed after cell cycle synchronization further confirmed that WDHD1 knockdown induced abnormal cell cycle distribution in GC cells, suggesting that WDHD1 plays an important role in cell cycle regulation. Together, these findings indicate that WDHD1 exerts a clear oncogenic role in GC progression and may represent an important molecular event in gastric carcinogenesis.

Our findings are generally consistent with previous reports showing that WDHD1 exerts oncogenic effects in multiple tumor types. Previous studies have shown that WDHD1 is aberrantly overexpressed in several malignancies, including esophageal cancer and non-small cell lung cancer, and is closely associated with cell proliferation, DNA damage response, and tumor progression [35, 36]. In addition, previous studies have suggested that WDHD1 deficiency is associated with cell cycle abnormalities, impaired DNA replication, and dysregulation of checkpoint control [37–39]. The present study further extends these observations to GC and, through CCK-8, colony formation, EdU, and flow cytometric cell cycle analyses, demonstrates from multiple perspectives that WDHD1 is closely associated with the proliferative capacity of tumor cells and cell cycle progression. Compared with previous studies that mainly focused on other cancer types, this work more systematically reveals the biological significance of WDHD1 in GC from multiple dimensions, including expression, prognosis, in vitro and in vivo function, and multi-omics characteristics.

From a mechanistic perspective, our findings suggest that the oncogenic role of WDHD1 is closely associated with cell cycle progression and DNA replication. Consistent enrichment of WDHD1-related molecules in cell cycle- and replication-associated pathways was observed in both differential gene analysis and proteomic profiling. In the present study, we further employed proteomic profiling to systematically examine alterations in the protein expression landscape of GC cells following WDHD1 knockdown, with enrichment analysis of DEPs revealing significant changes in multiple core cancer-related signaling pathways. Notably, our proteomic data revealed a previously uncharacterized regulatory feature of WDHD1. Specifically, WDHD1 knockdown significantly activated the ferroptosis pathway. This finding indicates that the biological functions of WDHD1 in GC are not limited to the canonical regulation of cell proliferation; instead, it also participates in the multi-layered modulation of cell death and cellular stress responses. Ferroptosis is a pivotal biological process that governs tumor initiation, progression and therapeutic response across various human cancers. Excessive intracellular iron accumulation triggers lipid peroxidation, impairs cell membrane integrity, and ultimately inhibits the proliferation of tumor cells [40]. Cumulative studies have confirmed extensive crosstalk between cell cycle progression and the ferroptosis pathway. For instance, GINS4 is a key molecule regulating DNA replication initiation and G1/S phase transition in eukaryotic cells, and it has been proven to modulate ferroptosis in lung adenocarcinoma. Knockdown of GINS4 effectively induced ferroptosis in tumor cells at multiple cell cycle phases, with the most pronounced effect observed in G2/M phase cells [41]. In addition, dysregulated iron metabolism in the tumor microenvironment (TME) activates ferroptosis, aggravates local inflammatory reactions, and eventually promotes tumor immune evasion [42–44]. Collectively, our study identifies an unreported potential of WDHD1 in regulating ferroptosis. Further exploration of WDHD1-mediated ferroptosis and its downstream effects on cell cycle progression and tumor immune infiltration will help to fully clarify the oncogenic regulatory network of WDHD1. Beyond the modulation of cell cycle and ferroptosis, transcription factor prediction analysis suggested that GATA-binding protein 1 (GATA1) may serve as a potential upstream regulator of WDHD1 expression. Given the established roles of GATA1 in transcriptional regulation and cell proliferation [45–48], this finding provides a clue for further exploring the upstream regulatory mechanisms underlying aberrant WDHD1 expression. Furthermore, the immune-related analyses in this study showed that WDHD1 expression was correlated with certain immune infiltration indicators and immune-related features, suggesting that WDHD1 may be associated with the tumor microenvironment status in GC. Previous studies have shown that immune and stromal components within the GC tumor microenvironment can influence antitumor immunity, treatment response, and sensitivity to immunotherapy [49–51]. In addition, studies in other cancer types have also suggested that immune-related molecular features, cancer stemness-related regulators, and stromal- or cytokine-associated alterations may be associated with treatment response [49, 52, 53]. Overall, WDHD1 is more likely to serve as a key node involved in cell cycle progression, DNA replication, and proliferative programs in GC. From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that WDHD1 has potential diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic value. On the one hand, the significant overexpression of WDHD1 in GC and its correlation with poor prognosis suggest that it may serve as a candidate tissue-based biomarker for risk stratification and prognostic evaluation in GC. On the other hand, both in vitro and in vivo experiments consistently demonstrated that WDHD1 markedly promoted GC cell proliferation and tumor growth, while flow cytometric cell cycle analysis further indicated its close association with cell cycle dysregulation, a core biological feature of cancer, suggesting that WDHD1 may represent a potential therapeutic target. Candidate compounds identified through CMap and PRISM analyses also provided preliminary clues for WDHD1-related targeted intervention. Although these findings remain mainly at the level of computational prediction, they nevertheless suggest that WDHD1-high GC cells may harbor biologically exploitable therapeutic vulnerabilities, thereby providing a reference for future therapeutic strategy development.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vitro functional experiments were mainly based on two GC cell lines, AGS and HGC-27. These limited models may not fully reflect the molecular heterogeneity of GC. Therefore, the related conclusions need to be further validated in additional cell models and larger sample cohorts. Second, although this study proposed the hypothesis that GATA1 may serve as a potential upstream regulator of aberrant WDHD1 expression, the relationship between GATA1 and WDHD1 was mainly based on database prediction, binding motif analysis, and expression correlation. These findings are insufficient to demonstrate direct transcriptional regulation of WDHD1 by GATA1, and further validation using ChIP-qPCR, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and GATA1 functional intervention experiments is required. In addition, the results regarding the relationship between WDHD1 and the immune microenvironment were mainly derived from transcriptome-level correlation analyses, and single-cell data also showed that WDHD1 was primarily expressed in tumor epithelial cells. Therefore, it cannot currently be concluded that WDHD1 directly participates in the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment or tumor microenvironment remodeling. The potential association between WDHD1 and immune-related features requires further validation in experimental studies. Finally, the diagnostic performance analysis of WDHD1 was mainly based on public databases and may still be affected by batch effects and differences in sample composition. Meanwhile, due to the limited availability of publicly accessible and well-annotated cohorts, the prognostic model has not been externally validated in an independent dataset. Further validation in well-annotated independent external cohorts is needed to confirm the clinical application value of WDHD1 in future studies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that WDHD1 was significantly overexpressed in GC and is closely associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis. By integrating multi-omics analyses, flow cytometry results following cell cycle synchronization, and in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, we propose that WDHD1 promotes GC progression by regulating DNA replication and cell cycle progression. These findings not only deepen the current understanding of the oncogenic role of WDHD1, but also suggest that WDHD1 may serve as a promising candidate molecule with diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic potential in GC.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that WDHD1 has potential diagnostic and prognostic value in GC. Functional investigations further showed that WDHD1 downregulation inhibited GC cell proliferation, DNA synthesis, and in vivo tumor growth, while inducing cell cycle redistribution. These findings suggest that WDHD1 promotes GC progression by regulating DNA replication and cell-cycle progression, and may serve as a potential target for therapeutic intervention in GC.
