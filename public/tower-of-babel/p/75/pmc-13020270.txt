The application of experimental models for the drug discovery for digestive tumors
Authors: Linxiao Zheng, Wen Shuai, Yinyang Liu, Yang Deng, Ji Bao, Xiuying Hu, Guan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drug development for digestive tumors depends on various preclinical models to evaluate their efficacy and safety. Traditional models, such as 2D cell lines and animal models, fail to recapitulate the complex pathology in the human body. Recently, novel models, such as 3D organoids, tumor spheroids, and organ-on-a-chip systems, have undergone rapid growth. These models can recapitulate tissue architecture and the microenvironment in a more faithful way, enhancing the translational relevance of in vitro experiments to clinical outcomes. Moreover, patient-derived xenografts and genetically engineered models retain the genetic heterogeneity and immune contexture of original tumors, which play critical roles in assessing drug efficacy and resistance mechanisms. This review aims to explore the strengths and limitations of diverse models in drug discovery for digestive tumors. In addition, we delineate their utility in target discovery or validation, lead compound screening, and preclinical mechanistic profiling. Current studies indicate that drug failure rates can be minimized by the integration of 2D high-throughput screening, 3D organoid/tumor spheroid screening, and in vivo animal validation. Introduction

Digestive tumors, including colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and other digestive cancers, pose a significant burden worldwide. For example, colorectal cancer (CRC) accounts for approximately 10% of new cancer cases worldwide, making it the second leading cause of cancer death. Similarly, gastric cancer ranks fifth among the most commonly diagnosed cancers and the fourth leading cause of cancer mortality [1]. Given this burden, intercepting carcinogenesis at its earliest stages is essential. Although endoscopic and imaging techniques can aid early detection of advanced precancerous lesions (APL) [2], mechanism-based molecular biomarkers in digestive cancer are expected to offer higher diagnostic accuracy, enabling earlier detection, risk stratification, and timely intervention. For advanced digestive tumors, although surgery and adjuvant therapies can prolong the survival period of digestive cancer patients, the prognosis for advanced-stage patients remains poor [3, 4]. Therefore, novel disease-specific targeted agents are urgently needed. However, the low success rate of clinical translation of novel drugs means that a mere 10% of candidate drugs can ultimately be marketed [5]. One factor contributing to the low success rate of oncology drug development is the limited predictive value of current preclinical models. Thus, there is an urgent need to establish reliable and accurate preclinical models that play critical roles in predicting drug efficacy and reducing the failure rates of clinical trials.

In the discovery of anticancer agents for digestive malignancies, preclinical research serves as a crucial bridge between laboratory findings and human clinical trials. Broadly, digestive tumor drug development can be divided into three major phases [6, 7]: (i) early discovery, including target identification, establishment of digestive tumor models, and initial hit/lead selection; (ii) preclinical screening and evaluation, encompassing lead optimization and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) characterization; and (iii) preclinical validation, in which toxicity, efficacy, and the therapeutic window are systematically assessed. Across these stages, the central goal is to eliminate ineffective or excessively toxic candidates as early as possible, thereby reducing development costs and late-stage failure. To support this process, a spectrum of digestive tumor models has been developed, ranging from in vitro two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) systems to diverse in vivo mouse models.

It is widely recognized that a single model cannot completely recapitulate human physiology or disease. Every model has strengths and limitations and performs well in particular situations. This review aims to delineate the role of key digestive cancer models, including 2D cell lines, 3D spheroids, organoids, organ-on-a-chip systems, inducible disease models, tumor transplantation models, and genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), in the critical stages of anti-digestive tumor drug discovery. We emphasize that every digestive tumor cancer model has advantages and disadvantages; an appropriate model should be applied considering the research phase and biological questions. The integration of multiple models is advocated to increase the predictive accuracy and translational success. By continually refining model systems and incorporating new technologies, we anticipate accelerated development of digestive tumor drugs and improved clinical trial outcomes.

Ex vivo models of digestive tumors

2D cellular models

2D models typically refer to in vitro monolayer cell cultures, including immortalized tumor cell lines and primary cultured cells. 2D cellular models are considered essential tools owing to their high efficiency and cost-effectiveness [8]. As early as 1963, a stably passaged pancreatic cancer cell line, which was employed for anticancer drug screening, was established from surgical specimens [9]. To date, cell repositories such as the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) have provided hundreds of standardized digestive system cancer cell lines, which are widely used in research on pancreatic, gastric, colorectal cancers, and others [10–12]. These cell lines grow as adherent monolayers in culture dishes. They are low-cost, highly reproducible, and manipulation-friendly, making them widely used in digestive cancer drug discovery for a long time. Under 2D culture conditions, cells proliferate rapidly under standardized protocols, enabling researchers to obtain sufficient samples within a short time and conduct parallel comparative experiments across multiple treatment groups. For example, the NCI-60 tumor cell line panel and the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database aggregate diverse digestive tumor cell lines, making it possible to establish correlative maps between drug sensitivity and tumor genotypes through large-scale compound screening [13]. Furthermore, many commonly used 2D cancer cell lines can maintain a relatively stable genomic profile over early passage numbers [14], which facilitates the reproducible application of gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR–Cas9. This property makes them widely used in the fields of functional gene screening and pathway mechanism research.

Role of 2D cell models in the discovery of digestive tumors

Phenotype-based drug screening

Drug discovery efforts predominantly employ two complementary strategies: target-based (biochemical) screening and phenotypic (cell-based) screening [15](Fig. 1). Target-based approaches focus on predefined molecular entities and depend on prior knowledge of the mechanism of action, whereas phenotypic approaches assess compound activity at the level of integrated cellular responses without requiring detailed mechanistic hypotheses in advance. The advent of high-throughput screening (HTS) in the 1990 s, facilitated by advances in robotics and assay miniaturization, enabled the parallel testing of thousands of compounds in cell-based assays, most commonly in two-dimensional monolayer cultures, with readouts such as cell viability, reporter activity and gene expression [16]. Within this framework, the development of large, molecularly annotated cancer cell line panels has substantially expanded the scope of 2D phenotypic screening. Jaaks et al. conducted an HTS of 2,025 drug combinations across 125 cancer cell lines encompassing molecularly distinct subtypes of breast, colorectal and pancreatic cancer, and identified a synergistic interaction between irinotecan and the CHEK1 inhibitor AZD7762 that elicited marked cytotoxicity in microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer and KRAS/TP53 co-mutant models, with antitumor efficacy corroborated in mouse xenografts [17]. Such large-scale phenotypic screening in genetically characterized 2D cell line systems enables the discovery of combination regimens with selective activity in defined molecular contexts and provides a basis for the rational design of profile-guided therapeutic strategies.

Target-based drug screening

CRISPR-Cas9 provides such a tool by using programmable guide RNAs to direct Cas9 to genomic loci adjacent to a protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM), where double-strand breaks are repaired by non-homologous end joining or homology-directed repair, resulting in stable genomic alterations [18, 19]. Relative to zinc-finger nucleases and transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), this platform affords higher editing efficiency, simpler experimental implementation, lower cost and broader targetability [20, 21]. Moreover, catalytically inactive Cas9, fused with transcriptional regulators such as dCas9-VP64/sgRNA-MS2 for gene activation or dCas9-KRAB for gene repression, enables transcriptional gain- or loss-of-function perturbations without altering the underlying DNA sequence [22–24]. Collectively, these modalities constitute a versatile toolkit for defining cancer-relevant genes and nominating therapeutic targets. At the same time, effective application in digestive cancer models requires attention to the genomic architecture of commonly used 2D cell lines, many of which are aneuploid or polyploid; multiple copies of target loci can attenuate knockout efficiency and obscure genotype-phenotype relationships, particularly when essential genes are interrogated [25].

Building on these perturbation capabilities, pooled genetic libraries have substantially expanded the use of CRISPR in target-based screening and the systematic identification of synthetic-lethal interactions [26, 27]. Genome-scale or pathway-focused screens using short hairpin RNA (shRNA) libraries, as exemplified by the identification of PARG as a sensitizer to 5-fluorouracil in colorectal cancer cells, can model pharmacologically relevant partial inhibition [28]. However, their use is constrained by seed-sequence off-target effects and incomplete knockdown [29, 30]. By contrast, CRISPR-based knockout, CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) and CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) enable more defined loss- or gain-of-function readouts when gRNA enrichment or depletion is compared between treated and untreated conditions [31](Fig. 1).

Mechanism study of drug action

Conventional 2D monolayer cultures of digestive system tumor cells offer a controllable, relatively homogeneous platform for reductionist dissection of tumor-intrinsic signaling. In these malignancies, mapping pathways that regulate proliferation, apoptosis and autophagy is central to clarifying disease mechanisms and determinants of therapeutic response, thereby informing drug development [32, 33]. In gastric cancer, Lu et al. [34] showed that cephalosporin suppresses autophagy via the FOXO3-FOXM1 axis and enhances chemosensitivity in multiple gastric cancer cell lines. Beyond rigid plastic monolayers, advanced 2D digestive tumor platforms with tunable matrix stiffness and topographical cues enable mechanistic interrogation of how biophysical signals shape mechanotransduction, migration and adhesion [35–37]. Collectively, 2D models remain a foundational tool for elucidating signaling and biomechanical dependencies across digestive system tumors.

Limitations and challenges

Despite the advantages mentioned above, the primary limitation of 2D tumor cell line models lies in their lack of 3D architecture and microenvironmental support of tumors. This intrinsic shortcoming results in significant discrepancies between the in vivo state and experimental parameters, including cellular morphology, proliferation rates, cell‒cell and cell‒matrix interactions, and signaling pathways [38–41]. For example, current CRC cell lines are biased towards the microsatellite instability (MSI) phenotype, which may not fully reflect the chromosomal instability (CIN) seen in most CRCs [42]. Therefore, drug responses observed in 2D culture often fail to predict clinical outcomes precisely [43–45]. Many candidate drugs that perform effectively in vitro models exhibit low activity in subsequent animal or clinical trials. Furthermore, prolonged in vitro culture may lead to transcriptional drift and genomic instability within tumor cell lines. This causes significant discrepancies across identical cell strains maintained in different laboratories, limiting experimental reproducibility and resulting in instability [46–48]. Moreover, the limited numbers and categories of the available repertoire of cell lines make it difficult to cover the diversity of tumor subtypes. Crucial tumor microenvironmental factors, such as immune cell infiltration [49–53], and tumor-stroma crosstalk [54, 55], are unfeasible for recapitulation in simple 2D models. Therefore, although 2D tumor cell line models are efficient and inexpensive, significant deficiencies remain in the in-depth investigation of the complex biological characteristics of tumors and their systemic pharmacological responses, necessitating the introduction of more advanced models.

3D models

3D models refer to in vitro models in which cells form 3D structures, including cell spheroids (tumor spheroids), organoids (Table 1), and microfluidic organ-on-a-chip systems. 3D models can simulate the structure and function of in vivo tissues more faithfully. They play increasingly important roles in various stages of drug development. In comparison to 2D models, 3D models have undeniably proven to be more effective in replicating the in vivo-like growth and differentiation of tissues [56, 57](Fig. 2B). The following sections discuss the application of three types of 3D models including tumor spheroids, organoids, and organs-on-a-chip in the development of digestive drugs.

Comparison between tumor spheroids and organoids

Tumor spheroids

Tumor spheroids are spherical cluster models formed by the spontaneous aggregation of tumor cells under nonadherent culture conditions, typically 3D cell clusters with a diameter of several hundred micrometers [58, 59]. Spheroids are typically generated using various methods, with 3D cell culture techniques classified into anchorage-dependent and anchorage-independent models, though hybrid techniques are becoming increasingly common [60–62]. 3D culture is mainly achieved by allowing cells to self-assemble into spheroids in specialized culture plates, with the most widely used methods being hanging drop, liquid overlay, and microfluidic techniques [63–65]. Spheroids exhibit key features such as intercellular interactions [66], Extracellular Matrix (ECM) production and deposition [67], localized hypoxic regions [68], and gradients of pH and nutrients [69–71]. These microenvironmental factors define cell behavior and function by altering gene and protein expression, which in turn affects drug penetration [72], response/resistance, and metabolism [73]. These 3D microtissues are employed to simulate various tumors [74–76], especially avascular tumor microregions [77], intervascular zones, and micrometastases [78, 79].

Role of tumor spheroids in the discovery of digestive tumors

Drug screening

2D cell culture remains the mainstay of cell-based HTS. However, monolayers recapitulate few salient features of the in vivo tumor microenvironment, thereby limiting translational relevance (Fig. 2B). 3D spheroid culture offers a pragmatic, HTS-amenable alternative [58, 59, 80, 81]. Among 3D approaches, scaffold-free (anchorage-independent) formats—including the liquid-overlay method [82], ultra-low-attachment (ULA) plates [83], and hanging-drop arrays in 96- or 384-well plates [84]—are currently best suited for HTS because they are fully compatible with automated liquid handling and robotic workflows.

In chemistry-driven screens, compounds are dispensed into multicellular tumor spheroids (MTSs) plates, incubated for a defined interval, and quantified by automated readouts such as viability, spheroid size/compactness, reporter activity, or fluorescent dye intensity [58]. Reproducible gastric-cancer MTSs in 96-well ULA plates enable screening. Several bufadienolides show lower IC₅₀ in MTSs than in 2D, and epirubicin attains MTSs IC₅₀ near therapeutic exposure [85]. Conversely, some agents exhibit greater activity in 3D—e.g., the hypoxia-activated prodrug TH-302 displays higher cytotoxicity in MTSs than in monolayers [86]. Drug screening in spheroids is less developed, largely due to poor penetration of nucleic acids and viral vectors into compact MTSs. A practical workaround is to perturb cells overnight in arrayed 2D (RNAi or CRISPR/Cas9), then trypsinize and transfer them to spheroid-forming plates. In colorectal-cancer models, prior KIF11 (Eg5) knockdown yielded MTSs with smaller diameter and volume than non-targeting controls [84]. These findings imply that incorporating genetic perturbations into spheroid-based HTS may help identify microenvironment-dependent vulnerabilities that are underdetected in 2D screens (Fig. 2B).

Microenvironmental complexity and implications for drug response

Spheroids derived from digestive system tumors arise through cell self-aggregation under non-adherent conditions and provide a 3D context in which architecture, microenvironment and drug response are closely coupled. By co-culturing cancer cells with stromal components such as infiltrating immune cells, endothelial cells and ECM, digestive tumor spheroids better reproduce key features of the tumor microenvironment, including dynamic tumor–stroma crosstalk and biochemical/biophysical gradients that underpin intratumoral heterogeneity [87–91](Fig. 2A, C). Within these aggregates, tumor cells form adherens and desmosomal junctions and secrete ECM proteins (collagen, fibronectin, laminin) and proteoglycans, creating a dense extracellular barrier; concomitant accumulation of ECM, together with cytokines and growth factors such as IL-6, SDF-1 and VEGF, can contribute to therapy-tolerant phenotypes [92–94]. (Fig. 2D). These structural features help explain why many agents appear less potent in spheroids than in 2D assays, while spheroid responses tend to align more closely with clinical behavior. The radial organization of spheroids further links 3D structure to drug resistance and penetration. As spheroids grow, diffusion constraints generate rim-to-core gradients of oxygen, nutrients and pH, yielding a proliferative, well-oxygenated outer zone and a hypoxic, acidic interior in which cells rely increasingly on anaerobic metabolism [95–99] (Fig. 2E). These coupled gradients modulate pharmacodynamics: oxygen-dependent, ROS-mediated cytotoxics such as doxorubicin, cisplatin and mitomycin C show reduced activity in hypoxic cores [58, 100–102]; similarly, agents that preferentially target rapidly cycling cells, such as paclitaxel, are less effective in low-proliferation inner regions [72, 103]. Extracellular acidic pH also alters drug ionization; for weakly basic chemotherapeutics including methotrexate, carboplatin and cisplatin, increased protonation can limit membrane diffusion and cellular uptake, further attenuating efficacy in the spheroid interior [104–106]. Consequently, digestive tumor spheroids provide a relatively reliable context for evaluating penetration and microenvironmental robustness: compounds active only in 2D or at the spheroid periphery are likely to require optimization, whereas agents that maintain activity in central regions are more likely to translate to solid tumors.

Limitations and challenges

However, tumor spheroid models still have obvious limitations. Generally, tumor spheroids are spontaneous aggregation by tumor cell lines, which lack tumor stromal components and an immune microenvironment. As we mentioned above, it does not rely on anchoring. Although cell‒cell contacts exist within the inner regions of the spheroid, excessively homogeneous cellular components, namely, tumor cells, make intercellular signaling between diverse cell types impossible. The absence of fibroblasts, endothelial cells, or immune cells hinders the recapitulation of the complexity inherent in native tumor tissue and its surrounding microenvironment [107, 108]. Furthermore, strictly controlling the size and morphology of tumor spheroids is challenging, which results in lower standardization compared to 2D culture systems, making it one of the limiting factors for high-throughput screening. Fortunately, emerging technologies such as 3D bioprinting provide new insight into the refinement of tumor spheroid models [109, 110]. Multiple cell types and biomaterials can be precisely assembled into three-dimensional architectures to construct “miniaturized tumor tissues” by bioprinting. Compared with traditional suspension spheroid cultures, novel models incorporate ECM scaffolds and diverse cell types. By taking advantage of this feature, a multicellular, layered structure of tumor tissue can be partially reconstructed. These approaches significantly improve the ability of 3D models to simulate the tumor microenvironment.

Organoid models

Organoids provide a more complex and physiologically relevant three-dimensional cellular model compared to tumor spheroids. 3D organoid models are novel in vitro models that have rapidly developed over the past few decades. These models are typically self-assembled by stem cells or tumor cells within tumor tissues, which are constructed by histoarchitecture and multicellular differentiation and resemble those of the primary tumor [111–113]. The concept of organoids was first introduced by Smith and Cochrane in 1946 to describe cystic teratomas in the pararenal tissue of infants [114]. Since then, organoids have been widely used to describe self-organizing cells or tumors. A significant breakthrough occurred in 2009, Sato et al. successfully established small intestinal organoids using LGR5+ mouse intestinal stem cells [115]. With the advancement of research, in 2012, Sato et al. successfully established tumor organoid models using tumor tissue as the source [116]. Since then, various tumor organoid models, including those for esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, and pancreatic cancer, have been constructed [117–119]. Mentioned above, patient-derived organoid (PDOs) are typically derived from primary tumor tissues of various cancers, such as colon, pancreatic, and gastric cancers, while patient-derived xenograft organoid (PDXO) refers to organoids derived from tumor stem cells sourced from patient-derived tumor xenograft (PDX) tissues, which are seeded into a specific matrix and cultured in a 3D environment enriched with customized growth factors. In other words, the difference between the two lies in the source of the tissue. Organoids have become a valuable platform for digestive tumor modeling, supported by multiple culture formats such as Matrigel-based domes [120, 121], air-liquid interface systems [122], polymer scaffolds [123], rotary cell culture system(RCCS) bioreactors [124, 125], and microfluidic chips [126, 127]. Adult stem cells (ASCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) serve as the major sources for digestive organoids (Fig. 3A), whereas the application of embryonic stem cells (ESCs) remains limited due to ethical concerns. ASC-derived organoids exhibit rapid establishment and adult-like morphology, whereas iPSC-derived organoids demonstrate broader differentiation potential but require longer maturation [128, 129]. PDOs can originate from tissue biopsies [130], liquid biopsy-isolated circulating tumor cells [131, 132], or tumor stem cell-enriched populations [133]. ECM is critical, with Matrigel™ as the most common matrix [134, 135], though its animal origin, batch variability, and potential contamination raise reproducibility and ethical concerns [136, 137]. These limitations have accelerated the development of synthetic ECMs with defined, tunable properties, which enhance standardization and align with 3R principles for clinically oriented digestive tumor organoid research [138, 139].

Role of organoids in the discovery of digestive tumors

Genetic modeling in digestive organoids

Drug discovery depends heavily on disease models that faithfully recapitulate human pathology. In digestive cancers, organoids are primarily engineered through genetic manipulation, which enables precise introduction of oncogenic drivers or loss-of-function mutations. With CRISPR-Cas9, even normal epithelial organoids can be systematically edited to reconstruct early tumorigenic events [140], forming the basis for the landmark gene-edited organoid studies that follow. Organoids derived from human intestinal stem cells marked a pivotal milestone in applying gene editing for the identification of oncogenic drivers. In a foundational study, Drost et al. established 3D intestinal organoids from human stem cells and sequentially introduced mutations in APC, TP53, KRAS, and SMAD4, thereby reproducing the stepwise mutational trajectory and malignant transformation characteristic of colorectal cancer [141]. Building on this framework, mismatch repair-deficient colon organoids were shown to spontaneously acquire mutations in the Wnt, p53, BMP, and RAS pathways under stepwise growth factor withdrawal, ultimately leading to fully transformed neoplastic phenotypes that closely mirrored the early stages of in vivo tumorigenesis [142]. Extending this model of early cancer evolution, Karlsson et al. Generated TP53-knockout gastric organoids to simulate preneoplastic states, observing that TP53 loss alone was sufficient to trigger progressive chromosomal instability and the gradual emergence of dominant pre-cancerous clones—closely reflecting molecular ecosystems observed in human tumors [143]. Another work by Canellas-Socias et al. further validated the utility of genetically engineered mouse tumor organoids: using Apc/Kras/Tgfbr2/Trp53-mutant (AKTP) murine colorectal organoids, they demonstrated a causal relationship between residual EMP1⁺ cells and metastatic relapse following therapy [144]. At the scale of functional genomics, the Verissimo team performed genome-wide CRISPR–Cas9 knockout screens in colorectal organoids, delineating pathway interactions and dependencies across WNT, MAPK, PI3K, TP53, and mismatch repair (MMR) [145]. These analyzes revealed distinct genetic liabilities arising from crosstalk among these pathways, thereby nominating actionable targets for digestive tumor subtypes.

Moreover, gene-edited models combined with advanced co-culture systems demonstrate the power of engineered organoids in replicating cancer progression and the tumor microenvironment (TME). For instance, in esophageal adenocarcinoma, matrix-embedded assembloid models composed of tumor-derived organoids and CAFs successfully recapitulate differentiation states and CAF phenotypes found in patient tumors[146]. Additionally, patient-derived organoids, including those from pancreatic cancer, reveal functional heterogeneity based on niche factors such as Wnt and R-spondin dependence[147], further enhancing our understanding of cancer evolution and progression.

Pharmaceutical development

In the drug-discovery workflow, PDOs are used predominantly in the downstream stages of phenotypic drug discovery and candidate validation, with comparatively limited adoption in early, target-based discovery [148, 149]. A common strategy performs high-throughput primary screening in 2D cell systems followed by higher-physiological-relevance confirmation in PDOs. For example, under a drug-repurposing paradigm—leveraging established safety and pharmacokinetic profiles for rapid clinical translation—albendazole identified in 2D screening was shown to suppress colorectal cancer progression and therapy resistance by targeting the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF20; this activity was recapitulated in colorectal cancer PDOs, providing a viable route for repositioned candidates [150], organoid platforms are moving upstream into absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET) assessment [151]. Kourla et al. developed a bidirectional monolayer assay that enables human intestinal organoids to profile drug disposition, metabolism, and enterotoxicity within a single framework, thereby allowing parallel quantification of efficacy and safety readouts [152].

Translational applications of digestive cancer organoids

PDO “living” biobanks represent a major advance for digestive cancer research. Unlike conventional tumor biobanks, which store non-renewable tissue or blood samples, PDOs can be expanded by serial passaging, cryopreserved, and revived on demand, thus supporting repeated assays and large-scale drug testing [117, 153–155](Fig. 3A). Such biobanks have now been created for major digestive malignancies, including colorectal [156–160], pancreatic [161], hepatic [162], and gastric cancers [117, 163], as well as other solid tumors, and are increasingly complemented by centralized resources such as the Human Cancer Models Initiative (HCMI). These biobanks provide scalable infrastructure for high-throughput drug screening and for mapping drug-sensitivity patterns across diverse patient cohorts.

Functionally, digestive PDOs preserve key characteristics of their parental tumors, including histoarchitecture, biomarker expression, copy-number alterations, and mutational landscapes [119, 164–166], and show high concordance in transcriptional programs. Consistently, ex vivo drug responses in PDOs frequently parallel clinical outcomes, underscoring their utility for functional precision oncology and individualized treatment selection (Fig. 3B). For example, in colorectal cancer, Kim et al. [167]. showed that 3D PDOs derived from spatially distinct tumor regions more faithfully recapitulated the shared molecular subtype than corresponding 2D patient-derived cells, highlighting the advantage of organoids in capturing intra-tumoral heterogeneity.

Although digestive PDOs recapitulate key features of the original tumors, conventional cultures are largely restricted to the epithelial compartment and therefore have limited capacity to model the TME. To enhance their physiological relevance, PDO systems have been extended either by exogenous reconstruction, co-culturing organoids with immune and stromal cells within an ECM, or by endogenous preservation using air–liquid interface (ALI) and microfluidic/tissue-fragment platforms that transiently retain intrinsic TME components [168]. In a prototypical ALI setup, PDOs are embedded in a collagen gel on an inner Transwell insert exposed to air for oxygenation, while medium in the outer well diffuses through the permeable membrane to supply nutrients [169, 170]. In an ALI-based model, Li et al. showed that KRASG12D combined with p53 loss was sufficient to drive adenocarcinoma formation in pancreatic and gastric organoids, whereas colonic organoids additionally required Apc and Smad4 alterations to achieve stable tumorigenicity [166]. In parallel, multi-omics technologies, including whole-exome/whole-genome sequencing, bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, and epigenomic profiling, are increasingly applied to PDOs to assess genomic fidelity, resolve intra-tumoral heterogeneity, and delineate regulatory programs underlying malignant progression and therapy resistance [171–175].Collectively, these advances position digestive organoids as versatile platforms for drug screening, biomarker discovery, and mechanistic dissection in digestive cancers.

Pre-clinical drug testing PDOs exhibit high concordance with matched tumors across multiple cancer types (Fig. 3C and D). Consistent with this, head-to-head comparisons of ex vivo drug responses and clinical outcomes generally confirm the predictive value of PDOs [176, 177]. For therapeutic-window definition, Herpers et al. leveraged a biobank comprising colorectal cancer PDOs and donor-matched healthy colonic mucosa organoids to high-content screen > 500 bispecific antibodies targeting the WNT and RTK axes, identifying MCLA-158, which selectively induces EGFR degradation in LGR5⁺ cancer stem cells and suppresses tumor growth with minimal toxicity to matched healthy LGR5⁺ intestinal stem cells; this parallel evaluation delineated an efficacious and tolerable concentration range with translational relevance [178]. This finding further indicates that, within a defined concentration window, the test compound can effectively modulate tumor-cell activity while exerting minimal impact on normal cells. Similarly, Ramesh et al. showed that BCL-XL inhibition elicits an FGF2-mediated FGFR4–ERK–MCL-1 adaptive pathway; co-inhibition of FGFR4 achieves robust antitumor activity at low BCL-XL doses, without overt toxicity in matched normal organoids or platelets—supporting rational combination therapy within defined safety margins [179]. Collectively, these examples indicate that PDOs can integrate discovery and validation for mapping genotype–drug response relationships, identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities, and quantifying therapeutic windows [180–182]. Despite the overall positive evidence, PDOs are not perfect predictors. In a prospective colorectal cancer study, Ooft et al. observed cases in which organoids appeared drug-sensitive ex vivo while patients failed to achieve objective clinical responses. Accordingly, extrapolation of PDOs findings to preclinical/clinical decision-making should be supported by prospective, adequately powered, coupled clinical–organoid studies [183]. Taken together, current data indicate that, in most settings, ex vivo responses of PDOs can predict patient responses to therapy [158, 184–186].

PDOs show predictive value across multiple therapeutic modalities—including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy (Fig. 3C and D). In an early phase I/II study enrolling patients with metastatic CRC and metastatic gastroesophageal cancer , ex vivo PDOs drug-response readouts predicted clinical responses to targeted agents and chemotherapy with 100% sensitivity and 93% specificity [158]. In the field of radiotherapy, PDOs platforms now enable prospective evaluation of radiosensitizers and dose-escalation strategies. Despite radiotherapy’s strong local control, favorable safety profile, and cost-effectiveness, the number and variety of approving radiosensitizers remain limited, owing in part to the lack of high-fidelity preclinical models. Ganesh and colleagues correlated CRC PDOs radiosensitivity with endoscopic assessments and patient outcomes before and after radiotherapy: patients whose PDOs exhibited radioresistant phenotypes ex vivowere more likely to experience post-treatment recurrence [187]. Overall, PDOs radiosensitivity measured in vitro closely mirrored clinical radiotherapy responses.

With the rise of immunotherapy, organoid–immune co-culture technologies have advanced rapidly and are now used to dissect the complex interactions between tumor cells and the immune system. Unlike traditional cultures containing only tumor epithelium, PDO co-cultures can incorporate multiple immune cell types—including lymphocytes [188, 189], cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [190, 191], dendritic cells (DCs) [192], natural killer (NK) [193] cells, and macrophages [194]—thereby rebuilding critical cell–cell crosstalk within a three-dimensional microenvironment. This platform is potentially pivotal for functional evaluation of novel immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and cellular therapies. Evidence for clinical response prediction is encouraging yet highlights room for improvement. In a study, Chalabi et al. observed that, under PD-1 blockade, CRC PDOs derived from some clinical responders were recognized by autologous T cells, supporting the feasibility of PDO-based ICI response assessment [195]. However, not all responders displayed in-vitro CD8⁺ T-cell reactivity in PDO–autologous T-cell co-cultures, indicating that current platforms require further refinement to achieve robust predictive accuracy.

Limitations and challenges

Despite their clear advantages, organoid models still have limitations: conventional tumor organoids lack tumor vasculature and immune components, and their culture conditions are complex and costly [196]. Since organoids typically lack endothelial cells and immune cells, fully reproducing the blood supply and immune microenvironment of solid tumors is difficult, which limits the direct application of organoid models for evaluating immunotherapeutic drugs. However, existing studies have partially reconstructed the immune microenvironment by incorporating patient-derived immune cells [194, 197, 198]. Second, organoid culture conditions vary significantly across cancer types. For example, gastric cancer and colorectal cancer organoids require various growth factors and inhibitors to ensure stable long-term passaging [156, 158]. Growth factors in the culture system are expensive and require strict batch-to-batch consistency control. Additionally, although PDOs preserve tumor heterogeneity better than other in vitro models, those derived from a single tumor region are insufficiently representative and fail to fully capture the patient’s in vivo biology, often leading to discrepancies between ex vivo and clinical drug responses [199]. To reduce this bias, current practice increasingly uses multi-region sampling to build PDOs cohorts, providing a more comprehensive view of spatial heterogeneity and tumor architecture, thereby improving the stability and generalizability of pharmacologic response predictions [166]. Moreover, although medium-throughput screening has been achieved in organoids, these methods are not yet able to fully replace traditional cell line models in high-throughput industrial screening and still need to be used in combination with cell lines. Furthermore, intestinal organoid models present specific challenges. For example, the 3D architecture and extracellular matrix limit drug and microbial access to the lumen, affecting accurate assessment of intestinal permeability and drug absorption [200]. In addition, the intestinal epithelium is sensitive to pH, fluid shear stress, and oxygen gradients. Although microfluidic technologies can partially address these issues, their complexity hinders their clinical translation. After all, replicating full intestinal functionality in multicellular systems remains highly challenging [201].

Organ-on-a-chip models

The origin of Organ-on-a-Chip (OoC) can be traced back to 30 years ago, initially based on the application of microfluidic technology in cell culture and biological analysis [202–205]. The core goal is to create an experimental platform that simulates the cellular structure and function of human organs, replicating the in vivo microenvironment. Using microfluidic technology, organ chips can simulate 3D ECM, biochemical factors, and biomechanical cues, which are then applied for disease modeling and drug screening [61, 205–207]. Compared to traditional two-dimensional culture systems, most 3D culture systems, while capable of partially mimicking the in vivo environment, often fail to perfectly replicate the mechanical forces and fluid dynamics of the human body. Organ chips provide a solution to this issue. Based on microfluidic technology, OoC systems precisely control oxygen perfusion, nutrient exchange, and the physiological microenvironment, allowing for a closer simulation of in vivo conditions. In addition, organ chips can provide the necessary mechanical force stimuli for tissues, which significantly improves the physiological relevance of the culture system [208, 209]. Another advantage of organ chips is that their volume is reduced by more than a thousand times [204], which opens up possibilities for improving efficiency, lowering costs, and enhancing control over volume and other experimental parameters (Fig. 4A).

A full-featured organ-on-a-chip system integrates optimized on-a-chip architecture, controlled fluid delivery, and precise real-time monitoring. Chip design covers material selection, microfabrication, and sealing strategies, and PDMS is widely used due to its biocompatibility and transparency, despite its high adsorption and solvent sensitivity [210]. Thermoplastics have higher chemical stability but are more difficult to process [211]. Microfluidic structures have been constructed using lithography, soft lithography, thermoforming, and various etching techniques [205, 212], ranging from plasma-assisted PDMS-glass sealing [213] to thermoplastic thermoadhesives or adhesives [214]. Fluid control systems mimic physiological flow, with active pumping methods such as syringe pumps [215–217], peristaltic pumps [218, 219], and pressure-driven or vacuum pumps [220] providing precise perfusion and shear stress regulation, while passive flow based on gravity or channel geometry provides a simplified alternative for multi-cell cultures [221, 222]. Real-time monitoring of biomolecules, oxygen, pH, and other microenvironmental parameters is essential for maintaining functional 3D cultures, and integrated sensors such as ISFETs [223, 224], LAPS [225], electrochemical probes [226, 227], optical oxygen sensors [228, 229], temperature detectors, and phenol red-based pH measurements [229] enable fast, noninvasive, and continuous readings without interfering with the system.

“Organoid-on-a-Chip (OoAC)” originates from the concept of organoids and integrates with microfluidic technology. It uses biocompatible materials to construct laminar flow systems with microchambers and microchannel networks, enabling organoid research and the recreation of in vivo-like microenvironments and dynamic conditions. The key difference between OoC and OoAC lies in the source of cells and tissue formation: the former typically uses a mixture of cell lines or primary cells, with engineered microenvironments providing precise biochemical and biophysical cues. In contrast, OoAC starts with ESC, iPSC or pre-formed organoids, which self-organize according to their intrinsic developmental program to form organ-like structures with functional characteristics [126, 230]. Therefore, organ chips and organoid chips represent two fundamentally different but complementary approaches toward the same goal of replicating the complexity of human organs in vitro.

Organoid-on-a-chip systems have progressed from single-organ chips to integrated multi-organ platforms. In 2010, the Ingber group at Harvard Medical School reported the Lung-on-a-Chip, building on the early work of the Takayama group, marking a significant milestone in OoAC development [231, 232]. Subsequently, various single-organ chips related to the digestive system, such as liver [233–235], pancreas [236, 237], and intestinal chips [238–240], were developed for disease progression and drug studies. Single-organ chips focus on simulating individual organ functions, while multi-organ chips integrate multiple units (e.g., intestinal absorption, liver metabolism, kidney excretion) in a single platform for more comprehensive studies [241, 242]. The next frontier, “Body-on-a-Chip” or “Human-on-a-Chip,” aims to conduct PK and PD analyzes on a single platform to reflect the physiology of the entire human body [243–245]. For instance, Miller and Shuler’s 13-organ system demonstrated the framework for studying inter-organ exchanges and drug responses [246]. As a fusion of microfabrication engineering and cell biology, organoid chips are leading cancer research into a new era of engineered, programmable, and precisely controlled models.

Role of organ-on-a-chip in the discovery of digestive tumors

Imaging-guided organoid-on-a-chip drug screening

Although digestive PDOs provide physiologically relevant 3D tumor models, their complexity complicates quantitative pharmacological assessment. Standard ATP-based viability assays remain widely used due to their scalability [181, 247], but they offer only bulk, destructive endpoint measurements, limiting temporal resolution and masking responses from individual organoids or subpopulations [248]. To address these constraints, label-free imaging modalities are increasingly adopted. Optical microscopy enables single-organoid evaluation and discrimination of cytotoxic versus cytostatic effects [249], while positron emission microscopy tracks metabolic activity dynamically and links drug responses to metabolic phenotypes [250]. These imaging-based strategies provide a more granular and mechanistic understanding of treatment effects in heterogeneous digestive tumor organoids.

To enhance throughput and enable dynamic control in digestive tumor drug discovery, OoC technologies are increasingly integrated into PDO workflows. These microfluidic devices are typically fabricated from optically transparent materials, allowing real-time imaging and on-chip fluorescent or antibody-based labeling [251–253]while avoiding the limitations of off-chip assays such as PCR, ELISA, and MS [254], which cannot capture continuous physiological or metabolic changes [258–260]. Moreover, microfluidic miniaturization reduces assay volumes by over 1000-fold [204], significantly improving throughput and lowering reagent costs—critical advantages when screening multiple drugs, concentrations, and replicates in digestive tumor PDOs. Recent advances, such as the 200-chamber automated microfluidic platform developed by Schuster et al. [255], further expand this capability by using multiplexed valve-controlled flow to culture, treat, and monitor PDOs under numerous drug conditions simultaneously, enabling real-time evaluation of treatment sequences and adaptive responses (Fig. 4B). Together, the integration of advanced imaging modalities with OoC microfluidics provides a scalable and dynamically controlled framework for high-throughput drug screening in digestive tumor organoid systems.

Drug resistance

Tumor chip technology offers a new platform for drug resistance research by accurately simulating the tumor microenvironment, thus aiding the optimization of cancer treatment strategies. Haque et al. [256]. developed a tumor chip that replicates the PDAC microenvironment and preserves pancreatic tumor PDOs function. Their study demonstrated that pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) and U937 monocytes significantly enhance PDOs growth and invasiveness, suggesting that targeting PSCs and macrophages may improve chemotherapy outcomes for pancreatic cancer. Similarly, Jang’s team [257] created a microfluidic 3D tumor model to investigate gastric cancer cell behavior and resistance mechanisms, revealing the crucial role of EMT in cancer progression and chemotherapy resistance. These studies imply that organ-on-a-chip systems may be a valuable complement to PDO models, providing additional insights into microenvironment-related drug resistance pathways in gut cancer.

Multimodal organ-on-a-chip and drug toxicity

The advent of multi-organ chips has provided crucial support for the application of PK-PD models in digestive tumors drug research [258, 259]. By connecting multiple organs via vascular perfusion, PK studies can analyze drug metabolism and concentration distribution in different organs, covering ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion). PD studies, on the other hand, focus on drug effects on target organs, such as the relationship between drug dose and pharmacological or toxicological responses [260]. Although drug absorption, distribution, and clearance are well-represented in multi-organ chips, reliability in other in vitro and single-organ models remains a concern. Maschmeyer et al. [137]. developed a multi-organ chip system capable of long-term co-culture of four human tissue types (such as intestinal, liver, skin, and kidney equivalents), using primary human intestinal epithelial cells to reconstruct the intestinal barrier. This system was the first to conduct ADME studies on candidate drugs over 28 days and perform repeated-dose systemic toxicity testing.

Accurately predicting the metabolic capacity and toxicity of a drug is essential during the drug development process [261]. The liver is a key organ in drug metabolism, particularly in Phase I (mainly mediated by CYP450 enzymes) and Phase II (primarily mediated by UDP-glucuronosyltransferases, UGT) metabolism [262]. Therefore, developing effective in vitro models to accurately simulate the complex structure and physiological function of liver, as well as to evaluate human liver responses to drugs, becomes particularly important in drug discovery. To predict the hepatotoxicity and efficacy of a drug, understanding its metabolism in the liver is crucial. Chen et al. [263]. Integrated a liver-on-a-chip (OoC) platform with a cancer chip module to investigate the liver metabolism of simvastatin. When simvastatin passed through the artificial liver and was subsequently perfused into the cancer chip, a significant reduction in cancer cell activity was observed. This result indicated that the liver OoC platform was capable of converting the prodrug simvastatin into its active form, atorvastatin. Additionally, the largest liver OoC study to date used 780 liver OoC devices to blind-assess the toxicity risk of 27 known hepatotoxic and non-toxic drugs. The results showed that the liver OoC had a sensitivity of 87% and a specificity of 100% in detecting toxicity [264]. With ongoing advancements, liver OoC platforms will play an increasingly important role in early drug toxicity prediction, helping to reduce the occurrence of adverse drug events.

Limitations and challenges

Although OoC possess advantages in mimicking the human tissue microenvironment, they still face several limitations. First, the sources of cells remain constrained. While there has been a gradual shift from traditional cell lines such as Caco-2 cells to patient-derived organoids, there is variability among donors. Meanwhile, differentiation protocols and passage numbers can compromise experimental reproducibility. Moreover, organoids derived from patients at different disease stages exhibit genomic alterations and varying microenvironmental requirements, further increasing experimental variability [157, 265, 266]. Second, OoC are usually made from Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), which tends to adsorb small hydrophobic drug molecules, influencing pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analysis. Although techniques such as mass spectrometry-based quantification of drug absorption or finite element modeling can partially solve this problem, adsorption still limits the applicability of OoC for certain compounds. Standardization and scalability also present difficulties. While some companies have introduced automated and standardized interfaces, academic laboratories often struggle with reproducibility. There is still no consensus on what constitutes a “qualified” organ-on-a-chip [267–271]. Finally, OOC experiments generate large and complex datasets that require collaborative analysis. Balancing data interpretation with clinical relevance—especially compared with animal models—remains challenging. These limitations underscore the need for researchers to carefully consider the context of use and data interpretability when designing OOC studies to fully leverage their potential in drug screening and disease modeling.

In vivo models for digestive tumors

In vivo models serve as an important complement to in vitro systems, providing a critical validation platform for drug discovery within an intact immune system and physiological microenvironment. In the following section, we introduce the most commonly used in vivo models, including inducible models, transplantation models (such as CDX, PDX, syngeneic transplantation, and humanized mouse models), as well as genetically engineered mouse models.

Induced models

Induced tumor models refer to experimental systems in which animals develop malignant lesions in specific tissues following exposure to exogenous carcinogenic factors, such as chemical carcinogens, ionizing or non-ionizing radiation, oncogenic viruses, microbiome dysbiosis, or chronic physical injury [272] (Fig. 5A). These models recapitulate the exposure conditions experienced by humans more faithfully, and therefore have substantial methodological value in digestive cancer drug development, carcinogenesis research, and the preclinical evaluation of innovative therapeutic strategies [273]. Chemical induction models in mice, in particular, accumulate a wide spectrum of somatic mutations, exhibit marked intratumoral heterogeneity, and display diverse driver gene alterations [274]. As a result, they can reproduce many molecular and phenotypic features characteristic of tumors arising from chronic exposure to environmental carcinogens.

Chemotherapeutic carcinogenesis models are widely used to study the initiation and progression of digestive tumors because they reproduce key pathological shifts observed in human digestive cancers. Tumor development typically proceeds through three sequential stages. The initiation stage involves metabolic activation of carcinogenic “initiators,” which induce irreversible genetic alterations in DNA and generate permanent mutations that are faithfully transmitted to daughter cells [275]. The promotion stage occurs when initiated cells undergo clonal expansion in response to tumor-promoting agents. Promoters generally do not induce direct DNA damage; rather, they enhance the proliferation and survival of mutated cells through signaling pathways triggered at the cell surface or within the cell [276]. In the advanced stage, early dysplastic lesions can develop into aggressive and metastatic digestive malignancies, manifested by genomic instability, chromosomal abnormalities, and significant phenotypic shifts [277]. Most digestive tumor chemical carcinogenesis models adopt the classic two-stage carcinogenesis paradigm, in which an initiator induces DNA damage, followed by sustained inflammatory stimuli, metabolic stress, or other promoting signals that accelerate tumor formation [278] (Fig. 6).

Mechanistically, digestive cancer chemical carcinogens can be categorized into genotoxic (GTX) agents [279], which directly damage DNA or chromosomal structures, and non-genotoxic (NGTX) agents, which promote tumorigenesis indirectly through inflammation, oxidative stress, endocrine disruption, or dysregulated cell proliferation [277]. A notable feature of chemical carcinogens is their pronounced tissue specificity. Although exposure may occur via oral, dermal, inhalation, or intraperitoneal routes, tumor development typically arises in organs where metabolic activation of carcinogens is most efficient [280–282]. This organ selectivity is strongly influenced by tissue-specific expression and activity of metabolic enzymes—particularly cytochrome P450 (CYP) isoforms—which determine the production of reactive metabolites and hence the extent of local DNA damage [283]. These features make chemoinduction a powerful method for mimicking gastrointestinal organ-specific tumorigenesis. Given that its application pattern is roughly similar to that of genetically engineered mouse models, it will not be discussed in detail in this section.

Limitations and challenges

However, induced models also present substantial limitations. First, mutation during chemical carcinogenesis occurs randomly, leading to high heterogeneity in different animals or tumors at different sites within the same animal. This makes it difficult to simulate specific genotype-driven cancer subtypes [284, 285]. Second, the variations in induction protocols across different studies result in insufficient reproducibility in outcomes such as tumor incidence, lesion number, and distribution, posing challenges for evaluating drug efficacy. Furthermore, the induction process is accompanied by synergistic effects of multiple factors, including DNA damage, chronic inflammation, and alterations in the microbiota. This ambiguity impedes the elucidation of the precise mechanisms underlying drug efficacy. Consequently, extensive additional mechanistic validation is often needed to interpret drug mechanisms [286]. Finally, the species differences in tumorigenesis, the immune microenvironment, and drug metabolism between humans and rodents may lead to failure in the clinical translation of some treatments that are potent in induced models [287]. Therefore, in the development of digestive malignancies, induced models are generally employed as valuable tools for preventive drug validation and mechanistic investigations. These models are frequently integrated with genetically engineered and transplantation approaches, thereby improving the robustness of experimental outcomes and strengthening their potential for clinical translation.

Xenograft models

Cell line–derived xenograft models (CDXs)

CDXs are generated by expanding human tumor cells in vitro and subsequently implanting them into immunodeficient mice. This model is rapidly established and exhibits low batch-to-batch variability [288, 289](Fig. 5B). Nevertheless, these models also exhibit notable limitations. Studies have shown that CDXs often fail to accurately predict clinical responses to therapies targeting oncogenic driver proteins [290], resulting in a poor correlation with therapeutic efficacy observed in patients. Moreover, long-term in vitro passaging exposes cell lines to artificial culture conditions, which can induce genetic and epigenetic alterations and enrich specific subclones [47], thereby further diminishing their representativeness. As a result, the National Cancer Institute (NCI) has recently reduced the priority of the NCI-60 human cancer cell line panel in large-scale drug screening [291]. CDXs models exhibit limited utility in optimizing complex therapeutic variables, such as drug combinations, dosing regimens, and drug delivery strategies [292].

Despite these limitations, CDXs remain valuable in several research contexts, including the initial screening of non-targeted cytotoxic agents, preliminary toxicity assessment [293], investigation of drug resistance mechanisms [294], and the early categorization of potentially effective targeted therapies for further validation in more representative models. Thus, CDXs continue to serve as essential foundational tools in digestive tumor pharmacology research.

Patient-derived tumor xenograft (PDXs)

PDXs are generated by implanting fresh patient-derived tumor fragments into immunodeficient mice and is widely regarded as a robust translational model because it retains the tissue structure, molecular heterogeneity, and certain microenvironmental characteristics of the original tumor [291, 295–302](Fig. 5B). However, the implantation site is a double-edged sword: while it can faithfully mimic human tumor biology, it also complicates model construction and subsequent assessment of tumor growth and metastasis. Therefore, xenograft models are divided into orthotopic and ectopic, and the choice of implantation site affects tumor behavior and influences the drug development process.

The goal of orthotopic tumor transplantation is to implant tumor cells or tissue into the anatomically appropriate location in experimental animals, thereby replicating the clinical environment for tumor growth and progression [289, 303–305]. In digestive tumor models, for example, colorectal cancer is often transplanted to various sites such as the cecal wall [306], rectal wall [307], cecum [308], or the intestinal surface [309]. Some orthotopic models generate primary tumors with unique technical requirements and metastatic patterns. For instance, cecal implantation is more likely to induce liver metastasis than other sites [310]. However, ectopic transplantation, particularly subcutaneous implantation, is widely used in routine pharmacological studies due to its simplicity, low technical demands, and high tumor survival rate [311]. Tumor xenografts derived from human cancer cell lines typically use subcutaneous ectopic models [312, 313], and mouse tumor cell lines can grow in mice with fully competent immune systems, with commonly used inbred strains such as C57BL/6 and BALB/c being more frequently used for orthotopic implantation [314–316]. However, ectopic models fail to effectively recapitulate the native tumor microenvironment and are less conducive to the development of distant metastases. In addition to conventional transplantation models, there are also specialized models designed for metastasis research. In vivo metastasis models can be broadly divided into experimental and spontaneous metastasis models. Experimental metastasis models are established by directly injecting tumor cells into the systemic circulation of the mouse (e.g., via splenic injection [317]). Depending on the injection site, metastasis typically occurs at specific anatomical locations. However, since this approach bypasses the formation of primary tumors, it is less suitable for studying the effects of drugs on cells originating from metastatic lesions [318]. In contrast, spontaneous metastasis models can faithfully replicate the entire metastatic cascade, providing a more accurate representation of tumor metastasis [319, 320].

It is also noteworthy that many syngeneic and CDXs use immortalized tumor cell lines, which have undergone prolonged passage. Although these methods promote rapid tumor growth and survival, they may also lead to significant and irreversible changes in cellular biology [321, 322], thus limiting the predictive value of these models for clinical outcomes [290]. PDXs, which use tumor tissue derived from patients, help address some of these issues [291, 299]. Similarly , patient-derived organoid-based xenograft models (PDOX),generated by transplanting patient-derived tumor organoids into immunodeficient mice, represent an alternative patient-relevant in vivo platform.Both PDXs and PDOX models maintain much of the structure and composition of the original tumor, more accurately replicate human disease [323–326], and provide a better prediction of how patients will respond to certain treatments [327].

However, the establishment of PDXs typically takes a long time, which limits their ability to provide timely drug selection guidance for patients in certain situations. To address this, Zhang et al. developed MiniPDX, which accelerates model development and drug testing by optimizing the implantation procedure and shortening the in vivo drug response evaluation period [328]. Moreover, in a study establishing a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma PDX model, Liu et al. demonstrated that by partially excising PDX tissue once it reached a certain size and continuing to culture it, this method significantly accelerated tumor growth compared to traditional passaging methods [329].

Syngeneic transplantation

An ideal tumor mouse model should spontaneously develop cancer, with a high incidence within in a short period, the ability to non-invasively monitor disease progression, and histological and molecular characteristics that closely resemble human cancer [330, 331]. In the field of digestive malignancies, a representative system approaching these criteria is the immunocompetent CRC model based on transplantation of mouse-derived organoids. In this approach, CRC organoids carrying defined oncogenic mutations or derived from spontaneous/induced tumors are orthotopically engrafted into the caecal wall or submucosa of syngeneic recipient mice, where they form primary tumors within a fully functional immune system. This strategy enables more faithful reconstruction of CRC initiation, progression and metastasis in vivo, as well as the associated tumor–immune microenvironment interactions. For example, O’Rourke et al. used orthotopic transplantation of colon organoids to establish an immunocompetent CRC model that recapitulated the full trajectory from adenoma to adenocarcinoma and, ultimately, metastatic disease over the course of several months [332]. Implemented in syngeneic, immunocompetent hosts, CRC mouse organoid transplantation models reduce variability in tumor initiation and progression compared with the traditional inducible GEMMs mentioned later, allow non-invasive monitoring of primary and metastatic growth through luciferase-based bioluminescence imaging (IVIS), and eliminate the need for AOM/DSS-induced, inflammation-driven tumorigenesis. Moreover, orthotopic implantation of mouse-derived CRC organoids into the caecum enables subsequent surgical resection of the primary tumor, closely mimicking clinical practice and providing an extended time window to study metastatic progression; for example, Cañellas-Socias et al. orthotopically implanted syngeneic CRC organoids into the cecal submucosa, performed microsurgical resection, and observed liver metastasis driven by a small population of residual EMP1⁺ cells within a few weeks [144].

Humanized mice

Traditional PDXs rely on severely immunodeficient mice, which lack a human-like immune system and therefore cannot fully recapitulate the immune interactions that occur in patients. Although PDXs retain some intrinsic components of the TME, they are inherently insufficient to mimic the dynamic immune processes that affect the biology of colorectal, gastric, and pancreatic cancers. To address this limitation, humanized mouse models have been developed to reconstruct an immune microenvironment that more closely resembles that of humans. Classical humanization strategies involve engrafting CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs), or bone marrow-liver-thymus (BLT) tissues into severely immunodeficient mice (Table 2), thereby reconstituting partial human immune function and providing a platform for investigating human-specific tumor biology and precision oncology [333–342].

Humanized mouse models can generally be categorized into two groups: (a) Genetic chimeras, in which human genes or chromosomal fragments are introduced into the mouse genome – these models generally refer to genetically engineered mouse models; and (b) Cellular chimeras, generated by transplanting human immune or tissue cells into immunodeficient mice, where they differentiate and form human-like tissues (Fig. 5B). Normal human cells can be genetically modified to carry specific mutations and subsequently engrafted orthotopically into mice, enabling researchers to trace tumor initiation and progression in vivo. This approach makes it possible to study early molecular events in tumorigenesis – an aspect that conventional PDXs cannot readily capture, as they typically represent tumors at more advanced stages of disease. Distinction of xenograft tumor models

Zebra fish

Zebrafish patient-derived xenograft models (zPDX) represent an innovative tumor model capable of determining drug testing protocols in approximately 5 days, with drug efficacy assessments completed within a month, significantly improving personalized drug testing (Fig. 7). The zPDX model offers several advantages, including simple morphology, short reproductive cycles, high reproductive output, short modeling time, low tissue requirements from patients, and high drug sensitivity. Studies have shown that zPDX requires only a small number of patient tumor cells, typically fewer than 500 cells, and in some cases, as few as 100–200 cells are sufficient for patient-specific drug sensitivity analysis [345–349]. Additionally, studies have shown that approximately 82% of pathogenic human proteins have orthologs in zebrafish [350, 351].

Due to its short experimental cycle and high drug sensitivity, zPDX has become an ideal choice for preclinical personalized drug testing. In colorectal cancer, zebrafish PDXs successfully mimicked the patient response to 5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin treatment, with the results consistent with clinical observations, showing more apoptotic cells in patients who did not experience relapse after treatment. Similarly, the zebrafish PDXs carrying KRAS-mutated tumors showed no response to cetuximab, which was consistent with clinical findings [346].In gastric cancer, Wu et al. successfully transplanted two human gastric cancer cell lines, AGS and SGC-7901, into zebrafish embryos and assessed their sensitivity to 5-FU. The results showed that SGC-7901 was more sensitive to 5-FU both in vitro and in vivo [352]. This model exhibited features such as embryo migration, angiogenesis, and tumor proliferation, while also reflecting the drug sensitivity to the selected chemotherapy agent. To create a zPDX model for pancreatic cancer, Di Franco et al. used CM-Dil stained small tissue fragments, which were implanted into the yolk of zebrafish embryos. This improved model may provide a new tool for predicting patient responses to chemotherapy [353].

Role of xenograft models in the discovery of digestive tumors

PDX clinical trials and biobanks

PDXs have become essential tools for evaluating drug efficacy and sensitivity, leading to the emergence of the concept of “PDX clinical trials.“ [299, 354, 355](Fig. 8) When integrated with clinical decision-making as co-clinical trials, PDXs enable more efficient prioritization of candidate therapies, stratification of responders, identification of predictive biomarkers, and elucidation of resistance mechanisms [356].

PDXs maintain highly stable biological and phenotypic characteristics over serial passaging [357], allowing comprehensive characterization of tumor mutational landscapes through genome and transcriptome profiling [358], thereby supporting personalized therapeutic strategies. A representative example is the NCI Pediatric Preclinical Testing Program, which established 87 pediatric tumor PDXs and demonstrated that these faithfully recapitulate the histological and molecular features of the original tumors [359, 360]. Importantly, PDXs exhibited treatment responses consistent with those observed in patients, underscoring their value in predicting therapeutic outcomes. In another prospective study involving pancreatic cancer, PDXs were used to evaluate 63 therapeutic regimens across 14 patients. Among the 11 patients who subsequently received PDXs-guided therapy, 9 achieved durable partial responses [358], further supporting the feasibility of PDXs-based co-clinical trial strategies for individualized treatment selection.

With the advancement of PDXs technologies, numerous biobanks containing diverse cancer types with extensive genomic and transcriptomic annotations have been established worldwide [361]. These repositories serve as critical resources for preclinical drug development. However, the creation and maintenance of PDXs biobanks are labor-intensive and costly, limiting their use in large-scale drug screening. To address this challenge, researchers have developed PDX-derived cancer cell lines [362, 363] and PDX-derived organoid (PDXO) models [364, 365]. These in vitro systems retain the key molecular and functional features of the original PDX tumors and exhibit drug responses comparable to their in vivo counterparts across multiple cancer types [366], offering practical alternatives for medium-throughput drug screening.

Drug efficacy

PDX models closely recapitulate patient responses to chemotherapeutic agents, likely due to their retention of human tumor stroma together with the acquisition of mouse-derived stromal components in vivo [367–370](Fig. 8). Multiple studies have shown that histological features and the expression of tumor-associated markers such as EpCAM, E-cadherin, CEA, and Ki-67 remain stable across serial passages in nude mice [371]. In addition, the sensitivity of PDXs to standard chemotherapies aligns well with clinical outcomes, supporting their use in drug screening and response prediction [297]. Large PDXs cohorts derived from the same tumor specimen further enable analyzes of genotype–response relationships and identification of optimal therapeutic strategies [372].

Despite substantial advances in targeted therapies, many digestive cancers exhibit limited responses, and resistance frequently emerges even with combination regimens, prompting ongoing efforts to uncover the mutational mechanisms that drive therapeutic resistance. In colorectal cancer, frontline chemotherapy commonly includes 5-fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan [373]; however, chemoresistance often emerges during disease progression, compromising therapeutic efficacy [374, 375]. Targeting oncogenic drivers is therefore essential. Approximately 30% of human malignancies are driven by activating mutations in the RAS proto-oncogene family (KRAS, HRAS, NRAS) [376, 377]. These mutations lock RAS proteins in an active GTP-bound state, amplifying oncogenic signaling and promoting downstream pathway activation that fuels tumor growth and metastasis [378, 379]. In digestive cancers—particularly CRC and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma—RAS mutations most frequently occur at codon G12 of KRAS [380, 381].

Although KRASG12C inhibitors such as sotorasib (AMG 510) and adagrasib (MRTX849) have received regulatory approval, they address only a small subset of RAS-mutant cancers [382, 383]. Recently, substantial progress has been made toward inhibiting non-KRASG12C variants. For example, the KRASG12D selective RAS(ON) inhibitor zoldonrasib (RMC-9805) induces deep and durable tumor regressions in multiple KRASG12D driven xenograft models and has shown promising objective responses in early clinical trials [384]. MRTX1133 demonstrates potent in vitro and in vivo activity against KRASG12D mutant pancreatic and colorectal cancers [385], while the multi-selective RAS(ON) inhibitor RMC-6236 (daraxonrasib) has achieved superior progression-free survival compared with chemotherapy in second-line PDAC, with favorable tolerability [386, 387]. Leveraging the PDX platform, these candidates can be evaluated in biologically faithful in vivo systems, supporting the transition from allele-specific targeting toward broader multi-mutation or pan-RAS inhibition strategies.

Humanized mouse immunotherapy

Humanized mouse models are widely used to evaluate a variety of digestive cancer immunotherapies, including cancer vaccines, adoptive cell therapies, and immune checkpoint blockade treatments [388] (Fig. 8). Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1/PD-L1 or CTLA-4 have been extensively tested in PBMC- or HSC-reconstituted humanized mice, either as monotherapies or in combination with approaches such as CAR-T cells or histone deacetylase inhibitors. For example, Zhao et al. [342] demonstrated that pembrolizumab-mediated PD-1 blockade or ipilimumab-mediated CTLA-4 blockade reactivated hCD8+ T cells, increased the M1/M2 macrophage ratio, and inhibited hepatocellular carcinoma growth. Bispecific T-cell-engaging antibodies targeting tumor antigens have also shown therapeutic promise in humanized models of multiple malignancies. CEA-directed bispecific antibodies were reported to enhance T-cell tumor infiltration and induce regression of poorly infiltrated colorectal and other solid tumors in PBL-HIS mice [389].

Notably, humanized mouse models generated by co-engrafting autologous immune cells and PDXs function as “avatar models,“ [390] offering a platform to predict individual responses to immunotherapy and to guide personalized treatment upon relapse. However, their clinical utility is limited by practical constraints: many patients with advanced cancers do not survive long enough to complete the time-consuming steps required-PDXs establishment (often faster in more aggressive tumors), in vivo expansion to produce sufficient material, and comparative preclinical testing of standard and experimental therapies. Despite these challenges, humanized mouse systems have been successfully applied in several studies and provide valuable tools for modeling patient-specific immune responses.

Limitations and challenges

Although xenograft models play important roles in tumor biology and drug development, their application still faces both practical and theoretical challenges. First, despite advantages such as operational convenience and strong controllability of CDXs, prolonged in vitro culture leads to genomic and phenotypic drift, loss of original tumor heterogeneity, and the absence of a native tumor microenvironment, resulting in limited clinical relevance. Second, PDXs require a long establishment process and high costs, requiring several months from patient sample collection to generating a usable model. Large-scale PDX libraries exist only in research centers with abundant resources, further limiting their application in real-time clinical decision making [299, 391–395]. Both conventional PDXs and CDXs are highly dependent on immunodeficient mice, which cannot replicate the complex interactions between tumors and the human immune system. This leads to an inability to directly evaluate immune therapies targeting human-specific markers, such as PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies [371, 390, 396–401]. Although humanized models partially address these limitations by reconstructing a human hematopoietic and immune cell system, they still present shortcomings such as incomplete immune development, dysfunctional immune cells (e.g., impaired T-cell activation, aberrant cytokine profiles), and a lack of human lymphoid structures. Additionally, these models are prone to GVHD and have limited observational windows, constraining extrapolation to real clinical responses [333, 402–405].

Additionally, the PDXs exhibited significant sample bias. Problems such as high proliferation, slow growth or low success rates in establishing models lead to a PDX biobank enriched with more aggressive and poor-prognosis tumor types [344, 406, 407]. Human stromal cells in PDXs are gradually replaced by murine stromal components as passaging progresses. Finally, only tumor epithelial cells retain their human origin, which affects the accuracy of evaluating drugs that depend on the microenvironment [408]. Ethical and animal welfare requirements also pose limitations for tumor growth and experimental timelines. In addition, microbiome differences, animal facility environmental factors, and genetic drift due to clonal evolution in the models impair the comparability and reproducibility of experimental data [409–411]. Therefore, transplanted tumor models need to be complemented with other platforms, such as genetically engineered mice, induced models, and organoids, to simulate tumor biology and drug response more comprehensively, providing diverse support for new drug development and precision.

Genetically engineered models (GEMs)

Genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) are widely regarded as the most comprehensive systems for recapitulating the full course of tumor initiation and progression. These models enable the reconstruction of tumor development - from initiation and expansion to invasion and metastasis - within an intact immune system and native stromal microenvironment, making them indispensable for elucidating oncogenic mechanisms, identifying driver genes, and evaluating targeted therapies.(Fig. 5C) Germline GEMs are generated by introducing stable genetic alterations into embryonic stem cells or fertilized oocytes, allowing cell type - specific inactivation of tumor suppressor genes through direct mutation or expression of interfering non-coding RNAs, as well as controlled expression of oncogenes at physiological or tumor-relevant levels [412].

With advances in genetic engineering technologies, traditional germline models have been complemented by more flexible non-germline GEM approaches. These include GEM-derived allograft (GDA) models, ESC-derived chimeras, and somatic gene-editing strategies. GDA models combine the precise genetic backgrounds of GEMMs with the technical accessibility of PDXs, enabling rapid expansion and analysis of tumors with defined genotypes [413]. ESC-derived chimeric mice are generated by introducing genetically modified embryonic stem cells into blastocysts, providing an efficient platform for producing animals containing distinct genetic cell populations and accelerating the development of compound mutant models [414]. Somatic gene-editing strategies introduce genetic alterations directly into the somatic tissues of adult mice - typically using viral vectors or CRISPR/Cas9 - thereby generating tumor models without the need for germline transmission and more closely reflecting the sporadic nature of human cancers [413].

Traditional mouse cancer models have largely relied on transgenic and gene-targeting technologies to drive tumorigenesis by persistently activating oncogenes or inactivating tumor suppressor genes in vivo. Early transgenic models were generated by microinjecting exogenous DNA into the pronucleus of fertilized oocytes, leading to random genomic integration and tissue-specific overexpression of viral or cellular oncogenes [415, 416]. Subsequent embryonic stem cell (ESC)-based transgenesis combined with homologous recombination markedly improved the precision of genetic manipulation. Gene targeting can be used to generate loss-of-function alleles through knockout, or to introduce point mutations, loxP sites, reporter cassettes, or oncogenes at defined loci via knock-in, thereby enabling assessment of oncogenic potential under endogenous regulatory control and facilitating the construction of targeted transgenic models using safe-harbor loci such as ROSA26s [417–420]. Building on these advances, conditional and inducible systems, including Cre–loxP, Flp–FRT, Cre-ERT/ERT2, and tetracycline-regulated Tet-On/Tet-Off systems [421–424], provide spatial and temporal control over somatic mutations and allow more faithful modeling of sporadic genetic events in restricted cell populations. When combined with these tools, chromosome engineering strategies can be used to generate mouse models carrying predefined chromosomal rearrangements [425]. In parallel, in vivo RNA interference [426, 427] and viral vector–mediated somatic gene transfer using adenoviral, retroviral, or lentiviral vectors [428]offer complementary approaches for rapidly interrogating gene function and reconstructing somatic cancer-driving events.

Application of genetically engineered models in the discovery of digestive tumors

Mechanistic model for digestive tumor

GEMMs play a central role in elucidating disease mechanisms and validating potential therapeutic targets. By enabling precise manipulation of specific genes, these models allow direct assessment of causal relationships between candidate genes and disease phenotypes in vivo (Fig. 9). Typically, researchers design mouse models based on recurrent, cancer-associated genetic alterations identified in human tumors, thereby recapitulating, as far as possible, the genetic, molecular and phenotypic features of human malignancies [429–431]. GEMMs thus serve as indispensable tools for target discovery, mechanistic dissection and functional evaluation.

In the field of digestive tumor research, the Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 knock-in mouse represents one of the most seminal genetically engineered models. Lgr5 (also known as Gpr49) was first identified in 2007 as a marker of intestinal stem cells (ISCs) [432]. Building on this discovery, Barker et al. used the Lgr5-EGFP-CreERT2 line in combination with a conditional Apc deletion model to demonstrate that Lgr5+ ISCs serve as cells of origin for colorectal cancer, thereby providing in vivo evidence that tumors preferentially arise from long-lived stem cell populations [433]. When this driver is combined with various oncogenic alterations (such as Apc loss or Kras activation), it further supports the notion that sustained tumor growth depends on stem cell populations with long-term self-renewal capacity rather than short-lived transit-amplifying progenitors. Complementary to the CreERT2-based lineage-tracing strategy, the Lgr5-DTR mouse generated by the Tian et al. provides a powerful ablation approach. In this model, the diphtheria toxin receptor (DTR) is knocked into the Lgr5 locus, rendering Lgr5+ cells highly sensitive to diphtheria toxin and allowing their selective elimination [434]. Studies using this system have shown that, upon depletion of Lgr5⁺ cells, secondary or “reserve” stem cell populations can be activated to restore tissue renewal, revealing pronounced plasticity and a hierarchical organization within the intestinal stem cell compartment. These findings indicate that targeting LGR5+ cancer stem cells alone is unlikely to achieve durable remission and that concomitant suppression of cell populations with the potential to reacquire stemness is likely required.

Building on these models, investigators have further dissected stem cell dynamics and clonal evolution using multicolor lineage tracing and organoid technologies. Snippert et al. activated a multicolor Confetti reporter in Lgr5+ cells and proposed a model in which crypt maintenance is achieved by a pool of Lgr5+ stem cells with broadly equivalent potential, maintained through neutral competition and stochastic clonal drift [435]. Schepers et al. subsequently combined Lgr5-Confetti labeling with inducible oncogenic mutations in intestinal adenomas and demonstrated that only a small subset of Lgr5+ stem-like cells sustains long-term tumor growth, functionally supporting the cancer stem cell hypothesis [436]. In parallel, Drost et al. used gene editing in intestinal organoids to sequentially introduce driver mutations in APC, TP53, KRAS, and SMAD4, thereby reconstructing the malignant progression of colorectal cancer in vitro [141]. These organoid-based, genetically engineered systems complement in vivo GEMMs, particularly for studying human-specific tumor biology and systematically evaluating the impact of defined genetic alterations on drug sensitivity.

It is important to note that the classical Lgr5-CreERT2 driver is active in Lgr5+ stem cells throughout the intestinal tract. In practice, however, simultaneous induction of Apc loss in all Lgr5+ cells often results in a high burden of small-intestinal adenomas, and mice frequently succumb to extensive small-intestinal disease before colonic tumors can fully develop, leading to a pronounced “small intestine bias." [432, 433] To overcome this limitation, recent work from the Barker laboratory [437] has identified novel markers that define region-specific colonic stem cell pools and used them to generate new CreERT2 driver lines targeting colonic stem cells. By exploiting regulatory elements of these genes, they established Nox1-CreERT2 and Npy1r-CreERT2 models, which selectively induce recombination in stem cell compartments of the caecum and distal colon, respectively. Such colon stem cell - targeted CreERT2 drivers not only facilitate dissection of the contributions of distinct stem cell subpopulations to colorectal cancer initiation, but also provide crucial tools for establishing advanced preclinical colon cancer models that more faithfully recapitulate human disease.

Immunology research

Although the induction model and GEMM differ in several ways, including the controllability and timing of genetic alterations, both ultimately represent endogenous or spontaneous forms of tumorigenesis. These models enable tumors to be generated in the native mesenchymal and immune microenvironment, providing a valuable platform for studying digestive cancer biology and treatment response. For example, mouse models of APC mutations carrying germline heterozygous Apc mutations, such as ApcMin/+, spontaneously develop multiple intestinal tumors and are widely used to study path-dependent tumorigenesis and evaluate therapies targeting APC/Wnt and p53 signaling [438]. In addition, because GEMMs retain an intact immune system, they are well-suited for evaluating immunotherapies associated with digestive malignancies, including oncolytic viruses, immune cell therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors. This advantage is demonstrated by the KPC pancreatic cancer model, which has been used to assess checkpoint blockade under immune conditions and reveal how the tumor-immune microenvironment shapes treatment response [439]. Although GEMMs may not capture the genetic diversity of human digestive cancer as fully as PDXs, their natural tumor development and intact immune environment make them an important component of preclinical drug evaluation in digestive oncology.

Limitations and challenges

Despite the evident advantages of GEMMs, there are still multiple limitations and challenges in their application. First, the process of establishing GEMMs is costly and time-consuming. It takes several months to more than a year from genetic design to the breeding of genetically engineered mice with stable phenotypes, significantly limiting the speed and efficiency of research progress [440, 441]. Second, tumorigenesis in GEMMs occurs naturally and exhibits considerable individual variability. Tumors in mice with the same genetic background differ in timing, location, and progression rate, posing challenges for experimental design and statistical analysis [442, 443]. Moreover, since tumors are formed within the original organ, it is difficult to employ simple and intuitive measurement methods, such as caliper measurements of subcutaneous masses. In this case, high-cost imaging techniques such as in vivo fluorescence imaging, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or ultrasound are employed to track tumor progression . However, these technologies are complex and cannot capture subtle tumor changes in real time, further increasing research costs [444, 445]. Therefore, mouse survival or terminal tumor burden are often used as primary indicators to assess the efficacy of GEMMs drugs. More animal models and longer observation periods are needed, further exacerbating resource consumption [446]. More importantly, interspecies differences significantly limit the direct clinical translation of GEMMs findings. Although mouse tumors resemble human tumors in terms of pathological features, discrepancies, including oncogene regulatory networks and immune system composition, lead to the clinical failure of many therapies that behave well in GEMMs [447–449]. Furthermore, owing to their technical complexity and high cost, GEMMs are not suitable for large-scale drug screening. Instead, they are often applied in in-depth investigations of tumor mechanisms and are used to conduct thorough efficacy evaluations of candidate drugs [274, 450, 451]. In practice, researchers often use cell models or organoids to obtain preliminary evidence before turning to GEMMs for more comprehensive and long-term mechanistic validation and efficacy assessment, thereby generating preclinical data that more closely reflect real clinical scenarios.

Conclusion

The inherent complexity, significant heterogeneity, and organ-specific microenvironment characteristics of digestive malignancies require a diverse and multi-level modeling framework to support mechanistic research and translational drug development. From traditional 2D cultures to advanced 3D systems and a wide range of in vivo mouse models, each method tier contributes irreplaceable advantages to tumor biology research and therapeutic evaluation. Due to their accessibility and reproducibility, 2D cell lines continue to be the basis for high-throughput drug screening and molecular mechanism exploration. Three-dimensional tumor spheroids can better simulate intratumoral gradients and structural constraints, thus providing a favorable platform for studying drug penetration and resistance. Patient-derived organoids faithfully preserve patient-specific genomes and phenotypic structures, which not only support routine drug testing and mechanistic dissection, but also have great potential in predicting individualized treatment response and advancing the development of precision oncology. Organ on a chip technology further expands these capabilities by integrating microfluidic kinetics and multi-organ communication, providing a more physiologically meaningful system for assessing drug pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and toxicity. In vivo models remain essential for evaluating treatment efficacy and faithfully reproducing disease progression. Chemically induced and genetically engineered mouse models enable the study of early tumorigenesis and gene-specific oncogenic pathways, while CDX and PDX models continue to function as important platforms for preclinical drug testing.
