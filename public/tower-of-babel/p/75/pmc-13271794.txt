Utility of genomic sequencing classifier in managing cytologically indeterminate oncocytic thyroid nodules
Authors: Sara Ahmadi, Anupam Kotwal, Athanasios Bikas, Benjamin F Altshuler, Anery Patel, Gunjan Arora, Elena G Hughes, Jetesh Sahadeo, Michael W Yeh, Masha J Livhits
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextOncocytic cells can occur in benign and malignant thyroid nodules, posing a diagnostic challenge. This has reduced the diagnostic performance of molecular testing for indeterminate oncocytic thyroid nodules.ObjectiveTo evaluate the performance of Afirma genomic sequencing classifier (GSC) in thyroid nodules with Bethesda III or IV cytology and oncocytic predominance.Design, setting, patients, and interventionA multicenter retrospective study was conducted in adults with Bethesda III and IV thyroid nodules showing oncocytic predominance who underwent a fine-needle aspiration biopsy and Afirma GSC testing between July 2017 and December 2023. Variables included presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis and Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TIRADS) classification.Main outcome measuresOutcomes included benign call rate (BCR), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of Afirma GSC testing.ResultsOf 359 nodules (57% Bethesda III, 43% Bethesda IV) tested with Afirma GSC, the mean nodule size was 2.0 cm. BCR was 81% and 74% for Bethesda III and IV nodules, respectively. GSC sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 89%, 86%, 24%, and 99% for Bethesda III nodules and 94%, 87%, 50%, and 99% for Bethesda IV nodules, respectively. A concurrent diagnosis of Hashimoto's thyroiditis significantly reduced the specificity and PPV in Bethesda III nodules. TIRADS classification did not affect the BCR or PPV in Bethesda III or IV nodules.ConclusionAfirma GSC has a high BCR and improved performance over earlier generation molecular tests in oncocytic thyroid nodules, particularly for Bethesda IV. However, the PPV in Bethesda III nodules remains low, especially in the presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

Thyroid nodules with indeterminate cytology, classified as Bethesda III-IV, carry a malignancy risk ranging from 15% to 35% (1). Among these, indeterminate nodules with oncocytic predominance pose an even greater management challenge due to the range of histopathology from the benign oncocytic adenoma to the aggressive oncocytic thyroid carcinoma (2-4). Oncocytic cells can arise due to inflammation in autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto's or chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis). The malignancy risk in indeterminate nodules with oncocytic predominance is lower than that of nononcocytic indeterminate nodules (11.4% vs 22.5%) (5). This appears to be impacted by the presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis, which is associated with a lower rate of malignancy in oncocytic nodules (6.2% vs 32%). This lower rate of malignancy in the presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis is likely due to the prevalence of oncocytic cells occurring from the underlying autoimmune inflammatory process rather than reflecting a feature of malignancy.

The primary role of molecular testing in refining the risk of malignancy in indeterminate thyroid nodules is to decrease unnecessary diagnostic surgeries. The first iteration of the Afirma gene expression classifier (GEC) molecular test demonstrated poor performance for indeterminate thyroid nodules with oncocytic predominance (2, 6, 7). Subsequently, the Afirma genomic sequencing classifier (GSC) was refined to improve performance but only included 26 nodules with oncocytic cell predominance in the validation study (8). Studies have confirmed the improved specificity and positive predictive value (PPV) of GSC compared with GEC, but the small number of oncocytic nodules precluded a separate analysis of this important subset (9, 10). In addition, the impact of Hashimoto's thyroiditis on the performance of Afirma GSC in indeterminate thyroid nodules with oncocytic predominance has not been investigated.

Our prior multi-institutional study (11) demonstrated that Afirma GSC is a key tool for managing thyroid nodules with indeterminate cytology, including the higher-risk Bethesda IV category, and that GSC-benign thyroid nodules can be observed similarly to thyroid nodules with benign cytology. We now analyze the performance of Afirma GSC in specific subgroups of indeterminate thyroid nodules with oncocytic predominance, including the impact of Hashimoto's thyroiditis. In this study, we report the performance of Afirma GSC from 3 different institutions to demonstrate its real-world utility in this challenging cohort of indeterminate thyroid nodules.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective multicenter study assessed all patients who underwent a thyroid nodule fine-needle aspiration (FNA) cytology classified as Bethesda III (atypia of undetermined significance [AUS] or follicular lesion of undetermined significance [FLUS]) or Bethesda IV (suspicious for follicular neoplasm or Hürthle cell/oncocytic neoplasm) and had molecular testing with Afirma GSC. As per the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) reclassification/refinement of Bethesda III and IV thyroid nodules, the cases classified as FLUS at our respective institutions, would be categorized as AUS but still be considered as Bethesda III (12, 13). Patients were included if their FNA sample exhibited oncocytic cell predominance between July 31, 2017 and December 31, 2023. Participating sites included University of California Los Angeles (UCLA), Brigham and Women's Hospital (BWH), and University of Nebraska Medical Center (UNMC).

Molecular testing for patients with indeterminate thyroid nodules was performed following shared decision-making. All patients with indeterminate cytology underwent reflexive molecular testing at UCLA, while some patients at BWH and UNMC went directly to surgery based on patient or clinician preference. The standard recommendation at each institution during the study period was for all indeterminate thyroid nodules with oncocytic cell predominance to undergo molecular testing. Patients were excluded if they were < 18 years old, lost to follow-up without having surgery or surveillance imaging (n = 4), or if their molecular test sample was inadequate for analysis. Institutional review board approval was obtained at each participating site, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Study design

Endocrine surgeons, endocrinologists, and radiologists performed thyroid FNAs at the respective institutions. The American Thyroid Association (ATA) or American College of Radiology-Thyroid Imaging, Reporting and Data System (ACR-TIRADS) guidelines were used to determine criteria for FNA. Nodule dimensions, sonographic characteristics, and TIRADS categories were extracted from imaging reports. If ACR-TIRADS classification was not present in the imaging report, then a thyroid-focused endocrinologist at each institution evaluated the thyroid ultrasound images and reported on the individual nodule characteristics, from which we determined the ACR-TIRADS classification for the nodule. Patients managed nonoperatively underwent annual surveillance ultrasound. Follow-up duration was determined as the time from initial FNA to the date of surgery or most recent surveillance ultrasound. Nodule growth was defined by either >20% increase in at least 2 nodule dimensions (with a minimum growth of 3 mm) or >50% increase in nodule volume. Treatment recommendations during follow-up including repeat FNA or surgical resection were made based on ultrasound findings.

FNA results were considered to have oncocytic cell predominance and included in the study according to the WHO definition: oncocytic cell population >75% in the sample, “predominance of oncocytic cells,” “predominance of cells exhibiting oncocytic features,” “oncocytic cell neoplasm,” or “suspicious for oncocytic cell neoplasm.” (14) Oncocytic cell predominance was established based on the cytology report (13). Since this study predated the change in terminology from Hurthle cell to oncocytic, the same results were included if they reported on Hurthle cell predominance. Head and neck pathologists at each institution evaluated the histopathology for patients who underwent surgery. When calculating GSC performance characteristics, GSC-benign nodules that underwent nonoperative management were presumed to be benign. Surgical pathology was considered malignant if thyroid cancer was identified in the same quadrant as the originally biopsied nodule.

Study outcomes and statistical analysis

Patient and nodule characteristics were assessed by Bethesda category for each institution. Variables including patient age, sex, median nodule size, presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis, TIRADS category, and histopathology when available were analyzed. Presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis was defined by 1 or more of the following: histologic evidence of lymphocytic thyroiditis on surgical pathology, positive antibody levels, heterogeneous thyroid parenchyma on ultrasound, or preoperative use of thyroid hormone supplementation after documented hypothyroidism based on thyroid function tests with no other known cause.

The primary study outcomes were benign call rate (BCR), sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and negative predictive value (NPV) of Afirma GSC in Bethesda III and IV oncocytic nodules. Surgical histopathology was considered the reference standard when available. Nonoperatively managed GSC-benign nodules were considered to be benign when calculating performance characteristics. Nodules with GSC-suspicious results that were not surgically resected were excluded from calculations of diagnostic test performance. The false-negative rate was determined by the number of malignancies identified on surgical histopathology among all GSC-benign nodules.

The sensitivity, specificity, NPV, PPV, and false-negative rates were calculated with 95% Wilson confidence intervals, considering P values <.05 statistically significant. Comparisons of test performance between institutions were performed using χ2 tests. Likelihood ratios (LRs) were calculated to assess the incremental diagnostic value of combining molecular test results with the presence or absence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis. The positive likelihood ratio (LR+) reflects the increase in malignancy probability when a GSC-suspicious result is present, while the negative likelihood ratio (LR−) reflects the degree to which a benign result decreases the probability of malignancy. Post-test malignancy rates (derived from Afirma GSC results stratified by Hashimoto's status) were divided by pretest malignancy rates (based on Hashimoto's status alone). All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.3 [2024-02-29, ucrt; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria]).

Results

Patients

A total of 359 Bethesda III/IV nodules with oncocytic predominance that were tested with Afirma GSC across the 3 institutions were included for analysis. Baseline characteristics of patients were similar between Bethesda III and IV nodules and across institutions (Table 1). The overall study cohort was predominantly female (82%) with a median age of 65 years (interquartile range [IQR], 56-72 years), and the median nodule size was 2.0 cm (IQR, 1.55-2.85 cm). Bethesda III nodules represented 57% of the cohort, and Bethesda IV nodules represented 43%. Hashimoto's thyroiditis was present in 10% of Bethesda III nodules and 14% of Bethesda IV nodules. The median follow-up period was 24 months (range: 9-76 months).

GSC performance characteristics in oncocytic cell Bethesda III and IV nodules

Of 203 Bethesda III nodules, 164 were GSC-benign, with a BCR of 81%. Sixteen of the 164 GSC-benign nodules were resected, with 1 malignancy identified. Surgical resection in the false-negative case was performed due to nodule growth on surveillance imaging, and histopathology revealed a 6.9 cm oncocytic carcinoma with minimal (<4 foci) vascular invasion. Of the 39 Bethesda III GSC-suspicious nodules, 34 were resected, with 8 malignancies identified. In Bethesda III nodules, GSC sensitivity was 89% (95% CI: 66.5%, 98%), specificity was 86% (95% CI: 80.6%, 90.4%), PPV was 24% (95% CI: 12.4%, 40%), and NPV was 99% (95% CI: 96.6%, 99.9%). Histopathologic subtypes in the malignant cases included 2 papillary thyroid carcinomas (PTC), 5 oncocytic carcinomas, and 1 follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC).

Of 156 Bethesda IV nodules, 115 were GSC-benign, with a BCR of 74%. Twenty-two of the 115 GSC-benign nodules were resected, with 1 malignancy identified. Histopathology of the 1 false-negative case revealed a 2.5 cm minimally invasive oncocytic carcinoma. Of 41 Bethesda IV GSC-suspicious nodules, 34 were resected, with 17 malignancies identified. Histopathologic subtypes included 1 poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, 2 medullary thyroid carcinomas (MTC), 9 oncocytic carcinomas, 1 FTC, and 4 PTCs. Both cases of MTC were correctly identified by GSC testing prior to surgery. In Bethesda IV nodules, GSC sensitivity was 94% (95% CI: 74.2%, 99%), specificity was 87% (95% CI: 80.2%, 93%), PPV was 50% (95% CI: 34.1%, 66%), and NPV was 99% (95% CI: 95.2%, 99.8%). The PPV of Afirma GSC was higher in Bethesda IV compared with Bethesda III nodules (50% vs 24%, P = .03).

Impact of Hashimoto's thyroiditis on GSC test performance

Among 20 Bethesda III nodules with Hashimoto's thyroiditis, 15 were GSC-benign and 5 were GSC-suspicious (Fig. 1). None of the Bethesda III GSC-benign or GSC-suspicious nodules with Hashimoto's thyroiditis were malignant, resulting in a 0% prevalence of malignancy in this subgroup. Among 183 Bethesda III nodules without Hashimoto's thyroiditis, 149 were GSC-benign and 29 were GSC-suspicious. One malignancy was found among the GSC-benign nodules and 8 malignancies were identified as GSC-suspicious, yielding a malignancy rate of 0.7% in GSC-benign nodules and 28% in GSC-suspicious nodules. In Bethesda III oncocytic nodules with Hashimoto's thyroiditis, the specificity was 75% (95% CI: 50.9%, 91.3%) and PPV was 0% (Table 2). In Bethesda III oncocytic nodules without Hashimoto's thyroiditis, the specificity was 88% (95% CI: 82%, 92%) and PPV was 28% (95% CI: 15%, 46%).

Performance characteristics of Bethesda III and IV nodules according to presence or absence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis

In Bethesda IV nodules with Hashimoto's thyroiditis (n = 22), 15 were GSC-benign and 7 were GSC-suspicious. One malignancy was found among GSC-benign nodules and 3 malignancies were identified as GSC-suspicious, resulting in a 6.7% malignancy rate for benign results and 43% for suspicious results. Among Bethesda IV nodules without Hashimoto's (n = 134), 100 were GSC-benign and 34 were GSC-suspicious. Zero malignancies occurred in the benign group and 14 malignancies were identified in the suspicious group, resulting in a 0% malignancy rate among GSC-benign nodules and 41% among GSC-suspicious nodules. In Bethesda IV oncocytic nodules with Hashimoto's thyroiditis, specificity was 78% (95% CI: 55%, 91%) and PPV was 43% (95% CI: 16%, 75%) (Table 2). In the Bethesda IV oncocytic nodules without Hashimoto's thyroiditis, specificity was 88% (95% CI: 81.3%, 93.2%) and PPV was 52% (95% CI: 34%, 69.3%).

Specificity was lower in patients with Hashimoto's thyroiditis compared with those without across both Bethesda categories. NPV remained consistently high in all subgroups (>93%), emphasizing the reliability of a benign GSC result in Bethesda III and IV oncocytic cell nodules. In oncocytic nodules without Hashimoto's thyroiditis, the LR+ for a GSC-suspicious result was >7, indicating a substantial rise in post-test malignancy probability compared with pretest expectations. The LR+ for a GSC-suspicious result was more modestly elevated at 3.4 in Bethesda IV nodules with Hashimoto's thyroiditis and was not improved in Bethesda III nodules with Hashimoto's thyroiditis (Fig. 1).

GSC test performance according to TIRADS category

Diagnostic test performance was stratified by ultrasound risk as defined by TIRADS (Fig. 2). GSC BCR was not affected by TIRADS category, and was 80-83% for BIII nodules and 75-78% for BIV nodules. PPV was also not significantly affected by TIRADS category; the PPV was 0-24% for BIII nodules and 42-67% for BIV nodules.

Performance characteristics of entire cohort classified by TIRADS category.

Ultrasound features, histopathology, and follow-up data were assessed for all 18 TIRADS 5 nodules in the cohort (15). Of 14 GSC-benign nodules, 3 nodules were resected and confirmed to have benign histopathology. One nodule showed significant growth during ultrasound surveillance, and repeat FNA was benign. The remaining 10 nodules remain stable under surveillance. Of 4 GSC-suspicious nodules, all were resected and 1 nodule was malignant. Review of ultrasound characteristics for nodules classified as TIRADS 5 revealed hypoechoic echogenicity, taller-than-wide shape, and lobulated margins. Notably, multiple nodules were classified as TIRADS 5 due to the presence of punctate echogenic foci on ultrasound; however, these nodules remained stable on ultrasound or were surgically resected and confirmed as benign (15).

Outcomes during surveillance

Ultrasound follow-up of initially nonoperatively managed, GSC-benign oncocytic thyroid nodules was performed over a 9 to 36-month period (Fig. 3). The majority of nodules remained stable over the follow-up period. A small subset of nodules showed minor growth in surveillance. Notably, 4 nodules—2 Bethesda III and 2 Bethesda IV nodules—showed significant growth prompting surgical resection. Of the Bethesda III nodules, one was confirmed benign on histopathology, while the other was found to be malignant. Both Bethesda IV nodules were confirmed benign on histopathology.

Discussion

Indeterminate thyroid nodules with oncocytic predominance continue to pose a challenge in clinical practice. Previous molecular test versions, in particular Afirma GEC, had a low BCR and limited clinical utility to decrease unnecessary diagnostic surgery for oncocytic nodules (7). Our multicenter analysis of 359 indeterminate oncocytic nodules tested with the updated Afirma GSC confirms a BCR of 81% for Bethesda III and 74% for Bethesda IV, demonstrating significant improvement compared with Afirma GEC. The presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis decreased the PPV for Bethesda III nodules, while ultrasound features did not impact the performance of Afirma GSC.

Our results are consistent with previous smaller, single-institution studies that showed improved performance for Afirma GSC in oncocytic thyroid nodules (16, 17). Raghunathan et al, in a prospective clinical comparison of 149 nodules, reported a BCR of 84% (17), and Angell et al, in another study of 31 nodules, reported a BCR of 71% for GSC in oncocytic nodules (16). The high BCR, sensitivity (89% in Bethesda 3% and 94% in Bethesda 4) and NPV (99% in Bethesda 3% and 99% in Bethesda 4) confirm that Afirma GSC reliably rules out malignancy in oncocytic thyroid nodules, allowing more patients with indeterminate nodules to avoid diagnostic surgery. The optimal surveillance strategy following a benign GSC result has not been established. Our institutional practice includes ultrasound at 1, 3, and 5 years after the initial FNA, adjusted based on ultrasound risk classification of the nodule as well as physician and patient preference.

The low specificity (12%) and PPV of Afirma GEC in thyroid nodules with oncocytic predominance were an area of concern in clinical practice (2, 6, 7). Brauner et al reported that 86% of patients with oncocytic nodules classified as suspicious by Afirma GEC underwent unnecessary surgery, reflecting the test's high false-positive rate (2). In the 2018 validation study of Afirma GSC, the specificity of this test for oncocytic lesions was reported at 59% but only 26 oncocytic nodules were included (8). In our study, the performance of Afirma GSC in Bethesda IV oncocytic nodules was similar to the 2018 validation study, with a PPV of 48%. However, while the BCR of Afirma GSC in Bethesda III oncocytic nodules was high, the PPV of 24% was lower than previously reported. Previous studies examined the performance of Afirma GSC in all oncocytic lesions, and they did not compare the test performance between Bethesda III and IV nodules.

Previous studies have suggested that the presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis may lower the risk of malignancy in thyroid nodules with indeterminate cytology. A retrospective study of 354 indeterminate thyroid nodules reported a malignancy risk of 6.2% in nodules with Hashimoto's thyroiditis compared with 32% in nodules without (5). The impact of Hashimoto's thyroiditis on molecular test performance has only been reported in a prior study evaluating Afirma GEC (18). In our study, the presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis was associated with decreased specificity and PPV in Bethesda III nodules but did not significantly affect the performance of Afirma GSC in thyroid nodules with Bethesda IV cytology.

The ATA and ACR-TIRADS ultrasound scoring systems have been widely adopted to predict the risk of malignancy in thyroid nodules, including those with indeterminate cytology (19, 20). However, in a multicenter study of 257 indeterminate nodules tested with Thyroseq v3, neither the ATA nor the TIRADS US scoring system improved cancer risk prediction (21). A retrospective study of 237 thyroid nodules with indeterminate cytology showed that the diagnostic performance of Afirma GSC was similar in ATA high-suspicion compared with ATA nonhigh-suspicion nodules (22). The performance of Afirma GSC in indeterminate oncocytic nodules in our study was not affected by the ACR TIRADS category. Interestingly, of 18 TIRADS 5 nodules in the current study, only 2 patients had malignant histopathology. Multiple nodules were classified as TIRADS 5 due to the presence of echogenic foci. Among all ultrasound features, echogenic interpretation has been shown to have the highest interobserver disagreement (23), suggesting that this ultrasound feature may be less reliable particularly in oncocytic nodules.

The limitations of our study include the small sample size of patients with Hashimoto's thyroiditis. In our study, preoperative use of thyroid hormone supplementation was counted as a diagnosis of Hashimoto's thyroiditis even if TPO/Tg antibodies were not tested or were normal. We understand that this may have led to potentially including other causes of hypothyroidism. However, we excluded surgical or any other known cause of hypothyroidism from this list, and since autoimmune hypothyroidism is the main cause of nonsurgical primary hypothyroidism in the US, most of the cases included with the criteria are still likely to be from Hashimoto's thyroiditis. Oncocytic predominance was determined at the time of initial cytologic evaluation according to the WHO definition. A second independent review was not feasible, but this can be considered in further studies. In addition, since not all patients underwent surgery, those with benign molecular test results and more than 1 year of follow-up were assumed to be benign. Given the slow rate of growth of most thyroid cancers, this follow-up duration may be inadequate to demonstrate nodule growth and may lead to missing some false-negative cases. Hence, NPV calculations in our study may be prone to overestimation. However, this methodology has been utilized by prior studies as the risk of missing malignancy in thyroid nodules with benign Afirma GSC is <2% (11).

In conclusion, the improvement in BCR and PPV for Afirma GSC compared with GEC in indeterminate thyroid nodules with oncocytic predominance has helped decrease the number of unnecessary diagnostic surgeries. However, in this study, the improved GSC performance was only observed in Bethesda IV nodules. The presence of Hashimoto's thyroiditis affects the performance of Afirma GSC Bethesda III but not Bethesda IV nodules with oncocytic predominance. Hence, clinicians should exercise caution when interpreting the results of a suspicious Afirma GSC in Bethesda III nodules with oncocytic predominance, as it may not significantly increase the risk of malignancy, especially when accompanied by Hashimoto's thyroidits.
