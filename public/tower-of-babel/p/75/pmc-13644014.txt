Threshold effect of the METS-VF index on hyperuricemia in adults with hypertension: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Meihui Wu, Congcong Yan, Shiping Li, Lingjuan Zhu, Chao Yu, Tao Wang, Wei Zhou, Weifang Zhang, Huihui Bao, Xiaoshu Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHyperuricemia (HUA) is a major public health concern that is closely linked to obesity. The novel visceral adiposity index (METS-VF) demonstrates significant associations with multiple chronic diseases, yet its association with HUA risk in hypertensive patients remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate this association in a Chinese hypertensive population.MethodsThis study utilized data from the Chinese Hypertension Registry Study, including 13,341 hypertensive patients. Data collection was conducted through standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. We evaluated the association between METS-VF and HUA using multivariate logistic regression models. Robustness was assessed via multivariable-adjusted models and subgroup analyses.ResultsThe overall prevalence of HUA was 55.3%. Each unit increase in METS-VF was associated with a 8.48 (95% CI: 6.64, 10.32) µmol/L increase in serum uric acid levels and a 25% higher risk of HUA (OR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.20, 1.30). HUA risk increased significantly across increasing METS-VF quartiles (P for trend < 0.001). Further analysis revealed a non-linear, threshold relationship. Subgroup analysis indicated a stronger association in women (P for interaction < 0.001).ConclusionIn patients with hypertension, the visceral adiposity index (METS-VF) was positively associated with the risk of hyperuricemia (HUA).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02415-7.

Background

Hyperuricemia (HUA) is a pathological state characterized by the accumulation of uric acid in the blood due to impaired purine metabolism. Its primary causes include excessive uric acid production and/or insufficient renal and intestinal excretion [1, 2]. Globally, the prevalence of HUA ranges from approximately 9.3% to 20.1%, closely linked to changes in dietary patterns, with a trend toward younger onset ages [3–5]. The situation in China is particularly severe, with the prevalence rate rising rapidly from 11.1% in 2015–16 to 14.0% in 2018–19 [6], affecting approximately 170 million people [7]. Concurrently, the prevalence of hypertension among Chinese adults has reached 31.6% in 2021–2022, representing an increase of 8.4% over the past decade [8].It has become a major metabolic health issue on par with hypertension. Extensive evidence indicates that HUA is not only a trigger for gout but also an independent risk factor for multiple diseases, including hypertension [9], diabetes [10], cardiovascular disease [11], stroke [12], chronic kidney disease [10], metabolic syndrome, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [13].

Obesity is a public health challenge affecting over 100 million patients worldwide [14]. Multiple studies [15, 16] have confirmed that obesity (particularly abdominal obesity) is an independent predictor of hyperuricemia. Data from Chinese adults [17, 18] also demonstrate a significant positive correlation between serum uric acid (SUA) levels and obesity severity. In recent years, Bello-Chavolla et al. [19] developed the Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF). This metric integrates the Metabolic Syndrome Index for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), age, and sex, effectively predicting chronic diseases such as diabetes [20], cardiovascular disease [21], and hypertension [22].

However, few studies have explored the association between METS-VF and HUA/SUA levels in hypertensive populations.Given the higher risk of adverse events in this group, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the associations of METS-VF with serum uric acid (SUA) levels and hyperuricemia (HUA) risk in Chinese patients with hypertension.This study sought to clarify the dose-response relationship and provide evidence for early identification of high-risk populations for HUA.

Methods

Participants

The data for this study were derived from baseline information of the China Hypertension Registry Study (Registration number: ChiCTR1800017274; registered on July 20, 2018), a prospective, observational, real-world study registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.This registry study is a multicenter, real-world observational study designed to investigate the clinical characteristics, management status, and long-term prognosis of hypertensive patients in rural China.Wuyuan County, Jiangxi Province, was selected as the site for this study’s field investigation. All participants included were hypertensive patients aged 18 years or older. Hypertension was defined as meeting any of the following criteria at baseline: (1) resting seated systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg; (2) self-reported history of hypertension diagnosed by a physician; or (3) current use of antihypertensive medication(s) [23]. Participants with cognitive or neurological impairment, or those unable to cooperate with study follow-up, were excluded. This study strictly adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Biomedical Sciences, Anhui Medical University (No.: CH1059) and the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (No.: 2018019). All participants signed written informed consent forms prior to enrollment.

A total of 14,234 hypertensive patients were included in this study, exclusions were based on missing data for METS-VF (n = 878), missing data on BMI (n = 5), FPG (n = 7), smoking status (n = 2), and drinking status (n = 1), finally, an analysis was conducted on 13,341 subjects (Fig. 1).

Data collection

All baseline data were collected by uniformly trained investigators following standardized procedures. Data on demographic characteristics (including age and sex), lifestyle factors (such as smoking and alcohol consumption history), medical history (e.g., diabetes, family history of coronary heart disease, and hypertension), and medication use (e.g., antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering drugs) were gathered through structured questionnaires [24]. Anthropometric measurements included height, body weight, and waist circumference, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Blood pressure was measured using an Omron electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron; Dalian, China). Participants rested quietly for at least 5 min, followed by three consecutive measurements at 1-minute intervals; the average value was recorded.

All study subjects were required to fast for at least 8 h prior to blood sample collection. Venous blood samples were centrifuged to separate serum, which was stored at -80 °C. Subsequently, the samples were sent to Shenzhen Biaojia Bioengineering Co., Ltd. for analysis. Fasting Plasma Glucose, a four-item lipid panel (HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, TC), and homocysteine (Hcy) levels were measured using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter, USA). All testing procedures adhered to standardized operating protocols and quality control requirements.The estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was computed using the CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) equation [25].

Definitions

According to the guidelines of the Chinese Society of Endocrinology, hyperuricemia is diagnosed when serum uric acid levels exceed 420 µmol/L (7.0 mg/dL) in males and 360 µmol/L (6.0 mg/dL) in females [26–28]. Diabetes was identified based on any of the following: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, or self-reported diagnosis by a doctor and current use of antidiabetic medication [29, 30]. Statistical analyzes

The presence or absence of HUA categorised the baseline characteristics of the study population. For normally distributed continuous variables, data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and intergroup differences were assessed using one-way ANOVA or t-tests. For skewed continuous variables (Hcy and TG), data were expressed as median (interquartile range), and comparisons across METS-VF quartiles were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, with intergroup differences assessed using chi-square tests.

Multivariate logistic regression was used to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between METS-VF and serum uric acid (SUA) levels and hyperuricemia (HUA). The relationship between the METS-VF index and SUA and HUA was visualised using fully adjusted smoothed curve fits (penalised spline method), with adjustment for major covariates in all three models. Model 1: Crude model (unadjusted); Model 2: Adjusted for Current smoking and Current drinking; Model 3: Model 2 plus DBP, SBP, Hcy, TC, LDL-C, eGFR, current smoking, current drinking, diabetes, lipid-lowering drugs, antiplatelet drugs, Antihypertensive drugs, and glucose-lowering drugs. Additionally, we conducted subgroup analyses to explore potential factors influencing this association.

All statistical analyzes were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; http://www.R-project.org) and EmpowerStats software (version 2.17.8; www.empowerstats.com). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

The final data analysis included 13,341 hypertensive patients. The overall mean age of the participants was 63.95 ± 9.30 years and included 6,187 men (46.38%). The prevalence of hyperuricemia was 55.29% (7,374/13,341), with rates across the first to fourth METS-VF quartiles of 48.28%, 52.35%, 57.65%, and 62.77%, respectively. Higher METS-VF was significantly associated with adverse body composition (higher WC and BMI) and a more adverse metabolic profile, characterized by elevated FPG, TG, and SUA, and reduced HDL-C. It was also associated with a higher prevalence of diabetes and use of related medications. Specifically, participants in higher METS-VF quartiles exhibited greater WC, BMI, DBP, FPG, SUA, and TG, and lower HDL-C. They were also more likely to be female, to have diabetes, and to use lipid-lowering and glucose-lowering drugs. Conversely, the lower proportions of current smokers and drinkers in the higher METS-VF groups may be related to their higher proportion of women. TC and LDL-C levels showed a trend of initial increase followed by a decline (Table 1).

Relationship between METS-VF index and SUA and HUA

Tables 2 and 3 show the association of the METS-VF index with serum uric acid (SUA) levels and hyperuricemia (HUA) risk across different models. The multiple linear regression model indicated that each 1-unit increase in the METS-VF index was associated with a 8.48 (95% CI: 6.64, 10.32) µmol/L increase in SUA levels and a 25% higher risk of HUA (OR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.20, 1.30).

Multivariable linear regression models (Models 1–3) demonstrated a positive association between the METS-VF index and SUA (Table 2). When grouped by METS-VF quartiles, the adjusted β coefficients for SUA in Q2-Q4, compared to Q1, were 9.10 (95% CI: 3.14, 15.06), 20.34 (95% CI: 14.27, 26.41), and 30.47 (95% CI: 24.24, 36.70) in the fully adjusted model (Model 3) (Table 2). Consistently, the ORs of HUA for subjects in Q2-Q4 of the METS-VF index were 1.26 (95% CI: 1.11, 1.43), 1.73 (95% CI: 1.52, 1.97), and 2.22 (95% CI: 1.94, 2.54) respectively, compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1). A significant dose-response relationship was observed, with the strength of association increasing across METS-VF quartiles (P for trend < 0.001) (Table 3).

Dose-response and threshold effect analysis

To characterize the non-linear relationships and threshold effects of METS-VF on serum uric acid (SUA) levels and hyperuricemia (HUA) risk, we applied smoothing curve fitting. As visualized in Fig. 2, a significant dose-response relationship was identified for both conditions. (Fig. 2).

As shown in Tables 4 and 5, the two-piecewise regression analysis identified a METS-VF inflection point of 8.5 in the fully adjusted Model 3. Below this threshold, each 1-unit increase in METS-VF was significantly associated with a 24.44 µmol/L rise in serum uric acid (SUA) levels (95% CI: 21.94, 26.93) and a higher risk of hyperuricemia (HUA) (OR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.49, 1.68). Above this threshold, the associations were no longer significant for both (β = 0.81, 95% CI: -2.05, 3.68) and HUA risk (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.94, 1.08) (Tables 4 and 5).

Subgroup analyses

We further conducted stratified analyzes to assess the impact of the METS-VF index (per 1-unit increase) on hyperuricemia across different subgroups. No significant interactions were observed in the subgroups of age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), BMI (< 24 vs. ≥24 kg/m²), eGFR (< 60 vs. ≥60 mL/min per 1.73 m²), current smoking (no vs. yes), current drinking (no vs. yes), or diabetes (no vs. yes) (all P for interaction > 0.05). However, a significant interaction was observed with sex. The association between the METS-VF index and hyperuricemia was stronger in women (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.35, 1.52) than that in men (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.14, 1.27; P for interaction < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this large, population-based study of 13,341 Chinese adults with hypertension, we identified a significant positive association between the METS-VF index and hyperuricemia, which exhibited a non-linear, threshold-dependent pattern (inflection point at METS-VF = 8.5) and was stronger in women.These findings extend prior literature by characterizing, for the first time in a hypertensive cohort, the dose-response relationship between METS-VF and HUA. Unlike previous studies in general populations [38, 39], our identification of a threshold effect suggests that interventions to reduce visceral adiposity may yield the greatest urate-lowering benefit in patients with METS-VF below 8.5. From a public health perspective, METS-VF could serve as a low-cost indicator to identify hypertensive patients at higher odds of coexisting HUA, particularly in women.

Although traditional indicators such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) have been widely demonstrated to correlate with hyperuricemia (HUA) [31–33], their clinical utility is limited. BMI cannot distinguish lean from fat mass and fails to reflect visceral fat accumulation, whereas WC and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) are susceptible to influences from ethnicity, sex, and age [34, 35]. Therefore, more precise indicators are needed to assess metabolic risk.In recent years, the Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) has garnered attention as a novel indicator for assessing HUA risk. The strength of METS-VF lies in its integration of multiple parameters, including age, sex, WHtR, and the Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR) [21, 36], enabling a more comprehensive assessment of visceral adipose tissue function.

Multiple studies support its predictive value. For instance, Ji et al. [37] conducted a multicenter cross-sectional study involving 8,877 patients with hypertension and hyperuricemia, finding a significant positive correlation between METS-VF and gout risk. Each one-standard-deviation increase in METS-VF was associated with an 82% higher risk of gout (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.62, 2.03). This finding aligns with the role of METS-VF in broader uric acid metabolism abnormalities. A large-scale retrospective cohort study by Liu et al. [38] confirmed that in non-obese adults, METS-VF was significantly and positively associated with HUA incidence, an association particularly pronounced in women. Xie et al. [39], using NHANES data, further validated that the highest METS-VF quartile carried a 6.07-fold higher HUA risk compared to the lowest quartile, with stronger associations observed in women and certain ethnic groups. Our findings in a hypertensive population are consistent, showing a significant association between METS-VF and HUA risk.

Our findings demonstrate a positive association between METS-VF and HUA, which can be explained by its representation of visceral adipose tissue dysfunction. Excess visceral fat leads to an increased influx of free fatty acids to the liver via the portal vein, which not only promotes triglyceride synthesis but also enhances uric acid production by altering purine metabolism [40]. Concurrently, adipose tissue dysfunction induces insulin resistance, which impairs renal function and reduces uric acid excretion [41], in hypertensive patients, hyperuricemia (HUA) commonly coexists with hypertension, and the two conditions often mutually exacerbate each other [42]. Basic research has shown that elevated serum uric acid (SUA) contributes to the progression of hypertension through mechanisms such as renal impairment, chronic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [43, 44]. Observational studies have consistently identified SUA as an independent risk factor for both hypertension and cardiovascular events [45–48]. This underscores the importance of investigating HUA in this population.

Importantly, the relationship between METS-VF and HUA/SUA exhibited a non-linear dose–response relationship with a threshold effect. Our two-piecewise regression analysis identified an inflection point at a METS-VF value of 8.5 (Tables 4 and 5; Fig. 2). Below this threshold, each 1-unit increase in METS-VF was strongly associated with elevated SUA and HUA risk. Above 8.5, the association plateaued and became non-significant.In our cohort, 2,650 participants (19.87%) had METS-VF values exceeding 8.5, indicating that this subgroup is clinically relevant.Notably, a multicenter study by Ji et al. [37] also reported a threshold effect for METS-VF and gout risk, with an inflection point of 7.42—above which the association became non-significant (OR ≈ 1.00). The difference in inflection points (8.5 vs. 7.42) likely reflects differences in outcome severity and population characteristics.Notably, our cohort consisted exclusively of hypertensive patients, who have a higher baseline metabolic burden and a right-shifted METS-VF distribution compared to general populations [49, 50].

Several factors may explain this saturation effect. First, excessive visceral fat impairs uric acid metabolism through insulin resistance, inflammation, and renal dysfunction. Once these pathways become maximally activated beyond a certain level, additional fat accumulation may not further increase SUA levels—a biological ceiling effect [51]. Second, the right-skewed distribution of METS-VF may reduce statistical power at the extreme upper tail. Third, similar saturation effects have been reported for METS-VF with gout 7.42 [37] and carotid atherosclerosis 8.09 [52, 53]. This suggests that while accumulating visceral fat critically increases HUA risk up to a point, extremely high levels may not confer proportionally higher risk.

Our study further identified a stronger association between METS-VF and HUA in women. This may be explained by menopausal changes: declining estrogen levels promote visceral fat accumulation and reduce fat-free mass [54], with postmenopausal women exhibiting significantly higher visceral adiposity than premenopausal women [54]. Notably, visceral fat poses greater metabolic hazards in women than in men [55], which aligns with the epidemiological trend of HUA prevalence in women exceeding that in men after the age of 65 [56, 57]. By incorporating age and sex, METS-VF effectively captures this sex-specific pattern of visceral fat accumulation and the consequent HUA risk.

In summary, in our hypertensive cohort, visceral obesity as captured by the METS-VF index and its related metabolic disturbances may form a shared pathophysiological link among obesity, HUA, and hypertension. For instance, obesity-related dyslipidemia can reduce uric acid excretion [58, 59], while SUA-associated inflammatory responses may further raise blood pressure [60, 61], thereby creating a vicious cycle. Given that visceral fat poses a greater threat to uric acid metabolism than subcutaneous fat [62], METS-VF offers a superior assessment capability. Therefore, the early use of METS-VF may help to identify hypertensive patients who have a higher likelihood of presenting with HUA. This cross-sectional association provides a rationale for future prospective studies to evaluate whether targeting METS-VF could inform targeted interventions and potentially improve cardiovascular outcomes.

The primary strength of this study lies in its pioneering investigation of the association between the Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) and hyperuricemia (HUA) in a Chinese hypertensive population. Additionally, the large, population-based sample provided substantial statistical power, and the robustness of the findings was supported by subgroup analyses.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the exact role of METS-VF in the progression of HUA warrants verification in prospective studies. Second, although multiple potential confounders were adjusted for, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Third, as all participants were of Chinese origin, the generalizability of the results to other ethnic groups requires further validation. Fourthly, as this study utilised data from a single-centre population, the findings require validation in multicentre clinical trials. Fifth, although the lower diagnostic threshold for hyperuricaemia (HUA) in women may partly explain the stronger association observed in women, a recent study by Ji et al. [37] using the same gender-specific criteria found that the predictive performance of METS-VF for gout was higher in men (AUC = 0.774) than in women (AUC = 0.690). This suggests that differences in diagnostic thresholds alone do not universally account for the stronger associations observed in women across all studies; biological mechanisms such as the accumulation of visceral fat after menopause [54–57] are likely to play a significant role. Finally, although our questionnaire recorded specific medications (e.g., thiazide diuretics, losartan, and aspirin) that may affect serum uric acid levels, these were not extracted as separate variables in our dataset. We were therefore unable to adjust for them individually in the regression models. However, we adjusted for overall antihypertensive, antiplatelet, and lipid-lowering medication use, which may partially capture the effects of these drugs. Additionally, no participants were on documented urate-lowering therapy in this rural cohort, as these medications are not routinely prescribed. Future studies should collect detailed medication information to minimize residual confounding.

Conclusion

In this large hypertensive cohort, the METS-VF index was positively and independently associated with hyperuricemia risk, particularly among women.This association was particularly pronounced in female patients. The METS-VF index, which integrates age, sex, anthropometric measures, and metabolic parameters, shows promise as an indicator for identifying hypertensive patients at higher odds of coexisting HUA, pending prospective validation. Its application could potentially improve hyperuricemia screening in the rural population of Jiangxi Province, China, and offer new avenues for developing urate-lowering strategies in obese, hypertensive patients.
