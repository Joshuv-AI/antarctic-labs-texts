Surface Roughness and Bond Strength of PEEK Posts: Comparing Chemical, Mechanical, and Laser Methods
Authors: Mohammed A Alrabiah, Fahad Alkhudhairy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAssessing the effect of different surface pretreatments, that is, sulfuric acid (SA), air-particle abrasion (APA), Er, Cr: YSGG laser, and nonthermal plasma (NTP)on the push-out bond strength (PBS) and surface roughness (Ra) of Polyether ether ketone (PEEK) post bonded to canal dentin.Materials and MethodsFifty caries-free single-rooted premolars were selected and decoronated. Ra was measured using a contact profilometer (n = 5). Surface morphology of the pre-treated PEEK posts was evaluated using SEM (n = 5). Ten posts from each group were cemented into the prepared canals using a dual-cure self-adhesive universal resin cement, followed by thermocycling. The specimens were then sectioned and subjected to PBS testing using a universal testing machine. Failure modes were analysed under a stereomicroscope. Whereas the minimum Ra scores were recorded in Group 1 (control) (0.6 ± 0.2 µm) samples. Whereas the minimum PBS values were recorded in the apical region of Group 1 (5.3 ± 0.3 MPa) samples.ConclusionAPA and Er, Cr: YSGG laser treatment might be considered effective alternatives to SA for pretreating PEEK posts.Clinical RelevanceSulfuric acid treatment, while producing the highest Ra and PBS values in PEEK surface treatment, carries inherent risks in a clinical setting — including tissue toxicity, handling hazards, and the need for strict neutralization protocols, making it less practical for routine chairside use. The finding that air-particle abrasion and Er, Cr: YSGG laser are effective alternatives. Both modalities are already present in well-equipped dental practices, require no hazardous chemical handling, and can be applied precisely and efficiently before post-cementation.Key words: Sulfuric acid; Air-particle abrasion; Nonthermal plasma; Surface characterization; Polyether ether ketone, Er; Cr YSGG laser

Introduction

Root canal therapy is generally performed on teeth that are extensively damaged by deep carious lesions, recurrent restorations, or fractures. The long-term success of this treatment depends largely on effective post-endodontic restoration, which is essential to prevent bacterial re-entry into the root canal system through oral fluids.1 When significant tooth structure is lost, placement of a post and core before crown restoration may be necessary to provide adequate support, retention, and durability of the final restoration.2,3 Consequently, clinical decisions and treatment strategies should be based on the most current evidence and the use of contemporary restorative materials. Polyether ether ketone-C19H12O3 (PEEK) has become popular in several dental and medical applications in recent years. High strength, stiffness, fatigue resistance, and wear resistance are all favourably combined in this linear, polyaromatic, semi-crystalline opaque grey shade thermoplastic polymer.4, 5, 6 Additionally, PEEK is easy to process, biocompatible, radiolucent, and demonstrates outstanding thermal and chemical stability.7, 8, 9 However, its naturally low surface free energy and inert hydrophobic nature make it difficult to achieve strong adhesion.10 Consequently, the PEEK post requires surface conditioning before it is bonded to canal dentin.

Available literature suggests that sulfuric acid-H2SO4 (SA) surface treatment markedly improves the push-out bond strength (PBS) of PEEK posts to canal dentin.11 When SA is applied, the surface becomes extremely porous, which enhances the bond strength with the resin adhesive by allowing its deeper penetration and augmenting the mechanical interlocking.12 Apart from SA, Air-particle abrasion (APA) is another effective surface-modification technique that increases surface roughness (Ra) and surface area of the PEEK post. APA increases the Ra through the high-velocity impact of abrasive particles, ultimately leading to better resin cement infiltration and subsequently enhancing the PBS.7,13 Nevertheless, evidence regarding the effects of these conditioning methods on Ra and PBS of PEEK posts remains limited, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Another method that has attracted considerable interest is the use of lasers for surface texturing of various post systems. Water and hydroxyl groups in dental materials strongly absorb the Er, Cr: YSGG laser at a wavelength of 2780 nm, initiating a photoablation process that produces micromechanical retention characteristics.14, 15, 16 Gomes et al. showed that the PBS of fiber posts was improved by non-contact Er, Cr: YSGG laser irradiation.17 Further research is necessary because it is yet unclear how this laser affects the binding strength and Ra of PEEK posts.

Among emerging surface modification strategies, non-thermal plasma (NTP) has drawn considerable scientific attention for its potential to enhance the adhesive properties of high-performance polymers, particularly PEEK.10 Operating at near-ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure, NTP generates a stream of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) that chemically activate the PEEK surface through the incorporation of polar functional groups, including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl moieties, leading to a measurable elevation in surface energy and wettability without causing any thermally induced structural deterioration.18,19 However, its role as a surface treatment for PEEK posts has not yet been investigated.

Based on the documented evidence, it was predicated that the Ra of PEEK post conditioned utilizing SA, APA Er, Cr: YSGG laser, and NTP would show no significant difference from the untreated control group. Additionally, it was also anticipated that the PBS of PEEK post bonded to canal dentin following surface conditioning with SA, APA, Er, Cr: YSGG laser, and NTP would not differ significantly from the untreated control. Therefore, the present study aims to assess how various surface conditioning techniques affect the Ra and bond strength of PEEK posts to the root dentin.

Materials and methods

Sample selection and sample size calculation

Fifty intact mandibular premolars were selected, which were extracted for orthodontic purposes. The sample size was determined based on previous published work by Alkhudhairy, AlRefeai, and Alkhudhairy, Alkhudhairy and Aljamhan.20, 21, 22 Using the World Health Organization sample size calculation, 95 % confidence level, absolute precision required: 1 %, population mean:14.66, population SD: 0.63, a sample size of 10 cases in each group was estimated.20, 21, 22 The study was approved by the ethical board of King Saud University. The inclusion selection criteria were: Single rooted with a single straight root canal and single fully developed apices. Root length 14 mm (after decoronation) to allow standardized post space preparation. Absence of root curvature greater than 5° (Schneider’s method) on radiograph. Teeth free from caries, cracks, or fractures (confirmed under × 20 magnification), internal or external root resorption, and previous endodontic treatment. To get rid of calculus and debris, the teeth were ultrasonically scaled (HP-5L) after being disinfected in thymol solution at a concentration of 0.2% (Sigma Aldrich). Every root canal procedure was carried out by a licensed endodontist.23

To remove the coronal sections and standardize the root length to 14 mm, sectioning of the roots was carried out at a standardized distance of 1 mm apical to the cementoenamel junction (CEJ). A No. 10 K-file (Diadent) was used to measure the working length, which was 1 mm short of the apex. The ProTaper Universal rotary system (Diadent) was utilized for mechanical preparation up to the F3 finishing file. In between each file, 5 mL of a 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution (Prime Dental Products PVT) was used as an irrigant. Following, 5 mL of 17% EDTA is used to irrigate the canals for a minute. Root canal obturation was performed using F3 gutta-percha and AH Plus sealer (Dentsply Maillefer). Afterwards, glass ionomer cement (Ionofil Plus; VOCO) was used to fill the access cavities and was kept for a week at 37°C in distilled water.24

Post space preparation

For post space preparation, Gates Glidden drills (Dentsply Sirona, England, UK) were used to eliminate 10 mm of gutta-percha from the root canal in the sequential order #2, #3, and #4 at 9,000 rpm, leaving 4mm of gutta-percha at the apical end to preserve the apical seal. To guarantee consistent performance, a fresh drill was utilized every 5 teeth. Blocks of self-curing acrylic resin (Pattern Resin LS, GC America) were then used to implant the specimens.25

Allocation of PEEK posts based on the pretreatment regime

A total of one hundred prefabricated PEEK posts (PEEK Optima Dental Disc, Juvora, Switzerland) were selected and randomly allocated into 5 groups according to the surface treatment protocol (n = 20)

Group 1(control): No surface pretreatment of the PEEK post surface.

Group 2: SA (H2SO4). Surface pretreatment of the PEEK posts was performed by immersing them in 95% sulfuric acid (Katayama Chemical Industries Co., Ltd., Hyogo, Japan) for 1 min. The specimens were rinsed with water for 30 s and air-dried for 5 seconds.12

Group 3: APA. In this group, PEEK post surfaces were treated using a grit-blasting device (Basic Quattro; Renfert) with 110-µm aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) particles for 10s. The grit blaster nozzle (2 mm internal diameter) was positioned perpendicular to the post surface at a standardized distance of 15 mm under a pressure of 2 bar, with continuous sweeping motion to ensure uniform coverage. Following sandblasting, surfaces were cleaned with oil-free compressed air for 20 seconds to remove loose particles, ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 5 minutes, and then air-dried for 10 seconds. The treated posts were immediately used for adhesive application to prevent surface contamination.25

Group 4: Er, Cr: YSGG Laser. Surface pretreatment of the PEEK posts was performed using an Er, Cr: YSGG laser system (Waterlase; Biolase, San Clemente, CA, USA) at a wavelength of 2780 nm. The laser parameters were output power 1 W, repetition rate 20 Hz, pulse duration 140 µs, delivering 50 mJ per pulse. Using an MZ8 sapphire tip (spot diameter 600µm), the calculated fluence was approximately 16 J/cm² per pulse. Irradiation was performed in non-contact mode for 10s with the laser tip positioned 1 mm perpendicular to the post surface. The system was set to 80% water and 60% air for continuous cooling. The laser beam was applied in a circumferential sweeping motion (approximately 5 complete cycles) while the post was rotated to ensure uniform coverage. Total energy delivered was 10 J per post (1 W × 10s).25

Group 5: NTP. PEEK post specimens allocated to this group underwent surface pretreatment utilizing a non-thermal atmospheric pressure plasma device (Neoplas Greifswald, Berlin, Germany) operating with Argon (Ar) as the carrier gas. The device was calibrated to standardized operating parameters of 3 kVpp voltage, 4.80 mA current, and a gas flow rate of 5.0 standard liters per minute (slm). Each PEEK post was individually mounted on a stable platform, and the plasma jet nozzle was positioned perpendicularly at a fixed standoff distance of 10 mm above the post surface to ensure uniform and reproducible plasma delivery. The treatment was applied continuously for 60 seconds per specimen under ambient atmospheric conditions at room temperature. The plasma jet was moved in a systematic, controlled manner across the entire post surface to ensure homogeneous coverage and consistent activation of all surface regions.26

Ra measurement. Ra was assessed on 5 samples of PEEK post from each group by contact profilometer (Surftest SJ-201, Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan; Elcometer 224/2, Elcometer Instruments, UK). Along the longitudinal axis of every post, 3 measurements were taken and the average Ra value calculated. Ra was measured in micrometers (µm). Surface topography analysis using SEM. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; TESCAN, Czech Republic) was used to evaluate the surface morphology of the PEEK posts following different conditioning regimes. Five posts from each group were fixed onto metallic stubs, gold-coated using an ion sputter coater (Balzers Ltd., London, Great Britain), and then analysed under the SEM.27

Bonding procedure

Before cementation, prepared root canals were irrigated with distilled water and dried using absorbent paper points to a moist surface. Post-fit was verified by trial insertion. Ten PEEK posts per group were cemented using dual-cure self-adhesive universal resin cement (RelyX Unicem, 3M ESPE). The cement capsule was activated and triturated for 10 s using an amalgamator at 4,000 rpm. Mixed cement was applied to the canal walls using a lentulo spiral motion and to the post surface with a microbrush. Posts were slowly seated with sustained finger pressure (∼20 N) for 10 s. Excess cement was removed immediately with a microbrush before setting, and posts were held in position for 3 minutes to allow initial chemical polymerization. Dual-cure activation was performed using an LED light-curing unit (LED.B, Woodpecker, Guilin, Guangdong, China; 1,200 mW/cm²) for a total of 90 s: 40 s at the cervical third, 30 s at the middle third (45° angle), and 20s final cervical cure. Samples were stored in distilled water at 37°C for 24 hrs before further testing.28

Thermocycling of specimens

Following post-cementation, all samples were thermocycled using a thermocycling device (Praj Metallurgical Lab., Pune; Nissin Seiki Co. Ltd.) to mimic intraoral thermal changes. Each specimen was subjected to 10,000 cycles of thermocycling, alternating between water baths maintained at 5 °C and 55 °C. Each cycle had a dwell time of 20 s in each bath and a transfer time of 5 s between baths.29,30

Sectioning of bonded samples to dentin

The samples were cut perpendicular to the post-root long axis, starting 2 mm above the root apex, using a hard-tissue microtome (Leica SP 1600, Germany) and water irrigation to prevent overheating. A cross-sectional area of 2.0 mm was cut into 3 parts, each comprising the apical, middle, and cervical parts, of every root specimen.24,31

PBS testing and failure mode assessment

A universal testing machine (Bongshin, Model DBBP-20, Korea; Triax, Controls S.P.A.) was used to measure the PBS. A custom-fabricated stainless steel jig with a centrally positioned punch hole was used, with the hole diameter designed to be 0.2 mm larger than the maximum post diameter. The specimens were positioned apico-coronally on the jig to reduce the impact of root canal taper. A cylindrical plunger was used to apply a load to each post segment at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. MPa were used to measure the force needed to debond the post from the root dentin. A stereomicroscope (SMZ645, Nikon Co.) with a 20x magnification was used to assess failure mode. Fracture patterns are categorized as adhesive, cohesive, or admixed.20,32

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (IBM Corp.). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test evaluated the normality of the data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test were used for data analysis. p < .05

Discussion

The present study was based on the hypothesis that PEEK post surface treated with SA, APA, Er, Cr: YSGG laser, and NTP would show no significant difference in Ra and PBS from the untreated control group. This hypothesis was partially rejected as SA, APA, and Er, Cr: YSGG laser-treated PEEK posts demonstrated significantly greater Ra and PBS values compared to the control. Whereas, PEEK post treated with NTP showed no significant difference in Ra and PBS compared to the control. In the present in vitro study, the push-out test was selected as the evaluation method due to its clinical relevance, measuring shear stress at the dentin–cement interface under standardized conditions with high reliability, particularly for low bond strength scenarios.33,34

The efficacy of APA in achieving Ra and PBS comparable to SA can be attributed to kinetic energy transfer mechanisms. When 110μm alumina particles are propelled at 2 bar pressure, collision-induced kinetic-to-thermal energy conversion creates localized microporosities on the PEEK surface.35 These topographical modifications enhance micromechanical interlocking through increased surface area and improved wettability via elevated surface energy, both critical for adhesive bonding. Importantly, the threshold parameters identified by Lee et al.—110 μm particle size at 2 bar—prevent subsurface damage while optimizing bond durability, a critical consideration for high-performance polymers exhibiting inherent chemical inertness.35 Scanning electron microscopy confirmed the development of irregular, retentive surface architecture consistent with previous findings in glass fiber post systems, validating the biomechanical rationale for improved PBS.

The comparable performance of Er, Cr: YSGG laser (λ = 2780 nm) represents a novel finding requiring mechanistic consideration. This wavelength corresponds to the absorption peak of hydroxyl groups, enabling selective photothermal ablation with minimal heat accumulation in polymer matrices.36,37 Previous investigations using Nd: YVO4 lasers demonstrated grid-like micropatterns on PEEK surfaces through controlled laser engraving, establishing precedent for laser-induced topographical modification without compromising bulk material properties.38 The present findings suggest Er, Cr: YSGG irradiation induces comparable micromechanical alterations, creating micro-retentive features that enhance physical interlocking with resin cement. However, as this constitutes the first investigation of Er, Cr: YSGG laser effects on PEEK post bonding, systematic parameter optimization studies are warranted to establish clinical protocols.

The superior performance of SA-treated PEEK posts aligns with established chemical modification paradigms reported by Alkhudhairy et al12 and corroborates mechanistic investigations by Kern and Wolfart et al.39,40 Sulfonation induces partial PEEK matrix dissolution, simultaneously generating surface roughness and functionalizing polymer chains with sulfonate groups (–SO₃⁻). These moieties form covalent bonds with methacrylate-based adhesive systems through free radical polymerization, establishing both chemical and mechanical retention.41 Additionally, SA-mediated oxidation cleaves aromatic rings, increasing surface polarity and introducing reactive functional groups that enhance interfacial compatibility with hydrophilic resin cements.42 This multifaceted chemical modification explains the consistently high Ra and PBS values observed in SA-treated specimens.

The Ra and PBS values of NTP-treated PEEK posts remained statistically comparable to the untreated control, an outcome attributable to the predominantly chemical rather than physical nature of argon-based plasma activation. Younis et al. similarly demonstrated that argon plasma failed to produce meaningful Ra elevation in PEEK specimens, confirming that inert carrier gases lack the capacity to generate sufficient micromechanical surface disruption necessary for enhanced resin cement interlocking.10 Since effective post-dentin bonding depends on both chemical adhesion and mechanical infiltration into surface irregularities, chemically-dominant NTP modification alone proved inadequate to significantly augment PBS.10 Compounding this, the densely packed semi-crystalline architecture of PEEK resists plasma-induced surface restructuring, while post-treatment hydrophobic recovery progressively diminishes any transient chemical activation before cementation.43 These collective limitations position NTP as an insufficient standalone conditioning strategy for PEEK posts.

Fracture mode analysis provided valuable mechanistic evidence on the quality of interfacial bonding across experimental groups. In SA, APA, and Er, Cr: YSGG laser groups, admixed failure patterns were consistently observed, reflecting a productive balance between interfacial cohesion and bulk material resistance, an outcome indicative of thorough resin cement penetration and stable adhesive adaptation within the structurally complex dentin environment.20,44 The durability of such interfaces is contingent not only on initial bonding quality but also on the capacity of the hybrid layer to withstand progressive hydrolytic and enzymatic challenge over time, as comprehensively outlined by Amin et al. in their evaluation of resin-dentin interface degradation and stabilization strategies.45 In contrast, interfacial debonding predominated in both NTP-treated and untreated control specimens, reinforcing the interpretation that chemically passive surfaces lacking adequate topographical modification cannot sustain sufficient cement-post adhesion under functional loading. The broader clinical implications of this observation are substantial; Meschi et al. demonstrated through systematic evidence that the integrity of adhesive interfaces constitutes a primary determinant of favorable long-term endodontic outcomes, directly elevating the clinical weight of surface conditioning decisions for PEEK posts.46 The bond strength evaluation approach adopted in this investigation is further contextualized by Palma et al., whose work on substrate preparation and restorative timing in endodontic bonding scenarios underscores how protocol standardization critically shapes the reliability and interpretability of interfacial testing outcomes.47

Several limitations warrant consideration. The in vitro design inherently cannot replicate the complex biomechanical environment of the oral cavity, including masticatory loading, hydrolytic aging, and thermal cycling. Alternative methodologies, including microtensile bond strength (µTBS) testing, offer more homogeneous stress distribution and greater sensitivity to localized interfacial variations. Incorporation of multiple complementary bond strength modalities in future investigations would afford a more comprehensive and methodologically robust characterization of adhesive performance. Future investigations should systematically evaluate laser parameter optimization (energy density, pulse duration, repetition rate) and chemical agent concentration-response relationships to establish evidence-based clinical protocols. Advanced analytical techniques, including atomic force microscopy for nanoscale topographical characterization and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy for surface chemistry analysis, would provide deeper mechanistic insights. Additionally, long-term in vitro aging protocols incorporating cyclic loading, followed by prospective clinical trials and case control studies, are essential to validate the clinical efficacy and durability of these pretreatment strategies under physiological conditions.

Conclusion

Airborne particle abrasion and Er, Cr: YSGG laser irradiation demonstrated surface roughness and push-out bond strength values comparable to sulfuric acid pretreatment, establishing them as viable alternatives for PEEK post conditioning. Conversely, NTP therapy yielded comparable bonding performance and Ra to the untreated PEEK post.
