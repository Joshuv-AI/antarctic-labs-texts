Nurse-led Intervention Program Builds Confidence Regarding Home Care among Mothers of Solid Organ Tumor Children
Authors: M. Swami, Shashi Mawar, A. K. Dhua, R. Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:To evaluate the impact of nurse-led intervention program on the knowledge and confidence of mothers regarding home-based care of children with solid organ tumor.Subjects and Methods:A quasi-experimental study was conducted among 60 mothers of children with solid organ tumors at the pediatric surgery ward of a tertiary hospital. The intervention group received a nurse-led video-based program on home-based care, while the control group received standard care. A structured knowledge questionnaire and confidence assessment scale were used to assess mothers’ knowledge and confidence before and after intervention at 1 week and 4 weeks.Results:The experimental group’s mean knowledge and confidence scores were significantly higher at 1 week and 4 weeks than the control group (P < 0.05). The majority of the experimental group demonstrated good knowledge at both time points. No significant association was found between knowledge/confidence and mothers’ demographics. However, a positive correlation between knowledge and confidence was noted in both the chemotherapy and postoperative groups at 4 weeks (r = 0.525 and r = 0.729, respectively).Conclusion:Video-based nursing intervention was effective in increasing knowledge and confidence among the mothers of children with solid organ tumors for imparting home-based care.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer remains a major global health challenge, with significant disparities in access to care globally. Pediatric solid tumors such as retinoblastoma, neuroblastoma, and hepatoblastoma require complex treatments, including chemotherapy and surgeries, necessitating effective postoperative care.[12] Mothers, as primary caregivers, are central to managing home-based care, but low confidence and limited knowledge can hinder recovery and exacerbate stress.[34] Effective home-based care, complemented by hospital services, is becoming increasingly crucial for pediatric cancer patients.[5] Nurse-led educational interventions, particularly video-based programs, have shown promise in enhancing mothers’ knowledge and confidence, enabling the better management of complications at home.[6] This study evaluates the impact of such interventions on improving maternal confidence and competence in caring for children with solid tumors.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

A quasi-experimental study (given the quasi-experimental design, the allocation was non-random and based on enrollment sequence; hence, this study does not qualify as a randomized controlled trial) was conducted in the pediatric surgery ward of a tertiary care teaching institution, including 60 mothers selected using a convenient sampling technique. Mothers of children aged 0–18 years with solid organ tumors who could comprehend English or Hindi were included, while those unwilling to participate or whose children had comorbidities or central nervous system (CNS) tumors were excluded. The sample size was determined using G-power 3.1 software based on the pilot study data employing a two-tailed test with an effect size of 0.88, alpha level of 0.05, and power of 0.95 (mean scores: 18.5 ± 2.6 and 20.6 ± 1.67). While this sample size was sufficient for detecting large effect sizes, it may be underpowered to detect the minor effects or subgroup differences. Ethical approval was obtained from the institution, and CTRI registration was done. Written informed consent was secured from participants in English and Hindi, ensuring confidentiality and autonomy.

Participants were approached individually and informed about the study, receiving a participant information sheet and consent form in both English and Hindi. Participants were sequentially allocated to either the experimental or control groups in a 1:1 ratio based on the order of enrollment, without randomization. The experimental group received a nurse-led intervention comprising a video presentation and a pamphlet on home-based management during chemotherapy and postoperative care, while the control group received standard care. Data were collected using structured questionnaires and confidence assessment scales to evaluate mothers’ knowledge and confidence at baseline, 1 week, and 4 weeks post-intervention. The knowledge questionnaire was a single, structured tool designed to cover the key domains of home-based care applicable to both chemotherapy and postoperative contexts. It included questions on infection prevention, symptom management, nutrition, and emergency recognition. The same questionnaire was administered at baseline, 1 week, and 4 weeks. Confidence was assessed using two self-developed 5-item Likert-type scales – one for chemotherapy-related care and the other for postoperative care – created specifically for this study based on the literature and clinical caregiver expectations. These tools were validated by a multidisciplinary expert panel (I-CVI ≥0.80) and tested for reliability in a pilot group, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85. These tools were validated by a multidisciplinary expert panel (I-CVI ≥0.80) and tested for reliability in a pilot group, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85. Given that confidence is a subjective psychological construct, a Likert scale was selected for its appropriateness in capturing gradations of self-perception. To ensure reliability, a test-retest method was employed prior to pilot testing, thereby contributing to the tool’s overall validity. Each correct answer in the knowledge questionnaire was scored as 1 point, with a total possible score of 25. Confidence scores were derived from 5 Likert type items per group (chemotherapy/postoperative), each rated from 1 (not confident) to 5 (completely confident), allowing a cumulative confidence score between 5 and 25. The full scale is included as the supplementary material. Baseline assessments reflected initial levels, while subsequent evaluations measured the intervention’s impact. To reduce potential testing bias due to repeated administration, participants were not informed of their responses or scores after baseline testing.

Questionnaire administration was spaced at 1-week and 4-week intervals to allow sufficient time for the intervention to have an effect independent of recall. The observed gains are thus more likely attributable to the educational content rather than repetition alone. Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2016 and analyzed using Stata STatistical Software: Release 12. StataCorp. (2011) College Station, TX: StataCorp LP. Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, standard deviation, percentage, and frequency, were used to describe the participants’ demographic characteristics. Inferential statistical methods, such as two-sample t-tests, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), Wilcoxon signed-rank sum tests, and one-way ANOVA, were applied to compare the mean and median scores between and within groups over time. Spearman correlation was used to assess the relationships between knowledge and confidence, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Interventions and evaluations adhered to ethical and methodological rigor throughout the study.

RESULTS

The mean age of mothers in the experimental and control groups was 25.11 ± 4.7 and 25.48 ± 4.48 years, respectively. Educationally, most mothers in the experimental group were postgraduates (n = 11, 36.6%), whereas the majority in the control group were graduates (n = 16, 53.3%). Most mothers were homemakers, constituting 80% (n = 24) in the experimental group and 66.6% (n = 20) in the control group. Although the experimental group had a higher proportion of mothers with postgraduate education and a higher socioeconomic class, no statistically significant association was found between these variables and outcome scores. While the sociodemographic characteristics were largely homogeneous between groups, a significant difference in socioeconomic status was observed, with most mothers in the experimental group belonging to the upper middle class and those in the control group to the lower middle class (P < 0.05).

The mean age of children in the experimental and control groups was 29.13 ± 25.9 and 27.97 ± 25.9 months, respectively. A majority of children in the experimental group were female (n = 19, 63.3%). The mean number of siblings per child was 0.82 ± 0.93 and 0.71 ± 0.67 in the experimental and control groups, respectively. None of the siblings in either group were affected by solid organ tumors. Clinical characteristics, including treatment regimens, were similar between groups (P > 0.05). Nearly all children (n = 29, 96.6%) in both groups were planned to receive chemotherapy, with 70% (n = 21) in the experimental and 76.6% (n = 23) in the control group undergoing chemotherapy before surgery. Surgery was planned for 80% (n = 24) in the experimental group and 73.3% (n = 22) in the control group, with comparable rates of chemo-naïve children at enrollment (53.3% vs. 46.6%) [Table 1].

Knowledge scores demonstrated significant improvement in the experimental group. At 1 week, the mean knowledge score was 20.7 ± 2.01 compared to 12.8 ± 1.9 in the control group, and at 4 weeks, it was 19.5 ± 1.8 compared to 12.6 ± 1.7 (P < 0.05) [Table 2]. Baseline knowledge was poor in both groups, with 83.3% and 90% of mothers in the experimental and control groups, respectively, scoring <60%. At 1 week, 73.3% of mothers in the experimental group demonstrated good knowledge (>80%), a proportion that remained higher at 4 weeks (56.6%), compared to the control group [Figure 1].

Confidence scores also improved significantly in the experimental group. The median confidence score of chemotherapy group at 1 week was 22 (interquartile range [IQR]: 17–24) in the experimental group versus 11 (IQR: 8–15) in the control group, and at 4 weeks, it was 20 (IQR: 15–24) compared to 10 (IQR: 7–14) in the control group (P < 0.05) [Table 3]. The median confidence score of postoperative group in the experimental group was 21 (IQR: 18–23) at 1 week and 19 (IQR: 17–22) at 4 weeks, which was significantly higher compared to the control group, where the median scores were 10 (IQR: 9–13) at 1 week and 11 (IQR: 8–13) at 4 weeks, with a P < 0.05 [Table 4]. In addition, no associations were found between baseline or postintervention knowledge and confidence scores and variables such as education, occupation, or socioeconomic status (P > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

This investigation aimed to assess nurse-led interventions’ efficacy in enhancing mothers’ knowledge and self-efficacy in providing home-based care to children diagnosed with solid organ tumors at the site of the study. The empirical evidence from this study illuminates the substantial benefits of such interventions, aligning with Rahmani et al.’s[7] findings that highlight the predominant caregiving role of mothers. The enhancement in maternal knowledge and confidence demonstrated in this study resonates with the extant body of literature and marks a significant step toward patient-centered care. The pivotal role of mothers in caregiving, underscored by Rahmani et al.,[7] is validated by the current findings and broadened to include educational initiatives as a means of empowerment. The demographic data concerning the mothers’ age closely mirrors that reported by Ebrahimi et al.[8] although with a younger cohort reflecting the lesser mean age of the children involved in this study. The study’s reflection of a younger maternal age group in the Indian context adds a dimension to the global understanding of pediatric oncology caregiving, suggesting that younger caregivers may benefit disproportionately from educational interventions. This insight beckons a reevaluation of demographic considerations in the design of caregiver support programs.

Educational attainment among the mothers in this cohort was notably high, resonating with findings by Park et al.,[9] which indicated a significant proportion of graduate-level education among mothers of pediatric oncology patients. Occupational status predominantly identified mothers as homemakers, consistent with Rahmani et al.’s[7] observations. In the present study, the mean age of children with solid organ tumors was 29.13 ± 25.9 months in the experimental group and 27.97 ± 25.9 months in the control group. The study conducted by Israels et al.[10] included children with Wilm’s tumor, where the median age of children was 3.5 years (range 9–144 months). In another study conducted by Ebrahimi et al.,[8] the mean age of the children was 9.25 ± 0.56 years in the experimental and 9.12 ± 1.74 years in the control group, which is much higher than the present study children because the children who experienced depressive symptoms were included in this study and depression among children occur at a higher age.

The current study demonstrated a significant postintervention increase in maternal knowledge scores, reflecting the effectiveness of the structured nurse-led video-based educational program. These findings are consistent with those of Kaur et al.,[11] who reported notable improvements in caregiver knowledge following structured educational interventions. Similarly, Cheng et al. observed statistically significant knowledge gains in parents of pediatric oncology patients after implementing a home-based symptom management program.[12] In addition to improved knowledge, our study revealed a substantial enhancement in maternal confidence related to home-based care. The median confidence scores increased from 11 (range: 7–17) at baseline to 22 (range: 17–24) postintervention. This aligns with the results of Frydenberg et al.,[13] who found that video-assisted structured education significantly improved parental confidence in managing childhood health conditions, including asthma. Recent Indian studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of video-based educational interventions in reducing parental anxiety and improving comprehension during the informed consent process. For instance, a randomized controlled trial conducted at AIIMS Jodhpur found that multimedia tools significantly decreased parental anxiety and enhanced understanding of surgical procedures in pediatric patients.[14] Similarly, a study from PGIMER Chandigarh reported that parents who viewed a preanesthetic educational video exhibited a substantial reduction in anxiety levels compared to those who received conventional verbal explanations.[15] These findings align with the results of our study, underscoring the potential of video-based interventions in empowering caregivers with the knowledge and confidence needed for home-based care. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between knowledge and confidence, with r = 0.525 for the chemotherapy group and r = 0.729 for the postoperative group. This reinforces the hypothesis that educational interventions that increase maternal knowledge can also enhance caregiver confidence.

However, the present study identified no significant correlations between the mothers’ knowledge levels and selected demographic variables such as education, occupation, and socioeconomic status. This finding diverges from Abd Elmoniem Syan et al.,[16] who reported significant associations between maternal education and occupation and overall knowledge scores. The lack of correlation between maternal knowledge and demographic variables challenges the presupposed educational hierarchy and suggests that the desire to provide optimal care transcends traditional socioeconomic barriers. It implies a universal receptivity to educational interventions among mothers, irrespective of their academic or occupational backgrounds. While this study offers promising short-term outcomes, the question of sustainability remains pivotal. Improvements in maternal knowledge and confidence observed at 1 and 4 weeks postintervention mark an encouraging trend, but it is essential to determine whether these benefits persist over time. Future longitudinal studies – preferably at 3-, 6-, and 12-month intervals – should assess long-term knowledge retention, consistency in caregiving behavior, emotional resilience of caregivers, and pediatric patient health outcomes. Such evidence would provide critical insights into the enduring effectiveness and translational value of nurse-led educational programs. Furthermore, comparative effectiveness research is needed to evaluate the impact of alternative delivery modes (e.g. mobile-based apps, telehealth sessions, or printed modules), especially in settings with limited access to digital tools.

While this study contributes valuable insights, it acknowledges several limitations, including a small sample size, which may affect the generalizability and statistical power of the findings. The sample size, although statistically justified based on the pilot data, remains modest and may limit the study’s ability to detect smaller or subgroup level effects. This raises the possibility of Type II errors, particularly for outcomes where no statistically significant differences were observed. In addition, the lack of randomization and the use of convenience sampling are the inherent limitations of the quasi-experimental design, which may introduce allocation and selection biases. The use of convenience sampling in this study, although practically necessary in a high-throughput tertiary hospital environment, introduces the potential selection bias. This limits the representativeness of the sample and constrains the generalizability of the findings to mothers from broader cultural, geographic, and socioeconomic contexts. While the intervention demonstrated effectiveness in this cohort, caution is warranted when extrapolating results. Future studies employing randomized controlled designs and stratified sampling are recommended to enhance internal and external validity. Furthermore, a statistically significant baseline difference in socioeconomic status was observed between the experimental and control groups (P < 0.05), raising the possibility of socioeconomic status acting as a confounding variable. While subgroup analyses revealed no significant associations between SES and knowledge or confidence scores at any time point (P > 0.05), the quasi-experimental nature of the design limits our ability to fully adjust for residual confounding. It is possible that mothers from higher socioeconomic backgrounds may have had greater access to supportive resources, which could have influenced the intervention’s effectiveness. Future studies should consider stratified sampling or apply multivariate statistical controls to address such disparities more rigorously. While subgroup analyses showed no significant correlation between these factors and outcomes, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies should consider stratified sampling or multivariate adjustment to control for such imbalances. Another key limitation is the exclusion of mothers caring for children with comorbidities or CNS tumors. This decision was made to maintain clinical homogeneity within the study population, allowing for focused evaluation of the intervention’s effect on solid organ tumor caregiving. However, it restricts the applicability of our findings to more complex pediatric oncology cases. Children with CNS tumors or multiple comorbidities often have heightened care demands, including neurocognitive management, mobility support, and additional medication regimens, which may require customized educational approaches. Future research should extend intervention testing to these groups to determine whether similar benefits in knowledge and confidence can be achieved under more demanding caregiving conditions. Moreover, while demographic characteristics such as education and occupation were broadly similar between the experimental and control groups, this homogeneity could inadvertently obscure how these factors may influence responsiveness to educational interventions. For instance, differences in health literacy, caregiving experience, and employment-related stress could interact with the intervention’s impact. The largely uniform demographic composition in our sample may therefore limit the generalizability. Future studies should aim to include a wider range of participants across educational, occupational, and socioeconomic strata, and perform subgroup analyses to evaluate potential moderating effects of these variables on intervention outcomes. It is also worthnoting that blinding of participants and outcome assessors was not feasible due to the nature of the intervention. This may have introduced response or observer bias. Future studies may benefit from incorporating blinded outcome assessment wherever possible to enhance methodological rigor. Finally, this study utilized a short follow-up period of 4 weeks, which, while sufficient to measure immediate postintervention effects, is insufficient to evaluate the long-term sustainability of knowledge retention and confidence in caregiving. Whether these improvements persist beyond the initial month remains unknown. Future longitudinal studies incorporating extended follow-up periods of 3–6 months – or more – are recommended to assess the durability of intervention effects, adherence to home-care practices over time, and potential downstream impacts on child health and caregiver burden. Another limitation pertains to the mode of intervention delivery. The nurse-led educational program utilized a video-based format, which may not be accessible to all caregivers due to variability in digital literacy, device availability, or internet access. Future adaptations should consider alternative delivery formats such as printed materials, community demonstrations, or voice-based messages for broader inclusivity.

Nevertheless, this research underscores the importance of integrating home-based care education into pediatric oncology care. Nurse-led interventions using video-based education, pamphlets, and posters offer an effective means of enhancing caregiver knowledge, especially in the resource-limited settings. These findings suggest that such interventions should become a core component of nursing education, with ongoing training and professional development to ensure their sustainability and impact. Administratively, nurse-led clinics and supportive strategies should be implemented to assist families of children with cancer. Future studies should investigate the long-term effects of these interventions and explore their psychological impacts on both caregivers and patients.

Further research could build on these findings by conducting multicentric studies or randomized controlled trials to provide stronger evidence. Comparative studies evaluating different educational strategies for home-based care could offer valuable insights into which methods are most effective in improving caregiving outcomes for pediatric oncology patients.

CONCLUSION

Video-based nursing intervention (education) was effective in increasing knowledge and confidence among the mothers of children with solid organ tumors regarding home-based care. Therefore, healthcare providers should consider implementing this intervention to improve the quality of home-based care for children with solid organ tumors. Structured home-based care education should be considered as a valuable component of discharge planning for children with solid organ tumors, especially in settings where caregivers play a central role in posttreatment recovery.
