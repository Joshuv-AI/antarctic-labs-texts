Controlling root rot and wilt diseases in faba bean (Vicia faba) using bio-filtrates and bio-stimulant substances
Authors: Nourah M Almimoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe effectiveness of certain bio-culture filtrates (Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus megaterium, Pseudomonas fluorescens, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Trichoderma harzianum and Trichoderma viride) and some bio-stimulant substances (humic acid and chitosan) against Rhizoctonia solani (responsible for root rot) and Fusarium oxysporum (responsible for wilt in faba bean) was evaluated under laboratory and greenhouse conditions. Ten isolates of both R. solani and F. oxysporum were tested.ResultsBoth tested pathogens caused harmful effects on the faba bean root structure, and the scanning with an electron microscope showed some hyphal growth. In vitro, all tested bio-filtrates and bio-stimulant substances inhibited the linear growth of both pathogens. Trichoderma filtrates led to the highest reduction, followed by humic acid. In the greenhouse, the lowest values of all disease assessments occurred under Trichoderma culture filtrate followed by Pseudomonas and Bacillus. Trichoderma filtrates were the most effective on catalase, peroxidase, and polyphenol oxidase, while the highest chitinase activity occurred under chitosan treatment. Except for the current plus 50% wp fungicide, all treatments increased chlorophyll a, b, and consequently, total chlorophyll, and humic acid were more effective. Moreover, bacterial culture filtrates were the most effective in increasing carotenoids content; Bacillus ones gave the highest values then Pseudomonas without significant differences between them. Moreover, there is a positive correlation coefficient between the plant resistance (healthy surviving plants) and the antioxidant enzyme activities.ConclusionThe use of Trichoderma vs. Bacillus as bio-culture filtrates or chitosan vs. humic acid as bio-stimulant substances surpassed, in general, the results of most parameters with foliar treatment by current plus 50% wp. The treatment with such materials could be considered as fungicide alternatives favoring Trichoderma or chitosan for controlling root-rot and wilt diseases and enhancing physiological activities in faba beans.Keywords: Bio-filtrates, Humic acid, Chitosan, Root rot, Wilt, Rhizoctonia solani, Fusarium oxysporum, Root anatomy, Faba bean

Introduction

Faba bean (Vicia faba L.) is the fourth primary dietary legume worldwide [1]. It remains an important part of the diets for humans and domestic animals in many parts of the world. The nutritional importance of faba beans is due to their high nutritional value of protein (28%), carbohydrates (56%), vitamins and some other compounds in dried seeds [2, 3]. Faba bean is not only an excellent source of protein, but also a good source of fiber and plant sterols [4]. Faba bean, as a member of the legume family, is responsible for biological nitrogen fixation and consequently providing nitrogen sources to cropping systems, thus reducing costs and minimizing pollution impact on the environment [5]. Therefore, increasing bean productivity is considered a major target of agricultural policies in many countries. There are some problems facing increased faba bean productivity, the most important of which is disease infection [6]. Damping-off, root rot and wilt are considered the most important soil-borne diseases affecting faba bean germination, growth and productivity. These diseases are caused by several complex fungi such as Rhizoctonia solani, Fusarium solani, Fusarium oxysporum, Pythium spp. and Sclerotium spp. [7, 8]. There is a difficulty in controlling these pathogens due to their multiple host ranges and their persistence in the soil. Consequently, the management of soil-borne pathogens depends mainly on synthetic chemicals. Although it is a better option, it is effective only at the phytotoxic level and reduces populations of beneficial microorganisms in the soil and produces fungicide-resistant pathogens [9, 10]. In addition, the excessive use of fungicide causes environmental pollution and thus poses serious risks to public health as well as leading to a menace to human and animal health and reducing the populations of beneficial microorganisms in the soil [11]. This creates an urgent need for new methods such as biotic agents to control plant diseases.

Plant Growth Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR) exhibited high effects against root rot and vascular wilt diseases caused by soil-borne pathogens [12, 13]. Bacillus spp. is used as a bio control of soil-borne pathogens due to their producing antibiosis and inducing resistance of plants [14]. Moreover, Pseudomonas bacteria are considered one of the most important biocontrol agents due to producing antifungal metabolites [15]. Likewise, Trichoderma fungi induced both localized and systemic resistance in many plants to several phytopathogens [16, 17].

On the other side, some authors used bio-stimulant substances for the management of plant diseases as an alternative to synthetic chemicals, especially these have no effects on the environment and human health, such as humic acid and chitosan. Humic acid is a naturally occurring heterogeneous organic compound that is rich in acidic functional groups significantly enhances nutrient availability and plant physiological performance by generating nutrient complexes, increasing solubility behavior, and changing soil chemical properties [18]. The mechanistic basis of humic acid is soil enhancement and agricultural bio-stimulant induced via combined physicochemical properties (amphipathic supramolecular architecture, buffering behavior, proton exchange capacity, and functional group heterogeneity). Chitosan is a natural molecule that stimulates many biological responses in plants such as improved defense responses to abiotic and biotic stresses [19]. According to Soares et al. [20], chitosan exhibits strong intermolecular interactions and structural stability, which supports its bio functional performance in plant and microbial systems via eliciting oxidative bursts and ROS like H2O2 thus activate systemic acquired resistance in plants. This triggers the production of protective secondary metabolites (polyphenolics and flavonoids) which enhance overall plant stress tolerance and defense against pathogens [21]. Chitosan was also suggested for enhancing plant resistance due to increased lignin biosynthesis and induced chitinase enzyme activities as well as inhibition of root rot growth induced pathogens [22].

The present investigation aimed to study the effectiveness of some safe agents, whether biotic as a bio-culture filtrate (Bacillus, Pseudomonas and Trichoderma) or a bio-stimulants (humic acid and chitosan) on the reduction of root rot and wilt diseases in faba bean plants compared with the current 50% wp fungicide.

Materials and methods

Soil sample collection and processing

Rhizospheric soil samples were collected from the farm of Mr. Ahmed Ghoramallah in the Beljurashi area, Al Baha province, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia located at coordinates 19° 51′ 34″ N latitude and 41° 33′ 26″ E longitude. It is situated at an elevation of approximately 2,000 m above sea level. Faba bean (cv. Giza 843) seeds were purchased by the farm owner from Legume Crops Research Department, Field Crop Research Institute, Agricultural Research Center, Dokki, Giza, Egypt. Isolates were collected and handled for this experiment during the growing season of faba bean during the winter season of 2024. From each sampling site, 50 g of root-adhering soil samples were collected from the standing faba bean plants in triplicate and composited into one and collected in alcohol-surface-sterilized polyethylene bags and stored at 4 °C until the isolation of bacteria and fungi (Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Trichoderma).

Isolation and identification of faba bean root rot and wilt causal pathogens

Ten isolates of both Rhizoctonia solani (main causal pathogen of root rot) and Fusarium oxysporum (main causal pathogen of wilt) were isolated from faba bean plants under natural infections. Infected plants showing typical symptoms of root rot and wilt were collected and washed thoroughly with tap water, then the roots and stems were cut into small pieces and surface disinfected with 1% sodium hypochlorite. The sterilized pieces were rewashed with sterilized water and dried between two sterilized filter papers, placed on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium supplemented with streptomycin sulfate (100 µg/ml) and incubated at 26ºC. Purification was carried out based on contemporary purification methods identified by Pobiega et al. [23] for handling and processing fungal cultures and identified according to Sneh et al. [24] for Rhizoctonia and Booth [25] for Fusarium.

Preparation of fungal inoculum

Fungal inoculum of each pathogen isolate was prepared using sorghum medium (sorghum: coarse sand: water "2:1:2 v/v"). The medium content was mixed, bottled, and autoclaved for 15 min at 1.5 air pressure at 121ºC. The sterilized medium was inoculated with agar discs obtained from the periphery of a 7- day- old colony of the isolated fungi. Inoculated media were incubated at 26ºC for 15 days and then used for soil infestation in a greenhouse to estimate the fungal pathogenic capability.

Pathogenicity test

A controlled pot experiment was conducted in Baljurashi, Al-Baha region, Saudi Arabia, to evaluate the pathogenic potential of the isolated fungal strains on Vicia faba (cv. Giza 843) and to identify the most aggressive isolates from each fungal genus for subsequent investigations. Sterilized plastic pots (25 cm diameter) were filled with 3.5 kg of sterilized loamy soil and artificially inoculated with fungal inoculum at a rate of 3% w/w. Five pots were prepared for each fungal isolate (as replicates), and three replicate pots containing sterilized, un-infested soil were included as controls. To ensure uniform colonization and distribution of the inoculum, infested pots were thoroughly watered twice at three-day intervals before sowing. Surface-sterilized faba bean seeds were sown at a rate of five seeds per pot. Disease assessment was performed at two growth stages: 30 and 90 days post-sowing. Specifically, damping-off and root rot incidence were recorded in pots infested with Rhizoctonia solani, while early and late wilt incidence was assessed in pots infested with Fusarium oxysporum. Disease incidence was expressed as the percentage of affected plants relative to the total number of plants per pot. The most aggressive isolates from each genus were identified based on their ability to induce the highest disease severity and were selected for further experimentation. In addition, the etiology of wilt disease was confirmed by re-isolating F. oxysporum from symptomatic plants, thereby fulfilling Koch’s postulates and verifying the causal relationship between pathogen infection and observed disease symptoms. This experimental setup in Baljurashi provides a reliable framework for screening pathogenic isolates and selecting highly virulent strains for subsequent studies, including host–pathogen interactions and biological control assessments. Each genus's most aggressive isolates were chosen for additional research after being determined to be able to cause the most severe illness. Furthermore, by re-isolating F. oxysporum from sick plants, the etiology of wilt disease was verified, satisfying Koch's postulates and confirming the causal link between pathogen infection and observable disease symptoms. This is done in order to screen pathogenic isolates and choose the highly virulent strains for further research, such as host–pathogen interactions and biological control.

Faba bean root anatomical structure

The anatomical changes of faba bean root structure due to both selected pathogenic fungi were determined 32 days from sowing. The specimens of faba bean roots "5 mm in length approximately" were killed and fixed in formalin: ethyl alcohol: glacial acetic acid "1:18:1v/v." The anatomical changes were studied under a light microscope and scanning electron microscope. Firstly, under a light microscope, the killing and fixing specimens were washed and dehydrated in an alcohol series and then embedded in paraffin wax. The sections were prepared by a rotary microtome at 13–16 µ thickness, double stained in crystal violet and erythrosine, then cleared in xylol and mounted in Canada balsam [26]. Sections were examined microscopically to determine the anatomical changes due to pathogenic fungi compared with healthy plants. Secondly, under an electron microscope, the changes in anatomical root structure, especially in xylem vessels, due to being infected with the causal pathogen of root rot and wilt were checked using a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) as described by Denise and Anjali [27].

Preparation of bio-culture filtrates and the tested bio-stimulants as well as fungicide

Bacteria and fungi (Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Trichoderma) were isolated from the same coat of healthy faba bean plants collected from the same fields from which the pathogens were isolated in Baljurashi, Al-Baha Region, Saudi Arabia. The bacteria were isolated by culturing bacterial strains in nutrient broth and identified according to Bergey et al. [28]. The Trichoderma fungi were isolated according to Elad et al. [29]; 10 g of each soil sample was dissolved in 90 mL of sterilized water in a 250 mL flask. The suspension was prepared to appropriate dilutions from which 0.1 mL of the final dilution was transferred onto Rose Bengal agar (RBA) medium (MgSO₄0.7H₂O, 0.20 g/L, K₂HPO₄, 0.9 g/L, KCl, 0.15 g/L, NH₄NO₃, 1 g/L, glucose, 3 g/L, rose Bengal, 0.15 g/L, and agar, 20 g/L). The plates were incubated at 25 + 2 °C for 7–10 days. Typical Trichoderma resembling colonies were picked according to Barnett and Hunter [30] and subcultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates for purification and characterization. The purified cultures were then compared with the characteristics and illustrations in the manual to confirm the isolates and were stored in PDA slants at 4 °C for further study. Furthermore, both Trichoderma species were purified using the fungal hyphae technique and identified based on their microscopic and culture morphological characteristics [31]. Humic acid, commercially known as Actosol, containing 80% humic acid and 6% potassium oxide, was obtained from the Egyptian Center for Fertilizer Development, while chitosan (Libonite) was obtained from Sigma Chemicals. In addition, a 50% wettable fungicide was used for comparison in this study.

Molecular characteristics of the tested Rhizobacteria and Trichoderma isolates

DNA extraction

DNA isolation was performed according to the method of Lee and Taylor [32]. At 14 days old cultures of each isolate were harvested from a fresh colony growing on PDA by scraping with a sterile scalpel and ground in liquid nitrogen. The samples were suspended in 500 μl of extraction buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH: 8, 150 mM NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 2% SDS) and incubated for 30 min at 65 °C. DNA was extracted with phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) twice and precipitated by the addition of one volume of isopropanol. DNA pellets were washed with ethanol, dissolved in ddH2O, and stored at −20 °C.

Identification of the selected Rhizobacteria and Trichoderma using Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) conditions

Molecular characterization of the Rhizobacteria was made using 16S rRNA analysis. DNA of the strains Bacillus subtilis, Priestia megaterium, Pseudomonas fluorescens and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were extracted and purified with a commercial DNA extraction kit, the GenJet™ genomic DNA purification kit (Thermo K0721). Then, the 16S rDNA was amplified by PCR using general bacterial primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) [33]. Then, purified PCR products were sequenced using ABI 3730 × 1 DNA sequences, the 16 s rDNA sequence was aligned with published sequences in GenBank using the BLAST program, and phylogenies were analyzed using MEGA (version 5.0) software.

The Internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of rDNA was amplified using ITS 1 (5′ TCC GTA GGT GAA CCT GCGG 3′) and ITS 4 (5’ TCC TCC GCT TAT TGA TATGC 3′) primers [34]. The thermocycler program for amplification of the ITS region was 95 °C for 3 min followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 68 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 90 s. A final extension was made at 72 °C for 8 min.

Sequencing and multiple sequence alignment

The sequences of DNA were generated from sequencing the amplified PCR products using the ABI Prism 3130xl Genetic Analyzer in both directions using the same primers, ITS 1 and ITS 4 [34] which performed at Macrogen Corp., Korea. The DNA sequences have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank.

Multiple Sequence Alignment by CLUSTALW, Alignment and phylogenetic reconstructions were performed using the function "build" of Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA 11; https://www.megasoftware.net/) [35].

Preparation of bio-culture filtrates

Bacterial filtrate was prepared by streaking on nutrient agar medium and incubated for 24 h at 30ºC; then each bacterial isolate was inoculated in a flask containing 100 ml nutrient broth medium and incubated at 30ºC for 27 h. The grown culture was filtered, centrifuged, and sterilized by a membrane filter "0.25 µm in size," and the results filtrate was kept in sterilized dark bottles at 5ºC in the refrigerator until use. Also, both Trichoderma species were grown in PD broth at 28ºC on a shaker at 140 rpm in the dark for 10 days. The grown fungal culture was filtered through filter paper and followed by centrifugation and sterilization using a membrane filters "0.22 µm in size." Results filtrates were kept under cooling in a refrigerator in a dark bottle till use.

Determination of fungal growth with bio-culture filtrates, bio-stimulant substances and fungicide

The above-mentioned preparation of bio-culture filtrates, humic acid at 2000 mg/L, chitosan at 8 mg/L, based on the recommendation of an earlier study [8], and current plus 50% wp fungicide at 1000 mg/L were added to 10 ml of sterilized PDA before solidification at the rate of 1:9 v/v and poured in sterilized petri dishes. After solidification, the plates were inoculated with a fungal disc "5 mm" in the plate center and incubated at 26ºC. Three plates for each treatment were used as a replicate, and three plates were prepared to serve as a control for each fungal genus. The fungal linear growth was observed daily, and the fungal colonies diameter was measured when the full growth of both tested fungi was observed in a check control treatment. Greenhouse experiment

To estimate the disease incidence of faba bean in response to bio-filtrates and bio-stimulant substances, a greenhouse experiment was carried out under artificial infection of the most aggressive isolate selected. The infestation of soil was done in the same way as mentioned before in the pathogenicity test. Faba bean seeds cv. 843 were soaked for 6 h in each tested bio-filtrate and bio-stimulant substance, while current plus 50% wp was used as a seed coating. The same treatments were also used as a foliar spray at 38 days from sowing as an active dose to estimate some enzyme activity and photosynthetic pigments. Seeds were sown in pots (5 seeds/pot.) for each treatment and check (sterilized seeds in uninfested soil), with three replications each. The disease occurrence was determined as damping-off at 30 days and root rot and survival of plants at 90 days after sowing under infestation with R. solani. Enzymes activity

The enzymatic activities of some plant enzymes (catalase, peroxidase, polyphenol oxidase and chitinase) associated with the plant defense system were estimated after two days from the foliar application "40 days from sowing" in faba bean leaflet tissues. An amount of 0.1 g of ground leaflets was mixed with 0.3 ml 0.5% H2O2 then the mixture was brought to a final volume of 3 ml with distilled water and filtered separately through four layers of cheesecloth and centrifuged for 20 min at 3000 ppm and 6 °C. A certain amount from the filtrate treated with 0.2 M sodium phosphate buffer at pH 7.6 for catalase, potassium buffer 0.05 M at pH 7 for peroxidase, sodium phosphate buffer 0.2 M (pH 7) for polyphenol oxidase and citrate–phosphate buffer 0.05 M at pH 6.6 for chitinase. Catalase and peroxidase were calculated according to Kato and Shimizu [36], polyphenol oxidase was calculated according to Esterbauer et al. [37] and chitinase was calculated according to Monreal and Reese [38].

Photosynthetic pigments

The blade terminal leaflet of the third leaf from the plant tip was taken to determine photosynthetic pigment (chlorophyll a, b, total chlorophylls and carotenoids) content. The pigments were extracted with 90% ethanol after adding traces of calcium carbonate [39], then determined and calculated according to Mackinney [40].

Statistical analysis

The obtained results were evaluated following the one-way ANOVA technique using the statistical analysis software package CoStat v 6.4. Duncan’s test was used for comparing means at the probability of P ≤ 0.05 according to Duncan [41]. Data were subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson’s correlation coefficient using Genstat Pro software.

Anatomical studies of faba bean roots as affected by Rhizoctonia solani and Fusarium oxysporum

The changes in the anatomical structure of faba bean root due to infection by R. solani and F. oxysporum as compared with healthy root were checked under a light microscope (Fig. 4) and an electron microscope (Fig. 5). The normal healthy root structure of a faba bean consists of the following: The epidermis "Ep" is a single layer (uni-seriate) of elongated cells with thin walls that covers the root; cortex "Co" cells may be arranged in orderly radial rows; and the vascular cylinder is a central root part that contains the vascular tissues (xylem and phloem "Ph") and is surrounded by the epidermis "Ep". Xylem is the principal water-conducting tissue within xylem vessels "Xv" while phloem is the food-conducting tissue.

Faba bean root cross sections (light microscope) as affected by R. solani and F. oxysporum infections (X 100). Ep = Epidermis C0 = Cortex, En = Endodermis, Xv = Xylem vessels, Ph = Phloem

Changes in anatomical root xylem vessels of Faba bean (Scanning electron microscope) as affected by R. solani and F. oxysporum infections (X500). Xv = Xylem vessels, MTy = Multiple tylosis, YTy = Young tylosis, Ppc = Plazmolized parenchyma cells

Cross sections of infected roots (Fig. 4) indicated severe structural changes, such as full disruption of certain epidermal cells, degradation of the main cell wall, and partial degeneration of cortical cell wall, all of which resulted in cellular integrity loss. Notably, Rhizoctonia solani produced more significant damage to the surface root tissues, whereas Fusarium oxysporum mostly affected the interior tissues. In comparison to F. oxysporum, R. solani was often more virulent when it came to causing significant damage to the root's anatomical structure. Oxysporum cross-sections revealed an internal effect under a light microscope (Fig. 4), and the results were confirmed using an electron microscope (Fig. 5).

On the other side, Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) observations (Fig. 5) revealed marked formation of tyloses (Ty) within xylem vessels and the presence of multiple tyloses (Mty), leading to vessel occlusion in plants infected with Fusarium oxysporum. In addition, plasmolization of some parenchyma cells was evident. Plasmolized parenchyma cells (Ppc) and fungal hyphae (Fh) were also observed both inside and along the vessel walls. In contrast, roots infected with Rhizoctonia solani exhibited comparatively less severe structural alterations.

Isolated fungi and pathogenicity tests

Ten isolates of two fungal genera were isolated from faba bean plants showing typical symptoms of root rot and wilt diseases under natural infection. They tested for their pathogenicity as causative pathogens of damping-off, root rot, and wilt of faba bean cv. Giza 843.

Data in Fig. 6 showed that all isolates of R. solani were pathogenic and caused symptoms of damping-off and root rot of faba bean. In this respect, R. solani isolate number 6 was the most aggressive isolate to cause these symptoms, recording 55.33% and 18.67% in damping-off and root rot, respectively with 28% survival of plants. On the other hand, all F. oxysporum isolates were pathogenic and caused typical symptoms of wilt disease in faba beans. F. oxysporum isolate number 9, gave the highest percentages of early and late wilt with 45.34% survival of plants. Based on the results of pathological ability tests, R. solani isolate No. 6, "the most aggressive causal pathogen of root rot" and F. oxysporum isolate No. 9, "the most aggressive causal pathogen of wilt" was selected for further studies.

Disease assessments

Damping-off, root rot, and wilt of faba bean as a response to bio-filtrates and bio-stimulant substances in artificially infested soil under a greenhouse are presented in Table 3. Generally, both culture filtrates and bio-stimulant substances significantly reduced Rhizoctonia damping-off and root rot as well as Fusarium wilt diseases, consequently increasing the number of healthy surviving plants. Regardless of fungicide, the lowest values of all disease assessments occurred under the application of culture filtrate of Trichoderms followed by culture filtrates of Pseudomonas genus then culture filtrates of Bacillus. Concerning the effects of bio-stimulant substances, chitosan was the most effective from humic acid. The highest survival plants were observed on healthy control followed by culture filtration of T. harzianum under infection of F. oxysporum, while the lowest survival occurred under infested control of R. solani. It was also noticed that T. harzianum gives the highest antifungal effects for reducing the disease assessments, which resulted in 85.37, 73.35, 76.47 and 81.82 reduction percentages of damping-off, root rot, early wilt and late wilt, respectively. While the lowest reduction % recorded was 48.78, 26.70, 45.12 and 39.41 of the same parameters due to humic acid treatment.

Both PC1 and PC2 together explained that more than 74.6% and 79.6% of the total variance found for both infestation sources (Rhizoctonia solani; Fig. 7a) and (Fusarium oxysporum; Fig. 7b), respectively. The healthy survival plants were associated with the bio-filtrate of T. harzianum, T. viride and P. aeruginosa. The damping -off %, root rot and the infested control were associated with bio-stimulant substances (humic acid and chitosan). The other traits of antioxidant enzymes (Catalase, peroxidase, polyphenol oxidase and chitinase) as well as photosynthetic pigments showed an association relationship with the bio-filtrate of B. subtilis, B. megaterium and P. fluorescens.

Discussion

Root rot and wilt diseases are among the most common diseases affecting broad bean plants at all growth stages. Fungicide current plus 50% wp eliminated the harmful effect of root rot and wilt diseases, which might be due to their degradation in the soil, leading to protection of plants from soil-borne fungi. In addition, the fungicides act as inhibitors for sterol synthesis in fungal cells as well as inhibitors of mitochondrial electron transport. Moreover, the role of antagonistic bacteria in controlling plant diseases may be due to many mechanisms, including their production of antibiosis and siderophores and/or competition for space and nutrients, as well as induction of systemic resistance in the host [42]. Moreover, bacterial culture filtrates inhibited fungal growth due to bacterial strains that can produce one or more antifungal antibiotics [43].

The recent trial’s findings position bio-stimulants and microbial filtrations as functionally distinct yet complementary tools to chemical fungicides. Fungicides frequently inhibit broad-spectrum pathogens quickly, whereas biological methods offer longer-term resilience by improving soil health, causing resistance, and modifying the microbiome—features central to next-generation sustainable crop protection systems. Accordingly, it was found that combining biological approaches followed in order to improve soil microbial diversity and plant growth while suppressing diseases to levels equivalent to those of synthetic pesticides. Studies have shown that combined Trichoderma–bacterial inoculants can achieve plant-protection results comparable to those of chemical pesticides, underscoring their value for sustainable farming and lowering reliance on pesticides [44]. Under greenhouse or field circumstances, comparative pathogen-control experiments also demonstrated that biological agents can offer quantifiable protection. Trichoderma and Bacillus antagonistic strains considerably decreased the development of necrosis brought on by grapevine trunk infections; several isolates of Bacillus velezensis showed potent protective activity on par with commercial bio-control formulations [45]. Moreover, the co-application of humic acid, chitosan, and Bacillus subtilis, for instance, demonstrated synergistic growth-promoting, antifungal effects and reducing disease incidence by about 45% in tomato [46].

In this research, bio-cultural filters (Trichoderma, Bacillus and Pseudomonas) are the most abundant microbiome member species for these diseases and bio-stimulant substances (humic acid and chitosan) are applied to reduce the use of fungicides and minimize their residues in agricultural products. The reason behind selecting such kinds of treatments is that these substances are inexpensive and safe (non-hazardous) to humans and the environment as mentioned in the findings of Abdel-Kader et al. [47] who utilized biological and chemical stimuli. The data in Table 1 and Figs. 1, 2 and 3 indicated that the tested isolates of all tested microbiota showed matching in sequence and its related global isolates in the GenBank. The anatomical studies (Fig. 4) showed that both fungal pathogens caused detrimental effects on the cell wall, complete disruption of some epidermal cells, and hydrolysis of some cortical cells, as demonstrated under a light microscope. These results were like those of Abdel-Hai and Ali [48, 49], who found that the tested pathogenic fungi caused damage to the anatomy of lentil and bean roots. Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 5) demonstrated the formation of multiple tyloses in the xylem vessels, leading to vessel blockage. These detrimental effects can lead to the destruction of lateral roots and reduced water and nutrient uptake, followed by a negative impact on all physiological activities. These findings were supported by the results of Mahmoud et al. [50] which showed that root fungal pathogens produce some enzymes and hormones that lead to the rotting of seeds and the fall of seedlings.

The data presented in Fig. 6 showed that all R. solani isolates were pathogenic and caused wilt and root rot symptoms in broad beans. On the other hand, all F. oxysporum isolates were pathogenic and caused early and late wilt. Data in Table 2 showed that the reduction % of the tested pathogenic fungi with the highest inhibition in the linear growth of R. solani and F. oxysporum, respectively was due to the antifungal effects of T. harzianum, while humic acid induced the lowest inhibition. Table 3 showed that culture filtrates and bio-materials greatly reduced the occurrence of Rhizoctonia wilt, root rot, and Fusarium wilt, resulting in higher healthy plant survival rates. Chitosan was more efficient than humic acid in this concern. Plant survival rates were best in healthy controls, followed by bio-culture filtrate of Trichoderma harzianum infected with F. oxysporum, and lowest in the R. solani infected control. The T. harzianum was discovered to have the greatest antifungal effect in decreasing disease scores for damping-off, root rot, early wilt, and late wilt, respectively. The lowest percentage of decline was recorded in the same parameters as a result of humic acid treatment. In line with the obtained results, Abdelaziz et al. [51] recorded a reduction in oxidative damage and controlled the damping-off diseases correlated with the increase in photosynthetic pigments and the antioxidant defense system including polyphenol oxidase, peroxidase and catalase enzyme activities when they used endophytic Aspergillus terreus. They considered A. terreus an effective tool (alternative to synthetic chemical pesticides which threaten the environment and human health) to control the suppression of Rhizoctonia solani in Phaseolus vulgaris and Vicia faba. The observed decrease in disease incidence and severity is mostly due to the combined biocontrol activity of PGPR bacteria and Trichoderma harzianum. Rather than operating in isolation, these microorganisms use complementary strategies to inhibit soil-borne diseases and increase host plant tolerance. In the case of PGPR, their antagonistic effect is primarily linked to the production of antimicrobial metabolites, competition for nutrients and rhizosphere niches, and modulation of plant defense responses, eliminating the need for repeated mechanistic explanations across different disease parameters. In this concern, Bacillus spp. found to inhibits F. oxysporum mycelia growth [52]. The primary ways that Trichoderma works as a mycoparasite are the release of hydrolytic enzymes (such as chitinases and glucanases), the synthesis of secondary metabolites, and the induction of plant systemic resistance. These processes allow pathogen growth suppression, membrane integrity disturbance, and fungal cell wall disintegration. The role of Trichoderma used as a biological control agent in crop protection can be explained by the improvement in resistance to soil-borne diseases by activating host plant defense signaling pathways due to Trichoderma–plant interactions [53]. Trichoderma produces antimicrobial lipopeptides, causing oxidative stress in pathogen cells, causing membrane permeabilization, and interfering with DNA and protein synthesis. Bacillus species, on the other hand, primarily inhibit phytopathogenic fungi via preventing mycelial development and germination. Additionally, Bacillus species increase plant tolerance to infection and promote host resistance, which lessens the severity of diseases brought on by harmful soil-borne fungi [54].

On the other side, culture filtrates of Pseudomonas fluorescence produces some antifungal metabolites which are considered one of the most important factors of biological resistance [5]. The plant resistance system in terms of the antioxidant enzyme system in the present work (Table 4) improved with the used treatments. The obtained results in this domain are supported by the suggested role of Plant Growth Promoting Rhizobacteria in the synthesis of enzymes. This suggestion assume that such effect may induce modulation in plant hormone levels, limit available iron by producing siderophores, and inhibit pathogens by producing antibiotics [55]. Moreover, several services and benefits are provided by microorganisms to plants through receiving metabolites [56] for their survival. Microbiota have been associated with almost all global plants since their earliest growth to support early terrestrial plants tackling challenges such as pathogens in various functional roles [57]. This may also explain the improvement in photosynthetic pigment content (Table 5) in soil infested with both root rot and wilt pathogens compared with control treatments. The increased antioxidant enzyme activities and photosynthetic pigment content found in the current study are consistent with recent publications revealing the role of chitosan as an effective biotic elicitor capable of stimulating plant defense responses under pathogen stress. Mukarram et al. [58] showed similar increases in catalase, peroxidase, polyphenol oxidase, and chitinase activities, which they attributed to chitosan-mediated plant immunity priming and antioxidant metabolism reinforcement. The accumulation of phenolic compounds observed in this study is supported by the findings of Sharma et al. [59] who emphasized that rapid phenolic deposition at infection sites is an important early defense strategy for limiting pathogen establishment. The observed stimulation of defense-related enzymes is also consistent with the findings of Abdelaziz et al. [60] who found that enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase contribute directly to fungal cell wall degradation and pathogen proliferation. These investigations back up the current study findings, demonstrating that chitosan-based treatments not only lower pathogen activity, but also activate systemic defense responses, hence increasing plant vigor and resilience. Chitosan's dual mode of action makes it one of the most promising eco-friendly instruments for long-term disease management in modern agricultural systems. Scientists have summarized the mechanism of action of Trichoderma fungi as follows: antagonism, antibiosis, competition for nutrients and space, inhibition of pathogens or plant enzymes, and complex interactions with plants in the root zone. Numerous processes, such as colonization, symbiosis, organic matter decomposition, nutrient exchange, and plant growth stimulation, are included in these interactions [61, 62]. The antifungal effect of humic acid can be attributed to altered enzyme activities [63]. Humic acid also increases the number of microbes in the soil, regulates pH, and affects enzymes [64]. Humic acid also improved the uptake of nutrients and reduced the incidence and severity of diseases [65]. According to studies, chitosan's antifungal activity is attributed to fungal growth inhibition [66], as well as its ability to induce strong plant defense responses against soil borne-pathogens [19]. Humic chemicals boost these responses by upregulating defense enzyme activity (e.g., peroxidase and polyphenol oxidase). This combined activation of defensive mechanisms minimizes pathogen colonization, disease severity, and improves overall plant health [67].

These synchronized molecular and biochemical reactions provide a comprehensive explanation for the observed decrease in disease incidence, confirming Trichoderma, chitosan, and humic acid's potential as effective biological inducers of plant defense. In this concern, Abdelaziz et al. [68] reported that conferring protection against Fusarium wilt with a notable decrease in disease index in tomato when they used a combination of A. oryzae and A. tubingensis. The use of these two fungi (single or mixed) and salicylic acid exhibited a decline in the influence of Fusarium wilt on plant capability accompanied by significant improvement in the levels of photosynthetic pigments and enhancement in the activity of antioxidant enzymes.

Regarding the relationship between enzymes and defense against pathogens, several studies on various plants, e.g. Cucumber annuum, tomato, and wheat [69] have shown a strong correlation relationship that came in line with obtained results (Fig. 8a & b) in the present work. Also, the obtained resulted that showed a significant correlation between the plant resistance and peroxidase as well as polyphenol oxidase activities agreed with Esmaeil et al. [70].

Conclusion

From the previous results it could be concluded that seed soaking and followed by foliar spraying of faba bean with both bio-culture filtrates (Trichoderma harzianum) or bio-stimulant substances (chitosan) reduced the incidence of damping-off, root rot and wilt diseases under artificial infested with R. solani and F. oxysporum. The activation of some enzymes and photosynthetic pigments may suggests the usage of Trichoderma culture filtrate or chitosan as seed soaking with foliar treatment as fungicide alternatives for controlling root-rot and wilt diseases and enhanced physiological activities in faba bean.
