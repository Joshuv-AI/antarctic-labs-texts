Effect of magnetically guided capsule endoscopy on gastrointestinal transit time and diagnostic yield: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Amar Lal, Ragini Gopagoni, FNU Manisha, Rumaisa Siddiqui, Asif Hussain, FNU Riya, Nayyar Iqbal Tiwana, Rahul Rai, Rabia Kamran, Sajida Moiz Hussain Qamari, Muhammad Burhan, Mohammad Jawwad, Hira Riaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCapsule endoscopy (CE) is a primary tool for small bowel visualization, but its diagnostic yield can be limited by prolonged transit times and finite battery life. Various interventions, including prokinetics, purgatives, and magnetic steering, are used to optimize CE performance. This systematic review and meta-analysis compare the effectiveness of these strategies in improving transit times and completion rates.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus was conducted for randomized controlled trials and observational studies evaluating magnetic control, prokinetics, or purgatives in adult patients undergoing small bowel CE. Primary outcomes were gastric transit time (GTT), small bowel transit time (SBTT), and completion rate (CR). A random-effects model was used for all meta-analyses.ResultsForty studies involving 3,041 participants were included. For reducing GTT, Among the included studies, erythromycin and magnetic control were each associated with shorter gastric transit times, while lubiprostone and PEG showed beneficial effects on small bowel transit—relative to their respective controls. Metoclopramide and castor oil were most strongly associated with an increased completion rate.ConclusionsNo single intervention optimizes all CE parameters. Erythromycin and magnetic control are effective for rapid gastric transit, while lubiprostone and PEG expedite small bowel transit. To maximize the likelihood of a complete examination, metoclopramide is a well-supported option. These findings allow clinicians to select a tailored pre-procedural strategy based on the specific goals of the CE examination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04232-z.

Introduction

Capsule endoscopy (CE) has transformed the diagnostic landscape for small bowel (SB) pathologies, offering a non-invasive alternative to traditional endoscopy with superior patient acceptability and mucosal visualization [1]. The procedure involves a vitamin-sized, wireless camera capsule, such as the PillCam™ (Medtronic), which captures high-definition images (156° field of view, three frames/second) as it traverses the gastrointestinal tract via natural peristalsis, achieving a diagnostic yield of 85–90% for conditions like obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB) and Crohn’s disease [2]. Its safety profile, combined with the ability to visualize otherwise inaccessible regions of the small bowel, makes CE an invaluable tool in the evaluation of unexplained anemia, suspected small bowel tumors, and mucosal inflammatory changes, especially when conventional methods yield inconclusive results.

Despite these advantages, CE has limitations that can reduce its diagnostic yield. These include technical difficulties, the inability of some patients to swallow the capsule, poor-quality imaging, gastric retention, and delayed transit time—all of which may result in incomplete small bowel examinations [3]. CE’s reliance on passive locomotion introduces critical limitations: up to 30% of exams are incomplete due to prolonged gastric transit time (GTT > 2 h) delaying small bowel imaging, compounded by the capsule’s finite battery lifespan (8–12 h), which risks incomplete SB coverage if total transit time exceeds this window [3]. This issue is particularly prevalent in patients with diabetes, gastroparesis, or prior abdominal surgery [4]. In such cases, up to 20% of patients require repeat procedures, increasing healthcare costs and delaying diagnoses [5].

Magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy (MCE) addresses these challenges by integrating real-time external magnetic steering, enabling active navigation of the capsule through the stomach and proximal SB [6]. MCE employs handheld or robotic controllers to adjust capsule orientation, tilt (0–90°), and velocity (0–20 mm/s), achieving gastric examination completeness rates of 95%—comparable to conventional esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) [7]. Additionally, delayed gastric emptying—a key factor contributing to incomplete small bowel evaluations—can be mitigated by actively reducing gastric and small bowel transit times. So, MCE also addresses the challenge of limited capsule battery life.

Conventional CE protocols often rely on pharmacological agents to optimize transit times. Prokinetics like domperidone and erythromycin accelerate GTT by 35–50%, while purgatives such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) enhance SB mucosal visibility [8]. However, these agents exhibit suboptimal efficacy in elderly populations, with studies reporting that 40% of patients fail to achieve complete SB visualization despite maximal dosing [9]. Adverse effects—nausea (25%), abdominal pain (15%), and electrolyte imbalances (5%)—further limit tolerability, particularly in those with renal or cardiac comorbidities [10].

Despite these advancements, significant evidence gaps persist. While previous systematic reviews have assessed prokinetics or magnetic steering in isolation [11], a comprehensive, direct comparison of the relative efficacy of pharmacological, purgative, and mechanical interventions is lacking. Clinicians are therefore left without clear guidance on how to best select an optimization strategy. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to fill this gap by synthesizing all available evidence to compare MCE against conventional CE with prokinetics and purgatives in reducing GTT and SBTT and improving examination completion rates.

Methods

Study design and protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines [12]. Its primary aim was to assess the effectiveness of various interventions—namely purgatives, prokinetic agents, and magnetic control systems—on gastric transit time (GTT), small bowel transit time (SBTT), and completion rates (CR) in patients undergoing capsule endoscopy (CE) using the PillCam Small Bowel wireless video capsule (Medtronic). These intervention types were selected because they represent the most commonly used and mechanistically distinct approaches in clinical practice aimed at modifying gastrointestinal transit and enhancing CE performance.

Gastric transit time (GTT), Defined as the time from capsule ingestion to its passage through the pylorus;

Small bowel transit time (SBTT), Defined as the time from pyloric passage to the first image of the cecum;

Completion rate (CR), Defined as the successful visualization of the cecum before battery exhaustion, in patients undergoing capsule endoscopy (CE).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or observational studies that evaluated the effects of purgatives, prokinetics, or magnetic control interventions on gastric transit time (GTT), small bowel transit time (SBTT), or completion rates (CR) in adult patients (≥ 18 years old) undergoing capsule endoscopy (CE) using the PillCam™ Small Bowel wireless video capsule (Medtronic). To be included, full-text articles had to report quantitative data for at least one of the primary outcomes, including the mean and standard deviation (or data allowing their calculation) for GTT or SBTT, and event numbers for both intervention and control groups to determine an odds ratio for the completion rate. Additionally, based on the protocols of the included studies, participants were eligible if they were adults aged 18 years or older, able to provide informed consent, and undergoing CE for standard indications such as obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB), suspected Crohn’s disease, or iron deficiency anemia. Patients were generally required to have no known or suspected gastrointestinal obstruction, strictures, fistulas, or motility disorders, and to demonstrate normal swallowing function without the need for capsule delivery assistance.

Studies were excluded if they were non-peer-reviewed (such as conference abstracts, editorials, or letters), focused on pediatric populations (< 18 years old), as gastrointestinal physiology and transit times can differ significantly from adults, limiting direct comparability; investigated other types of capsule or wired endoscopy (e.g., colon capsule, esophageal capsule, or conventional flexible endoscopy), as these devices have different technical specifications, procedural goals, and battery longevity than the small bowel capsule, which was the focus of this review; or included patients with contraindications to capsule endoscopy such as prior extensive small bowel surgery, implanted electronic devices (e.g., pacemakers or defibrillators), pregnancy, or severe swallowing difficulties. Case reports, review articles, opinion pieces, and prior systematic reviews or meta-analyses were also excluded. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria were harmonized based on those used within the individual studies to ensure consistency across the meta-analysis.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus, up to April 2025. The search strategy utilized a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to “capsule endoscopy,” “gastric transit time,” “small bowel transit time,” “purgatives,” “prokinetics,” and “magnetic control.” In addition, hand searches of the reference lists of all included studies and previously published systematic reviews of bowel preparation for CE or MCE were performed. No geographical restriction was applied. The search strategy was developed in consultation with a medical librarian with expertise in systematic review searching. The search terms were adjusted to the specificities of the different databases.

Screening and data extraction

Two reviewers and independently screened all titles and abstracts identified by the search strategy to assess their eligibility. The full texts of potentially relevant studies were then retrieved for a detailed review against the inclusion criteria. Any disagreements regarding study eligibility were resolved through discussion and consensus or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer.

Data from the included studies were extracted independently by the same two reviewers using a pre-piloted, standardized data extraction form. This form captured the following information: (1) study details (first author, year of publication, study design, country of origin); (2) participant characteristics (sample size, age, sex, clinical indication for CE); (3) intervention and control details (type of intervention, dose, timing, and route of administration); and (4) primary outcome data. For continuous outcomes (GTT and SBTT), we extracted the mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size for each group. For the dichotomous outcome (completion rate), we extracted the number of events and total sample size for each group. The specific definitions of GTT, SBTT, and completion rate as reported in each study were also extracted. If SDs were not reported, we planned to calculate them from confidence intervals or p-values if available. Any discrepancies in extracted data between the two reviewers were resolved by re-examining the source article until a consensus was achieved.

During data extraction, we noted the specific definitions for primary outcomes used in each study. We observed variations in these definitions, such as GTT being measured from the time of capsule ingestion versus confirmed entry into the stomach, and completion rate being defined as either cecal visualization or passage of the ileocecal valve. These definitions were recorded for qualitative assessment. For consistency in units, all the GTT and SBTT values were converted to minutes.

Quality assessment

Two authors analyzed the quality of included Randomized Control trials (RCTs) using the Cochrane risk of bias tool 2.0 (RoB2.0) and Observational Studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. This tool assesses the risk of bias associated with patient selection, study performance, outcome detection, data attrition, study findings reporting, and other types of bias. The judgments can be ‘Low’ or ‘High’ in terms of bias risk, or they can suggest ‘Some concerns’.

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model to account for expected heterogeneity across studies. This model was applied to estimate pooled mean differences (MD) for gastric transit time (GTT) and small bowel transit time (SBTT), and odds ratios (ORs) for completion rate (CR), each with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Mean differences in GTT and SBTT were calculated in minutes, while CR outcomes were analyzed as binary events. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values < 25% indicating low, 25–50% moderate, and > 50% high heterogeneity. Publication bias for the primary outcomes (GTT, SBTT, and CR) was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots.

Given the clinical and methodological diversity among the included studies—including differences in intervention type (e.g., magnetic steering, prokinetics, purgatives), patient populations, and study design—a random-effects model was deemed most appropriate. To address concerns related to mechanistic heterogeneity, each intervention was analyzed in comparison to its respective control group; no direct comparisons between intervention types were made. As such, this meta-analysis was designed to provide a descriptive synthesis of the effects of individual interventions rather than to infer relative superiority between mechanistically distinct strategies.

Additionally, to assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating the primary meta-analyses restricted to randomized controlled trials (RCTs) only. These analyses aimed to determine whether conclusions were consistent when considering only the highest-quality evidence.

All statistical analyses, data synthesis, and generation of forest and funnel plots were performed using Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4; Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014).

Our meta-analyses were conducted to assess the effect of each intervention compared to control. While multiple interventions are reported in parallel, we did not conduct direct comparisons between mechanistically different classes. The pooled estimates should be interpreted within the context of individual intervention types.

Results

Study selection

The initial systematic literature search yielded a total of 567 records from three major electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. These databases were chosen due to their comprehensive coverage of biomedical and clinical research. After importing all retrieved citations into reference management software and removing duplicates, 123 unique articles remained for further screening. A two-step screening process was then undertaken. First, titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by two researchers to assess their relevance to the predefined inclusion criteria, leading to the exclusion of 60 articles that were either unrelated to the topic, involved non-human subjects, or lacked necessary outcome data. This left 63 full-text articles, which were thoroughly examined in detail for methodological quality, eligibility, and completeness of data. Following this full-text review, 23 articles were excluded for reasons such as insufficient data for extraction, non-compliance with inclusion criteria, or being reviews, editorials, or conference abstracts. Consequently, a total of 40 studies met all inclusion criteria and were deemed suitable for inclusion in the final meta-analysis. These 40 studies collectively included data from 3041 participants, providing a robust and comprehensive evidence base for quantitative synthesis. Study characteristics

The meta-analysis included 40 studies published between 2004 and 2025, comprising a total of 3041 participants. All studies were either randomized controlled trials or observational studies that compared an intervention group to a control group. The primary outcomes assessed were gastric transit time (GTT), small bowel transit time (SBTT), and completion rate (CR).

All included studies enrolled adult participants undergoing capsule endoscopy or other gastrointestinal transit assessments. The clinical indications varied across studies and included suspected small bowel bleeding, inflammatory bowel disease, and gastrointestinal motility disorders. The mean age ranged from 32.4 to 67.1 years, reflecting a predominantly middle-aged adult population.

Intervention strategies varied widely. Among the 40 studies, 8 evaluated the use of polyethylene glycol (PEG), 3 investigated lubiprostone, 9 assessed metoclopramide, 3 focused on erythromycin, 6 explored magnetic steering systems, 2 examined castor oil, 2 used chewing gum, and 7 evaluated simethicone. These interventions aimed to enhance capsule propulsion, reduce retention, or improve mucosal visualization.

Most studies reported baseline characteristics such as age, sex distribution, and relevant comorbidities, which were generally well balanced between the intervention and control groups. Further details regarding study design, sample size, interventions, and outcomes are summarized in Table 1.

For the 29 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB2.0) tool revealed that 19 studies were at a low risk of bias and 10 had ‘some concerns’. A summary figure illustrating the risk of bias for the included RCTs is presented in Figure S22.

For the 11 observational studies, scores on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) ranged from 7 to 9 out of a possible 9 stars, with a mean score of 8. We categorized 11 studies as high quality (≥ 7 stars). The detailed quality assessment score for each individual study is provided in Table 2.

Gastric transit time (GTT)

The analysis of GTT outcomes across various interventions revealed variable effects (Table 3).

Magnetic intervention

The magnetic steering group showed the greatest reduction in gastric transit time (GTT), with a mean difference of −31.41 min (95% CI: −67.86 to 5.05, I² = 100%, p < 0.00001) (Figure S1). This suggests a potential improvement in gastric emptying, although the wide confidence interval includes the null, indicating statistical uncertainty.

Pharmacological interventions

Among pharmacological agents, metoclopramide demonstrated a modest reduction in GTT, with a mean difference of −7.34 min (95% CI: −23.56 to 8.87, I² = 94%, p < 0.00001) (Figure S14), indicating a possible benefit but without statistical significance. A more pronounced reduction was observed with erythromycin, which showed a mean difference of −25.06 min (95% CI: −69.90 to 19.78, I² = 74%, p = 0.05) (Figure S9); however, the wide confidence interval again reflects uncertainty.

In contrast, lubiprostone increased GTT by 20.80 min (95% CI: −9.87 to 51.46, I² = 0%, p < 0.50) (Figure S12), suggesting a potential delay in gastric transit. Other agents, including polyethylene glycol (Figure S4), chewing gum (Figure S12), and simethicone (Figure S20), exhibited minimal or negligible effects on GTT, with mean differences close to zero and confidence intervals crossing the null.

Small bowel transit time (SBTT)

Magnetic intervention

Magnetic steering showed a slight increase in SBTT by 9.87 min (95% CI: −0.70 to 20.43, I² = 95%, p < 0.00001) (Figure S2). While this suggests a potential delay in small bowel transit, the confidence interval crosses the null, indicating that the result is not statistically significant and should be interpreted with caution.

Pharmacological interventions

Among pharmacological interventions, results for small bowel transit time (SBTT) varied considerably. Metoclopramide was associated with a notable reduction in SBTT by −20.03 min (95% CI: −66.50 to 26.43, I² = 99%, p < 0.00001) (Figure S15), though the wide confidence interval encompassing the null limits the strength of this finding. Polyethylene glycol demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in SBTT, with a reduction of −17.94 min (95% CI: −30.49 to −5.40, I² = 73%, p = 0.002) (Figure S5), suggesting enhanced small bowel motility. Lubiprostone also reduced SBTT by −23.55 min (95% CI: −64.21 to 17.11, I² = 0%, p = 0.50) (Figure S13), although this effect did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, erythromycin slightly increased SBTT by 13.10 min (95% CI: −13.29 to 39.48, I² = 0%, p = 0.51) (Figure S10), again without statistical significance. Other agents, including simethicone (Figure S19), chewing gum (Figure S17), and castor oil (Figure S7), showed no consistent or clinically meaningful effects on SBTT in the included studies.

Completion rate (CR)

Magnetic intervention

Magnetic steering was associated with a non-significant increase in completion rates, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.29 (95% CI: 0.25 to 6.54, I² = 61%, p = 0.08) (Figure S3). While suggestive of a possible benefit in facilitating capsule progression through the gastrointestinal tract, the wide confidence interval and moderate heterogeneity limit the interpretability of this finding. Similarly, polyethylene glycol, though not a magnetic method, was included as a preparatory agent and showed no meaningful effect on completion (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.41 to 2.32, I² = 78%, p = 0.0001) (Figure S6), indicating little to no enhancement in completion rates.

Pharmacological interventions

Among pharmacological agents, castor oil demonstrated the strongest association with increased capsule completion, yielding an OR of 4.00 (95% CI: 1.55 to 10.34, I² = 54%, p = 0.14) (Figure S8), suggesting a significant improvement in capsule passage. Metoclopramide also had a favorable impact on completion rates, with an OR of 2.42 (95% CI: 1.55 to 3.77, I² = 33%, p < 0.17) (Figure S16). Erythromycin showed a moderate but non-significant improvement (OR: 2.00, 95% CI: 0.87 to 4.59, I² = 0%, p = 0.60) (Figure S11). Simethicone demonstrated a modest effect (OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 0.43 to 3.27, I² = 0%, p = 0.90) (Figure S21), though not statistically significant. Data for lubiprostone and chewing gum (Figure S18) were insufficient to draw conclusions about their impact on completion rates.

Subgroup analysis according to the study type

To evaluate the robustness of our findings and ensure the highest level of evidence, we performed a subgroup analysis that included only randomized controlled trials (RCTs), whereas the original meta-analysis combined both RCTs and observational studies. This approach was undertaken to minimize potential bias from non-randomized designs and to obtain the most precise and reliable estimates of effect. Overall, the sensitivity results remained largely consistent with the original analysis, supporting the stability and validity of our conclusions. The results for each intervention are presented separately below. These are visualised in Supplimentary Figures S22-S37.

Magnetic steering

In the original analysis (RCTs + observational studies), gastric transit time (GTT) was reduced by − 31.41 min (95% CI: −67.86 to 5.05). When including only RCTs, this effect attenuated to − 7.18 min (95% CI: −37.43 to 23.08). Small bowel transit time (SBTT) increased slightly from 9.87 min (95% CI: −0.70 to 20.43) to 12.04 min (95% CI: −17.39 to 41.48). The completion rate (CR) estimate also decreased from 1.29 (95% CI: 0.25 to 6.54) to 0.78 (95% CI: 0.26 to 2.35).

Metoclopramide

For metoclopramide, the original analysis estimated a reduction in GTT of − 7.34 min (95% CI: −23.56 to 8.87), which shifted to a slight positive difference of 0.64 min (95% CI: −29.52 to 30.79) when restricting to RCTs. SBTT and CR remained comparable (SBTT: −20.03 min to − 8.69 min; CR: 2.42 [95% CI: 1.55–3.77] to 2.38 [95% CI: 0.99–5.73]).

Simethicone

For simethicone, GTT changed from 1.33 min (95% CI: −1.09 to 17.40) to 3.05 min (95% CI: −7.18 to 13.28) in the RCT-only analysis. SBTT varied slightly (− 1.32 min [95% CI: −69.86 to 73.86] vs. − 4.92 min [95% CI: −64.64 to 54.79]), and CR remained unchanged at 1.18 (95% CI: 0.33–4.27) in both analyses.

Polyethylene glycol

For polyethylene glycol, the GTT estimate was − 0.05 min (95% CI: −2.75 to 2.64) in the overall analysis and − 1.30 min (95% CI: −2.46 to − 0.14) in the RCT-only analysis. SBTT showed a slightly larger reduction (− 17.94 min [95% CI: −30.49 to − 5.40] vs. − 27.31 min [95% CI: −37.15 to − 17.48]), while CR increased from 0.98 (95% CI: 0.41–2.32) to 1.56 (95% CI: 0.35–6.97).

Erythromycin

In the original meta-analysis including both RCTs and observational studies, erythromycin was associated with a reduction in gastric transit time (GTT) of − 25.06 min (95% CI: −69.90 to 19.78). When restricting the analysis to RCTs only, the GTT estimate changed to − 7.28 min (95% CI: −15.04 to 0.48). Small bowel transit time (SBTT) showed a minor change from 13.10 min (95% CI: −13.29 to 39.48) in the overall analysis to 8.72 min (95% CI: −32.84 to 50.28) in the RCT-only analysis. Completion rate (CR) slightly decreased from 2.00 (95% CI: 0.87 to 4.59) to 1.71 (95% CI: 0.52 to 5.66).

These findings suggest that focusing solely on RCTs does not materially change the effect estimates for erythromycin, with only slight shifts in the point estimates.

Castor oil

In the original analysis, castor oil’s effect on gastric transit time (GTT) was not reported, while SBTT was reduced by − 9.33 min (95% CI: −68.49 to 49.83). The completion rate (CR) was estimated at 4.00 (95% CI: 1.55 to 10.34). Results were reported for both cohorts combined, preventing separate estimation of GTT, SBTT, and CR exclusively for RCTs.

This indicates that while castor oil shows a potential improvement in completion rates, the limited RCT-only data preclude robust sensitivity analysis conclusions.

Lubiprostone

In the original meta-analysis including both RCTs and observational studies, lubiprostone was associated with a mean change in gastric transit time (GTT) of 20.80 min (95% CI: −9.87 to 51.46). When restricting the analysis to RCTs only, the GTT estimate increased to 30.21 min (95% CI: −7.49 to 67.90). Similarly, small bowel transit time (SBTT) changed from − 23.55 min (95% CI: −64.21 to 17.11) in the overall analysis to − 18.96 min (95% CI: −61.60 to 23.69) in the RCT-only analysis. Completion rate (CR) data were not reported for lubiprostone in either analysis.

These findings suggest that limiting the analysis to RCTs does not substantially alter the estimated effects of lubiprostone on capsule endoscopy transit outcomes.

Discussion

Our systematic review and meta-analysis underscore the pivotal role of pre-procedural interventions in optimizing the diagnostic performance of capsule endoscopy (CE) for small bowel (SB) visualization. Adequate visualization of the entire small bowel is critical for maximizing the diagnostic yield of CE, and our findings highlight how various preparatory strategies can significantly influence procedural efficacy. The comparison of different interventions—such as bowel purgatives, prokinetic agents, and magnetic control (MC) technologies—provides important insights into tailoring pre-procedural protocols to improve specific clinical outcomes.

Notably, the use of purgatives was associated with improved small bowel cleanliness and, in some studies, enhanced completion rates (CRs), thereby reducing the likelihood of incomplete examinations. Similarly, prokinetic agents demonstrated benefits in reducing gastric transit time (GTT), ensuring timely entry of the capsule into the small intestine, which is often a limiting factor in incomplete studies. Magnetic control (MC), a more recent innovation, offers the advantage of externally navigating the capsule through the stomach, potentially bypassing gastric delays and improving overall efficiency. Our results suggest that multiple intervention strategies, when evaluated individually against control, are associated with potential improvements in CE outcomes. However, given the fundamental differences in mechanism and clinical application, we did not compare these interventions directly. Therefore, conclusions about optimal or combined strategies cannot be drawn from this analysis and should be investigated in future head-to-head or network meta-analyses.

These findings carry important implications for clinical practice, particularly in patients at high risk of incomplete CE or delayed gastric emptying. Further randomized controlled trials are warranted to refine current protocols and determine the most effective combinations or sequencing of interventions. Ultimately, the integration of evidence-based pre-procedural measures may enhance the diagnostic utility of CE, streamline workflow, and improve patient outcomes.

One of the key findings is the effectiveness of metoclopramide, which demonstrated the highest odds of successful CE completion and effectively reduced SBTT. This result supports the established role of metoclopramide in accelerating gastric emptying, suggesting its potential as an adjunct therapy to improve CE efficacy [14]. Similarly, erythromycin, a macrolide drug, emerged as a useful agent for reducing GTT, thereby facilitating faster capsule progression through the upper gastrointestinal tract [15, 16]. Given the constraints imposed by the short battery life of CE devices, optimizing GTT and SBTT can substantially enhance diagnostic yield [17, 18].

Although magnetic steering (MSG) showed promising trends in completion rates, its association with prolonged SBTT warrants further investigation. The potential trade-offs between improved navigation and longer transit times may influence the clinical application of this technique, suggesting the need for additional studies on its impact on diagnostic accuracy [19].

Interestingly, lubiprostone demonstrated contrasting effects, lengthening GTT while shortening SBTT. This dual effect complicates the clinical decision-making process, as while some agents expedite SB visualization, they may inadvertently delay gastric clearance [13]. Such findings emphasize the need for individualized selection of pre-CE interventions, as some pharmacological agents may provide benefits in one aspect of the procedure but complicate others.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG), on the other hand, was associated with a reduction in SBTT without negatively affecting completion rates, further supporting its role as a beneficial agent in CE preparation [20]. Additionally, agents like chewing gum and simethicone had negligible effects on transit times, suggesting that more targeted strategies may be needed for improving CE efficiency [21, 22].

Separating RCTs from observational studies in the sensitivity analysis did not meaningfully alter the overall conclusions of the meta-analysis. For most interventions, effect sizes for gastric transit time, small bowel transit time, and completion rate showed only minor numerical changes when restricting the analysis to RCTs, and the direction of the effects remained consistent. This suggests that the inclusion of observational studies in the primary analysis did not introduce substantial bias or distort the pooled estimates. However, for certain interventions, such as magnetic steering and erythromycin, we observed slight attenuation of the estimated effects in the RCT-only subgroup, indicating that observational studies may have contributed to some overestimation of treatment benefit. Conversely, polyethylene glycol and lubiprostone showed modest increases in effect size when analyzed in RCTs alone, suggesting that observational studies may have diluted these effects. Overall, the separation of study designs demonstrates that while observational data largely align with randomized evidence, RCT-only analyses provide more precise and methodologically robust estimates, thereby strengthening the confidence in the findings of this meta-analysis.

Limitations

A significant limitation of this meta-analysis is the conceptual challenge of pooling interventions with fundamentally different mechanisms. We grouped mechanical interventions like magnetic steering with various pharmacological agents (prokinetics and purgatives). While the random-effects model accounts for the statistical variance, readers should interpret the pooled estimates with caution. These interventions are not clinically interchangeable, and our analysis does not establish the superiority of one class over another. Rather, it provides a comprehensive overview of the current evidence for each strategy compared to control. Future research, ideally in the form of a network meta-analysis, is required to facilitate indirect comparisons between these different intervention classes. Additionally, we could not provide robust scientific evidence to compare the mechanistically different interventions so the results should be interpreted with care.

The validity of our pooled estimates is substantially impacted by the definitional inconsistencies of the primary outcomes across the included studies. For example, the starting point for GTT measurement and the anatomical landmark for a ‘complete’ examination varied, representing different physiological and clinical endpoints. Due to inconsistent reporting of these specific definitions in the primary studies, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses to explore these differences. This clinical heterogeneity in outcome measurement is a likely source of the statistical heterogeneity observed in our analyses, and the pooled effect sizes should therefore be interpreted as an average effect across these varied, non-standardized definitions.

Specifically for completion rates, a planned subgroup analysis based on the endpoint definition could not be performed due to inconsistent or absent reporting of these definitions in the primary studies. This highlights a critical need for standardized outcome reporting in future capsule endoscopy trials.

Our findings generalizability is constrained by our inclusion criteria. The exclusion of pediatric studies means our conclusions are not applicable to patients under 18 years of age. Furthermore, restricting the search to English language studies can also limit the amount of analysable data on capsule endoscopy. Similarly, by focusing exclusively on the PillCam™ Small Bowel capsule to ensure a degree of technological homogeneity, our results may not extend to other small bowel capsule systems available on the market. In addition, existing comorbids like diabetes and hypertension might influence the outcome of the interventions whic needs further research and analysis in future.

Furthermore, the generalizability of our findings may be influenced by the clinical heterogeneity of the patient populations in the included studies. The mean age of participants spanned a wide range, from young adults to older individuals. It is well-established that aging can affect gastrointestinal physiology, potentially leading to delayed gastric emptying and altered small bowel transit times. This age-related variation represents a potential, unmeasured source of heterogeneity in our analysis. Because the included studies did not report outcomes stratified by age group, we were unable to perform a subgroup analysis to investigate the impact of age on intervention efficacy. Similarly, while we noted the presence of comorbidities like diabetes, which can also affect gut motility, insufficient data prevented a formal analysis of their influence. Future studies should aim to report age- and comorbidity-stratified results to allow for a more granular understanding of these interventions in different patient subgroups.

Given the pooling of pharmacological, purgative, and mechanical interventions—each with distinct mechanisms and clinical use cases—our findings do not support direct comparisons between these strategies. Although our meta-analytic approach provides a descriptive summary of the available evidence for each intervention type, the mechanistic heterogeneity significantly limits any inferential claims about relative efficacy. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as independent intervention-versus-control effects, not as comparative assessments between intervention classes.

Conclusion

Our analysis provides a descriptive overview of how individual pre-procedural interventions may influence capsule endoscopy outcomes. While various strategies were associated with improvements in transit times or completion rates relative to controls, direct comparisons between these mechanistically distinct interventions are not methodologically supported. Clinicians should interpret these findings as intervention-specific, and future research is needed to evaluate their relative efficacy. By improving completion rates and optimizing transit times, clinicians can ensure better visualization of the small bowel mucosa, ultimately leading to improved diagnostic outcomes. Future research should focus on refining these interventions and exploring their applicability across diverse patient populations to further solidify the role of these strategies in clinical practice.
