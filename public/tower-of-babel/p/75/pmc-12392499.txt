Disaggregated level child morbidity in Zambia: an application of small area estimation method
Authors: Audrey M. Kalindi, Sumonkanti Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh rates of child morbidity and developmental challenges among children under five remain critical challenges in sub-Saharan Africa. Despite Zambia’s progress in reducing under-five morbidity, the rates remain high, with provincial-level disparities. These disparities are likely to be more pronounced at finer geographic levels, such as districts. However, demographic health surveys, designed for national and provincial estimates, lack sufficient data to produce reliable district-level morbidity statistics.ObjectiveThis study investigates the geospatial distribution of child morbidity prevalence across disaggregated administrative units using small area estimation (SAE) methods.Data and methodsData from the 2018 Zambia Demographic and Health Survey and the 2010 Zambian Census were used to derive direct estimates of child morbidity for small domains cross-classified by district and age group. A hierarchical Bayesian SAE model was developed to account for spatial and unobserved heterogeneity at provincial and district levels, including cross-classifications by age group.Results Model-based estimates show lower standard errors compared to the direct estimates and significant differences in morbidity levels within and between districts and provinces. Under-five morbidity prevalence remains high at 25%, with the highest rates in Luapula (approximately 40%) and Western provinces (around 35%) and among children aged 11–23 months (nearly 40%). SAE estimates at the district and district-by-age levels were numerically consistent when aggregated to higher levels, such as province or child age group. ConclusionThese data-driven detailed level estimates provide critical insights into the spatial distribution of child morbidity, supporting targeted interventions and informed policymaking at disaggregated levels.

Introduction

Morbidity remains a critical problem among children under five years of age in sub-Saharan Africa, serving as a key indicator of health status and development in the region. It has profound implications for cognitive and physical growth, future population health, and overall national development [15]. While global under-five mortality has reduced by 59% - from 93 deaths per 1,000 live births in 1990 to 38 deaths in 2021 - infectious diseases such as pneumonia, diarrhoea, and malaria remain leading causes of under-five deaths, along with preterm births and intrapartum complications [26]. Furthermore, progress in reducing child morbidity and mortality is uneven, with low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa and Asia, continuing to bear the highest burden [26].

Zambia has made profound progress in reducing under-five mortality, yet morbidity levels remain high and vary considerably across regions, reflecting the country’s broader socioeconomic and geographic inequities. Between 1996 and 2024, under-five mortality rates declined substantially, from 191 to 42 deaths per 1,000 live births. However, certain regions and demographic groups continue to experience disproportionately high morbidity rates [3, 20, 32]. These disparities are driven by a combination of socioeconomic, geographic, and demographic factors, with rural and peri-urban areas often facing the greatest challenges. The uneven distribution of child health outcomes underscores the urgent for targeted interventions to address inequities at subnational levels [33].

Geographic disparities in population density and resource distribution further exacerbate Zambia’s health inequities. Urban provinces like Lusaka, with 140.1 persons per square kilometre, stand in stark contrast to sparsely populated regions like North-Western Province, which has only 10.1 persons per square kilometre. These disparities contributed to unequal access to health care services, economic resources, and social resources, disproportionately affecting children in rural or remote areas. This highlights the critical importance of district-level health data to guide planning and development interventions tailored to local needs [20], [32].

While subnational-level morbidity data is crucial for planning child health interventions, few studies have examined child morbidity at levels below the province in Zambia. National surveys like the Zambia Demographic and Health Survey (ZDHS) provide reliable national and provincial health estimates but lack the granularity required for district-level analysis due to small sample sizes. This limitation hinders the development of targeted health policies and programmes. Although some studies in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia have explored subnational nutrition, births [1, 2, 23], mortality [27] and morbidity [11] patterns, evidence on the spatial and socioeconomic factors influencing child morbidity in Zambia remains scarce (Das et al., 2000, [12, 25]. In Zambia, studies employing spatial-temporal smoothing and small-area estimation (SAE) methods have predominantly focused on population health trends, with limited exploration of district-level child morbidity. Furthermore, most of this research has utilised data from 1980 to 2010 [13, 16, 21]. This study aims to address these gaps by applying SAE methods [22] to analyse the prevalence of district-level child morbidity, specifically fever, diarrhoea, and acute respiratory infections, in Zambia. Using data from the 2018 Zambia Demographic and Health Survey (ZDHS) and the 2010 Census of Population and Housing, the study investigates the prevalence of child morbidity across Zambian districts. It focuses on how spatial patterns and geographic disparities reveal the unequal distribution of child morbidity at the district level. Bayesian multilevel model applied for count data [4, 9, 10] is employed to capture these spatial variations, offering detailed insights into the geographic inequities in child morbidity.

The study builds on existing demographic literature to enhance the understanding of geographic variation in child mortality at the district level in Zambia [13]. It demonstrates how district-by-age level direct estimates are used as inputs for developing a small area model, expressed within a hierarchical Bayesian framework, to account for spatial and unobserved heterogeneity at both the province and district levels. Advancements in SAE methodology, coupled with the availability of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo Small Area Estimation R package “mcmcsae” [6], provided the motivation for this study. By disaggregating morbidity data at the district level and incorporating their variation by age group, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of child health inequities in Zambia. As Zambia endeavours to achieve the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2 on maternal and child health, the results of this study can help to identify high-morbidity and inequitable areas, enabling more targeted and effective health interventions.

This study aims to investigate child morbidity in Zambia by addressing two key objectives. First, it examines child morbidity levels across finely disaggregated domains, i.e. the district level and by district-age combinations, to provide a more granular understanding of the prevalence and patterns of morbidity. Second, it explores the extent to which child morbidity levels are unequally distributed across the country, identifying geographic and demographic disparities that underscore the need for targeted health interventions.

Data and Methods

Data source and input estimates

This study utilised data from the 2018 Zambia Demographic and Health Survey (ZDHS) and the 2010 Zambia Population and Housing Census [31, 33]. The ZDHS is a nationally representative survey of 13,625 households, providing comprehensive demographic and health information. The 2018 ZDHS employed a stratified two-stage sampling design with an impressive response rate of 98%. The sampling process began with the formation of strata based on 10 provinces and their urban-rural classifications. Within each stratum, clusters corresponding to census enumeration areas from the 2010 Census [31] were selected systematically using probability proportional to size. A total of 545 clusters were chosen and 25 households were sampled from each selected cluster through an equal probability systematic selection method, resulting in the inclusion of 13,625 households. The eligible households included women aged 15–49 years. Ultimately, 13,683 women were successfully interviewed during the 2018 ZDHS.

Mothers aged 15–49 were asked whether any of their children under five experienced fever, diarrhoea, or symptoms of acute respiratory infection (characterised by a chesty cough with short or rapid breathing) in the two weeks preceding the survey. The outcome variable, under-five child morbidity, captures the presence of any of these conditions—fever, diarrhea, or ARI—during that period. The analysis included 9,556 children under five, born to the surveyed women, who were reported to have either experienced at least one of these conditions or not in the two weeks before the survey.

The target parameter is the proportion of under-five children experiencing morbidity within a specific domain (e.g., district or age group). The targeted geo-demographic domains are defined as the cross-classification of 10 provinces and 115 districts by three child age groups (0–11 months, 12–23 months, and 24+ months). Using the ZDHS microdata, the proportion of child morbidity and the corresponding sampling errors were estimated for 345 district-by-age domains. These estimates were then utilised in developing a hierarchical Bayesian model. Child morbidity statistics at higher aggregation levels, such as province, age group, and province-by-age, were derived by aggregating the estimates from the district-by-age level domains. Our main motivation for combining the three indicators into a single comorbidity measure was methodological—to address data sparsity, especially for ARI (see Appendix Table 2 and Appendix Fig. 6) which had very low prevalence at the district-by-age level, and to enhance the stability and reliability of the small area estimates.

Since the number of districts changed in 2018 ZDHS, the district locations were matched with the geo-locations of the surveyed clusters by comparing the cluster shapefile with the updated district boundaries. This process ensured accurate assignment of clusters to their corresponding districts, reflecting the revised administrative divisions. Geographic Information System (GIS) tools were used to overlay the cluster coordinates on the district boundary map, allowing for precise spatial alignment and verification. This step was crucial to maintain consistency between the survey data and the current administrative framework, enabling reliable district-level analysis.

Statistical model

A Bayesian multilevel model was employed in the form of small area estimation at the district-age level, leveraging both cross-sectional and spatial correlations among neighbouring domains. This approach enhances the precision of estimates by borrowing strength from related data points and ensures numerical consistency between micro- and macro-level estimates. The developed model at the district-age level accounts for cross-sectional and spatial variations across multiple aggregation levels, including province, age, district, province-age, and district-age levels, providing robust and coherent estimates of child morbidity.

Results

Model development

The study developed district-age-level hierarchical Bayesian binomial models to analyse the number of children exposed to child morbidity during the two weeks preceding the survey. These models incorporate both fixed and random effects. Fixed effects include classification variables such as children’s age groups (0–11, 12–23, and 24+ months) and the regional variable province, accounting for variation at respective levels. Although district and district-age-level contextual variables (extracted from census data) could have been included, they were excluded due to inconsistencies in administrative structures across census years, such as having 74 districts in 2010 compared to 115 districts in subsequent years.

Random effects are designed to account for variations across district, district-age, and province-age levels. At the detailed district-age level, unstructured random effects (denoted as RE_AGE_DIST) are assumed to follow a normal distribution with a common variance. Spatial correlations among the outcomes are modelled using structured random effects (denoted as SP_DIST) at the district level. Additionally, to address variations at the province-age level, the model includes unstructured random effects (denoted as RE_AGE_PROV). While the RE_AGE_DIST and SP_DIST components are capable of capturing much of the age-province-level variation, the inclusion of RE_AGE_PROV provides additional assurance of unbiased estimates at this level.

Together, these random effects components address cross-sectional and spatial variation in the target outcomes. Model performance is assessed using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) and the Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC), also known as the Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC) [28, 29, 30]. A simpler model, which includes only the RE_AGE_DIST component, corresponds to the traditional area-level Fay-Herriot model [14]. Comparisons based on the information criteria (WAIC: 1476 for Fay-Herriot model and 1433 for the selected one) indicate that the extended model, which includes the additional random effects, provides improved performance.

Bias and consistency

To evaluate the unbiasedness of the model-based estimates, they are compared with direct estimates at various levels of aggregation and disaggregation, including province, age, district, and district-age levels. The estimated standard errors (SE) and relative standard errors, also known as coefficients of variation (CV), are examined to assess the consistency of the model-based estimates.

The SAE estimates are less extreme and exhibit lower standard errors, indicating higher consistency than DIR estimates. Furthermore, the relative standard errors for model-based estimates are consistently smaller than those for direct estimates. Child morbidity at aggregated levels

At the national level, over one-quarter of children under five years old are found to have experienced child morbidity. However, this rate varies significantly by age group: approximately 40% of children aged 12–23 months are affected, nearly double the rate for children aged 24 months and older. At the province level, child morbidity ranges from 18% in Central Province to 34% in Western Province and 40% in Luapula Province. These findings closely align with the direct estimates, underscoring the accuracy and reliability of the small area estimation method used (Fig. 2).

A breakdown of the province-level estimates by age group reveals that more than half of the children aged 12–23 months in Western and Luapula provinces are at risk of child morbidity, compared to 30% in Central Province (Fig. 3). In several provinces, such as Copperbelt, Eastern, Lusaka, Northern, and Southern, the risk among children aged 12–23 months is notably higher than that of infants and older children. This is evidenced by the fact that the 95% error bars for these age groups do not overlap. While non-overlapping 95% credible intervals suggest potential differences, we further examined the differences using pairwise comparisons of posterior distributions (Fig. 3). Child morbidity at disaggregated level

The model-based estimates provide more reliable and consistent measurements of child morbidity at both the district and district-age levels by incorporating variations across these levels. This approach helps to address the inherent variability in child morbidity rates and offers a more nuanced understanding of the patterns. The estimated range of child morbidity exposure highlights significant disparities across districts. For instance, the lowest morbidity rate, approximately 5%, was observed in Mumbwa district, located in Central Province. On the other hand, the highest morbidity rate, approximately 50%, was recorded in Chipili district, situated in Luapula Province (Fig. 4). These figures underscore the wide geographic differences in child health outcomes within the region.Fig. 4Model-based (SAE) estimates of childhood comorbidity—defined as exposure to either fever, diarrhoea, or acute respiratory infection (ARI)—with 95% credible intervals at the district and district-by-age-group levels

When disaggregating the data by age groups, children aged 12–23 months consistently show statistically significantly higher morbidity rates than the overall morbidity levels. For instance, in Lusaka, the capital district, the overall morbidity rate was estimated at 22%, while the rate for the 12–23 months age group was notably higher at 35%. Similarly, in Kalomo district, located in Southern Province, child morbidity levels were estimated at approximately 30% for under-5 children and 50% for those aged 12–23 months. These findings highlight that children in this specific age group are particularly vulnerable to higher morbidity levels compared to the broader population of children under five.

In some districts, such as Lunga, Milenge, Mpulungu, and Nsama, morbidity rates for infants were found to exceed 50%. This is notable, as the overall morbidity rates in these districts were already high, estimated to be over 40%. Furthermore, for children aged 12–23 months in these districts, the morbidity rates exceeded 60%, further illustrating the heightened risk for this age group in certain regions.

Conversely, in Kalumbila district, located in North-Western Province, the morbidity rates were comparatively low. The overall morbidity rate, as well as the rates for infants and older children, were all estimated to be below 15%. This demonstrates that some districts experience substantially lower morbidity levels, providing a contrast to the high rates observed in other parts of the region.

These results underscore the importance of localised analysis to identify specific areas and age groups with elevated morbidity risks. The model-based estimates presented in Fig. 4 reveal both the geographic and age-related variations in child morbidity, thereby highlighting areas where targeted interventions may be needed the most.

Discussion

This study applied a small area estimation technique to estimate disaggregated level prevalence of child morbidity in Zambia, aiming to understand geo-spatial disparities in child morbidity across the country. The findings reveal a high prevalence of child morbidity nationally with marked variation across provinces and districts as well as their stratification by children’s age. Much as there has been a reduction in child mortality in Zambia, morbidity prevalence is still high, exceeding 25%. Despite substantial efforts by the Zambian government and other child health stakeholders to address the causes of child morbidity, these rates remain persistently high and vary considerably, underscoring widespread geographical inequities at the disaggregated levels. Conventional data sources like the ZDHS are often insufficient for estimating disaggregated level official statistics to understand the disparities. This emphasises the need for advanced methods to produce detailed, disaggregated statistics for effective monitoring and targeted intervention.

To address the challenge of estimating child morbidity at the district-by-age level, the study developed a Bayesian multilevel model in the form of small area estimation. This approach enabled spatial-demographic cross-classified analysis across 115 districts and three age groups (0–11, 12–23, and 24+ months) in Zambia. The model captured variation at provincial level and age groups through fixed effects, while accounting for cross-sectional and spatial variation at the district, district-age, and province-age levels through random effects. These random effects accounted for unobserved differences at district and provincial levels, improving the model’s precision and reliability.

A comparison of model-based SAE with direct estimates at various aggregation levels showed that the model-based estimates had smaller standard errors and lower coefficients of variation (CVs), indicating higher consistency and reliability, at the detailed levels such as district, province-age, and district-age levels. At higher aggregation levels, such as national and province levels and as well as age, direct estimates are unbiased and consistent, and model-based estimates closely align with direct estimates. These comparisons across micro and macro levels demonstrate the reliability of model-based estimates, particularly for detailed disaggregated analyses.

Nationally, more than a quarter of children under five years old were affected by child morbidity, with notable differences between age groups. Children aged 12–23 months experienced considerably higher morbidity rates, roughly double those of children aged 24 months and older. Significant provincial disparities were observed, with the lowest rates in Central Province and the highest in Luapula Province. At the district level, the variation was even more pronounced, with some districts, such as Mumbwa in Central Province, reporting very low rates, while others, like Chipili in Luapula Province, faced exceptionally high levels of morbidity. Districts such as Lunga, Milenge, and Nsama reported alarmingly high rates (more than half) among infants, while Kalomo and Lusaka recorded particularly elevated rates (nearly three-fifths) for children aged 12–23 months. These findings underscore the critical need for region-specific interventions to address the varying challenges. In contrast, districts like Kalumbila in North-Western Province had relatively lower morbidity rates, highlighting the uneven distribution of child health outcomes across region.

While survey data such as the ZDHS may indicate provinces with less child morbidity such as urban provinces, it often fails to provide district-level insights. This is particularly concerning given Zambia’s high urbanisation rate (40.0%), where the urban poor live in overcrowded slums with limited access to healthcare and sanitation, leading to adverse health outcomes. Economically disadvantaged populations may also struggle to afford the high costs living and health care associated with cities [17].

The discrepancies in morbidity prevalence across districts and provinces highlight the socioeconomic inequities in healthcare access and utilisation. Notably, some districts with higher morbidity rates are located within provinces generally perceived to have better health indicators. For example, Livingstone, Zambia’s tourist capital, has higher morbidity than expected. Similarly, Kalomo, despite receiving extensive maternal and child health interventions from both the government and cooperating partners, also continues to experience high under five morbidity rates [7].

The study highlights the geographic concentration of child morbidity in districts within Luapula, Western, and Northern provinces. Notably, Mongu, the provincial capital, exhibits higher morbidity rates compared to more remote districts within the same province. Strikingly, some districts in close proximity, such as Mumbwa and Nkeyema or Chibombo and Kapiri Mposhi, display starkly different morbidity levels, underscoring significant local disparities. This finding emphasises the heightened vulnerability of children aged 12-23 months, especially in these regions, suggesting the urgent need for targeted interventions focused on these age groups and areas with high morbidity. Urban districts like Lusaka also displayed concerning morbidity levels, reflecting the persistent socioeconomic inequities in access to healthcare services even in urban settings.

The findings highlight a critical gap in current health practices, particularly the emphasis on in-patient care over preventive, community-based healthcare. The high prevalence of preventable health conditions — such as fever, diarrhoea, and acute respiratory infections (ARI)— among children under five years of age show the need for a shift in focus towards addressing both clinical and social determinants of health. The study highlights the need for a holistic approach to child health, integrating community-level interventions that target both clinical care and the broader social and environmental factors influencing child health [24]. This approach calls for policy interventions tailored to specific regions and age groups with high morbidity, addressing underlying cause of health disparities across Zambia [7, 17]. Integrated Management of Childhood Illnesses (IMCI), the Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI), and Child Health Weeks remain critical platforms for addressing childhood morbidity. The results of this study can be instrumental in guiding these programmes to areas of greatest need. For instance, high-morbidity districts such as Chipili, Milenge and Mongu could be prioritised for enhanced IMCI training, commodity distribution, and targeted outreach during Child Health Weeks. The study also highlights the importance of integrating SAE into district level planning and resource allocation. By integrating these findings, District Health Management Teams (DHMTs) can tailor morbidity responses to the specific morbidity profiles of their populations. This calls for greater investment in local capacity to analyse and act on disaggregated data, ensuring that policy and programme decisions are evidence-informed and equity-driven [18, 19].

Limitations

Defining the child morbidity through aggregation of various morbidity conditions (like fever, diarrhoea and ARI) may obscure which specific conditions disproportionately affect certain districts. Furthermore, district-level covariates—such as maternal education, female autonomy, and wealth—widely recognized as key determinants of child morbidity and mortality [20], were not included in the developed model due to a mismatch between the administrative structures in the survey and census data. Additionally, the study made use of data from the 2010 Zambian Census, as the 2022 census data is publicly unavailable. This posed challenges due to the increase in the number of districts from 74 to 115, although the provincial boundaries remained unchanged. To address this, the survey clusters’ geolocations were matched with the updated district boundaries to identify the new districts. However, a limitation remains due to a 5-10 kilometre misalignment in the locations of some clusters.

Despite its limitations, this study makes significant contributions in advancing the understanding of child morbidity in Zambia. By using detailed cross-classified multilevel models, it provides reliable and disaggregated morbidity estimates at district level, addressing key gaps in conventional survey methods. The spatial distribution of child morbidity at the detailed level will enhance the practical utility of the findings, offering policymakers a clear visual tool to guide resource allocation and targeted interventions. Importantly, the study highlights age-specific and regional disparities, providing actionable insights for addressing health inequities. While some challenges remain — such as the lack of recent census data and the exclusion of district-level covariates, the methodological rigour of this study and focus on disaggregated data offer a robust foundation for designing region-specific policies aimed at improving child health outcomes.

Conclusion

The study reveals that child morbidity remains alarmingly high at the national level, with substantial variation across provinces and districts, and some districts reporting estimates that significantly exceed the national average. Notably, children aged 12–23 months are identified as the most vulnerable group across all administrative units, highlighting the need for targeted interventions for this age group. The findings also reveal heightened vulnerability among children in Luapula Province, where disease prevalence and undernutrition are particularly severe, and in Western Province, where poverty exacerbates poor health outcomes, stand out as areas requiring urgent attention. These areas stand out as requiring urgent attention, consistent with findings from prior studies [33].

The application of small area estimation method uncovers these variations in child morbidity across provinces and districts, showing areas with higher rates of morbidity and developmental challenges. Such disaggregated data is invaluable for health planners, policymakers, and healthcare providers, enabling more effective resource allocation and the design of interventions tailored to the specific needs of each district. By highlighting these disparities, the study facilitates the development of targeted strategies to address underlying causes of child morbidity and reduce inequities in child health outcomes. Additionally, understanding the geographic distribution of child morbidity offers critical insights into the spatial dynamics of health disparities, providing a foundation for more equitable health planning and resource allocation in Zambia. The SAE methodology employed in this study is transferable to other low-resource settings where national surveys, such as Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), are available but routine data systems lack subnational granularity. By combining survey data with geospatial location of surveyed clusters, small area estimation can generate locally relevant insights to guide targeted child health interventions, especially in contexts marked by geographic and socioeconomic disparities. As such, this approach holds substantial potential for supporting evidence-based planning and resource allocation in similar settings across sub-Saharan Africa and beyond.
