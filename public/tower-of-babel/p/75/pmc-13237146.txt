Biomarker, treatment patterns, and survival differences in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer by race in the United States
Authors: Pegah Farrokhi, Leah Park, Howard Weston Schmutz, Samantha L Thompson, Clara Lam, Julian Bryan, David D Stenehjem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) disproportionately affects Black women, with higher incidence rates and worse outcomes compared with White women. This study included 670 adult patients with metastatic TNBC diagnosed between January 2017 and March 2022 from the Flatiron Clinico-Genomic database. All patients underwent NGS-based testing and were assessed for prevalence of BRCA1/2, PIK3CA, AKT1, and PTEN alterations; treatment patterns; and survival outcomes. Of 670 patients, 28% were Black and 72% White. Black patients had higher BRCA1/2 alteration prevalence (12% vs. 7%; p < 0.04) but similar rates of other mutations. First-line therapy rates were comparable between groups. No significant differences in overall survival were observed (median 13.7 months for Black patients vs. 18.9 months for White patients; p = 0.15). Black patients with metastatic TNBC were nearly twice as likely to have BRCA1/2 alterations, suggesting potential benefit from PARP inhibition. Although survival differences were not statistically significant, larger studies are needed to understand racial disparities in treatments and outcomes.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), defined by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2 expression, comprises 15–20% of breast cancer cases and often presents with aggressive clinical behavior1,2. The lack of targetable receptors makes TNBC unresponsive to hormonal and conventional HER2-directed therapies, resulting in the lowest survival rates among breast cancer subtypes1.

Notably, several studies have reported significant racial differences in the incidence and outcomes of TNBC in the United States3–5. The incidence of TNBC among Black women is two to three times greater than that observed in White women2,3. Furthermore, Black women experience a disproportionately higher mortality rate, with TNBC-related deaths ~17% higher in this population6–8. Several factors may contribute to this disparity, including biological differences and access to healthcare3,6,9,10. At the time of diagnosis, Black patients are characterized by younger age and more aggressive tumor features, including advanced staging and less differentiated histology3,9,10. Systemic barriers further exacerbate these disparities, as Black Patients often have lower socioeconomic status (SES), inadequate insurance coverage, and lower screening rate6.

The emergence of comprehensive genomic profiling, particularly next-generation sequencing (NGS), offers a novel therapeutic approach in TNBC by tailoring treatment based on genetic alterations, including breast cancer susceptibility gene 1/2 (BRCA1/2) mutations, in addition to programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression profiling by immunohistochemistry (IHC)11,12. Studies have shown racial differences in the prevalence of TNBC biomarkers, including variations in germline BRCA mutation rates between Black and White patients1,9. These genomic differences may have important clinical implications as they could influence treatment response to therapies such as poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and immunotherapies, affecting overall outcomes12,13.

Recent research demonstrates that Black patients have reduced access to genomic profiling and targeted therapy options compared with White patients, despite ongoing advances in biomarker testing and precision medicine13,14. This difference in utilization may adversely impact clinical outcomes and further widen existing health disparities12–15.

Although existing literature has demonstrated racial inequities in TNBC care and management from diagnosis to treatment, research gaps persist in our understanding of how genomic heterogeneity, social determinants of health, treatment approaches, and clinical outcomes intersect among different racially diverse populations in the precision oncology era. Our study aims to address these limitations by conducting a comprehensive analysis of biomarker prevalence, treatment utilization patterns, and survival outcomes in a large, geographically diverse cohort of mTNBC patients who received somatic NGS-based molecular profiling in the United States. By systematically examining both biological variations and potential healthcare delivery barriers, this research aims to identify modifiable factors contributing to outcome disparities. Understanding these interconnected disparities is essential for developing strategies that can improve treatment selection and enhance survival outcomes for patients irrespective of race.

Results

Patient and disease characteristics of mTNBC

The study identified 670 patients with mTNBC, comprising 185 (28%) Black patients and 485 (72%) White patients (Fig. S1). Among all patients with mBC in the database during the study period, 23% of Black patients were diagnosed with mTNBC compared with 11% of White patients, resulting in a prevalence ratio of 2.1. Black patients were younger than White patients at metastatic diagnosis (median age 59.0 years, vs. 64.0 years, p < 0.01). However, menopausal status was comparable between groups, with similar proportions diagnosed during pre-menopause (Black: 22%, White: 18%, p = 0.27) and post-menopause (Black: 36%, White: 42%, p = 0.16). ECOG performance status was similarly distributed between the groups, with comparable proportions of patients having an ECOG score ≤ 1 (Black: 61%, White: 64%, p = 0.15). Socioeconomic disparities were evident, with more Black patients in the lowest SES category (30% vs 9%, p < 0.01) and fewer in the highest SES category (2% vs. 16%, p < 0.01). Practice settings were similar, with 81% Black patients and 76% White patients receiving care in community oncology settings (p = 0.2). Insurance coverage varied by race, with Black patients more frequently covered by Medicaid (6% vs 4%, p < 0.01) and less frequently covered by Medicare than White patients (35% vs 48%, p < 0.01). Histologically, Black patients presented less commonly with invasive lobular carcinoma than White patients ( ≤ 5% vs. 2%, p = 0.06). Grade 3 tumors occurred at similar rates (Black: 58%, White: 55%, p = 0.45). No significant differences were observed in the proportions of Black or White patients with stage 3 (31% vs. 25%, p = 0.10) or stage 4 disease (31% vs. 26%, p = 0.23) (Table 1).

Biomarker testing rates by patient race

Among the 670 patients with mTNBC, Black patients were more likely to present with somatic/germline BRCA1/2 mutations compared with White patients (12% vs. 7%, p = 0.04). No significant racial differences were detected in the prevalence of PIK3CA (19% vs. 22%, p = 0.50), PTEN (15% vs. 17%, p = 0.40), or AKT1 (2% vs. 5%, p = 0.13) alterations. PD-L1 CPS test results were limited in the dataset, and were available for around 15% of patients, preventing a reliable determination of differences between groups (Fig. 1, Table S1). Regarding the timing of NGS testing, 60% of Black patients and 53% of White patients received NGS testing after initiating first-line treatment (p = 0.40). The time interval between mTNBC diagnosis and the first NGS test was comparable between Black and White patients (2.1 vs. 1.8 months, p = 0.09). Similarly, the median time from the NGS test to first-line treatment initiation showed no significant difference between racial groups (0.4 vs. 0.5 months, p = 0.08).

Treatment patterns

A total of 530 patients received first-line (1 L) treatment: 81% of Black patients and 79% of White patients. There were no significant differences observed for utilization of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor-based regimens between Black and White patients (19% vs. 27%; p = 0.08) and chemotherapy usage (70% vs. 63%; p = 0.09, Table 2) in 1 L. A total of 328 patients received second-line (2 L) therapy, consisting of 64% Black and 61% White patients. There were no significant differences in 2 L treatments received by Black and White patients, with most patients receiving chemotherapy (69% vs. 58%, p = 0.07) and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor-based regimens (18% vs. 24%, p = 0.44), for Black and White patients, respectively. PARP inhibitors use in 1 L ( ≤ 3% vs. 2%, p = 0.9) and 2 L ( ≤ 2% vs. 5%, p = 0.3) was not significantly different between Black and White patients. We examined the use of PARP inhibitors in patients with BRCA mutations prior to 1 L and 2 L treatment; however, the sample size was insufficient to support meaningful analysis or reporting. Survival outcomes

No significant differences were observed in rwOS between Black and White patients (median 13.7 months [95% CI: 10.9–16.7] vs. 18.9 months [95% CI: 16.4–20.4], p = 0.15) (Fig. 2) in rwPFS (median 4.4 months [95% CI: 3.5–5.6] vs. 5.4 months [95% CI: 4.9–6.2], p = 0.12, Table 3, Fig. 2) or in DOT (median 4.0 months [95%CI: 3.1–4.5] vs. 4.5 months [95% CI: 3.8–5.1], p = 0.14, Table 3, Fig. 2). The rwOS findings persisted in multivariable Cox regression analysis, after adjusting for baseline characteristics, treatments, and biomarkers. Black race was not associated with reduced OS compared with White patients (HR 1.19 [95% CI: 0.94–1.51], p = 0.14). Medicare insurance was associated with reduced mortality (HR 0.71 [95% CI: 0.56–0.9], p < 0.01, Table 4, ECOG performance status, with ECOG > 2 (HR 1.88 [95% CI: 1.33–2.66], p < 0.01) and ECOG 1 (HR 1.33 [95% CI: 1.04–1.70], p = 0.02) associated with increased mortality compared with ECOG 0 (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides important insights into racial differences between Black and White patients with mTNBC. Our findings demonstrate statistically similar testing and treatment patterns between Black and White patients, though several important differences were identified, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to promote health equity. In our study, the prevalence of mTNBC among Black patients was roughly double that observed in White patients. This pattern is consistent with previous real-world evidence, which reported a prevalence of 23% in African American patients compared with 12% in White patients, corresponding to a ratio of 1.9216.

Black patients are diagnosed at a significantly younger age with a trend toward more aggressive disease at diagnosis than White patients. This finding aligned with previous research showing Black patients present with higher-grade disease and have worse clinical outcomes15. Recent molecular studies have identified distinct DNA methylation patterns and microRNA profiles in TNBC samples from Black patients, associated with more aggressive disease, increased activation of pro-metastatic pathways, and contributing to lower survival among patients17,18.

In addition to biological differences, we found disparities in SES and insurance status, which suggest important confounders and complex interactions with patient race in the US. Black patients were disproportionately represented in the lowest SES category. Consistent with our findings, results of several studies demonstrated that patients with low SES or who reside in areas with lower rates of mammogram access are often diagnosed with more aggressive disease at later stages19–21. Additionally, disparities in physical activity, access to healthy food, and lack of safe neighborhoods negatively affect Black women and may contribute to aggressive TNBC biology5.

The timing and utilization of NGS showed comparable patterns between racial groups, with similar intervals between diagnosis and first NGS test and from testing to first-line treatment initiation. However, about 60% of Black patients and 53% of White patients received NGS testing only after initiating first-line treatment. This timing of testing pattern reflects clinical practice during the study period, in which established first-line therapies, often selected without consideration of molecular profiling, are prioritized; and comprehensive molecular testing was typically reserved to inform subsequent treatment decisions upon disease progression. As targeted therapy options continue to expand, determining the optimal timing of comprehensive genomic profiling remains an important area for future consideration.

In terms of racial differences in genomic alterations, Black patients showed a significantly higher prevalence of BRCA1/2 mutations compared with White patients. This difference is consistent with other research findings that reported higher BRCA mutation rates in Black patients22,23. Among the overall patient population, no significant difference in PARP inhibitor use was observed between Black and White patients, even though the prevalence of BRCA1/2 mutations was higher among Black patients. Due to the limited number of patients with BRCA1/2 mutations who received PARP inhibitors, we were unable to determine whether disparities exist in PARP inhibitor use within this subgroup. However, the disconnect between higher BRCA1/2 prevalence and similar PARP inhibitor utilization warrants examination of potential barriers, including: delayed genomic testing and potential missed opportunities for early targeted therapy14,24; structural barriers such as insurance coverage and out-of-pocket costs, which may disproportionately affect Black patients who have higher proportion of lower SES25–27 and provider-level factors, such as varying familiarity with PARP inhibitor eligibility criteria and potential implicit biases28. This observation carries important clinical implications, emphasizing the need for equitable access to BRCA testing, genetic counseling, and ensuring access to PARP inhibitor therapy within this population.

Other mutation rates were similar between racial groups, including PIK3CA, PTEN, and AKT1. Our findings were consistent with some studies that show non-significant differences in somatic mutation among TNBC patients, with a lower rate of PIK3CA mutation in Black patients29–32.

PD-L1 positivity rates were similar across patients with available CPS test results, and there were no significant differences in anti-PD-1/PD-L1 targeted therapy utilization or chemotherapy use between Black and White patients. However, we could not specifically evaluate PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor use in patients with positive CPS scores as PD-L1 expression data were not available for all patients in the database. Thus, our data on use of PD-1/PD-L1 regardless of PD-L1 level of expression needs to be interpreted in that context. Notably, a recent analysis found no significant differences in overall survival among mTNBC patients treated with immunotherapy, underscoring the importance of ensuring equitable access to targeted therapies for all patients13.

Despite the importance of genetic testing for TNBC treatment, it is reported that Black patients often face systemic barriers to counseling and testing. Studies demonstrated Black women are nearly 50% less likely than White women to undergo genetic testing due to healthcare provider biases, distrust of the healthcare system, logistic barriers, and lack of insurance coverage14,24,28,33. Our study included only patients who underwent somatic NGS molecular testing; therefore, we were unable to evaluate the factors influencing the likelihood of receiving such testing. Future studies should evaluate the evolving landscape of biomarker testing across different racial groups to ensure that all eligible patients are identified and able to receive the most appropriate targeted therapies.

Our study observed no significant difference in rwOS between Black and White patients, consistent with findings from recent real-world studies that have likewise reported comparable survival outcomes across these racial groups16,34. However, the 5.2-month numerical difference represents a clinically important gap that suggests Black patients may still experience a clinically meaningful worse survival. The hazard ratio of 1.19 (95% CI: 0.94–1.51) aligns with prior studies documenting approximately 20% increased mortality risk among Black patients with TNBC and is consistent with results showing that biological aggressiveness and systemic barriers continue to negatively affect outcomes of Black patients.8,35–37. Notably, many of these previous studies enrolled both patients with early-stage TNBC and those with mTNBC, and included data from earlier periods than our present analysis. The discrepancies observed between our findings and earlier reports may also be partially explained by differences in study populations, including the narrower timeframe, the exclusive inclusion of patients with NGS results in our cohort, and the comparatively smaller sample size of our study, which made it difficult to detect survival differences due to lower statistical power 13,16,34.

Recent research has identified several factors potentially contributing to survival disparities. The financial burden of treatment is a particular concern in cancer treatment. Studies demonstrated that financial toxicity and increasing out-of-pocket costs, leading to lower access to healthcare, delay in diagnosis, and treatment adherence, all contribute to inferior survival outcomes38,39. This financial strain is associated with an increased risk of delay in treatment initiation, treatment discontinuation, or receiving guideline-recommended treatment, and a higher mortality rate25,26, highlighting the urgent need for policy interventions to address treatment affordability and accessibility.

This research highlights the need for interventions to address treatment and survival disparities by providing more affordable and accessible care for patients with mTNBC. Addressing these inequities requires multifaceted approaches targeting systemic barriers, enhancing provider education, implementing policy changes to reduce financial burdens, increasing insurance coverage, and expanding genomic testing for patients in lower SES.

Our study has several limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, our analysis included only patients who received somatic NGS testing, potentially affecting generalizability and underestimating disparities in the broader population with mTNBC. Although patients with HER2-low status could not be identified at the time of this study, it is important to note that in current clinical practice, individuals with HER2-low status may receive treatment approaches that differ from those used for mTNBC. Another limitation of this study is the inability to distinguish between somatic and germline BRCA1/2 mutations within the dataset. This limitation prevents assessment of hereditary cancer risk and genetic counseling needs. Given that Black patients had nearly twice the BRCA1/2 mutation prevalence, understanding this distinction is critical for family risk assessment and cancer prevention strategies that could not be fully explored in our analysis. The sample size for Black patients was relatively small in certain subgroups, such as those defined by biomarker prevalence and treatment patterns, warranting caution in these data interpretation. Nevertheless, the overall study included a large, nationally representative cohort drawn from more than 280 academic and community oncology practices across the United States. This approach supports a sizable overall sample and enhances the generalizability of the findings within the population studied.

Additionally, PD-L1 CPS test results were insufficiently captured for most of patients, limiting our ability to fully assess biomarker-driven treatment decisions. The use of CGDB database may introduce survival bias, as patients must survive long enough to undergo testing, and could result in immortal time bias between diagnosis and testing. Thus, we tested left truncation adjustments methods to account for delayed study entry. The findings were similar regardless of whether adjustments for survival bias were applied, with no notable differences identified. However, p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons, so findings should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, we lacked comprehensive data on treatment adherence patterns, patient preferences and decision-making processes, financial barriers, social support systems, and care coordination. Despite these limitations, linking detailed EHR data with comprehensive NGS reports allows in-depth evaluation of clinical, sociodemographic, and genomic variables.

This US study of patients with mTNBC who underwent NGS found that Black patients had a higher prevalence of somatic/germline BRCA1/2 alterations compared with White patients, indicating that they may derive greater benefit from PARP inhibitors. While survival analysis suggested a trend toward shorter overall survival among Black patients, these differences did not reach statistical significance. These findings highlight the importance of ensuring equitable access to comprehensive genomic testing and targeted therapies. Future research with larger, more diverse cohorts and systematic evaluation of testing disparities is essential to fully characterize and address differences in mTNBC care and outcomes.

Methods

Study design and data source

This research used the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine Clinico-Genomic database (FH-FMI CGDB). The CGDB encompasses nationwide, deidentified information from more than 280 US oncology practices, mainly community cancer centers (about 75%). Clinical information from the Flatiron Health Network was deterministically matched to genomic data generated through comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) using the FH-FMI CGDB. Genomic alterations were detected through CGP of over 300 cancer-associated genes utilizing FMI’s next-generation sequencing assays (FoundationOne® CDx or FoundationOne® Liquid CDx)40–42. The study period was from January 2017 through September 2022. Race information in the FH-FMI CGDB was collected through self-reporting and extracted from electronic health records (EHRs). SES was measured using Flatiron’s SES Index, which analyzes seven neighborhood-level socioeconomic variables from the American Community Survey (2015-2019). This index is based on patients’ most recent residential addresses at the census block group level and ranks areas in quintiles, with 1 representing the lowest socioeconomic areas and 5 representing the highest. The database contains research-quality specimens, genomic, demographic, and biomarker information from Foundation Medicine’s clinical testing. All analyzed patients had comprehensive genomic profiling available through somatic NGS-based tissue testing and/or liquid biopsy. We identified alteration status using pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. BRCA1/2 findings were available, though without differentiation between somatic and germline mutations. While PD-L1 IHC expression wasn’t included in the NGS panel, these data were extracted from physician notes in the linked EHR. PD-L1 positivity was defined as a combined positive score (CPS) ≥10. The University of Utah Institutional Review Board (IRB) granted exempt status to this study after review. Given the retrospective design and use of de-identified data, the IRB exempted the study from requiring informed consent. All procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable guidelines and regulations.

Patient eligibility

The study population included adult patients ( ≥18 years) with a primary diagnosis of breast cancer who had a recorded diagnosis date for metastatic breast cancer (mBC) with hormone receptor-negative (HR-) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2-) status between January 01, 2017, and March 31, 2022. The data cutoff date was September 2022, allowing for a minimum potential follow-up of 6 months from the index date (defined as the date of first mBC diagnosis).

Exclusion criteria excluded patients who participated in clinical trials during the study period and patients with missing data for race or HR/HER2 status Patient monitoring continued from the index date until death, last visit, or study conclusion, whichever occurred first.

For HER2- classification, patients were either IHC negative (0–1 + ) or IHC equivocal (2 + ) with fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) negative/not amplified results. At the time of the study, the database did not distinguish between IHC 0 and IHC 1, limiting the ability to identify patients with HER2 low status. The study excluded patients with any indicators of HER2+ disease during the study period, such as IHC positive (3 + ), FISH positive/amplified, positive not otherwise specified (NOS), or receipt of HER2-targeted therapy (except trastuzumab deruxtecan [T-DXd]). HR negative status was characterized by estrogen and progesterone receptor negativity.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic biomarker alterations and timing of tumor biomarker testing relative to mBC diagnosis, stratified by race. Secondary outcomes included characterization of demographic and clinical characteristics at baseline, treatment patterns across available therapy lines by race, and assessment of clinical outcomes by race.

These clinical outcomes were evaluated from the index date by line of therapy (LOT) until death or last follow-up. Start of a LOT was defined as the treatment regimen initiated following a mBC diagnosis. The subsequent LOT was initiated when a new treatment was added or when the existing therapy was changed to a different one. Real-world overall survival (rwOS) was measured from index date until death, with surviving patients censored at their last observation date in the Flatiron database. Real-world progression-free survival (rwPFS) was defined as the time from the treatment initiation until progression or death. Disease progression was determined based on the treating physician’s clinical notes, indicating a clear event of tumor growth or worsening disease. For patients without documented progression, the censor date was the last clinical note in the patient’s chart, representing the final point at which progression could have been assessed before data abstraction. Duration of therapy (DOT) represented the interval from treatment initiation to the last non-canceled order or administration, including the last date of oral therapy regimens. Treatment discontinuation was determined when patients initiated subsequent therapy, died (considered discontinued at their last administration/order date), or had >90 days gap between last administration and last known activity. Patients not meeting these criteria were censored at their last administration or order date.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics of mBC patients were described using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were summarized with means, standard deviations, medians and interquartile ranges, and presented in tables and figures. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Comparisons between Black and White patients were conducted using appropriate statistical tests (e.g. Chi-square, Fisher exact).

Time-to-event outcomes (rwOS, rwPFS and DOT) were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate medians and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR). Multivariable models adjusted for baseline demographic and clinical variables, incorporating factors with a univariate association at p < 0.1. Backward selection was used to retain variables with p < 0.05 in the final model. All potential interaction terms were assessed, and those found to be statistically significant were included. Two-sided tests were used, with significance set at p ≤ 0.05.
