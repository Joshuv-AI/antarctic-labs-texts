Variation in type IV pilus stability modulates DNA uptake and biofilm formation
Authors: Yafan Yu, Rabab Mahdi, Ahmad Al-Hilfy Leon, Nam Vo, Reese Lofgren, Jean Luc Mutabazi, Kurt H. Piepenbrink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Type IV pili (T4P) are helical filaments composed of protein subunits, which are produced by numerous taxa of bacteria, including Acinetobacter. T4P are extended out from the cell by extension of enzyme complexes, which extract subunits from the membrane and insert them into the base of the filament, but can also be retracted by reverse rotation catalyzed by a retraction enzyme. T4P have diverse functions, including twitching motility and DNA uptake, which require retraction, and host adhesion and bacterial aggregation, which do not require retraction. Acinetobacter bacteria, including International Clone I (IC-I) and International Clone II (IC-II) strains, show variable phenotypes in assays of T4P-dependent functions. Here, we show that this variation is the result of differentiation of T4P subtypes in Acinetobacter, which we defined based on the sequence of the major subunit, PilA. These subtypes show variable efficiency in pilus retraction between pilus subtypes, and from that, a differential balance between retraction-dependent and retraction-independent functions. In both naturally occurring pilA variants from the IC-I and IC-II groups and isogenic strains complemented with IC-I or IC-II pilA, the IC-I pilus subtype promotes greater twitching motility and DNA uptake, whereas the IC-II pilus subtype promotes biofilm formation while showing reduced capacity for DNA uptake and twitching motility, similar to a retraction-deficient mutant and consistent with the hypothesis that pilus retraction of the IC-II pilus is naturally deficient. This defect in retraction was sufficient to increase the level of piliation on the cell surface when we compared the yields of T4P sheared from the cell surface from IC-I pilA and IC-II pilA complements in an isogenic background. Complementation with IC-II pilA results in greater levels of surface PilA per cell than equivalent complementation with an IC-I pilA gene. In addition, direct comparisons of pilus stability between T4P isolated from IC-I pilA and IC-II pilA complements show greater thermostability for the IC-II pili, supporting the hypothesis that pilus stability can impede retraction and increase piliation.

Type IV filaments, encompassing type IV pili (T4P) and related fibers, Tad/Flp pili, and competence (com) pili, are found in a wide variety of bacterial taxa and show significant variation in both structure and function (1, 2, 3, 4, 5). However all these fibers are united by similarities in structure and function; they are assembled and extended by polymerizing protein subunits into a helical, extracellular assembly; they are adherent to DNA or other biomolecules; and they all appear to be retractile; that is, they can be depolymerized with the subunits returning to the membrane, shortening the fiber and potentially reeling in attached biomolecular cargo.

The divisions between com pili, T4P, and Tad/Flp pili are based on structural differences between the subunits (4), but clear functional differences also exist; all com pilus systems mediate DNA uptake but may have no other function, whereas T4P have been shown to promote biofilm formation or bacterial aggregation (6, 7), subsurface motility (through twitching) (8, 9), host–cell adhesion (10, 11, 12), and DNA uptake (13, 14), with some systems showing all four functions (6, 8, 15, 16). T4P also have historically been divided into multiple subtypes; type IVa and type IVb, with many recent publications categorizing Tad/Flp pili as within T4P as type IVc pili (17, 18). Some species produce multiple T4P systems with distinct functions. As an example, the Vibrio cholerae genome contains three sets of T4P genes, producing Chitin-Regulated T4Pa pili, mannose-sensitive hemagglutinin T4Pa pili, and the T4Pb toxin–coregulated pili (19, 20, 21), each with a distinct function, com for Chitin-Regulated pili, host attachment for mannose-sensitive hemagglutinin pili, and bacterial aggregation for the T4Pb toxin–coregulated pili. The consequences of naturally occurring variation within T4P systems is less well understood.

Variation in T4P subunits could result from diversifying selection with evolutionary pressure being applied by the host adaptive immune system, both antibodies and class II major histocompatibility complex molecules, as well as phage virions that recognize T4P (22, 23, 24, 25, 26). This variation includes the glycosylation of the major pilin subunit in both Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas (22, 23, 27). In addition to variation in glycosylation, natural variation in the polypeptide sequence of the major pilin of Pseudomonas aeruginosa appears to limit the host ranges of T4P-dependent phages (28). Efforts to use the major T4P subunit for vaccine development have shown substantial variation in serology, leading to efforts using subunits from a range of bacterial strains (29) or the creation of chimeric constructs (30).

In the Acinetobacter baumannii–calcoaceticus complex (Acb), nonflagellated, Gram-negative coccobacilli associated with multidrug-resistant infections in health care settings, T4P are involved in several distinct physiological processes (natural com, twitching motility, host–cell adhesion, and biofilm formation) and structurally variable (16, 27). Previously, we found structural divergence in the atomic structures of PilA, the major pilin, from three strains of A. baumannii (15, 27). We resolved the X-ray crystal structures of PilA from three A. baumannii strains, ACICU, AB5075, and BIDMC57. For each of these proteins, the resemblance was closer to a homolog in another species (either P. aeruginosa or Dichelobacter nodosus) than to either of the other two A. baumannii PilA structures (15, 27, 31, 32, 33) (Fig. S1). These three bacterial species, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and D. nodosus, are all members of the family Moraxellaceae and are all opportunistic pathogens of mammals, suggesting that their T4P may be functionally similar.

In an isogenic background, complementation of a pilA-null mutant with these three pilA genes also resulted in distinct phenotypes for T4P-dependent functions, with +pilAACICU (from the International Clone II [IC-II] group) showing very low twitching motility but higher in vitro biofilm formation than +pilAAB5075 (from the International Clone I [IC-I] group) (15). We attributed these phenotypes to differences in surface electrostatics between the two PilA proteins, which we hypothesized would lead to greater bundling of pili between cells for the IC-II T4P. Pilus bundling between cells would nucleate biofilm, as observed for the bundle-forming pilus (34, 35), but would reduce cellular motility by limiting the ability of cells to move independently of each other. However, observations from other bacterial species show that in some cases bundling of pili from the same cell promotes, and may even be essential for, twitching motility (36, 37, 38).

One potential resolution to this conundrum is to consider DNA uptake, another T4P function that requires retraction, the ability to depolymerize the pilus fiber, restoring the pilin subunits to the inner membrane, and correspondingly shortening the pilus. The Acinetobacter T4P system is capable of DNA uptake, the first step in natural com, along with twitching motility, and the induction of biofilm formation and host–cell adherence (Fig. 1). Conceptually, we can divide these four functions into retraction-dependent functions (twitching motility and DNA uptake) and retraction-independent functions (biofilm formation and host–cell adherence), which are purely adhesive and independent of retraction. These differences can be observed in T4P systems through mutations of T4P-associated cytoplasmic AAA + ATPase motors. Extension ATPases (PilB in A. baumannii, Acinetobacter baylyi also expresses TfpB) are required to polymerize the pilins into the pilus fiber, and pilB-null mutants, or cells where PilB is inhibited, show no T4P activity (39, 40). Retraction ATPases (PilT and PilU in A. baumannii) depolymerize the pilus fiber, and pilT-null mutants show no twitching motility or natural com (16), but the pilT mutant is hyperpiliated (16), and we previously found an increase in host–cell adhesion in a pilT mutant (27).Figure 1Retraction-dependent and retraction-independent T4P activities. Here, we compare retraction-dependent T4P functions, DNA uptake, and twitching motility, as well as retraction-independent biofilm formation in our isogenic pilA complement model as well as between naturally occurring IC-I and IC-II A. baumannii strains. We found that IC-II T4P, both in an isogenic background and in naturally occurring A. baumannii strains, show defects in both twitching motility and DNA uptake but show a greater induction of biofilm when compared with their IC-I counterparts, which may be explained by greater surface T4P expression. Both of these features, loss of retraction-dependent T4P functions and gain of biofilm formation, appear to be the consequences of a natural defect in T4P retraction resulting from the IC-II T4P structure.

Results

Differences in surface electrostatics cannot explain differential T4P phenotypes

Previously, we found that in this isogenic background, bacteria with AB5075 (IC-I) T4P showed higher twitching motility and lower biofilm formation than those with ACICU (IC-II) T4P (15). We also found that, based on X-ray crystal structures of PilAACICU and PilAAB5075, the AB5075 pilus would have a more electronegative surface area than its ACICU counterpart. We hypothesized a structure–function relationship where T4P bundling between adjacent cells would promote biofilm formation by nucleating assembly and hinder motility by preventing entangled cells from moving independently. We also proposed that the relatively electronegative AB5075 T4P fibers would be less prone to bundling. However, as the results of functional comparisons show in Figure 2, C and D, neither of these alterations resulted in the high-motility, low-biofilm phenotype seen from PilAAB5075. Complementation with pilAACICU-loop resulted in a pilA-null phenotype for both twitching motility and biofilm formation (Fig. 2), suggesting that the chimeric protein was unable to fold efficiently. The pilAACICU-negative complement produced results identical to pilAACICU in twitching motility (Fig. 2C) and biofilm formation (Fig. 2D). These results suggest that the difference between the AB5075 and ACICU T4P phenotypes is not a product of differential surface electrostatics but some other differential aspects of the two pilus structures.

In an isogenic background, IC-II T4P show decreased com compared with IC-I T4P

Natural com or natural transformation, the ability to take up, internalize, and replicate extracellular DNA, is directly reliant on DNA uptake, and previously, mutants of the T4P system abrogating either pilus biogenesis (41), pilus retraction (16), or DNA binding (42) have been shown to have defects in natural com. If we attributed the motility defect of the +pilAACICU complement to a defect in T4P retraction, we would expect to see a defect in DNA uptake as well. To compare the relative ability of IC-I and IC-II T4P systems to take up extracellular DNA, we used isogenic chimeric strains, complementing a ΔpilA mutant of a genetically tractable model organism, Acinetobacter nosocomialis M2 (originally described as A. baumannii M2) (16). Each of the constructed strains expresses pilA from strains AB5075 (IC-I) or ACICU (IC-II) using the pUCP20GM plasmid as previously described (15). All strains were grown to the exponential phase and incubated with an mCherry plasmid in broth. Uptake of the plasmid was quantified through red fluorescence for each strain using flow cytometry as described above, with methods adapted from Godeux et al. (43). IC-II strains show defects in retraction-dependent T4P activity

Based on our findings for +pilAACICU and +pilAAB5075 complements in the M2 background, we hypothesized that the structural differences in IC-I and IC-II PilA proteins were responsible for the differential phenotypes for T4P functions. However, a defect in pilus retraction observed in a chimeric +pilAACICU cell could potentially be an artifact not fully representative of naturally occurring strains expressing ACICU-like PilA proteins. The pilA gene is more variable in Acinetobacter than other T4P genes (15, 27), but differences in other genes, including other pilin subunits or pilus biogenesis genes, could also impact T4P phenotypes. Prior studies by other groups have shown differences between IC-I and IC-II A. baumannii strains in surface behavior, particularly twitching motility (44, 45), generally in qualitative assays. To quantitatively compare retraction-dependent functions, twitching motility and DNA uptake, and biofilm formation (which T4P promote without retraction (46)), we used naturally occurring A. baumannii strains expressing PilA protein variants.

Previously, our group divided A. baumannii PilA into three subtypes based on sequence analysis and our resolution of the X-ray crystal structures of PilA from three A. baumannii strains, AB5075 (IC-I), ACICU (IC-II), and BIDMC-57 (fitting into neither of the IC groups and with PilA sequences similar to the model system A. nosocomialis M2 and strain A. baumannii American Type Culture Collection [ATCC] 19606) (15, 27). Although the overall conservation for A. baumannii PilA is quite low (PilAAB5075 and PilAACICU are ∼40% identical), conservation within the three proposed subtypes is over 90% (Fig. S5). To quantitatively compare T4P functions in naturally occurring strains, we obtained eight A. baumannii strains from the ATCC, including two with AB5075-like (IC-I) pilA genes, four with ACICU-like (IC-II) pilA genes, and two with BIDMC-57-like PilA sequences. All four IC-II isolates were defective for retraction, as evidenced by a lack of both DNA uptake and motility. In contrast, the IC-I strains all showed robust twitching motility and DNA uptake. The two isolates from the third group (those with BIDMC-57-like PilA proteins) were nonmotile, but ATCC 15308 showed robust DNA uptake and modest biofilm formation, similar to the IC-I strains. On average, IC-II isolates formed more biofilm than IC-I isolates, but ATCC 49466 showed a defect for biofilm formation, similar to the pilA mutant. We hypothesize that ATCC 49466, along with ATCC 19606, are largely unable to generate T4P under these experimental conditions, as they were negative for all three functions. Because A. baumannii ATCC 15308 and ATCC 19606 have identical pilA sequences, as do ATCC 49466 and ATCC 17961 (Fig. S5), these functional defects must stem from differences in other pilus genes or the regulation of pilus function rather than the T4P themselves. This observation is consistent with negative results for ATCC 19606 twitching motility observed by others (44, 47) despite the fact that PilA in A. baumannii ATCC 19606 and PilA in A. nosocomialis M2 are 92% identical (Fig. S2) and M2 shows twitching motility under identical conditions (Fig. 3C).

To evaluate the impact of retraction deficiency on biofilm formation, we measured static biofilm formation for this panel of nine naturally occurring A. baumannii strains. As expected, IC-II (ACICU-like) strains generally showed enhanced biofilm formation compared with IC-I (AB5075-like) strains (Fig. 4D). Notably, despite belonging to IC-II, A. baumannii ATCC 49466 exhibited reduced biofilm formation (along with poor twitching motility and DNA uptake), likely because of incompatibility with the growth conditions used in these assays, similar to the results described above for A. baumannii ATCC 19606. These findings suggest that biofilm formation does not require T4P retraction, and in fact, retraction deficiency promotes biofilm formation in A. baumannii.

Retraction deficiency induces biofilm formation in A. baumannii

To test the hypothesis that retraction deficiency promotes retraction-independent T4P functions, including biofilm formation, we measured static biofilm formation in A. nosocomialis M2 and A. baumannii AB5075 for the WT strains and pilT mutants (M2 ΔpilT and AB5075 Tn-pilT-). Prior studies in multiple organisms have shown that pilT is required for efficient retraction of T4P, as evidenced by DNA uptake/natural transformation and macroscopic twitching motility (11, 16, 48). Our group previously found that an M2 pilT mutant showed a defect in twitching motility but showed significantly greater adherence to A549 and Detroit 562 monolayers (15). Complementation with IC-II T4P leads to greater surface expression of T4P

If defects in retraction can explain the greater induction of in vitro biofilm formation shown by pilT mutants and IC-II T4P systems, we would expect both to be products of greater surface expression of T4P, either in the number of pili per cell or through longer pili. In a comparison of transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, greater numbers of pili were observed previously in A. nosocomialis for a pilT mutant (16), but unambiguously differentiating T4P and chaperone-usher (CU) pili is difficult at these resolutions because of their similar width, 6 to 8 nm (23).

To compare T4P surface expression between the IC-I and IC-II subtypes, we used growth conditions expected to favor the production of T4P over CU pili, selective precipitation to reduce levels of contaminants, and compared levels of surface PilA by gel electrophoresis and densitometry. Comparisons of A. baumannii AB5075 WT and Tn-pilT- showed greater surface PilA expression for the pilT mutant (Fig. S6). Isolated IC-II T4P show greater thermal stability than IC-I T4P

Previously, our group has used thermal denaturation to observe the stability of the folded state of pilin proteins using circular dichroism (27), but observing the transition from assembled pili into dissembled subunits presents several difficulties for this method. More recently, Chlebek et al. (49) showed that differences in pilus stability can be measured using a combination of thermal denaturation and fluorescence microscopy. Building upon this method, we sheared the surface of A. nosocomialis M2 ΔpilA + pilAAB5075 (IC-I) and + pilAACICU (IC-II) and isolated pili from these distinct structural subtypes as described in the Experimental procedures section. Discussion

The type IV filament structure is unique in its reversible assembly. Unlike CU or sortase pilus systems, type IV pilins can be polymerized into pili and pili depolymerized into pilins through reversed rotations of cytoplasmic ATPase motors (PilB, PilT, and PilU) attached to the same biogenesis machinery (PilC, PilM, PilN, PilO, and PilP). Pilus retraction provides unique benefits for T4P systems by enabling twitching motility and DNA uptake, which, in Acinetobacter, are absolutely dependent upon T4P function. This stands in contrast to retraction-independent T4P adhesive activity, promoting host cell adhesion and biofilm formation, which occur through multiple molecular mechanisms.

Despite the obvious benefits of efficient pilus retraction, data from retraction ATPase mutants indicate that it imposes costs as well. Mutants of pilT in P. aeruginosa were observed previously to be hyperpiliated (46), and Harding et al. (16) were able to observe T4P on the surface of an A. nosocomialis M2 ΔpilT mutant but not the parent strain. To the extent to which T4P act as adhesins, reductions in their number will result in decreased adhesion (and vice versa), and indeed, we show here that Acinetobacter pilT mutants show an induction of biofilm compared with their parent strains.

If pilus retraction provides novel functions for T4P and also restricts adhesive functions, this implies that (i) T4P retraction should be viewed as a scalable quality and that an optimum likely exists between the most extreme possible cases; pili being incapable of retracting from the cell surface or retraction outstripping extension to completely remove T4P from the cell surface and (ii) that the optimum level of retraction is likely to vary depending on environmental conditions and for specific T4P functions.

As we have noted previously, the pilA gene in Acinetobacter is more variable, not only than the pilus processing/extension/retraction machinery (PilBCD, PilMNOP, etc) but also other pilin genes (15, 27). Variation in surface-exposed residues may be driven by diversifying selection (50), but the variation in Acinetobacter PilA is not limited to surface-exposed residues but includes substantial differences in regions likely to impact pilus assembly. Taken together, these features imply that variation in pilA may be a critical mechanism for modulating T4P functions, not only in A. baumannii but also in related species, which are found both in the environment and as opportunistic pathogens and where the major T4P subunit shows similar variation, including P. aeruginosa and D. nodosus. However, we note that P. aeruginosa strains with PilA subunits structurally similar to PilAACICU, PAO1, and PAK are more motile than A. baumannii IC-II strains (51, 52), suggesting that other factors may also impact the balance between extension and retraction.

Previously, Chlebek et al. (49) identified pilus-stabilizing interactions as a key factor in motor-independent, spontaneous retraction, where differences could be observed based on specific single amino acid mutations. The target amino acids, found at the interfaces between pilus subunits, were identified as suppressors of the natural transformation defect of a ΔpilT mutant, and the mutations converted these generally bulky residues to glycines, destabilizing the pilus fiber. These differences were only observed through an increase in spontaneous retraction independent of the retraction ATPase; no differences were observed for single mutations in conventional motor-dependent retraction.

Here, we show that naturally occurring variation in T4P structure leads to significant differences in motor-mediated pilus retraction, with consequences for retraction-dependent and retraction-independent T4P functions. Contrary to our expectations, variation in surface-exposed residues between two T4P subtypes, PilAACICU and PilAAB5075, has no direct impact on the functional differences we observed both in an isogenic background and in the naturally occurring IC-I and IC-II strains. This is evidenced by the commonality of phenotypes between the M2 ΔpilA + pilAACICU, pilAACICU_negative, and naturally occurring IC-II bacteria. Instead, differences between the two are driven by a combination of decreased pilus retraction and increased surface piliation for the IC-II T4P system, both of which could be explained by increased pilus stability creating an energetic barrier to pilus retraction.

Clinical epidemiology also suggests that Acinetobacter isolates vary in T4P-mediated surface behavior based on their origin, with lung isolates showing, in aggregate, greater biofilm formation and blood isolates greater twitching motility (53). We hypothesize that these differences stem from the distribution of T4P subtypes within these populations. In a survey of Acinetobacter infections in Japan, Matsui et al. (54) found that IC-II strains accounted for 43% of A. baumannii respiratory infections but none of the blood infections and only 2% of all other infections. Comparing surface behavior from different A. baumannii isolates, Eijkelkamp et al. (44) characterized only 9% of IC-II isolates as positive for twitching motility, but 100% of IC-I isolates were positive. Conversely, median biofilm formation was found to be approximately 70% greater for IC-II isolates. Skerniškytė et al. (45) also found greater twitching (but not swarming) motility for IC-I strains compared with IC-II and greater desiccation resistance for IC-II strains. Vijayakumar et al. (53) also found that A. baumannii isolates varied considerably in their surface behavior based on the site of isolation. Their results showed that respiratory isolates formed more biofilm but were less motile in twitching assays than blood isolates. We believe that all these results can be explained through differences in pilus retraction similar to those observed here.

Despite the strong association in the literature between T4P and twitching motility (16, 36, 38, 42, 48, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61), the evolution of retraction-deficient T4P systems in Acinetobacter is plausible not only because of the potential advantages for adhesive functions but also because pilus retraction may be more useful in the environment than in a host lifecycle and may carry associated risks. Although mutants of pilA have been found to be less pathogenic in an animal model (62), twitching motility itself appears to be less important than surface motility in vivo (63), and IC-II strains with poor motility are still virulent in murine infections (45). Pilus retraction also carries risks in terms of phage infection, as retraction is an essential step in T4P-mediated phage infection, and pilT mutants are immune (22, 24).

If A. baumannii T4P systems are, in fact, specialized for distinct functions, that specialization must stem from measurable physical differences between some component(s) of pilus itself or the apparatus responsible for pilus extension and retraction. Here, we show that we can reproduce the phenotypic differences found in naturally occurring IC-I and IC-II strains in an isogenic background solely through modifications in pilA. In A. baumannii, there are over a dozen gene products necessary for pilus biogenesis and function, including seven putative pilin subunits, but it is nevertheless unsurprising that PilA, which makes up the vast majority of the pilus, has a controlling role. Because the vast majority of the subunits in a T4P are PilA, interactions between the pilus machinery during extension and retraction are primarily with PilA, and interactions between PilA subunits are the principal stabilizing force of pilus assembly. In Figure 6B, we present a model by which an increase in stabilizing interactions between assembled PilA subunits could create an energetic barrier to retraction, which decreases retraction speed by opposing the force of the PilT motor. We believe this model represents the best current explanation for the phenotypic differences in T4P function, both because, as we have shown here, these differences can be reproduced solely through variation in pilA in an isogenic background, and because complementation with an IC-II pilA gene produces relative defects in both retraction-mediated T4P functions when compared with an IC-I pilA complement.

Taken together, the results here suggest that the evolution of pilus retraction is a double-edged sword, with the addition of new capabilities, twitching motility and DNA uptake, balanced against a reduction in adhesive capability. This balancing act may favor different pilus structures with different levels of potential retraction depending on the conditions in the host or other environment and suggests that variation on the structures of T4P subunits within bacterial species is partially a product of lifecycle adaptation.

Experimental procedures

Strains and plasmids

Details on A. nosocomialis M2 and mutants can be found here (16) (where it was originally identified as A. baumannii M2). A. baumannii AB5075 WT and mutants are from the University of Washington AB5075-UW transposon mutant library maintained by the Manoil group (64). Strains from the IC-I and IC-II groups were provided by the ATCC. Details of complementation by pilA genes from A. baumannii AB5075, ACICU, and BIDMC57 can be found in the study by Ronish et al. (15). The pilA ACICU negative and αβ-loop mutants (sequences available in Table S1) were synthesized by GenScript and ligated into pUCP20GM (46) using BamH1 and HindIII restriction sites. The resulting vectors were electroporated into A. nosocomialis M2 ΔpilA (16) using standard protocols (65). The presence of the plasmids was confirmed by both resistance to gentamycin and PCR of the pilin genes. For plasmid maintenance, strains complemented with pilA::pUCP20GM were grown on solid media supplemented with 50 μg/ml gentamycin before their use in the assays described later.

Twitching motility

Macroscopic twitching motility was assessed using standard protocols (15, 27, 66). Briefly, Acinetobacter strains, including mutants and complemented strains, were grown on 1.5% MacConkey agar plates overnight. Colonies were selected and stabbed through the centers of 1% MacConkey agar plates in polystyrene petri dishes. The plates were incubated in sealed bags at 37 °C for 3 days. The agar was then removed, and the bacteria adhered to the polystyrene Petri dish stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 5 min. Excess crystal violet was removed by gentle washing with deionized water three times. The subsurface twitching area on each plate was accessed by measuring the diameters of the ellipse and then calculating the area (67). Statistics were calculated for three replicates, and significance was determined by the Student’s t test.

Static biofilm formation

Static biofilm formation was quantified using horizontal growth on 6-well plates, with frequent changes of media and quantification through staining with crystal violet. Briefly, Acinetobacter strains, including mutants and complemented strains, were grown in LB no salt broth (tryptone 10 g/l, yeast extract 5 g/l) overnight. The bacteria were inoculated into 6-well plates to have a final absorbance of 0.1 at 600 nm in 5 ml total volume. The 6-well plates were incubated at 37 °C for 3 days (72 h). To remove nonadherent, planktonic bacteria and introduce fresh growth media, the medium was changed every 20 to 24 h using a pipette. After 72 h, 5 ml of PBS was added to and removed from the plates to wash the planktonic bacteria. Five milliliters of 0.1% crystal violet with 2.5% glutaraldehyde was added to the plates and stained for 10 min. Excess crystal violet was removed by gentle washing with PBS. One milliliter of 100% ethanol was then added to the plates to dissolve the dye. Relative biomass was assessed using the final concentration of released crystal violet; the absorbance value was read using a NanoDrop-C at 570 nm.

DNA uptake through flow cytometry

Uptake of extracellular DNA was quantified using flow cytometry to measure the proportion of fluorescent bacterial cells after incubation with a plasmid encoding mCherry. This method is adapted from the study by Godeux et al. (43). Briefly, Acinetobacter strains, including mutants and complemented strains, were grown in LB no salt broth overnight. The cultures were then diluted 1:200 into 200 μl LB no salt broth, and 500 ng of plasmid encoding mCherry was added. The broth was then grown at 37 °C for 4 h. Bacterial suspensions were fixed with formaldehyde (final concentration, 3.7%) for 30 min at room temperature and then washed twice with PBS and further resuspended in 200 μl PBS. Flow cytometry was performed using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX LX. Samples were run at a collection rate of 60 μl/min, the mCherry was excited by the 561 nm laser, and fluorescence emission was detected using a 610/20 band pass filter. Data were acquired using CytExpert software (Beckman Coulter) and further analyzed in FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

Quantifying pilus expression

Pilin proteins were isolated using the methods of Voisin et al. (68) with modifications. Bacteria were spread on 10 LB no salt agar plates and grown at 30 °C overnight. Next day, the bacteria were gently scraped from each plate using a sterile cell scraper and resuspended in a 3 ml buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl and 150 mM NaCl (pH 8.3). The cells were injected through a needle (diameter 18G 1” blunt needle+ 25G 1”) three times to shear off the pili. The cell suspension was then spun down for 5 min at 13,300g at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to new 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged for an additional 25 min for the same speed at 4 °C. For precipitating the sheared pili, 1 M MgCl2 was added to the supernatant to a final concentration of 0.1 M, and the samples were incubated at 4 °C overnight. Samples were further centrifuged for 25 min at 13,300g at 4 °C. Then the pellet was resuspended in 100 μl of buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.3) and stored at 4 °C. Relative PilA surface expression was compared using densitometry by ImageJ from silver-stained SDS-PAGE gels (67); with relative expression calculated by comparing the PilA band to a control band found in both WT and ΔpilA samples.

Measuring pilus stability as a function of temperature

T4P were isolated from A. nosocomialis M2 ΔpilA + pilAAB5075 (IC-I) and A. nosocomialis M2 ΔpilA + pilAACICU (IC-II) as described previously. Resuspended pili were incubated for 1 h at either 25 °C, 45 °C, 55 °C, or 65 °C, fixed by adding glutaraldehyde to a final concentration of 2.5%, pipetted onto 200-mesh carbon formvar–coated copper grids, stained with 1% uranyl acetate, and examined at 80 kV using a Hitachi HT-7800 TEM. The length of intact pili in each field was quantified by tracing pili using the GNU Image Manipulation Program, and this analysis was performed in duplicate independently by K. H. P. and R. M., who reported similar values.

Figures and diagrams

Data figures were created using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Images of the PilAACICU (Protein Data Bank ID: 4XA2) and PilAAB5075 (Protein Data Bank ID: 5VAW) X-ray crystal structures were made using UCSF ChimeraX (69), and the dendrogram of PilA sequences was created using Interactive Tree of Life (70).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software). All significant differences were determined at p < 0.05. Relevant statistical tests used for comparisons are discussed in the figure captions.
