Temporal trends in risk of acute kidney injury among patients with preeclampsia in Canada: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: KS Joseph, Azar Mehrabadi, Amélie Boutin, Sarka Lisonkova, Sid John, Mackenzie Campbell, Victoria Allen, Monica Beaulieu, Emmanuel Bujold, Sarah D McDonald, Susie Dzakpasu, Angela Vinturache, Carmen B Young, Wee-Shian Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Several studies have documented increasing rates of obstetric acute kidney injury (AKI) in Canada, particularly among females with preeclampsia. We aimed to quantify recent patterns in obstetric AKI and explore the cause for the rising rates.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study of all hospital deliveries in Canada (excluding Quebec) between April 2013 and March 2023, using data obtained from the Canadian Institute for Health Information. We estimated temporal changes in rates of AKI and pulmonary edema by province and hospital, and changes in markers of illness severity and gestational age–specific rates of AKI. We quantified temporal trends in AKI rates, adjusted for risk factors, using logistic regression.Results:We identified 2 796 152 deliveries; 1710 patients developed AKI. Rates of AKI increased from 3.92 per 10 000 deliveries in 2013 to 2014 to 7.84 per 10 000 deliveries in 2022 to 2023 among all deliveries, and from 109.9 to 160.1 per 10 000 deliveries among patients with preeclampsia (rate ratio 1.46, 95% confidence interval 1.07 to 1.99). Rates of pulmonary edema remained stable and showed a weak inverse association with rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia. Patients with preeclampsia, with and without a diagnosis of AKI, showed similar temporal changes in markers of illness severity. Rates of AKI increased at all gestational ages. Adjustment for risk factors did not explain the temporal increase in rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia.Interpretation:Rates of obstetric AKI increased substantially in Canada between 2013 and 2023, with spatiotemporal patterns suggestive of an underlying change in preeclampsia management. Detailed clinical investigation is required to definitively explain and address the rising rates of obstetric AKI.

Several recent studies have documented a rising incidence of obstetric acute kidney injury (AKI) in Canada, England, and the United States.1–7 In Canada, rates of obstetric AKI increased from 1.6 per 10 000 deliveries in 2003 to 2004 to 4.4 per 10 000 deliveries in 2012 to 2016,1–4 while in the US, rates increased from 2.4 per 10 000 deliveries in 1999 to 2001 to 6.3 per 10 000 deliveries in 2010 to 2011.5 A study from England documented a rise in rates of obstetric AKI from 2.9 per 10 000 deliveries in 1998 to 2002 to 11.2 per 10 000 deliveries in 2013 to 2017.7

Most of the observed increases in obstetric AKI occurred among females with preeclampsia.3,6 Rates of AKI among females with preeclampsia in Canada (excluding Quebec) increased from 45.5 per 10 000 deliveries in 2003 to 2004 to 109.6 per 10 000 deliveries in 2009 to 2010,3 while these rates increased from 12 per 10 000 deliveries in 1989 to 1994 to 71 per 10 000 deliveries in 2007 to 2012 in Quebec, Canada.6

Increased rates of obstetric AKI over the last 2 to 3 decades may have occurred as a consequence of changes in the management of preeclampsia, specifically changes in oral and intravenous fluid intake that promote a negative fluid balance. Vascular permeability is increased in patients with preeclampsia, and administration of intravenous fluids increases the risk of pulmonary edema.8 This tendency toward pulmonary edema is aggravated by hypoalbuminemia caused by proteinuria, which is a common feature of preeclampsia.8 However, preeclampsia is also marked by intravascular volume constriction, which predisposes patients to develop AKI, and euvolemia is required to prevent and treat AKI.9 These opposing concerns are highlighted in clinical guidelines, which warn that rigid fluid restriction protocols, designed to prevent fluid overload and pulmonary edema, may lead to AKI through hypovolemia and renal hypoperfusion.10–22

Other factors that could explain the temporal increase in rates of obstetric AKI include changes in the clinical criteria for diagnosis of AKI. Given that International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD) codes for AKI have not changed in the last 25 years,3,5 this explanation implies a lowering of the diagnostic threshold for AKI, and a temporal reduction in the severity of cases. Another possibility is that increased rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia may be due to improved management of blood pressure and delivery of patients with preeclampsia at later gestation.23 A related hypothesis implicates the 2014 and 2022 Canadian preeclampsia guidelines, which specified abnormal serum creatinine values that warrant close follow-up and immediate delivery.15,21 Lastly, the observed increase in rates of AKI may have occurred because of temporal changes in the frequency of risk factors for AKI, including increased use of nephrotoxic drugs.

We aimed to assess the evidence supporting these hypotheses by quantifying spatiotemporal and temporal patterns in rates of both AKI and pulmonary edema (to indirectly assess potential changes in fluid management), markers of AKI illness severity (to identify potential changes in the diagnostic threshold for AKI), gestational age–specific rates of AKI (to identify potential changes in the timing of medically indicated delivery), and rates of AKI adjusted for changes in patient characteristics (to identify the influence of changes in risk factors for AKI).

Methods

The study population included deliveries that resulted in a live birth or a stillbirth in all hospitals in Canada, excluding Quebec, between Apr. 1, 2013, and Mar. 31, 2023. We obtained information on these deliveries from the Discharge Abstract Database of the Canadian Institute for Health Information, which includes approximately 98% of all births in Canada, excluding Quebec.24 Records in the database included information on patient characteristics, pregnancy details, and maternal and neonatal diagnoses and procedures. The information was abstracted from medical charts by trained health records personnel using standardized definitions and processes. We obtained information on diagnoses, medical interventions, and procedures from clinician notes in the medical chart that were recorded in the database using the Canadian version of the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10-CA), and the Canadian Classification of Health Interventions codes (Appendix 1, Table S1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251293/tab-related-content).25,26 Validation studies have shown that information in the database is accurate.27–29 A 2009 study estimated the sensitivity and specificity of chronic hypertension to be 83.3% and 99.9%, of preeclampsia to be 75.2% and 99.5%, and of any gestational hypertensive disorder to be 87.9% and 99.6%, respectively.27 A 2016 chart abstraction study showed 93% agreement for 6-character ICD-10-CA codes and 96% agreement for 3-character ICD-10-CA codes for diagnoses that affected treatment or increased length of stay.29

Statistical analysis

We used 2 different approaches to assess temporal changes over the study period, using rates quantified annually. In some analyses we compared 2013 to 2016, 2017 to 2019, and 2020 to 2022, while in others we compared 2013 to 2017 and 2018 to 2022. We used analyses with longer time intervals if the number of patients was relatively small. We first characterized the study population in terms of patient characteristics and risk factors for obstetric AKI. We quantified temporal changes in risk factors by epoch using rate ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We quantified rates of AKI and pulmonary edema by year, and evaluated temporal trends using a χ2 test for linear trend.

We evaluated the hypothesis that temporal increases in AKI were due to changes in fluid management in patients with preeclampsia by comparing between-region and between-hospital rates of AKI and pulmonary edema (we expected to see an inverse relationship between rates of pulmonary edema and AKI if fluid management played a role). We restricted hospital-specific analyses to large hospitals (≥ 1000 patients with preeclampsia). We examined the relationship between pulmonary edema and AKI by calculating the weighted Pearson correlation coefficient using rates of pulmonary edema and AKI among patients with preeclampsia in each province and hospital in 2013 to 2022. We addressed clustering of AKI and pulmonary edema rates by hospital using random effects models. We then used ecological Poisson regression to quantify the pulmonary edema–AKI relationship among patients with preeclampsia, with the number of patients with AKI in each province as the dependent variable (offset by the number of deliveries) and the rate of pulmonary edema as the independent variable.

We evaluated temporal changes in markers of severity of illness of AKI among patients with preeclampsia to determine whether criteria for AKI diagnosis had changed over time. We assessed illness severity based on prolonged hospital stay (≥ 7 d), admission to a special care unit (SCU), and need for dialysis and assisted ventilation in 2013 to 2017 and 2018 to 2022. We quantified temporal changes using odds ratios. We repeated these analyses among females with preeclampsia who did not have a diagnosis of AKI. We assessed homogeneity of the odds ratios expressing differences in the temporal change in markers of illness severity among patients with and without a diagnosis of AKI using the Breslow–Day test with Tarone adjustment.30

We compared gestational age distributions and gestational age–specific rates of AKI in 2018 to 2022 versus 2013 to 2017 to assess whether temporal changes in the management of preeclampsia resulted in more patients delivering at later gestation in recent years.31 We used logistic regression to assess whether changes in risk factors could explain temporal trends in rates of AKI. We stratified the analyses by presence or absence of preeclampsia (since modification of the temporal trend in AKI by preeclampsia has been previously demonstrated)3 and addressed potential clustering of patients with AKI by region and hospital using random effects models. We used SAS and Stata software to conduct the analyses.

Ethics approval

We obtained approval for the study from the ethics review board of the University of British Columbia (H17-00587).

Results

Patient characteristics

The study population included 2 796 152 deliveries; 1710 patients developed AKI (6.12 per 10 000 deliveries). The proportion of patients aged 35 years or older increased from 21.0% in 2013 to 2016 to 26.1% in 2020 to 2022, while the frequency of nulliparity and multifetal pregnancies decreased slightly (Appendix 1, Table S2). Diagnoses of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, pre-existing diabetes mellitus, and connective tissue disorders increased. Pregnancy complications such as sepsis, other puerperal infections, and cardiac failure decreased, while other complications — such as preeclampsia, polyhydramnios, placenta previa, uterine rupture, and postpartum hemorrhage — increased. Rates of labour induction and cesarean delivery also increased from the 2013 to 2016 period to the 2020 to 2022 period (Appendix 1, Table S2).

Rates of AKI and pulmonary edema among all deliveries

Rates of AKI among all deliveries increased from 3.92 per 10 000 deliveries in 2013 to 7.84 per 10 000 deliveries in 2022 (rate ratio 2.00, 95% CI 1.59 to 2.52), while rates of pulmonary edema were stable at 1.94 per 10 000 deliveries in 2013 and 2.30 per 10 000 deliveries in 2022 (rate ratio 1.18, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.71) (Appendix 1, Table S3).

Rates of AKI and pulmonary edema among patients with preeclampsia

The frequency of preeclampsia increased from 1.8% in 2013 to 3.0% in 2022, while the frequency of severe preeclampsia was stable (0.58% in both 2013 and 2022) (Table 1). The frequency of AKI was substantially higher among patients with preeclampsia, and rates increased significantly from 109.9 per 10 000 deliveries in 2013 to 160.1 per 10 000 deliveries in 2022. Rates of AKI among patients without any hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were low but also increased significantly from 2013 to 2022 (Table 1).

Spatiotemporal patterns of AKI and pulmonary edema

Among patients with preeclampsia, rates of AKI were highest in Alberta and lowest in the Atlantic provinces in 2020 to 2022 (Figure 1). Rates of AKI increased significantly in Alberta and Ontario from the 2013 to 2016 period to the 2020 to 2022 period, while rates of pulmonary edema did not show any significant spatiotemporal change, except for a decrease in Saskatchewan (Figure 1). Provincial rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia were nonsignificantly and negatively correlated with rates of pulmonary edema in 2013 to 2022 (weighted correlation coefficient −0.33, p = 0.4; Appendix 1, Figure S1), while hospital-level rates were nonsignificantly and positively correlated (weighted correlation coefficient 0.44, p = 0.09). Ecological Poisson regression analysis showed that provincial rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia in 2013 to 2022 were negatively associated with pulmonary edema rates, with a 1% reduction in rates of AKI per unit (i.e., 1 per 10 000 deliveries) increase in pulmonary edema rates (rate ratio 0.99, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.00).

Temporal changes in severity of AKI

We observed a reduction in rates of prolonged hospital stays and SCU admissions from the 2013 to 2017 period to the 2018 to 2022 period among patients with preeclampsia, both among those with and those without AKI (Figure 3). Nevertheless, rates of prolonged hospital stays (35.6 per 100 deliveries to patients with preeclampsia) and SCU admissions (23.5 per 100 deliveries to patients with preeclampsia) remained high among patients with preeclampsia and AKI in 2018 to 2022. Rates of dialysis showed a nominally significant temporal decrease among patients with preeclampsia who had AKI, while there was no significant change in dialysis rates among those with preeclampsia who did not have AKI. Lastly, rates of assisted ventilation did not change among patients with preeclampsia and AKI but decreased significantly among patients with preeclampsia without AKI. Tests for homogeneity showed that odds ratios expressing temporal changes in the markers of illness severity did not differ significantly between patients with preeclampsia who had AKI and those with preeclampsia who did not have a diagnosis of AKI (Figure 3).

Temporal change expressed using odds ratios (ORs); test for homogeneity of the OR indicates if the magnitude of change in illness severity from 2013 to 2017 to 2018 to 2022 was different among patients with preeclampsia with AKI versus those without AKI.

Temporal changes in gestational age–specific AKI

The frequency of preterm deliveries (< 37 wk) and deliveries at 39 weeks’ gestation or later decreased, while deliveries at 37 to 38 weeks’ gestation increased from the 2013 to 2017 period to the 2018 to 2022 period among patients with preeclampsia and patients with preeclampsia and AKI (Appendix 1, Figure S2 and Figure S3). The pattern among patients without hypertension was slightly different; the frequency of deliveries at 37 to 38 weeks’ gestation increased, deliveries at 39 weeks’ gestation or later decreased, and deliveries at 34 to 36 weeks’ gestation increased slightly. We observed a temporal increase in rates of AKI at all gestational ages from the 2013 to 2017 period to the 2018 to 2022 period among patients with preeclampsia, whereas among patients without hypertension, rates of AKI decreased among deliveries at 34 to 36 weeks’ gestation and those at 37 to 38 weeks’ gestation and increased in all other gestational age categories (Appendix 1, Figure S4).

Rates of AKI adjusted for risk factors

Among all deliveries, adjustment for hypertension subtypes (e.g., chronic hypertension and preeclampsia) attenuated but did not abolish the temporal increase in rates of AKI (Figure 4A). Additional adjustment for other confounders (e.g., patient age, diabetes mellitus, cesarean delivery) did not further alter this temporal trend (Figure 4A and Appendix 1, Table S4). The temporal trend in rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia was mostly unaffected by adjustment for hypertension subtypes (e.g., preeclampsia and eclampsia) and other potential confounders; unadjusted rates of AKI in 2022 were 50.0% (95% CI 0.1% to 124.8%) higher than in 2013, while adjusted rates of AKI were 48.1% (95% CI 6.8% to 105.4%) higher (Figure 4B and Appendix 1, Table S5). However, the temporal trend in rates of AKI among patients without hypertension was attenuated by adjustment for confounders; unadjusted rates increased significantly by 69.9% (95% CI 13.4% to 154.5%) from 2013 to 2022, whereas adjusted rates showed no significant increase (47.6% increase, 95% CI 4.4% decrease to 127.9% increase, p = 0.08) (Figure 4C and Appendix 1, Table S6).

Discussion

We found a progressive increase in rates of obstetric AKI among all deliveries in Canada (excluding Quebec) between April 2013 and March 2023. This increase was associated with a moderate increase in the frequency of preeclampsia, and a substantial increase in rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia. Although the frequency of pulmonary edema did not change from 2013 to 2022, rates of AKI and pulmonary edema were weakly and negatively correlated among patients with preeclampsia across provinces and regions. Markers of severity of AKI among patients with preeclampsia (prolonged hospital stay, SCU admission, and need for dialysis or assisted ventilation) showed similar temporal patterns among those with and without a diagnosis of AKI, suggesting no change in illness severity among females with preeclampsia diagnosed with AKI. Temporal changes in gestational age–specific rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia were not consistent with the hypothesis that better management of hypertension and delayed delivery was responsible for the rising rates of AKI. Finally, the rising temporal pattern in rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia was not attenuated by changes in risk factors for AKI, whereas adjustment for risk factors did attenuate the increase in rates of AKI among females without hypertension.

The similarity of changes in illness severity among patients with preeclampsia who did and did not have AKI suggests that there was no change in the diagnostic threshold for AKI over time, but rather changes in hospital care. The low rates of AKI among patients without hypertension, and the attenuation in the temporal increase in rates of AKI in this subpopulation following adjustment for risk factors (not including nephrotoxic drugs), suggest that the unadjusted increase in rates of AKI among these patients was caused by changes in these risk factors. However, the absence of any attenuation in rates of AKI among patients with preeclampsia following adjustment for risk factors, and the higher rates of AKI during the pandemic years, suggest that changes in preeclampsia management may underlie the increasing rates of AKI.

Preeclampsia guidelines and protocols place varying emphasis on fluid restriction, although optimal management remains uncertain.32 Some guidelines focus on euvolemia as the management goal (e.g., “Fluid balance should aim for euvolemia […] . There is no rationale to ‘run dry’ a pre-eclamptic woman”),33 whereas others emphasize fluid restriction (e.g., “Intravenous and oral fluid intake should be minimized in women with preeclampsia,” 13,21 “Fluid should not be routinely administered to treat oliguria,”21 and “Oliguria should not be treated with fluids and should be tolerated to avoid pulmonary edema”).34 Moreover, the 2014 and 2022 Canadian preeclampsia guidelines stated that an elevated serum creatinine level required close monitoring, while AKI (creatinine > 150 μM/L) was a severe complication that warranted immediate delivery.15,21 These statements could have resulted in some patients being managed expectantly until AKI occurred (i.e., until creatinine exceeded 150 μM/L).

Rising rates of AKI are a cause for concern because of the short-and long-term consequences of renal injury. In our study, patients with preeclampsia and AKI showed substantial illness severity in terms of prolonged hospital stay, SCU admission, and need for dialysis and assisted ventilation; long-term follow-up studies show substantially higher rates of end-stage renal disease among such patients.35–37

Limitations

Our study was limited by its retrospective design and our use of information from a large database of hospitalizations that did not have information on some maternal factors (e.g., body mass index), fluid management (including fluid intake and urine output), laboratory tests (e.g., serum creatinine), and medication use (including nephrotoxic drugs). However, information was available for key diagnoses, such as AKI and pulmonary edema, based on clinician-documented diagnoses in medical charts, which validation studies have shown to be accurate.27–29

Conclusion

Rates of obstetric AKI have increased in Canada for more than 2 decades, especially among patients with preeclampsia. Our study provides preliminary evidence that the rising rates of AKI may be linked to changes in management of preeclampsia; however, in-depth clinical investigation of hospitals with low and high rates of AKI is urgently required to definitively identify and address the causes of the progressive increase in this serious complication of pregnancy.
