Authorship identification for Chinese literature based on a pyramid deep bidirectional gated recurrent unit network with voting strategy
Authors: Bowen Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identifying the author of given textual excerpts is a challenging and significant task in computational linguistics. In this paper, we propose a novel deep-learning model structured based on a Pyramid Deep Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (biGRU) network for authorship identification of Chinese literature. Our model introduces a hierarchical architecture where the output sequence of each GRU layer is progressively downsampled by an optimizable convolutional module before serving as the input for the subsequent layer. To enhance computational efficiency, we implement a parameter-sharing strategy across all layers. Furthermore, we incorporate a multi-level soft-voting mechanism, where hidden features extracted at each layer independently contribute to the final prediction, effectively capturing diverse levels of characteristic abstraction. We evaluate our approach on a Chinese literary dataset comprising over 90 k tokens derived from short, fragmented utterances, where coherent and extensive contextual information is inherently limited. Experimental results demonstrate that our model achieves a superior F1 score of 0.8224 while requiring only approximately 41% of the FLOPs compared to a conventional deep biGRU network of equivalent depth, confirming its effectiveness and efficiency for authorship identification.

Introduction

Authorship identification

There is a great deal of textual information generated, transmitted, and received in our daily lives every day, but meanwhile, anonymous textual content is also increasing1. The technology of identifying the actual author with given anonymous textual data from a set of “suspects”, is often called “authorship identification”2. Recently, authorship identification has become a technology of significant scientific interest because in many fields, it is quite important to figure out the exact authorship of the given texts, such as criminal investigation, forensic evidence, historical philology, etc. For a specific example, the authorship identification technology can be utilized to decide whether a literary work is actually composed by a specific writer, like Shakespeare. It has long been suspected that many plays of Shakespeare were not composed by Shakespeare alone. As for the play Henry VI, Christopher Marlowe is believed to be a co-author of it3. Segarra et al.4 used Markov chains to represent word adjacency networks for entire author canons, and attribute the authorship of Henry VI. Their quantitative analysis corroborated that the play contains measurable amounts of Marlowe’s writing, and these contents are not simply due to literary impersonation. Besides, Boyd and Pennebaker5 discussed the authorship of the play Double Falsehood by creating psychological signatures with multiple textual analytic approaches. They found that the influence of Shakespeare is most predominantly revealed in the early parts of the play, whereas the influence of the potential co-author Fletcher is most apparent in later acts. Most recently, with the rapid development of generative artificial intelligence, researchers like Kumarage6 are also attempting to develop new methods to detect and figure out whether the authorship of a provided discourse is artificial intelligence (AI) or human.

Related work

The authorship identification task can be realized in many approaches, among which probably the most classical one is to analyze the statistical variables of the provided texts. These extracted and analyzed statistical variables can be the word / character / letter frequency7–12, vocabulary richness13–16, punctuation marks13,17,18, part-of-speech patterns19–22, word length15,23,24, errors13, etc. The basic principle of these methods is to calculate the distance measured based on the statistical features between different textual data, and the data with a smaller distance value illustrates that they share a greater similarity, and in other words, it is more possible to consider them to come from the same author. Conversely, if the calculated distance between the two datasets has a larger value, it means that they are more likely to be generated by different authors. Some researchers prefer to utilize more abstract features with the provided textual data. For example, Savoy25 realized authorship attribution with a probabilistic topic model, and Chen et al.26 made a cross-domain analysis of gender and age prediction. Such kind of methods are simple, direct, and somehow indeed effective in realizing the authorship identification task. However, since it is based on statistical features of the textual data, it often requires a large scale of discourse containing adequate words and phrases for convincing statistical indices, which usually requires a long duration for computation and analysis. On the other hand, when the accessible textual data contains only fragmented and uncorrelated clauses instead of complete discourses, these kinds of methods will fail to pick up any statistical features, because the indices like the word frequency, word length, etc., will result in no statistical significance within a mere clause.

Recently, machine learning approaches have revealed great achievements in various natural language processing (NLP) tasks, including textual analysis. Machine learning approaches make it possible that when the input of the model is even a mere fragmented clause, the model is still able to predict the identification of the author, instead of relying on a complete and massive discourse. Of course, the prerequisite is that the model has been previously trained with the provided textual data. For example, Wang et al.27 proposed to realize the textual mining task with a data-driven method, but they could merely classify the gender of the author. Some other existing work28–30 adopted support vector machine (SVM) classifiers to identify the authorship. However, the SVM-based model still requires previously computed statistical linguistic variables. In most cases, the specific categories of linguistic variables need to be artificially assigned, and this number usually can be quite large (e.g., Pavelec et al.29 utilized 94 different categories of variables for the input of their SVM model). On the one hand, this operation will be tricky for researchers, because they have to design and implement a verbose computer program to compute all the required linguistic variables. On the other hand, it is impossible to thoroughly enumerate all the features of the given textual data with limited linguistic variables, because some features are abstract and intangible, hidden beneath the surface of the given clauses, which can never be analytically represented by those variables. Besides, the majority of linguistic variables utilized in the paper mentioned above still highly rely on statistical significance, which demonstrates that they are still not able to predict the identification of the author with only a single given clause, but a complete discourse is still needed instead.

In addition to the models relying on statistical theories and approaches like the SVM model mentioned above, a more effective and flexible kind of deep-learning model called the “neural network” has aroused the attention. For example, Weerasinghe and Greenstadt31 established a two-layer fully-connected neural network as a classifier to categorize the absolute difference between stylometric features extracted from each pair of documents. Besides, Mohsen et al.2 applied a stacked denoising auto-encoder (SDAE) module based on fully-connected layers to extract document features, before using the subsequent SVM classifier for classification. The training of SDAE contains two procedures: unsupervised pre-training and supervised fine-tuning. In this way, hopefully the SVM module will be able to utilize the abstract features hidden in the textual data. Another possible way to utilize the abstract features of the textual data is to apply a word embedding model before the classifier. Essentially, the word embedding model is aimed at transforming the textual data into a series of numerical vectors with a parameter-optimizable module, so the word embedding model is also usually called “Word2Vec”. Win32 designed an authorship identification system utilizing the word embedding strategy at the input end of the classifier, and he tried two kinds of Word2Vec modules, including CBOW (continuous bag of words) and Skip-gram.

In addition to fully-connected modules, more kinds of neural network structures are also promising to achieve better authorship identification performance. For example, Abuhamad et al.33 proposed and implemented multiple kinds of convolutional neural network (CNN) based models for code authorship identification. Before the data is input to the CNN-based module, they tried both TF-IDF (term frequency-inverse document frequency) and word embedding representation strategies to represent the features of the original textual data. Besides, their CNN models are structured in two types: concatenated CNN (C-CNN) architecture and stacked CNN (S-CNN) architecture. Ruder et al.34 developed a character-level and multi-channel CNN model for large-scale authorship attribution, for its efficiency and ability to process character-level signals and differentiate between a large number of classes. CNN-based authorship identification models are also usually designed in conjunction with word embedding approaches. For example, Khatun et al.35 established a character-level CNN with kernel size decreasing along each of the CNN layers for author identification of Bangla literature. The input data of the CNN module is the features of the textual data encoded with CBOW or Skip-gram approaches. Recently, cross-disciplinary methodological advancements have also provided novel perspectives potentially for textual analysis tasks. For instance, hybrid architectures that integrate Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) have been effectively utilized to capture both local and global representations for cross-domain learning, which often incorporates dynamic consistency regularization to align feature spaces across domains36,37. Although initially developed for visual tasks, adapting such hybrid representation paradigms (e.g., combining 1D-CNNs with Transformers) and dynamic regularization strategies could inspire future robust textual models capable of generalizing across diverse writing styles.

However, since the textual data possess apparent sequential characteristics, compared with CNN-based models, the RNN-based deep-learning model may be better at dealing with such kind of data. Zhang et al.38 proposed an end-to-end approach based on the long short-term memory (LSTM) and bidirectional RNN to realize online writer identification. With pre-trained word embeddings, Gupta et al.39 made comparative studies on the RNN models based on LSTM and gated recurrent unit (GRU), and they found that GRU-based RNN achieved more remarkable authorship identification performance. Moreover, Bagnall40 proposed a multi-head RNN for authorship identification. According to his description, the multi-headed RNN language model has multiple sets of output probabilities, and each indicates slightly different predictions. Alsulami et al.41 applied LSTM and bidirectional LSTM models to the Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) based source code authorship attribution task. Dmitrin et al.42 evaluated and analyzed various neural network models, including RNN, LSTM, CNN, and bidirectional LSTM, for the author identification task over three Russian datasets in their work. Romanov et al.43 have also evaluated multiple kinds of deep-learning approaches to authorship identification, including SVM, LSTM, and CNN with the attention mechanism. Especially, they also evaluated the performance of the newly proposed architecture Transformer44 on the authorship identification task, which has been proven to be effective and efficient in many walks of the NLP field. Besides, Álvarez-Fidalgo et al.45 have also applied Transformer-based framework to authorship identification.

Considering that every deep learning model has its advantages along with shortcomings, researchers are trying to take advantages of multiple kinds of deep learning models in a comprehensive and synchronous way. Ensemble learning is a direct strategy to realize this objective. The ensemble learning strategy utilizes and fuses the initially predictive results generated by multiple machine learning algorithms with various voting mechanisms, which will be hopefully to achieve better performances than those obtained from any constituent algorithm alone46. For authorship identification, Abbasi et al.1 established an ensemble learning model which consists of an NLP modeling module composed of a multi-depth DistilBERT model, and a conventional learning model composed of XGBoost, random forest, and multi-layer perceptron models. The final predictive result is generated by applying a voting mechanism to the features extracted by these sub-modules above. Other existing methods proposed to establish a deep-learning model being a hybrid of CNN-based and RNN-based models. For example, Kurtukova et al.47 not only evaluated the authorship identification performance of deep LSTM and CNN with attention, but also tried some hybrid models like combining the CNN module with the subsequent RNN-based models, such as GRU and LSTM, along with their bidirectional versions. Besides, Uchendu et al.48 have also established a hybrid deep-learning model composed of a stacked-CNN model followed by a GRU network to realize authorship attribution for neural text generation.

The approaches for authorship identification listed above, especially the deep-learning approaches have achieved satisfactory performance on the authorship identification task, with given textual data presented in various languages and registers. However, we may find that there are still many possible improvements that can be further taken into consideration. First, the performance of deep-learning models is greatly influenced by the number of layers. Specifically, the larger number of layers means more non-linear procedures during the feed-forward process of the deep-learning model, which helps the model to get better adapted to complex tasks and input conditions, and further improve the performance of the model. Nevertheless, as the depth of the designed deep learning model further increases, problems like gradient vanishing, gradient explosion49, and overfitting50 will arise. Besides, it may be difficult for the deep neural network to utilize the hidden features far from the last layer to yield the final prediction. Moreover, with computational resources limited, the economy of the designed deep-learning model has also aroused our attention these days, but a large quantity of deep-learning layers will also bring about a larger quantity of parameters, more computational complexity, and longer training and inference duration. Lastly, although it can be witnessed that a lot of methods have been developed for authorship identification based on textual data written in various languages, there is still a lack of authorship identification systems designed based on Chinese textual data.

In order to accomplish the objectives above, first, we adopt a Chinese textual dataset containing various literary works written by 6 different Chinese writers. Besides, the textual data of this dataset are a series of fragmented and independent clauses, instead of complete discourses or articles, i.e., the proposed method is supposed to predict the authorship identification with the input being only a single clause, and no statistical features like the word frequency, word length, or spelling errors are accessible. Then, we propose a novel deep-learning model for effective and efficient authorship identification. The model is structured as a pyramid deep bidirectional GRU (biGRU) network, where each output sequence of one layer is downsampled with a CNN-based module along the temporal axis before serving as the input of the next layer, in order to reduce the computational complexity and make the deep-learning model pay more attention to the valuable abstract features closely related to the authorship identification. Besides, every layer of the deep biGRU network share the same group of parameters, which helps to control the quantity of parameters. Moreover, in order to make the predictive result fully utilize the hidden features extracted at different layers inside the deep biGRU structure, every hidden state vector generated at the last time step of each biGRU network layer will pass through a fully-connected layer to yield a vector that represents the possibility distribution of the predicted authorship identification. All the predictive results decided by those output vectors will then generate the final authorship identification prediction based on a soft-voting strategy51. The structure designed above is promising to improve the adaptivity of the deep-learning model and alleviate the overfitting and gradient vanishing/explosion problem, finally enhancing the authorship identification performance.

Data pre-processing

The pre-processing operation on the utilized Chinese textual dataset mainly includes the process of removing the illegal characters involved in the input texts. In this paper, since the textual data come from Chinese literature, we regard Chinese characters and full-width punctuation characters as legal ones. Correspondingly, any other types of characters in the texts will be regarded as illegal ones and will not be included in the subsequent deep-learning model.

Moreover, we further define a string “ < eos > ” as a marker that indicates the end of a clause. The marker will be placed at the end of every textual item before it is fed into the deep-learning model for training and evaluation. Besides, we also define a string “ < pad > ” as a marker indicating the redundant characters inserted into the input texts so that the deep-learning model can be trained and evaluated in a mini-batch manner. The padding operation is necessary because the textual data contained in the dataset may not have the same number of characters, but sequences contained in a mini-batch must possess the same length.

Then, we transform each text composed of a series of characters into a sequence composed of numeric values, since the deep-learning model cannot directly dispose the textual data. We iterate through the whole dataset (including its train, validation, and test sets) and index each legal character encountered by the order of appearance. The maximum index acquired is 5113 according to our computation, i.e., there are 5113 different types of legal character involved in the dataset. Lastly, a dictionary mapping a character to its corresponding index is generated and stored.

Overall architecture

As is shown in Fig. 1, the kernel structure of the model is a pyramid deep biGRU network module, which contains multiple layers of biGRU network. But before being fed into the GRU module, the original textual data (i.e., the Text in Fig. 1) with a length of T, will first be converted to a numeric sequence with a word embedding module. The word embedding module utilized in this paper is a lookup table that maps the index of a character to a numeric vector with D features. The vectors stored in the lookup table are optimizable, i.e., their values can be dynamically updated during the training process. Accordingly, texts with a length of T will be transformed to a feature map with a shape of T× D via the word embedding operation.

By establishing a bidirectional GRU (biGRU) structure, we can also make the output feature at each time step not only related to the hidden features extracted at previous time steps, but also take the features at the subsequent time steps into account. Specifically, in a biGRU structure, the output sequence includes two parts: one is obtained by disposing the input sequence with the GRU network in the forward direction, while the other is obtained in the reversed direction. Furthermore, since the input and output data of a biGRU network are both sequential data, we can make the output of a biGRU network simultaneously serve as the input of another biGRU network. Through iterating the process above for L-1 times, we may get a deep biGRU network structure with L layers. Compared with a single-layer biGRU network, the deep biGRU network contains more parameters and non-linear procedures. Thus, the deep biGRU network may reveal a better performance over various tasks and complex data distributions.

However, the deep biGRU network may also bring about some problems. First, compared with the biGRU network with only a single layer, the deep biGRU network results in a larger quantity of parameters and computational complexity, which is not friendly to users without adequate computational resources. Second, a neural network with too deep a structure is likely to suffer from an overfitting problem and difficulties in the training procedure. Besides, since the predictive vector is directly generated solely by the last layer, the hidden layers far from the last one will have little access to contributing their abstract features to the final predictive output. As is shown in Fig. 1, in this paper, we propose two solutions to deal with these problems.

First, inspired by the residual learning strategy and skip-connection structure utilized in the famous deep-learning model ResNet56, we also propose to connect the input and output ends of a GRU network layer to realize the residual learning. This is hopefully to boost the training procedure and achieve a faster convergence process.

Second, we adapt the deep biGRU into a pyramid-like structure, i.e., the output sequence of each biGRU network layer will be downsampled along the temporal axis before it is input to the next biGRU network layer. The structure of a downsampling module placed subsequently at the L th biGRU network layer is displayed in Fig. 3. Since the length of the sequential feature will be decreased to half of its original length once it passes through a biGRU network layer, the computational complexity will be reduced compared with the condition where every hidden sequence keeps its original length as its initial one.Fig. 3A CNN-based downsampling module placed subsequently at the L th layer of the deep biGRU network model. The downsampling operation is carried out based on “pixel unshuffle” operation, and the output length is half of the original one.

A CNN-based downsampling module placed subsequently at the L th layer of the deep biGRU network model. The downsampling operation is carried out based on “pixel unshuffle” operation, and the output length is half of the original one.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the downsampling module is a 1D CNN-based network. While CNN-based models do not inherently require fixed sequence lengths, the channel dimensionality (corresponding to the hidden feature size) must remain consistent. However, the output of a biGRU layer possesses a fixed feature dimension of 2D (since the GRU layer is bidirectional), whereas the input dimension is D. In the downsampling module, to deal with this discrepancy, initially we permute the dimensions of the sequence from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T/{2}^{L}\times (2D)$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(2D)\times T/{2}^{L}$$\end{document}. Subsequently, a 1D convolutional layer with a kernel size of 1 is applied to the permuted sequence to merge the channels, effectively mapping the feature dimension from 2D back to D.

Following this channel merging, the sequential data is fused with the input of the current GRU layer via a skip-connection to facilitate gradient flow. The resulting feature map is then processed by a 1D convolutional layer with a kernel size of 3 for adjacent feature extraction. Each convolutional layer in this module is followed by a Group Normalization layer57 and a GELU non-linear activation function58. Subsequently, a downsampling operation is performed based on a pixel-unshuffle strategy. The pixel-unshuffle is actually the inverse process of the “pixel-shuffle” operation59. Pixel-shuffle is originally proposed for the efficient upsample of data with the shape of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[C\times H\times W\right]$$\end{document}, because the pixel-shuffle strategy can directly utilize the feature grids along the channel axis. Suppose that the upsampling rate is r, the shape of the upsampled data will be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[\left(C/{r}^{2}\right)\times \left(H\times r\right)\times \left(W\times r\right)\right]$$\end{document}. However, here we need to downsample a sequential data, instead of upsampling an image-like 2d data (with channels). Accordingly, we adopt a 1d pixel-unshuffle operation. Besides, since the pixel-unshuffle operation also utilizes the features along the channel axis, the number of channels will become r times greater than the original; an additional convolutional layer is supposed to be placed before the pixel-unshuffle module for channel adjustment. Specifically, this convolutional layer has a kernel size of 1 and double the number of channels (i.e., from D to 2D). Then, the pixel-unshuffle module downsamples the sequential feature from the shape of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[\left(2D\right)\times T/{2}^{L}\right]$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[D\times T/{2}^{L+1}\right]$$\end{document}. Ultimately, with another dimension permuting operation, the downsampled sequential feature of the downsampling module is generated, and will serve as the input of the next biGRU network layer.

Soft-voting strategy

Embedding learning is usually a satisfactory approach to alleviate the overfitting problem and enhance the performance of the deep-learning model. Generally, we can train multiple models and synthesize the predictive results generated by each model with an appropriate embedding learning strategy. As for classification (identification) tasks, it can be a voting strategy60.

However, simply training multiple models will definitely result in longer training and inference duration and higher computational complexity. Conversely, the embedding learning can probably be realized just on the basis of the existing structure. For instance, as is shown in Fig. 1, the hidden state vectors generated by each layer of biGRU networks at their last time steps will pass through a 2-layer FC module to generate a prediction of authorship identification corresponding to the input textual data, respectively. In this way, the hidden features lying in all the biGRU network layers in the model will have access to contributing towards the final authorship predictive result, even for those layers far away from the output end of the model. This will be helpful for the model to utilize more hidden features across the entire model, which will further alleviate the overfitting problem and improve the authorship identification performance.

Since these predictions are generated by different indices of layers in the deep biGRU network, and each layer of the biGRU network takes different sequential features with different scales of length, it is most likely that the predictions yielded above will not always be completely the same. Hard-voting strategy may be a direct and convenient way to synthesize these predictions and generate the final authorship identification result, which follows the principle that “the minority obeys majority”, e.g., if there are 5 layers in the deep biGRU network, and the predictions are {1,1,2,0,1}, since the predicted authorship index “1” takes up the majority of the predictive list, it will be selected to be the final predictive result.

However, in this paper, instead of hard-voting, we adopt a soft-voting strategy61 to generate the final prediction. Compared with hard-voting, the soft-voting strategy takes the specific possibilities predicted by different sub-models on each possible authorship identification index into account, which reveals the tendency of the prediction results in a more accurate and quantitative way, and thus soft-voting usually shows a better performance. The hidden status vectors generated by different layers of biGRU networks and disposed by 2-layer FC modules can be represented as a matrix in the shape of L× C, i.e.,6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\boldsymbol{p}}{\boldsymbol{r}}{\boldsymbol{o}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{L\times C}=\left[\begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{c}{prob}_{\mathrm{1,1}}, {prob}_{\mathrm{1,2}},...,{prob}_{1,C}\\ {prob}_{\mathrm{2,1}}, {prob}_{\mathrm{2,2}},...,{prob}_{2,C}\end{array}\\ ...\\ {prob}_{L,1}, {prob}_{L,2},...,{prob}_{L,C}\end{array}\right],$$\end{document}where L and C are the number of biGRU network layers and the total number of authorship identifications in the dataset, respectively. For the dataset utilized in this paper, C=6.

The i th row of the matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{p}}{\boldsymbol{r}}{\boldsymbol{o}}{\boldsymbol{b}}$$\end{document} is a vector generated by the i th biGRU network layer along with its corresponding 2-layer FC module, and represents the predicted possibilities of each authorship identification. Ultimately, the final predictive authorship identification corresponding to the input text is generated by selecting the index with the greatest value in the vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\boldsymbol{p}}{\boldsymbol{r}}{\boldsymbol{o}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}_{\mathrm{v}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document}, i.e.,9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${pred}_{\mathrm{f}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}=\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}\left({{\boldsymbol{p}}{\boldsymbol{r}}{\boldsymbol{o}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}_{\mathrm{v}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}}\right).$$\end{document}

Experiment

Evaluation metrics

In this paper, we adopt three quantitative metrics for evaluating the effectiveness of our proposed pyramid deep biGRU network structure and the soft-voting strategy on the authorship identification task, including the precision, recall, and F1 score63:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP};$$\end{document}14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN};$$\end{document}15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F1=2\times \frac{precision\cdot recall}{precision+recall},$$\end{document}where TP refers to the number of true predictive positive samples; FP refers to the number of false predictive positive samples; FN refers to the number of false predictive negative samples.

Besides, FLOPs are adopted to assess the computational complexity of the model, which is defined as the number of floating-point operations per second. The FLOPs are influenced by the length of the input textual data, since the sequential data with a longer length will result in larger FLOPs. In this paper, the FLOPs of all the evaluated models are calculated based on the input text with a length of 28, which according to Table 2, is the average length of the validation and test data.

Implementation details

Our proposed deep-learning model is implemented with the Pytorch framework (version 1.13)64 and Visual Studio Code 1.96.2, based on Windows 10 operation system. We train and evaluate the deep-learning models and algorithms involved in this paper with a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 Super GPU and two Kingston KF552C40-32 RAMs. For efficient and robust training, we adopt AdamW65 as the optimization strategy, with the weight decay set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{1}=0.9$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{2}=0.999$$\end{document}. Besides, the learning rate utilized in different epochs is reduced following the cosine annealing strategy66, from the initial value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{-4}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{-6}$$\end{document}. The model is trained for altogether 100 epochs with a batch size of 32, taking around 50 min according to our settings. We also save the trained models at the end of every training epoch, and pick out the one that performs best on the F1 score metric over the validation set to be further evaluated over the test set. The metrics acquired based on the test set will be regarded as the final index that assesses the authorship identification performance of a trained model.

Comparison with transformer-based methods

Since the self-attention mechanism based deep-learning model Transformer44 was proposed in 2017, today there are a great number of Transformer-based models established to deal with various NLP tasks. In the field of authorship identification, Romanov et al.43 have also evaluated the performance of the Transformer-based models over this task. Hence, in this paper, we have also compared our proposed model with two types of Transformer-based models: the original Transformer 44 and SwinTransformer82, the latter of which utilizes a hierarchical design and shift-window strategy to improve the efficiency and flexibility.

In this paper, the Transformer-based models are implemented with depths (i.e., the number of network layers) of 2, 4, and 6. The output sequence of the Transformer module is averaged by the temporal axis before passing through the 2-layer FC module to yield the final authorship prediction. Other hyperparameter settings of the Transformer module follow the default values provided by the Pytorch online documents (pytorch.org/docs/1.13/generated/torch.nn.Transformer.html).

However, we must explicitly acknowledge a crucial limitation in this comparative analysis. The number of layers of the pyramid deep biGRU network

Our proposed pyramid-structured deep biGRU network contains multiple biGRU layers with shared parameters, and the experiments in Chapter 4 are conducted with the pyramid deep biGRU network containing 5 deep layers. However, in this section, we will discuss how the number of layers will influence the authorship identification performance and the changes in the quantity of parameters as well as computational complexity. However, as the number of layers increases, the enhancement of the authorship identification performance is not apparent. Limitations and future work

In this paper, we proposed an effective and efficient pyramid-structured deep biGRU network deep-learning model for the authorship identification task based on Chinese authors and textual data. However, there are still some promising avenues for future research to further enhance its capabilities and broaden its applicability.

First, although the Chinese textual dataset utilized in this paper contains more than 90 k data tokens, which is adequate for training and evaluating an RNN-based deep-learning model, it is still not enough for an LLM, especially the models based on Transformer structures. Accordingly, in the future, we may consider investigating methods for acquiring or collecting more Chinese textual data suitable for authorship identification studies. This may facilitate the establishment of more robust LLMs with significantly enhanced practical authorship identification abilities across various contexts.

Second, the downsampling operation on each output sequence of the biGRU network layer is implemented with pixel-unshuffle layers in this paper. A characteristic limitation of the pixel-unshuffle strategy is that the downsampling rate must be an integer (usually should be a power of 2), instead of a continuous number. Some scaling strategies, like Meta-SR85, are able to scale the data with arbitrary sampling rates, which can hopefully be taken into consideration in the future.

Third, the methodological strategies introduced in this work, including the pyramid hierarchical processing, parameter sharing, and multi-level soft voting, were demonstrated on a GRU-based backbone. We believe these strategies offer significant potential for improving the efficiency, scalability, and robustness of other deep-learning architectures, including large language models. Future research may explore adapting these strategies to design more parameter-efficient, context-aware, and interpretable Transformer-based or other cutting-edge LLMs, potentially leading to advancements in their architectural optimization and fine-tuning for complex downstream tasks like authorship identification.

Fourth, evaluating the robustness of the proposed method under cross-domain settings (e.g., training on literary novels and testing on essays or social media texts) represents a highly valuable direction for future exploration. The current study focuses exclusively on the highly specific and constrained stylistic markers inherent in Chinese literary works. Constructing a rigorous cross-domain dataset poses a fundamental challenge, as data generated by historical authors is inherently unavailable on modern mediums. Future research may aim to explore the potential of leveraging advanced LLMs to simulate these complex stylistic transfers, thereby facilitating the creation of comprehensive cross-domain datasets to further evaluate and enhance the generalizability of our framework.

Conclusion

The authorship identification technology has a lot of significant applications in a wide range of fields. In order to further enhance the performance of authorship identification, while saving the quantity of parameters along with computation complexity in the meantime, we propose a novel pyramid-structured deep bidirectional GRU network deep-learning model, supplemented with a soft-voting strategy in this paper. Extensive comparative and ablation experiments above have evidenced that, over the utilized Chinese literary textual dataset, compared with various existing deep-learning approaches, including FC-based, CNN-based, RNN-based, and Transformer-based models, our model not only achieves SOTA authorship identification performance, but also is capable of saving the quantity of parameters and computational complexity. Specifically, compared with a conventional 5-layer deep biGRU network, our proposed pyramid deep biGRU network model with the same number of layers can improve the F1 score index by over 2%, with only around 41% FLOPs of the compared deep biGRU network model. Besides, we publish our source codes and pre-trained models online to make our model reproducible. Lastly, it is promising that our contributions are meaningful towards the research on automatic and accurate authorship identification, and are also inspiring for future design of LLM-based approaches to textual analysis tasks.
