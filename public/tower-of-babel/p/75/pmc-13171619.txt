A drug–microbiome–drug interaction impacts co-prescribed medications for Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Andrew A Verdegaal, Joonseok Oh, Bahar Javdan, Ruojun Wang, Qihao Wu, Timothy R W Wang, Jaime A González-Hernández, Mohamed S Donia, Jason M Crawford, Andrew L Goodman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimultaneous prescription of multiple drugs is widespread in medicine. Although the gut microbiome is implicated in drug responses, its role in mediating drug–drug interactions is unexplored. Catechol-O-methyltransferase inhibitors (COMT-I), a class of drugs used alongside levodopa (L-DOPA) to treat Parkinson’s disease symptoms, can alter microbiome composition in patients. Here we characterize the antibiotic properties of COMT-I drugs in vitro, ex vivo and in vivo and dissect how these interactions alter microbiome-mediated L-DOPA metabolism in vitro and ex vivo. Notably, in vitro iron availability determines COMT-I antibiotic activity at multiple levels: extracellular iron can drive non-enzymatic inactivation of COMT-I, rescuing COMT-I-mediated bacterial iron starvation responses. However, limitation of intracellular iron can protect sensitive bacteria from COMT-I antibiotic activity. Co-administration of COMT-I and L-DOPA to human faecal microbial communities ex vivo results in COMT-I-dependent alterations to L-DOPA metabolism in an individual-specific manner. These studies highlight a role for the gut microbiome in mediating drug–drug interactions and identify microbial features that could predict individual responses to co-prescribed drugs.Subject terms: Clinical microbiology, Microbiome, Clinical microbiology, Bacterial genetics, Bacterial physiology

Main

Co-prescription and co-administration of multiple drugs is a common practice in medicine. Drug–drug interactions can both augment and limit treatment efficacy and toxicity1,2. Notably, multiple studies have revealed an extensive and growing list of interactions between prescription drugs and the gut microbiome3,4, affecting both drug efficacy5–9 and adverse events10–13. Gut microbial communities can alter drug metabolism directly and indirectly14,15; drugs can also alter microbiome composition8,9,12. Although interactions between the gut microbiome and individual drugs can be beneficial (for example, methotrexate9) or harmful (for example, irinotecan12), the impact of the microbiome on drug–drug interactions is largely unexplored.

Parkinson’s disease (PD), which impacts approximately six million individuals worldwide16, provides an example of the importance of multidrug treatment regimens. Patients with PD fail to produce sufficient levels of dopamine in the brain and central nervous system, resulting in tremor, diminished mobility and other symptoms17. Levodopa (L-DOPA) is the central pillar of PD therapy; unlike dopamine itself, this oral dopamine prodrug can effectively cross the blood–brain barrier18. Subsequent metabolism to dopamine within striatal tissue provides transient symptom relief. However, peripheral L-DOPA metabolism limits drug efficacy19. One such process is methylation of L-DOPA to 3-O-methyldopa by catechol-O-methyltransferases (COMT)20. To inhibit L-DOPA methylation, patients are frequently prescribed COMT inhibitors (COMT-I) in conjunction with L-DOPA. COMT-I drugs, including tolcapone and entacapone21, share a common nitro-catechol moiety that acts as a competitive inhibitor of host COMT.

In addition to host-mediated peripheral metabolism, L-DOPA can also be metabolized by gut microorganisms through multiple routes, including decarboxylation and deamination5,6,10. Members of the genus Enterococcus frequently encode the TyrDC tyrosine decarboxylase responsible for microbiome-mediated L-DOPA decarboxylation5,6,22. TyrDC-dependent L-DOPA decarboxylation affects bioavailability of L-DOPA in rodents, which is reversed by TyrDC-specific inhibitors5,6. Furthermore, human data correlate tyrDC gene abundance with decreased levels of L-DOPA6,23 and decreased L-DOPA efficacy7 in patients. Intriguingly, Enterococcus and tyrDC abundances are positively associated with COMT-I administration in patients24–26. As a result, COMT-I-related increases in tyrDC gene abundance could counteract the role of COMT-I in increasing L-DOPA bioavailability through inhibition of host COMT. The mechanism by which this occurs remains unclear, but COMT-I can both inhibit bacterial growth in monoculture27,28 and alter human faecal microbiome composition ex vivo29. Nitrocatechols, including COMT-I drugs, can bind and sequester iron30,31, suggesting that these drugs could inhibit bacterial growth through iron starvation and raising the possibility that iron supplementation could be used to reverse the negative effects of COMT-I drugs on the gut microbiome in patients29.

Here we dissect the network of interactions between the gut microbiome, L-DOPA and COMT-I drugs. These studies reveal how interpersonal variation in microbiome community composition determines whether and how COMT-I treatment impacts microbiome-mediated L-DOPA metabolism.

Results

Characterization of COMT-I antibacterial activity and microbial metabolism

Co-administration of L-DOPA and COMT-I drugs represents a common and clinically important example of co-administration of multiple drugs (Fig. 1a). Because COMT-I drugs are reported to show direct antibacterial activity27–29, we first determined the concentrations of two widely used COMT-I drugs, tolcapone and entacapone, that resulted in 50% inhibition of normal growth (IC50) across a diverse panel of human gut commensal microorganisms (Supplementary Tables 1–3). Consistent with previous reports27,29, these drugs showed substantial antibacterial activity against human gut Bacteroidetes and certain Firmicutes; other Firmicutes (for example, Enterococcus faecalis), Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria were resistant (Fig. 1b, Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Tables 1–3). Tolcapone showed an IC50 of ~10–60 µM towards sensitive species, while entacapone exhibited IC50 measurements of ~100–400 µM in similar species. For both drugs, these concentrations are physiologically relevant (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c and Supplementary Tables 4–6; see Methods for further details).

Tolcapone kills the representative gut anaerobe Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron at concentrations close to the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 25 μM, suggesting bactericidal activity (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1d). Interestingly, multiple species including B. thetaiotaomicron efficiently nitroreduce COMT-I drugs, with certain species performing a subsequent N-acetylation step (Fig. 1b; M1 and M2 refer to nitroreduced and N-acetylated metabolites, respectively). Deletion of known15 or putative nitroreductases in B. thetaiotaomicron decreases (but does not eliminate) nitroreduction of tolcapone and entacapone (Extended Data Fig. 1e). Heterologous expression of several of these enzymes in Escherichia coli increases nitroreduction of these drugs, suggesting that multiple B. thetaiotaomicron enzymes can catalyse this transformation (Extended Data Fig. 1f). However, deletion of a single nitroreductase gene (nfsB) in E. coli is sufficient to significantly reduce M1 formation (Extended Data Fig. 1g,h), while deletion of a single N-acetyltransferase gene (nhoA) prevents M2 formation (Extended Data Fig. 1i,j). Nitroreduced and N-acetylated tolcapone metabolites accumulate in the gut and serum of conventional mice administered the parent drug and are absent in germ-free animals, indicating that microorganisms are necessary and sufficient to produce these metabolites in mammals (Extended Data Fig. 1k and Supplementary Table 7). These metabolites are also observed in faecal samples from tolcapone-treated patients, although metabolite abundance distributions may differ between mice and humans32.

Nitroreduction of tolcapone reduces its antibacterial effects

We next examined factors that contribute to tolcapone resistance or sensitivity. Unlike wild-type E. coli, a ΔbamB ΔtolC strain33 with membrane integrity and drug efflux defects is sensitive to tolcapone (Fig. 1c) and shows increased tolcapone accumulation (Extended Data Fig. 2a), highlighting a role for barrier and export functions as one stage of tolcapone resistance (Fig. 1b,c). In addition, drug sensitivity generally correlates with metabolism of tolcapone to M1 and M2 across bacterial species (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 2b), raising the possibility that these metabolites either cause toxicity or represent detoxification activity by sensitive species. We synthesized both M1 and M2 tolcapone metabolites (see Methods for details) and determined the impact of each compound on growth of both tolcapone-sensitive and resistant species; notably, both metabolites show drastically reduced antibacterial activity compared with the parent drug (Fig. 1c). Consistent with these results, deletion of nfsB from the E. coli ΔbamB ΔtolC strain significantly decreases tolcapone nitroreduction (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d) and further increases tolcapone sensitivity (Extended Data Fig. 2d). The lack of antibiotic activity of M1 is not due to reduced access to the cell, because introduction of M1 to E. coli ∆bamB ∆tolC results in cellular M1 accumulation and conversion to M2 in an NhoA-dependent manner (Extended Data Fig. 2a).

E. faecalis V583 provides an exception to the trends observed in other species: it is resistant to tolcapone antibiotic activity (Fig. 1c) while efficiently metabolizing the parent drug without significant production of M1 or M2 (Fig. 1b). A comparative metabolomics study revealed that this strain instead produces a previously uncharacterized cysteinylated M1 metabolite (Fig. 1d–f and Supplementary Table 8). None of the other tested species produce this metabolite to similar levels (Fig. 1f) suggesting that E. faecalis not only is resistant to COMT-I but also may have a more complicated means of inactivating COMT-I drugs.

Iron binding to COMT-I drugs modulates their antibacterial activity

Previous literature describes the iron chelating ability of catechols34,35, including nitrocatechols such as entacapone30. Similar to previous reports of other nitrocatechols35, incubation of tolcapone with either ferrous iron (Fe2+ in FeSO4; Fig. 2a) or ferric iron (Fe3+ in FeCl3; Fig. 2b) results in a red shift in ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) light absorbance, consistent with iron binding. This shift does not occur when M1 or M2 is incubated with ferrous or ferric iron, suggesting a lack of iron binding by these metabolites (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b).

We next investigated whether the antibacterial activity of tolcapone can be attributed to iron starvation via iron sequestration by COMT-I. Consistent with iron–tolcapone interactions leading to iron sequestration, both B. thetaiotaomicron and a tolcapone-sensitive E. coli strain (WO153) show a partial iron starvation transcriptomic response to tolcapone exposure (for example, increase in feoAB in B. thetaiotaomicron), although the most differentially expressed genes are not linked to iron homeostasis36,37 (Fig. 2c, Extended Data Fig. 3c–e and Supplementary Tables 9–13). In agreement with previous reports29, iron supplementation with either ferrous or ferric iron alleviates tolcapone antibacterial activity against B. thetaiotaomicron (Fig. 2d,e and Extended Data Fig. 3f); lower concentrations of ferrous iron are required to rescue B. thetaiotaomicron from tolcapone-mediated growth inhibition compared with ferric iron. Notably, direct addition of 1 mM ferrous iron to either tolcapone or entacapone in sterile medium causes rapid non-enzymatic conversion of these drugs to their nitroreduced, non-toxic metabolites (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 3g–i), as observed with other nitro-containing compounds such as metronidazole38. Addition of ferric iron under aerobic conditions also decreases tolcapone and entacapone levels, but does not result in detectable non-enzymatic nitroreduction of either compound (Fig. 2g and Extended Data Fig. 3j–l). This distinction between ferrous and ferric iron may be partially responsible for the difference in growth of B. thetaiotaomicron during iron supplementation with tolcapone challenge (Fig. 2d,e).

As an independent approach, we examined the impact of a widely used intracellular ferrous iron chelator, 2,2-bipyridyl (BIP)39 on B. thetaiotaomicron tolcapone sensitivity. Strikingly, BIP increases growth of tolcapone-treated B. thetaiotaomicron even at BIP concentrations that inhibit B. thetaiotaomicron growth by >50% in the absence of tolcapone (Fig. 2h). Together, these results suggest that the bactericidal activity of COMT-I drugs is not solely attributed to iron sequestration.

Intracellular iron homeostasis determines COMT-I antibacterial activity

We conducted in vitro evolution studies to identify bacterial factors that impact COMT-I sensitivity in three bacterial species (Bacteroides. fragilis NCTC 9343 Δtdk, B. thetaiotaomicron VPI-5482 Δtdk and E. coli BW25113 ΔbamB ΔtolC ΔnfsB). Incubating each species in the presence of increasing concentrations of tolcapone resulted in strains with significantly increased resistance to both tolcapone (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 4a,b) and entacapone (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Whole-genome sequencing of these evolved isolates identified mutations unique to tolcapone exposure (Supplementary Table 14). In both B. thetaiotaomicron and B. fragilis, all tested tolcapone-evolved isolates carried mutations in feoAB (B. thetaiotaomicron: BT_1592; B. fragilis: BF_1287) (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Targeted, nonpolar deletion of this gene in an unevolved strain of B. thetaiotaomicron is sufficient to increase tolcapone and entacapone resistance, which is reversed by complementation (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 4c).

In B. fragilis, FeoAB is a primary importer of inorganic ferrous iron across the intracellular membrane40. Consistent with this reported function, B. thetaiotaomicron ∆feoAB and the B. fragilis tolcapone-evolved isolates with feoAB mutations show reduced growth in iron-limited conditions (Extended Data Fig. 4d,e), lower intracellular iron content (Fig. 3d) and increased sensitivity to BIP compared with the parental strain (Extended Data Fig. 4f). B. thetaiotaomicron upregulates feoAB among other iron-responsive genes during tolcapone treatment37 (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 3c), potentially to restore intracellular iron homeostasis. FeoAB mutation does not further protect B. thetaiotaomicron from tolcapone during BIP treatment or alter drug transport (Extended Data Fig. 4g–k). Evolved tolcapone-resistant E. coli isolates carry mutations in other genes linked with iron import and iron homeostasis, including exbB41, exbD42 and yqjH43 (Supplementary Table 14 and Extended Data Fig. 4b). Together, these results suggest that bacteria are protected from COMT-I antibiotic activity by decreasing intracellular iron levels.

Multiple lines of evidence indicate that intracellular iron is directly involved in tolcapone antibacterial activity. Total intracellular iron levels (reflecting ferric and ferrous, chelated and free iron) are elevated following tolcapone treatment compared with untreated or M1-treated cultures (Extended Data Fig. 5a). However, tolcapone decreases levels of ferrous iron in the cell, possibly through conversion of ferrous iron to ferric iron after binding (Extended Data Fig. 5b), consistent with previous studies of entacapone using other methods29. In addition, decreasing intracellular iron levels (via feoAB deletion or BIP treatment) reduces tolcapone bactericidal activity (Fig. 3e–g), even though conversion of tolcapone to non-toxic M1 is slower in iron-limiting conditions (Extended Data Figs. 4i and 5c). Together, these data suggest that tolcapone-mediated toxicity is iron dependent and that tolcapone-mediated increases in total intracellular iron may promote its antibacterial effects.

Additional studies highlight parallels between tolcapone and metronidazole, a nitro-containing antibiotic associated with multiple mechanisms of action. First, metronidazole nitroreduction is accelerated by intracellular iron44; feoAB mutations increase B. thetaiotaomicron resistance to metronidazole (Extended Data Fig. 5d) as observed in other bacterial species45,46. Second, metronidazole nitroreduction produces DNA-damaging intermediates47,48, and one hallmark of metronidazole-induced toxicity is that deletion of the DNA repair gene recA increases metronidazole sensitivity47. Indeed, deletion of recA from B. thetaiotaomicron increases sensitivity to both metronidazole and tolcapone (Fig. 3h,i). Third, metronidazole can also damage proteins through ferrous iron–protein–drug interactions49 and can induce protein chaperone gene (for example, dnaJ) expression in B. fragilis at concentrations below the MIC50. Consistent with this activity, tolcapone treatment of E. coli WO153 and B. thetaiotaomicron induces upregulation of known or putative chaperone or protein aggregation-associated genes (for example, E. coli spy51, zraP52 and ibpB53; Fig. 3j, Extended Data Fig. 5e and Supplementary Tables 12 and 15) in addition to an increase in insoluble and aggregated proteins in E. coli (Fig. 3k and Extended Data Fig. 5f). Together, these results indicate that iron can impact the antibacterial activity of COMT-I drugs at multiple levels (Extended Data Fig. 5g).

Tolcapone disrupts human gut community composition ex vivo

Next, we examined how the antibiotic activity of tolcapone leads to remodelling of complex human gut microbial communities. To this end, we cultured 26 human faecal microbial communities in the presence of 50 µM tolcapone, 50 µM M1 or vehicle control (DMSO) for 24 h under anaerobic conditions (Fig. 4a). This drug concentration is ~1–2 × MIC for sensitive species (Fig. 1b) and within the range of estimated intestinal concentrations in humans and measured concentrations in tolcapone-treated mice (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c,k). Tolcapone exposure significantly decreased community alpha diversity compared with DMSO or M1 (Fig. 4b) and altered the relative abundance of multiple genera (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 16). Tolcapone metabolism varied greatly across communities (Extended Data Fig. 6a), and communities with higher initial rates of nitroreduction were least affected by tolcapone disruption (Extended Data Fig. 6b). This correlation between rapid tolcapone nitroreduction and reduced microbiome disruption could be due to lower tolcapone exposure in communities that rapidly nitroreduce the drug. Beta diversity, measured by constrained redundancy analysis (RDA) or Bray–Curtis dissimilarity analysis, reveals significant changes to community structure after tolcapone exposure compared with either M1 or DMSO controls (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 6c–e).

Many taxa with tolcapone-sensitive representative strains (Fig. 1b) show decreased relative abundance in the community context following tolcapone treatment (for example, Blautia), while the relative abundance of taxa with tolcapone-resistant representative strains such as Enterococcus, Bifidobacterium and Enterobacteriaceae increases following tolcapone treatment (Fig. 4c,e and Extended Figs. 6e and 7a,b). Notably, these taxa are also significantly altered in patients administered COMT-I drugs, in the same direction as observed in these in vitro studies24,25,54,55. Importantly, species- and gene-specific quantitative PCR (qPCR) of tolcapone-, M1- and DMSO-treated communities reveals a significant expansion of E. faecalis, Enterococcus faecium and tyrDC (Extended Data Fig. 7c–j) in response to tolcapone treatment. Presence of E. faecalis strongly correlates with tyrDC gene levels in tolcapone-treated communities, suggesting that this species encodes a large proportion of the tyrDC genes in these communities (Extended Data Fig. 7f,g). E. faecium also significantly increases with tolcapone treatment, but shows a weaker association with tyrDC (Extended Data Fig. 7h,i), probably owing to better growth and representation of E. faecalis in these experimental conditions. These data suggest that the positive correlation between tyrDC gene abundance and COMT-I prescription in patients26 can be explained by direct antibacterial activity of COMT-I drugs that enrich for tyrDC-encoding taxa such as E. faecalis and E. faecium.

Tolcapone can promote expansion of tyrDC-encoding bacteria in gnotobiotic mice

We aimed to determine whether COMT-I administration can drive expansion of Enterococcus in an animal model in which potential confounding factors (for example, host genetic and environmental variability, disease progression and other drugs) are controlled. To this end, we colonized groups of genetically identical germ-free mice with faecal microbial communities from three unrelated human donors (MV20, MV25 and MV12) that each showed low but detectable levels of Enterococcus and tyrDC15. After colonization, the mice were maintained on tolcapone-containing diet or a matched control diet (Fig. 5a); caecal contents from the tolcapone group of MV20-colonized mice contained both the parent compound (~10 µM) and microbial tolcapone metabolites (~40 µM) as expected32 (Extended Data Fig. 8a). Consistent with the observation that tolcapone binds iron in vitro (Fig. 2a,b), the tolcapone-treated mice showed increased caecal iron levels compared with the untreated animals (Extended Data Fig. 8b). We monitored individual mice for faecal levels of tyrDC across two individual experiments for each community (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 17). Using a linear mixed-effects model incorporating most experimental variables as fixed effects (for example, community, sex, experiment, treatment), we find a significant three-way interaction between treatment, time and community across 559 data points (P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 18). Tolcapone treatment is significantly positively associated with tyrDC+ Enterococcus colonization in both MV20 (P < 0.001) and MV25 (P = 0.026) over time (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 18). In addition, in MV20-colonized mice, tolcapone treatment significantly increases peak levels of tyrDC gene abundance (Extended Data Fig. 8c) and tyrDC+ Enterococcus colony forming units (CFU) (normalized to pretreatment abundance) (Extended Data Fig. 8d).

Gnotobiotic mice carrying a third human faecal community, MV12, did not show a significant increase in tyrDC+ Enterococcus upon tolcapone treatment (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Tables 17 and 18). Conventional (specific pathogen free) mice trend towards expanded tyrDC+ Enterococcus in response to short-term oral tolcapone treatment, but differences were not significant at this sample size (n = 5 per group; P = 0.072 when considering a two-way interaction between treatment and time) (Extended Data Fig. 8e,f and Supplementary Tables 18 and 19). These results suggest that tolcapone-mediated tyrDC+ Enterococcus expansion does not occur in every gut microbiome. In humans, COMT-I administration is associated with increased Enterococcus and tyrDC at the population level, but not in every individual24–26, as observed in human gut communities exposed to tolcapone ex vivo (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 7j).

Importantly, the small intestine is the main site of L-DOPA absorption56. In patients, jejunal tyrDC and Enterococcus abundances are significantly correlated with faecal tyrDC and Enterococcus levels23, and increases in tyrDC and E. faecalis measured in faeces are negatively associated with L-DOPA responsiveness7,23. We colonized germ-free mice with a human gut microbiome (MV25), placed animals on tolcapone or control diet as above, and collected small intestinal contents after 14, 29 or 43 days (Fig. 5c). Consistent with the faecal measurements, tolcapone treatment also significantly increased tyrDC+ Enterococcus in the small intestine, suggesting that this drug can alter the abundance of L-DOPA-metabolizing species at the main site of L-DOPA absorption in the gut (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Tables 18 and 20).

Community MV3 provides an example: this microbiome shows low levels of the Enterococcus genus (as measured by selective plating; Fig. 6h), E. faecalis and tyrDC (as measured by qPCR; Extended Data Fig. 7c–e), and L-DOPA decarboxylation capacity (Fig. 6i,j). Tolcapone treatment of MV3 alone has no effect on L-DOPA-d3 decarboxylation as expected (Fig. 6i,j). We also inoculated the Enterococcus-negative community MV3 with an E. faecalis tyrDC::TN mutant5,57; this strain maintains tolcapone resistance but lacks L-DOPA decarboxylation activity (Extended Data Fig. 10d,e). Discussion

In this Article, we describe a drug–microbiome–drug interaction between COMT-I and L-DOPA, two drugs that are frequently co-prescribed for the treatment of PD symptoms. While COMT-I drugs provide important benefit to many patients with PD and are a mainstay of treatment, these drugs are associated with gut microbiome alterations24,25,54,55, and the mechanisms and consequences of these interactions remain largely unknown. We show that tolcapone and entacapone both exhibit iron-mediated antibacterial activity against many gut bacterial species; this activity can remodel the microbiome in a manner that can significantly alter its capacity to metabolize L-DOPA.

Iron administration has been proposed as an adjuvant therapy to preserve microbiome homeostasis during COMT-I treatment29. However, our studies indicate that this type of intervention may be problematic because further iron supplementation could contribute to non-enzymatic COMT-I inactivation. Given that gut microorganisms mitigate tolcapone-mediated antibiotic activity through genetic mutations that reduce, rather than increase, intracellular iron, iron supplementation could also exacerbate the antibiotic effect in certain contexts. Consistent with this possibility, pharmacological approaches that reduce iron concentrations also protect gut microorganisms from COMT-I toxicity. Iron supplementation may also be problematic for other aspects of PD therapy by reducing the efficacy of both L-DOPA and carbidopa58.

The antibiotic activity of COMT-I drugs may impact PD therapy at multiple levels. In patients, COMT-I administration is associated with increases in both tyrDC26 and Enterococcus24,25. The studies in this report suggest that this association results from the antibacterial effects of COMT-I drugs. Because faecal tyrDC and E. faecalis abundances in patients are negatively associated with L-DOPA maximum plasma concentration (Cmax)23 and efficacy7, these effects may limit L-DOPA efficacy in patients prescribed this drug combination. COMT-I-mediated antibiotic activity may also impact local responses to L-DOPA in the gut. For example, ex vivo tolcapone treatment reduces the capacity of human gut microbiomes to convert L-DOPA into deaminated metabolites; these metabolites are implicated in decreased ileal motility10 and amelioration of inflammation59,60. In addition, dopamine derived from peripheral L-DOPA decarboxylation may have adverse effects locally or systemically, including vomiting and cardiac arrythmias61,62. Thus, COMT-I-driven changes in the fate of L-DOPA and its microbiome-derived metabolites in the gut may impact multiple positive and negative consequences of L-DOPA treatment, which remain to be explored.

Together, these studies show that the gut microbiome can mediate drug–drug interactions. Future progress in this area could identify drug combinations most likely to interact via the microbiome, patients most likely to experience these interactions and interventional strategies to allow optimum benefit to patients who manage their health through more than one drug.

Methods

Animal experiments

All mouse experiments were performed using protocols approved by the Yale University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Germ-free C57BL/6 mice (7–12 weeks old) were used in this study. A mix of both sexes was used for all gnotobiotic studies. The animals were co-housed based on litters and sex and colonized with human faecal communities (100 µl glycerol stock per mouse). The animals were then singly housed at the beginning of treatment with specialized chow in flexible plastic gnotobiotic isolators, with each treatment group in a separate isolator. The temperature was maintained between 20 °C and 26 °C, and the humidity was maintained between 30% and 70%. Mice were kept in a 12-h light–dark cycle and provided specialized diets, either control chow (TD.110180; Inotiv) or tolcapone chow (TD.230455; control chow supplemented with 360 mg kg−1 tolcapone; Inotiv), and autoclaved water for the duration of the experiment.

For conventional mouse experiments, 6–8-week-old female specific-pathogen-free (SPF) C57B6/NTac mice were purchased from Taconic Biosciences. The mice were acclimated to our facility for 10 days before experimentation. The mice were kept in a 12-h light–dark cycle and gavaged twice daily with either vehicle (1× sterile PBS + 5% DMSO) or tolcapone (30 mg kg−1 in vehicle). Faecal pellets were collected and used for monitoring Enterococcus and tyrDC levels in all experiments (‘Enterococcus CFU enumeration’).

Bacterial culture conditions

Strains and communities used for in vitro and ex vivo experiments were grown in an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratory Products) containing 20% CO2, 10% H2 and 70% N2. Samples for all in vitro and ex vivo experiments were grown and incubated at 37 °C within the anaerobic chamber. For single-species experiments, single-use aliquots of each bacterial species or strain in culture medium plus 20% glycerol were created in anaerobic conditions.

All anaerobic steps were performed using pre-reduced, sterilized medium and materials. Bacteria for monoculture studies were grown in liquid mega medium63, TYG medium (10 g l−1 tryptone, 5 g l−1 yeast extract, 10 mM glucose, 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), 20 mg l−1 MgSO4·7H2O, 400 mg l−1 NaHCO3, 80 mg l−1 NaCl, 0.0008% CaCl2, and 4 mg l−1 FeSO4·7H2O, 10 mg l−1 haemin (Sigma), 0.5 g l−1 cysteine and 1 mg l−1 vitamin K3), Bacteroides minimal medium (100 mM KH2PO4, 15 mM NaCl, 8.5 mM (NH4)2SO4, 5 g l−1 glucose, 4 mM L-cysteine, 1.9 µM histidine–haematin, 200 µM histidine, 100 µM MgCl2, 1.4 µM FeSO4, 1 µg ml−1 vitamin K3, 50 µM CaCl2, 3.7 nM vitamin B12), LB broth or Mueller Hinton Broth II (MHBII, Sigma-Aldrich, catalogue number 90922). Before growth in liquid medium, strains were streaked on either brain–heart infusion (BHI; Becton Dickinson catalogue number 237500) agar supplemented with 10% horse blood (Quad Five) or Brucella (Becton Dickinson catalogue number 211086) agar supplemented with 10% horse blood. Experiments measuring B. thetaiotaomicron CFUs during tolcapone exposure were completed using plating onto BHI–HK agar plates (BHI with 0.5 mg l−1 vitamin K and 50 mg l−1 haemin).

Experiments involving complex or human faecal communities were completed using a 70:30 Bryant and Burkey medium (BB)–modified Gifu anaerobic medium (mGAM) preparation14. BB and mGAM were obtained from HiMedia. Growth curve assays were completed in mGAM, mGAM plus sugar (0.1% cellobiose, 0.1% fructose, 0.1% maltose) and mGAM plus Tween (0.1% Tween-80), TYG, LB or MHBII.

E. coli strains used in growth curves were grown aerobically at 37 °C (200 rpm shaking) in either LB or MHBII. For RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) experiments, E. coli WO153 was grown in MHBII at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. B. thetaiotaomicron cultures used in RNA-seq experiments were grown in TYG.

Human faecal material

Human faecal material (MV1–MV29) used for ex vivo culturing experiments and in vivo mouse studies were obtained and prepared according to the Yale University Human Investigation Committee protocol number 1106008725 (ref. 15) with volunteers providing written consent. Briefly, samples were obtained from healthy human donors aged 20–60 years old and stored in single-use aliquots with 20% glycerol in an anaerobic environment. Samples were immediately stored at −80 °C once aliquoted until time of use. The health status of human donors is based on self-reporting for health status and recent use of antibiotics. These samples (with the exception of MV25) were described in a previous report11. MV26–MV29 in this study correlate with MV25–MV28 in the previous report11.

Chemicals

Drugs, metabolites and other chemicals used in this study were purchased from Cayman Chemical, Santa Cruz Biosciences, MilliporeSigma/Sigma-Aldrich or MedChemExpress. Tolcapone was purchased from MedChemExpress (HY-17406), Cayman Chemical (20768) or Sigma-Aldrich (1670207). Entacapone used in this study was purchased from Cayman Chemical (14153). L-DOPA-d3 used was purchased from Cayman Chemical (22089). Dopamine HCl used for standards was purchased from MilliporeSigma (H8502). Metronidazole was purchased from Cayman Chemical (9002409). All compounds were weighed and dissolved in DMSO for the creation of concentration stock solutions, except L-DOPA-d3, which was dissolved in water containing 5% DMSO and 0.5% cysteine for all ex vivo assays and standard preparations. Chemical structures drawn in the paper were produced using ChemDraw (v 19.1, PerkinElmer) software. All generated oligonucleotides used as primers in these experiments were generated at the Yale Keck Oligo Synthesis Resource Center.

Estimation of intestinal concentrations of tolcapone and entacapone

We used two approaches to determine whether the COMT-I concentrations in these studies are physiologically relevant.

Method 1: faecal recovery method

Intestinal drug concentrations were estimated from faecal recovery data using previously published methods27,64. Briefly, this method uses measured faecal recovery data for each drug and published estimates for intestinal volumes to estimate drug concentrations in the gut lumen. Faecal recovery for tolcapone and entacapone was estimated at 40% and 90%, respectively, based on published literature32,65,66.

On the basis of this faecal recovery data, predicted maximal tolcapone concentrations in the gut lumen range from 214 µM in the small intestine to 856 µM in the colon. Predicted entacapone concentrations are slightly higher (218 µM in the small intestine to 1.9 mM in the colon).

Method 2: pharmacokinetic modelling using GastroPlus

As an alternate approach, we used pharmacokinetic modelling software (GastroPlus) to estimate gastrointestinal concentrations of tolcapone and entacapone using parameters based on drug structure (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5) and published measurements32,67,68. GastroPlus was used as previously described64 with some alterations. Briefly, physicochemical properties for each drug (for example, solubility) were calculated from two-dimensional structures (.sdf files) using ADMET Predictor (version 13.0). Additional parameters (volume of each intestinal compartment, mean precipitation time, diffusion coefficient, drug particle density and effective permeability) were determined using GastroPlus (v10.2; SimulationsPlus). Drug clearance was estimated at 7 l h−1 for tolcapone and 50 l h−1 for entacapone, based on previous publications32,68. Dose for both compounds was set to 200 mg (refs. 32,68). We also included host glucuronyltransferase (for example, UGT1A9) activity for both compounds67. The full set of input parameters for each drug is provided in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5. With these parameters established, we used GastroPlus to construct a compartmental model of an 85-kg human male with fasted physiology. This model was evaluated for concordance with published clinical measurements of serum drug pharmacokinetics41 and then used to estimate intestinal concentrations of each drug. To limit the complexity of the model, we omitted other host metabolic pathways and did not include bacterial metabolism of parent compounds or their glucuronidated metabolites (that is, bacterial β-glucuronidase activity). The GastroPlus model predicts serum tolcapone pharmacokinetics that are consistent with clinical measurements32 (Supplementary Tables 4–6) and predicts tolcapone and entacapone concentrations in the small intestine that match or exceed the estimates from the faecal recovery model (Extended Data Fig. 1b). GastroPlus predictions of drug concentrations in the large intestine are lower than the estimates from the faecal recovery model, potentially because bacterial β-glucuronidase activity was not included in the model.

In vitro COMT-I growth inhibition assays

Single-use aliquots of each bacterial species were opened in an anaerobic chamber and streaked onto solid agar media for single colonies. Colonies were grown between 24 h and 48 h anaerobically at 37 °C. Single colonies were selected and inoculated in liquid medium and grown for 18–24 h. If cultures were turbid after overnight growth, they were back-diluted 1:4 3–4 h before the experiment so that cultures used for growth assays were in exponential phase. optical density measured at 600 nm wavelength (OD600) was then measured using a Genesys 30 Visible Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) to determine dilution needed for final inoculum into the growth plate. Drugs and metabolites were prepared by diluting a concentrated stock solution in DMSO into the first column of a 96-well flat-bottomed culture plate (Corning, number 3595) and subsequently diluting two-fold into culture medium in each following column, whereby each respective well contained 2 × final compound concentration (for that well) diluted to 1× by addition of 2 × concentration of the bacterial inoculum. Plates were then covered with Breathe-Easy sealing membrane (Sigma, Z380059).

Bacterial cultures mixed with compounds were then grown anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h (48 h for Parabacteroides distasonis) with either constant shaking (E. coli, Salmonella enterica) or brief shaking (10 s before OD measurements for all others) using double orbital shaking in either a BioTek Epoch2 or BioTek Eon microplate spectrophotometer. OD600 was monitored in 15-min intervals. For experiments comparing wild-type and mutant E. coli, strains were grown aerobically in either MHBII or LB and incubated with drugs with constant shaking aerobically at 37 °C in a BioTek Synergy HTX microplate spectrophotometer with 15-min OD600 measurement intervals. Background absorbance based on media control wells was subtracted from each measurement. BioTek Gen5 software was used to operate spectrophotometers and retrieve data. Area under the curve (AUC) measurements were manually integrated using Excel, with a species-specific cut-off for each AUC measurement (set to ~1 h after the no-drug control for the species that reached stationary phase), or at a time stated in the text. Final growth (percentage growth) was determined by normalizing growth in each condition compared with growth in the no-drug control for each species or strain. Technical duplicates or triplicates were completed for each growth curve, and growth curves with aberrant growth or large disruptions to the growth profile were manually curated and repeated. Checkerboard assays using B. thetaiotaomicron were completed as above with modifications based on previously published protocols69. Specifically, 640 µM and 1,280 µM stocks of tolcapone were prepared in TYG. Then, 180 µl of the 640 µM stock was added to wells B2–B10 of a 96-well plate, and 180 µl of the 1,280 µM stock was added to well B11. Subsequently, 90 µl of TYG medium was added to wells C2–G11 of the 96-well plate. To establish the tolcapone dilution series across rows of the plate, 90 µl of wells B2–B11 was transferred to wells C2–C11. After pipetting wells C2–C11 to mix the contents within the well, 90 µl was then transferred to wells D2–D11. This process was repeated through wells F2–F11. After wells F2–F11 were pipetted to mix the contents of each well, 90 µl of each well was discarded. To establish the FeSO4 dilution series across columns of the same plate, 90 µl of 1 mM FeSO4 was added to wells B11–G11. After wells B11–G11 were pipetted to mix the contents within each well, 90 µl was then transferred to wells B10–G10. This process was repeated through wells B3–G3. After wells B3–G3 were pipetted to mix the contents of each well, 90 µl of each well was discarded. After the checkboard plate was assembled in this manner, a B. thetaiotaomicron overnight culture was diluted to OD 0.05 and 90 µl of this culture was added to wells B2–G11. Plates were then sealed with Breathe-Easy film and incubated and monitored for growth using a plate reader as above.

Single-species metabolism

For tolcapone metabolism experiments, single-use glycerol aliquots for each isolate were opened in an anaerobic chamber and streaked onto solid agar medium for single colonies. About 5 colonies were picked for each species, inoculated into mega medium and grown for 24 h anaerobically at 37 °C. Overnight cultures were then diluted 1:10 into dilute culture medium (1:5 TYG–water for B. thetaiotaomicron and B. fragilis; 1:5 mega medium–water for all other species) containing 10 µM tolcapone and 0.5% cysteine. Treated cultures were incubated in triplicate anaerobically for 12 h at 37 °C in a covered, sterile 96-deep-well plate (SP Bel-Art, F37860-0001) at a final volume of 1 ml. Subsequently, 30 µl of treated cultures was then aliquoted into a 96-v-well polypropylene storage plate (Thermo Fisher, AB1058) at 10 min, 30 min, 60 min, 180 min, 360 min and 720 min and stored at −80 °C until further processing. For CFU plating, 0-h and 12-h cultures with drug added were diluted 10−5 to 10−10 in PBS anaerobically. Then, 10 µl of each dilution was spot streaked onto square Petri plates containing either BHI–blood or Brucella–blood agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h.

To prepare aliquots for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis, plates containing aliquots were thawed from −80 °C on ice and diluted 1:1 with addition of 30 µl acetonitrile to quench any further metabolism. Then, 20-µl quenched samples were moved to a new plate at which point 5-µl internal standards (4 µM final concentrations of caffeine, sulfamethoxazole, ipraflavone and yohimbine) diluted in water were added. Subsequently, 75 µl ice-cold methanol–acetonitrile (50:50) was added to the quenched samples containing internal standard. Samples were incubated at −20 °C for 1 h and centrifuged at 3,200 g at 4 °C for 20 min. Then, 15 µl of the sample supernatants was aliquoted into new plates and used for LC–MS analysis (‘LC–MS analysis’). Standards used for quantification of tolcapone, M1 and M2 were prepared in parallel in mega medium using the same method.

The nine-nitroreductase-deletion B. thetaiotaomicron strain (Δtdk ΔBT0181 ΔBT0217 ΔBT0942 ΔBT1006 ΔBT1148 ΔBT1316 ΔBT1680 ΔBT2144 ΔBT3392) was tested alongside B. thetaiotaomicron Δtdk as above with minor modifications. Overnight cultures of both strains were grown anaerobically in TYG. Then, 100 µl of these cultures was added to 900 µl diluted TYG (1:5 TYG–water) containing 2 µM tolcapone in triplicate. Of each culture, 30 µl was removed and quenched in 30 µl ice-cold acetonitrile. Samples were diluted 1:1 in water before being analysed via LC–MS.

Genetic manipulation of E. coli

For heterologous expression of B. thetaiotaomicron nitroreductases in E. coli, verified and putative nitroreductase genes were first identified in the B. thetaiotaomicron genome (Supplementary Tables 1, 21 and 22). Primers were created to amplify each gene with overlapping regions at both 5′ and 3′ ends of the target vector (pET22b (Novagen); Supplementary Table 21). For putative nitroreductase genes, primers captured the region beginning from the first start codon (ATG) and ending immediately before the first stop codon at the 3′ end of the coding region. Genes were also processed through SignalP (v5.0)70 to check for previously unidentified signal peptides encoded from alternate candidate start sites. Gene inserts were then amplified from purified B. thetaiotaomicron VPI-5482 genomic DNA using Q5 High-Fidelity Master Mix (New England Biolabs). The vector used for expression of these genes (pET22b; Novagen) was restriction digested using NotI and NdeI. Digestion at these locations allows for expression of a gene insert to be driven by an isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG)-inducible promoter and incorporates a 6× His tag at the C-terminus of the expressed protein. The lengths of both inserts and vector were verified using gel electrophoresis before PCR clean-up of amplified or digested products using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer’s protocol.

The vector and insert were annealed together using HiFi DNA Assembly 2× Master Mix (New England Biolabs) at a ratio of either 1:3 or 1:10 at 50 °C for 1 h. Reaction mixes were then dialysed on nitrocellulose above deionized water for 2 h. Subsequently, 2 µl of these reaction mixes was used to transform E. coli 10G electrocompetent cells (Lucigen), which were then rescued with fresh LB and plated on LB plates containing 100 µg ml−1 ampicillin. Single colonies were selected and verified for proper inserts using PCR amplification on primers (Supplementary Table 21) flanking the gene insertion site followed by Sanger sequencing at Quintara Bio. Verified constructs were purified using Qiagen MiniPrep kits according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by subsequent transformation into electrocompetent BL21(DE3) (New England Biolabs) cells that were used for protein expression and drug metabolism studies. A similar procedure was used to purify His-tagged NhoA from BL21 expressing a pET28a vector (Novagen), whereby NhoA was expressed using IPTG and isolated using nickel bead extraction.

Measurements of B. thetaiotaomicron nitroreductase enzyme activity in E. coli

For studies of E. coli heterologously expressing B. thetaiotaomicron nitroreductases, bacterial strains with pET22b vectors containing B. thetaiotaomicron nitroreductase inserts were streaked for single colonies on LB–ampicillin (100 µg ml−1) plates (Supplementary Table 1). Single colonies were picked and grown overnight in LB containing carbenicillin (100 µg ml−1). Cultures were then back-diluted 1:100 into 5 ml LB plus carbenicillin. Back-diluted cultures were grown aerobically at 37 °C with shaking for ~2 h until cultures reached mid-log (~0.5 OD600) at which point they were induced with 1 mM IPTG (AB00841; AmericanBio) for 2.5 h and continued to be incubated aerobically at 37 °C with shaking. Then, 1.5 ml of each culture was removed and pelleted at 3,000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed. Pellets were moved to an anaerobic chamber and resuspended with 1.5 ml pre-reduced LB medium. Of each culture, 100 µl was then aliquoted to 900 µl water plus LB medium (1:5 LB–water) containing 2 µM tolcapone in triplicate. Samples were then grown anaerobically at 37 °C without shaking for 22 h.

For sample preparation for LC–MS analysis, 30-µl aliquots were then removed at 1 h, 9 h and 22 h, followed by quenching with equal-volume ice-cold acetonitrile and frozen at −80 °C until further extraction. For further extraction, 20 µl of quenched cultures was moved to a new plate and centrifuged at 3,200 g for 20 min at 4 °C. Then, 7 µl of the supernatant was added to 7-µl internal standards diluted in water (4 µM final concentrations of caffeine, sulfamethoxazole, ipraflavone and yohimbine). Samples were further diluted with 7 µl water and analysed on LC–MS.

P1 phage transduction

To produce an nfsB mutant in the E. coli ΔbamB ΔtolC strain, lysates of an E. coli ∆nfsB::kanR strain71 were prepared for phage transduction according to previously published methods72. Specifically, E. coli ∆nfsB::kanR was grown aerobically overnight at 37 °C with shaking and back-diluted 1/50 into 6 ml fresh LB medium containing 20 mM MgCl2, 5 mM CaCl2 and 0.2% glucose. Cultures were grown aerobically at 37 °C with shaking for 45 min before being split into 2-ml cultures. Each 2-ml culture was then treated with either 0 µl, 10 µl or 50 µl of a P1 phage lysate sourced from an E. coli BW25113 wild-type strain. Cultures were then grown until those containing phage lysate appeared clear (2.5 h). The culture with 10 µl P1 lysate treatment was selected and sterile filtered with a 0.22-µm filter to generate a P1 lysate.

P1 lysates were then used to transfer the nfsB::kanR mutant genomic region to E. coli ΔbamB ΔtolC as previously described73. To conduct phage transduction, E. coli ΔbamB ΔtolC (the ‘recipient’ culture) were grown aerobically overnight at 37 °C with shaking. Then, 1 ml of this culture was spun at 5,000 g for 2 min and the supernatant was removed to isolate the cell pellet. The cell pellet was then resuspended in 300 µl fresh LB supplemented with 10 mM MgSO4 and 5 mM CaCl2 (LB supplemented (LBS)). Multiple lysate preparations were then prepared: (1) 100 µl P1 phage lysate plus 100 µl recipient culture, (2) 10 µl lysate plus 90 µl fresh LBS plus 100 µl recipient culture, (3) 100 µl LBS plus 100 µl recipient culture and (4) 100 µl P1 phage lysate plus 100 µl LBS. Conditions (3) and (4) were included as negative controls. All treatments were then incubated aerobically at 37 °C with shaking for 30 min. Samples were treated with 1 ml fresh LB and 200 µl sodium citrate (1 M) before being incubated again at 37 °C with shaking for 60 min. After this incubation, samples were centrifuged at 5,000 g for 5 min, supernatants were removed and cell pellets were resuspended in 100 µl LB supplemented with 100 mM sodium citrate. All samples were then fully plated onto LB plates containing 30 µg ml−1 kanamycin and 5 mM sodium citrate. Plates were grown for 24 h, and colonies were selected for confirmation of successful transduction and subsequent assays. Successful transduction was confirmed using PCR and Sanger sequencing. To remove the kanR cassette, both E. coli nfsB::kanR and E. coli ΔbamB ΔtolC nfsB::kanR were first made competent using a previously published protocol74. Briefly, E. coli cultures were grown overnight in LB medium containing kanamycin (50 µg ml−1). Then, 100 µl of this culture was spread on LB plates containing kanamycin using a sterile cell spreader. Inoculated plates were grown for 5 h at 37 °C aerobically, after which the bacterial lawn was scraped and resuspended in 5 ml of ice-cold, sterile water. Cells were then spun at 3,220 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and cells were resuspended in ice-cold, sterile water. This step was repeated for a total of three washes. Finally, cells were resuspended in 200 µl ice-cold, sterile water. Then, 100 µl of electrocompetent cells for both E. coli strains was transformed with pCP20 (a heat-sensitive plasmid containing an ampicillin resistance cassette) to remove the kanamycin cassette as described75. The pCP20 plasmid contains an encoded Flp enzyme, allowing the removal of the kanamycin FRT-flanked cassette from the chromosome of both E. coli nfsB::kanR and E. coli ΔbamB ΔtolC nfsB::kanR. Transformed cells were rescued in 1 ml fresh LB medium and incubated at 30 °C aerobically with shaking for 90 min. LB agar plates with 100 µg ml−1 carbenicillin were then inoculated with each culture and grown aerobically overnight at 30 °C. Multiple colonies were selected and again grown overnight without antibiotics at 42 °C to cure pCP20 from the bacterial cultures. Cured cultures were re-streaked on fresh LB agar plates without antibiotics and grown overnight at 37 °C. Finally, individual colonies were verified for removal of the kanamycin cassette through colony PCR amplification of individual colonies, as well as streaking on both kanamycin and ampicillin plates to confirm both loss of pCP20 and kanamycin sensitivity.

Drug metabolism studies with E. coli nitroreductase mutants

Method 1

In Extended Data Fig. 1, E. coli strains were cultured overnight (18 h) in LB medium. Cultures were then transferred to an anaerobic chamber and incubated with 33 µM tolcapone in triplicate and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C without shaking for 24 h. Heat-killed cultures of wild-type E. coli were heated at 95 °C for 30 min before use as controls. For LC–MS analysis, cultures were quenched with ethyl acetate, air-dried and then stored at −20 °C. Samples were thawed, resuspended with methanol and analysed via LC–MS.

Method 2

In Extended Data Fig. 2, E. coli strains were cultured aerobically for 18 h in LB medium. Cultures were transferred to an anaerobic chamber, normalized to the lowest OD600 of the set using pre-reduced LB, and 100 µl of each culture was then added to 900 µl LB diluted in water (1:5 LB–water) containing 33 µM tolcapone in triplicate and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C without shaking for 24 h. For LC–MS analysis, 20 µl of each culture was taken at 24 h and immediately quenched in 20 µl ice-cold acetonitrile. Samples were then frozen at −80 °C. Samples were thawed and centrifuged at 3,200 g for 20 min at 4 °C. Then, 10 µl of the supernatant was diluted in 10 µl water and used for LC–MS analysis.

E. coli in vitro enzymatic assays for M1 and M2 production

M1 metabolism

NfsB (Sigma-Aldrich, catalogue number N9284) was used for tolcapone metabolism studies in vitro in the following conditions: 50 µM tolcapone, 2 mM NADPH, 4 µM NfsB and 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7).

M2 metabolism

NhoA (purified from E. coli RW45) was used for tolcapone M1 metabolism studies in vitro in the following conditions: 200 µM tolcapone M1, 40 µg ml−1 NhoA, 400 µM acetyl-CoA and 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0, 1 mM fresh dithiothreitol).

For both studies, samples were incubated for 1 h before extraction using ethyl acetate, air-dried, resuspended in methanol and frozen at −80 °C until analysis via LC–MS.

Genetic manipulation of B. thetaiotaomicron

The parental (wild-type) strain of B. thetaiotaomicron in this paper is B. thetaiotaomicron VPI-5482 Δtdk (Supplementary Table 1) unless otherwise noted.

Chromosomal deletions

Genetic mutants of B. thetaiotaomicron were produced using unmarked chromosomal modification through a counter-selectable allelic exchange procedure76. To this end, engineered pExchange-tdk plasmids for each individual target gene were created using Gibson cloning. Each construct contains 500–1,000 base-pair regions flanking both upstream and downstream of the targeted nitroreductase gene, feoAB (BT_1592) or recA. Nitroreductase and recA deletion constructs included start and stop codons and no other nucleotides from the gene’s coding region, whereas the feoAB deletion construct included 250 bp of the 5′ region of feoAB followed by a stop codon, mimicking the transposon insertion disruption isolated from the directed evolution experiment. These plasmids were introduced to electrocompetent E. coli S17-1 cells, which were then used to produce transconjugants of B. thetaiotaomicron Δtdk through mating. B. thetaiotaomicron Δtdk transconjugants were then selected for merodiploids by plating on BHI–blood agar containing erythromycin (25 µg ml−1). Merodiploids were then replated on BHI–blood agar containing 200 mg ml−1 5-fluoro-2deoxy-uridine (FUdR) (Abcam) to select for loss of the pExchange plasmid. Erythromycin-sensitive colonies were isolated, and deletions were verified through Sanger sequencing of the targeted genomic region. To produce the nine-nitroreductase mutant in B. thetaiotaomicron, this process was repeated for each targeted gene.

Complementation of chromosomal deletions

To complement deletion of feoAB, pNBU2 integration methods77 were used with minor modifications. E. coli S17-1 donor strains containing the pNBU2 plasmid containing the feoAB gene with its native promoter were grown for 18 h aerobically in LB with carbenicillin. Then, 100 µl donor S17 cells was mixed with 1 ml of B. thetaiotaomicron recipient culture grown anaerobically for 18 h, spread on non-selective BHI–blood agar plates and grown for 24 h at 37 °C under aerobic conditions to allow for transfer of the pNBU2 construct. Lawns were scraped and resuspended in 1 ml 1× PBS and plated on BHI–blood agar plates containing gentamicin and erythromycin using serial dilutions to isolate single transconjugants. Transconjugants were re-streaked on BHI–blood agar plates containing gentamicin and erythromycin, followed by verification of insertion into the att1 phage attachment site using PCR amplification and gel electrophoresis.

FeoAB gene annotation

B. thetaiotaomicron feoAB (BT_1592; BT_RS08075) genetic domains were annotated using InterPro78. Graphs of domains were generated using gggenes79 and ggplot2 (ref. 80).

Ex vivo tolcapone community disruption and tolcapone metabolism assays

For ex vivo assays comparing tolcapone, M1 and DMSO disruption of human faecal communities, single aliquots of human faecal communities15 were inoculated into pre-reduced BB–mGAM (1:50). Inoculated cultures were grown anaerobically for 24 h at 37 °C. Cultures were treated by adding 15 µl of one compound (5 mM tolcapone, 5 mM M1 or DMSO) and 30 µl of each overnight culture into 1,455 µl BB–mGAM and mixed in 96-deep-well plates (SP Bel-Art, F37860-0001) in triplicate. After incubation, three communities failed to grow in all conditions (including the DMSO control) and were removed from further downstream analysis.

For sample preparation for LC–MS analysis, 30 µl of each well was transferred into a 96-V-well polypropylene storage plate (Thermo Fisher, AB1058) at 10 min, 60 min, 180 min, 360 min, 720 min and 1,440 min and stored at −80 °C. Samples were thawed on ice and briefly spun, and 10 µl of samples was added to new plates containing 5 µl of internal standard diluted in water (4 µM caffeine, sulfamethoxazole, ipraflavone and yohimbine) and 30 µl acetonitrile–methanol (50:50) for quenching. Extractions were incubated at −20 °C for 1.5 h and then centrifuged at 3,200 g at 4 °C for 20 min. After centrifugation, 10 µl of the supernatant was moved to a new plate containing 10 µl water for a final 1:1 dilution. These samples were then used for LC–MS analysis (see below).

Samples used for 16S rRNA analysis from these same community samples were taken from the overnight cultures at 0 h (1 ml) and 24 h (0.5 ml) and aliquoted to new 96-deep-well plates. Cultures were then spun at 3,200 g at 4 °C for 20 min. Supernatants were discarded, and bacterial pellets were stored at −80 °C until further processing (‘Genomic DNA extraction, 16S rRNA amplification and sequencing’).

Ex vivo tolcapone and entacapone community disruption and L-DOPA metabolism assays

For ex vivo assays assessing effects of COMT-I drugs on Enterococcus levels, tyrDC levels and L-DOPA metabolism, single-use human faecal aliquots were directly inoculated into respective treatment conditions, each containing 500 µM L-DOPA-d3 and either 50 µM tolcapone or an equivalent amount of DMSO, to a final dilution of 1:200 into BB–mGAM in 96-deep-well plates in triplicate. Treated cultures were then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C.

For experiments assessing effects of tolcapone- or entacapone-mediated community disruption on decarboxylation or deamination of L-DOPA, aliquots diluted into BB–mGAM were treated with 500 µM L-DOPA-d3 and either tolcapone, entacapone or DMSO. All experiments using only one concentration of tolcapone or entacapone were completed with 50 µM tolcapone or 200 µM entacapone.

For experiments with human communities artificially colonized with Enterococcus species, E. faecium was isolated from community MV16 by selective plating (Enterococcosel agar) followed by Sanger sequencing of the 16S rRNA region, alongside PCR verification using E. faecium (AAV 318/319) and tyrDC (AAV 306/307) specific primers (Supplementary Tables 1 and 21). Human faecal samples were diluted into BB–mGAM. Enterococcus cultures were grown overnight, diluted in 1× PBS with 0.5% cysteine and inoculated into the aforementioned samples at a 1:75,000 dilution (for E. faecalis MMH594/MMH594 tyrDC::TN) or a 1:750,000 dilution (for the E. faecium isolate). A PBS control (without Enterococcus inoculation) was also included. Samples were then treated with 500 µM L-DOPA-d3 and either 50 µM tolcapone or DMSO.

For LC–MS preparation and analysis of metabolism of L-DOPA-d3 in these community treatments, samples of 30–100 µl were removed from each treated culture, placed in a 96-V-well polypropylene storage plate (Thermo Fisher, AB1058) and kept at −80 °C until extraction (analysed time points in each experiment are specified in the figure legends). For extraction, samples were thawed on ice and 10-µl samples were diluted in 90 µl ice-cold methanol in addition to 5 µl internal standard (4 µM final concentrations of caffeine, sulfamethoxazole, ipraflavone and yohimbine) diluted in water. Extracted samples were then centrifuged at 3,200 g and 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was then removed and used for LC–MS analysis (see below).

For experiments in which qPCR was used for tyrDC relative gene abundance analysis, bacterial pellets were isolated as described above for genomic DNA.

In vitro non-enzymatic conversion of COMT-I

For anaerobic experiments, 50 µM tolcapone or 200 µM entacapone was added to sterile BB–mGAM medium containing 0 mM or 1 mM FeSO4. For aerobic experiments, 50 µM tolcapone or 200 µM entacapone was added to sterile MHBII medium containing 0 mM or 1 mM FeCl3. Time points were taken at 0 h, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 24 h and 48 h. Samples were quenched 1:9 in ice-cold methanol and placed at −20 °C for 1 h. Samples were then removed and centrifuged for 20 min at 3,220 g at 4 °C to remove any debris, and the supernatant was run on LC–MS in negative mode (see below).

E. faecalis metabolism of L-DOPA-d3

E. faecalis MMH594, MMH594 tyrDC::TN and E. faecium MV16 isolate were streaked out for single colonies on BHI–blood agar plates and grown at 37 °C anaerobically for 24 h. Four or five colonies were picked for each strain and grown anaerobically for 18 h in BB–mGAM at 37 °C. Overnight cultures were then back-diluted 1:200 in BB–mGAM containing 1 mM L-DOPA-d3 in 5-ml cultures in triplicate. After 24 h of incubation, 100 µl of treated culture was removed from each sample and stored at −80 °C until extraction. The extraction procedure for LC–MS analysis was carried out according to the method described in ‘Ex vivo tolcapone and entacapone community disruption and L-DOPA metabolism assays’.

Enterococcus CFU enumeration

For CFU enumeration of Enterococcus from in vitro or ex vivo cultures, samples were collected at specified time points and diluted to 10−7 in PBS in 10-fold intervals. With the use of square Petri dishes containing Enterococcosel agar (Becton Dickinson, BD212205), 10 µl of dilutions from 10−0 to 10−7 were plated using a spot-drip method.

For in vivo CFU enumeration, faecal samples obtained from mice were weighed and vortexed for 10 min in 1 ml sterile PBS. After the samples were vortexed, they were briefly centrifuged at 100 g for 10 min to clear debris. Supernatants were collected, diluted in PBS in 10-fold steps to 10−5. Subsequently, 10 µl of dilutions from 10−0 to 10−5 was plated on Enterococcosel plates using a spot-drip method.

After the samples were aliquoted onto plates, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h and CFUs were enumerated once colonies were visible. Enterococcosel agar allows the selection of Enterococcus isolates according to the manufacturer; however, this medium also allows the selection of other genera or species (albeit with different morphological presentations). Thus, putative Enterococcus colonies were first determined and separately enumerated according to the manufacturer’s instructions by visual inspection according to colony size, morphology and presence of a black halo around colonies, and further through isolation of single colonies, re-streaking and verification of morphological presentation. Enterococcus and tyrDC-positive colonies were further confirmed via genomic extraction and PCR for 16S rRNA specific for Enterococcus81 and presence of tyrDC22. These colonies were then tracked as Enterococcus tyrDC+ isolates for the remainder of the experiment through their morphological presentation.

Tolcapone pharmacokinetics and in vivo tolcapone metabolism analysis

For tolcapone pharmacokinetics, 18 conventional (C57BL/6N, SPF, aged 6–8 weeks, all males) and 17 germ-free (C57BL/6N, aged 7–10 weeks, 16 males and 1 female) mice were used to assess the in vivo pharmacokinetics and metabolism of tolcapone. Food was removed from each cage 2 h before gavage with 10 mg kg−1 tolcapone in sterile PBS plus 5% DMSO, which was further sterilized using a 22-μm filter. Three conventional mice and two germ-free mice were killed without tolcapone treatment to serve as controls for all subsequent assays. Five mice from each group were killed at 4 h, 8 h and 12 h, at which point duodenum and caecal contents were collected. For in vivo tolcapone metabolism, caecal samples were collected from gnotobiotic MV20-colonized mice at the time they were killed. Intestinal contents for all mice were frozen using liquid nitrogen immediately after they were killed and stored at −80 °C until extraction.

Extraction

Samples were thawed on ice, aliquoted and weighed. After the samples were weighed, acetonitrile–methanol (50:50) containing internal standard (caffeine, sulfamethoxazole, ipraflavone and yohimbine) was added at a 1:3 ratio of caecal weight to solvent. Silica beads were added before bead beating samples for 2 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 8,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant from the samples was removed and aliquoted to a 96-V-well polypropylene plate (Thermo Fisher, AB1058) and then centrifuged again for 8,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to ensure that no insoluble material remained. Then, 10 µl of this supernatant was diluted in 40 µl water and then used for LC–MS analysis (see below).

Genomic DNA extraction, 16S rRNA amplification and sequencing

For both faecal material and bacterial pellets collected from in vitro and ex vivo cultures, samples were transferred to 2-ml sterile cryotubes (SCT-200-SS-C-S, Axygen) followed by resuspension using 500 µl CP Buffer (Omega), 250 µl 20% SDS (Bio-Rad) and 550 µl 25:24:1 phenol–chloroform–isoamyl alcohol (Sigma or VWR). In addition, 200 µl of zirconia silica beads (Biospec) and 1 sterile metal ball were added to sample tubes. Samples were then homogenized using bead beating (BeadBeater) twice for 2 min followed by centrifugation at 3,200 g, 4 °C, for 20 min. Then, 100–300 µl aqueous phase was removed and processed for genomic DNA using an Omega EZ-96 Cycle Pure kit (Omega) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

For samples to be analysed for bacterial community composition, the V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced82. In brief, genomic DNA from samples was quantified using a Quant-IT PicoGreen dsDNA assay kit (Thermo Fisher) followed by normalization to 5 ng µl−1 using an EPMotion 5075 (Eppendorf). The 16S rRNA V4 variable region was amplified via PCR using barcoded primers (AccuPrimer Pfx Supermix)83. Amplified products were processed using the SequalPrep Normalization Plate Kit (Thermo Fisher) for clean-up and normalization before they were pooled. Cleaned, normalized and barcorded PCR products were then pooled for sequencing at the Yale Center for Genome Analysis. Sequencing was completed on an Illumina MiSeq using the following parameters: 2 × 250 bp, dual 8 bp indexing and 15% PhiX spike-in.

16S rRNA analysis

Reads from Illumina MiSeq sequencing were analysed via QIIME2 (ref. 84) with additional packages85,86. First, QIIME v1.8 was used to extract barcodes from sequenced reads, with all further downstream analysis completed using a combination of QIIME2 v2022.8, QIIME2 v2020.11 and QIIME2 v2024.10. Emp-paired was used to demultiplex reads, followed by truncation (220 for forward reads, 180 for reverse reads) and denoising using DADA2 (ref. 87). Next, alpha and beta diversity were determined using the QIIME2 core-metrics-phylogenetics function with a sampling depth of 10,000. All downstream analysis was conducted using a 10,000 read cut-off. A Ribosomal Database Project naive Bayesian classifier trained with National Center for Biotechnology Information Reference Sequence was used for taxonomic assignments at a default confidence cut-off of 0.7. Fold change of genera in Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 7a was calculated by adding a pseudocount of 1 to all samples from communities with detectable levels of the respective genus in at least one of the treatments and normalizing data to the DMSO sample mean.

Alpha diversity calculations for each community–treatment pairing, represented by Shannon diversity, were completed using qiime2R88 (v 0.99.6) software in RStudio (v 2023.06.01).

Bray–Curtis beta dissimilarity vector calculations were performed using QIIME2 ASV outputs in CODYN89,90 software in RStudio (v 2023.06.01). Briefly, centroids were determined for each set of replicates from the same community–treatment pair based on shared ASV composition and abundance. Centroids were then compared with one another (for example, tolcapone versus M1) for each individual community to produce a distance vector between each pairing. Distance metrics were then used to calculate a dissimilarity factor based on distance between treatments for each community. To further assess beta diversity, we analysed the effect of treatment on microbial community structure while controlling for baseline community differences. We performed an RDA to create a constrained ordination plot using vegan (v2.6-6)91. Feature abundances were transformed via Hellinger transformation before ordination. The statistical significance of the overall model, the constrained axes and the treatment terms were assessed using a permutation-based ANOVA with 999 permutations.

Plotting of relative abundance fold changes of genera between tolcapone and DMSO treatment was completed using ASV data imported into Morpheus92 using its online plugin. Hierarchical clustering for human faecal samples was also completed using Morpheus by comparing shared genera relative abundance changes between communities.

qPCR from extracted genomic DNA

qPCR was performed using genomic DNA extracted from both in vitro and in vivo samples, targeting relative gene abundance of tyrDC, E. faecalis-specific 16S rRNA and universal 16S rRNA using a CFX96 thermocycler (Bio-Rad). All qPCRs were completed using a KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR MasterMix (2×) kit (KAPABiosytems). Extracted gDNA was diluted in nuclease-free water (1:100 dilution for detection of 16S universal rRNA, E. faecalis and E. faecium, and a 1:50 dilution for detection of tyrDC gene abundance) and used as templates for each qPCR, which were completed in technical duplicate. tyrDC relative gene abundances were analysed using previously published methods and primers22 (Supplementary Table 21). Primers used for quantification of E. faecalis and E. faecium were previously validated and published93 (Supplementary Table 21).

Relative gene or species abundances were calculated using 2−∆∆Ct. Previously published universal primers for 16S rRNA94 (Supplementary Table 21) were used to normalize tyrDC, E. faecalis and E. faecium abundances. Data reporting fold change of gene or species abundances were calculated using 2−∆∆Ct differences between groups of averaged replicates. Samples or replicates without detected gene or species abundances were removed from the dataset.

RNA-seq experimental methods

E. coli WO153 was used for measuring E. coli transcriptomic response to tolcapone and M1. Previous literature describes this strain (AB1157; asmB1 tolC::kan) as sensitive to various antibiotics due to lack of TolC-related efflux and increased permeability95. Experiments were conducted as described96 with slight modifications. The MIC95 of tolcapone was determined for E. coli WO153 using growth inhibition assays (see above). E. coli WO153 was then grown aerobically with shaking for 18 h at 37 °C in MHBII. This culture was then back-diluted (1:1,000), grown to mid-log phase (~0.5 OD600) and split into 5-ml triplicate cultures for each treatment. Triplicate cultures were treated with either 1× MIC95 tolcapone (25 µM), tolcapone M1 (25 µM) or DMSO for 30 min. After 30 min, cultures were pelleted at 3,200 g for 5 min at 4 °C, washed with RNAprotect Bacteria Reagent (76506, Qiagen), vortexed briefly and pelleted again. The supernatant was removed and pellets were stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

B. thetaiotaomicron VPI-5482 Δtdk was used for measuring B. thetaiotaomicron transcriptomic response to tolcapone and M1. B. thetaiotaomicron was grown anaerobically without shaking for 18 h at 37 °C in TYG, back-diluted (1:250), grown to mid-log phase (~0.3–0.5 OD600) and split into 5-ml triplicate cultures for each treatment. Triplicate cultures were treated with either 50 µM tolcapone, 50 µM M1 or DMSO for 60 min. After 60 min, cultures were pelleted at 3,200 g for 5 min at 4 °C, washed with RNAprotect Bacteria Reagent (76506, Qiagen), vortexed briefly and pelleted again. The supernatant was removed, and pellets were stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

For RNA extraction, cell pellets were thawed on ice. After the pellets were thawed, 200 µl of freshly prepared lysis buffer (5 ml sterile filtered 1× TE buffer, pH 8.0; 5 mg lysozyme; and 25 µl 20 mg ml−1 proteinase K) was added to each pellet. Pellets were incubated at 22 °C for 10 min with brief vortexing every 2 min. Subsequently, 700 µl RLT buffer (Qiagen), 10 µL β-ME and 500 µl 100% molecular-grade ethanol (VWR) were added to each sample. RNA was then isolated using an RNeasy mini spin kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was eluted using RNase-free water and quantified by NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher). Then, 10 µg of RNA for each sample was treated with DNase (TURBO DNA Free Kit, Thermo Fisher) by adding 58.5 µl DNase-free water, 6.5 µl 10× DNase I buffer and 1 µl DNase I. Samples were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, at which point an additional 1 µl DNase was added, and the samples were mixed and incubated for an additional 30 min. Then, 13 µl of DNase-inactivating reagent was added and the samples were incubated for 2 min at room temperature and spun at 10,000 g for 1.5 min. Supernatant containing RNA was moved to a new tube, where RNA was then isolated again using an RNeasy mini spin kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was stored at −80 °C until submission for RNA-seq.

RNA-seq was conducted at the Yale Center for Genome Analysis. Samples were processed by rRNA depletion, normalized and sequenced using a NovaSeq6000 instrument producing single-end, 75-bp reads for E. coli samples, while B. thetaiotaomicron samples were sequenced using paired-end 150-bp reads. All raw sequencing data have been deposited under BioProject accession PRJNA1237363.

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA-seq transcriptomic data were processed and analysed by first aligning the unpaired reads to the genome sequences of a closely related, publicly available E. coli strain (MG1655, GCA_000005845.2_ASM584v2) or the B. thetaiotaomicron genome (VPI-5482, GCF_000011065.1_ASM1106v1) using BowTie2 (ref. 97). Each sample was manually verified to ensure that alignment was occurring at acceptable rates (>89% overall alignment rate). Before BowTie2 alignment, B. thetaiotaomicron samples required further processing of adaptor regions using TrimGalore (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). Files were then converted from bam to sam format and indexed using SAMtools98. Aligned reads per gene were then determined using Deseq2 (ref. 99), which additionally was used to calculate fold change and determine adjusted P values for genes between treatments. Further plotting and analysis of differential transcriptomic response of Fur-regulon genes were completed in Excel, GraphPad Prism and RStudio (v 2023.06.01). Significantly differentially expressed genes were then identified in E. coli samples (log2(fold change (FC)) ≥ 1 or ≤−1, Padj (adjusted P value) < 0.05) and B. thetaiotaomicron samples (log2(FC) ≥ 1 or ≤−1, Padj < 0.01).

Previous data regarding E. coli Fur regulon expression changes36 or B. thetaiotaomicron transcriptomic response to iron starvation from BIP treatment37 were used to evaluate iron starvation responses in each respective organism. For B. thetaiotaomicron, we re-analysed the published dataset37 to determine differentially expressed genes (log2(FC) ≥ 1 or ≤−1, Padj < 0.01). We then determined whether differentially expressed genes in this dataset were also significantly differentially expressed in our dataset (log2(FC) ≥ 1 or ≤−1, Padj < 0.01) and annotated those genes based on whether they were increased or decreased during 150 µM BIP treatment in the published report37. Genes potentially related to a protein aggregation response in B. thetaiotaomicron were manually curated based on previous annotation of genes100,101.

qRT-PCR for B. thetaiotaomicron response to tolcapone exposure

Cultures of B. thetaiotaomicron were grown overnight (18 h) anaerobically at 37 °C followed by a 1:200 back-dilution in TYG medium. When cultures reached the mid-log phase (OD ~0.3), they were separated into 3-ml aliquots in triplicate for the following treatments: 50 µM tolcapone, 50 µM M1 or DMSO. Treated cultures were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C anaerobically, at which point the samples were collected and processed for RNA (‘RNA-seq experimental methods’). RNA was then reverse transcribed using SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher catalogue number 18064014) and hexameric random primers (Life Technologies catalogue number N8080127) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative analysis using real-time PCR was completed using KAPA Sybr Fast Universal qPCR mix (Roche Diagnostics catalogue number 07959389001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions using a CFX Opus Real-time PCR System (Bio-Rad catalogue number 17007991). Primers can be found in Supplementary Table 21. Data reporting fold change of gene or species abundances was calculated using 2−∆∆Ct differences between groups of averaged replicates, normalized to the DMSO controls.

Bioaccumulation assay

Tolcapone and M1 bioaccumulation in E. coli strains (BW25113 and BW25113 ΔbamB ΔtolC) was conducted using a previously published method102. Briefly, cells grown overnight in LB were back-diluted and grown again to the mid-log phase (~0.55 OD600), pelleted and resuspended in PBS. Cultures were then plated for CFUs to normalize compound levels measured by LC–MS. Cultures were then split into aliquots in triplicate, 10 µM tolcapone or M1 was added, and the cultures were incubated for 10 min followed by washing (or ‘filtration’) through silicone oil (1:9 AR20 (10836, Sigma-Aldrich): high-temperature silicone oil (175633, Sigma-Aldrich)). Cells were then lysed using three freeze–thaw cycles and pelleted, and the lysate was collected. The pellet was resuspended in methanol and centrifuged, and the supernatant (processed cell pellet) was collected. For each sample, the lysate and the processed cell pellet were mixed and injected on LC–MS.

For B. thetaiotaomicron strains (Δtdk (parental strain), ΔfeoAB, ΔfeoAB att1::feoAB) and B. fragilis evolved isolates, the same process was followed with the exception that all culturing was completed anaerobically before the preparation of cellular lysates completed outside of the chamber. Time points for B. thetaiotaomicron include 10 min, 30 min and 60 min, while B. fragilis isolates were tested at 30 min of tolcapone exposure. CFUs were enumerated as described above.

General experimental procedures

UV–vis spectra were obtained using an Agilent 1260 Infinity system equipped with a photo diode array detector (Agilent Technologies). nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) datasets were acquired on an Agilent 600 MHz NMR spectrometer (DD2) equipped with an inverse cold probe (3 mm), using standard Agilent NMR pulse libraries. LC–MS analysis was achieved on an Agilent 1260 Infinity system using a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 (100 Å) 5 μm (4.6 × 150 mm) column and a photo diode array detector coupled with a single quadrupole electrospray ionization mass spectrometry instrument (Agilent 6120B). Purification of tolcapone M1 and M2 was performed using an Agilent Prepstar HPLC system with a Phenomenex Luna C18(2) (100 Å) 10 μm (10 × 250 mm) column and an Agilent fraction collector (440-FC). Fractions were dried using Genevac HT-4X.

LC–MS analysis

Extracts sourced from either in vitro, in vivo or ex vivo metabolism assays were analysed using an Agilent 1200 UHPLC system and 6550 QTOF LC-ESI/MS. All samples were kept at 4 °C before injection for each experiment.

For tolcapone metabolism studies of human gut faecal communities and E. coli mutant strains, a set of C18 Kinetex Evo (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7-mm particle size with guard columns and filters, Phenomenex) columns were used in an alternating, dual-injection format with 5-µl injections of samples. For the main pump, the LC method is the following: 0–0.5 min 0% B (100% A), 0.5–7.30 min 0–95% B, 7.30–7.50 min 95% B, 7.50–7.90 min 95–0% B and 7.9–8.5 min 0% B; flow rate 0.4 ml min−1; A = water + 0.1% formic acid; and B = acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The recovery method for the non-injecting column is the following: 0–2 min 0–95% B, 2–5.5 min 95% B, 5.5–6.5 min 95–0% B and 6.5–8.5 min 0% B; flow rate 0.4 ml min−1; A = water + 0.1% formic acid; and B = acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. Parameters for the quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QTOF-MS) source are as follows: positive scanning mode, gas temperature: 225 °C, drying gas: 13 l min−1, nebulizer: 20 psi, sheath gas temperature: 225 °C, sheath gas flow: 12 l min−1, VCap: 3,500 V and nozzle voltage: 2,000 V.

For tolcapone metabolism studies of single species in vitro and detection of tolcapone metabolism in mouse caecal contents, a single C18 Kinetex Evo (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7-mm particle size with guard columns and filters, Phenomenex) column was used for UHPLC separation of compounds. The LC method for these samples is as follows: 0–0.1 min 0% B (100% A), 0.1–5.5 min 0–95% B, 5.5–7.50 min 95% B and 7.50–10.5 min; post-time = 1 min; flow rate 0.4 ml min−1; A = water + 0.1% formic acid; and B = acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. Source parameters were identical to those of the human gut faecal community tolcapone metabolism experiments described above.

For analysis of tolcapone metabolism by E. coli strains with heterologous expression of B. thetaiotaomicron nitroreductases, B. thetaiotaomicron strains carrying gene deletions, and bioaccumulation and metabolism of tolcapone and tolcapone M1 in E. coli strains, samples were analysed as above except that solvent B consisted of methanol plus 0.1% formic acid.

For monitoring L-DOPA-d3 metabolism by single species and human faecal microbiome communities, a single C18 Kinetex Evo (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7-mm particle size with guard columns and filters, Phenomenex) column was used to separate 5-µl injections of samples. The LC method for these samples is as follows: 0–9 min 0–95% B, 9–12 min 95% B and 12–16 min 95–0% B; post time = 2 min; flow rate 0.2 ml min−1; A = water + 0.1% formic acid; and B = acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. QTOF-MS source parameters are identical to those of methods described above, except in negative ion scanning mode.

For analysis of tolcapone, entacapone and their metabolites in iron-supplemented culture medium, samples were run using the method described for tolcapone metabolism studies of single species in vitro, except in negative scanning mode.

Online calibration for positive scanning methods was conducted using a persistent injection and monitoring of masses 121.050873 m/z and 922.009798 m/z. For negative scanning methods, online calibration was conducted using persistent injection and monitoring of masses 119.036 m/z and 966.0007 m/z. For each method, standards (if available) for compounds being quantified were run in sample matrix lacking those compounds to determine the correct retention time of the compound of interest.

MassHunter Quantitative (version B.07.01/Build 7.1.524.0 for QTOF) was used to integrate ion intensities for all samples in all experiments.

Identification of tolcapone metabolites produced by E. faecalis

E. faecalis was cultivated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h for growth and then incubated in the presence or absence of 10 μM tolcapone under anaerobic conditions for an additional 24 h at 37 °C. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Following incubation, the resulting cultures (50 μl each) were chemically quenched with an equal volume of methanol (HPLC grade, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were centrifuged (Centrifuge 5420, Eppendorf) at 15,871 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and 5 μl of the supernatants was subjected to LC–QTOF-MS. LC–QTOF-MS data were obtained using an Agilent iFunnel 6550 QTOF-MS equipped with an ESI source. The instrument was coupled to an Agilent 1290 Infinity HPLC system and a Phenomenex Luna C18 (2) column (100 Å, 10 μm, 10.0 × 250 mm2). Separation was performed using a water–acetonitrile gradient method containing 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 0.7 ml min−1. The gradient conditions were 0–12 min, 10–100% acetonitrile. The resulting paired chromatograms (E. faecalis with tolcapone and E. faecalis alone) were analysed using comparative metabolomics with an XCMS-based molecular feature extraction algorithm (XCMS Online) using a previously published methodology104. The raw Lme knC-QTOF-MS data files (E. faecalis with tolcapone and E. faecalis alone) were converted to ‘mzXML’ format using MSConvert. The ‘Pairwise’ mode of XCMS was used to analyse two sets of triplicate samples: E. faecalis alone and E. faecalis with tolcapone. The default ‘HPLC/QTOF’ parameters were selected. The threshold for maximum intensity (‘maxint’) of the selected chemical features was set at ‘greater or equal’ to 1 × 105 ‘intensity’, resulting in 3,281 molecular features rank-ordered by intensity. Volcano plots were created by plotting log2(FC) of E. faecalis with tolcapone/E. faecalis alone) against the corresponding −log10(P value). Molecular features of interest were selected based on a fold change of at least 16 (log2(FC) ≥ 4) and a statistical significance threshold of P value ≤ 0.01 (−log10(P value) ≥ 2). A total of 38 candidate molecular features were identified based on the selection criteria. A comprehensive evaluation of the candidate molecular features was conducted through manual inspection using two approaches: (1) internal fragments, isotopes and ion adducts (such as [M+Na]+) associated with each molecular feature were grouped into a single molecular identity, and (2) molecular features sharing a fragment ion with tolcapone (fragment d, m/z 119.0491; Fig. 1e) in MS–MS were recognized as potential tolcapone metabolites and were prioritized. The final list of drug metabolites, including three potential tolcapone metabolites, was obtained following the complete molecular feature selection pipeline.

In vivo community disruption experiments

Allometric dose scaling

To determine appropriate drug dosing for mice, we used an allometric scaling approach105–107. For example, maximum dosing of tolcapone in humans is 3 doses of 200 mg (600 mg d−1). In a 60-kg human, 600 mg d−1 equates to 10 mg kg−1 d−1; allometric scaling suggests 123 mg kg−1 d−1 for 20-g mice as the animal equivalent dose. To ensure that drug dosing was equivalent to or lower than the animal equivalent dose suggested by allometric scaling, we used twice-daily oral gavage of 30 mg kg−1 tolcapone (60 mg kg−1 d−1) or tolcapone-supplemented chow estimated to provide 90 mg kg−1 d−1.

Conventional mouse experiments

A total of 10 female specific-pathogen-free C57BL6/NTac mice aged 6–8 weeks (Taconic Biosciences) were acclimated to LabDiet chow (catalogue number 5K67) for 1 week before treatment. After acclimation, the mice were weighed once daily and treated twice daily via intragastric gavage with either a vehicle control (PBS plus 5% DMSO) or 30 mg kg−1 tolcapone suspension in PBS plus 5% DMSO. Faecal samples were collected at the time of each gavage (2× daily), followed by immediate preparation and plating for CFUs on Enterococcosel selective agar (‘Enterococcus CFU enumeration’). The mice were treated for 5 days. This experiment was repeated and data from all mice were combined for analysis.

Gnotobiotic mouse experiments

For each human faecal microbiome community (MV12, MV20 and MV25), two replicate experiments were completed, each with ten germ-free C57BL6N/Tac mice (7–9 weeks old, male and female) co-housed according to gender and litter. The mice were first acclimated to the control chow diet (Inotiv catalogue number TD.110180) for 1 week. After 1 week, each mouse was colonized with 100 µl human faecal glycerol stock. The faecal community was allowed 1 week to stably colonize the mice, and the Enterococcus colonization status was monitored with selective Enterococcosel plating (‘Enterococcus CFU enumeration’). During this time, bedding swaps between all cages were performed daily. After 1 week, the mice were tested for faecal Enterococcus levels before separation of groups for treatments. These data were used to split mice into two groups of five mice (unless otherwise noted), with each group representing an equivalent distribution of initial Enterococcus colonization. The mice were then singly caged, with one group receiving control chow and the other group received chow containing 360 mg kg−1 tolcapone (Inotiv custom diet, TD.110180 with 360 mg kg−1 tolcapone, catalogue number 230455). Estimated dosing of tolcapone chow for each mouse is ~90 mg kg−1 d−1. The mice were then monitored for faecal Enterococcus CFUs every 3–4 days. After 28 days of treatment, MV20-colonized mice were killed with intestinal contents and blood plasma collected and stored at −80 °C.

Ileal Enterococcus colonization experiments using MV25-colonized mice were conducted identical to other gnotobiotic experiments with minor alterations. After colonization with human faecal aliquots, groups of mice were kept on either control or tolcapone-containing chow for either 14 days, 28 days or 42 days. At each of these time points, the respective mouse groups were killed and distal ileal contents were collected and processed for CFU enumeration as above.

Statistical analysis

To analyse the effect of treatment on tyrDC+ Enterococcus levels over time in the context of different communities in gnotobiotic mice, the lme4 (ref. 108) package in R was used to fit a linear mixed-effects model to log10-transformed CFU counts with an added pseudocount of 1. Fixed effects in the model were set as treatment, time, community, sex, experiment batch and all their interactions. A random intercept was included for each mouse (based on unique mouse IDs) to account for repeated measures (1/mouse). Using the lmerTest109 package in R, P values were calculated using Satterthwaite’s method. Finally, we verified assumptions of our model through manual assessment of residual diagnostic plots.

B. thetaiotaomicron survival assay

B. thetaiotaomicron VPI-5482 Δtdk was grown overnight and back-diluted to OD600 0.025 in the presence of varying levels of tolcapone (0–200 µM) in a 96-well culture plate (Corning) in triplicate. The inoculum used for all conditions was diluted in sterile PBS down to 10−7, and 10 µl of dilutions 100 to 10−7 were plated on BHI–blood agar plates using a spot-drip method and grown anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. The culture plate was grown 18 h, at which point OD600 was assessed for each well. MIC95 was determined by comparing the OD600 of each treatment to the no-drug control. For treated cultures at or above the MIC95, each replicate was diluted in sterile PBS down to 10−7. Then, 10 µl of dilutions 100 to 10−7 was plated on BHI–blood agar plates using a spot-drip method and grown anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Colonies were enumerated for each condition, and percentage survival was calculated by dividing the CFU ml−1 of each tolcapone condition replicate by the mean of the initial inoculum CFU ml−1.

B. thetaiotaomicron time-kill assay

B. thetaiotaomicron Δtdk (wild type) or B. thetaiotaomicron Δtdk ΔfeoAB was grown anaerobically in TYG overnight (18 h), back-diluted 1:4 into fresh TYG medium and grown an additional 2 h. Cultures were then back-diluted to OD ~0.01 (~107 CFU ml−1) in 1 ml TYG in a 96-deep-well plate in the presence of DMSO or BIP (50 µM) in the presence of varying tolcapone concentrations (0 µM, 25 µM, 100 µM or 400 µM). Cultures were then incubated anaerobically at 37 °C with periodic CFU plating (‘B. thetaiotaomicron survival assay’).

UV–vis spectrophotometric assay for iron binding with tolcapone, M1 and M2

UV–vis spectra were recorded with a spectrophotometer (Avantes AvaSpec-ULS2048CL-EVO). The spectrophotometer was first calibrated using a blank with pure methanol. Before the analysis, 100 µg ml−1 of tolcapone, M1 or M2 was incubated in methanol at room temperature for 10 min with varying concentrations of added FeSO4 heptahydrate (10–500 µg ml−1) or FeCl3 (10µg ml−1 or 50 µg ml−1). Spectra were normalized to values between 0 and 1 based on the maximum height of each peak and manually graphed.

Directed evolution experiments

Directed evolution in E. coli

Directed evolution experiments involving E. coli were completed using a BW25113 ΔbamB ΔtolC ΔnfsB mutant, which is sensitive to tolcapone and lacks a known enzyme that could be readily mutated to provide protection (nfsB). BW25113 ΔbamB ΔtolC ΔnfsB was streaked onto an LB agar plate, where five colonies were selected and placed into separate tubes containing MHBII. These colonies, deemed ‘ancestors’, were grown for 24 h shaking (200 rpm) at 37 °C. Ancestor cultures were then split into two lines of passaging (tolcapone treated or DMSO treated) through a 1:1,000 dilution. Tolcapone-treated cultures included four different tolcapone concentrations (6.25 µM, 12.5 µM, 25 µM, 50 µM) while DMSO-treated cultures contained added DMSO as a negative control (all cultures had equivalent DMSO concentrations). Every 24 h for 10 days, cultures with visible turbidity at the highest tolcapone treatments were selected for further passaging; DMSO-treated cultures were passaged in parallel. Tolcapone-treated and DMSO-treated cultures were passaged 1:1000 into MHBII medium, with tolcapone concentrations increasing in coordination with 24 h turbidity. Final tolcapone concentrations reached 200 µM by the end of the experiment. On the final day of the experiment, both tolcapone- and DMSO-treated populations were plated onto LB agar plates. Single colonies (evolved isolates) were then selected and grown individually in MHBII medium overnight (18 h). Cultures were either processed for genomic DNA followed by whole-genome sequencing or used in growth assays in the presence of tolcapone (50 µM).

Directed evolution in B. fragilis

A similar approach was used for directed evolution studies of B. fragilis. In brief, B. fragilis Δtdk was initially plated onto BHI–blood agar plates for single colonies, five of which were then cultured in TYG as ancestral cultures. Passaging was completed every 24 h for 12 days using a dilution of 1:250. Tolcapone exposure of tolcapone-treated populations began at 12.5–100 µM and ended at 50–200 µM. Day 12 populations of both tolcapone- and DMSO-treated cultures were streaked onto BHI–blood plates, where single colonies were cultured and used for genomic DNA isolation and whole-genome sequencing, measurement of tolcapone and entacapone resistance, and bioaccumulation assays.

Directed evolution in B. thetaiotaomicron

A similar approach was used for directed evolution studies of B. thetaiotaomicron. In brief, two ancestral populations of B. thetaiotaomicron Δtdk were passaged for 12 days as above. On day 12, single isolates were cultured and used for genomic DNA isolation, whole-genome sequencing and measurement of tolcapone resistance.

Whole-genome sequencing and chromosomal mutational analysis of evolved bacterial isolates

For whole-genome sequencing of isolates from directed evolution experiments, genomic DNA was prepared from bacterial pellets of each isolate as previously described (‘Genomic DNA extraction, 16S rRNA amplification and sequencing’). Purified genomic DNA was sequenced by the Yale Center for Genomic Analysis using 150-bp paired-end sequencing on a NovaSeq instrument (Illumina) with a final coverage of 40× per genome. Quality control of sequencing reads was completed using FastQC110. For assembly and initial mutational analysis, reads were aligned to the reference genome for each species, respectively (National Center for Biotechnology Information Reference Sequence for each: E. coli BW25113, CP009273.1; B. fragilis NCTC 9343, CR626927; and B. thetaiotaomicron VPI-5482, NC_004663.1), using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner111 followed by assessment of mutations compared with the reference genomes using PILON112. Additional analysis was completed using BreSeq to align and determine consensus on mutations among reads in each isolate113.

Bacteroides growth in minimal media with defined iron concentrations

Bacteroides minimal medium (‘Bacterial culture conditions’) was produced using iron-replete water. All materials used were iron-free plastics. Ultrapure water (MilliQ Type 1 Ultrapure Water Systems, MilliporeSigma) was treated with Chelex 100Na salts (MilliporeSigma, catalogue number C7901) (2 g l−1) for 1 h while mixing at 22 °C. Water was then filtered through 0.22-µM filters (Millipore Stericup Quick Release Vacuum Filtration System 500 ml, MilliporeSigma, catalogue number S2GPU05RE) before being used for preparation of all reagents needed for Bacteroides minimal medium. The culture medium was prepared without FeSO4 and haematin and moved to anaerobic conditions >12 h before the experiments. Before incubation, B. thetaiotaomicron or B. fragilis strains were grown overnight on BHI–blood agar plates. Single colonies were then used to inoculate minimal medium with added 10 µM FeSO4 and 1.9 µM haematin. Cultures were grown for 24 h owing to slow growth in iron-limited conditions, especially for feoAB mutants. After 24 h of culturing, 1 ml of each culture was subcultured in 3 ml fresh minimal medium containing 10 µM FeSO4 (no haematin added). Cultures were then grown for another 3 h, at which point they were back-diluted 1:50 in minimal medium containing 10 µM FeSO4. Subsequently, 96-well growth plates containing Bacteroides minimal medium with either 10 µM FeSO4 and no added haematin, or 10 µM FeSO4 plus 0.39 µM haematin, were prepared. Back-diluted cultures were then added to the plate at a 1:1 ratio (90 µl culture and 90 µl medium) for a final dilution of cells to 1:100. Final concentrations of haematin for the supplemented iron condition was 0.22 µM, based on residual haematin plus 0.195 µM of added haematin, while the low iron condition was 0.015 µM based on residual haematin after dilutions. Cultures were then grown for 24–48 h. Wells were monitored for OD600 absorbance every 15 min with brief shaking (10 s before OD measurements) using double orbital shaking in either a BioTek Epoch2 or BioTek Eon microplate spectrophotometer. BioTek Gen5 software was used to operate spectrophotometers and retrieve data. Background absorbance based on media control wells was subtracted from each measurement.

ICP–MS analysis of iron content in B. thetaiotaomicron

All samples in these experiments used metal-free plastics and/or metal-free Teflon vials for processing. B. thetaiotaomicron cultures were grown anaerobically overnight (18 h) in deferrated minimal medium (‘Bacteroides growth in minimal media with defined iron concentrations’) with iron reintroduced (1.4 µM FeSO4 and 1.9 µM haematin). Cultures were subcultured 1:20 into minimal medium containing similar iron concentrations and grown to the mid-log phase (between 0.3 and 0.6 OD600) in pre-weighed metal-free plastic conical vials. Cultures were then split into biological triplicates and treated with respective compounds (25 µM tolcapone, 25 µM M1, 50 µM BIP or DMSO) for 1 h at 37 °C. After 1 h, cultures were tested for CFU ml−1 by plating on BHI–HK plates (‘B. thetaiotaomicron survival assay’). Remaining cultures were then pelleted at 3,220 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Cell pellets were washed with 1× PBS three times, pelleting each time. After the final wash, the pellets were weighed and then frozen at −80 °C until further processing.

For processing of samples for Inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) analysis, cell pellets were thawed and treated with 300 µl trace-metal-grade nitric acid (Fisher Scientific, number A509P500) and 100 µl trace-metal-grade hydrogen peroxide (MilliporeSigma, number 11-101-9705) and transferred to Teflon vials. The reaction was then carried out overnight and allowed to evaporate at 85 °C, leaving behind a dry residue. The samples were then resuspended in 2 ml 3.5% nitric acid in UltraPure water (Thermo Fisher, number 10977015). The samples were either spiked with 1 part per billion (ppb) indium or scandium as an internal standard for normalization. The samples were then analysed via MC–ICP–MS (Thermo Fisher Element XR). A multi-elemental standard solution was diluted to various degrees to create a calibration curve for each element. After internal standardization and concentration calculation, samples were then normalized based on both the mass of the pellet and the CFU ml−1 of the sample.

Intracellular Fe2+ detection in B. thetaiotaomicron via fluorescent probe

B. thetaiotaomicron Δtdk was cultured for 24 h anaerobically in defined medium with 1.4 µM FeSO4 and 1.9 µM haematin (‘Bacteroides growth in minimal media with defined iron concentrations’). The strain was then subcultured 1:20 in minimal medium with 1.4 µM FeSO4 and 1.9 µM haematin until it reached mid-log growth (OD600 ~0.4). This culture was then split into 3-ml aliquots treated with DMSO, 25 µM tolcapone or 50 µM BIP for 1 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cultures were then washed three times in anoxic, sterile 1× PBS pH 7.4 (spin 3,220 g 5 min at 4 °C). Cultures were then resuspended in 3 ml 1× PBS and split into triplicate tubes of 1 ml each. CFU were determined at this point through dilution and spot plating method as before. HMRhoNox-M, an Fe(II)-selective fluorescent probe (Lumiprobe, number 3317), was then added to each replicate at a final concentration of 1 µM. Cultures were incubated covered from light at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were then washed once more with 1× PBS and resuspended in 1 ml fresh PBS. Samples were placed in a 96-well plate (Corning, number 3631), and fluorescence was determined using a SpectraMax i3x (Molecular Devices) using excitation of 555 nm and emission detection at 575 nm.

While we note that fluorescent probes used to detect iron speciation should be interpreted with caution, our results are in agreement with previous studies on COMT-I entacapone and its effect on iron speciation29.

Protein aggregation experiments

Both E. coli ΔbamB ΔtolC were studied for protein aggregation after tolcapone exposure due to precedence in using E. coli for protein aggregation studies114,115. Cultures were processed using previously published methods with modifications114,115. E. coli cultures were grown overnight (18 h) in MHBII, subcultured (1:200) and grown to the mid-log phase (OD600 ~0.4). Then, 5-ml aliquots of mid-log cultures were treated with varying concentrations of tolcapone, 400 µM M1 or DMSO (all with the same final percentage of DMSO in media). Cultures were then incubated for 30 min at 37 °C aerobically with shaking. After 30 min, cultures were pelleted at 3,220 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Cell pellets were washed once with 1× PBS and pelleted again. Cell pellets were then placed on ice and resuspended in 120 µl buffer A (50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mg ml−1 lysozyme and 20% sucrose). Pellets were incubated on ice for 30 min. Buffer B (Tris pH 7.4 plus 1 mM EDTA) was added to each lysate followed by sonication (1 s on, 1 s off, 10 cycles, 25% amplitude; Qsonica Q500). Unlysed cells were then pelleted at 2,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed from the unlysed cells and moved to a new tube. The supernatant was then subjected to centrifugation at 17,000 g for 25 min at 4 °C with the aim of pelleting protein aggregates. The pellet was washed with buffer C (buffer B plus 2% NP-40) and centrifuged at 17,000 g for 35 min at 4 °C at which point this step was repeated. At the end, there were only visible pellets in tolcapone-treated samples. Regardless of pellet visibility, each sample was resuspended in 50 µl reducing buffer (1:3 4× Bolt LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Fisher, B0008): buffer B with 10% beta-mercaptoethanol), boiled for 10 min at 95 °C and analysed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE). In Extended Data Fig. 5e, soluble protein fractions represent material collected after lysis of cells and before isolation of protein aggregates.

Iron measurements in caecal material and media

Total iron was detected in caecal material from mice or from fresh bacterial media using a colorimetric Iron Assay Kit (MAK025, Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For caecal detection, material was weighed and dissolved in provided buffer. For all samples, provided iron reducer was used to transform any ferric iron (Fe3+) to ferrous iron (Fe2+). An iron probe was added to associate with all iron, at which point this association allows for detection at an absorbance of 593 nm. Samples were quantified by comparison to an iron standard curve prepared in parallel.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 or R (v4.3.1). Details related to individual statistical analyses can be found in the figure legends of each associated dataset, in each respective methods section or in supplementary tables. Data are representative of at least two independent experiments. A bar is used to represent either median or mean values, as indicated in the respective figure legends. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes of the experiments. For mice used in gnotobiotic experiments, groups were determined using tyrDC+ Enterococcus CFU g−1 enumeration 1 day and 2 days before treatment with chow diets. Mice were grouped to evenly distribute Enterococcus colonization levels across both groups. Some data were excluded from analysis. For in vitro studies, growth curves were excluded if there were aberrations in growth detection unrelated to the growth of the bacteria (for example, a spike in OD reading due to liquid on the lid above the well). For ex vivo studies using human faecal communities, three faecal communities were excluded (MV 13, MV18, MV23) owing to poor growth even in vehicle-treated control cultures. 16S rRNA sequencing data were excluded if reads were below the threshold of 10,000 reads per sample. For in vivo studies, mice were excluded only if they were required to be killed before the end of the experiment owing to pre-established protocols for humane use of animals (that is, hunched posture, immobile, scruffy fur, weight loss).

Figures were generated using GraphPad 9, RStudio (v2023.06.01), ChemDraw (PerkinElmer, v19.1) or BioRender online software (https://www.Biorender.com).
