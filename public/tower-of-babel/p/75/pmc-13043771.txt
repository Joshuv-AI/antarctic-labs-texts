Unlocking the potential of computational phenotypic drug discovery: methods, challenges, and future directions
Authors: Shivam Kumar, Aman Pal, Samrat Chatterjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phenotypic drug discovery (PDD) identifies new drugs by observing the effects of compounds on living systems without prior knowledge of their targets. Advances in biological data and machine learning have made PDD more systematic and data-driven. This review outlines a computational framework, including phenotype representations, key tools, and public datasets. It also discusses major challenges and strategies to improve PDD’s efficiency and translational potential, offering a practical guide for researchers in the field.

Introduction

Drug discovery focuses on identifying potential therapeutic compounds that can treat different disease conditions. It is a complex process with multiple steps that requires synergy among various scientific domains, including biology, chemistry, computer science, and pharmacology. These fields together hunt for compounds that could become life-saving drugs. Researchers first identify potential drug candidates, then refine their chemical properties to enhance their effectiveness and safety. When a standout compound emerges, medicinal chemistry techniques synthesize and refine the molecule. This compound is further investigated for its biological activity within a living system because what happens in vitro doesn’t always match what happens in our bodies. The experimental phase includes safety, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy assessment to determine the optimal therapeutic dose1.

The modern drug discovery pipeline is structured in phases: target identification, validation, lead discovery, optimization, patent filing, and preclinical and clinical development (Phases I, II, and III). This process is resource-intensive, requiring several years and investments that can reach billions of dollars before a candidate reaches the market1–4. Despite significant technological progress, accurately predicting drug candidate interaction with a biological target and how that interaction translates into therapeutic efficacy remains a formidable challenge in biomedical science5. This major drawback has sparked new interest in alternative approaches, such as phenotypic drug discovery (PDD), which circumvents the need for a predefined molecular target. It looks for drugs that produce beneficial changes in whole cells or tissues. Drug discovery has come a long way from its early roots. Initially, scientists relied on trial and error to observe how compounds affected diseases. This trial-and-error approach was central to traditional medicine and led to the serendipitous discovery of several therapeutics6. As science advanced with breakthroughs in understanding genes and molecules, we learned to design drugs that hit specific disease-causing molecules7.

Drug discovery relies on three main approaches to find promising new treatments. First, high-throughput screening (HTS) rapidly tests thousands of compounds for biological activity. Second, computer-aided drug design (CADD) uses digital tools to model and optimize drug candidates. Third, structural modification tweaks known molecules to improve their effects. The process begins in the prediscovery phase, where scientists hunt for malfunctioning genes or proteins that could drive disease. This is followed by identifying initial “hits” using either HTS or in silico approaches such as virtual screening8. These hits are further validated in preclinical animal models to assess their suitability as investigational new drug candidates.

The current drug discovery pipeline has made impressive progress, yet it remains an expensive, slow, and often inefficient process with high failure rates. However, machine learning (ML) transforms the field by streamlining decision-making, eliminating costly dead ends, and accelerating the search for new therapies. This review examines PDD, a target-agnostic approach that investigates how compounds impact entire disease states rather than individual molecules. Instead of targeting proteins, PDD observes a compound’s effect on living cells, allowing treatment mechanisms to emerge. This makes it particularly powerful for complex diseases, where defining a single actionable target is challenging. Several approved therapies have demonstrated a substantial impact on PDD. For instance, Ivacaftor for cystic fibrosis was identified through a cell-based screen that rescued CFTR function without prior knowledge of the drug’s target9. Similarly, Daclatasvir emerged from an HCV replicon phenotypic assay and later revealed NS5A, a previously “undruggable” protein10. Modern PDD has also delivered breakthrough treatments such as Risdiplam for spinal muscular atrophy11. It was discovered from assays correcting SMN2 splicing, and Lenalidomide, whose unique MoA involving cereblon-mediated degradation was uncovered long after phenotypic screening identified its therapeutic effect6. These successes highlight the ability of PDD to surface first-in-class mechanisms that would have been difficult to predict using a target-based approach. Modern drug discovery is supported by new emerging technologies that amplify the strengths of PDD. High-throughput screening, detailed phenotypic assays, and artificial intelligence collaborate to rapidly identify active compounds and elucidate their mechanisms of action. When combined, these tools make PDD a practical and increasingly powerful route for finding drugs that work through previously unknown biology12.

To enhance PDD’s impact, this review emphasizes the significance of reducing gaps between mechanistic knowledge and therapeutic development while attempting to solve translational problems. This review will help researchers explore technological advancements, such as algorithms and datasets, related to phenotypic drug discovery. The outlined workflow provides a structured and practical roadmap for new researchers in the early stages of discovery, supporting the development of therapies with multi-target mechanisms.

Limitations of target-based computational drug discovery

Understanding the three-dimensional shapes of biological molecules, such as proteins, is crucial for designing more effective medicines. The function of a protein is determined by its precise folding into a unique structure. Determining the architecture of a single protein was painstaking, requiring years of laboratory experiments. Advances in computational biology, however, have significantly transformed this process. For example, tools like AlphaFold13 can now predict protein structures with very high accuracy just by analyzing their amino acid sequences. This breakthrough has substantially reduced the time and cost traditionally associated with structure determination. Biomolecular simulations with multiscale models aid the study of the structure and behavior of target proteins. These simulations reveal key dynamics of conformational changes and binding sites, helping scientists investigate a drug’s binding and its function14. Building on structural insights, virtual screening is used to identify potential drug candidates by examining their interactions with the binding sites of the target protein. This computer-aided approach shrinks the drug discovery timeline significantly by predicting which molecules will be an optimal fit in the pocket of the disease-associated target protein15,16. Additionally, artificial intelligence (AI), including machine learning and deep learning, is now commonly used to enhance the computer-aided drug discovery pipeline17–20. Quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models and virtual screening, combined with experimental methods, enhance the precision and effectiveness of drug development. This interdisciplinary convergence has transformed early discovery into a faster, more scalable process21. Recent developments in target discovery methods like drug affinity response target stability (DARTS), network-based approaches, machine learning algorithms, gene editing, nonsense-mediated mRNA degradation (NMD), and multi-omics analysis have expanded the druggable space22. However, despite these advances, translating molecular insights into effective phenotype outcomes remains a bottleneck. Developing safe, effective drugs is still a process of trial and error, mainly because connecting molecular changes to real-world biological effects remains incredibly complex. While the traditional key-lock paradigm of drug discovery has been useful, it falls short in capturing the complex and nuanced functional outcomes of molecular interactions.

However, even with these innovations, most target-based approaches struggle to capture functional phenotype, since they focus on isolated molecular interactions. Meanwhile, experimental pharmacology fills in critical gaps, revealing the mechanism of action and determining the efficacy of a drug23. However, their limitations in capturing functional phenotypes highlight the growing need for phenotypic drug discovery pipelines.

Phenotypic drug discovery

The origins of PDD date back to the earliest days of pharmacology, when new therapeutics were discovered through empirical observation of phenotypic changes in biological systems24–26. In the late 20th century, target-based drug discovery gained prominence due to advancements in genomics and molecular biology. The need for increased throughput efficiency in screening a large number of chemicals was the driving force behind this change. After using this technique, targeting specific molecular pathways became more accurate27. This “mechanism-first” approach to drug discovery promised greater precision by targeting specific molecular pathways, exemplified by drugs like vemurafenib (BRAF inhibitor for melanoma)28. PDD takes a practical approach to discovering new medicines; instead of targeting specific molecules, it seeks compounds that produce visible changes in living systems. The “desired effect” refers to a measurable phenotypic outcome, such as inhibiting pathogen growth, restoring normal cell function, or activating a specific signaling pathway. These effects are typically elicited by exposing cells, tissues, or whole organisms to chemical stimuli (e.g., small molecules or biologics) and observing their physiological impact. PDD doesn’t require knowing how the drug works beforehand, so it can uncover new ways to treat diseases that target-based approaches might miss. The fundamental characteristic that sets PDD apart from hypothesis-driven target-based drug discovery (TDD) is this target-agnostic methodology29,30. TDD is a hypothesis-driven approach that develops better treatments by first understanding which molecule in the body contributes to a disease, then creating drugs that specifically target it, leading to more effective and tailored therapies23. We gained insights into how drugs interact with disease pathways; however, this strategy has certain limitations. A significant number of molecular targets identified through TDD failed to translate into clinically effective therapies, revealing a gap between molecular targets and physiological relevance23–25. This approach largely relied on observable changes in phenotype without prior assumptions about the molecular targets involved31. PDD has re-emerged as a powerful platform in modern drug discovery, marked by breakthroughs such as high-resolution imaging, robotic microscopes, and the development of sophisticated in vitro and in vivo models. Numerous accessible and scalable technologies now support high-throughput phenotypic screening. These include flow cytometry, laser scanning fluorescence microscopy, microfluidics, and electrophysiological techniques32–37. Among these, cell painting has proven particularly valuable for multiplexed PDD assays. This approach captures incredibly detailed snapshots of cell behavior, measuring over 1500 features like shape, texture, and organization38. These comprehensive profiles provide in-depth insights into the compound effects across various cellular processes, as well as understanding how compounds interact with living systems. PDD’s key advantage is its potential to bridge mechanistic knowledge gaps at various biological levels. Swinney and Anthony7 noted that directly observing these real-world biological responses can be far more revealing than relying only on theoretical molecular targets. While the genetic revolution and molecular biology have contributed significantly to TDD, they still struggle to turn promising discoveries into treatments that work in clinical patients. PDD’s whole system perspective could help close that gap.

PDD and TDD work best when combined, like two halves of a complete puzzle. TDD follows a “mechanism-first” paradigm, which begins with understanding the disease mechanism and then designing drugs to target specific sites. PDD adopts a “compound-first” approach, which tests compounds first and determines how they work later39. These methodologies are synergistic, and when used together, can significantly enhance the likelihood of identifying novel and effective drugs. PDD’s unique potential lies in its ability to exploit a disease phenotype to develop treatments for conditions whose underlying causes are unknown due to insufficient scientific understanding39. Since phenotypic screens monitor individual medications, toxicity and MOA, they are physiologically more relevant than target-based assays.

Technical landscape for phenotypic drug discovery

What is phenotype?

A phenotype represents all the observable traits of a living system, from its shape and structure to biochemical processes, physiological functions and behaviors in a biological system. These traits result from the complex interaction between an organism’s genetic makeup (genotype) and environmental factors. Phenotypes manifest across multiple scales from molecular changes and structural features to dynamical processes. By analyzing phenotypic variation, researchers can better understand how genetic and environmental factors shape health and disease. This understanding is crucial in drug discovery, where phenotypes serve as a proxy for real-world treatment40,41.

Phenotypes studied in PDD can broadly be categorized based on the biological system they represent:

Cellular phenotypes

PDD uses cell-based assays to track phenotypic changes, including cell viability, morphology, and cellular functions. Without being aware of the molecular targets involved, these tests can be used to find substances that induce desirable changes in cell behavior37. Recent advancements in analysis algorithms and instrumentation have improved the cell-based PDD setup. We can identify different stages of the cell cycle, like metaphase, anaphase, telophase, etc., along with simple cell morphology37.

Organism phenotype

Several studies observe phenotype at the organism level using models like Caenorhabditis elegans, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Drosophila melanogaster, zebrafish, etc.42,43. Multiple signaling systems and tissue interactions can be evaluated by testing an in vivo system, which is not possible with a cell-based approach. There is a greater likelihood that a small molecule found in an in vivo system would be bioactive and clinically significant. Each drug generates a behavioral signature (a set of traits or values) compiled into a phenotypic profile. This method helps select an appropriate lead compound by providing precise and accurate information about compound toxicity and dose-specific efficacy36.

Network-based embedding

Network-based drug embedding represents drugs and their relationships using graphs (networks), where drugs are nodes and edges reflect various types of interactions57,58. These connections include drug–drug interactions (DDIs), drug–target interactions (DTIs), drug–disease associations, and chemical similarity networks. Network-based embeddings look at the whole biological system to understand the complex relationships and hidden patterns rather than analyzing each drug separately. These embedding techniques preserve both the topological structure of the network and associated biological information, leading to superior performance compared to conventional methods. That is why they are increasingly applied in drug research and predictive modeling. Popular methods include random walks, matrix decomposition, and deep learning approaches, particularly graph neural networks (GNNs)59,60. However, this kind of interaction model depends heavily on domain knowledge, which is often uneven. Some drug–protein interactions are well-studied in specific diseases, so the model naturally assigns them more weight. As a result, familiar drugs and targets tend to be favored, while obscure or less-studied compounds may be overlooked because of this built-in bias.

Chemical and textual embeddings

Chemical embedding transforms chemical structures into numerical representations (vectors) that serve as input to machine learning algorithms. These embeddings capture important pharmacological characteristics, including chemical composition, molecular and physicochemical properties. This includes fingerprint-based, SMILES, and deep learning-based representations61 and algorithms like Molecular Access System (MACCS) and featmorgan62. A chemical-based approach is a classic, trusted tool in chemistry. Because it only uses the drug’s chemical structure, it’s a universal language that can be applied to any compound. This makes it incredibly reliable for predicting a drug’s basic properties (QSAR modeling), finding chemicals that look alike (similarity search), and discovering new compounds with the same core structure (scaffold hopping). The main limitation is that this approach does not account for molecular interactions in a biological systems context. Structural similarity does not necessitate similar activity in a system. Additionally, SMILES-based models are sensitive to how the molecular string is encoded, which can introduce bias into the predictive models.

An NLP-based method called textual embedding transforms scientific and medical texts, such as research papers, into vector representations to derive significant connections between drugs and diseases. Some textual embedding methods include word embeddings, contextual embeddings and knowledge graph-based embeddings. Word embedding converts words into dense vectors that capture their meaning based on context. For example, “aspirin” and “anti-inflammatory” have similar embeddings because they frequently appear together in text63. One example of the contextual embedding is BioBERT (Biomedical Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers)64. Other examples include BERT65, SciBERT66, and BlueBERT67, which are used to identify drug–target interactions from experimental literature. Drug repurposing has shown potential when textual information from medical journals is combined with embeddings based on chemical structures68. However, some limitations exist, such as word co-occurrence does not always reflect true biological relevance. The models also require significant computational resources, and updating them with scientific knowledge is challenging. Likewise, 3D-based structural information, such as capturing molecules’ correct folding and conformation, remains a significant challenge for string-based embeddings. While string-based or text embeddings provide linear symbolic representations of molecules or biological entities, they do not explicitly encode 3D geometry. Consequently, important structural features such as folding, conformation, and spatial interactions are difficult for these embeddings to capture, making 3D-dependent tasks more challenging.

Hierarchical drug embedding

A machine learning approach organizes drugs into a structured, tree-like representation, a geometric framework well-suited for modeling nonlinear relationships. By this method, drugs are mapped such that structurally or functionally similar drugs are positioned close together, while more dissimilar drugs are positioned farther apart. The methodology was developed to overcome the limitations of traditional drug representation methods, which often struggle to encode complex, multi-level relationships between molecules. By leveraging the properties of hyperbolic space, hierarchical embeddings can more accurately model the “taxonomy” of drugs, including shared functional groups, therapeutic targets, and metabolic pathways. This technique forecasts novel therapeutic applications and adverse effects, since it faithfully replicates chemical structures and hierarchical relationships69,70. While promising, this emerging technology remains complex and less standardized, with few established best practices. The underlying mathematics is unfamiliar to many researchers, making it harder to intuitively understand the model’s reasoning for placing a drug within the hierarchy.

Omics-based embeddings

Transforms complex gene expression data into numerical representations using deep learning models. These models are trained to extract and encode the most relevant patterns from raw gene expression measurements. The key idea behind this approach is to reduce the complexity and inherent noise in high-dimensional expression data. Traditional gene expression analysis has limitations, including data sparsity, technical noise, and the challenge of interpreting thousands of gene-level measurements across diverse experimental conditions. The deep embedding technique addresses the above limitations. It can learn abstract representations, unveiling intricate connections between genes, drugs, and diseases that standard data analysis may overlook. Researchers are using AI to analyze how drugs change gene activity in cells (their “transcriptomic signature”). Donner et al71 demonstrated the utility of such embeddings by showing that compounds with similar phenotypic effects could be clustered based on their learned transcriptomic representations. This proof-of-concept highlighted the potential of embeddings to group drugs by mechanism of action and to uncover opportunities for repurposing. In a related study, Wang et al72. proposed a graph convolutional autoencoder network (GCAN) to embed drug-induced transcriptome data from the LINCS L1000 project, coupled with an LSTM for predicting drug–drug interactions (DDIs)73. Their AI framework forecasted clinical issues, such as hypoglycemia from combining specific sulfonylurea drugs, and Lactic acidosis connected with metformin74. The embeddings are also well-suited for integration with other data types, such as chemical structures or clinical outcomes, making them a versatile tool in modern PDD75. Despite their promise, these functional fingerprints come with hurdles. High-quality omics data doesn’t exist for most drugs. Results can vary depending on the cell type, drug dose, or laboratory running the experiment, raising concerns about reproducibility. The process requires data standardization protocols, significant computing power and expertise. These models can become too tailored to the initial dataset, leading to overfitting.

Early efforts often borrowed general machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) algorithms; however, more recent studies have developed frameworks explicitly tailored to phenotypic datasets. Feature-based models

Feature-based models are a fundamental class of supervised machine learning approaches in drug discovery. It is designed to identify relations between chemical/biological features and measurable biological effects. These models are trained on structured datasets where each entry includes input features and a corresponding output label. These features include molecular descriptors (e.g., atom/bond configurations, physicochemical properties), high-throughput screening results, or gene expression profiles. The range of outputs can span cellular responses (e.g., viability, apoptosis, and proliferation inhibition) to biomarker modulation or morphological changes76. Feature-based models are good at predicting compound activity and flagging off-target interactions. Their utility is further enhanced when applied to high-dimensional data from advanced profiling technologies (e.g., Cell Painting and RNA-seq), enabling predictions of complex cellular. For example, support vector machines (SVMs) have been widely used for tasks like classifying compounds as toxic or non-toxic, where toxicity may be defined by cell death, reduction in viability, or disruption of cellular processes. Zhao et al.77 demonstrated that the performance of SVMs is good at generalizing toxic activity predictions across diverse chemical datasets, highlighting their robustness. Ensemble methods such as Random Forests (RFs) and Gradient Boosting Machines (e.g., XGBoost) are particularly effective for high-dimensional datasets like transcriptomics, where thousands of genes are used as features78. Platforms such as RepurposeDrugs79, DrugRepo72 have integrated structural similarity, pathway data, and disease–gene associations to predict repurposing opportunities across hundreds of diseases. These approaches are computationally efficient and interpretable, but they rely heavily on existing annotations and are therefore less effective in uncovering first-in-class phenotypic mechanisms.

Deep learning models

These models learn biologically meaningful features from large-scale data. Applications include toxicity prediction, where deep learning models can integrate data from lab tests, animal studies, and human clinical trials to predict a compound’s toxicity (e.g., multi-task and transfer learning approaches). Scientists can now use AI to predict how a drug will change a cell’s genetic activity (its transcriptomic response) just by analyzing the drug’s chemical structure. Deep generative approaches, such as DrVAEN80, demonstrated that embedding pretreatment transcriptomes can predict patient-specific drug responses and survival outcomes with reduced overfitting, outperforming many traditional approaches. Similarly, a graph convolutional autoencoder (GCAN) coupled with an LSTM model73 was used to predict drug–drug interactions, successfully identifying clinically relevant interactions, such as sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia. MolDiffusion is a diffusion-based model that can generate new molecules by learning smooth transformations in chemical space and conditioning them on desired phenotypic signatures81. scGen predicts how cells change in response to perturbations using single-cell transcriptomic data, allowing researchers to identify compounds that could reverse disease-like cellular states78. Perturbation autoencoders (PAE/PertVAE) extract the essential features of cellular perturbations, making it possible to propose molecules that can recreate or counter their biological effects82. cGAN-Cell Painting models use image-based morphological profiles from Cell Painting assays to generate molecules that match visual cellular effects83. These models are powerful in capturing phenotypic complexity but may face challenges from data sparsity, batch effects, and the need for large-scale training datasets.

Network-based models

Network-based models turn biology into a web of connections. They use computational approaches to map how genes, proteins, drugs and diseases interact, showing how changes in one part of the system might affect others. Nodes in the network can be genes, proteins, drugs, or diseases, and edges represent the interactions or relationships between them. These models mimic the complex web of molecular interactions that occur in living systems and are used to understand how drugs affect biological pathways and disease phenotypes. The core hypothesis behind network-based models is that biological function and drug response emerge not from single molecules acting alone, but from dynamic interactions across entire molecular networks. By modeling these interactions, researchers can predict how a drug might affect a cell or organism, identify potential targets, and even uncover new uses for existing drugs (drug repurposing). One powerful implementation of this approach is the GNN, which anticipates new drug targets and phenotypic effects by modeling complex drug–gene and drug–protein interactions. Such integrative models have been used to anticipate downstream effects of drug treatment on complex cellular processes, particularly in diseases like cancer, where signaling networks are highly dysregulated84. Several approaches are used to predict drug–target interaction, like MMAtt-DTA, DTiGEMS+85, and DGraphDTA86, which utilizes graph neural networks to obtain deeper representations for drug–target activity prediction, based on structural information of both molecules and proteins, where the two network graphs of drug molecules and proteins are built up respectively. Pharmacogenomics (PGx), the study of the interplay between an individual’s genetic profile and their response to pharmaceutical agents. PGxDB87 represents an emerging and rapidly advancing field within personalized medicine. It is a highly interactive and user-friendly platform that integrates diverse data types, including molecular target profiles, adverse reactions, and indications associated with both approved drugs and investigational compounds.

Single-cell and multi-omics embedding models

Scientists are now building tools to predict how a drug will affect individual cell types within our body. Using new, incredibly detailed data from single-cell sequencing, tools like DrugReSC88 compare the “fingerprint” of a diseased cell against a library of known drug effects. This allows them to find a drug that can precisely shift a diseased cell back to a healthy state, offering a much more targeted approach to treatment. Drug2Cell89 and scTherapy90 can pinpoint exactly which cells a drug will impact most. Find drug combinations that are highly effective against diseases like cancer while having low toxicity (fewer side effects). The most powerful models don’t rely on just one type of data. They integrate multiple sources, such as a drug’s chemical structure, its impact on genes, and its interaction with proteins, into a single, multidimensional profile. This “multi-modal” approach leads to more reliable and broadly applicable predictions. While revolutionary, this technology is complex. It requires massive computing power and, most importantly, access to large amounts of high-quality data from these intricate single-cell experiments, which are still expensive and time-consuming to produce.

Overall, the trajectory of prediction models in PDD reflects a shift from general-purpose ML toward embedding-based frameworks explicitly optimized for phenotypic datasets. Feature-based embeddings provide interpretability and scalability, transcriptome-driven embeddings capture functional drug responses, and single-cell/multi-omics embeddings push the boundary toward personalized and systems-level predictions. Together, these approaches underscore PDD’s growing sophistication and translational potential in modern drug discovery.

Explainable AI (XAI) in PDD

It refers to a set of techniques developed to make the decisions of complex machine learning models more transparent and understandable to humans. There are XAI methods like SHAP (SHapley Additive ExPlanations), and LIME (local interpretable model-agnostic explanations), which quantify the importance of the features, such as specific gene expression levels, chemical substructures, which contribute most to a model’s prediction91. For example, SHAP assigns a score to each feature based on its impact on the prediction, offering global insights (how the model behaves overall) and local insights. Similarly, LIME creates surrogate models around individual predictions explaining a particular outcome. XAI empowers researchers in many ways, such as by pinpointing promising drug targets and identifying the molecular features that govern a biochemical activity profile. This clarity extends to the clinic, where XAI helps design better trials and stratify patients by identifying key biomarkers, ensuring the right treatments reach the right people. It has been used in several fields, such as anticancer peptides92, antimicrobial peptides. Studies have shown that features based on overall energy estimation (RCEMT), physicochemical properties (ExPseAAC), and sequence-derived characteristics (DDE) play an important role in accurately predicting anti-MRSA peptides93. By providing a clear window into the AI’s reasoning, XAI builds confidence in its predictions, facilitates smarter decision-making, and paves the way for smoother regulatory approval94. In anticancer peptide (ACP) research, t-SNE visualization and SHAP analysis were applied to show how different modules of the model capture features, highlighting the significant role that physicochemical and compositional properties play in shaping the prediction outcome94. In a related study, XAI helped to decode the complexity of multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway systems such as Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) to explore personalized drug delivery95,96. Along with drug identification, it helps capture factors affecting a compound’s safety/ADMET properties. In chemical drugs, XAI has been used to predict molecular properties, study drug–drug and drug–target interactions97,98, and assess toxicity99,100.

Challenges

Despite the renewed interest in PDD and the promise of uncovering first-in-class therapeutics, its full potential has yet to be fully harnessed. PDD offers a systems-level approach that is well-suited to address multifactorial diseases. It is particularly valuable for identifying compounds with novel mechanisms of action. However, its broader adoption remains limited due to several challenges. Three interrelated bottlenecks that continue to hinder progress:

Data scarcity and standardization deficit

The scarcity of high-quality, standardized datasets is a significant barrier to PDD advancement. Phenotypic assays generate highly diverse and complex data, ranging from transcriptomics and proteomics to morphological and functional phenotypes29. However, these data are often fragmented and complex to integrate across platforms and studies. The training quality of ML models reduces because of this lack of harmonization, leading to weak predictive performance and generalization. Such shortcomings can contribute to drug failures at later stages. For example, Mylotarg, an antibody–drug conjugate, was withdrawn due to a combination of low efficiency101. Gefitinib (Iressa), which failed to demonstrate a survival benefit in a broad population, was discontinued at the phase III or pre-registration stage. Fialuridine (antiviral) and TAK-875(metabolic disease), which caused liver injury102. To reduce such failures—where substantial resources, time, and patient lives are at stake—public datasets must include richer contextual details specific to disease models. Currently, existing datasets contain significant gaps that hinder the development of robust computational predictions, thereby limiting the effective application of PDD models.

Validation bottlenecks in phenotypic screening

Modern high-throughput technologies have opened the door to large-scale phenotypic drug screening; however, these complex assays present real-world challenges. Unlike target-based screens, which measure a single, defined activity (e.g., enzyme inhibition), phenotypic screens often rely on multi-parametric readouts. Traits like cell morphology, behavior, or viability make them challenging to automate, interpret, and reproduce consistently. Phenotypic hits must be validated in increasingly complex biological systems, progressing from cell-based assays to animal models that better represent human disease103. Therefore, it is essential to establish accurate models for phenotype validation. In vivo disease models often face challenges in translating findings to humans due to methodological differences and species-specific disparities. For example, the flavonoid DMXAA showed promise in preclinical studies but failed in clinical trials because it selectively activated mouse STING, not the human version104. Researchers developed BIA 10-2474 as a supposedly “selective” FAAH inhibitor because early phenotypic studies looked promising. However, they never carefully validated its true mechanism. In a Phase I trial, the compound caused severe neurotoxicity, including one death. Only after the incident did researchers perform detailed profiling and reveal that the compound actually inhibited many off-target brain serine hydrolases. This case strikingly illustrates how skipping rigorous target deconvolution can lead to catastrophic consequences105.

Regulators originally approved thalidomide solely for its sedative effects, long before anyone understood how it actually worked. Decades later, scientists explained its devastating teratogenic effects when they identified cereblon (CRBN) as its molecular target. This illustrates how failing to validate mechanisms early can hide serious, mechanism-linked toxicities until it is too late106.

Researchers observed impressive results with NXY-059, a potential neuroprotective drug, in rodent stroke models, but they never established a clear mechanism of action. These models also lacked strong relevance to human biology. When researchers advanced NXY-059 to large clinical trials, the drug failed completely. This case demonstrates how relying on non-predictive phenotypic models creates a major bottleneck in translating preclinical findings into real clinical benefit107.

The failure of models to capture relevant physiological or pathological processes risks advancing false-positive compounds. Together, these cases highlight a critical weakness of animal models and underscore the urgent need for alternative approaches that more accurately capture human biology. Additionally, whole-cell or organism-based PDD platforms must account for variables such as drug metabolism, clearance, and distribution.

Mechanistic ambiguity in target deconvolution

Identifying a promising compound in the screening phase is only the beginning; the next critical challenge is understanding its mechanism of action. Unlike target-based methods, where the mechanism is predefined, phenotypic hits require scientists to engage in a game of biological sleuthing. Target deconvolution can be a long, labor-intensive process, adding extra steps (and time) to the already lengthy drug development journey108. Studies suggest that only a few phenotypic hits achieve successful target identification, with many candidates stalled due to unresolved MoAs6. The challenge becomes even trickier because these compounds often hit multiple targets simultaneously (polypharmacology) or work through entirely new biology, making them harder to unravel. These case studies, which showcase examples of drugs withdrawn from use or repurposed for new indications, underscore the importance of target deconvolution. For example, Epoprostenol, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), sildenafil, and tadalafil are successful examples of drug repositioning for the treatment of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH)109. Sildenafil, was initially developed for angina and hypertension, but was found to treat erectile dysfunction and later PAH. Imatinib, a BCR-ABL inhibitor, was first approved for chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) but later found to be effective for targeting the protein tyrosine kinase KIT (CD117) in gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs)110. Thalidomide was initially developed for anxiety and morning sickness, but was later developed and approved for multiple myeloma102. Minoxidil was repositioned from an antihypertensive drug to a topical treatment for hair loss111. Other examples include the anticancer drugs sorafenib, azacitidine and decitabine, all of which failed clinical trials for their first intended oncology indication but were subsequently approved for other cancer types112. PDD seeks to find drugs with unique modes of action, but doing so poses a risk of leaving the scientific path unclear.

Future directions

Researchers are turning to new technologies and strategies to overcome the challenges of PDD and improve its success. AI and multi-omics technologies have played a crucial role in enhancing the identification of cellular disease phenotypes, thereby making PDD results more generalizable. AI, in particular, is transforming phenotypic screening by enhancing predictive modeling, data integration, offering a powerful means to tackle key limitations37. Additionally, advanced cell imaging techniques enable researchers to decipher the mechanisms behind promising phenotypic hits, converting these previously unexplained observations into well-understood biological processes113,114. One promising direction is the use of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to create patient-specific disease models. These cells are taken from a patient’s skin or blood and reprogrammed into any cell type, providing a platform for personalized phenotypic screening. When coupled with 3D organoid cultures, these cells self-organize into mini tissues that behave much more like real human organs than traditional lab cultures. We can now predict how drugs will work by combining advanced biological models with computer simulations.

Alongside these biological advances, generative models represent a powerful new frontier. By learning from existing knowledge and applying it in novel contexts, they can significantly advance phenotypic drug discovery and enable new applications, such as in rare diseases and personalized medicine. Foundation models, capable of learning from very small datasets, offer promise for developing treatments for conditions with limited data. Generative AI can also simulate gene expression patterns to match individual-level signatures. While still ambitious, this approach holds great potential to broaden the scope and precision of PDD, paving the way for more efficient, targeted, and personalized drug discovery. In the coming years, PDD is expected to expand its role across various stages of the drug development process.
