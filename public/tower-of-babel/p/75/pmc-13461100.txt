Robot-assisted Ivor-Lewis esophagectomy with intrathoracic anastomosis for adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction: a cohort study of 156 cases
Authors: Xiaofeng Duan, Haochen Wang, Yuxuan Zhang, Jie Yue, Chuangui Chen, Zhao Ma, Zuoyu Chen, Chen Zhang, Shangren Wang, Chunyu Hou, Wencheng Zhang, Hongjing Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:To compare the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant therapy (NT) followed by robot-assisted Ivor-Lewis esophagectomy vs. upfront surgery (S) in patients with locally advanced adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG).Methods:We retrospectively analyzed 156 patients with locally advanced AEG (cT2–4a/N0–2) who underwent robot-assisted Ivor-Lewis surgery with intrathoracic anastomosis between 2017 and 2024. Baseline characteristics, pathological responses, and surgical outcomes were compared between the NT and S groups. Survival data were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis.Results:Of the 156 patients, 41 (26.3%) received NT. The NT group achieved a pathological complete response (pCR) rate of 24.4% (vs. 0% in the S group) and higher node-negative rates (pN0: 56.1% vs. 32.2%, P = 0.009), with significant downstaging in pT stage (T0–2: 41.5% vs. 17.4%, P < 0.001), TNM stage (I–II: 53.7% vs. 23.5%, P < 0.001), and lymphovascular invasion (7.3% vs. 27.0%, P = 0.008). Surgical outcomes were comparable between groups, including conversion rates (9.8% vs. 10.4%, P = 1.000), anastomotic leaks (2.4% vs. 4.3%, P = 1.000), and pneumonia (7.3% vs. 10.4%, P = 0.761), despite more frequent use of overlap anastomosis in the NT group (68.3% vs. 34.8%, P = 0.001). After a median follow-up of 21 months (n = 129), the NT group showed superior 2-year overall survival (OS) (87.5% vs. 71.0%, P = 0.042), with pCR patients achieving 100% 2-year OS.Conclusion:NT significantly improves pathological responses and survival in advanced AEG without increasing surgical risks. The robotic-assisted approach enables the safe implementation of this multimodal treatment strategy.Visual Abstract: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B274

Introduction

The incidence of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) has been rising annually[1,2]. Owing to its anatomical location, AEG is associated with a high risk of early metastasis[3]. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment, particularly for early-stage or locally advanced disease after neoadjuvant therapy. With advances in minimally invasive techniques, robot-assisted surgery, especially using the da Vinci surgical system, has been increasingly adopted for esophageal cancer and AEG. Its technological advantages, including three-dimensional magnified visualization, enhanced dexterity, and tremor filtration, facilitate precise dissection in the narrow mediastinal space, potentially enabling more radical lymph node dissection, safer tumor resection, and improved intrathoracic anastomosis[4,5].

For locally advanced AEG, current standard neoadjuvant therapies, including chemoradiotherapy and chemotherapy (e.g., the FLOT regimen), are still associated with high recurrence rates and suboptimal pathological complete response (pCR), particularly in cT3–4/N+ disease[6–9]. Although the incorporation of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has recently improved pCR rates, long-term survival outcomes remain under evaluation[10–13].

While previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility and short-term benefits of robotic esophagectomy, large-scale evaluations focusing on AEG, particularly within multimodal treatment including neoadjuvant therapy, are still limited. This study aims to evaluate the clinical outcomes of robot-assisted Ivor–Lewis esophagectomy in 156 patients with AEG, with emphasis on surgical quality, pathological staging, and survival. We further compare patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy followed by robotic surgery vs. those undergoing upfront surgery to assess the integration and safety of robotic platforms within a multidisciplinary treatment framework. The work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria[14].

Methods

Patient cohort

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Cancer Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria[14]. We enrolled patients with locally advanced AEG (cT2–4N0–2M0) who underwent surgical resection at our institution between January 2021 and November 2024. Inclusion criteria were: Siewert type I or II AEG, histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma, and performance of robot-assisted Ivor–Lewis minimally invasive surgery. Exclusion criteria included esophageal adenocarcinoma or Siewert type III AEG, squamous cell carcinoma or other histological types, and open surgery.

Preoperative assessment and staging

All patients underwent comprehensive preoperative evaluation, including physical examination, hematological tests, cardiopulmonary function assessment, neck and upper abdominal ultrasound, gastroscopy/endoscopic ultrasound with biopsy, contrast-enhanced chest and abdominal computed tomography (CT), and tumor biomarker profiling using pretreatment biopsy specimens. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography was performed when necessary to confirm disease staging, rule out surgical contraindications, and detect distant metastases. Staging followed the AJCC 8th edition esophageal cancer staging system, with Siewert classification based on tumor location.

Neoadjuvant therapy

Patients with clinically staged cT2–4aN0–2M0 disease were typically indicated for neoadjuvant therapy. Modalities included neoadjuvant chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, or chemoimmunotherapy. Neoadjuvant immunotherapy involved progressive disease-1 (PD-1) inhibitors such as pembrolizumab, tislelizumab, toripalimab, sintilimab, camrelizumab, or serplulimab. Chemotherapy regimens consisted of platinum-based doublets or triplets, including FP (cisplatin + 5-fluorouracil), SOX (oxaliplatin + S-1), FLOT (fluorouracil + oxaliplatin + docetaxel), TS (paclitaxel + S-1), and XELOX (oxaliplatin + capecitabine). After three cycles of neoadjuvant treatment, patients underwent comprehensive reevaluation. Surgical resection was performed 4–6 weeks after the completion of neoadjuvant therapy.

Surgical procedure

All patients underwent minimally invasive esophagectomy using the da Vinci robotic surgical system (Si/Xi models). The abdominal phase utilized four robotic arms for gastric mobilization, abdominal lymphadenectomy, and gastric conduit formation. The thoracic phase employed three robotic arms and one assistant port for esophageal mobilization, mediastinal lymph node dissection (middle and lower), and esophagogastric anastomosis. Intrathoracic esophagogastric side-to-side anastomosis was performed using laparoscopic linear staplers. Initially, a “T-shaped” anastomosis was used (Fig. 1); later, the technique was modified to an overlap anastomosis, with the common entry closed using barbed suture in a continuous fashion (Fig. 2). Data collection

Collected data included: baseline characteristics (clinical T, N, and TNM stage); neoadjuvant treatment regimens and response; perioperative parameters (operative time, blood loss, complications, length of stay); pathological data [histological type, pT/N/TNM stage, pCR, MPR, tumor regression grade (TRG)]; and follow-up data [duration, overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS)].

Key definitions

pCR: ypT0N0 (absence of viable tumor cells in both the primary site and lymph nodes). Major pathological response (MPR): ≤10% viable tumor cells in the primary tumor bed. TRG for the primary tumor was estimated as follows: T1, no residual tumor; T2, residual tumor ≤10%; T3, residual tumor ≤50%; T4, residual tumor <100%; and T5, no tumor regression. OS: Time from the surgery date to death from any cause or the last follow-up. DFS: Time from the surgery date to recurrence, metastasis, or death from any cause. Tumor staging utilized the AJCC 8th edition Esophageal Cancer TNM Staging System[3]. Major complications were evaluated according to the Esophagectomy Complication Consensus Group criteria[17]. Adverse events (AEs) were categorized and graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (%) and compared using Fisher’s exact or chi-square tests. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum or unpaired t-tests. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis was conducted using Cox proportional hazards regression. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS v25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Neoadjuvant treatment response

Among the 41 NT patients, 33 (80.5%) received chemoimmunotherapy, 7 (17.1%) received chemotherapy alone, and 1 (2.4%) received chemoradiotherapy. In the NT cohort, 26 (63.4%) achieved a partial response (PR), 14 (34.1%) had stable disease (SD), and 1 (2.4%) had PD. Treatment-related AEs of grade ≥3 occurred in 9.1% of patients.

The NT group showed significantly better pathological outcomes compared with the S group, including higher node-negative rates (56.1% vs. 32.2%, P = 0.009) and lower lymphovascular invasion rates (7.3% vs. 27.0%, P = 0.008).

Follow-up and survival analysis

With a median follow-up of 21 months (n = 129), the 1- and 2-year OS rates were 90.2% and 74.8%, respectively. Neoadjuvant therapy was associated with significantly improved 2-year OS compared to surgery alone (87.5% vs. 71.0%, P = 0.042). Lower pathological T stage (T1–2 vs. T3–4, 2-year OS 90.1% vs. 69.4%, P = 0.039) and node-negative or limited nodal involvement (N0-1 vs. N2-3, 2-year OS 82.7% vs. 66.2%, P = 0.035) were correlated with better OS (Fig. 3). Notably, patients achieving pCR after neoadjuvant therapy had 100% 2-year OS and DFS, though this did not reach statistical significance compared to non-pCR patients.

Discussion

This retrospective analysis of 156 AEG patients undergoing robot-assisted Ivor–Lewis esophagectomy demonstrates the feasibility and safety of robotic surgery and compares outcomes between neoadjuvant therapy and upfront surgery. Robot-assisted surgery was safe overall, with low blood loss, acceptable conversion rates, and manageable complications, without increased surgical risk even after neoadjuvant treatment. Importantly, neoadjuvant therapy significantly improved pCR and node-negative rates and was associated with better short-term survival. These findings indicate that robot-assisted Ivor–Lewis esophagectomy is not only technically feasible and safe but can also be effectively integrated into multimodal treatment for locally advanced AEG, providing a minimally invasive and clinically effective surgical option.

The ESOPEC trial compared perioperative FLOT chemotherapy with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in esophageal adenocarcinoma or AEG, showing superior 3-year OS and PFS with FLOT[18]. Consequently, surgery combined with perioperative FLOT has become standard for locally advanced AEG. The ESOPEC trial began in 2016, prior to the establishment of immunotherapy in AEG[19]. Several phase III trials, including ORIENT-16[20], CHECKMATE-649[21], and RATIONALE-305[22], have confirmed that chemotherapy plus immunotherapy improves OS and PFS in advanced AEG. Thus, chemoimmunotherapy is now first-line for advanced or metastatic AEG, and evidence supporting its perioperative use is accumulating[23].

In our NT cohort, pCR was achieved in 19.5% and MPR in 41.5%. Among those receiving chemoimmunotherapy (n = 33), the pCR rate was 24.2%, suggesting that combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy may enhance pathological responses compared to chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy alone. Two phase II studies, DANTE[10] and NEOSUMMIT-01[24], showed that immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy significantly improves pCR rates in resectable AEG, achieving 24% and 22%, respectively – consistent with our results and superior to doublet chemotherapy (7.4% pCR) or FLOT triplet chemotherapy (15–16.7%)[18]. We found no significant impact of chemotherapy regimen or ICI type on pCR rate, though a trend favored SOX over FP. Notably, grade ≥3 AEs with FLOT in the ESOPEC study reached 58%, vs. 35.2% with SOX. Thus, with immunotherapy, developing more effective and less toxic perioperative regimens will be a key research focus[25–28].

Neoadjuvant therapy was associated with significantly better survival, with a 2-year OS of 87.5% vs. 71.0% in the surgery-alone group. A recent study also found that perioperative durvalumab plus FLOT improved event-free survival over FLOT alone in resectable gastric or GEJ adenocarcinoma. Two-year event-free survival was 67.4% among the participants in the durvalumab group and 58.5% among those in the FLOT group (P < 0.001)[18]. All pCR patients were alive without recurrence at follow-up, underscoring the profound survival benefit of complete pathological response and highlighting the prognostic value of pCR[25]. Additionally, we found that lymph node metastasis status was a critical factor influencing survival.

Another key finding is that all patients successfully underwent robot-assisted Ivor–Lewis esophagectomy, even after neoadjuvant therapy. The Ivor–Lewis procedure is widely used for Siewert I/II AEG. While open surgery is associated with significant trauma and high complication rates, robotic technology reduces morbidity and improves quality of life[29]. A major challenge in minimally invasive Ivor–Lewis surgery is digestive reconstruction and intrathoracic anastomosis. Conventional circular staplers often require extended incisions[30]. Some surgeons use hand-sewn end-to-end reconstruction under robotic assistance[31], leveraging the system’s advantages, but this requires expertise and carries a high risk of leakage during the learning curve[32,33].

In this study, we optimized the technique using a side-to-side overlap anastomosis with hand-sewn closure of the common entry[16], reducing anastomotic time and leak rates[31]. This method is particularly beneficial in neoadjuvant-treated patients with larger tumors, reducing the required gastric conduit length while ensuring radical resection. Consistent with these advantages, the NT group had a higher rate of overlap anastomosis.

Despite surgery’s central role in solid tumors, locally advanced AEG remains prone to recurrence and metastasis. While ICIs offer revolutionary perioperative opportunities, they also pose surgical challenges due to tissue fibrosis and altered anatomy. This study shows that robot-assisted surgery mitigates these risks. In the NT group, conversion rates (9.8% vs. 10.4%), anastomotic leak (2.4% vs. 4.3%), and pneumonia (7.3% vs. 10.4%) were comparable to the S group, confirming that the robotic platform’s precision, stereoscopic vision, and stability preserve safety even after aggressive neoadjuvant therapy. By enabling meticulous dissection and radical lymph node clearance in altered planes, the robotic approach ensures high R0 resection rates and reduces trauma. The integration of effective systemic therapy (“immune-enhanced downstaging”) with precision surgery (“robotic radical resection”) creates a synergistic model that improves survival and quality of life in locally advanced esophageal cancer[34].

Limitations

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, several limitations should be considered. First, this was a retrospective, single-center analysis with a relatively small sample size, especially in the neoadjuvant therapy group (n = 41), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the median follow-up time of 21 months is relatively short for assessing long-term survival outcomes. Finally, variations in neoadjuvant regimens and surgical techniques over the study period, including the evolution of anastomotic methods, may have influenced outcomes.

Conclusions

Neoadjuvant therapy significantly improves pathological responses and survival in advanced AEG without increasing surgical risks. The robot-assisted approach enables the safe implementation of this multimodal treatment strategy. Future prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to validate these results.
