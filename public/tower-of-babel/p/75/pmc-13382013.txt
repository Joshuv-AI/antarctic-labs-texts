An Anas pangenome graph reveals the role of structural variations in duck domestication
Authors: Zijia Ni, Zhou Zhang, Mengfei Ning, Wenwu Xu, Lizhi Lu, Huashui Ai, Yinhua Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the significant phenotypic divergence between domestic ducks and their wild progenitors, the structural variants (SVs) underlying this differentiation remain largely unexplored due to the limitations of linear references in capturing genetic diversity across the genus Anas. Current pangenome efforts either lack base-level resolution or are constrained by genomic anchoring, hindering the exploration of genetic signatures of domestication. Here, we assembled chromosome-level genomes for a mallard (Anas platyrhynchos) and an eastern spot-billed duck (Anas zonorhyncha) that retained substantial domestic components. By integrating these with 14 high-quality Anas assemblies, we constructed a base-level resolution pangenome graph encompassing 243.4 Mb of non-reference sequences. Our graph-based pipeline demonstrated superior mapping performance and identified 67,165 population-level SVs, representing a five-fold increase in detection sensitivity compared to linear-based methods. Notably, SVs explained more genetic variance (PC1: 8.75% vs. 3.25%) and provided finer resolution of local ancestry than single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), highlighting the unique advantage of SVs in resolving duck population structure. Leveraging the graph, we recovered 163 ancestral SVs that are prevalent in distant species but persist at low frequencies in domestic and wild populations. The loss of these ancestral sequences in gravity sensing and cilium assembly likely provided the physiological plasticity necessary for domestication. Moreover, highly divergent exonic SVs exhibited a non-random distribution, preferentially accumulating in UTRs over CDS regions. Specifically, highly divergent SVs located in UTRs of TMEM123 and FAM13A may be associated with viral resistance and the regulation of adipogenesis. Similarly, we identified a highly divergent 75 bp insertion located 3.66 kb upstream of the FAM184B gene, which may be associated with changes in muscle growth during duck domestication. In conclusion, the study establishes a comprehensive base-resolution pangenome resource for the Anas. Our findings reveal that SVs are essential for resolving complex evolutionary histories and suggest that SV-mediated regulatory evolution is an important driver of rapid phenotypic change during duck domestication.

Introduction

Domestic ducks are important global livestock, primarily derived from the Mallard (Anas platyrhynchos) following a domestication process that began over 2,000 years ago (Zhang et al., 2018). Over two millennia of artificial selection have driven significant morphological and physiological divergence in domestic breeds compared to their wild progenitors (Lavretsky et al., 2023). In duck domestication, as observed across various avian species, structural variations (SVs) have emerged as important drivers of phenotypic divergence, particularly regarding traits such as body size, plumage coloration (Recuerda and Campagna, 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Despite their critical role in shaping these domestication-related traits, SVs remain poorly characterized in ducks due to the inherent limitations of traditional pipelines based on short-read mapping to linear genomes (Huddleston et al., 2017).

This gap highlights a fundamental challenge in genomics: the lack of representativeness of any single linear reference genome. Even in the human genome, the GRCh38 reference fails to capture approximately 10% of the species' total genetic diversity (Sherman and Salzberg, 2020). This challenge is particularly acute within the genus Anas. These species, especially the mallard, exhibit remarkable interspecific hybridization capacity (Lavretsky et al., 2015). Recent studies have documented widespread and complex gene flow among domestic ducks, mallards, eastern spot-billed ducks (Anas zonorhyncha) (Guo et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). This complex admixture has resulted in extensive mixed ancestry within mallard populations and undoubtedly contributed to high genetic diversity in ducks (Feng et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018). Under these circumstances, a solitary linear reference acts as a bottleneck, masking the intricate ancestral SVs that define the Anas. Consequently, moving beyond a single linear genome is essential to capture the full landscape of genomic variation in ducks.

Pangenome approaches have emerged to address the limitations of the single linear reference. A linear pangenome for ducks from five genomes via whole-genome alignment successfully captured approximately 41 Mb of non-reference sequences (NRSs) (Wang et al., 2024). Despite this progress, the linear pangenome remains constrained by two key issues: NRSs remain difficult to anchor precisely on genomic coordinates (Miao et al., 2024), and continuous expansion of sequences under a linear format substantially increases computational time costs for storing and searching reference genomes (Sherman and Salzberg, 2020).

Graph-based pangenomes offer a powerful alternative, providing superior capabilities for storing complex variation information and facilitating the utilization of alternative sequences (Sherman and Salzberg, 2020). Previous work established an Anas pangenome graph using Minigraph (Li et al., 2020), which incorporated nine duck genomes and captured approximately 124 Mb of NRSs (Zhang et al., 2025). Although Minigraph-based graphs effectively represent large and complex variants, their lack of base-level resolution for small variants (< 50 bp) can introduce biases (Wang et al., 2023b). While PGGB (Garrison et al., 2024) also resolves base-level variations, Minigraph-Cactus (Hickey et al., 2024) demonstrates enhanced computational efficiency and superior support for downstream analysis (Andreace et al., 2023).

In this study, we assembled chromosome-level genomes for a mallard and an eastern spot-billed duck, both sharing substantial genetic components with domestic populations. By integrating these novel assemblies with 14 published high-quality Anas genomes, we constructed an Anas pangenome graph up to base-pair resolution. In addition to evaluating the mapping and genotyping performance of the pangenome graph, we demonstrate that SVs provide a distinct and higher-resolution depiction of population structure compared to single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Crucially, the Anas pangenome graph recovered SVs missing from the linear backbone, including hundreds of lineage-specific SVs originating from distant wild Anas species. By further characterizing these low-frequency ancestral SVs shared across domestic ducks and their wild congeners, we identified a loss of ancestral sequences that likely contributed to the physiological plasticity essential for adapting to human-mediated environments. Finally, we examined the potential functional impact of these population-differentiated SVs on genes linked to domestication traits.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

One female mallard (MaM) and one female eastern spot-billed duck (SBM), both exhibiting wild-type phenotypes despite carrying domestic duck ancestry, were collected for genomic sequencing from Jiangxi Pohu Lake Ecological Breeding Co., Ltd. All research was approved by the Beijing Association for Science and Technology (approval ID 789 SYXK, Beijing, 2007-0023) and performed in compliance with the Beijing Laboratory Animal Welfare and 790 Ethics guidelines, as issued by the Beijing Administration Committee of Laboratory Animals, and in 791 accordance with the China Agricultural University (CAU) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee 792. guidelines (ID: SKLAB-B-2010-003) approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of CAU.

Data generation

Genomic DNA was isolated from fresh blood of two ducks. For each sample, standard and ultralong Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) reads were generated using the Nanopore PromethION sequencer (Supplementary Table S1). Optical molecules of high-quality genomic DNA (labeled restriction enzyme DLE1) were produced using the BioNano Genomics instrument Saphyr system. Additionally, short paired-end reads from genomic DNA and high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Supplementary Table S1). All library preparations and sequencing were performed at the Genome Center of GrandOmics Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Quality control of sequencing data

The ONT reads with quality scores ≥ 7 were utilized for downstream analyses. Unless otherwise stated, all next-generation sequencing (NGS) DNA used in downstream analyses was subject to quality control using fastp (v0.20.0) (Chen et al., 2018) with default parameters. The RNA-Seq data downloaded from NCBI (Supplementary Table S17) were filtered by fastp (v0.24.0) with the following parameters: "--trim_front1 15 --trim_front2 15 --trim_tail1 5 --trim_tail2 5". The whole-genome sequencing data downloaded from NCBI (Supplementary Table S12) was filtered by fastp (v0.24.0) with the parameter "--qualified_quality_phred 20".

Phylogenomic and admixture analysis

Population-wide SNPs dataset including 85 samples (83 available samples and 2 newly sequenced samples, MaM and SBM) was generated for Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and admixture analyses.

For the datasets, clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (Pekin_C18, supplemented with the W chromosome from ZJU1.0) using BWA (Li and Durbin, 2009) with default parameters. Sorting and duplicate marking used SAMtools (v1.11) (Danecek et al., 2021) and Sambamba (v1.0.1) (Tarasov et al., 2015), respectively. Variant calling and joint genotyping were performed using GATK. These SNPs were filtered with "QD < 2.0, MQ < 40.0, FS > 60.0, SOR > 3.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, ReadPosRankSum < −8.0", and only autosomal biallelic SNPs were retained.

For PCA and admixture analysis, the core dataset was converted to PLINK format using PLINK (v1.9) (Chang et al., 2015). Analysis of PCA was performed using PLINK with the parameters "--pca --freq". Linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning was applied using PLINK with the parameters "--indep-pairwise 50 5 0.5". Admixture analysis was inferred using ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) (Alexander et al., 2009) for K values ranging from 2 to 10, results visualized using pong (v1.5, https://github.com/ramachandran-lab/pong). For the window-based admixture analysis, 47 individuals with unambiguous ancestry (maximum ancestry proportion ≥ 0.99 at K = 2) were selected to define ancestry lineages, and the analysis was performed across 100 kb non-overlapping windows, resulting in 10,560 windows for individuals MaM and SBM.

To identify genomic outliers, we analyzed ancestry proportions using a pipeline in R (v4.4.1). For each target sample (MaM or SBM), a reference background was defined by filtering for the corresponding wild breed while excluding the other candidate sample. The Q values were logit-transformed to satisfy normality assumptions, and the statistical significance of each outlier was determined using Grubbs’ Test and the squared Mahalanobis distance with a Chi-square P value.

Genome assembly

Genome size was estimated at 1.085 Gb for MaM and 1.064 Gb for SBM using Jellyfish (v2.2.10) (Marcais and Kingsford, 2011) and GenomeScope 2.0 (K = 19) (Ranallo-Benavidez et al., 2020). Preliminary contigs were de novo assembled from ONT long and ultralong reads using NextDenovo (v2.2) (Hu et al., 2024) and subsequently polished with their corresponding NGS data by NextPolish (v1.1.0) (Hu et al., 2020). These polished contigs were then integrated with de novo assembled BioNano optical maps using BioNano Solve (v3.3, https://bionano.com/software-downloads/) with manufacturer-recommended parameters to produce hybrid assemblies.

To achieve chromosome-level assembly, scaffolds were anchored into linkage groups using Hi-C data. Following quality control using fastp with the parameters "-f 10 -t 30 -F 10 -T 30", Hi-C reads were processed through Juicer (v1.5) for automated scaffolding, followed by manual curation in Juicerbox (v1.11.08) (Durand et al., 2016) and 3D-DNA (v180922) (Dudchenko et al., 2017). Gaps were closed using TGS-GapCloser (v1.1.1) (Xu et al., 2020), redundant unplaced scaffolds were removed, and the assemblies underwent a final round of polishing with NextPolish.

Evaluation of genome quality and synteny analysis

The completeness of the two assemblies was assessed with Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO, v5.8.3) (Manni et al., 2021) in genome mode using default settings and the aves_odb10 dataset (containing 8,338 conserved protein models). BUSCO was run with Augustus (v3.5.0) (Stanke et al., 2008) as the gene predictor. The quality value (QV) was evaluated using NGS reads with Merqury (v1.3) (Rhie et al., 2020). The optimal k-mer size (K = 21) was determined using the “best_k.sh” script provided in Merqury.

Genome synteny was identified by pairwise genome alignments using Minimap2 (v2.28-r1209) (Li, 2018) with the parameters "-ax asm5 --eqx", and analyzed with SyRI (v1.7) (Goel et al., 2019). The results were visualized using plotsr (v1.1.1) (Goel and Schneeberger, 2022) with the parameter "-s 100" (Supplementary Fig. S4). Chromosome-level synteny (Chr1-30 and sex chromosomes) and micro-chromosomes synteny (Chr31 and beyond) were further characterized using NGenomeSyn (v1.41) (He et al., 2023) with minimum alignment lengths of 200 kb and 10 kb, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3). Additionally, large-scale synteny across 16 assemblies was analyzed for Chr1-29 and ChrZ using NGenomeSyn with a 500-kb threshold.

Phylogenomic analysis and pangenome graph construction

Genetic distances among the 16 assemblies and the Muscovy duck outgroup genome (GCA_048319975.1) were estimated using Mash (Ondov et al., 2016), focusing on autosomes and the Z chromosome. A neighbor-joining tree was then constructed from the resulting distance matrix using the "nj" function in the R package ape (Paradis and Schliep, 2019) and visualized using FigTree (v1.4.3, http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree).

The pangenome graph was constructed using the Minigraph-Cactus pipeline (v2.9.1) (Hickey et al., 2024), with Pekin_C18 as the backbone. The construction followed a three-step process: (1) generation of an initial SV graph using cactus-minigraph; (2) assembly-to-graph mapping via cactus-graphmap; (3) base-level alignment refinement using cactus-graphmap-split and cactus-align-batch. The final step involved utilizing cactus-graphmap-join to generate three hierarchical graph versions: the Full graph (all sequences), the Clipped graph (omitting unaligned sequences ≥ 10 kb), and the Filtered graph (retaining only nodes supported by at least two assemblies). Here, nodes represent distinct segments of DNA sequence.

Pangenome graph dynamics and variant annotation

Pangenome representativeness and core-pan growth dynamics were quantified using Panacus (v0.3.3) (Parmigiani et al., 2024) with the parameters "ordered-histgrowth" and "histgrowth", focusing on non-reference sequences/nodes accumulation by excluding backbone nodes.

For variant characterization, multiallelic sites were decomposed into biallelic records using BCFtools (v1.21) (Danecek et al., 2021) with "bcftools norm". To reduce redundancy for downstream analysis, highly similar biallelic SVs were merged into a collapsed set of 312,416 records using Truvari (v4.3.1) (English et al., 2022) with the default parameters of "truvari collapse". For SVs with reference (REF) or alternative (ALT) sequences longer than 50 bp, we implemented a customized annotation strategy by modifying the “truvari anno repmask” tool to enable simultaneous assessment of both REF and ALT sequences. Unlike the default criterion which relies on the length difference (∣ALT−REF∣), our modified approach independently assessed the repeat coverage proportions (≥ 50%) within both REF and ALT sequences. The annotation utilized a composite TE library, generated by merging a 16-assembly panTElib constructed via panEDTA (Ou et al., 2024) with the RepeatMasker essential library (RepBase release 20181026).

Pangenome graph mapping performance evaluation

Performance differences between the pangenome graph and the linear genome were evaluated in terms of read alignment speed, peak memory usage, alignment accuracy mentioned on Rice et al. (2023).

For accuracy assessment, reads were simulated from the Filtered graph using the command "vg sim" by VG (v1.60.0) (Garrison et al., 2018) with error rates of 0.24% (substitutions) and 0.029% (indels). These reads were mapped to the pangenome graph using the command "vg giraffe" with the parameters "--parameter-preset fast" and to the linear reference using Minimap2 with the parameters "-ax sr --secondary=no". To ensure comparability, linear alignments were converted to graph alignment format using the command "vg inject". Alignment accuracy was then quantified using the command "vg gamcompare" against the truth set, mentioned in (Sirén et al., 2021).

Additionally, alignment rates were evaluated using 1 million read pairs subsampled from 81 resequencing datasets by seqtk (v1.4-r122, https://github.com/lh3/seqtk). These reads were mapped to the graph using vg giraffe.

Genotyping of SVs and small variant detection using the pangenome graph

The NGS reads from 81 individuals across 7 breeds/species (Supplementary Table S12) were aligned to the Filtered graph using vg giraffe.

For SV detection, graph alignments were processed using "vg pack --min-mapq=5" and genotyped using "vg call -A -a –gbz" (Hickey et al., 2020). Raw SVs were filtered using BCFtools with the parameters "PASS & QUAL ≥ 30 & (GQ/(FORMAT/DP)) > 2", and multiallelic sites were decomposed into biallelic records. We retained SVs (|length| ≥ 50) calculated by vcflength (vcflib) (Garrison et al., 2022) and merged them into a population-level dataset. Redundant records were collapsed using truvari collapse to generate a non-redundant set for downstream analysis. Final population-level filtering was applied with the parameters "F_MISSING < 0.20 & MAF ≥ 0.01". Breed-specific SV sets were generated using identical procedures.

For small variant detection, graph alignments were projected onto the reference genome (Pekin_C18) using vg surject (v1.60.0) to generate Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files. After sorting and duplicate marking with Sambamba (v1.0.1), SNPs were called using Freebayes (v1.3.8) (Garrison and Marth, 2012). We applied stringent individual-level filtering (QUAL ≥ 30 & FORMAT/DP < 100 & GQ/(FORMAT/DP) > 2 & SAF/(SAF+SAR) ≥ 0.2) followed by population-level merging and filtering (F_MISSING ≤ 0.20 & MAF ≥ 0.01) to produce the final SNP dataset.

Detection of SVs using the linear reference

To detect SVs using the linear genome approach, NGS reads was aligned to the linear reference (Pekin_C18) using Bowtie2 (v2.5.4) (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012) with the parameters "--no-mixed -X 2000". The SVs were called by Manta (v1.6.0) (Chen et al., 2016) with the default parameters.

Validation and ancestry tracing of population-level SVs

To assess the reliability of our population-level genotyping, we performed a comparative analysis against SVs decomposed directly from the Filtered pangenome graph (GraphSV). Prior to benchmarking, redundant variants in the GraphSV were merged using truvari collapse. The population-level SV set was then compared against the collapsed GraphSV with "truvari bench --sizemax 100000".

To trace variants originating from distant Anas wild ducks, we extracted 64,926 unique biallelic SVs (INS ≥ 50 bp) specific to Anas wild ducks from the Clipped graph. These SVs were subsequently compared with our population-level SV set with "truvari bench --sizemax 100000", resulting in a total of 630 retrieved SVs (AF > 0). To further characterize these retrieved SVs at the subpopulation level, we applied a threshold requiring a minimum of 20 supporting alleles (AN ≥ 20) for each SV in its respective subpopulation.

Analysis of SNP and SV density patterns

To investigate the genome-wide correlation between SNP and SV densities, we followed a previously described method (Stuart et al., 2023). Firstly, Bedtools (v2.31.1) (Quinlan and Hall, 2010) makewindows was used to make 1 Mb bins for the Pekin_C18 linear genome (including autosomes and the Z chromosome). Subsequently, Variant counts for each window were calculated with "bedtools coverage" for both the filtered population-level SNP set and the filtered population-level SV set. Finally, the correlation between binned SNP and SV densities was assessed using R (v4.4.1) with the glm() function (family = quasipoisson) and the residuals were retained for subsequent analysis. The effect size of the model was calculated using McFadden’s pseudo-R2.

Population structure of SNPs and SVs

For SNPs, the filtered population-level SNP dataset was converted to PLINK format using PLINK (v1.9) (Chang et al., 2015). PCA was performed using PLINK with the parameters "--pca --freq". The LD pruning was applied using PLINK with the parameters "--indep-pairwise 50 5 0.5". Population structure was inferred using ADMIXTURE (K = 2 to 8), results visualized using pong.

For SVs, the filtered population-level SV dataset was processed similarly: it was converted to PLINK format, followed by PCA and ADMIXTURE analysis (K = 2 to 8) as described for SNPs. No LD pruning was performed for SV datasets.

Distribution of SV across genomic feature

We extract genomic feature regions, including 5 kb upstream of transcription start sites (TSS), 5 kb downstream of transcription end sites, exons, genes, and intergenic regions, from the Pekin_C18 genome annotation file. We then analyzed the relationships between SVs of each breed and genomic features by BEDtools (Quinlan and Hall, 2010) with the parameters “bedtools intersect”.

Analysis of SV divergence

The quality-filtered population-level SV dataset of 81 samples, detected via graph pipeline was used to calculate the fixation index (FST) of SVs. We constructed six comparison pairs (Mallard vs. Pekin, Mallard vs. IND, Mallard vs. Domestic, Mallard & SB vs. Pekin, Mallard & SB vs. IND, Mallard & SB vs. Domestic) to calculate SV FST using VCFtools (v0.1.16) (Danecek et al., 2011). SVs with FST values within the top 5% in each comparison were considered highly divergent.

Transcriptome analysis

We obtained 99 transcriptome samples from Mallard and Pekin ducks at three developmental stages (two, four, and six weeks) across multiple tissues (Pectoral muscle, Liver, and Sebum) from public databases (Supplementary Table S17). These samples were categorized into 9 groups for differential expression analysis (Supplementary Table S18). Reads were aligned to the linear genome (Pekin_C18) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) (Kim et al., 2019). Gene-level raw counts were generated with feature Counts (v2.0.8) (Liao et al., 2014), which were subsequently transformed into transcripts per million (TPM) for normalization.

Sequence conservation analysis

To investigate the evolutionary conservation of the regulatory regions of the candidate gene FAM184B, the 5 kb upstream sequences flanking the TSSs were extracted from both the duck (Anas) and human (GRCh38.p14, GCF_000001405.40) genomes. Multiple sequence alignment was conducted using the ClustalW algorithm implemented in MEGA11 (Tamura et al., 2021). The alignment was performed using default parameters (DNA Weight Matrix: IUB; Gap Opening Penalty: 15.00; Gap Extension Penalty: 6.66; Transition Weight: 0.50).

Enrichment analysis

We annotated orthologs in protein sequences of duck telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome (GCF_047663525.1) using eggNOG (Cantalapiedra et al., 2021), packaged the results into an OrgDb database via R/AnnotationForge (v1.48.0). We conducted GO enrichment analysis for gene list with R/clusterProfiler (v4.14.0) (Yu et al., 2012).

Result

De novo assembly of two admixed wild duck genomes

Admixture analysis revealed varying degrees of domestic components among the wild populations. Domestic components were generally limited across the 23 wild samples; 9 individuals showed no detectable domestic background, and the other 14 individuals exhibited proportions ranging from 4.92% to 65.21% (Supplementary Fig. S1a and c). However, two wild individuals were identified as outliers with high domestic admixture: a mallard (MaM) and an eastern spot-billed duck (SBM) carrying 65.2% and 39.2% domestic genetic components, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1c and d). Whole-genome admixture analysis using 100 kb non-overlapping windows uncovered a highly heterogeneous genetic ancestry landscape in these individuals, with domestic and wild ancestry proportions across chromosomal windows exhibiting sharp fluctuations (MaM: σ = 35.17%; SBM: σ = 31.70%; Supplementary Fig. S2).

To resolve the structural complexities arising from this domestic-wild admixture, we performed de novo assembly for MaM and SBM using a combination of standard Nanopore reads and ultra-long Nanopore reads, NGS reads, Hi-C reads and Bionano reads (Supplementary Table S1). The two assemblies were all anchored on 39 autosomes and two sex chromosomes, with contig N50 lengths of 54.5 Mb and 66.0 Mb, and scaffold N50 lengths of 76.3 Mb and 76.6 Mb (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Table S2). Comprehensive evaluation revealed high completeness (BUSCO: 97.1%, 97.3%) (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Table S3) and sequence accuracy (QV: 35.4, 35.3), with robust synteny preserved across species except for micro-chromosomes and the W chromosome (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. S3 and S4; Supplementary Table S2). Generation of Anas pangenome graph

To systematically resolve the genus-wide Anas SV landscape, a comprehensive pangenome graph was constructed by integrating our two novel assemblies, MaM and SBM, with 14 high-quality public assemblies (Supplementary Table S4). These assemblies encompass: (i) six domestic ducks, including three Pekin and three indigenous breeds (two Shaoxing ducks and one Liancheng White duck); (ii) five mallard-complex wild ducks (three mallards and two spot-billed ducks); and (iii) five other Anas wild ducks (A. acuta, AAC; A. crecca, ACR; A. falcata, AFA; A. clypeata, ACL; A. formosa, AFO) (Supplementary Table S4). Using the highly continuous Pekin_C18 as the structural backbone for chromosome-level autosomes and the Z chromosome (Supplementary Fig. S5; Supplementary Table S5), we generated a base-resolution Anas pangenome graph with the Minigraph-Cactus pipeline.

Phylogenomic analysis yielded a topology consistent with established evolutionary relationships (Zhang et al., 2025), while MaM and SBM exhibited distinct phylogenetic trajectories due to their domestic introgression (Fig. 2a). Chromosomes 1-29 and the Z chromosome displayed high synteny across all assemblies (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table S6). The Anas pangenome graph represents diverse NRSs and repeat-rich variations

We generated four hierarchical graph representations: SV graph, Full graph, Clipped graph, Filtered graph (see Methods for details). The Clipped graph, produced by clipping unaligned sequences exceeding 10 kb to preserve graph topology and synteny, served as the primary reference for characterizing pangenome variations. The graph comprised 204.8 million nodes and 282.7 million edges (representing the genomic connections between segments), encompassing 1,382.7 million bases. Compared with backbone genome (Pekin_C18), the graph contained an additional 243.4 million bases of NRSs, representing 17.6% graph sequence (Supplementary Table S7).

Phylogenomic distance was strongly correlated with the accumulation of NRS. While each assembly contributed an average of 16.2 Mb of novel sequences, the assemblies of more divergent species (ACL, AFO) yielded higher amounts, with 37.7 Mb and 24.0 Mb recovered from ACL and AFO, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6b; Supplementary Table S8). To characterize variation patterns in the graph, we analyzed variants deconstructed from the graph (Pekin_C18 as reference), excluding complex nested variants and SVs exceeding 100 kb. The Full graph exhibited the largest number of variant sites (70,936,096), with comparable variant type distributions between the full and clipped graphs (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Table S9). From the 396,369 SV sites identified, we merged highly similar alleles and retained 204,572 non-reference alleles exceeding 50 bp. Among these, individual assemblies exhibited a range of 3,046 to 28,433 unique non-reference alleles (Fig. 3c). For these non-reference alleles, we observed a marked enrichment for repetitive elements, which constituted 60.86% of all identified alleles (204,572). These elements accounted for 28.38% of the total length of these alleles, which is higher than the 19.01% baseline repetitive content of the assemblies (Supplementary Table S10). The repeat landscape of these SVs was dominated by long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs, 28.47%) and simple repeats (24.17%), followed by long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons (5%) and DNA transposons (2.55%) (Supplementary Fig. S7).

The pangenome graph also exhibited robust capability in SV genotyping. We identified 67,165 SV sites across 81 individuals representing 7 species/breeds using the Filtered graph (Supplementary Table S12). Benchmarking showed that the precision, recall, and F1 score reached 83.8%, 80.0%, and 81.8%, respectively. Despite this overall trend, SVs displayed significant spatial heterogeneity, with underrepresentation across 57.5% of the genome and a marked enrichment toward chromosome ends (Fig. 5a). To examine the characteristics of SNPs and SVs detected by the graph-based pipeline at the population level, we respectively investigated genetic relationships between domestic and wild ducks using SNPs and SVs. PCA demonstrated that both SVs and SNPs clearly separated the three populations (Fig. 5, Fig. 5), consistent with previous SNP-based result (Feng et al., 2021). Notably, SVs explained substantially more variance (PC1: 8.75% vs. 3.25%, PC2: 6.48% vs. 2.97%). Although the population-level analysis encompassed only 24,782 SV sites versus 244,476 unlinked SNPs, the total length of SVs (∼8.97Mb) was ∼36.71 times greater than that of population SNPs. This suggests that SVs capture more lineage-specific genetic information, potentially explaining their higher contribution to variance in PCA.

Admixture analysis further revealed discordant patterns between SNP and SV datasets. Compared with SNPs, SV admixture analysis revealed that admixed individuals within the Mallard group exhibited fewer indigenous breeds (IND: Shaoxing and Shanma, Jingding ducks) components but a higher proportion of Pekin duck and Cherry Valley duck (Pekin + CV) ancestry. The IND group displayed stronger signals of Pekin + CV admixture, while both Pekin and CV populations showed greater evidence of admixture originating from Mallards (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. S10).

Interestingly, in the outlier samples MaM and SBM, SVs estimated lower overall domestic proportions than SNPs (0.652 to 0.479; 0.392 to 0.370). However, a window-based comparison using 100-kb non-overlapping bins revealed a contrasting trend. Within the shared 100 kb bins (1,276) where both variants were sufficiently detected, the domestic ancestry fractions derived from SVs were consistently higher than those estimated from SNPs (Supplementary Fig. S11). This indicates that within localized genomic regions, SVs identify a higher density of domestic-derived components that are less detectable via SNP-based analysis.

Recovery of missing SVs via the Anas pangenome graph reveals genetic signatures of duck domestication

Among the 67,165 population-level SVs, we identified 630 SVs derived from other Anas wild ducks. These SVs were predominantly low frequency (AF: 0-0.1 and 0.1-0.3), with lengths primarily distributed within the 50-100 bp and 100-500 bp (Supplementary Fig. S12a and b).

Further analysis of the distribution of these 630 SVs revealed that 278 were detectable in the mallard-complex wild ducks (mallards and spot-billed ducks), while 181 were retained in domestic breeds. Notably, 163 sites were shared between these two populations (Supplementary Fig. S12c and d). Given that these insertions were originally derived from distant Anas wild ducks, their extremely low frequencies in both the mallard-complex and domestic ducks indicate an extensive loss of these ancestral sequences during the divergence of the mallard-complex lineages. To explore the biological significance of this lineage-specific genetic loss, we specifically focused on 40 low-frequency insertions (AF: 0-0.1) among these 163 shared sites. For these rare ancestral remnants, we identified 31 candidate genes harboring or located adjacent to them (within 5 kb upstream or downstream). For genes in the common cluster, enrichments were detected related to cilium assembly and organization (GO:1902017, GO:0060271, and GO:0044782), gravity sensing (GO:0042332 and GO:0009629), intracellular signal transduction (GO:0032483 and GO:0032456) (Supplementary Fig. S11e).

In addition, we identified three highly differentiated SV sites (FST > 0.3) between domestic and mallard-complex wild populations. These specific SVs maintained high frequencies in wild populations (0.56; 0.44; 0.22) but were rare in domestic breeds (0.12; 0.04; 0.00), suggesting a loss of ancestral alleles during domestication. Mapping these sites to the genome revealed that they were located within the intronic regions of several candidate genes associated with muscular and neuronal functions: SH3TC1 (whose conserved paralogs regulate myelination and endosomal trafficking linked to neuropathic muscle atrophy), ABLIM2 (a key regulator of actin cytoskeleton dynamics in muscle cells), ZNF143 (a transcription factor governing mitochondrial biogenesis and gene expression), and NCALD (a calcium sensor modulating neuronal and muscular homeostasis).

We identified a total of 628 non-redundant candidate high-FST SVs sites. Among these, 64 were located within 5 kb upstream of TSSs, 50 within 5 kb downstream of TESs, and 349 within gene regions, including 24 within exons (Fig. 6a). Among them, one SV was comprised in the pangenome graph but lacked support from HiFi reads, indicating a false positive SV (Supplementary Fig. S13). Three SVs were present in the pangenome graph, but the corresponding assemblies lacked HiFi reads, precluding validation of their presence or absence (Supplementary Fig. S13). Excluding the false positive SV, 22 of the remaining 23 exonic SVs were located within 5′ or 3′ UTRs (Supplementary Fig. S13).

We examined the top 10 FST-ranked SVs with each of the six population comparisons. None of these highly divergent SVs were located within exons. The majority were situated in intronic or intergenic regions. A particularly divergent SV (FST = 0.834, INS = 75 bp) is located 5 kb upstream of FAM184B and NCAPG, at position Chr04:16267331 in the Pekin_C18 genome (Fig. 6b). Allele frequency ranges from 85.7% in mallard and ESB to 6.0% in Shaoxing duck, and 4.5% in Pekin duck and Cherry Valley duck (Fig. 6c). Notably, this SV sequence was also present in Anas wild species (Fig. 6b). Differential expression analysis across 9 comparison groups (Supplementary Table S18) revealed that FAM184B was predominantly expressed in pectoral muscle, with mallards exhibiting significantly higher expression than Pekin ducks across developmental stages (Fig. 6d).

We hypothesize that the loss of sequence during domestication may have disrupted cis-regulatory elements (CREs) upstream of FAM184B. To test this, we compared the upstream sequence of FAM184B across Anas species and aligned them with the human reference genome (GRCh38.p14, GCF_000001405.40) (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. S14). This comparison revealed a high degree of sequence identity among Anas species (Supplementary Fig. S14a). Further alignment with the human FAM184B upstream region revealed that this sequence (75 bp in wild ducks and 86 bp in humans) is adjacent to the nearest CRE (GRCh38.p14, Chr4:17,782,780-17,782,957) annotated by the ENCODE project, with an intervening genomic distance of 40 bp (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. S14b).

Discussion

Although previous duck pangenome studies have greatly advanced our understanding of genetic diversity (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2021), comprehensively capturing complex genomic variants across diverse duck populations remains a significant challenge (Wang et al., 2023b). This limitation stems primarily from the constraints of traditional linear references and earlier low-resolution graph construction methods. By integrating 16 assemblies, including two newly assembled chromosome-level assemblies of admixed individuals, we successfully captured two to six times more NRSs compared to previous studies (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2021). Our benchmarking confirms that the Anas pangenome graph significantly enhances mapping performance and SV genotyping accuracy over traditional linear references, particularly in resolving complex insertions within repetitive regions (Kosugi and Terao, 2024; Zhao et al., 2021). In addition to providing these baseline evaluations, this graph framework addresses a fundamental evolutionary question: what genetic factors provided mallards with their high potential for domestication compared to other Anas wild ducks? We identified 163 ancestral SVs that are prevalent in distant Anas species but have become low-frequency in both domestic and mallard-complex wild ducks. These SVs are significantly enriched in pathways critical for wild survival, such as gravity sensing and cilium assembly. We propose that the loss of these ancestral sequences provided the necessary physiological plasticity that allowed mallard-complex wild ducks to adapt to human-mediated environments. Our pangenome graph facilitated this finding by leveraging distant Anas wild ducks to recover ancestral genomic states that are largely lost in domestic and mallard-complex wild ducks.

An important feature of our study is the utilization of individuals with substantial domestic components, such as MaM and SBM. In traditional genomic surveys, such admixed samples are frequently treated as "outliers" and filtered out to maintain population purity. However, given the extensive gene flow reported between domestic ducks and their wild congeners in East Asia (Guo et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025), these mosaic genomes may harbor critical genetic information that was previously overlooked in traditional genomic surveys. Standard NGS pipelines mapping to linear references can detect ancestry fluctuations at the SNP level, but they frequently fail to resolve the complex structural transitions that characterize these hybrid zones.

Our study reveals that SVs and SNPs provide divergent yet complementary resolutions of this history. Although their densities are globally correlated, SVs exhibit significant spatial heterogeneity, concentrating near chromosome ends and gene-flanking regions. This non-random distribution, combined with the fact that the total sequence length of SVs far exceeds that of SNPs, explains why SV-based PCA captures substantially more genetic variance. This structural complexity further underlies the discordant admixture patterns observed. While SVs estimated a lower global proportion of domestic ancestry in wild populations due to their lower density across the background genome, they were significantly more sensitive in localized windows. This suggests that SVs can effectively pinpoint specific genomic windows rich in domestic components, which may be diluted or overlooked in global SNP-based analyses.

Moving from population-level patterns to functional impacts, we found that highly differentiated SVs in exonic regions were predominantly localized to 5′ and 3′ UTRs rather than CDS regions. This pattern suggests strong purifying selection acts against fitness-costly SVs within CDS regions, while favoring UTR-associated variations as a flexible mechanism for fine-tuning gene expression (Alonge et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023b), which represents a hallmark of rapid phenotypic evolution during domestication. Our findings in specific candidate genes exemplify this regulatory divergence. For instance, TMEM123, a broad-spectrum host antiviral factor that inhibits infections by viruses such as IAVs (Dabbagh et al., 2021), contains a highly differentiated SV in its UTR region that likely modulates viral resistance in domestic ducks. Similarly, an SV in the UTR of FAM13A, a gene implicated in adipocyte differentiation and distribution (Fathzadeh et al., 2020) and its expression negatively correlates with diet-induced obesity in mice (Wardhana et al., 2018), exhibits allele frequency difference (25.9%; 65.4%) between Shaoxing ducks (lower fat deposition rate) and Pekin ducks (higher fat deposition rate) (Chen et al., 2024), suggesting it as a primary regulator of duck body fat deposition rate.

The NCAPG-FAM184B locus further exemplifies this pattern of regulatory evolution during duck domestication. A highly differentiated SV was located 5 kb upstream of NCAPG and FAM184B which are significantly associated with growth and development traits across animals. First, NCAPG-LCORL genomic region is a well-documented locus influencing body size. Extensive genetic research has linked this locus to human stature (Gudbjartsson et al., 2008; N'Diaye et al., 2011) and developmental metrics, including infant birth (van der Valk et al., 2015) and weight (Horikoshi et al., 2013), a pattern conserved in poultry species regarding the body weight of chickens (Wang et al., 2023a) and ducks (Tarsani et al., 2025). Functional studies in sheep further demonstrate that interfering with NCAPG expression inhibits the proliferation and differentiation of embryonic myoblasts (Gong et al., 2023), underscoring its role in muscle development. Similarly, FAM184B is also implicated in growth-related traits. Specifically, it has been associated with spleen weight (An et al., 2018) and carcass weight (Alam et al., 2023) in cattle, body size divergence in donkeys (Liu et al., 2022), and metabolic body weight in broilers (Luo et al., 2025). Although its functional role remains unclear, these associations with growth and reproduction traits make FAM184B a potential target for further investigation. Focusing on this specific SV, our population data show a highly allele frequency divergence for this ancestral sequence. This sequence is highly prevalent in distant Anas wild ducks but virtually absent in domestic breeds, indicating that this SV was a target of intense artificial selection during domestication. This genomic depletion aligns tightly with our transcriptomic evidence, where FAM184B exhibits profound down-regulation in domestic pectoral muscle compared to mallards.

Cross-species alignment provides a plausible regulatory mechanism for this divergence. In the human genome, the orthologous region is highly conserved and situated in close linear proximity to an annotated CRE upstream of FAM184B. This chromosomal proximity suggests that the ancestral sequence serves as a functional extension of the nearby CRE, harboring transcription factor binding sites required for sustaining robust gene expression. Consequently, the loss of this conserved sequence during domestication likely disrupted this local cis-regulatory landscape, leading to target gene down-regulation and ultimately contributing to the phenotypic remodeling of domestic duck muscle development.

Several limitations in the study should be noted. First, due to the fragmented assembly and poor sequencing quality of avian microchromosomes, our constructed pangenome graph reliably covers only chromosomes 1 to 29 and chromosome Z. Second, the pangenome graph has an inherent limitation: it cannot easily detect completely new structural variations that are missing from the graph. While short reads can genotype known variants already in the graph, short-read data lack the length that is needed to identify these novel variations. Third, to maintain a balance between structural sensitivity and the computational efficiency of short-read mapping, we pruned the nodes supported by fewer than two assemblies, following established protocols (Hickey et al., 2024). Lastly, although the advanced subgraph sampling approach has recently emerged (Siren et al., 2024), it’s difficult to implement in the present study given our objective to maximize the representation of genetic diversity in the Anas genus. Additionally, although a gapless duck T2T genome has recently been released, and utilizing a T2T reference as a backbone has been shown to improve pangenome graph performance (Hickey et al., 2024), such a T2T backbone was not integrated into our current graph.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we present a base-resolution pangenome graph that redefines our understanding of Anas genetic diversity. Beyond technical superiority over linear references, our results demonstrate that the loss of ancestral sequences as recovered through our genus-wide graph facilitated the physiological plasticity essential for adapting to human-mediated environment. Ultimately, our findings highlight that regulatory evolution mediated by SVs in cis-acting regions, which exemplified by the NCAPG-FAM184B locus, is a primary mechanism driving the rapid phenotypic divergence in domestic ducks. This pangenome resource provides a critical structural foundation for future functional genomics and the genetic improvement of waterfowl.
