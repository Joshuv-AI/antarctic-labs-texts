Sepsis and septic shock caused by Porphyromonas gingivalis: a case report
Authors: Meifang Lin, Guang Yang, Cong Shen, Yinglun Xiao, Cha Chen, Xuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPorphyromonas gingivalis is a predominant pathogen in periodontitis and is closely associated with the progression of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).ObjectiveThis case report aims to describe a case of sepsis caused by P. gingivalis in a patient with COPD and a history of dental pain, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and clinical implications.DesignThis single case report was based on clinical data collected from medical records, with the pathogen identified from blood cultures by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). A comparative analysis was performed between the present case and previously reported cases of P. gingivalis bacteremia or sepsis based on a literature review. The patient was discharged after his general condition improved as a result of the potentially effective antimicrobial agents and anti-infective treatments through literature review.ResultsA 73-year-old man with COPD and a prolonged history of dental pain presented with a 30-year history of recurrent cough, expectoration, and dyspnoea, with symptoms exacerbating over the past 3 d and the recent onset of high fever for 1 d. Clinical evaluation revealed sepsis with rapid progression to septic shock. Blood cultures confirmed the presence of P. gingivalis.ConclusionsThis case highlights the need to consider anaerobes like P. gingivalis in septic patients with poor oral health, especially for patients with dental pain or periodontitis, and highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with slow-growing pathogens.KEYWORDS: Porphyromonas gingivalis, anaerobes, community-acquired pneumonia, sepsis, septic shock, blood culture, bloodstream infections, MALDI-TOF MS

Introduction

P. gingivalis, a gram-negative anaerobe, involves several virulence factors, such as lipopolysaccharides, fimbriae, hemagglutinins, and extracellular proteases, which enable the bacterium to colonise, invade, and cause inflammation within the host [1]. P. gingivalis is widely present in the oral cavity and is the main pathogen of chronic periodontitis, and chronic periodontitis serves as a potential risk factor for exacerbations in COPD [2]. P. gingivalis influence the pathogenesis of pneumonia and COPD through various pathogenic factors also cause a variety of systemic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, cardiovascular pathologies, and neurodegenerative pathologies etc. [1,3–5]. It is uncommon in bloodstream infections; this may be attributed to the limited sensitivity and specificity of traditional culture-based pathogen diagnosis methods, which may underestimate the prevalence of infections caused by P. gingivalis. We report a case of sepsis and septic shock caused by P. gingivalis in a man with COPD in China.

Case report

A 73-year-old man with a 30-year history of recurrent cough, expectoration, and dyspnoea was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) in the early morning hours due to worsening symptoms over the past 3 d and a persistent high fever for 1 d, with a peak temperature of 39.8 °C. A Chest CT performed 3 d prior to admission had revealed bilateral multifocal pneumonia and a small amount of pleural effusion in the right pleural cavity.

Upon admission, his temperature was 39.4 °C Laboratory tests indicated elevated inflammatory markers, including high white blood cell (WBC) and procalcitonin levels (Figure 1). Tests for Influenza A/B virus antigen, SARS-CoV−2 antigen, and Dengue virus NS1 antigen were all negative. Given the high likelihood of bacterial infection, empirical antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin/tazobactam (4.5 g every 8 h intravenously) was initiated. The patient's symptoms initially improved, and he was transferred to the Respiratory Department for continued treatment at 2:30 pm. However, his condition worsened at 7:00 pm, with a temperature spike to 39.9 °C and a respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute. One aerobic and one anaerobic blood culture were obtained, and the antimicrobial therapy was escalated to imipenem/cilastatin (1.0 g every 8 h intravenously).

On day 2, resuscitation measures were initiated due to the patient's shock. A nucleic acid detection panel for six respiratory viruses (influenza A virus, influenza B virus, respiratory syncytial virus, adenovirus, human rhinovirus, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae) was performed, all of which returned negative results. On day 3, his condition continued to deteriorate with psychological agitation, prompting his transfer back to the ICU and a diagnosis of sepsis and septic shock. On day 4, the patient became somnolent. Considering the possibility of a special pathogen, empirical treatment with Moxifloxacin (0.4 g daily intravenously) was added. After the antibiotic adjustment, his condition improved rapidly, with decreased inflammatory markers and improved liver and kidney function (Figure 1).

On day 6, the anaerobic blood culture bottle turned positive after 4.61 d of incubation. Smear examination revealed Gram-negative bacilli (Figure 1). The specimen was then inoculated onto Columbia blood agar and chocolate agar plates. We placed the plates into an anaerobic jar and then evacuated the jar using the Anoxomat® Mark III Anaerobic Culture System (Model: AN3) from MART Microbiology in the Netherlands Subsequently, the anaerobic jar was placed into an incubator set at 35 °C for cultivation. After 2 d of incubation, the isolate formed a growth on Columbia blood agar. This growth was analysed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation time-of-fight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS) of Zybio EXS3000 microbial identification system（Zybio Inc., China）and was ultimately identified as P. gingivalis, achieving a score of 2.10. Due to the slow growth characteristics of anaerobes, antimicrobial susceptibility testing for P. gingivalis was not performed. Given the negative results of routine aerobic cultures from sputum, pleural fluid, and urine (Table 1), and considering the patient's long-term use of fixed dentures, clinicians suspected that the sepsis and septic shock were caused by P. gingivalis. Due to the increasing trend of WBC and the patient's persistent fever, moxifloxacin was discontinued and replaced with ornidazole 1 g/d administered intravenously.

On day 17 of admission, the patient was free of significant fever. The sputum culture detected the presence of Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, the patient's symptoms have improved, and infection indicators continued to decrease, continued with the current anti-infective treatment plan. By day 26, the patient's sepsis and septic shock had been corrected. However, the chest CT showed no significant improvement. Considering the patient's 30-year history of COPD, it was unlikely that the pulmonary inflammation would improve in the short term. He was then transferred to a primary hospital for continued treatment. During the three-month follow-up, we learned that the patient had been discharged after recovery. We advised the patient to maintain good oral hygiene to prevent similar occurrences. The patient expressed sincere gratitude and agreed to adhere to the recommendations.

Discussion

P. gingivalis is a Gram-negative rod, obligate anaerobe, and asaccharolytic bacterium [1]. P. gingivalis grows slowly, has a long culture cycle, and has high nutritional requirements, including the need for heme or hemin and vitamin K in its culture medium, and primarily acquires energy through the metabolism of proteins and amino acids [6]. It possesses several virulence factors, including lipopolysaccharides, fimbriae, capsules, hemagglutinins, gingipains, extracellular proteases, and outer membrane vesicles. These factors enable the bacterium to colonise, invade, and induce inflammation within the host [1,7]. P. gingivalis is a significant component of the subgingival microbiota and represents one of the over 700 bacterial species found in the oral cavity. It is widely present in the mouth and can colonise the gingival epithelium even in the absence of overt disease [1,8] With high proteolytic activity, P. gingivalis is considered a keystone pathogen in the development and progression of periodontal disease [9]. Studies have shown that it is detected in 36.8% of healthy individuals and in 87.1% of those with periodontitis [10]. Moreover, P. gingivalis can influence the pathogenesis of pneumonia and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) through various pathogenic factors. It is also associated with a range of systemic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and neurodegenerative conditions [1,4,5].

According to the patient's description, he has visited the dental clinic multiple times annually due to dental pain. Two years ago, he consulted a dental physician for oral issues and had dentures installed, after which the frequency of dental pain significantly decreased. Two weeks prior to this admission, the patient developed oral ulcers. We speculate that the patient's previous oral problems were likely associated with an infection of P. gingivalis. Although the installation of dentures reduced the occurrence of oral issues, it is highly probable that P. gingivalis did not disappear but rather became a colonising bacterium. To confirm our hypothesis, we conducted an oral examination on the patient and found suspicious inflammatory exudates in the periodontal area (Figure 1). We performed anaerobic culture on these exudates but did not detect P. gingivalis. One reason is that the patient wears fixed dentures, and there are some gaps between the dentures and the gums. Our sampling swabs could not reach into these gaps and only collected specimens by swabbing the edges adjacent to the gums and dentures. Due to the superficial sampling site, it is highly likely that we did not obtain specimens from areas colonised by the pathogen, thus failing to collect valuable samples. Another possible reason is that the patient had been on antimicrobial therapy for several days. In this case, the patient's condition improved rapidly after the combination of imipenem/cilastatin (1.0 g every 8 h, intravenously) and moxifloxacin (0.4 g daily, intravenously), allowing the patient to be transferred to the general ward. The reduction in bacterial load in both the oral cavity and bloodstream likely resulted in an undetectable level of the organism during our oral sampling conducted after pathogen identification (on day 9). Studies have shown that patients with chronic periodontitis have an increased risk of developing pneumonia [2]. Specifically, the risk of community-acquired pneumonia is 4.4 times higher in patients with moderate to severe chronic periodontitis [11]. Research indicates that 45% of healthy adults experience saliva aspiration during deep sleep [12]. However, the risk of aspiration is significantly higher in the elderly population due to reduced pharyngeal sensitivity [13,14]. Previous studies have confirmed that chronic periodontitis is a risk factor for aspiration pneumonia in the elderly [15,16]. According to follow-up with the patient's family members, the patient paid insufficient attention to oral hygiene, potentially allowing periodontal pathogens to persist long-term. Although P. gingivalis can also be detected at other body sites, no other possible sites of infection were found in this patient. Therefore, we postulate that the patient may have developed aspiration pneumonia due to the aspiration of P. gingivalis colonising the oral cavity, which then entered the bloodstream from the lungs, leading to bloodstream infection and subsequently progressing to sepsis. Reducing the bacterial load of P. gingivalis through oral health management may not only benefit oral health but also help prevent lower respiratory tract diseases and more severe conditions.

Blood culture is one of the most critical tests in clinical microbiology laboratories and is considered the gold standard for diagnosing bloodstream infections and bacteraemia. Commercial blood culture systems primarily monitor changes in CO2 concentration to determine positivity. P. gingivalis produces a minimal amount of CO2 through pathways such as the decarboxylation or deamination of amino acids and the metabolism of heme [17]. The low rate of CO2 production significantly impacts detection efficiency. In this case, the blood culture bottle required 4.61 d to yield a positive result. If the detection had been delayed by just a few more hours, a false-negative report might have been erroneously issued. To enhance the positivity rate for slow-growing bacteria, blood culture bottles can be incubated for up to 7 d. In this case, only one set of blood cultures was collected on the day of admission, yet P. gingivalis was successfully detected. To increase the positivity rate of blood cultures, especially in patients with fever of unknown origin and suspected bloodstream infections, we recommend strictly adhering to blood culture collection guidelines and obtaining 2–4 sets of blood cultures from different sites. For high-risk patients with suspected bloodstream infections and severe sepsis or septic shock, we suggest concurrent nucleic acid-based pathogen detection alongside blood cultures. This approach provides a rapid diagnostic basis for clinical management and treatment.

A total of five cases of P. gingivalis bacteraemia or sepsis, including the current case, have been previously reported, as detailed in Table 2. Regarding their prior medical history, all patients had a history of gingival-related diseases. Out of these cases, three resulted in death, while two were cured (Case 4 and this study). In case 4 [18], metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was carried out on both blood and alveolar lavage fluid samples on the second day of admission, and P. gingivalis was detected in both specimens. In this case, One aerobic and one anaerobic blood culture were obtained upon admission, and the isolate was ultimately identified as P. gingivalis. In case 4 [18], a combination treatment involving levonidazole phosphate and imipenem rapidly improved the patient's condition, enabling successful weaning from mechanical ventilation and transfer to the general ward. In this case, the patient's condition improved rapidly after the combination of imipenem/cilastatin (1.0 g every 8 h, intravenously) and moxifloxacin (0.4 g daily, intravenously), allowing the patient to be transferred to the general ward. Two case of the patients was discharged after their general condition improved as a result of prompt initiation of appropriate antimicrobial agents inhibited bacterial growth. When patients with compromised immune systems (such as those with COPD, diabetes, tumours, or autoimmune diseases), particularly those with poor oral health, develop severe pneumonia that cannot be explained by common pathogens, oral pathogens should be considered. Timely implementation of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) analysis is crucial for guiding successful treatment, especially when patients are critically ill. Although mNGS is relatively expensive, its diagnostic efficacy is significantly superior to that of conventional blood cultures.

According to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 guidelines [22], the agar dilution method is the preferred technique for antimicrobial susceptibility testing of anaerobes. However, a limited number of anaerobic species, such as Bacteroides fragilis, may be tested using the microdilution broth method. Given the slow growth characteristics of anaerobes, routine in vitro susceptibility testing is generally not performed in clinical settings, and empirical therapy is often guided by established guidelines or clinical experience. P. gingivalis exhibits a complex array of resistance mechanisms, rendering it resistant to a variety of commonly used antibiotics. Moreover, the prevalence of resistance varies across different regions and populations, which adds to the complexity of treatment. Studies have shown that carbapenem antibiotics may be effective against P. gingivalis [23]. Although in this case the treatment did not significantly improve the patient's symptoms, it might have affected the concentration of the bacterium in the blood, leading to a decrease in bacterial levels, which could explain why the bacterium was not detected again in the blood culture taken on day 3. In this case, the patient's condition improved rapidly after the addition of moxifloxacin (0.4 g daily, intravenously), allowing the patient to be transferred to the general ward. The combination of o imipenem/cilastatin and moxifloxacin contributed significantly to the patient's recovery before the pathogen was identified. Multiple studies have demonstrated that P. gingivalis is highly susceptible to moxifloxacin, with extremely low resistance rates (typically 0%) [24–26]. In this case, it appears that moxifloxacin has worked well. The resistance rate to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid is also near 0% [25,27,28], indicating that moxifloxacin and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid are currently the preferred antibiotics for treating P. gingivalis infections. Research indicates that the resistance rates of P. gingivalis to various antibiotics are as follows: 0−26% for amoxicillin [24,25,29], 0−22% for azithromycin [24,29,30], 0−25% for metronidazole [24,25,27,29–31], and 0−24% for clindamycin [25–28,30,31]. Data on resistance to other antimicrobial agents are relatively scarce. In clinical practice, hospitals with the capability are encouraged to conduct antimicrobial susceptibility testing and select appropriate antimicrobial agents based on the test results to enhance therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusion

We identified a rare case of sepsis and septic shock caused by P. gingivalis. For patients with periodontal disease, there should be a heightened awareness of the possibility of anaerobic infections, such as those caused by P. gingivalis. Given that most anaerobic bacteria grow slowly, it is recommended to extend the duration of blood culture when suspecting bloodstream infections to avoid false-negative results. MALDI-TOF MS technology is beneficial for the identification of bacterial species from positive cultures. Furthermore, for patients with sepsis and septic shock, combining blood culture with molecular biological techniques, such as metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), can facilitate the rapid detection of pathogens.
