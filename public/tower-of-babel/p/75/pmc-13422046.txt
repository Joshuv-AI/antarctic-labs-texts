Opioid overdose decedents and palliative care: a population-level descriptive study
Authors: Jenny Lau, Emilie Matheson, Sarina Roslyn Isenberg, Rebecca Bagnarol, Karl Everett, Mary Scott, Peter Tanuseputro, Tara Gomes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe number of opioid overdose deaths in Canada has tripled from 2019 to 2023. Our objective was to determine the proportion of opioid overdose decedents who received palliative care, and to identify how they differed from those who did not receive palliative care.MethodsWe conducted a population-level descriptive study of opioid overdose decedents in Ontario, Canada, between 1 July 2015 and 31 December 2022. Specifically, we identified recipients of palliative care in the last 2 years of life, and age-matched and compared them to opioid overdose decedents who did not receive palliative care.ResultsAmong 11 645 opioid overdose decedents, 30.1% (n=3500) received palliative care mainly in acute care (96.9%). A mean of 4.7 days (SD 11.1) of palliative care was provided in the last 2 years of life. Compared with age-matched decedents not receiving palliative care, palliative care recipients were more likely to live in lower-income neighbourhoods, have higher morbidity, die in hospital and receive prescription opioids for pain at the end of life.ConclusionsApproximately one in three opioid overdose decedents received short-term palliative care in acute care. Future studies should examine palliative care access and delivery for people who present to the hospital with opioid overdose.Keywords: Palliative Care, Service evaluation, Opiate Overdose, Cohort Studies

Introduction

Canada declared a public health crisis in 2016 due to increased opioid overdose deaths, defined by Health Canada as a death ‘caused by intoxication/toxicity (poisoning) resulting from substance use, where one or more substances are opioids’.1 In Ontario, Canada’s most populous province, there were 15 705 such deaths from 2016 to 2023.2 Given that opioid overdose deaths are usually sudden, palliative care is historically not considered for these patients.3 Some people, however, can experience opioid overdose and have protracted dying periods from complications like hypoxic brain injury.4 Patients receiving palliative care for pre-existing illnesses can also die prematurely from opioid overdose.3 Further, people with opioid use disorder who are at high risk of opioid overdose can develop life-limiting illnesses and receive palliative care.3 Despite the continuous opioid overdose crisis, limited knowledge exists about palliative care provision to people who die from opioid overdose. We therefore conducted a study to determine the proportion of opioid overdose decedents in Ontario who received publicly funded palliative care and compared the characteristics of age-matched opioid overdose decedents who did and did not receive palliative care.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a population-level descriptive study of opioid overdose decedents in Ontario between 1 July 2015 and 31 December 2022 using administrative data.5

Setting and study participants

We identified Ontarians over 18 years old with coverage under the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) who died of opioid overdose between 1 July 2015 and 31 December 2022.6 We identified people who received palliative care in the last 2 years of life using established case definitions (online supplemental Appendix A).7 We excluded people with missing/invalid data for sex or postal code, non-Ontario residents at date of death, those ineligible for OHIP during the 3 years before death, and those whose date of last contact with the healthcare system was five or more years before death.

Data sources and ethics

Health administration datasets provided by the Canadian Institute for Health Information, Ontario Health, the Ontario Ministry of Health and the Ontario Registrar General were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analysed at Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences (detailed in online supplemental appendices B and C). Data adapted from the Statistics Canada Postal Code Conversion File contain data copied under licence from Canada Post Corporation and Statistics Canada. The use of the data in this project is authorised under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act and does not require review by a Research Ethics Board.

Statistical analyses

We used summary statistics to describe decedent demographics and their receipt of palliative care. Decedents were also age-matched 1:1 on those who did and did not receive palliative care (±2 years). We used χ2, analysis of variance and Kruskal-Wallis tests to compare characteristics, manner of death and types of substances detected between decedents who did and did not receive palliative care in their last 2 years of life. All statistical tests used an alpha level of 0.05.

Results

We identified 11 645 people who died from opioid overdose (figure 1, online supplemental table 1). Most deaths (92.0%, n=10 709) were accidental in nature, and few occurred in hospital (3.9%, n=455). Palliative care was provided to 30.1% (n=3500) of the decedents in their last 2 years of life and was delivered mostly in acute care hospitals (96.9%, n=3390). The first palliative care encounter occurred a median of 338 days (IQR 107–552 days days) before death, and the last palliative care encounter occurred a median of 148.5 days (IQR 25-379 days) before death. Opioid overdose decedents received a mean of 4.7 days (SD 11.1) of palliative care in the last 2 years of life.

Prior to matching, those receiving palliative care were slightly older (43.0 years vs 41.9 years, p<0.0001; online supplemental table 1). Among 3188 age-matched pairs, palliative care recipients were more likely to be female (33.0% vs 24.7%, p<0.0001) and to live in the lowest income quintile neighbourhoods (41.6% vs 36.1%, p<0.0001). They had more comorbidities in the last 3 years of life (Charlson score ≥2: 17.0% vs 2.1%, p<0.0001 including hospitalisations (22.6% vs 3.2%, p<0.0001), emergency department visits (27.8% vs 13.7%, p<0.0001) and opioid agonist therapy (OAT) (42.0% vs 31.7%, p<0.0001) for opioid use disorder. They were also more likely to have emergency department visits and hospitalisations related to benzodiazepines (6.0% vs 1.5%, p<0.0001), stimulants (26.8% vs 8.5%, p<0.001) and alcohol (28.0% vs 11.3%, p<0.001).

Palliative care recipients were more likely to die in hospital (5.6% vs 3.3%, p<0.0001). Non-pharmaceutical opioids were detected in most deaths in both groups, but less often among palliative care recipients (74.3% vs 77.4%, p=0.004). A total of 1038 opioid overdose decedents received prescription opioids indicated to treat pain in the last 90 days of life; palliative care recipients were more likely to receive these prescription opioids (58.6% vs 48.1%, p<0.0001). We did not identify significant differences in specific pharmaceutical opioids detected in postmortem toxicology between opioid overdose decedents who did and did not receive prescription opioids.

Discussion

Our study found 30.1% of Ontarians who died of opioid overdose received palliative care in the last 2 years of life. Almost all (96.9%) palliative care was delivered in emergency departments or acute care hospitals. Although opioid overdose decedents had their first palliative care encounter a median of 338 days (IQR 107–552 days) before they died, the average amount of palliative care that they received was only a total of 4.7 days (SD 11.1) in the last 2 years of their lives. Compared with those who did not receive palliative care, opioid overdose decedents who received palliative care had higher morbidity, including harms associated with opioids and other substances. They were also slightly more likely to receive prescription opioids and die in hospital.

This study demonstrates that palliative care is provided to a considerable proportion of people who die of opioid overdose. Our findings suggest that palliative care providers may have an opportunity to assist with identifying people with life-limiting illnesses who are at risk of opioid overdose. If patients are high risk, providers can offer naloxone education and connect them with addiction and harm reduction resources and services.8

Our finding that opioid overdose decedents received most of their palliative care in hospital is aligned with existing knowledge that people who use drugs tend to rely more on acute care than the general population.9 Even when acutely unwell, patients with substance use disorders often discharge themselves before medically advised—a pattern attributed to stigma and discrimination encountered in healthcare settings, cravings to use drugs, uncontrolled pain and undertreatment of opioid withdrawal during acute care admissions.10 11 This healthcare use pattern may partly explain why opioid overdose decedents received fewer palliative care days in their final 2 years than the Ontario average of 35 days.7 12 Other possible reasons why palliative care provision was limited for opioid overdose decedents are that these patients may not have wanted further follow-up with palliative care or palliative care follow-up was not deemed necessary by their healthcare providers after discharge from acute care. Regardless, it is critical that healthcare for people who use drugs is non-judgemental, trauma-informed and promotes harm reduction strategies and practices.13 Treating pain and opioid withdrawal should be prioritised to alleviate distress and build a therapeutic alliance to address the primary reasons for hospitalisation.12 These strategies may increase engagement with healthcare, including palliative care.

Education and novel clinical strategies are needed to support palliative care providers in caring for patients at risk of opioid overdose. Our study found no significant difference in prescription opioid detection between decedents who received palliative care and those who did not in their postmortem toxicology assessments. This finding suggests that palliative care involvement and end-of-life opioid prescribing likely have minimal impact on opioid overdose deaths from prescription opioids. In the era of a toxic drug supply, the greater concern is that patients may use unregulated opioids to manage pain or withdrawal, putting them at a high risk of death due to this supply being increasingly potent and unpredictable.14 To address knowledge gaps, existing expert consensus recommendations guide palliative care providers on identifying at-risk populations, prescribing and monitoring opioid use, and managing pain for patients on OAT.8 15

Our study used routinely collected health administrative databases, which come with several limitations. Our databases do not contain data about gender, goals of care, specific reasons for why palliative care was involved or information on care received outside of formal healthcare (ie, from friends, family, outreach and housing workers). We were unable to differentiate between people who required palliative care for life-threatening complications related to opioid overdose and those with pre-existing life-limiting illnesses who experienced opioid overdose. Further, our data could only capture palliative care delivered, and not palliative care declined by patients.

Conclusion

One in three opioid overdose decedents received palliative care in their last 2 years of life. Although most palliative care was delivered in acute care, almost everyone died in the community. This study highlights the need for palliative care providers to know how to identify and care for patients at risk of opioid overdose, especially those in acute care. Future research is needed to determine which patients at risk of opioid overdose would benefit the most from palliative care, the role and benefit of palliative care involvement, and how to optimally deliver palliative care to people who are at risk of or experience opioid overdose.
