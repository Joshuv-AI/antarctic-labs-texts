Inferior Clinical Outcomes and Increased Conversion to Total Hip Arthroplasty Following Hip Arthroscopy for Femoroacetabular Impingement Syndrome in Patients With Inflammatory Joint Disease: A Minimum 5-Year Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Ady H. Kahana-Rojkind, Andrew R. Schab, Drashti Sikligar, Roger Quesada-Jimenez, Benjamin D. Kuhns, Benjamin G. Domb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate midterm clinical outcomes of hip arthroscopy for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) in patients with inflammatory joint diseases (IJD) compared to a matched control group without IJD.MethodsData were prospectively collected on all hip arthroscopies performed between February 2008 and December 2018 at a single center. Patients diagnosed with IJD undergoing primary hip arthroscopy for FAIS with labral tears were included. Exclusion criteria were prior ipsilateral hip conditions (except IJD), hip dysplasia (lateral center-edge angle <18°), and Tönnis grade >1. Patients with a minimum 5-year follow-up were matched 1:3 to controls based on age, sex, body mass index, and Tönnis grade. Patient-reported outcomes (PROs), rates of achieving clinically relevant thresholds, and secondary procedures were compared between groups.ResultsA total of 27 hips (23 patients) with IJD were matched to 81 hips (79 patients) in the control group. At a minimum 5-year follow-up, both groups showed significant improvement in all PROs. However, the IJD group had lower postoperative PRO scores and fewer patients meeting patient acceptable symptom state for modified Harris Hip Score and Non-Arthritic Hip Score (29.6% vs 61.7% and 40.7% vs 64.2%). The IJD group had significantly higher odds of requiring a second hip surgery (odds ratio, 3.57; 95% confidence interval, 1.04-12.23; P = .042) and lower odds of maintaining arthroplasty-free survivorship (odds ratio, 0.14; 95% confidence interval, 0.04-0.56; P = .003).ConclusionsPatients with IJD undergoing hip arthroscopy for FAIS experience significant improvement in PROs at midterm follow-up. However, they achieve clinically significant thresholds less frequently, exhibit increased revision arthroscopy rates, and have higher rates of conversion to total hip arthroplasty compared to the control group.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative case series.

Inflammatory joint diseases (IJDs) such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus are severe, debilitating conditions characterized by systemic inflammation and progressive joint degradation, resulting in pain, disability, diminished quality of life, and a reduced life span.1, 2, 3 Advancements in medical management, particularly with disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, have improved outcomes and reduced the need for surgical interventions.4,5 However, despite this progress, patients often still require total hip arthroplasty (THA) due to progressive joint disease.6 The outcomes and complications of patients with IJD undergoing total hip arthroplasty have been extensively studied, revealing higher perioperative complication rates related to cardiovascular disease, bone loss, poor soft tissue quality, and qualitative or quantitative deficiencies of the immune system.7, 8, 9, 10 Additionally, the use of immunosuppressive medication may increase the risk of perioperative infection, whereas the discontinuation of these medications perioperatively, as sometimes advised, may predispose patients to postoperative flare-ups and potentially impact recovery.6

Hip arthroscopy has emerged as a viable treatment option for addressing labral pathology and femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) in patients with inflammatory joint diseases.

In the general population, treating FAIS with hip arthroscopy has been proven to yield excellent outcomes.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Yet, the outcomes of hip arthroscopy among patients with IJD have been minimally explored beyond short-term follow-up.17, 18, 19 The purpose of this study was to evaluate midterm clinical outcomes of hip arthroscopy for FAIS in patients with IJDs compared to a matched control group without IJDs. We hypothesize that hip arthroscopy in patients with inflammatory joint disease will result in significant improvements in pain and function at a minimum 5-year follow-up, but the magnitude of improvement may be lower compared to the control group due to the systemic nature of IJD and potential disease-related complications.

Methods

All patients included in this study participated in the American Hip Institute Registry. Data on some of these patients (n = 19) were reported in prior studies,17 but the present study is a unique analysis. All data collection and reviews retrospectively received institutional review board approval. No funding was received for this study.

Patient Selection Criteria

Patient data were prospectively collected from patients who underwent hip arthroscopy from February 2008 to December 2018 (Fig 1). Patients were excluded if they had previous ipsilateral hip conditions besides IJD, a lateral center-edge angle (LCEA) <18°, previous ipsilateral hip surgery, or preoperative Tönnis osteoarthritis grade >1. The inclusion criteria for this study were patients undergoing primary hip arthroscopy with a rheumatologist-confirmed diagnosis of IJD (rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus) at the time of surgery. Additional inclusion criteria included completion of preoperative and a minimum 5-year follow-up of patient-reported outcomes (PROs). The PROs assessed in this study included the modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), Non-Arthritic Hip Score (NAHS), Hip Outcome Score–Sports-Specific Subscale (HOS-SSS), and visual analog scale (VAS) for pain.20,21 Patient satisfaction was rated on a 0 to 10 scale, with 0 being completely dissatisfied with their surgical outcomes and 10 being completely satisfied with their surgical outcome.22, 23, 24 Rates of meeting previously defined patient acceptable symptom state (PASS), substantial clinical benefit (SCB) thresholds, and maximal outcome improvement were compared.22, 23, 24, 25, 26 Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was calculated via the distribution method using preoperative PROs.27 Complications were postoperatively documented in clinical visits and follow-up questionnaires.Fig 1Patient selection flow diagram.

Patient selection flow diagram.

Surgical Technique

All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia in the supine position on a traction table by the senior author (B.G.D.). For each arthroscopy, a minimum of 2 portals were used (the anterolateral and mid-anterior portals). Other portals were used on a case-by-case basis. Interportal capsulotomy with a blade was used to access the joint. While in the joint, diagnostic arthroscopy was used to initially assess the labrum, intra-articular cartilage, and ligamentum teres. Labral tears were classified using the Seldes classification system.28 Femoral head chondral pathologies were noted with the acetabular labrum articular disruption and Outerbridge classifications.29,30 Tear of the ligamentum teres were defined using the Domb and Villar classifications.31,32

With the use of a burr under fluoroscopic guidance, acetabular osseous deformities and femoral head-neck osseous deformities were corrected. Considering labral pathology, labral tears were selectively debrided or repaired to maximize stability based on arthroscopic findings. If the labral tear was unrepairable, hamstring allografts were used to perform a labral reconstruction. If full-thickness chondral defects were present, microfractures were performed. Patients who had a presurgical history of painful, internal snapping hip syndrome received an iliopsoas fractional lengthening. Iliopsoas fractional lengthening was performed by cutting the iliopsoas tendon at the muscle-tendon junction, leaving the muscular region intact. Ligamentum teres tears were treated with debridement. With a multifactorial consideration of age, range of motion, generalized ligamentous laxity, acetabular coverage, and arthritic changes, the capsule was repaired or plicated.

Matching Design

Using R (version 4.3.2), propensity matching to a cohort of patients without inflammatory joint disease was used to control for potentially confounding variables. IJD hips were matched to control hips in a 1:3 ratio based on age at the time of surgery, sex, preoperative Tönnis grade, and body mass index (BMI) as matching parameters with an optimal pair matching algorithm. Matching was performed until no further matches could be made.

Statistical Analysis

An a priori power analysis was used to determine the sample size needed in each matched group to achieve 80% power with an α of 0.05. A treatment sample size of 17 was determined to achieve 80% power. For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to test for normal distribution, with P > .05 indicating normality. The paired 2-tailed t test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare continuous data between groups. To detect significant differences between categorical variables, the χ2 and Fisher exact tests were used. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and proportions, were reported when relevant.

Using the distribution method, the MCID for the IJD and control groups was 8.97 and 6.71 for the mHHS, 10.02 and 6.68 for the NAHS, and 12.07 and 9.95 for the HOS-SSS. SCB and PASS values for the mHHS, NAHS, and HOS-SSS were compared to previously defined values from the literature.25,33,34

Radiographic Findings

Preoperatively, the IJD group had 5 (18.5%) patients with Tönnis grade 1, an average LCEA of 32.91° ± 8.53°, an average α angle of 59.15° ± 17.60°, and an average Tönnis angle of 5.50° ± 4.96°. The control group had 9 (11.1%) patients with Tönnis grade 1, an average LCEA of 31.69° ± 6.17°, an average α angle of 58.69° ± 10.85°, and an average Tönnis angle of 4.30° ± 5.11°. There were no significant differences between the 2 cohorts for preoperative Tönnis grade (P = .51), LCEA (P = .42), α angle (P = .88), and Tönnis angle (P = .62).

Postoperatively, the IJD group had 4 (15.4%) patients with Tönnis grade 1, an average LCEA of 30.47° ± 6.59°, an average α angle of 44.04° ± 5.63°, and an average Tönnis angle of 4.88° ± 3.60°. The control group had 7 (10.4%) patients with Tönnis grade 1, an average LCEA of 30.46° ± 5.91°, an average α angle of 43.67° ± 5.16°, and an average Tönnis angle of 4.70° ± 4.30°. There were no significant differences between the 2 cohorts for preoperative Tönnis grade (P = .76), LCEA (P = .99), α angle (P = .77), and Tönnis angle (P = .84).

Complications

Discussion

The most important finding of this study is that patients with IJD had inferior outcomes following hip arthroscopy compared to matched controls, despite achieving significant improvements in pain and function. At a minimum 5-year follow-up, patients with IJD had lower postoperative PROs (mHHS 73.4 vs 85.2; NAHS 75.0 vs 86.8; HOS-SSS 57.1 vs 73.8), achieved PASS for NAHS and mHHS less frequently, and had significantly higher rates of revision arthroscopy (22.2% vs 7.4%) and conversion to THA, with a 74.1% arthroplasty-free survivorship rate compared to 95.1% in controls. These findings suggest that hip arthroscopy may lead to functional improvements in patients with IJD, but its efficacy may be limited in this population. The underlying systemic inflammation and progressive cartilage and soft tissue degeneration characteristic of diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and lupus likely contribute to suboptimal outcomes. Additionally, the higher prevalence of severe cartilage damage, labral tearing, and ligamentum teres pathology observed intraoperatively in the IJD group may also help explain the increased failure rate, despite matching for key demographic and radiographic variables. In the general population, hip arthroscopy has been established in the literature as an effective treatment for FAIS. Jan et al.15 carried out a systematic review of 15 studies focusing on the mid-term outcomes of hip arthroscopy and reported that patients undergoing primary hip arthroscopy for FAIS experienced substantial improvement at the 5-year time point and met MCID, PASS, and SCB at high rates. Also in this study, the survival rate of hip arthroscopy at the 5-year time point was consistently high, with conversion to THA or revision arthroscopy occurring in 0.0% to 17.9% and 1.3% to 26.7%. When examining the outcomes of hip arthroscopy in patients with IJD, Ashberg et al.17 compared a cohort of 26 hips in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) to a benchmark control group of 52 hips with RA with a 2-year minimum follow-up. In this study, patients with RA exhibited nonsignificant improvement in all PROs and lower average postoperative VAS when compared to a control group at the latest follow-up. Additionally, there was a notable trend toward a higher frequency of secondary arthroscopy procedures among patients with RA (19.2% vs 7.7%, P = .47), although rates of total hip arthroplasty were similar (P > .99). Zhou et al.19 presented a retrospective clinical study examining 40 hips with inflammatory arthritis, with an average follow-up of 67.2 ± 8.4 months. Harris Hip and Oxford scores showed significant improvement, increasing from 66.9 ± 12.1 and 69.4 ± 16 to 78.4 ± 19.3 and 80.2 ± 18.8 at the latest follow-up (P < .05), respectively. Another study by Kouk et al.18 showed no significant difference in 2-year survivorship (76.2% vs 83.3%, P = .496) and significant improvement in the mHHS and NAHS for both groups at the 2-year timeline.

The findings in this study add to the literature by displaying that patients undergoing hip arthroscopy for the treatment of FAIS and labral tears with IJD experience improvement at midterm follow-up. However, the IJD group displayed significantly lower mean postoperative PROs and, contrary to short-term studies, revealed a significant rate of revision arthroscopy (odds ratio, 3.57) and arthroplasty-free survivorship (odds ratio, 0.14). This discrepancy may be attributed to the degenerative nature of IJDs, which can potentially lead to less favorable outcomes beyond short-term time frames. The progressive course of inflammatory joint diseases, with ongoing damage accumulation, can result in the eventual need for revision surgery or conversion to arthroplasty. Our findings also stand in contrast to those of Rynecki et al.,36 who reported no significant differences in reoperation or conversion to THA rates at the 5-year follow-up between patients with systemic inflammatory diseases and matched controls undergoing hip arthroscopy for FAIS. In their cohort of 15 patients with systemic inflammatory diseases and 25 controls, THA conversion rates were 7% and 12%, respectively (P ≥ .999), and survivorship curves for both reoperation and THA did not differ significantly. In our study, however, patients with IJD had significantly lower arthroplasty-free survivorship (74.1% vs 95.1%) and higher revision arthroscopy rates compared to controls. Several factors may account for this discrepancy, including differences in the underlying inflammatory disease spectrum, medication use, or surgical indications. Notably, our cohort included a higher proportion of patients with rheumatoid arthritis and lupus, whereas the Rynecki et al.36 cohort included a broader mix of conditions (including Crohn disease and sarcoidosis), some of which may have a less direct impact on joint integrity. As noted in the results, patients with IJD had a significantly higher incidence of severe cartilage damage, labral tears, and ligamentum teres tears. Since we controlled for confounding variables such as age, BMI, and sex through the matching process, these results are more likely to be attributed to the inherent characteristics of IJDs; these factors serve as well-established risk factors for the premature onset of osteoarthritis and the necessity of a THA. It is crucial to recognize that while the treatment of labral tears and correction of bony morphology in the setting of FAIS can alleviate symptoms and improve outcomes, it does not address the underlying inflammatory condition itself. Nearly 1 in 4 patients with IJD required a second hip surgery within 5 years, and only 74% remained arthroplasty-free, significantly lower than the 95% rate observed in controls. These findings raise important considerations regarding the appropriateness of hip arthroscopy as an index procedure in patients with inflammatory joint disease. While statistically significant improvements were observed in PROs at midterm follow-up, these improvements were consistently inferior to those seen in the matched control group. The IJD cohort had lower postoperative functional scores, lower rates of achieving clinically meaningful thresholds, and a markedly higher risk of both revision arthroscopy and conversion to total hip arthroplasty. These results suggest that for patients with underlying systemic inflammatory pathology, hip arthroscopy may offer only temporary symptomatic relief without altering the longer-term progression toward joint replacement. In certain patients—particularly those with advanced or poorly controlled disease—primary total hip arthroplasty may warrant consideration as a more definitive and durable treatment strategy.

Building on prior studies, this study provides further insight into the midterm outcomes of hip arthroscopy in patients with inflammatory joint diseases. The sample size exceeded the predetermined requirement established through a priori power analysis, effectively mitigating the risk of type II errors. The use of various validated functional hip outcome measures further bolstered the credibility of the findings. Additionally, the study provides valuable insights by documenting the rates of achieving clinically significant thresholds for multiple outcome scores within the same population. These thresholds provide tangible benchmarks that go beyond statistical significance, offering clinicians valuable information regarding the patient’s perception of their function, pain, and condition.

Limitations

We recognize several limitations in this study. First, the disease status—whether it was well controlled or not—was not specified, potentially influencing the prognosis. Poorly controlled inflammatory arthropathies often correlate with inferior surgical outcomes. Second, we were unable to evaluate patient compliance with medication regimens, and dosing was left to the discretion of their treating physician. While we were able to calculate cohort-specific MCID values, our small sample size limited the ability to generate accurate receiver operating characteristic curves for PASS and SCB calculations. Therefore, we compared our results to previously published PASS and SCB thresholds. Lastly, patients were not specifically matched for preoperative diagnoses, such as cam versus pincer impingement. However, we addressed this limitation by meticulously comparing intra-articular findings during arthroscopy.

Conclusions

Patients with IJD undergoing hip arthroscopy for FAIS experience significant improvement in PROs at midterm follow-up. However, they achieve clinically significant thresholds less frequently, exhibit increased revision arthroscopy rates, and have higher rates of conversion to THA compared to the control group.
