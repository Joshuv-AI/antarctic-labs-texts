Relative importance of blood-based biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease-specific neurodegeneration and cognitive decline
Authors: Keun You Kim, Hyunsun Ham, Eun Jin Yoon, Eosu Kim, Yu Kyeong Kim, Jun-Young Lee, Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough blood-based biomarkers are now available for diagnosing Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the best biomarker for AD-specific neurodegeneration and cognitive decline remains unclear. This study aimed to determine the relative importance of four plasma biomarkers—phosphorylated tau (p-tau) 217, p-tau181, neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)—in AD-specific neurodegeneration and cognition.MethodsWe analyzed cross-sectional data from two independent, ethnically distinct cohorts spanning the clinical spectrum from cognitively unimpaired to dementia: 150 participants from the SAMD cohort (100% Asian) and 284 participants from the ADNI cohort (94.0% White). Plasma biomarker levels were quantified using Single-Molecule Array (Simoa) assays. We employed dominance analysis to determine the hierarchical contributions of these biomarkers to AD signature regions of interest (ROI) thickness (entorhinal, inferior temporal, middle temporal, and fusiform regions), total cognition, and memory, stratified by amyloid-PET status. Three sensitivity analyses were further conducted to validate the findings across these cohorts, mitigating potential biases arising from differences in demographic characteristics and clinical severity. All analyses were adjusted for age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 status.ResultsThe dominance hierarchy differed markedly according to the amyloid status. Plasma GFAP and p-tau217 emerged as the dominant predictors for AD signature ROI thickness and cognitive impairment in amyloid-positive participants. Specifically, GFAP demonstrated superior dominance in explaining cortical atrophy within the SAMD cohort, whereas p-tau217 was the dominant predictor in the ADNI cohort. P-tau217 generally outperformed the others in explaining total cognition and memory in the amyloid (+) group. In contrast, among amyloid (−) participants, plasma NfL showed greater explanatory power than GFAP for both neurodegeneration and cognitive decline across both cohorts.ConclusionThe efficacy of plasma biomarkers in reflecting AD-related neurodegeneration varies significantly depending on the presence of amyloid pathology. While GFAP and p-tau217 are robust indicators of AD-associated changes linked to plaque pathology, NfL better reflects non-specific neurodegeneration involving axonal damage. Consequently, a stratified approach based on amyloid status is essential for the optimal application of blood-based biomarkers in monitoring disease progression and evaluating therapeutic efficacy in future clinical trials and precision medicine.

Introduction

The revised diagnostic criteria for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) emphasize that the confirmation of core AD pathology is essential for diagnosing AD [1]. These criteria also categorize biomarkers such as amyloid (A), tau (T), neurodegeneration (N), and inflammation (I), which can be used not only to diagnose AD but also to predict disease progression and monitor treatment responses [[2], [3], [4]]. In particular, blood-based biomarkers (BBMs) have advantages over conventional cerebrospinal fluid or positron emission tomography (PET) due to their lower invasiveness, cost-effectiveness, and greater accessibility [5]. As the first BBM for the diagnosis of AD was recently approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration [6], BBMs are expected to be routinely used in real-world clinical settings [5,7].

Although patients with typical AD present slowly progressive amnestic problems with cortical atrophy in specific regions [8,9], the question of which BBM is the best at explaining AD-related neurodegeneration or cognitive impairment remains largely unanswered. Previous studies on BBMs have mainly focused on predicting the amyloid or tau status. It is important to identify the appropriate BBM for validating AD-specific neurodegeneration or cognitive function to monitor the efficacy of anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody treatments. The blood levels of neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), which are fluid biomarkers of neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation [1], can be increased not only in AD but also in various other neurodegenerative diseases [10]. Some studies have reported associations between blood levels of phosphorylated tau (p-tau) species, NfL, or GFAP and AD-specific neurodegeneration [[11], [12], [13]] or cognitive impairment [13,14]. However, these studies only presented the correlation coefficients of each biomarker without direct comparisons.

In this study, we examined the association between four BBMs (p-tau217, p-tau181, NfL, and GFAP) and AD-specific neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment. In addition to total cognitive function, we focused on memory, which is characteristically impaired in patients with AD. We simultaneously explored these associations and employed dominance analysis to determine the relative importance of the four BBMs. Given the unique disease progression of AD initiated by amyloid deposition [15], we conducted subgroup analyses to explore the differences in the relative importance of the four BBMs according to amyloid status. The primary aim of this study was to determine the relative importance of BBMs in explaining AD-specific neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment. A secondary aim was to evaluate whether the associations between BBMs and these outcomes could be generalized across two distinct cohorts: an Asian population (Software as a Medical Device [SAMD] lab cohort) and a predominantly White population (Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative [ADNI] cohort).

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional observational study included 152 participants from the SAMD lab cohort (https://samdlab.snu.ac.kr/) from the Seoul Metropolitan Government-Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center, Seoul, South Korea. We recruited participants aged 50 years or older who met the following criteria: no visual or auditory impairment that could interfere with cognitive function assessments and suitability for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or PET scans. We excluded participants with acute medical or psychiatric conditions that could affect cognitive function, history of major psychiatric or neurological diseases. Those suspected of having non-Alzheimer pathology during clinical interviews, such as Lewy body disease or frontotemporal dementia, were also excluded. Participants were classified as either cognitively unimpaired (CU), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or AD dementia. The diagnosis of MCI and AD dementia was based on the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) criteria [16,17]. Cognitive functions were assessed using the Korean version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD-K) neuropsychological battery [18,19].

Independent data of 290 participants were obtained from the ADNI database (http://adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial MRI, PET, BBMs, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of MCI and dementia. ADNI participants were recruited from multiple sites in the United States and Canada, aged 55 to 90 years, without a history of major psychiatric or neurological conditions that could affect cognition. Participants with a high risk of vascular dementia were also excluded. The ADNI data included in this study were obtained from the ADNI phases 2, GO, and 3. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study participants have been outlined elsewhere [20].

To bring the two cohorts into alignment, we excluded eight participants (two from the SAMD lab and six from the ADNI) with extreme outlier BBM levels, defined as below the first quartile - 5 interquartile range or over the third quartile + 5 interquartile range [21]. The final sample size for analysis in this study was 150 in the SAMD lab and 284 in the ADNI. Global clinical dementia rating (CDR) and CDR sum of boxes (CDR-SB) were measured to assess clinical severity in both the SAMD lab and ADNI cohorts. The apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status of each participant was categorized as non-carrier, heterozygote (having one ε4 allele), or homozygote (having two ε4 alleles).

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of each participating institution. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

MRI acquisition and AD-specific neurodegeneration analysis

The SAMD lab participants underwent brain MRI scans using an Ingenia 3T CX scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands). Cortical thickness was assessed using 3D T1-weighted images calculated by FreeSurfer parcellation based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas (v.7.4.1, https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). The ADNI participants underwent 1.5T or 3T MRI at each site (protocol available at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/data-samples/adni-data/neuroimaging/mri/), with cortical thickness assessed using FreeSurfer v7.2.0 by the University of California at San Francisco team.

To assess AD-specific neurodegeneration, the cortical thickness of the AD signature ROI was calculated in the following regions, which are typically vulnerable to atrophy related to AD [9]: bilateral entorhinal, inferior temporal, middle temporal, and fusiform regions.

Amyloid status assessment

We assessed cerebral amyloid deposition using amyloid PET. In the SAMD lab cohort, a Biograph Vision 450 (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) scanner with [18F] florbetaben tracer was used. The acquired PET images were registered to the segmented and parcellated anatomical MRI images of the same participants, processed using FreeSurfer v7.4.1. In the ADNI cohort data, multiple scanners with [18F] florbetapir tracer were used (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/data-samples/adni-data/neuroimaging/pet/). PET images were processed by the team from University of California Berkeley, using FreeSurfer v7.1.0. The composite standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was calculated as the voxel-weighted mean cortical uptake value from the frontal, parietal, temporal, anterior cingulate, and posterior cingulate cortical regions, with the whole cerebellum as a reference region [22,23]. SUVR values were converted to Centiloids [[24], [25], [26]], and a threshold of ≥ 30 defined amyloid (+) [27].

Total cognition and memory assessment

In SAMD lab participants, total cognition was measured using the total CERAD-K score [18], summing the six subtests (Verbal Fluency, Boston Naming Test, Word List Learning, Word List Recall, Word List Recognition, and Constructional Praxis). The total CERAD-K score was expressed as an age-, sex-, and education-adjusted z-score [19]. Memory function was assessed using the composite memory score, derived from the mean z-scores of four subtests: Word List Learning, Word List Recall, Word List Recognition, and Constructional Praxis Recall [19]. Higher scores indicate better performance on both measures.

In ADNI participants, total cognition was assessed using the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog), which includes 13 subtests, range from 0 to 85, with higher scores indicating poorer performance. Memory function was assessed using the Logical Memory Delayed Recall test (score range from 0 to 25, with higher scores indicating better performance).

Blood sampling procedure and BBM measurements

Blood samples were collected after overnight fasting in EDTA tubes and centrifuged for 15 min (2000 g in SAMD lab; 1500 g in ADNI). Plasma samples were transferred and immediately stored in a −80 °C deep freezer until analysis. Plasma p-tau217 levels were measured using the Alzpath p-tau217 Single Molecular Array (Simoa) assay. Plasma p-tau181 levels were measured using the Simoa p-tau181 Advantage kit. Plasma NfL and GFAP levels were quantified using the Neurology 4-Plex E Advantage kit. All assays were performed using the HD-X instrument (Quanterix).

Statistical analyses

We employed Spearman partial correlation models to assess the association of BBMs with AD signature ROI thickness, total cognition (total CERAD-K in the SAMD lab; ADAS-Cog in the ADNI), and memory (composite memory score in the SAMD lab; Logical Memory - Delayed Recall in the ADNI). All Spearman models were adjusted for age, sex, years of education, and APOE ε4 status. ADAS-Cog scores were inverted (85 ˗ raw score) so that higher scores indicated better performance. Inter-biomarker correlations among the four BBMs were also evaluated using Spearman partial correlation, stratified by amyloid status, to assess the extent of shared variance among biomarkers.

We applied dominance analysis [28,29] to determine the relative importance of four BBMs (p-tau217, p-tau181, NfL, and GFAP) in explaining the variance of three outcomes: AD signature ROI thickness, total cognition, and memory. Covariates were age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 status. For each outcome, the four BBMs and four covariates were entered as predictors, yielding 28 = 256 subset linear regression models defined by the inclusion or exclusion of each variable. Among eight variables, we present the relative importance of the four BBMs, which were the variables of interest in this study. Dominance analysis was specifically chosen to address potential multicollinearity among BBMs reflecting overlapping AD-related pathologies. Because dominance analysis evaluates each biomarker's incremental contribution to R2 across all subset models, it provides a more robust ordering of biomarker importance under collinearity. Specifically, when determining the dominance hierarchy between two biomarkers, incremental R2 contribution from one biomarker was compared with that from the other across 26 = 64 subset base models composed of the remaining six variables. We assessed dominance at three hierarchical levels: complete, conditional, and general dominance. Complete dominance, the most stringent criterion, is established when one biomarker’s incremental contribution to R2 exceeds that of the other biomarker across all possible subset models. Conditional dominance, a less strict criterion, is established when the average incremental contribution of one biomarker within every model size exceeds that of another. General dominance, the least stringent level, is determined by comparing the overall average incremental contribution between biomarkers. The same analyses were repeated in amyloid (+) and amyloid (−) subgroups. The BBM levels were log-transformed and continuous variables were standardized as z-scores within each cohort prior to model fitting.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted in three ways to evaluate the robustness of our findings against potential confounding by disease stage differences between the two cohorts. In sensitivity analysis 1, we additionally adjusted for CDR-SB in the complete dominance models for AD signature ROI thickness. Since CDR-SB is inherently related to cognitive measures, the dominance models for total cognition and memory were not included in sensitivity analysis 1 due to potential over-adjustment. In sensitivity analysis 2, we restricted complete dominance analyses to participants with global CDR 0.5, which is the most common clinical stage in both cohorts (n = 106 in SAMD; n = 116 in ADNI). These sensitivity analyses enable the direct comparison between the two cohorts within the same clinical stage. In sensitivity analysis 3, Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was employed to minimize the demographic and clinical disparities between the two cohorts. Propensity scores were yielded by logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, education, and global CDR. To balance the reduction in sample size and exact matching, we applied 1:2 PSM with the nearest neighbor method (caliper of 0.2). PSM yielded sample size of 61 from a total of 150 in the SAMD lab and 103 from a total of 284 in the ADNI cohort. The Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was calculated to confirm successful matching. Following the PSM, identical dominance analyses were conducted.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0, with statistical significance at p < 0.05.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we investigated the association between four BBMs (p-tau217, p-tau181, NfL, and GFAP) and cortical atrophy in AD-related regions and cognition using two independent cohorts. Spearman models showed that most BBMs were significantly associated with AD signature ROI thickness and total cognition across both cohorts. Dominance analyses revealed contrasting results according to amyloid status: in amyloid(+) participants, plasma GFAP or p-tau217 was dominant for AD signature ROI thickness, and p-tau217 was dominant for total cognition and memory in both cohorts. In contrast, plasma NfL was more sensitively associated with AD signature ROI thickness, total cognition, and memory in amyloid(−) participants.

In Spearman models, all BBMs in the SAMD lab and p-tau217, p-tau181, and NfL in the ADNI showed significant associations with AD signature ROI thickness (Fig. 1). These results are consistent with previous studies showing that these biomarkers reflect AD-related neurodegeneration [11,12,30]. Similarly, total cognition and memory were significantly associated with all four BBMs in the SAMD lab cohort and with p-tau217 in the ADNI cohort (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). These findings, consistent with previous studies [11,14,31], support the possible role of these biomarkers in representing cognitive decline due to neurodegenerative diseases such as AD. Interestingly, correlations observed among SAMD lab participants were stronger than those in ADNI participants, as indicated by higher absolute Spearman’s rho values. Given that p-tau217, p-tau181, NfL, and GFAP can reflect the biological stage of AD and co-pathologies [1,[11], [12], [13], [14]], the broader range of CDR scores within SAMD lab participants could have resulted in these higher correlation coefficients.

However, several BBMs reflect partially overlapping pathological processes, raising the concerns about inter-biomarker collinearity when comparing their relative importance. Indeed, Spearman partial correlation analyses among the four BBMs (Supplementary Table 4) revealed significant correlations between all BBM pairs except for p-tau181 and GFAP among amyloid (−) ADNI participants. To address this issue, we employed dominance analysis, which is specifically designed to disentangle the relative importance of correlated predictors by averaging each predictor's incremental R² contribution across all subset regression models [28,29].

Notably, dominance analyses revealed that plasma GFAP was the most sensitive biomarker for AD signature ROI thickness in amyloid (+) SAMD lab participants (Fig. 4B). A previous study from the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging also observed a significant association between plasma GFAP and the AD signature ROI [12]. Our finding further suggests that this association may be confined to individuals with AD pathology. The dominance of GFAP over NfL suggests that AD-specific cortical atrophy may be more effectively explained by astrocyte activation in response to amyloid pathology rather than by axonal damage. Our findings are consistent with recent observations that glial activity mediated the early AD pathological process [[32], [33], [34]]. Given that immune dysfunction via neuroinflammation is now recognized as a contributor to AD pathophysiology [35], further studies investigating the causal relationship between neuroinflammation, amyloid pathology, and cortical atrophy are required.

The dominance of p-tau217 for AD signature ROI thickness in amyloid (+) ADNI participants (Fig. 4E) indicates that p-tau217 may serve as a staging biomarker representing neurodegeneration specific to AD, complementing its well-known diagnostic properties [7]. This is consistent with previous observations that p-tau217 was sensitive to tau PET abnormalities used for biological staging of AD [36]. Moreover, plasma p-tau217 was superior in explaining total cognition and memory in both cohorts, supporting this hypothesis. Prior findings from the TRIAD and BioFINDER-2 cohorts demonstrated the dominance of p-tau217 over other p-tau species [14]. Our study extended these findings by comparing p-tau217 with NfL and GFAP. Further replication in diverse populations is needed to validate the potential of p-tau217 as a standalone biomarker for the diagnosis, staging, and monitoring of cognitive impairment.

Among amyloid (+) participants, GFAP was significantly associated with AD signature ROI thickness in the SAMD lab (Fig. 4B), but not in the ADNI (Fig. 4E). The different characteristics of the two cohorts may account for the discrepancy in the results. SAMD lab participants were recruited from a psychiatric memory clinic and exhibited a wider clinical severity spectrum, which may have increased GFAP sensitivity through comorbidities such as depression that could affect GFAP [37] and cortical atrophy [38]. Despite comparable amyloid PET positivity, the more advanced clinical severity in the SAMD lab suggests greater co-pathology beyond AD. Notably, the dominance of GFAP in amyloid (+) SAMD lab participants was consistently preserved across all three sensitivity analyses that addressed the clinical severity discrepancy between the two cohorts (Supplementary Figures 7B, 8B, and 11B). Nevertheless, given the residual differences in CDR-SB (in sensitivity analysis 2) and education, possible lower cognitive reserve and greater co-pathology in the SAMD lab participants may still partially have contributed to this finding. Differences in ethnicity between the two cohorts may also have contributed. Although BBM levels are reportedly comparable across ethnicities [39,40], the relationship between GFAP and AD-related neurodegeneration has not been directly compared between Asian and White populations. Methodological factors related to plasma assay are unlikely to fully explain this discrepancy, as both cohorts used identical Simoa assay platforms and reagents (Methods Section 2.5). Further investigation into the relationships among neuropsychiatric symptoms, BBMs, brain atrophy, and the contributions of ethnic/biological factors is warranted in future studies with matched designs across diverse populations.

In amyloid (−) participants, the reduced dominance of p-tau217 relative to amyloid (+) group is expected given its established role in reflecting AD-associated early amyloid and tau pathology [1,21]. More informatively, we directly compared plasma NfL and GFAP, both of which reflect neuropathology non-specific to AD. As a result, plasma NfL demonstrated dominance over GFAP in explaining AD signature ROI thickness (Figs. 4C and 4F) and total cognition (Figs. 5C and 5F) across both cohorts, and memory (Fig. 6C) in the SAMD lab. A previous study observed that NfL was associated with prospective cortical atrophy in amyloid (−) individuals [41], consistent with our results. Our findings further suggest that, in the absence of amyloid pathology, axonal damage (NfL) may be more informative than astrocyte reactivity (GFAP) for capturing cortical atrophy and cognitive decline. These observations may have practical relevance for differential interpretation: in amyloid (–) individuals with cognitive impairment, elevated plasma NfL may better reflect the underlying neurodegenerative process than GFAP, whereas in amyloid (+) individuals, GFAP may capture additional information about AD-related neurodegeneration. Meanwhile, among amyloid (−) ADNI participants, p-tau181 was the most sensitive biomarker for memory (Fig. 6F). Whereas the composite memory score used in the SAMD lab was expressed as a z-score, which has no minimum or maximum values, the Logical Memory Delayed Recall test score used in ADNI was expressed as a raw score. Notably, 12 out of the total 284 ADNI participants had a Logical Memory Delayed Recall test score of 0. This floor effect may have biased the results. Another possible explanation is that participants in the ADNI might have fewer co-pathologies than those in the SAMD lab, which may have resulted in the superiority of p-tau181 over NfL or GFAP.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare the relative explanatory power of these four BBMs regarding AD-related atrophy and cognition. Beyond comparing rho values in Spearman models, we applied dominance analysis to ensure statistical robustness. We conducted stratified analysis by amyloid status, revealing that the selection of a proper biomarker can differ under AD pathology. The use of two independent cohorts of different ethnicities strengthens the generalizability of our findings. In the three sensitivity analyses (CDR-SB adjustment, restriction to global CDR 0.5 participants, and PSM), the amyloid status-dependent dominance patterns reported in our main analyses were largely preserved. These findings indicate the robustness against disease stage differences between the two cohorts.

Our study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about the longitudinal influence of BBMs on atrophy or cognitive decline. Second, comorbidities known to affect biomarker levels, such as cardiac disease, obesity, or kidney disease[42], were not accounted for. Third, cognitive outcomes were measured using different tools across cohorts, potentially limiting direct cross-cohort comparison of effect sizes. These differences might have contributed to the cross-cohort discrepancies of the results for total cognition and memory. However, because Spearman correlation is rank-based and dominance analysis is R²-based (both of which are scale-invariant), the within-cohort dominance hierarchy of biomarkers is robust to this difference. Despite this methodological reassurance, the floor effect observed in the Logical Memory Delayed Recall test (12 of 284 ADNI participants scored 0) may have biased the dominance pattern for memory in the amyloid (−) ADNI subgroup. The use of norm-based standardized scores, rather than raw scores, might have mitigated this floor effect. Further studies using harmonized cognitive measures across cohorts are warranted. Fourth, the two cohorts differed substantially in disease stage distribution, with the ADNI cohort comprising a larger proportion of cognitively unimpaired participants. This may have contributed to the disparity of the results of Spearman models. While the three sensitivity analyses (detailed in Methods Section 2.6) supported the robustness of main dominance findings, residual confounding from differences in disease stage cannot be entirely excluded. Although sensitivity analysis 2 (global CDR 0.5 only) and 3 (PSM) addressed the inter-cohort disparities, they substantially reduced sample sizes, which may have attenuated statistical power in subgroup analyses. Fifth, the proposed amyloid status-stratified interpretation of BBMs requires prior confirmation of amyloid status by PET or CSF testing. Despite the high accuracy of p-tau217 in predicting amyloid status, BBMs should meet highly stringent criteria to serve as a confirmatory test [7,43]. While this amyloid status-stratified approach has clear utility for reassessment after amyloid status is confirmed, it is not directly applicable as a stand-alone first-line screening strategy. Further studies are needed to develop algorithms that combine multiple BBMs to simultaneously predict amyloid status and AD-related neurodegeneration. Sixth, our analysis did not include plasma endogenous microtubule-binding region tau243 (eMTBR-tau243), which has been shown to reflect AD-related tau pathology and cognitive decline [44,45]. The inclusion of eMTBR-tau243 in future studies may refine the relative importance rankings of BBMs reported here. Lastly, plasma GFAP and NfL may be influenced by non-AD psychiatric or neurological conditions [10,46], and participants with undiagnosed subtle comorbidities may have affected the results.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that plasma GFAP and p-tau217 are the most robust indicators of cortical atrophy and cognitive impairment in amyloid (+) individuals, while plasma NfL serves as the dominant marker in amyloid (−) individuals. Although BBMs are typically measured before amyloid status is known in clinical practice [5,7,43], our findings provide a framework for reinterpreting the initial BBM profile after amyloid status is established by a confirmatory test. This stratified reinterpretation can refine the optimal use of BBMs in monitoring disease progression and evaluating therapeutic efficacy, providing important evidence for the future implementation of precision medicine.
