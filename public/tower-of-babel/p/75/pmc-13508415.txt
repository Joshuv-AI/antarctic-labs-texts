Otological conditions and autism spectrum disorder in Medicaid beneficiaries aged 1 to 20 years: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lauren L Zhang, Carolyn DiGuiseppi, Ashley Blanchard, Caleb Ing, Matthew T Russell, Anil K Lalwani, Guohua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOtological conditions are known to be a contributing factor for delayed diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD). The co-occurrence of otological conditions and ASD, however, has not been adequately studied. This study aims to estimate the prevalence of ASD in children according to otological conditions and to assess the associations of otological conditions with ASD.MethodsA cross-sectional study of Medicaid beneficiaries aged 1 to 20 years using 2020 data on diagnoses and beneficiary demographics from the Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System of the Medicaid and Children's Health Insurance Program was performed. Otological conditions examined included otitis media, sensorineural and conductive hearing loss, tinnitus, and hyperacusis. Prevalence and odds ratios of ASD adjusted for age group, sex, and race and ethnicity (aORs) were estimated according to otological conditions.ResultsOf 40,990,295 participants, 10.4% were diagnosed with at least one otological condition. The prevalence of ASD was 2.79% in those with any otological condition and 1.73% in those without (aOR, 1.78 [95% confidence interval, 1.77–1.79]).The aOR of ASD increased 51% in children with otitis media (1.51 [1.50–1.52]), 54% in children with tinnitus (1.54 [1.43–1.65]), 227% in children with sensorineural hearing loss (3.27 [3.19–3.35]), and 274% in children with hyperacusis (3.74 [3.26–4.28]).ConclusionsASD prevalence is significantly higher in those with otological conditions. The association with ASD is particularly strong in children with sensorineural hearing loss and hyperacusis. The presence of sensorineural hearing loss and hyperacusis warrants heightened clinical awareness and consideration of neurodevelopmental evaluation and screening for ASD.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Hyperacusis, Otitis media, Otologic disease, Sensorineural hearing loss, Tinnitus

Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex heterogeneous condition with varying etiology and a range of characteristics that include impaired social communication and interaction, restrictive or repetitive interests and behaviors, and sensory abnormalities [1–3]. While pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, twin and family studies indicate a strong genetic component, with heritability estimated to be 64–91% [4]. ASD prevalence in the United States has steadily risen over recent decades, reaching an estimated 6.3 per 1000 children and adults in 2022 and as high as 30.3 per 1,000 children [5].

Otological conditions, including otitis media (OM), hearing loss (HL), tinnitus (the perception of sound in the absence of corresponding external sources), and hyperacusis (a chronic condition defined as decreased tolerance to sounds), are frequently reported among individuals with ASD [6–10]. These comorbidities may be associated with differences in presentation, diagnosis, and prognosis [8, 11, 12]: communicative difficulties caused by hearing impairment may overlap with core ASD symptoms, potentially complicating recognition [13–16]. On average, children with hearing impairment were found to be formally diagnosed with ASD three years later than those with typical hearing [17]. Delayed recognition of ASD has been associated with poorer language development and social and mental health outcomes [18].

OM is among the most common childhood infections globally [19, 20]. Although prior work has not found a consistent association between OM with ASD [21–23], more recent studies suggest increased risk of ear infections in children with ASD [6, 24, 25]. OM-related conductive HL may overlap with ASD-related communicative differences [6]. The American Academy of Pediatrics considers children with ASD to be especially vulnerable to OM-related developmental effects [6]. Non-infectious otological conditions including HL, tinnitus, and hyperacusis have also been linked to ASD, but epidemiologic evidence remains limited and inconsistent [7, 9, 16, 26–31].

Clarifying the association between ASD and otological conditions has important clinical implications. Otological conditions and ASD may co-occur, and shared features could complicate the clinical evaluation of either condition. Since children presenting with otological disorders may have undiagnosed ASD, or those with ASD may have unrecognized otological conditions, it is important to investigate both perspectives. Improved understanding of the epidemiology may inform clinical diagnosis, screening, and multidisciplinary management. Thus, the objectives of this study were twofold: to estimate the prevalence of ASD according to otological conditions and assess the associations between ASD and otological conditions in Medicaid beneficiaries aged 1 to 20 years.

Materials and methods

Data source

This cross-sectional study was based on data for children aged 1 to 20 years enrolled in Medicaid (inclusive of the Children’s Health Insurance Program) in 2020. Jointly sponsored by the US federal and state governments, Medicaid is a health insurance program providing coverage for over 80 million low-income individuals and people with disabilities, including almost half of all children [32, 33]. In most states, individuals with ASD are eligible for Medicaid through a specific waiver or a 1915(c) waiver for individuals with intellectual or developmental disabilities (i.e., home- and community-based services authorized under Sect. 1915(c) of the Social Security Act) [34].

Data for this study came from the Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System (T-MSIS), which aggregates Medicaid data from states, territories, and the District of Columbia, owned and maintained by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. T-MSIS Analytic Files (TAF) are T-MSIS data optimized for research, with individual-level data on demographics and claims for healthcare services, including inpatient care, outpatient care, long-term care, laboratory tests, and prescription medications.

Study design and participants

This research was approved by the Columbia University Irving Medical Center Institutional Review Board (No. IRB00008612), and a waiver of informed consent was obtained. The procedures used in this study adhere to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study sample consisted of children aged 1 to 20 years who were enrolled in Medicaid in 2020 and who had at least one inpatient claim or two non-drug outpatient claims of any service type between 2019 and 2020. Beneficiaries without sufficient claims data (n = 878,252) were excluded because it was not possible to determine whether they had ASD. Infants (n = 2,959,138) were excluded because the diagnosis of ASD is more reliable beginning at 14 months of age [35]. Diagnoses of ASD and otological conditions were identified from the 2019–2020 TAF using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes F84.0, F84.3, F84.5, F84.8, and F84.9 for ASD and H60-H95 for otological conditions. Otological conditions were grouped into OM (ICD-10-CM codes H65-H67), conductive hearing loss (CHL; ICD-10-CM codes H90.0-H90.2), sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL; ICD-10-CM codes H90.3-H90.5), tinnitus (ICD-10-CM code H93.1), and hyperacusis (ICD-10-CM code H93.23) [36].

Statistical analysis

Demographic statistics were described with percentages and frequencies for categorical variables. Age was categorized into 1–4, 5–9, 10–14, and 15–20 years. The prevalence of ASD and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated according to otological conditions. Either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to find the statistical significance of differences in prevalence between groups, as appropriate. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were estimated using multivariable logistic regression to assess the associations of ASD with otological conditions when adjusted for age, sex, and race and ethnicity. Statistical analyses were conducted with SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC, USA) and R, version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Participant characteristics

The study sample included 40,990,295 children aged 1 to 20 years in the 2020 Medicaid beneficiary database. Of them, 50.7% were male, 31.4% non-Hispanic White, 25.2% Hispanic, and 18.2% non-Hispanic Black (Table 1). The mean (SD) age of the study participants was 10.0 (5.6) years.

Of the study participants, 1.83% had a diagnosis of ASD (Table 1). The prevalence of ASD varied significantly by age, sex, and race and ethnicity, with significantly higher rates found in those who were 5 to 9 years of age, who were male, or who were non-Hispanic White (Table 1).

The prevalence of ASD among children diagnosed with any otological condition was 2.79%, compared with 1.73% among those without otological conditions (P < 0.001; Table 1). Children with hyperacusis had the highest prevalence of ASD (6.54%), followed by those with SNHL (5.94%), CHL (4.40%), tinnitus (2.88%), and OM (2.41%) (Table 1).

Overall, 10.4% of the participants had at least one diagnosis of otological conditions (i.e., diseases of the ear and mastoid process) (Table 1). The most common otological condition was OM (7.0%), followed by SNHL (0.29%), CHL (0.16%), tinnitus (0.063%), and hyperacusis (0.0085%). For most diagnoses investigated, the prevalence decreased with increasing age except tinnitus, which rose from 0.004% in those aged 1 to 4 years to 0.13% in those aged 15 to 20 years. All otological conditions were more prevalent in female than male participants with ASD, whereas in those without ASD, males demonstrated comparable or higher prevalence (Table 2). Within each racial or ethnic group investigated, all otological diagnoses had significantly higher prevalence among those with ASD (Table 2).

Discussion

This study characterizes the co-occurrence of ASD and otological conditions in children aged 1–20 years using the 2020 Medicaid claims database. We found that the prevalence of ASD was significantly higher among individuals with each otological condition examined than those without. The highest prevalence of ASD was found in children with hyperacusis (6.54%) and SNHL (5.94%), representing approximately 4- and 3.5-fold the prevalence in children without these conditions (1.73%). The prevalence of ASD among children with OM (2.41%) and tinnitus (2.88%) represented more modest absolute differences.

Our findings also show that otological conditions were significantly more common among individuals with ASD than in those without ASD. The odds of ASD associated with hyperacusis and SNHL each increased over 3-fold, consistent with prior reports that auditory hypersensitivity and peripheral auditory abnormalities tend to occur more frequently in autistic children than in neurotypical children [7, 37–41]. The aOR of 3.27 for SNHL is generally consistent with population-based estimates. Using caregiver-reported data from the National Survey of Children’s Health, Talge et al. [42] found an OR of 2.7 (95% CI, 1.8–3.9) for the association between “deafness or problems with hearing” and ASD in US children aged 3–17. An analysis of Scotland’s 2011 Census reported an OR of 5.4 (95% CI, 5.1–5.6) for self/proxy-reported deafness or partial hearing loss [43].

Hyperacusis has been hypothesized to arise from increased central neural gain or impaired inhibitory control in auditory pathways, leading to exaggerated perception of loudness even at moderate intensities [44–47]. However, the aOR of 3.74 for hyperacusis requires careful contextualization. The claims-based prevalence of hyperacusis (0.0085%) is substantially lower than the prevalence of hyperacusis (3.2–17.1%) reported in systematic reviews of pediatric populations [30, 48] and the prevalence of decreased sound tolerance (40–60%) estimated by meta-analysis of clinical ASD cohorts [40, 49]. The children who do receive a hyperacusis diagnosis in claims may represent a highly selected subgroup, e.g., those presenting with the most severe symptoms or those seen by specialists familiar with the diagnosis, which could contribute to the elevated aOR.

Prior work on OM and ASD has produced inconsistent results, with reports of no association and other studies finding elevated rates of OM in ASD [24, 50, 51]. Our results demonstrate that OM was associated with 51% increased odds of ASD, which is consistent with the aOR of 1.83 (95% CI, 1.71–1.95) found in a Danish population-based cohort [25]. While the elevated odds ratio is statistically significant, this modest effect size should be interpreted in the context of OM being common in childhood: around 80% of children experience at least one episode before school age [52]. Proposed explanations of the connection, however, may be related to innate immunity, the inflammatory response, and orofacial anatomy [25, 53, 54]. Providers may also be more likely to treat and diagnose OM with less significant exam findings in patients with limitations in verbal communication. CHL can be a complication of prolonged OM, potentially impeding language development and masking social communication differences in ASD. It is plausible that recurrent or chronic CHL in early childhood may impair speech perception and interfere with early language development [55–60], and could contribute to delayed diagnosis of CHL [56]. The claims-based prevalence of CHL (0.16%) was substantially lower than reported in a low-income early-childhood hearing screening study (2.9%) [61], further suggesting that claims data underestimate the prevalence of milder or transient CHL.

Tinnitus showed a modest association with ASD, and its prevalence in children with ASD increased steadily with age. This pattern is consistent with its known relationship to cumulative noise exposure [62–64]. Our results are congruent with the finding that tinnitus is not so commonly reported to co-occur with ASD [7]. However, the overall claims-based prevalence (0.063%) is far below prior estimates of 8.5–21% in children aged 5–17 years [65] and pooled severe tinnitus prevalence of 2.7% [66]. There are no validated assessments for tinnitus in younger children, which may limit the diagnosis and raises the possibility of misclassification in claims-based studies [65]. Our results highlight the need for further investigation into tinnitus and diagnostic measures in those with ASD.

The mechanisms underlying the observed associations are likely multifactorial. It has been hypothesized that tinnitus and hyperacusis may both be driven by the central auditory system’s compensation for decreased auditory input, and that tinnitus results from increased central noise while hyperacusis arises from increased central gain [67]. Anatomical differences, such as hypoplasia of the cerebellum and brainstem, superior canal dehiscence, and altered connectivity between structures preceding the medial olivary complex, have also been proposed to play a role in tinnitus and hyperacusis [68, 69]. Immune dysregulation, increasingly recognized in ASD, may also predispose to recurrent OM or complicate recovery from infection [70, 71]. The heterogeneity of ASD suggests that no single mechanism is sufficient to explain all observed associations.

This study has several limitations. First, detection bias could be present in both directions: children with ASD typically have more frequent healthcare encounters and specialist evaluations [72–74], which may increase the likelihood of identifying and coding otological conditions that might go undiagnosed in neurotypical children. Conversely, children with chronic otological complaints such as recurrent otitis media or hearing loss may undergo more frequent developmental evaluations, increasing the probability of ASD detection [30]. This bidirectional detection bias, a form of surveillance bias in which intensity of clinical contact systematically differs between groups, could result in overestimating the strength of observed associations and should serve as an important interpretative consideration [75, 76].

Second, our multivariable models did not adjust for major confounding factors such as prematurity, congenital abnormalities, and healthcare utilization intensity, thus making the results susceptible to bias from residual confounding. The roles of certain variables within the causal structure warrant careful consideration, due to difficulty in distinguishing confounders from mediators or components of the exposure in observational studies. For example, intellectual disability co-occurs in more than 30% of children with ASD and is classified as a specifier of ASD diagnosis in the DSM-5-TR, rather than as a separate comorbidity [77, 78]. Because intellectual disability shares genetic underpinnings with ASD and may reflect overlapping neurodevelopmental processes rather than an independent risk factor, its role in the causal framework is unclear; it could function as a confounder, mediator, or component of the ASD phenotype itself [79, 80]. Adjusting for variables with such ambiguous roles risks overadjustment bias [81]. Similarly, congenital syndromes associated with both ASD and otological conditions (e.g., Fragile X syndrome) may represent shared etiologic pathways rather than independent confounders [82, 83]. Given that the primary objective of this study was to characterize patterns of co-occurrence of these otological conditions with ASD rather than to estimate causal effects, adjustment was limited to key demographic variables; the potential for residual confounding should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Third, otological diagnoses were not mutually exclusive; some participants may have carried multiple otological diagnoses. The condition-specific odds ratios should therefore be interpreted as reflecting the association between each diagnosis and ASD among children carrying that diagnosis, rather than as independent effects, and associations for individual conditions may not be fully separable from one another [84]. Future studies may employ methods such as latent class analysis or clustering approaches to better characterize co-occurrence of otological conditions [85].

Fourth, administrative claims data are subject to limitations in diagnostic accuracy, due to misclassification and underestimation [86]. Moreover, diagnostic codes cannot convey clinical nuance, such as the severity, duration, or functional impact of a condition. As detailed previously, the claims-based frequencies for all conditions examined were lower than population-based on screening-based estimates, indicating that these data capture only a fraction of true prevalence. More broadly, conditions were identified based on claims from 2019 to 2020; participants who did not seek Medicaid services for a given condition during that period would not have been captured, also potentially contributing to underestimation of prevalence.

In addition, the participants were Medicaid beneficiaries, who represent a socioeconomically disadvantaged population group and thus may face barriers to quality care and timely diagnosis that would limit the generalizability of our findings to all children and adolescents nationally [87]. Finally, because of the cross-sectional study design, the temporality of the association between ASD and otological conditions could not be established, and no causal or directional inferences can be drawn from these data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study examined the association of ASD with otological conditions, utilizing a large sample size that enabled the investigation of otologic conditions that are relatively uncommon in childhood. We found significant associations between all the otological diagnoses investigated and ASD. The prevalence of ASD was significantly elevated in children with each otological condition compared to those without, with particularly high prevalence in children with hyperacusis. Although this association does not imply causality, multiple explanations for the co-occurrence of these conditions with ASD have been proposed, ranging from anatomical abnormalities to altered neural signaling and immune deficiencies [6, 7, 44, 53, 54].

Our findings underscore a potentially important diagnostic consideration for otolaryngologists and general pediatricians. Otological conditions may co-occur with ASD, and shared clinical features could complicate clinical evaluation; future longitudinal investigation is needed to determine if this contributes to diagnostic delays. These findings suggest that clinicians should maintain awareness of co-occurrence of ASD and otological conditions when assessing young patients with auditory complaints or severe otitis media [14–17]. In children with hyperacusis, the observed overlap with ASD may warrant heightened clinical awareness for neurodevelopmental differences and consideration of developmental evaluation. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, the rarity of hyperacusis diagnoses in claims data, and potential for detection bias, formal screening recommendation would require validation in prospective studies. Likewise, the elevated prevalence of otological conditions among children with ASD supports consideration of audiologic and otologic evaluation in this population, congruent with existing guidelines that recommend hearing assessment in children with developmental concerns.
