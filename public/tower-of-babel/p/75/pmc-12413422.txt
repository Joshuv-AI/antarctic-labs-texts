Identification of pathogenic variants in six Chinese families with keratoconus of autosomal dominant inheritance: pathogenicity analysis and variable phenotype
Authors: Xiaoyu Huang, Meijiao Ma, Xiaofei Chen, Yuanyuan Lian, Huiping Li, Xunlun Sheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeKeratoconus (KC) is a bilateral, asymmetric disease causing corneal thinning, irregular astigmatism, and vision decline, with unclear etiology. This study aims to investigate pathogenic variants of candidate genes in Chinese KC families via whole exome sequencing (WES).MethodsThe Pentacam 3D anterior segment analysis system was applied for keratectasia detection, and the Corvis ST was used for corneal biomechanics measurement. Probands from KC families were screened via WES and further verified in other family members through Sanger sequencing. Additionally, qPCR was used to validate copy number variants and identify pathogenic gene loci. The identified variants were then classified according to the Standards and Guidelines for the Interpretation of Sequence Variants published by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). Finally, STRING protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks analysis was performed to investigate interactions among candidate gene-related proteins.ResultsUsing WES, four heterozygous missense variants were detected in the ZNF469, KRT12, COL8A2, and COL18A1 genes: c.4384G > A: p.Asp1462Asn, c.1229T > G:p.Val410Gly, c.505A > G:p.Ile169Val, and c.1159G > A:p.Gly387Arg. Additionally, a heterozygous frameshift variant was detected in the PMS2 gene: c.1551_1572del:p.Ser517Argfs*71. The affected parents carried the same variants as the probands verified by Sanger sequencing. A copy number variant was detected in the DPP6 gene: seq[GRCh38] dup(7)(q36.2q36.2) chr7:g.153782360_ 153982491dup. According to ACMG guidelines, ZNF469, KRT12, COL8A2, and COL18A1 gene variants are Likely Pathogenic; PMS2 and DPP6 gene variants are Pathogenic. STRING analysis highlights a tightly interconnected network centered on COL8A2, involving COL18A1, FN1, ZNF469, and KRT12. DPP6 was involved in KC via affecting FN1. In four of six autosomal dominant KC (adKC) families, affected parents had the same variants as probands but milder phenotypes.ConclusionIn this study, six novel variants in ZNF469, KRT12, COL8A2, COL18A1, PMS2, and DPP6 were linked to adKC. Family phenotypes showed variable expressivity with irregular dominance inheritance. Abnormal KC-related gene protein expression may contribute to corneal structural instability. This study broadened KC genetic screening candidates and suggested genetic testing could aid early KC diagnosis and intervention.

Introduction

Keratoconus (KC) is a bilateral and asymmetric disease characterized by progressive thinning and steeping of the cornea leading to irregular astigmatism and decreased visual acuity. Some patients may develop acute corneal edema, leaving scars after the edema subsides [1]. KC occurs most often in adolescence, and it is one of the important blinding ophthalmopathies in China. The disease typically manifests during the teenage years and tends to level off by the time individuals reach their forties [2, 3]. Among Caucasians, the incidence of KC is estimated to be between 2 and 4 cases per 100,000 people annually, while in Asia and the Middle East, this figure jumps to about 20 per 100,000 per year [4]. Our insight into the processes driving the emergence of KC remains fairly rudimentary. According to disease progression, KC can be divided into incubation stage, incipient stage, completion stage, and cicatricial stage [1]. Presently, treatments for KC are quite restricted, encompassing only procedures like corneal collagen cross-linking during its nascent stages to impede further progression and corneal transplants when it has reached an advanced phase. The stealthy inception of KC, coupled with the absence of classic clinical signs at the outset, renders detection exceedingly challenging and confounds eye care professionals greatly.

The exact mechanisms behind KC remain elusive, but it appears that abnormalities in collagen synthesis or degradation within the cornea may play a role, influenced by a variety of factors including genetics, environmental influences, viral infections, auto-metabolism, and immune responses. As advancements in genetic testing and research continue, a growing body of evidence indicates that genetic factors significantly contribute to the development of KC. Tuft SJ et al [5] reported on thirteen sets of identical twins and four sets of fraternal twins who both had KC, which confirmed the role of genetic factors in KC and indicated that monozygotic twins have higher genetic consistency than dizygotic twins. Moreover, international research indicates that roughly 13.5% of individuals diagnosed with KC possess a familial background concerning the disorder [6]. The hereditary patterns can persist through two to three generations [7], showcasing prevalence rates among relatives of KC patients that range from 3.34% [8] to as high as 25.3% [9]. The mode of inheritance through which KC is believed to be passed down within families involve both autosomal dominant (AD) transmission as well as autosomal recessive (AR) patterns [1], with mitochondrial inheritance also playing a role [10].

Research into the genetics of KC has revealed a complex picture marked by considerable genetic diversity, with various genes in the nuclear genome playing critical roles in the disease's progression. So far, several pathogenic genes associated with KC have been pinpointed, particularly those involved in collagen synthesis such as ZNF469, COL4A3, and COL4A4. Additional significant genes include VSX1, which is related to eye development; SOD1, associated with oxidative stress; and IL1B, which is involved in inflammation. In a 2022 study by Cheng WY [11] and colleagues, the team employed targeted sequence capture alongside high-throughput sequencing techniques to uncover six novel pathogenic variants in genes such as TGFBI, HMX1, SLC4A11, ZEB1, and PIKFYVE among five probands from KC families, four of which exhibited AD inheritance patterns. An examination of keratectasia alongside corneal biomechanical indicators in probands and their first-degree relatives from families with autosomal dominant KC (adKC) revealed that first-generation relatives exhibited various abnormalities in corneal morphology and biomechanical assessments, indicative of early-stage KC. The results indicated a correlation between certain gene variants—specifically TGFBI, HMX1, SLC4A11, ZEB1, and PIKFYVE—and the onset of KC. The clinical phenotype of adKC shows irregular dominant inheritance. Different individuals within the same family carry the same heterozygous gene mutation, but there are differences in the severity of corneal morphological changes. KC displays considerable genetic diversity, with mutations across multiple genes contributing to its development. Currently identified genes account for only a fraction of the pathogenesis associated with KC, leaving many patients without identifiable genetic explanations for their condition, underscoring the likelihood of numerous undiscovered genes at play.

For this research, we collected clinical information from six KC families with Pentacam 3D anterior segment analysis system, Corvis ST visual corneal biomechanical analyzer and related examinations. The variants in candidate gene were screened by whole exome sequencing (WES) and the relationship between pathogenic variants and corneal morphological and biomechanical characteristics of KC probands and their parental generations were analyzed, which will provide reference data for early diagnosis and intervention of KC.

Materials and methods

Diagnostic criteria for keratoconus

The research initiative was granted the green light by the Ethics Board at the Gansu Aier Ophthalmology and Optometry Hospital [Approval ID: GSAIER2023IRB04], adhering strictly to the Helsinki Declaration. Each participant, or their legal custodians, provided explicit written consent prior to joining the study.

This research employed the diagnostic and classification standards outlined in the Chinese Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Keratoconus (2019) [1]. The diagnostic criteria encompassed the following: (1) progressive nearsightedness, accompanied by fluctuating nonstandard curvature of the cornea and inadequate vision improvement with traditional eyeglasses; (2) corneal topographical scans that displayed characteristic alterations, including unusual heightening of both the front and back aspects of the KC-affected cornea, along with a noticeable thinning in the central or near-central regions; and (3) a corneal biomechanics assessment indicating diminished values in key indices, including corneal hysteresis and the corneal resistance factor. The staging criteria for KC are as follows: the incubation phase is characterized by an eye exhibiting topographical indicators of KC or questionable features upon routine slit-lamp evaluation, with the fellow eye already affected by KC, and an uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) that is 1.0 or better. The incipient phase is marked by confirmed KC with a best spectacle corrected visual acuity (BSCVA) that is 0.8 or higher. The completion phase is identified by confirmed KC, a BSCVA below 0.8, and the presence of classic KC symptoms, categorized into three grades: Grade 1, where the corneal curvature is less than 53.0D, the thinnest corneal point is more than 400μm, and BSCVA is below 0.8; Grade 2, with a corneal curvature below 55.0D, a thinnest point thickness above 300μm, and a BSCVA below 0.3; and Grade 3, featuring a corneal curvature over 55.0D, a corneal thinnest point thickness of 300μm or less, and a BSCVA below 0.05. The cicatricial stage pertains specifically to the residual corneal scarring left behind once the acute edema associated with KC has resolved.

Clinical data collection

In this study, the collected probands and their first-degree relatives (parents) of KC family were inquired in detail about their medical history, family history, and relevant ophthalmologic examinations were made accordingly. The Fleischer's ring and Vogt’s striae were observed using a slit-lamp biomicroscope (Topcon, SL-2G, Japan). The Snellen eye chart and phoropter (NIDEK, RT-5100, Japan) were used to record UCVA and BSCVA. Utilizing the Pentacam 3D anterior segment analyzer (OCULUS, 70100, Germany), keratectasia was identified by examining several parameters: the corneal thickness, the corneal flat keratometric reading at a 3 mm radius from the center (K1), the steeper keratometric reading (K2), the peak keratometry value (Kmax), the minimum corneal thickness at the thinnest point (TP), and the deviations from normal in anterior/posterior elevation (Df/Db), pachymetric progression (Dp), corneal thinnest point (Dt), and relational thickness (Da). Additionally, the Corvis ST Visual Corneal Biomechanics Analyzer (OCULUS, 72100, Germany) was employed to assess corneal biomechanics, capturing data on the corvis biomechanical index (CBI) and the tomographic biomechanical index (TBI).

In the research conducted by Ambrósio Jr et al [12], the standard thresholds for Df/Db/Dt/Dp/Da (white) were identified as being below 1.6 standard deviations (SD) for normal values, between 1.6 and 2.6 SD for suspicious values (yellow), and exceeding 2.6 SD for pathological values (red). For the Critical Bone Index (CBI), suspicious values fell in the range of 0.25 to 0.5, while pathological values were marked at above 0.5, which served as benchmarks for critical assessment. Similarly, for Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI), suspicious values were classified between 0.25 and 0.75, with pathological values surpassing 0.75, providing a reference for critical evaluation.

Whole exome sequencing, Sanger sequencing and quantitative PCR validation

Blood samples of approximately 2 to 3 milliliters were collected from the KC family probands and their first-degree relatives using EDTAK2 vacuum anticoagulation tubes. Subsequently, genomic DNA was isolated with the TIANamp Blood DNA Kit, catalog number DP348-03. These samples underwent WES with an average coverage depth of 150X. During the analysis of the sequencing data, any reads that failed to meet the quality standards were discarded. The Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) tool was then employed to align the sequences to the hg19 human genome reference sequence from the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/). Identification of the variants was carried out using GATK version 3.70, and the potentially pathogenic variants were confirmed through Sanger sequencing and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

In silico analysis

Variants were checked in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) to confirm their reported pathogenic status. Novel or de novo variants were analyzed using the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), with a Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) threshold of <0.005 to exclude benign variants in both the general and East Asian populations. Evolutionary conservation of gene sequences was assessed using GERP++, where higher scores indicate greater deleterious effects. The functional and structural impacts of variants were predicted through multiple platforms: SIFT (D: Deleterious [≤0.05]; T: Tolerated [>0.05]); Mutation Taster (A: Disease_causing_automatic; D: Disease_causing; N: Polymorphism; P: Polymorphism_automatic; higher scores indicate greater pathogenicity); FATHMM (D: Deleterious; T: Tolerated; lower values indicate greater pathogenicity); M-CAP (D: Damaging [score ≥0.025]; T: Tolerated [score <0.025]). Variants were classified according to the 2015 ACMG guidelines into five categories: Pathogenic, Likely Pathogenic, Variant of Uncertain Significance, Likely Benign, and Benign [13]. Finally, protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks among ZNF469, KRT12, COL8A2, COL18A1, DPP6, and PMS2 were constructed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/).

Pathogenicity analysis of genetic variants

The familial co-segregation analysis showed that the parent of each proband in family 1 to 6, who exhibited abnormal corneal morphology, possessed a heterozygous variant at the identical locus, while the phenotypically normal parent displayed the wild type at that location. These findings indicate a co-segregation between genotype and clinical phenotype (PP1_Supporting) and an AD pattern of inheritance. Additionally, the phenotype of the variant carriers closely aligned with KC (PP4_supporting).

Family 1 The proband exhibited a novel heterozygous variant c.4384G>A (p.Asp1462Asn) located in the ZNF469 gene (Table 2 and Fig. 7a, b). Specifically, the nucleotide at the 4384th position of the coding sequence was altered from guanine (G) to adenine (A), leading to a missense variant where aspartic acid (Asp) is replaced by asparagine (Asn) at the 1462nd amino acid in the protein sequence. The missense variant lead to Brittle cornea syndrome 1 (https://omim.org/entry/612078?search=ZNF469&highlight=znf469) (PP2_Supporting, off-label application). The variant frequency in the general East Asian population is 0.00048 (<0.005) in the gnomAD database (PM2_Medium). The variant was predicted to be deleterious in SIFT, M-CAP with a GERP++ score of 3.38. As predicted by the above in silico predication software, such variant may adversely impact the gene or its product (PP3_Supporting) (Table 3). Discussion

Genetic studies on the corneal traits in KC patients have progressed considerably with the improvement of the corneal morphology methods. Some research suggests that approximately one out of every eight individuals diagnosed with KC has a hereditary component, while roughly between 11 and 14 close blood relations who appear symptom-free might exhibit signs suggestive of early keratoconic changes upon receiving thorough eye examinations designed specifically for their immediate kin [15]. Wang Y et al [8] found that the incidence of first-degree relatives of KC, as measured by corneal topography, is 15 to 67 times higher than in the general population. At present, the vast majority of the studies on KC heritability focus on polymorphisms of genes, and few studies on KC-related pathogenic gene variants. Genes studied for polymorphism include FNDC3B, PNPLA2, LOX, COL5A1, RAB3GAP1, CAST, HGF, FOXO1, MAML2, TGFBI, MPDZ/NF1B, SOD1, WNT10A, IMMP2L, COL4A3, VSX1, COL4A4, TSC1, IL1A, IL1B [16], ALDH3A1 [17], POLG, XRCC1 [18], STON2 [19] and others. In recent years, it has been found that KC can be inherited in a Mendelian manner, with AD expression or AR expression in some of the gene variants in the family, such as DOCK9 [20], ZNF469 [14], SLC4A11, TGFBI, PIKFYVE, ZEB1 [11] and TUBA3D [21] as AD genes, and HMX1 [11], USH2A [22] and GALNT14 [23] are AR genes. In this study, six KC families were diagnosed by Pentacam 3D anterior segment analysis system, Corvis ST visual corneal biomechanics analyzer and clinical history and examination. WES was used to find that each of the six probands carried six AD genes, and their affected parents carried the same gene variants, suggesting that all six families were inherited from AD.AD inheritance contains five types, including complete dominance, incomplete dominance, irregular dominance, co-dominance, and delayed dominance [24]. Among them, irregular dominance refers to heterozygotes (Aa) individuals may or may not exhibit phenotypes under different internal and external environments, and the degree of expression may also be different, making the transmission somewhat irregular. In this study, the affected parents in four AD families carried the same variant as the probands, but the clinical phenotype was mild, and variable expressivity, showing irregular dominance inheritance.

AD inheritance contains five types, including complete dominance, incomplete dominance, irregular dominance, co-dominance, and delayed dominance [24]. Among them, irregular dominance refers to heterozygotes (Aa) individuals may or may not exhibit phenotypes under different internal and external environments, and the degree of expression may also be different, making the transmission somewhat irregular. In this study, the affected parents in four AD families carried the same variant as the probands, but the clinical phenotype was mild, and variable expressivity, showing irregular dominance inheritance.

The ZNF469 gene (MIM 612078), found at 16q24.2, comprises a single exon with 3953 amino acids. It is found across numerous tissues, including the human cornea [25]. Notably, the protein encoded by ZNF469 features three classic C2H2 zinc finger motifs at its C-terminal end, which are crucial to its function [25]. Furthermore, ZNF469 exhibits a 30% similarity to the helical segments of three collagen types—COL4A1, COL1A2, and COL1A1—that play a role in forming the structure of the human cornea [25]. Zinc finger proteins, or ZNFs, serve as specialized DNA-binding modules that fine-tune particular transcriptional activities [25]. They're considered to play a pivotal role in controlling the production and organization of collagen fibrils [25]. Mutations within the ZNF469 gene have the potential to disrupt the consistency and order of corneal collagen's primary fibers. Previously, such gene was reported to frequently cause AR-inherited Brittle cornea syndrome 1 (MIM 229200), characterized by extreme corneal thinning and fragility [26]. However, KC associated with this gene has also been reported in recent years [14]. Stanton CM et al [27] employed genome editing techniques to replicate a mutation associated with human ZNF469 BCS in the corresponding mouse gene, Zfp469. Their findings revealed that a homozygous mutation in Zfp469 leads to marked thinning of both the central and peripheral cornea. This thinning is attributed to a reduction in stromal thickness and a decline in the cornea's biomechanical strength. Bao J et al [28] created zebrafish with the 4694del/4del mutation by utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 technology, revealing that a homozygous mutation in ZNF469 results in reduced production of several extracellular matrix (ECM) components, including collagens and proteoglycans. Yu X et al [14] reported that seven new variants (c.3466G>A, c.2059G>A, c.7262G>A, c.4300G>A, c.2137C>A, c.4684G>A, and c.3749C>T) were identified as Likely Pathogenic in ZNF469 in Chinese KC patients. The ZNF469 variant was not detected in highly myopic patients or healthy controls. All seven variants in KC patients were heterozygous. The above studies suggest that ZNF469 could be a significant transcription factor in regulating the synthesis and degradation of the corneal ECM. It can result in AR genetic disorders as compound heterozygous or homozygous variants or AD disorders as heterozygotes. In family 1 of the study, the proband carried the new ZNF469 heterozygous variant c.4384G>A (p.Asp1462Asn), and his father carried the same heterozygous variant. Corneal morphology revealed that Belin's analysis of both eyes of the proband's father showed suspicious values for Dt, the right eye had a pathological value for TBI, and the left eye had a suspicious value for TBI, which suggested a risk of keratectasia. The genetic test along with the clinical observations suggested that the variant site was co-segregated in the family, showing irregular dominance.

The KRT12 gene (MIM 601687) is located at 17q21.2 and consists of eight exons containing 469 amino acids. It plays a role in the organization and integrity of corneal epithelium and the expression of corneal keratin [29]. As reported previously, this gene is associated with Meesmann corneal dystrophy 1 (MIM 122100), which is marked by the presence of microcysts within the epithelial layer of the central cornea [30]. Nishino et al. [31]showed that Meesmann corneal dystrophy impacts not just the corneal epithelium but also extends its influence to the corneal stroma and Bowman membrane. In family 2 of the study, the proband and her mother shared an identical heterozygous variant of the KRT12 gene. Corneal morphology revealed that the height of the posterior surface of the proband mother's cornea at the thinnest point exceeded the normal value. Belin analysis of the various testing indexes showed the state of the pathological values, abnormal TBI in both eyes, and the clinical diagnosis of KC (incipient stage in both eyes). The combination of genetic testing and clinical phenotype analysis revealed that the variant site was consistently inherited within this family, indicating an AD inheritance.

The COL8A2 gene (MIM 120252) is located at 1p34.3 and consists of a single exon containing 703 amino acids. It encodes a polypeptide chains (α2) of collagen Type VIII which is expressed in both corneal (Descemet membrane) and vascular endothelial cells [32]. Such gene was previously reported to cause AD-inherited Polymorphous Posterior Corneal Dystrophy 2 (MIM 609140) and Fuchs Endothelial Corneal Dystrophy (MIM 136800) in manifestation of abnormalities in corneal endothelial cells and basement membrane [33]. Lam et al. [34] reported the clinical manifestations of KC in a patient with Polymorphous Posterior Corneal Dystrophy 2. Hwang et al. [35] found enhanced corneal opacity in rat corneas transfected with siCOL8A2. In family 3 of the study, the proband and his mother shared with the identified heterozygous variant of the COL8A2 gene. Corneal morphology showed that the mother of proband had a suspicious value for Db and a suspicious value for TBI in both eyes as analyzed by Belin, suggesting a risk of keratectasia. Genetic testing alongside the clinical phenotype revealed that the variant site was co-segregated in this family, which showed an irregular dominance.

The COL18A1 gene (MIM 120328) is located at 21q22.3 and consists of 43 exons containing 1754 amino acids. It likely significantly influences both retinal structure and neural tube closure [36]. This gene has been previously reported to cause AD- inherited primary angle-closure glaucoma [37](MIM 618880) and Knobloch syndrome1 inherited from AR [38](MIM 267750). Ocular clinical manifestations of Knobloch syndrome1 include nystagmus, high myopia, corneal leukomalacia, congenital cataracts, lens dislocation, retinal detachment, and macular dysplasia [38] . MarnerosAG et al [39] discovered that irregularities in the iris and ciliary body of COL18A1−/− mice highlighted the crucial function of collagen Type XVIII in maintaining the integrity of ocular basement membranes. In family 4 of this study, the proband and his father shared an identical heterozygous variant of the COL18A1 gene. As revealed by corneal morphology, the Belin analysis of the father of proband showed that the Dp of the right eye and the CBI of the left eye were pathological values, suggesting that there was a risk of keratectasia. Genetic testing along with the clinical manifestation revealed that the variant site was co-segregated in the family, which showed an irregular dominance.

The PMS2 gene (MIM 600259) is located at 7p22.1 and consists of 15 exons containing 862 amino acids, which belongs to one of the mismatch repair genes. It is involved in correcting the mispairing of single nucleotides during DNA replication, and its mutation causes microsatellites instability (MSI), DNA replication cannot be carried out normally or genetic information is incorrectly transmitted [40]. This gene has been previously reported to cause AD- inherited Lynch syndrome (MIM 614337), often presenting as colon and endometrial cancers [41]. Jakobiec FA et al [42] reported a patient with a cortical tumor occurring at the meibomian glands on the upper eyelid, which has not been reported in any other ocular tissue structures. In family 5 of this study, the proband and his father both presented with an identified heterozygous variant of the PMS2 gene. A closer examination of the corneal structure revealed that Belin analysis for the proband's father flagged the CBI and TBI values in the right eye as concerning. The genetic assessment, when considered alongside the clinical phenotype, suggested that this candidate gene variant has co-segregated within the family, exhibiting an irregular dominance.

The DPP6 gene (MIM 126141) is located at 7q36.2 and consists of 26 exons containing 865 amino acids. It serves as a supportive subunit for A-type K+ channels that contain Kv4, playing a crucial role in modulating the excitability and adaptability of neurons and other electrically active cells [43]. The gene has been previously reported to cause AD-inherited paroxysmal ventricular fibrillation [44] (MIM 612956) and mental retardation [45] (MIM 616311). There is no relevant literature reporting ocular diseases caused by the DPP6 gene, but a query through the LifeMap database (https://discovery.lifemapsc.com/) revealed that the DPP6 gene was found to be expressed in embryonic and adult retinal pigment epithelial cells by microarray analysis. In family 6 of the study, the proband and his mother shared a heterozygous variant of the DPP6 gene. Examination of the corneal structure indicated a reduction in corneal thickness in both eyes, along with an elevated posterior surface height at the thinnest area of the right cornea, which was above the typical range. Belin analysis of the multiple testing indexes showed the state of the pathological values, pathological values for TBI in both eyes, and the clinical diagnosis of KC (level I completion stage in the right eye and incipient stage in the left eye). The combination of genetic testing and clinical phenotype revealed that the candidate gene variant site was consistently inherited within this family, indicating an AD inheritance.

The ZNF469, KRT12, COL8A2, and COL18A1 genes identified in this study were correlated with the corneal tissue's typical structure and biomechanical function. The ZNF469 gene variant may reduce the thickness of corneal stroma by affecting the synthesis of various extracellular matrices (e.g., collagen and proteoglycans) [25, 27, 28]. The KRT12 gene variant may alter corneal epithelial cells, stroma and Bowman membrane by affecting cornea-specific keratins [29, 31]. The COL8A2 gene variant may result in decreased proliferation of corneal endothelial cell and increase of corneal opacity by affecting collagen VIII [32]. The COL18A1 gene variant may alter Bowman membrane structure by affecting collagen XVIII [39]. All the aforementioned gene variants may impair corneal biomechanics and significantly contribute to the progression of KC. The PMS2 and DPP6 genes were reported for the first time to associate with ocular corneal disease, and both genes may be the candidate genes related to KC, which the further study should be conducted for validation.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis showed that COL8A2 protein acts as a central node and forms direct interactions with other key proteins such as COL18A1, FN1, ZNF469, and KRT12 proteins. This suggests that COL8A2 is crucial for preserving the structure and function of the cornea. ZNF469, KRT12, and DPP6 proteins interact with the COL8A2-COL18A1-FN1 complex through different mechanisms, and could be significant in the development of KC. COL8A2 and COL18A1 are collectively involved in the formation and maintenance of the corneal stroma, providing mechanical support and structural stability. FN1 promotes cell attachment and stromal remodeling by regulating cell-collagen interactions [46]. COL18A1 is crucial to the basement membrane and maintains its structural integrity. FN1 acts as a bridge between the basement membrane and the stroma, regulating cell-stroma interactions. FN1 regulates cell signaling by interacting with receptors such as integrins and influences cell attachment, migration, and differentiation [47]. Abnormalities in COL8A2 and COL18A1 may affect the structure of the ECM, which in turn affects FN1 function and signaling. An imbalance in the interplay among these three proteins could result in structural instability within both the corneal stroma and the basement membrane, ultimately causing thinning of the cornea and alterations in its morphology. In patients with KC, defective or abnormal expression of COL8A2 and COL18A1 could be a significant element contributing to the cornea's structural instability, and abnormal expression and dysfunction of FN1 may affect the repair and reconstruction process of the corneal epithelium and stroma, exacerbating corneal deformation.

Although no direct interaction between PMS2 and other proteins has been identified, some studies suggest that PMS2 may contribute to KC pathogenesis through mitochondrial dysfunction. Recent studies increasingly point to mitochondrial dysfunction as a central contributor to the pathogenesis of KC, with findings showing decreased mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) integrity, reduced mtDNA content, and elevated mitochondrial transcript levels in KC corneas [48]. These alterations reflect a state of oxidative stress and impaired mitochondrial homeostasis. PMS2, a critical DNA mismatch repair protein, is traditionally known for maintaining nuclear genome stability, but it also plays a growingly recognized role in safeguarding mitochondrial DNA under conditions of cellular stress. Dysfunction or downregulation of PMS2, potentially induced by environmental factors such as ultraviolet radiation, oxidative damage, and mechanical trauma through epigenetic mechanisms, could compromise the repair of mtDNA and exacerbate mitochondrial instability [49]. Accumulation of mtDNA damage would impair energy metabolism, increase reactive oxygen species production, and trigger apoptosis or matrix degradation in corneal stromal cells, accelerating the corneal thinning and ectasia seen in KC. Furthermore, the precedent set by dysregulation of DNA repair genes in other corneal dystrophies, such as Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy, supports the plausibility that DNA repair failure—particularly involving mitochondrial maintenance pathways—may also underlie KC development [50]. Thus, PMS2 dysfunction offers a compelling link between environmental insults, mitochondrial genome damage, and the progressive stromal degeneration characteristic of keratoconus, warranting further investigation into its role as a novel mediator of disease pathogenesis.

In this study, we employed bioinformatics analysis methods to predict the pathogenicity of genetic variants. Currently, a vast number of variants in the human genome are being continuously discovered and have been widely documented in numerous databases. When genetic variants are detected and require pathogenicity analysis and classification, valuable reference information can be found in existing databases and published literature. For the pathogenicity assessment of detected genetic variants, it is necessary to obtain the frequency of the variants in the population. Population databases provide information on the frequency of specific variants in large-scale populations. In this study, we used the gnomAD database to query the frequency of point mutations in normal populations. This database comprises 730,947 samples, covering populations from Africa, East Asia, Europe, South Asia, the Americas, and others, with all samples having whole-genome sequencing data. There are still some limitations present in our study. This study primarily relied on predictive evidence to assess the pathogenicity of genetic variants and did not include functional experimental research. Functional experiments are a powerful tool for investigating variant pathogenicity, providing validation evidence at different levels—molecular, cellular, organ, and animal—with varying strengths. For the novel genes or new variants in known pathogenic genes associated with KC disease identified in this study, functional studies could complement genetic and bioinformatics analyses. Additionally, collecting more KC cases and establishing a normal control group could strengthen the evidence for pathogenicity. If a variant is found to occur at a significantly higher frequency in the affected population compared to the control group, it could serve as strong pathogenic evidence (PS4).

Conclusion

In this study, six novel variants in ZNF469, KRT12, COL8A2, COL18A1, PMS2, and DPP6 were identified to associate with adKC and the irregular expression of proteins associated with KC-related genes could play a significant role in the cornea's structural instability. The phenotypes of adKC patients in the same family were variable expressivity, presenting irregular dominance inheritance. Our study expanded the candidate genes for KC genetic screening and suggested a combination of genetic testing and clinical data analysis for first generation relatives of proband could assist in the preliminary detection of KC. The further exploration of functional studies is needed for the pathogenesis of these variants.
