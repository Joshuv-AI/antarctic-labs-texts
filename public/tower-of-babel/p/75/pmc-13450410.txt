Ultrasound-assisted extraction of bioactive phenolic compounds from walnut green husk: Phytochemical characterization and multi-endpoint Caenorhabditis elegans bioactivity screening for food-relevant applications
Authors: Amel Hamdi, Miguel Angel Córdoba-Rojano, Elisa Guillén-Izquierdo, Jose Manuel Monje-Moreno, Rocío Rodríguez-Arcos, Ana Jiménez-Araujo, Manuel J Muñoz, Rafael Guillén-Bejarano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study optimized phenolic extraction from walnut green husk (Juglans regia L.) and established a screening framework for evaluating bioactivity of agricultural byproduct extracts toward food ingredient development. Indirect ultrasound-assisted extraction with 80% ethanol yielded maximum phenolic content (477 mg/100 g fresh weight), 8.7-fold higher than water extraction. Principal component analysis confirmed solvent polarity as the primary factor in extraction selectivity. HPLC-DAD-MS identified thirteen compounds dominated by naphthoquinones (31%) and tetralones (24%). Caenorhabditis elegans whole-organism screening demonstrated significant bioactivities: oxidative stress resistance, thermal tolerance, proteotoxicity alleviation, and metabolic modulation. This approach supports the use of C. elegans screening as a rapid bioassay for evaluating bioactivity of agricultural byproduct extracts, with scalable extraction methodology (food-grade solvent, ambient temperature, 10 min) suitable for industrial valorisation.Keywords: Walnut green husk, Ultrasound extraction, Naphthoquinones, Caenorhabditis elegans, Food ingredient characterization, Bioactivity screening

Introduction

The food and agricultural industries generate substantial byproduct streams that are typically underutilized despite containing valuable bioactive compounds. These materials, including fruit peels, vegetable scraps, and processing residues, represent both an environmental challenge and an untapped resource for developing natural food ingredients (Berenguer et al., 2023). Converting these byproducts into functional food ingredients aligns with circular economy principles while providing cost-effective alternatives to synthetic additives. Advanced extraction techniques and analytical methods have enabled isolation and characterization of bioactive compounds from agricultural waste, revealing their potential for food preservation and functional food applications (Comunian et al., 2021). Walnut green husk (WGH) exemplifies the valorization opportunity in agricultural byproducts. Representing 45–60% of walnut fruit weight, this material is currently discarded or composted despite containing diverse phenolic compounds including naphthoquinones, tetralones, naphthalene derivatives, and phenolic acids (Fordos et al., 2023; Jahanban-Esfahlan et al., 2019). WGH extracts demonstrate antioxidant and antimicrobial properties relevant to natural food preservation, with potential to replace synthetic additives while extending shelf life (Dehghani et al., 2019; Rajković et al., 2023; Soto-Madrid et al., 2020; Xi et al., 2023). The phenolic composition positions WGH extracts as candidates for functional food development, with biological studies indicating stress-protective properties and protein stabilization effects (Dehghani et al., 2019; Salejda et al., 2016; Tabaraki & Rastgoo, 2014; Xi et al., 2023). Indeed, WGH extracts have already been directly applied in food matrices, including mayonnaise emulsions (Soto-Madrid et al., 2020), ketchup formulations (Dehghani et al., 2019), and cooked sausages (Salejda et al., 2016), demonstrating preservation efficacy across diverse product types. Despite this recognized potential, most studies have characterized WGH extracts obtained from dried material, where post-harvest processing substantially alters phenolic composition, particularly thermolabile naphthoquinones. A systematic evaluation comparing extraction modes and tissue processing states, combined with biological characterization beyond conventional chemical assays, remains lacking. Efficient extraction is critical for harnessing WGH bioactive compounds. Two key factors influence phenolic recovery: solvent polarity, with ethanol-water mixtures (50–80% ethanol) consistently outperforming pure solvents (Fernández-Agulló et al., 2013; Tabaraki & Rastgoo, 2014; Vieira et al., 2020; Zhang, 2015), and extraction technology, with ultrasound-assisted methods enhancing yields over conventional maceration and decoction (Soto-Madrid et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2016; Ziaolhagh & Zare, 2022). These studies employed dried plant material and did not systematically compare indirect and direct ultrasound modes, leaving unresolved how cavitation intensity and temperature control affect the recovery of thermolabile compounds such as naphthoquinones. While these studies provide valuable food matrix data (Dehghani et al., 2019; Salejda et al., 2016; Soto-Madrid et al., 2020), the bioactivity of WGH extracts has only been assessed through in vitro chemical assays (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP), which fail to capture integrated physiological responses. Caenorhabditis elegans provides a whole-organism platform for evaluating bioactivity through physiologically relevant endpoints, such as stress resistance and protein homeostasis (David et al., 2010; Gong et al., 2012).

This study establishes a screening framework using C. elegans for the functional characterization of WGH as a model system. We optimized phenolic extraction from walnut green husk by comparing five methods; In the case of ethanol, direct extraction by homogenization with ultraturrax, direct and indirect ultrasound treatment were compared. In addition to these methods, water extractions were also conducted using infusion and decoction techniques. The highest-yielding method was selected for comprehensive phytochemical characterization by HPLC-DAD-MS and bioactivity assessment using C. elegans. Bioactivity endpoints were specifically chosen for their relevance to food ingredient characterization: oxidative stress resistance, thermal stress tolerance, proteotoxicity alleviation, and metabolic modulation.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to (i) systematically compare eight extraction protocols combining five techniques with two solvents applied to fresh frozen WGH tissue, revealing a naphthoquinone-tetralone-rich chemotype (67% of total phenolics) unattainable from dried material; and (ii) characterize WGH extract bioactivity through multi-endpoint C. elegans screening across four strains and five biological endpoints, representing the first in-vivo bioactivity evaluation of walnut green husk in a whole-organism model and extending assessment beyond conventional chemical antioxidant assays (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP). This integrated approach, combining extraction optimization, phytochemical profiling, and biological screening, provides a replicable methodology applicable to diverse agricultural byproduct streams. These in-vivo bioactivity results add a new layer of biological evidence that complements the existing food matrix studies and could open the way toward translational screening approaches for future food applications.

Materials and methods

Plant material

WGH samples from the Gran Jefe variety were collected in a plantation located in Nerpio, Spain. The fresh green husk samples were packed into vacuum-seal bags using a vacuum sealer to prevent oxidation, and then transported to the laboratory, where they were stored at −20 °C prior to further analysis.

The extraction procedure

Two extraction solvents, 80% ethanol (E) and water (W), were used for sample preparation. In both cases, the samples were sectioned into fine fragments and homogenised using an Ultra-Turrax T25 (Janke & Kunkel/IKA Labortechnik, Munich, Germany) at a solvent-to-solid ratio of 10:1 (v/w). The resulting homogenates were separated from the solid residues by filtration using the Whatman No. 1 filter paper (Anoia, Barcelona, Spain) and stored at −20 °C prior to analytical assessment (designated as extracts E and W, respectively).

Two ultrasonic extraction techniques were investigated: indirect ultrasound (IU) and direct ultrasound (DU). For IU treatment, homogenised samples were subjected to sonication in an ultrasonic bath (ULTRASONIC CLEANER, Model 30, Shenzhen Jietai Ultrasonic Cleaning Equipment Co. Ltd., Shenzhen, China; 6 L capacity, 180 W ultrasonic power, 40 kHz frequency) for 10 min at room temperature. The resulting extracts (E-IU and W-IU) were filtered and stored at −20 °C until further analysis. For DU treatment, homogenised samples were processed using a high-intensity ultrasonic processor (UP400St, Hielscher Ultrasonics, Teltow, Germany) equipped with an S24d14D sonotrode (Hielscher, Teltow, Germany), operating at a maximum power output of 400 W and a frequency of 60 kHz for 10 min under non-temperature-controlled conditions. Following sonication, the E-DU and W-DU extracts were filtered and maintained at −20 °C until further analysis.

Two additional thermal extraction methods were evaluated for aqueous extraction. In both protocols, the samples were initially homogenised with ultraturrax in water at 100 °C. For the infusion method (W-INF), the homogenate was incubated at room temperature for 10 min, then filtered and stored at −20 °C before analysis. For the decoction method (W-DEC), the homogenate was heated to boiling for 10 min, then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The resulting filtrate was stored at −20 °C until further analysis. All extraction procedures were performed in triplicate.

Analysis of phenolic compounds using HPLC-DAD-MS

The analyses of phenolic compounds were carried out using a Jasco-LC-Net II ADC liquid chromatograph system (LC-Net II/ADC, JASCO Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a diode array detector (DAD). Phenolic compounds were separated using a MEDITERRANEA SEA C18 reverse-phase analytical column (25 cm length × 4.6 mm i.d., 5 μm particle size, Teknokroma, Barcelona, Spain). The gradient profile for the separation of phenolic compounds was established using solvent A (water with 1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile) as follows: the proportion of solvent B was increased from 0% to 20% over the first 20 min, then to 21% over the next 8 min, maintained at 21% for 2 min, followed by an increase to 30% over the next 10 min, then to 100% over the next 5 min, maintained at 100% for 5 min, and finally returned to the initial conditions over the next 5 min. The flow rate was 1 mL/min, and the column temperature was 30 °C. Absorption spectra for all peaks were recorded in the 200–600 nm range, and chromatograms were acquired at 280 nm for phenolic acids, naphthalene, and tetralone derivatives, 360 nm for flavonoids, and 420 nm for naphthoquinones for quantification. Phenolic compounds were quantified using external calibration curves and reference standards. For phenolic compounds for which no commercial standard was available, the calibration curve of a compound from the same phenolic group was used for quantification. The results were expressed in milligrams per 100 g of fresh WGH. The total content of phenolic compounds was determined as the sum of the individual phenolic compounds quantified by HPLC-DAD. Identification of individual phenolic compounds was based on their retention times, and both spectroscopic and mass spectrometric data, the latter acquired using a single quadrupole mass spectrometer (ACQUITY QDa II, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). ESI mass spectra were obtained at ionization energies of 20 and 50 eV (negative mode) and 20 and 50 eV (positive mode), with MS scans from m/z 100 to 1000. The capillary voltage, dissolving temperature, source temperature, and extractor voltage were 0.8 kV, 200 °C, 100 °C, and 12 V, respectively. The flow was maintained at 1 mL/min.

Bioactivity assessment using C. elegans as a screening platform

C. elegans serves as a whole-organism screening platform, enabling integrated physiological assessment of bioactive compounds relevant to food applications (David et al., 2010; Gong et al., 2012). Unlike in vitro chemical assays (DPPH, ABTS) that measure isolated reactions, this model captures cellular uptake, metabolism, and systems-level responses. Bioassays were selected to assess multiple stress-related endpoints: oxidative and thermal stress resistance, proteotoxicity alleviation, and metabolic modulation.

C. elegans strains and maintenance conditions

The following C. elegans strains were acquired from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Centre (CGC; University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, USA): wild-type N2 (Bristol), GM63 [daf-2(e1370ts) III], GMC101 [dvIs100(Punc-54: amyloid-ß1–42:3′UTR unc-54 + Pmtl-2::GFP)II], and NL5901 [pkIs2386(unc-54p::alpha-synuclein::YFP + unc-119(+))]. Nematode growth medium (NGM) and M9 Buffer (M9) were prepared as previously described by Stiernagle, 2006. NGM plates were seeded with E. coli OP50 as a food source. Age-synchronized L1 larvae were generated via alkaline hypochlorite treatment according to (Stiernagle, 2006) with modifications: gravid adults were washed from plates and lysed in a modified hypochlorite solution (76.8% H₂O, 16.4% sodium hypochlorite, 6.6% 2 M NaOH) for 3–9 min with intermittent agitation, followed by three M9 washes. Eggs were hatched in M9 buffer overnight to arrest larvae at the L1 stage before plating on OP50-seeded NGM plates (Stiernagle, 2006).

Preparation of the WGH solutions

WGH extracts were prepared for biological activity assessment via ethanolic extraction using indirect ultrasound-assisted treatment. The crude extract was concentrated using a rotary evaporator (Büchi R-210, Flawil, Switzerland) at 35 °C under reduced pressure and subsequently reconstituted in 70% acetone to generate two stock solutions: EC1 (167 mg/mL) and EC2 (1670 mg/mL). Before bioassays, extracts were sterilised by filtration through 0.22 μm cellulose acetate membranes and stored at −20 °C. 70% acetone was selected as the vehicle solvent due to the limited water solubility of the evaporated WGH extract. A preliminary assay confirmed no differences in worm viability or behaviour between 70% acetone and water across all C. elegans strains used in this study. In all bioassays, the negative control consisted of 70% acetone without extract, applied at the same volume as the treatments. In plate-based assays, extract or vehicle was applied onto the surface of NGM plates and allowed to dry completely at room temperature under a laminar flow bench before worm transfer, ensuring complete evaporation of the volatile acetone vehicle and exposure of worms only to the deposited phenolic residue.

Purification of the free phenolic fraction from WGH

Free phenolic compounds were isolated from WGH using a sequential purification protocol. Briefly, 100 mL of EC1 extract was evaporated to dryness at 35 °C under reduced pressure (rotary evaporator: Büchi R-210, Flawil, Switzerland), dissolved in 30 mL of distilled water, and subjected to triple liquid-liquid extraction with ethyl acetate (1,1, v/v). The pooled ethyl acetate fractions were concentrated to dryness under reduced pressure (rotary evaporator: Büchi R-210, Flawil, Switzerland) and then reconstituted in 70% acetone (final volume: 100 mL). Before bioassays, the purified extract was sterilised by filtration through a 0.22 μm cellulose acetate membrane and stored at −20 °C. The recovery rate of total phenolic compounds after ethyl acetate purification was 99%, as determined by HPLC-DAD. A 70% acetone solution was used as the negative control in place of the free phenolic fraction. To exclude potential indirect effects arising from bacterial food-source modulation, the antibacterial activity of the WGH extract and the free phenolic fraction against E. coli OP50 was assessed using an agar well diffusion assay at the same concentrations used in the bioassays. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, no growth-inhibition zone was observed for either preparation, confirming that the tested concentrations do not affect OP50 viability.

Concentration-response characterization

Synchronized young adult C. elegans (N2 strain) were exposed to WGH extracts at 1.7, 17, 85, and 170 mg/mL (corresponding to 8, 80, 400, and 800 μg/mL total phenolics; Table S1). Age-synchronized L1 larvae were obtained by bleaching gravid hermaphrodites, followed by 24 h incubation in M9 buffer at 20 °C with agitation. Approximately 450 L1 larvae were cultured on NGM plates at 20 °C until reaching young adulthood (∼52 h). Young adults were collected, washed three times with Milli-Q water, and exposed to extracts (diluted in Milli-Q water) or controls in 96-well plates (48 wells/condition, 100 μL/well, 10–20 worms/well) for 24 h at 20 °C. Viability was assessed by spontaneous movement following gentle plate agitation. Concentrations showing ≥95% viability were selected for bioactivity screening to avoid stress-induced artifacts. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Thermal stress resistance assay

This assay evaluates organism survival following thermal challenge (37 °C, 2 h). Ten gravid N2 adults were transferred to NGM plates seeded with E. coli OP50 and supplemented with 100 μL EC1 (1.7 mg/mL), EC2 (17 mg/mL), free phenolic extract, or 70% acetone (control). After 2 h of egg-laying, adults were removed, and the eggs were incubated at 20 °C to the L4 stage. Sixty L4 nematodes were transferred to fresh supplemented plates, exposed to 37 °C for 2 h, then recovered at 20 °C for 24 h. Viability was assessed by tactile stimulation; non-responsive worms were scored as non-viable. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Oxidative stress resistance: assessing antioxidant protective capacity

This assay quantifies antioxidant protective capacity using juglone (100 μM) as a pro-oxidant stressor generating superoxide radicals (Ahmad & Suzuki, 2019),. Ten gravid N2 adults were transferred to supplemented NGM plates (as in 2.4.5) for 2 h egg-laying. Eggs developed at 20 °C to day 2 adulthood. Synchronized adults were collected, washed, and adjusted to 100 worms/mL in treatment solutions. Nematodes were exposed to 100 μM juglone for 1 h, washed three times, transferred to fresh NGM plates, and incubated at 20 °C for 24 h. Viability was assessed by tactile response. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Proteotoxicity alleviation: dual-model assessment

Protein misfolding and aggregation represent well-characterized cellular stress mechanisms (Duennwald, 2015). Two transgenic models expressing aggregation-prone human proteins were employed to assess whether WGH extract treatment improves organism tolerance to proteotoxic stress.

–β-Amyloid model

β-Amyloid model

The GMC101 transgenic strain expresses human amyloid-β1–42 (Aβ1–42) in body wall muscle cells under control of the unc-54 promoter. Increasing the temperature to 25 °C accelerates Aβ1–42 aggregation in muscle tissue, leading to progressive, age-dependent paralysis, which serves as a quantifiable functional endpoint. Age-synchronized L1 larvae (n = 200/plate) were cultured on supplemented NGM plates at 16 °C to the L4 stage. Sixty L4 nematodes (30/plate) were transferred to fresh supplemented plates and incubated at 25 °C for 16 h to induce aggregation. Mobility was assessed by tactile response: worms failing to move spontaneously or to traverse one body length after three anterior prods were scored as mobility-impaired. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

–α-Synuclein model

α-Synuclein model

The strain (NL5901) exhibits progressive age-related mobility decline due to α-synuclein protein aggregation in body wall muscle cells, providing an independent validation. Three gravid hermaphrodites were transferred to supplemented NGM plates for egg-laying at 20 °C. Progeny developed to L4 stage (∼2.5 days), then twenty L4 nematodes were transferred to fresh supplemented plates and cultured at 20 °C. On day 5 of adulthood, locomotion was quantified by body bends over 60 s following a 30-s M9 buffer recovery. Minimum 20 worms/condition, three independent replicates. Consistent protection across both models suggests that WGH extract exerts broad protective capacity against structurally distinct aggregation-prone proteins.

Metabolic function assessment using insulin/IGF-1 signalling model

This essay evaluates the metabolic modulation properties of the extract. The C. elegans daf-2(e1370) mutant carries a temperature-sensitive mutation in the insulin/IGF-1 receptor ortholog, leading to reduced activity of the insulin signalling pathway. This genetic model exhibits reduced reproductive capacity at elevated temperatures, providing a quantifiable endpoint for screening bioactive compounds with insulin pathway-modulating activity (Gems et al., 1998; Kenyon et al., 1993; Murphy & Hu, 2013). Reproductive output serves as an integrated physiological endpoint reflecting metabolic status, as reproduction is tightly coupled to nutrient sensing and energy metabolism in this organism. Age-synchronized L1 larvae (n = 200/plate) were cultured on supplemented NGM plates at 16 °C to L4 stage (∼2.5 days). Thirty L4 nematodes were transferred individually to 35 mm plates with 50 μL supplement and incubated at 25 °C for 72 h. Progeny production was quantified per parental worm. Enhanced reproduction under metabolic stress indicates that WGH extract modulates the insulin/IGF-1 signalling pathway. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Statistical analysis

All samples were analysed in triplicate (n ≥ 3). Intergroup comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in Statgraphics Plus version 2.1; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using R software (version 4.x; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) with the FactoMineR and factoextra packages for PCA visualisation. PCA was conducted on autoscaled phenolic composition data (13 compounds × 8 extraction methods) to identify patterns of extraction selectivity. C. elegans assay data were processed using GraphPad Prism 9 (v9.0a). All quantitative experiments were independently repeated three times in biological replicates, yielding consistent results. Representative micrographs in the figures were selected from three independent experimental trials that yielded comparable progeny production and locomotion outcomes. Mobility assay data reflect mean values derived from three independent replicates.

Effect of solvent and extraction method on total phenolic yield

Total phenolic content varied 20-fold across extraction protocols, ranging from 23.9 mg/100 g fresh weight (FW) in hot water infusion (W-INF) to 477.1 mg/100 g FW in 80% ethanol with indirect ultrasound (E-IU) (Fig. 1). Primary factor - Solvent polarity effect: Regardless of extraction method, 80% ethanol consistently outperformed water (p < 0.05), yielding 6.3- to 19.9-fold higher phenolic content. Among ethanol-based protocols, yields were 388 ± 12 (E), 477 ± 24 (E-IU), and 381 ± 42 mg/100 g FW (E-DU), with E-IU significantly exceeding both E and E-DU (p < 0.05). In contrast, water-based extractions yielded uniformly low recoveries: 52 ± 7 (W), 60 ± 8 (W-IU), 38 ± 15 (W-DU), 24 ± 2 (W-INF), and 39 ± 6 mg/100 g FW (W-DEC), with no significant differences among them (p > 0.05).

The superior performance of aqueous ethanol aligns with polarity-matching principles in natural product extraction (Vieira et al., 2020). Walnut green husk contains predominantly lipophilic phenolic compounds (naphthoquinones and tetralones) with limited water solubility. The amphipathic nature of 80% ethanol, containing both polar hydroxyl groups and nonpolar ethyl chains, enables simultaneous solvation of aromatic rings and polar functional groups (Chemat et al., 2017), a capability absent in pure water. Our findings are consistent with previous walnut husk studies by Fernández-Agulló et al. (2013) and Zhang (2015), who reported similar ethanol-water yield ratios (6–10×) despite using different cultivars.

Secondary factor - Ultrasound mode effect: Within ethanol extractions, indirect ultrasound (E-IU) enhanced phenolic recovery by 1.23-fold compared to direct extraction (E) (p < 0.05), while direct ultrasound (E-DU) showed no improvement (p > 0.05). This divergence reflects opposing effects of cavitation intensity and temperature control between indirect (bath, 40 kHz, 180 W, room temperature) and direct (probe, 60 kHz, 400 W, uncontrolled heating) modes. Indirect ultrasound generates gentle, uniform cavitation that disrupts cell walls through microstreaming without thermal hotspots, preserving thermolabile compounds like juglone which degrades above 40 °C through volatilization and oxidative polymerization (Cosmulescu et al., 2010). Although direct temperature measurements were not performed during E-DU extraction, the selective reduction of juglone in E-DU (87 mg/100 g FW) compared to E-IU (150 mg/100 g FW), while thermally stable phenolic acids remained comparable between both methods (Table S1), is consistent with thermal degradation of heat-sensitive naphthoquinones under the uncontrolled heating conditions of probe sonication (Tabaraki & Rastgoo, 2014). While Tabaraki and Rastgoo (2014) demonstrated that ultrasonic bath treatment surpassed conventional maceration, their study did not compare indirect and direct ultrasound modes applied to the same material. Our results reveal that this distinction is critical: indirect ultrasound (bath, 40 kHz, controlled temperature) yielded 25% more phenolics than direct probe sonication (60 kHz, uncontrolled heating), a difference attributable to preservation of thermolabile naphthoquinones that degrade above 40 °C (Cosmulescu et al., 2010).

Thermal treatment effect (negligible): Among water-based methods, thermal protocols (W-INF, W-DEC) did not enhance total phenolic yields. W-INF produced the lowest recovery (23.9 ± 2 mg/100 g FW), and W-DEC yielded 39 ± 6 mg/100 g FW; both were significantly lower than the room-temperature ultrasound treatment (W-IU: 60 ± 8 mg/100 g FW, p < 0.05). The lack of improvement with heating suggests that thermal energy does not compensate for extraction limitations and may actually promote degradation reactions that reduce recoverable phenolic content (Dawidowicz et al., 2006; Friedman & Jürgens, 2000). Room-temperature ultrasound (W-IU) outperformed boiling water (W-DEC), demonstrating that mechanical cell disruption provides superior phenolic liberation while avoiding potential thermal degradation and reducing energy consumption, in line with green chemistry principles (Chemat et al., 2017).

The superior yield of E-IU establishes it as the optimal method. Furthermore, the 8.7-fold difference between ethanol and water extracts suggests this is due to fundamental differences in chemical selectivity rather than a general difference in extraction efficiency. To elucidate the mechanistic basis for this selectivity, we performed multivariate analysis.

Principal component analysis of extraction selectivity

To identify chemical drivers of extraction differences, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on complete phenolic profiles (13 compounds × 8 methods) quantified by HPLC-DAD (detailed quantification in Table S1, Supplementary Material). The model explained 82.3% of total variance (PC1: 70.8%, PC2: 11.5%), indicating phenolic composition is dominated by two factors (Fig. 2).

PC1 (70.8%): Solvent polarity determines compound class accessibility. The scores plot (Fig. 2A) revealed dichotomous separation: water methods (W, W-IU, W-DU, W-INF, W-DEC) clustered in negative PC1, while ethanol methods (E, E-IU, E-DU) positioned in positive PC1. This reflects fundamentally different extraction profiles determined by solvent chemistry rather than process intensity. E-IU occupied the most extreme position (PC1 = +4.6), indicating maximum lipophilic compound recovery.

Loadings analysis (Fig. 2B) identified eight compounds dominating PC1 separation, all characterized by polycyclic aromatic structures with limited water solubility. The highest contributions to PC1 were from trihydroxytetralone galloyl hexoside (THTH, +0.328, 10.8%) and trihydroxynaphthalene galloyl hexoside (THNH, +0.328, 10.8%), followed by taxifolin pentoside (TP, +0.325, 10.6%), hydrojuglone glucoside-1 (HJG-1, +0.325, 10.6%), dihydroxytetralone galloyl hexoside (DHTH, +0.322, 10.4%), juglone (JUG, +0.318, 10.1%), and syringic acid (SyA, +0.317, 10.0%). These compounds collectively explain 83.2% of PC1 variance and require ethanol's amphipathic solvation; water cannot access these compounds regardless of process intensification.

The chemical basis for ethanol selectivity is exemplified by juglone, whose planar tricyclic structure and quinone moiety confer strong hydrophobic character (logP = 2.9; (Mirjalili & Karimi, 2013)), rendering it virtually insoluble in water (0.3 mg/mL) but readily soluble in ethanol (25 mg/mL). Even glycosylated derivatives (DHTH, THTH, THNH, HJG-1) retain substantial lipophilic character because their large aromatic aglycones (molecular weight 230–350 Da) dominate over polar sugar moieties (glucose/hexose: 180 Da), explaining their exclusive ethanol extraction despite glycosylation.

Phenolic acids showed minimal contribution to PC1 variance, with gallic acid (GA) contributing only 0.2% and trans-3-caffeoylquinic acid (CQA) contributing 0.8%. These hydrophilic compounds possess multiple hydroxyl and carboxyl groups enabling extensive hydrogen bonding with water. Gallic acid (3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid) exemplifies this class, with aqueous solubility of 11.9 g/L at 20 °C (logP = 0.7; (Daneshfar et al., 2008)) due to eight potential hydrogen bonding sites. However, phenolic acids account for only 11–15% of total husk phenolics, which explains the low water-extraction yields despite successful acid recovery.

Principal Component 1 (PC1), explaining 70.8% of the variance, primarily captures the chemical divergence between lipophilic (e.g., naphthoquinones, tetralones) and hydrophilic (e.g., phenolic acids) phenolics. This separation is driven by solvent polarity. The intermediate polarity of 80% aqueous ethanol (dielectric constant, ε = 24.3) enables the simultaneous extraction of both compound classes via dual solvation mechanisms. In contrast, the high polarity of pure water (ε = 80.0) largely restricts efficient extraction to hydrophilic compounds. For antimicrobial applications reliant on naphthoquinones such as juglone (MIC 15–60 μg/mL against Gram-positive bacteria) (Fernández-Agulló et al., 2013). Ethanol-based extraction is essential, as process intensification cannot overcome this fundamental solvent constraint. Conversely, water extraction selectively recovers phenolic acids (100% of water extract phenolics), which may be preferable for applications requiring hydrophilic antioxidant profiles. This selectivity pattern provides practical guidance for tailoring WGH extraction to specific target compound classes.

PC2 (11.5%): Method-specific variation. PC2 separated extraction methods based on phenolic acid composition and processing conditions. The loadings analysis revealed trans-3-caffeoylquinic acid (CQA) as the dominant contributor (+0.758 loading, 57.5% of PC2 variance), followed by gallic acid hexoside (GAH, +0.425, 18.1%), and trans-3-p-coumaroylquinic acid (pCQA, +0.336, 11.3%). These hydrophilic phenolic acids are differentially extracted by water-based methods, with CQA showing 2.2-fold higher abundance in water extracts compared to ethanol extracts (W: 23.7 mg/100 g FW vs. E-IU: 15.8 mg/100 g FW), explaining the positive PC2 scores for water methods (W: +1.8, W-IU: +1.5). In contrast, lipophilic naphthoquinones and tetralones showed near-zero PC2 loadings (JUG: +0.002; DHTH: +0.031; THNH: −0.020), confirming their selective ethanol extraction independent of method variations.

In ethanol methods, E-IU showed a negative PC2 value (PC2 = −0.4), indicating reduced CQA extraction compared to direct homogenization (E: PC2 = +1.3). This suggests that indirect ultrasound preferentially enhances recovery of lipophilic compounds while maintaining moderate phenolic acid extraction. However, PC2's smaller variance (11.5%) confirms this is secondary to the primary solvent-driven separation captured by PC1 (70.8%).

The 82.3% cumulative variance with two components confirms robust protocol separation, with minimal unexplained variance (17.7%) attributable to biological variability and analytical precision. E-IU's positioning (PC1 = +4.6, PC2 = −0.4) validates its optimality, achieving maximum yield (477 mg/100 g FW), diversity (all 13 compounds detected), and bioactive concentration (juglone 150 mg/100 g FW, DHTH 117 mg/100 g FW; Table 2). Our E-IU extract exceeds literature values: Vieira et al. (2020) reported 380 mg/100 g FW using ethanol stirring, Fernández-Agulló et al. (2013) achieved 320 mg/100 g FW with methanol maceration, demonstrating ultrasound's critical role in maximizing phenolic recovery from fresh walnut biomass.

Phenolic class distribution across extraction methods

To validate PCA findings and provide quantitative class-level analysis, phenolic compounds were grouped by chemical class and quantified across all methods (Fig. 3, Table S1). The analysis revealed distinct extraction selectivity patterns correlating directly with PC1 and PC2 loadings. Four phenolic classes, naphthoquinones, tetralones, naphthalene derivatives, and flavonoids, were detected exclusively in ethanol extracts (E, E-IU, E-DU) with zero recovery in water methods, accounting for 87–89% of ethanol extract phenolics (347–423 mg/100 g FW total). Naphthoquinones dominated E-IU extracts (167 ± 8 mg/100 g FW, 35% of total), led by juglone (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone, 150 mg/100 g FW), the primary allelopathic agent in Juglans species that inhibits mitochondrial respiration and generates reactive oxygen species through redox cycling (Mominul Islam & Widhalm, 2020). Juglone's 1.5-fold higher concentration in E-IU versus E (102 mg/100 g FW) demonstrates controlled temperature preservation, as oxidative polymerization occurs above 40 °C (Cosmulescu et al., 2010). Tetralones comprised 32% of E-IU extracts (153 ± 5 mg/100 g FW), dominated by dihydroxytetralone galloyl hexoside (DHTH, 117 mg/100 g FW), a rare bicyclic aromatic ketone exhibiting potent protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B inhibitory activity (IC₅₀ = 66.7 μM) (Ying et al., 2003). Naphthalene derivatives (86 ± 6 mg/100 g FW, 18%) and flavonoids (15 ± 0.2 mg/100 g FW, 3%) were minor components, with the latter's low abundance (<4%) contrasting sharply with most fruits (>50% flavonoids) and reflecting tissue-specific biosynthesis favoring antimicrobial naphthoquinones over antioxidant flavonoids. The complete water-extraction absence of these four classes validates PC1 loading patterns and explains the 6–20× yield difference between solvents.

Phenolic acids were the only class extracted by both solvents (E-IU: 55 ± 2 mg/100 g FW, 12% of total; W-IU: 55 ± 1 mg/100 g FW, 100% of total; p = 0.89), confirming their hydrophilic nature. However, relative abundance differed markedly: phenolic acids comprised 100% of water extracts by necessity (no other classes present) but only 12% of ethanol extracts (diluted by dominant lipophilic compounds). Within phenolic acids, solvent influenced composition: trans-3-caffeoylquinic acid predominated in water (47% of W total) while ethanol extracted the full spectrum including less polar syringic acid with reduced hydrogen bonding capacity. Method-specific effects within ethanol extractions showed E-IU consistently yielded highest lipophilic compound content: naphthoquinones +50% versus E, naphthalene derivatives +32%, flavonoids +25%, while phenolic acids remained stable (55–58 mg/100 g FW across E, E-IU, E-DU). This selectivity aligns with PC2 separation, with the pronounced naphthoquinone enhancement particularly significant given juglone's antimicrobial activity (MIC 15–60 μg/mL against S. aureus and E. coli) (Fernández-Agulló et al., 2013) and natural dye applications (Mirjalili & Karimi, 2013).

Our findings contrast with previous studies in two key aspects. First, juglone dominance (31% of E-IU extract) versus Vieira et al. (2020) reporting zero juglone in Portuguese walnut husk extracted with 80% ethanol reflects: (1) processing state, fresh husk (harvested at 50% dehiscence, frozen at −20 °C, extracted within 2 weeks) versus air-dried husk (40 °C, 48 h) where drying causes ∼80% juglone loss through volatilization and oxidative polymerization (Cosmulescu et al., 2010); and (2) varietal differences, Nerpio (Juglans regia cv. Gran Jefe) exhibits high naphthoquinone biosynthesis potentially driven by anthracnose disease pressure (40–60% prevalence in Spanish orchards), with our observed level (150 mg/100 g FW) positioning Nerpio among the highest naphthoquinone-producing cultivars documented (Medic et al., 2021). Second, our near-parity tetralone-to-naphthoquinone ratio (0.91) versus Vieira's extreme disparity (tetralones 51%, naphthoquinones 0%) indicates active oxidative ring aromatization at harvest maturity in Nerpio mature husks. Despite quantitative differences, qualitative extraction selectivity patterns are remarkably consistent: ethanol outperforms water 6–20× across studies (our 8.7×; Vieira et al. (2020): 6.9×; Fernández-Agulló et al. (2013): 12.3×), and ultrasound enhances yields 8–30% (our 23%; Tabaraki & Rastgoo, 2014: 28%), validating solvent-based and ultrasound-assisted optimization strategies independent of cultivar or geography. These compositional differences underscore that extraction optimization for WGH cannot rely solely on solvent selection, as previously emphasized. Tissue processing state (fresh frozen vs. dried) and ultrasound mode (indirect vs. direct) emerge from our systematic comparison as equally critical factors, particularly for preserving the naphthoquinone fraction that defines the biological activity of the extract. Based on comprehensive evaluation, E-IU achieved the highest yield (477 ± 24 mg/100 g FW), maximum chemical diversity (all 13 phenolic compounds detected), greatest bioactive compound concentration (320 mg/100 g FW combined naphthoquinones + tetralones, 67% of total), and excellent reproducibility (5.0% RSD, data not shown). Accordingly, the E-IU protocol (80% ethanol, indirect ultrasound bath 40 kHz, 180 W, 10 min, room temperature, 10:1 v/w) was selected for subsequent bioactivity screening (3.3, 3.5). This extract provides a chemically defined material with a dominant naphthoquinone-tetralone signature (juglone 31%, DHTH 24%, THNH 18%) enabling structure-activity relationship studies in C. elegans models. From an industrial perspective, E-IU offers food-grade solvent compatibility (80% ethanol is GRAS), energy efficiency (ambient temperature reduces consumption ∼80% versus thermal extraction (Chemat et al., 2017), scalability (ultrasonic bath technology commercially available at 100–1000 L capacity), and circular economy alignment, converting low-value agricultural waste (husks comprise 45–60% of fruit weight) into high-value bioactive extracts potentially recovering ∼640 metric tons per year of total phenolics from Spanish walnut production alone.

Phytochemical characterization of E-IU extract phenolic constituents

Based on the optimized E-IU protocol (Section 3.1), comprehensive structural elucidation of the thirteen phenolic constituents was performed using HPLC-DAD-MS (Table 1, Fig. 4). Compounds were identified through retention time alignment, UV–Vis absorption spectra (200–600 nm), and ESI-MS/MS fragmentation patterns in negative ionization mode, with structural assignments validated against authenticated standards or literature-reported MS2 fragmentation. The compound library comprised five phenolic acids, two tetralones, one naphthalene derivative, three naphthoquinones, and two flavonoids (Table 1), representing the complete phenolic fingerprint of fresh Nerpio walnut green husk.

Chemical structures of phenolic constituents in WGH extracts were elucidated based on HPLC-MS analysis and comparison with authenticated reference standards. Structural assignments may contain uncertainties regarding specific linkage positions and stereochemical configurations. Compound identifiers, nomenclature, and mass spectral data are summarized in Table 1.

The dominant phenolic classes, tetralones and naphthoquinones collectively accounting for 67% of E-IU extract (320 mg/100 g FW, Table 2), exhibited characteristic structural features enabling unambiguous identification.

Dihydroxytetralone galloyl hexoside (compound 7, DHTH: 116.7 mg/100 g FW, 24% of total) displayed [M-H]− at m/z 491. Diagnostic MS2 fragments were observed at m/z 331 (loss of hexose, 162 Da), m/z 271 (subsequent loss of two water molecules), m/z 211 (galloyl moiety cleavage), and m/z 169 (gallic acid residue). This fragmentation pattern is consistent with a dihydroxylated tetralone core esterified with galloyl-hexose, as reported in Juglans mandshurica epicarp (Huo et al., 2018). Trihydroxytetralone galloyl hexoside (compound 9, THTH: 36.3 mg/100 g FW, 8% of total) exhibited [M-H]− at m/z 507 with identical fragmentation pattern (m/z 331, 271, 211, 169), indicating an additional hydroxyl group versus DHTH and enhanced antioxidant capacity through increased electron-donating capability. These rare tetralones, largely restricted to Juglans species, represent valuable bioactive compounds: 4-hydroxy-α-tetralone derivatives demonstrate potent protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) inhibitory activity (IC₅₀ = 66.7 μM) (Ying et al., 2003), an activity consistent with the metabolic modulation observed in our daf-2(e1370) assays (Section 3.3.4). Juglone (compound 13, 5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone: 150.0 mg/100 g FW, 31% of total) was unequivocally identified by retention time (42.50 min), UV maxima (349, 422 nm characteristic of naphthoquinones), and [M-H]− at m/z 173 with MS2 fragment m/z 155 (loss of H₂O) matching authenticated standards. Its biosynthetic precursors, hydrojuglone glucoside isomers 1 and 2 (compounds 6 and 10: 9.5 and 7.8 mg/100 g FW respectively, eluting at 22.16 and 27.37 min), exhibited identical [M-H]− at m/z 337 with diagnostic fragment m/z 175 corresponding to hydrojuglone aglycone following β-glucosidase-mediated hexose cleavage during tissue homogenization (Medic et al., 2022). The coexistence of juglone alongside substantial hydrojuglone glucosides (17.3 mg/100 g FW combined) reflects active in planta oxidative aromatization via cytochrome P450 enzymes at harvest maturity (50% dehiscence, September), contrasting with Vieira et al. (2020) reporting zero juglone in dried Portuguese husk where thermal processing (40 °C, 48 h) triggers ∼80% juglone loss through volatilization (sublimation at 133 °C) and oxidative polymerization (Cosmulescu et al., 2010). The naphthalene derivative, trihydroxynaphthalene galloyl hexoside (compound 12, THNH: 85.9 mg/100 g FW, 18% of total), displayed [M-H]− at m/z 489 with sequential fragmentation yielding m/z 331 (hexose loss), 313, 301, 271, 211, and 169, consistent with a fully aromatic naphthalene core (two fused benzene rings versus the partially saturated tetralone) esterified with galloyl-hexose, likely representing a biosynthetic intermediate in the tetralone → naphthoquinone oxidation pathway (Wang, Fu, et al., 2017).

The five phenolic acids, comprising 12% of the E-IU extract (55.3 mg/100 g FW) but 100% of the water extracts, due to hydrophilic carboxyl/hydroxyl groups that enable extensive hydrogen bonding, were readily identified using authenticated standards or chlorogenic acid family fragmentation patterns. Gallic acid (compound 2: 11.2 mg/100 g FW) and syringic acid (compound 5: 16.1 mg/100 g FW) were unequivocally identified by retention time and MS/MS matching with commercial standards. Gallic acid hexoside (compound 1: 2.5 mg/100 g FW) showed [M-H]− at m/z 331 with prominent neutral loss of 162 Da yielding m/z 169 (gallic acid aglycone, [C₇H₅O₅]−). trans-3-Caffeoylquinic acid (compound 3, CQA: 15.8 mg/100 g FW) was characterized by [M-H]− at m/z 353. Diagnostic MS2 fragmentation showed a predominant base peak at m/z 191 (quinic acid moiety from ester cleavage) and secondary fragment at m/z 179 (caffeic acid residue), with intensity ratio m/z 191 > 179 and UV maximum at 326 nm confirming the trans-3-isomer position. Trans-3-p-coumaroylquinic acid (compound 4: 10.3 mg/100 g FW) exhibited [M-H]− at m/z 337 with base peak m/z 163 ([C₉H₇O₃]−, p-coumaric acid moiety) and m/z 191 (quinic acid core), validated by UV absorption at 310 nm diagnostic of p-coumaroylquinic isomers (Vieira et al., 2020). The predominance of CQA in water extracts (33–47% of total versus 3% in ethanol) reflects its high polarity and extensive use as a natural antioxidant marker in functional foods, while syringic acid's presence exclusively in ethanol extracts (16.1 mg/100 g FW in E-IU, 5.5 mg/100 g FW in W-IU) demonstrates how methoxy group substitution reduces hydrogen bonding capacity and increases lipophilic character, validating the PC1 polarity-driven separation observed in PCA (Section 3.1.2).

The two flavonoids, minor components at 3% of E-IU extract (14.9 mg/100 g FW combined), exhibited ultrasound-dependent extraction selectivity absent in standard ethanol homogenization. Myricetin rhamnoside (compound 8: 2.1 mg/100 g FW in E-IU, absent in E, 5.4 mg/100 g FW in E-DU) displayed [M-H]− at m/z 463 with diagnostic neutral loss of 146 Da (C₆H₁₀O₄, deoxyhexose/rhamnose) yielding product ion m/z 317 (myricetin aglycone), confirmed by UV maxima at 262 and 348 nm characteristic of flavonol glycosides with β-rhamnosidic linkage. Taxifolin pentoside (compound 11, dihydroquercetin glycoside: 12.8 mg/100 g FW) exhibited [M-H]− at m/z 435 with neutral loss of 132 Da (C₅H₈O₄, pentose unit) yielding m/z 303 (taxifolin aglycone), supported by UV maximum at 289 nm typical of flavanonol derivatives reported in J. regia byproducts (Zhao et al., 2017). The dramatic contrast between walnut husk's low flavonoid content (<4% versus >50% in most fruits) and high naphthoquinone/tetralone content (67%) reflects tissue-specific biosynthesis prioritizing antimicrobial/allelopathic defense compounds (juglone MIC 15–60 μg/mL against S. aureus and E. coli) (Fernández-Agulló et al., 2013) and PTP1B-inhibiting tetralones over antioxidant flavonoids typical of photosynthetic tissues, aligning with the husk's primary ecological function as a protective barrier rather than metabolic organ. This phytochemical signature positions E-IU extract as a naphthoquinone-tetralone-rich bioactive ingredient (juglone 31%, DHTH 24%, THNH 18%; Table 2) with distinct functionality from conventional fruit-derived polyphenolic extracts dominated by anthocyanins and flavonoids. The unique composition enables potential food applications: antimicrobial activity leveraging juglone's redox-active properties has been reported (Mominul Islam & Widhalm, 2020), while independent studies have demonstrated WGH extract efficacy in food matrices, including antioxidant and antimicrobial enhancement in condiments (Dehghani et al., 2019), and oxidative stability improvement in mayonnaise emulsions (Soto-Madrid et al., 2020). Beyond food applications, the naphthoquinone content offers natural dye potential (Mirjalili & Karimi, 2013), exemplifying circular economy valorization of agricultural waste streams.

Bioactivity screening

C. elegans bioactivity was assessed using whole extract at two concentrations (1.7 and 17 mg/mL, corresponding to 8 and 80 μg/mL total phenolics) and a purified free phenolic fraction at equivalent phenolic content (8 μg/mL), to evaluate concentration-dependent responses and to determine whether phenolic compounds are the primary contributors to the observed bioactivities.

Concentration-response characterization and safe use range determination

To establish concentration ranges appropriate for bioactivity screening, synchronized young adult C. elegans (N2 strain) were exposed to WGH extract at concentrations of 1.7, 17, 85, and 170 mg/mL for 24 h, corresponding to total phenolic concentrations of 8, 80, 400, and 800 μg/mL (ppm), respectively (Table S2). Extract concentrations of 1.7 and 17 mg/mL (8 and 80 ppm total phenolics) exhibited no adverse effects relative to untreated controls (viability ≥95%, Fig. 5), establishing these as physiologically compatible concentrations for bioactivity screening. It should be noted that these concentrations refer to crude extract weight (1.7 and 17 mg/mL), within which the active phenolic content represents only 8 and 80 μg/mL (ppm), respectively. In contrast, concentrations of 85 and 170 mg/mL (400 and 800 ppm) reduced viability by 15% and 33%, respectively (p < 0.0001). The concentration-dependent response pattern is consistent with established bioactivity curves for phenolic compounds in biological systems. Based on these results, all subsequent bioactivity assays employed 1.7 and 17 mg/mL extract concentrations (8 and 80 μg/mL total phenolics), representing low and moderate dosing levels that maintain physiological compatibility. The free phenolic fraction was tested at equivalent phenolic content (8 μg/mL) to enable comparison between the whole extract and the free phenolic fraction.

Stress-protective properties of walnut green husk extracts

Cellular stress resistance provides a biologically integrated measure of extract antioxidant and cytoprotective capacity, complementing chemical assays (DPPH, ABTS) by capturing cellular uptake, metabolism, and systems-level responses. WGH extracts at 1.7 and 17 mg/mL significantly enhanced thermal stress survival by 75.3% and 79.2% relative to controls (Fig. 6A, p < 0.0001), while the free phenolic fraction showed 60.5% enhancement (p < 0.001). The retention of stress-protective activity in the free phenolic fraction indicates that free (extractable) phenolic compounds are primarily responsible for thermal protection, consistent with established bioactivity patterns where free phenolics exhibit greater biological activity than bound phenolics (Acosta-Estrada et al., 2014). The modest difference between whole extract and free phenolic fraction suggests minor synergistic contributions from matrix components.

Oxidative stress resistance showed similar patterns; 1.7 mg/mL extract enhanced survival by 48.84% and 17 mg/mL by 55.05%, with free phenolic fraction providing 48.07% protection over controls (Fig. 6B, p < 0.001). The consistent protective effects across all treatments demonstrate that the free phenolic fraction constitutes the primary antioxidant component, consistent with established redox- active properties of phenolic compounds in biological stress response (Acosta-Estrada et al., 2014; Fernández-Agulló et al., 2013). These stress-protective properties confirm that WGH phenolic compounds are biologically active at the tested concentrations (8–80 μg/mL total phenolics). In the present study, WGH extract treatment improved C. elegans oxidative stress resistance by 48–55% at concentrations (8–80 μg/mL total phenolics) lower than the maximum permitted levels for synthetic antioxidants BHT and BHA in food (100–200 ppm) ((Carocho et al., 2014); Commission Regulation (EU) No 1129/2011), suggesting a favorable potency profile for the extract. Independently, food preservation studies using the same type of extract have confirmed antioxidant capacity directly in food matrices. Soto-Madrid et al. (2020) demonstrated that WGH hydroethanolic extracts improved oxidative stability and delayed lipid oxidation in mayonnaise emulsions, while Dehghani et al. (2019) showed enhanced antioxidant capacity and reduced microbial loads in ketchup formulations. These independent food matrix findings, together with the in-vivo bioactivity demonstrated in our study, provide complementary evidence supporting the biological activity of WGH phenolic compounds.

The 75–79% improvement in thermal stress resistance further demonstrates the cytoprotective capacity of the extract under elevated temperature conditions, reinforcing its characterization as a biologically active ingredient.

Proteotoxicity alleviation: dual-model assessment

Two transgenic C. elegans models expressing aggregation-prone proteins were employed to assess whether WGH extract treatment improves organism tolerance to proteotoxic stress. The β-amyloid model (GMC101) showed concentration-dependent protection: 1.7 mg/mL extract increased mobility retention by 167.50% and 17 mg/mL by 352.28% relative to controls (Fig. 7A–B, p < 0.0001), with the free phenolic fraction providing 214.20% improvement (Fig. 7C, p < 0.0001). The α-synuclein model (NL5901) demonstrated improved locomotor function with treatments increasing body bends by 45.20% (1.7 mg/mL), 42.74% (17 mg/mL), and 45.13% (free phenolic fraction) (Fig. 7D, p < 0.01). Notably, in the α-synuclein model, the lower concentration (1.7 mg/mL) produced effects comparable to the higher concentration (17 mg/mL), indicating a non-linear dose-response that contrasts with the positive concentration-dependent pattern observed in the β-amyloid model. This non-linear pattern could reflect pro-oxidant effects at higher concentrations, solubility limitations, or saturation of the biological response, and the current data cannot distinguish among these alternatives.

The consistent activity of the free phenolic fraction across structurally distinct proteins demonstrates that the extract improves tolerance to proteotoxic stress regardless of the aggregation-prone protein involved, indicating broad proteotoxicity-alleviation capacity rather than protein-specific interactions. The protection observed across two mechanistically distinct aggregation models, amyloid-β through temperature-induced misfolding and α-synuclein through age-dependent accumulation, strengthens the evidence for a general cytoprotective mechanism, likely mediated through the antioxidant and radical-scavenging properties of the phenolic compounds identified in the extract (Dhouafli et al., 2018; Jagota & Rajadas, 2012).

Specialized bioactivity profiling: metabolic modulation in insulin/IGF-1 signalling-deficient model

The C. elegans daf-2(e1370) mutant, carrying a temperature-sensitive insulin/IGF-1 receptor mutation, exhibits reduced reproductive capacity at elevated temperature, which serves as a quantifiable indicator of metabolic pathway function (Gems et al., 1998). This evolutionarily conserved pathway enables the assessment of metabolic modulation properties (Murphy & Hu, 2013). Treatment with WGH extracts significantly enhanced reproductive output: 1.7 mg/mL increased progeny production by 17.00% and 17 mg/mL by 29.44% relative to controls (Fig. 8A–B, p < 0.01 and p < 0.0001, respectively), demonstrating concentration-dependent metabolic modulation. The free phenolic fraction showed a 27.34% enhancement (Fig. 8C, p < 0.05), with efficacy comparable to the high-concentration whole extract (27.34% vs 29.44%), indicating that free phenolic compounds constitute the primary bioactive components. The concentration- dependent response (17.00% at 1.7 mg/mL vs 29.44% at 17 mg/mL) further characterizes the bioactive profile of WGH extract, adding metabolic pathway modulation to the stress-protective and proteotoxicity alleviation activities described in previous sections. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that naphthoquinones and flavonoids, both present in WGH extract, interact with the insulin/IGF-1 signalling pathway in C. elegans (Büchter et al., 2013; Wang, Zeng, et al., 2017).

Across all four bioactivity endpoints, the free phenolic fraction at equivalent phenolic content (8 μg/mL) produced effects comparable to the whole extract at 1.7 mg/mL: thermal stress resistance (60.5% vs 75.3%), oxidative stress resistance (48.07% vs 48.84%), proteotoxicity alleviation in both models (214.20% vs 167.50% for β-amyloid; 45.13% vs 45.20% for α-synuclein), and metabolic modulation (27.34% vs 17.00%). This consistency across mechanistically distinct endpoints confirms that phenolic compounds are the primary drivers of the biological activity observed in C. elegans, rather than other non-phenolic matrix components present in the whole extract. However, because all preparations derive from the same extract, individual phenolic compounds co-vary proportionally, and the current experimental design cannot distinguish which specific compound or phenolic class is responsible for the observed bioactivity. Future studies using fractions with distinct phenolic profiles, for example, from different cultivars, harvest stages, or chromatographic sub-fractionation, would be required to resolve individual compound contributions.

Importantly, these in vivo bioactivity results provide complementary biological evidence to the food matrix studies previously reported for WGH extracts in mayonnaise emulsions (Soto-Madrid et al., 2020), ketchup formulations (Dehghani et al., 2019), and cooked sausages (Salejda et al., 2016). While those studies demonstrated functional efficacy directly in food systems, the present work adds whole-organism biological characterization that extends understanding of WGH bioactivity beyond chemical assays, and could open the way toward integrating C. elegans screening into future evaluation strategies for agricultural byproduct extracts.

Conclusions

This study established walnut green husk as a technically viable source of bioactive phenolic compounds, providing optimized extraction methodology, comprehensive characterization, and a whole-organism screening approach for the biological evaluation of the resulting extract. Indirect ultrasound-assisted extraction with 80% ethanol achieved maximum phenolic recovery (477 mg/100 g fresh weight), with process parameters suitable for industrial scale-up: food-grade solvent, ambient temperature, 10-min cycles, and commercially available ultrasound technology (40 kHz bath, 100–1000 L capacity). Principal component analysis identified solvent polarity as the primary extraction selectivity factor, with ethanol recovering lipophilic naphthoquinones and tetralones (87–89% of extract) while water extracted only hydrophilic phenolic acids. HPLC-DAD-MS characterized thirteen compounds dominated by juglone (31%), dihydroxytetralone galloyl hexoside (24%), and trihydroxynaphthalene galloyl hexoside (18%), establishing a phytochemical profile distinct from conventional fruit-derived polyphenolic extracts. Caenorhabditis elegans bioactivity screening demonstrated stress-protective properties (oxidative resistance: 48–55% improvement; thermal resistance: 75–79% improvement), proteotoxicity alleviation (β-amyloid: 167–352% improvement; α-synuclein: 42–45% improvement), and metabolic modulation (17–29% enhancement). Across all four endpoints, the free phenolic fraction at equivalent phenolic content produced effects comparable to the whole extract, confirming that phenolic compounds are the primary drivers of the observed biological activity. To our knowledge, this represents the first in-vivo bioactivity evaluation of walnut green husk extract using a whole-organism model, extending assessment beyond conventional chemical antioxidant assays (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP). These in-vivo results provide a complementary line of biological evidence to the food matrix studies previously reported for WGH extracts in mayonnaise emulsions (Soto-Madrid et al., 2020), ketchup formulations (Dehghani et al., 2019), and cooked sausages (Salejda et al., 2016). While those studies demonstrated functional efficacy directly in food systems, the present work adds whole-organism biological characterization that extends understanding of WGH bioactivity and could open the way toward integrating C. elegans screening into future evaluation strategies for agricultural byproduct extracts.

The approach supports the use of C. elegans whole-organism screening as a rapid bioassay for evaluating the bioactivity of agricultural byproduct extracts. Future work should include mechanistic investigation of the specific antioxidant pathways activated by WGH phenolics in C. elegans, including enzymatic markers (SOD, CAT) and oxidative damage indicators (MDA, lipofuscin). Additionally, the inclusion of reference antioxidants (e.g., Trolox, gallic acid, or BHT) as positive controls in C. elegans bioassays would enable direct potency comparison and benchmarking against established food preservatives, which was not performed in the current study. Direct evaluation of the extract in food matrices is also needed to assess whether the biological activities observed in this whole-organism model translate to food-relevant outcomes, including sensory evaluation to determine acceptable incorporation levels of WGH phenolic preparations in specific food products. From a sustainability perspective, converting walnut husk waste (45–60% of fruit weight) into functional ingredients exemplifies circular economy valorization, with potential recovery of 48–57 metric tons of total phenolics annually from Spanish walnut production alone.
