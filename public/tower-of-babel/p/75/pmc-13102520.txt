In Vitro Modulation of Murine Tenocyte Behavior by Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy
Authors: Afton K. Limberg, Lily M. Giurleo, Victoria Ruiz, Amritha Anup, Jay C. Buckey, Frances D. Faro, Douglas W. Van Citters, Katherine R. Hixon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTendon injuries often result in prolonged healing and excessive scar tissue formation due to chronic hypoxia in this poorly perfused tissue. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT), a non‐invasive treatment currently used for chronic wounds, may improve tendon healing by relieving hypoxia. However, the effects of HBOT on tendon cells, particularly tenocytes, remain poorly understood. This study investigated the in vitro effects of HBOT on murine Achilles tenocytes cultured under basal and inflammatory conditions induced by interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β). Tenocytes were exposed to 30, 60, or 90 min of HBOT at 2.5 atmospheres absolute (ATA) and assessed for changes in in vitro wound healing using a scratch assay, mitochondrial activity, cell viability, collagen deposition, and gene expression at 24‐ and 72‐h post‐treatment. HBOT increased collagen deposition, while transiently suppressing gene expression of collagen types I and III immediately following HBOT treatment, along with reduced expression of the oxygen responsive gene HIF‐1α. Mitochondrial activity increased significantly at 24 h following longer HBOT exposure but remained unchanged with IL‐1β. Cell viability remained high across all groups, although HBOT‐treated wells visually showed slightly more dead cells. Inflammatory conditions revealed a significant reduction in wound closure in HBOT‐treated tenocytes compared to controls. These findings suggest that HBOT can modulate mitochondrial activity, extracellular matrix (ECM) production, and gene expression in tenocytes, with differential effects in the presence of inflammation. Overall, this study provides new insights into the cellular effects of HBOT on tendon biology and supports further investigation into its therapeutic potential for tendon repair.

Introduction

Despite the presence of approximately 4000 tendons throughout the human body, the biological mechanisms underlying their healing remain poorly understood. This gap in knowledge limits scientifically‐based clinical practice, and contributes to suboptimal treatment strategies for the 40%–50% of all musculoskeletal injuries involving tendons or ligaments [1, 2]. In the absence of a robust scientific basis for treatment, many patients face impaired healing outcomes. This substantial burden highlights the need for both improved therapeutic strategies and a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying biological processes that drive tendon repair.

A major challenge in tendon healing is the tissue's inherently hypovascular nature, which limits oxygen availability even under healthy conditions [3]. Following injury, local hypoxia is further exacerbated. While acute hypoxia can be beneficial by enhancing stem cell proliferation and differentiation, as well as angiogenesis through the activation of specific genetic pathways such as hypoxia inducible growth factor 1 (HIF‐1), chronic hypoxia has been associated with fibrosis, persistent inflammation, and impaired healing [4, 5, 6]. Interestingly, similar pathways activated by acute hypoxia can also be stimulated through hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT, also referred to as HBO or HBO2), offering an alternative approach to modulating these regenerative responses [7, 8].

HBOT utilizes a pressurized chamber—above atmospheric pressure (e.g., greater than 1 atmosphere absolute [ATA])—to deliver 100% oxygen to the lungs. This elevated oxygen pressure significantly increases the partial pressure of oxygen in the blood, enhancing oxygen diffusion into the tissues. The hypoxia relieving effect of HBOT leads to its use in situations where acute hypoxia is a factor such as air embolism, carbon monoxide poisoning, crush injuries, and compromised flaps. Repeated administration of HBOT, where a patient receives a daily pulse of a high oxygen level, has beneficial effects on tissues where chronic hypoxia is a problem. Daily treatments are used for conditions such as radiation injury, diabetic foot ulcers, and some other wounds where chronic hypoxia and inflammation are important contributors to poor outcomes (e.g., pyoderma gangrenosum). HBOT is currently FDA‐approved for a range of clinical indications including decompression sickness, air or gas embolisms, severe skin or bone infections, radiation‐induced injury, and chronic non‐healing skin wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers [9].

Notably, very few studies have investigated the effect of HBOT on tendon biology and healing, where existing research on HBOT in the tendon and ligament fields has largely focused on in vivo animal models [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. While some in vitro studies have examined the response of other cell types (e.g., endothelial cells [16], chondrocytes [17], and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [18]) to HBOT, to the authors' knowledge, no in vitro studies to date have evaluated its impact on tenocytes. In those studies, both endothelial cells and MSCs exhibited significantly enhanced cellular response to inflammatory stimuli when treated with HBOT. The endothelial cell study showed reduced expression of pro‐inflammatory adhesion molecules and increased production of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, a key regulator of vascular health, after just 1 h of HBOT [16]. Further, the MSCs exhibited enhanced mineral deposition and upregulation of osteogenic markers [18]. These findings provide a strong rationale and motivation for the present study, which aims to investigate the effects of HBOT on tenocytes under both basal and inflammatory conditions.

One key inflammatory mediator relevant to tendon healing is interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β). IL‐1β is an early pro‐inflammatory cytokine that is elevated after tendon injury and plays a critical role in initiating the inflammatory phase of healing [19]. Specifically, it promotes the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), prostaglandins, and other catabolic factors that contribute to extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation [20, 21]. While this initial inflammatory response is necessary for tissue remodeling, prolonged or excessive IL‐1β signaling can impair healing by promoting fibrosis, matrix breakdown, and cellular apoptosis [22]. Understanding how tenocytes respond to IL‐1β—and whether therapies like HBOT can modulate this response—could provide valuable insights into therapeutic interventions aimed at optimizing tendon regeneration.

Taken together, these findings provide the rationale for the present study, which investigates the effects of HBOT on murine Achilles tenocytes in vitro under both basal conditions and IL‐1β‐induced inflammation. By exploring changes in cell viability, mitochondrial activity, collagen deposition, and matrix‐related gene expression, this study aims to elucidate whether HBOT can beneficially influence tenocyte responses in a manner that promotes tendon repair. We hypothesized that under basal conditions, HBOT would reduce cell proliferation, increase mitochondrial activity, increase collagen deposition, and upregulate ECM‐related genes, reflecting a more metabolically active and matrix‐productive phenotype. Under IL‐1β‐induced inflammatory conditions, we further hypothesized that HBOT would attenuate the inflammatory response, potentially mitigating the catabolic effects of IL‐1β on tenocytes.

Methods

Tenocyte Extraction

Achilles tendons were harvested from both the left and right hindlimbs of healthy adult C57BL/6 mice (12–16 weeks old, both sexes; Jackson Laboratory). All studies were exempt from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Dartmouth College in accordance with the Animal Welfare Act and PHS Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Tendons were harvested immediately after sacrifice by making a skin incision slightly lateral to the Achilles tendon, exposing the tendon, isolating it by passing a pair of sterile dissecting scissors underneath, cutting at the proximal end (myotendinous junction), and then cutting at the distal end (enthesis) [23]. A minimum of four tendons were required to proceed with the digestion protocol. Dissected tendons were immediately placed in a solution of Alpha MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA) and 2 mg/mL collagenase type 1 (Worthington Biomedical Corporation; Lakewood, NJ). The tendon‐containing solution was left in a 37°C water bath for 1 h and vortexed every ~10 min. After this incubation period, the tendon‐containing solution was filtered using a 50 μM filter to remove any remaining tissue pieces. The solution was then spun down at 125 G for 7 min to obtain a cell pellet and resuspended in a media solution of alpha MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA), 1% pen strep (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA), 1% glutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA), and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA). The cell solution was placed in a tissue culture flask and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were cultured according to standard technique and utilized for experiments at passages 4–7, based on preliminary data and published studies [24, 25]. For all experiments, cells were seeded on 12‐well treated plates at a density of 10,000 cells/cm2 and were cultured for 48 h prior to any further treatments or testing.

Scratch Assay

Cell confluency was first confirmed under bright field microscopy after 48 h of culture. At this time, half of the wells on each 12‐well plate were subjected to a scratch assay based on the method of Liang et al. to replicate in vitro wound healing [26]. Briefly, a linear scratch was made through the center of each well using a 200 µL pipette tip, mechanically disrupting the adherent tenocytes along the scratch line. All wells, including controls, were then washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and filled with fresh media to remove any detached or disrupted cells. Control and scratched wells were then immediately imaged at time point zero, and then subsequently imaged every 6 h until 24 h from the time of scratch. Measurements of the scratch were taken at the top, center, and bottom of each image using Image J (NIH; Bethesda, Maryland). Averages were calculated from these three measurements at each timepoint and compared between treatment groups.

Hyperbaric Oxygen Treatment

Immediately following scratch assay imaging at timepoint zero, half of the well plates (with both scratched and control wells) were placed into a hyperbaric oxygen chamber (Reimers Systems Inc; Highland Heights, OH) without lids. The other half of the well plates (both scratched and control wells) remained on a lab bench for the allotted time to serve as controls. The chamber door was sealed and the pressure slowly increased with 100% O2 to reach the desired pressure (either 1.5 ATA [1140 mmHg O2] or 2.5 ATA [1900 mmHg 02]). The temperature and pressure inside the chamber were monitored using sensors, and the well plates were visually monitored through small chamber windows. Once the chamber reached the desired pressure, a timer was started for either 30‐, 60‐, or 90‐min. Following completion of the time at pressure, the chamber was slowly depressurized, and the well plates were removed. All well plates were then returned to the incubator until further analysis.

To better approximate clinically relevant treatment paradigms, HBOT was administered on two sequential days in an additional experiment, with all assays performed at 24‐h after the final exposure.

Mitochondrial Activity

Mitochondrial activity was measured 24 h post‐HBOT using a 3‐(4,5‐Dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)−5‐(3‐carboxymethoxyphenyl)−2‐(4‐sulfophenyl)−2H‐tetra‐zolium (MTS) assay (Promega; Madison, WI). The manufacturer's protocol was followed, and the assay was read at 490 nm using a SpectraMAX spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices; San Jose, CA). Background readings were subtracted from each readout, and the treatment groups were normalized to their control.

Live/Dead Staining

Live and dead cells were visually assessed using fluorescence dyes at 24 h post HBOT. Briefly, the CytoCalcein Green solution was utilized to label live cells green, and the Propidium Iodide was utilized to stain the dead cells red, according to the manufacturer's protocol (AAT Bioquest; Pleasanton, CA). After incubating the stain for 30 min, the plates were read on a SpectraMAX spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices; San Jose, CA) at two different wavelengths specified by the manufacturer to correlate to the live and dead stains. Following this, images were taken of each well at 4X and 10X zoom on a microscope (EVOS M5000, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA). Three images of each well were then randomly selected and qualitatively assessed for cell death.

Picrosirius Red Staining

Collagen deposition was assessed and quantified using picrosirius red staining at 24 h post‐HBOT. Briefly, the cells were fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin for 15 min, rinsed twice with PBS, and then stained using a picrosirius red staining kit (Polysciences Inc.; Warrington, PA). Following staining, the wells were left to dry for at least 24 h and then wrapped in aluminum foil and stored in a cool dry place. Three brightfield images per well were then taken and quantified using ImageJ. All treatment groups were normalized to control groups for each experiment.

Gene Expression

At defined timepoints after HBOT, immediately post‐treatment (0 h), 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h cells were lysed using Trizol and RNA was extracted from both HBOT‐treated and control cells using the Direct‐zol RNA Miniprep kits (Zymo Research; Orange, CA). RNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific; Waltham, MA), and cDNA was synthesized using a Promega synthesis kit (Promega; Madison, WI). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) was run for genes of interest including: i) housekeeping gene: Glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), ii) tendon‐specific genes: scleraxis (SCX), and tenomodulin (TNMD), iii) collagen genes: collagen type 1 (COL1A1), and collagen type 3 (COL3A1), iv) vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), vii) interleukin 1 beta (IL‐1‐β) vii) an oxygen‐responding gene: hypoxia inducible factor 1 (HIF‐1α). Gene expression was assessed using SYBR™ green universal master mix on a QuantStudio 5 machine (Applied Biosystems; Waltham, MA), and all genes of interest were normalized to GAPDH.

ROS Assay

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed using a fluorometric assay kit (Sigma‐Aldrich; St. Louis, MO) at two timepoints: immediately following treatment (0 h) and 24 h post‐treatment in both control and HBOT‐treated groups. Fluorescent images were acquired from all wells using an EVOS M5000 microscope (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using a SpectraMAX spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices; San Jose, CA).

IL‐1β Treatment

All experiments (scratch assay, HBOT, mitochondrial activity, live/dead staining, and picrosirius red staining) were repeated with the addition of mouse recombinant IL‐1β (STEMCELL Technologies; Vancouver, Canada). The dose of IL‐1β was chosen from preliminary testing various concentrations on these cells [27, 28]. It was determined that 5 ng/mL was the optimal dose that did not lead to changes in metabolic activity in the cells (Supplemental Figure S1).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyzes and graphical figures were created using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.0 (GraphPad Software; San Diego, CA). For all studies, technical triplicates were run for all analyzes. For statistical analyzes of the MTS assay and quantification of the picrosirius red staining, the treatment groups were normalized to the control groups (no scratch, no HBOT). Students’ unpaired t‐tests were utilized when comparing two groups, and one‐way ANOVA tests with Tukey post hoc were used when comparing multiple groups. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Screening for the Influence of Pressure and Number of Treatments

Initial studies were completed to measure the mitochondrial activity of the tenocytes using the 60‐min exposure to HBOT to determine the appropriate ATA (1.5 or 2.5) pressure. The mitochondrial activity at both 24 and 72 h showed no significant differences at a lower pressure of 1.5 ATA (p = 0.05; Figure 1A). However, at the higher pressure of 2.5 ATA, a significant difference was observed demonstrating increased mitochondrial activity at 24 h between the scratch control and no scratch HBOT groups (p = 0.034; Figure 1B). When assessing this at 72 h, no differences were appreciated. Overall, HBOT had a limited effect on mitochondrial activity of the tenocytes after 24 h. Based on these results and due to published in vivo data [10, 11, 13, 14], it was determined to move forward with the 2.5 ATA pressure.

Metabolic activity assessed using an MTS assay for two different HBOT pressures: 1.5 ATA and 2.5 ATA. Data points were collected at 24 and 72 h after HBOT treatment. (*p < 0.05).

Two sequential daily HBOT treatments were performed, and metabolic activity (MTS) and cell viability (live/dead staining) were assessed 24 h after the second exposure. HBOT‐treated cells exhibited significantly reduced metabolic activity compared to the controls. Consistent with these findings, the live/dead staining demonstrated a decreased proportion of viable (green) cells in the HBOT‐treated groups (Supplemental Figure S2). Based on these results, a single HBOT exposure was selected for subsequent in vitro studies.

Scratch Assay

The scratch assay was performed to replicate wound healing in vitro. The in vitro scratch assay at 24 h showed no significant differences in scratch closure in the wells treated with 30, 60, or 90 min of HBOT (Figure 2, Supplemental Figure S3; p = 0.05). Interestingly the 30‐min exposure showed the largest difference between the control and HBOT groups (p = 0.1088).

Percent closure of the scratch between 0 and 24 h for each of the HBOT timepoints. Representative images from the scratch assay at 0 and 24 h for 60 min of HBOT exposure. Black lines indicate where the measurements were taken on each image.

Mitochondrial Activity

An MTS assay was performed to assess mitochondrial activity in response to HBOT. Results showed significant increases in mitochondrial activity with 60 and 90‐min of HBOT. For the 90‐min treatment, these differences were significant regardless of if the scratch was present or not (Figure 3; p = 0.05).

Quantification of metabolic activity measured utilizing an MTS assay at 24 h after 30, 60, or 90‐min of HBOT. (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01).

Live/Dead Staining

Cell viability was assessed using live and dead staining. Quantitively, the percentage of live cells decreased with 30, 60, and 90 min of HBOT; however, this change was not statistically significant (p = 0.05). Live and dead staining qualitatively demonstrated increased dead (red) cells in the HBOT group compared to the control group. This was observed in both the scratch and non‐scratched wells at all exposure timepoints (Figure 4, Supplemental Figure S4).

Live and dead staining of control and 60‐min HBOT groups, and representative images of the scratched and non‐scratched wells.

Picrosirius Red Staining

Picrosirius red staining was performed to identify differences in collagen deposition. When quantified, the staining showed increased collagen deposition when comparing the HBOT and control groups at 60 min and 90 min. For the 60‐min exposure, the largest difference was noted between the control scratch group and the HBOT no scratch group (p < 0.001). While at 90 min, the scratched wells in both the control and HBOT groups showed greater pixel intensity, correlating to higher collagen deposition (Figure 5).

Representative images of picrosirius red staining for 60‐min of HBOT including the scratched and non‐scratched wells (A). Quantification of picrosirius red staining at each of the exposure timepoints (B). (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Gene Expression

Gene expression analyzes aimed to evaluate tendon‐specific genes as well as the oxygen responsive gene HIF‐1ɑ (Figure 6). Within the 60‐min exposure group, RT‐qPCR data showed a decrease in COL1A1 and COL3A1 expression in both HBOT groups, compared to control at 0 h post treatment. Similar patterns were observed for SCX and TNMD with HBOT treated groups showing consistently lower expression across all timepoints. Expression of IL‐1β was near the detection threshold and therefore it was difficult to make any meaningful interpretations of the HBOT effects. Similarly, VEGF also demonstrated decreased expression at 0 h in the HBOT groups, with no consistent trends at the later timepoints. Notably, HIF‐1α expression was reduced in the HBOT treated tenocytes throughout the entire time course. Although none of these differences reached statistical significance, the consistent directional trends provide insight into the potential mechanisms of action for HBOT.

Normalized RT‐qPCR results for the control and 60‐min HBOT groups at various timepoints after treatment. These timepoints included 0 h (immediately after HBOT/control treatment), 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h. ns = no scratch and s = scratch.

ROS Assay

Spectrophotometer analysis of the intracellular ROS assay revealed significantly increased intracellular ROS levels in the HBOT‐treated groups immediately following treatment (0 h), consistent with increased fluorescence observed in corresponding microscopy images. By 24 h post‐treatment, intracellular ROS levels in HBOT‐treated cells were only modestly elevated relative to controls (Figure 7).

Representative images of the intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay at 0 h (immediately after HBOT/control treatment) and 24 h after. Bar graphs show the quantification of the fluorescence as measured on a spectrophotometer at each timepoint (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01).

Scratch Assay With IL‐1β

In vitro wound healing was again assessed using a scratch assay, but this time with the addition of IL‐1β. When assessing the scratch assay with the addition of IL‐1β, a significant decrease in scratch closure was noted in the HBOT group compared to the control (p = 0.0323; Figure 8).

Representative scratch assay images with the addition of IL‐1β and quantification of the scratch closure from 0 to 24 h. Black lines indicate where the measurements were taken on each image. (*p < 0.05).

Mitochondrial Activity With IL‐1β

Mitochondrial activity was assessed to validate cellular viability and proliferation with the addition of IL‐1β. The 60‐min HBOT groups with IL‐1β trended to have lower mitochondrial activity compared to the control groups; however, this did not reach statistical significance (Figure 9A; p = 0.05).

Metabolic activity measured using an MTS assay of the tenocytes with the addition of IL‐1β, both with and without HBOT (A). Quantification of the live and dead staining of the tenocytes with IL‐1β, both with and without HBOT (B).

Live/Dead Staining With IL‐1β

Similarly, live and dead staining was again performed in the presence of IL‐1β to assess cell death. No significant differences were observed between the control and HBOT groups when quantitively assessing this staining (Figure 9B; p = 0.05).

Picrosirius Red Staining With IL‐1β

Likewise, the effects of IL‐1β on collagen deposition was evaluated using picrosirius red staining. When quantified, no differences were noted between any of the groups with the addition of IL‐1β (Figure 10; p = 0.05).

Quantification of picrosirius red staining of tenocytes with the addition of IL‐1β and representative images.

Discussion

This study sought to investigate the effects of HBOT on tenocytes isolated from murine Achilles tendons under basal and inflammatory conditions. The findings demonstrated increased metabolic activity, collagen deposition, and changes in gene expression with HBOT, accompanied by minimal cell death across treatment groups. Further, the induction of an inflammatory state to the cultures, with the addition of IL‐1β, resulted in decreased scratch closure in the in vitro wound healing assay with HBOT. The increase in ROS and trend toward increased cell death, suggested that HBOT at these doses may be toxic to the tenocytes.

The in vitro scratch assay yielded unanticipated results with no differences in the closure between the 30, 60, or 90‐min HBOT group and the control. Previous HBOT studies suggest that under normal conditions, this assay may have limited sensitivity, particularly at early time points [16]. However, with the addition of IL‐1β a significant decrease in scratch closure was observed with the addition of HBOT. This may be due to increased stress on the cells with the combination of the inflammatory marker and HBOT at that early timepoint. More experiments should be completed to understand this further, including the time course of inflammation and HBOT.

One of the most intriguing results was the enhanced mitochondrial activity seen at 24 h in the 60 and 90‐min treatment groups. HBOT has been shown to increase ATP synthesis by elevating the amount of oxygen available for cellular respiration, which likely explains the observed rise in metabolic activity [29, 30]. Additionally, differences between 1.5 ATA and 2.5 ATA exposures, as well as between single and the two sequential daily HBOT exposures, were observed. These results validated the use of a single HBOT exposure at 2.5 ATA for subsequent in vitro experiments, aligning with previous in vivo work demonstrating superior ligament healing at 2.5 ATA compared to 1.5 ATA [12]. Importantly, repeated HBOT exposure was directly evaluated in this study and associated with increased cytotoxicity, underscoring a key limitation of applying clinically relevant HBOT regimens to simplified in vitro systems. In the intact animal, tenocytes likely experience low partial pressures of oxygen, approximately 10–20 mmHg. HBOT administered at 2.5 ATA will increase oxygen tension in tissue. Direct measurements of oxygen in glioblastomas in humans when HBOT is given show an increase in oxygen tension from ~10 mmHg to ~70 mmHg [31]. While derived from a different tissue type, this suggests that although the increase is substantial, these oxygen levels are considerably lower than those experienced by tenocytes in the chamber (1140–1900 mmHg).

Despite this, few studies have systematically examined the influence of pressure on in vitro cell activity. This is likely due to the lack of studies on HBOT in general. It is also interesting to note that no differences were observed in the mitochondrial activity with the addition of IL‐1β when comparing across groups that received HBOT and those that did not. However, the groups that received HBOT did trend to have lower activity compared to the controls when the inflammatory marker was added.

The results of the live and dead staining were largely expected and highlighted the possible toxic effects of oxygen at high levels. Exposing cells to such high levels of oxygen, combined withtemperature and pressure changes compared to their ‘native’ environment inside of the incubator, most likely contributed to the increased cell death observed in the HBOT exposure groups, especially at the 60 and 90‐min exposure times, though this did not reach statistical significance. This was also true with the addition of IL‐1β.

Picrosirius red staining showed significant differences in collagen deposition at 24 h between 60‐ and 90‐min HBOT and control groups. When quantified, more collagen deposition was noted in the HBOT groups, as depicted by increased red staining and increased pixel intensity (Figure 5). This was surprising given the short timeframe, since changes in matrix synthesis can take longer to manifest at the protein level. Other studies have noted an increase in collagen deposition specifically for human dermal fibroblasts;, however, this was after several HBOT sessions [32]. Future studies should include longer collection time points to assess if these responses in ECM production are prolonged. No differences were noted in the picrosirius red staining with the addition of IL‐1β.

Similarly, gene expression analyzes revealed time‐dependent transcriptional responses following HBOT exposure. At 24 h, modest differences were observed in the 60‐min exposure group; however, more pronounced changes occurred earlier in the time course. HBOT exposure led to decreased expression of COL1A1 and COL3A1 in both the scratched and non‐scratched groups immediately following treatment (0 h). We noted a peak in collagen gene expression at 12 h before declining by 24 h, consistent with the increased collagen deposition observed by picrosirius red staining at 24 h. The changes in HIF‐1α expression were unexpected. While prior studies have hypothesized increased HIF signaling following HBOT exposure [7, 33, 34], our results indicate the opposite, showing reduced HIF‐1α expression throughout the 24 h gene expression time course. Similarly, HBOT exposure trended toward decreased expression of tendon‐specific genes (SCX and TNMD) as well as VEGF, particularly immediately following treatment. Minimal transcriptional changes were observed in response to IL‐1β, likely reflecting low baseline expression of inflammatory markers in this model. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HBOT‐induced transcriptional responses are highly dynamic and time dependent, and that single late time points may fail to capture key molecular events. Future studies would benefit from increased temporal resolution to better track these transient changes.

This study has limitations. Applying HBOT to tenocytes in vitro does not fully recapitulate the in vivo tendon environment, which includes vascular, neural, and ECM interactions absent from this simplified system. Additionally, the oxygen concentrations experienced by the cultured cells during HBOT exposure are likely much higher than physiological levels, potentially contributing to increased ROS generation and cytotoxic stress. In vivo, oxygen must diffuse through multiple tissue layers before reaching the tissue of interest (tendon), resulting in substantially lower dissolved oxygen levels. While intracellular ROS levels were assessed in this study and demonstrated increased oxidative stress following HBOT exposure, future studies should incorporate broader oxidative stress markers and antioxidant responses. Another key limitation is that this study employed a single HBOT treatment, whereas most clinical and animal protocols involve repeated treatments [12, 14, 35]. Although repeated HBOT exposure was directly evaluated here and found to be cytotoxic in vitro, this highlights an inherent limitation of simplified cell culture systems in modeling clinical regimens. Accordingly, a single exposure paradigm was selected to minimize cellular stress and isolate the acute effects of HBOT on tenocytes. Furthermore, incorporation of three‐dimensional cell culture systems, ex vivo tendon culture models, and in vivo studies will be necessary to evaluate the potential systemic effects and translational potential of HBOT for tendon healing. Incorporation of these complementary models will be critical for bridging the gap between cellular responses and clinically meaningful tendon healing outcomes. In summary, this study highlights the impact of HBOT on murine Achilles tenocytes in vitro. While HBOT had minimal influence on cell viability, it significantly increased metabolic activity, and altered ECM deposition and gene expression. These findings support continued investigation into the role of HBOT in tendon biology through both in vitro and in vivo models.
