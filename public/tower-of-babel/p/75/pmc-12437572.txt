Influence of bladder outlet obstruction on age-related changes in male bladder contractility
Authors: Jin Bong Choi, Kang Jun Cho, Joon Chul Kim, Young Kyu Han, Ju Hun Ahn, Seong Joo Yang, Jun Sung Koh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChanges in bladder contractility due to aging are among the important causes of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in elderly males. The purpose of this study was to observe changes in bladder contractility due to aging in male patients taking medications commonly used to treat LUTS in clinical practice.Materials and MethodsMale patients aged over 40 years who underwent urodynamic studies for LUTS from January 2012 to December 2024 were included. The bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) index was used to distinguish between males with BOO and those without BOO. Changes in bladder contractility according to age in each group were analyzed using the bladder contractility index. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used to calculate the strength of the linear relationship between age and the variables.ResultsA total of 449 male patients who underwent urodynamic studies for LUTS were eligible for this study (311 in the BOO group and 138 in the BOO group). Age and the maximum flow rate were inversely related in both groups. Age and bladder contractility were significantly correlated in the non-BOO group (p=0.021) but not in the BOO group (p=0.057).ConclusionsThis study revealed that BOO might act as a confounding variable in measuring the contractility of an aged bladder. Excluding the effect of BOO, bladder contractility significantly decreases with age. Therefore, when male LUTS are treated, changes in bladder contractility due to aging vary depending on BOO.Keywords: Aging, Bladder, Bladder outlet obstruction, Lower urinary tract symptoms

INTRODUCTION

The elderly population is growing rapidly worldwide, with the rate of growth expected to continue to increase [1]. The number of people aged 65 and older is projected to more than double from 2021 to 2050 [2]. The elderly population aged 65 years or older in 2024 accounted for 19.2% of Korea’s total population. This percentage is expected to continue to increase in the future, exceeding 20% in 2025, 30% in 2036, and 40% in 2050 [3]. Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) are common in older adults. The prevalence and severity of LUTS increase with age [4]. LUTS are associated with morbidity in older adults, thus affecting their quality of life [5,6]. Therefore, LUTS could also contribute to increased social and economic costs [7].

Changes in bladder contractility due to aging are among the important causes of LUTS in elderly males [8,9,10]. Preclinical studies conducted thus far have revealed that bladder instability increases due to bladder wall fibrosis caused by aging [11,12]. However, there is controversy regarding bladder contraction. Although some studies suggest that contractility in aged animals is reduced due to detrusor dysfunction, some studies report that detrusor contractility is generally preserved [13,14,15,16,17].

Moreover, there is a lack of well-controlled clinical studies analyzing the correlation between age and bladder contractility. In particular, urodynamic testing is essential for evaluating bladder contractility. However, it is a highly invasive test. Thus, it might be difficult to obtain consent from the subjects. In addition, since elderly males often have bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) due to benign prostate hyperplasia, urethral stricture, etc., the potential confounding effect of BOO on bladder contractility should be considered.

For these reasons, realistically conducting a well-controlled longitudinal study is difficult. Therefore, in this study, changes in bladder contractility due to aging were observed in male patients taking medications commonly used to treat LUTS in real practice. Since BOO could act as a confounding factor for bladder contractility in male patients, we analyzed males with BOO and males without BOO separately.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Subjects

In this retrospective cohort study, male patients aged over 40 years who underwent urodynamic studies for LUTS from January 2012 to December 2024 at our hospital were included. The exclusion criteria were diabetes mellitus, dementia (Alzheimer’s disease or Parkinson’s disease), spinal cord injury, a history of pelvic radiation or radical pelvic surgery, or other neurological disorders. In addition, none of the patients had undergone transurethral surgery to improve LUTS. Serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA), transrectal ultrasonography, the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), and a 24-hour voiding diary were routinely performed before urodynamic study. Additional assessments included maximum flow rate and voided volume during noninstrumented uroflowmetry, post-void residual volume (PVR) using an automatic scanning device, and functional bladder capacity (FBC), the largest voided volume recorded in a 24-hour voiding diary. To investigate age-related changes in bladder contractility among male patients receiving commonly prescribed medications for the treatment of LUTS in clinical practice, we did not perform medication washout in the study participants.

2. Study design

Urodynamic studies included the filling cystometry for measuring detrusor pressure during continuous filling of the bladder at 30 mL/min. Bladder outlet obstruction index (BOOI) was used to distinguish between males with BOO and males without BOO. The BOOI is the most widely adopted method for diagnosing BOO. The BOOI was expressed by the following equation: PdetQmax-2×Qmax (PdetQmax, detrusor pressure at the maximum urine flow rate; Qmax, the maximum urine flow rate). Subjects were classified into three groups: unobstructed (BOOI ≤20), equivocal (20< BOOI ≤40), and obstructed (BOOI >40) [18]. In this study, the BOO group included only obstructed patients, and the non-BOO group included only unobstructed patients. To clearly distinguish the effect of BOO on bladder contractility, the equivocal group was excluded from this study. Changes in bladder contractility according to age in each group were analyzed using the bladder contractility index (BCI). The BCI was calculated as PdetQmax+5×Qmax. The presence of detrusor overactivity (DO) was defined as the occurrence of involuntary detrusor contraction during the filling phase [19]. A minimum sample size of 84 is required to detect a correlation coefficient of 0.3 with an alpha of 0.05 and a power of 80.0%.

3. Statistical analyses

SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute) was used for all the statistical analyses. The data are presented as the mean±standard deviation for continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables. Two-sided t-test was used to conduct between-group comparisons of continuous data. Comparative analyses of the distribution of DO were performed using the chi-square test. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used to calculate the strength of the linear relationship between age and the variables. Due to the unequal distribution of patients between the BOO and non-BOO groups (2.3:1), a multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the independent association between BOO and bladder contractility. The model also assessed the association between age and BCI while adjusting for BOO and other clinical covariates, including PSA, prostate volume, maximum flow rate, PVR, FBC, and IPSS scores. Statistical significance was considered when p-value was less than 0.05.

4. Ethical approval

This study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) [20]. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Catholic University of Korea (IRB approval number: HC25RISI0016). Since anonymized and deidentified information was used for analysis, the need for informed consent was waived by the IRB.

RESULTS

1. Comparison of clinical characteristics according to the presence of BOO

A total of 449 male patients who underwent urodynamic studies for LUTS were eligible for this study. There were 311 patients in the BOO group whose BOOI was greater than 40 and 138 patients in the BOO group whose BOOI was 20 or less. The mean age was 68.46±7.22 years for the BOO group and 69.11±8.36 years for the non-BOO group. The mean PSA level, prostate volume, and maximum flow rate were significantly greater in those with BOO. The BCI was also greater in the BOO group because the calculation process used the maximum urine flow rate (Table 1).

Table 2 shows the strength of the linear relationship between age and the variables using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r). An inverse relationship was found between age and the maximum flow rate in both groups. However, no significant correlation was observed between FBC or IPSS scores with age in either group (Fig. 1). On the other hand, the PVR tended to increase with age in the non-BOO group (p=0.017) (Fig. 2B) but not in the BOO group (p=0.598) (Fig. 2A). The distribution of DO according to age also differed between the BOO and non-BOO groups. The tendency for the presence of DO to increase with age was observed only in the non-BOO group. The presence of DO was not significantly correlated with age in the BOO group.

3. The association between age and bladder contractility was influenced by the presence of BOO

In terms of the association between age and bladder contractility, the primary outcome was the difference in the strength of the relationship between the BOO and non-BOO groups and between the BOO and PVR groups. There was a significant inverse relationship between age and bladder contractility in the non-BOO group (p=0.021) (Fig. 3B) but not in the BOO group (p=0.057) (Fig. 3A). Excluding the effect of BOO, bladder contractility was found to significantly decrease with age.

DISCUSSION

The main findings of this retrospective cohort study were as follows: (1) there was an inverse relationship between age and the maximum flow rate in both groups; (2) there was a significant correlation between age and bladder contractility in the non-BOO group but not in the BOO group; and (3) PVR and DO tended to increase with age only in the non-BOO group.

Previous preclinical studies have shown that detrusor contractility is generally preserved in aging animals. In aging rats, the bladder muscle layer thickness and voiding pressure significantly increase, but the bladder capacity, voiding volume, and PVR do not change with age [15,17]. A study examining aged rabbit bladders also revealed no change in peak pressure, although aged rabbits were unable to sustain bladder contraction long enough to empty their bladders completely [16]. In contrast, a relatively recent animal study demonstrated voiding and storage dysfunctions resembling detrusor hyperactivity with impaired contractility in aged mice [14]. Therefore, well-designed clinical studies of the aging bladder are needed, as animal studies have revealed complex and interdependent changes in bladder contractility across strains and species [11].

Unfortunately, only a few clinical studies have reported changes in bladder contractility due to aging. This finding might be due to the following reasons. First, urodynamic studies are essential for evaluating bladder contractility. However, it is a fairly invasive test, and there might be ethical issues with obtaining consent for the test in the general elderly without symptoms. Second, many elderly patients take multiple medications for various underlying diseases. It is difficult to stop all medications for such studies. Additionally, analyzing how each drug affects bladder contraction individually is very difficult. Third, even if a urodynamic study is performed with results obtained, it is difficult to distinguish whether this is due to an underlying disease that has not been investigated or is solely a result of aging.

Nevertheless, when the results of several studies conducted thus far on changes in the contractility of an aging bladder are analyzed, there is still controversy. In a urodynamic study on 324 males, there was no difference in bladder contractility at the initiation of voiding according to age, although PVR was greater in older patients of both sexes [21]. Ameda et al. [22] examined the video urodynamic study results of 193 symptomatic males without BOO. Among these patients, 31% had impaired contractility, and 11% had DO with impaired contractile function. They reported that contractility of the bladder did not correlate with age [22]. Madersbacher et al. [23] compared age-related changes in urodynamic parameters in both sexes (253 males and 183 females) and reported no age-related changes in maximum detrusor pressure in either sex.

In contrast, when urodynamic study results for a total of 1,179 patients with LUTS were analyzed, the prevalence of BOO was found to be consistent across age groups in males, although the prevalence of detrusor underactivity and DO increased with age [24]. In a study of females, Pfisterer et al. [25] reported that the strength of detrusor contraction and the maximum flow rate decrease with age regardless of the presence of DO. Homma et al. [26] investigated urodynamic parameters in a normal elderly population, including 65 males and 51 females. A urodynamic study in both sexes revealed a significant decrease in maximum detrusor pressure with age only in females (r=−0.513, p<0.01), although the maximum flow rate was found to decrease significantly with age in both sexes, with a significant increase in PVR in males [26].

The main reason why there is controversy regarding changes in bladder contractility according to age among studies is that various other factors that can affect bladder contractility are adjusted differently in each study, including whether the effects of BOO are sufficiently controlled and whether underlying diseases that might affect bladder contractility are controlled. This might also explain why PVR and DO tended to increase with age only in the non-BOO group but not in the BOO group. Therefore, this study is meaningful in that it analyzes males with BOO and those without BOO separately to control for the effect of BOO, which might act as a confounding factor.

Although a statistically significant inverse correlation between age and the BCI was observed in the non-BOO group (r=−0.196, p=0.021), the effect size was small in this study. These findings suggest that aging alone may not strongly affect bladder contractility and that other factors, such as comorbidities, detrusor remodeling, or medication use, may also contribute. Therefore, the clinical relevance of these findings should be interpreted with caution.

This study has the following limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, patients who responded to initial treatment without undergoing a urodynamic study were excluded from this study. Therefore, a future multicenter prospective study is needed to draw more reliable conclusions. Second, this was a cross-sectional study, not a longitudinal study of each individual. However, performing repeated invasive studies on individuals to determine changes in bladder contractility with aging may raise ethical concerns. Third, although this study did not conduct medication washout, it was meaningful in that it investigated changes in bladder contractility with aging in male patients taking medications commonly used to treat LUTS in actual clinical practice.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, bladder contractility significantly decreased with age. However, significant changes were observed only in the group without BOO. Therefore, when male LUTS are treated, the degree of change in bladder contractility with age can vary depending on the presence or absence of BOO. In addition, when interpreting the results of other studies on changes in bladder contractility with aging in males, whether BOO is considered a confounding factor should be considered.
