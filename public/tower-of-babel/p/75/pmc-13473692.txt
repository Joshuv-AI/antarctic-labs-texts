Structural basis of lipid-dependent allosteric gating mechanisms for PC1-PC2 ion channel
Authors: Mengying Chen, Zhifei Wang, Yan Shi, Gaoxingyu Huang, Tiago D C Morais, Akhilraj R Pillai, Yan Wang, Moushumi Afroza Mou, Dan Jing, Rongyan Fan, Fayang Zhou, Zhangsuo Liu, Qiang Su, Yong Yu, Yigong Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe polycystin complex, consisting of one polycystin-1 (PC1) and three polycystin-2 (PC2), forms a cation channel localized to the primary cilium and is critically involved in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). This study reveals an allosteric gating mechanism of the PC1-PC2 channel modulated by specific membrane lipids. In typical membrane environments, phosphatidylglycerol (PG) and phosphatidic acid (PA) bind to the channel central pore, maintaining it in a closed state. Dissociation of these lipids transitions the channel to a pre-open state. The cilia-enriched oxysterol 7β,27-dihydroxycholesterol (7β,27-DHC) stabilizes the channel in a more open but still non-conductive conformation through an allosteric mechanism. Lipid-mediated regulation is coupled to large conformational rearrangements of the TOP and voltage-sensor-like domains (VSDs) of the third PC2 subunit, which eventually leads to pore opening. This lipid-dependent modulation is also observed in a gain-of-function channel. These findings reveal a distinct gating mechanism for the asymmetric 1:3 PC1-PC2 complex.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Transient receptor potential channels

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a prevalent genetic disorder that affects at least 1 in every 1000 individuals and manifests as multiple cysts in the kidney1,2. The cysts enlarge progressively and cause renal dysfunction. ADPKD is genetically attributed to mutations in PKD1 and PKD2, which encode polycystin-1 (PC1) and polycystin-2 (PC2), respectively3,4. PC1 is a large membrane protein that contains 11 transmembrane (TM) helices5. PC2 contains six TM helices, belongs to the Transient Receptor Potential Channel family, and is also known as TRPP26. PC1 and PC2 play crucial roles in a diverse range of cellular processes, including calcium sensing and signaling7,8, regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation9–11, and sensing and response to mechanical stimuli12–16.

Previous animal studies demonstrate that aberrant regulation of primary cilia contributes to the development of kidney cysts in ADPKD17–21. PC1 and PC2, forming a Ca2+-permeable ion channel complex12,22–25, are localized to the primary cilia of kidney tubule cells26. Notably, a distinct subset of lipids, such as oxysterol 7β,27-DHC and phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate (PI4P), is enriched in the primary cilia, creating a distinct microenvironment for signal transduction27–32. Recently, 7β,27-DHC was found to potentiate the wild-type (WT) PC1-PC2 channel activity in an in vitro system; moreover, the potentiation effect induced by 7β,27-DHC can be disrupted by PI4P33, suggesting a critical role of specific lipids in regulating PC1-PC2 channel function.

Although the ion channel activity of the PC1-PC2 complex has been documented24,34, the gating mechanism for this channel remains elusive. The closed state of the PC1-PC2 channel has been reported in our previous research35, however, the lack of a well-established PC1-PC2 activator renders capturing the open state of the wild-type PC1-PC2 channel challenging. Through structure-guided mutagenesis and electrophysiology analyses, several gain-of-function (GOF) channel mutants have been identified, including PC2_F604P channel36, PC1-PC2_AA channel22 and PC1_AAA-PC2_AA channel37. Specifically, the PC1-PC2_AA mutant, harboring L677A/N681A mutations in the S6 of PC2, functions as a constitutively activated channel and thereby serves as an ideal model for capturing distinct conformational states of the PC1-PC2 channel, which may provide critical insights to understand the activation mechanism of the PC1-PC2 channel.

The cryo-EM structure of the truncated PC1-PC2 complex that we previously reported has an average resolution of 3.6 Å and reveals a 1:3 assembly between PC1 and PC235,38. In this structure, the truncated PC1 C-terminal fragment (CTF)39, which contains all 11 TM helices and the C-terminal tail, uses its last six TM helices to assemble with three PC2 subunits into a tetrameric TRP channel-like architecture. The extracellular Tetragonal Opening for the Polycystins domains (TOP40, also known as Polycystin-Mucolipin Domain (PMD)41) of PC1 and PC2 interacts with the pore domains, suggesting a potential role in pore regulation35. Despite these advances, a single channel conformation at moderate resolution limits our understanding of PC1-PC2 function.

In this work, utilizing cryo-EM methods, we report three conformations of the WT PC1-PC2 channel at improved resolutions of 2.3-3.2 Å, which enable identification of key channel pore-binding phospholipids and dynamic conformations of the third PC2 subunit, which may be involved in channel gating. Four distinct conformations of the PC1-PC2 gain-of-function (GOF) channel, which has the L677A/N681A mutation in PC2 (hereafter referred to as the AA-channel), also support this distinctive allosteric activation mechanism. In addition, we determine the structures of the PC1-PC2 channel bound to 7β,27-DHC, which reveals how the channel pore is regulated by the cilia-enriched lipid through allostery. These structures, together with the associated electrophysiological and biochemical analyses, provide mechanistic insight into lipid-dependent gating and regulation of the PC1-PC2 channel.

Results

Different conformational states of the PC1-PC2 channel

To investigate the gating and activation mechanism of the PC1-PC2 channel, we sought to capture different conformation states of the PC1-PC2 channel through cryo-EM. However, the exceptionally low expression levels of the PC1-PC2 channel pose a formidable challenge to structural investigation35. To surmount this hurdle, we systematically optimized the expression vector (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c), cell line, and transfection reagents. Our efforts led to a drastic increase in the final yield, from ~5 to ~500 μg for each liter of cell culture, expressing PC1-CTF with full-length PC2 (see Methods for details).

Following an established protocol42, we obtained different states of PC1-PC2 channel, the conformation discrepancy indicated an inactivation mechanism and allosteric regulation of PC1-PC2 channel (Fig. 1a). Three distinct conformations, designated as WT-Class I, WT-Class II, and WT-Class III were determined from WT PC1-PC2 complex. In WT-Classes I/II, the endogenous phospholipid, PG or PA, occludes the ion permeation pathway and locks the channels in an inactivated state (Fig. 1b, upper). In contrast, in WT-Class III, the phospholipids are dissociated, concurrent with the rotation of PC2III. Similarly, four conformations of constitutive activated AA-channel, exhibiting stepwise movements on TOPIII, which lead to an open state of PC1-PC2 channel in AA-Class IV (Fig. 1b, down).

Structures of the PC1-PC2 channel

The WT-Class I, WT-Class II and WT-Class III, with average resolutions of 2.3 Å, 2.8 Å, and 3.2 Å (Supplementary Figs. 2-3, Supplementary Table 1), are represented by 38%, 35%, and 27%, respectively, of the total particles. The PC1-PC2 channel exhibits a domain-swapped non-canonical TRP channel architecture (Fig. 2a–c)35,38. Despite their overall similarity, the three structures display notable differences in the conformation of TOPIII (the TOP domain of the third PC2 subunit; for consistency, we use the same nomenclature for various domains as before35) and VSDIII (the voltage-sensing domain of the third PC2 subunit). Compared to WT-Classes I and II, TOPIII in WT-Class III is rotated on the membrane plane by about 30° away from TOPII (Fig. 2a–c). Similarly, VSDIII in WT-Classes I and II exhibit modest differences; but VSDIII in WT-Class III displays a pronounced rotation compared to WT-Classes I and II (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Comparison of these structures reveals a correlation among the conformations of VSDIII, S5PC1, and S6a (the N-terminal half of S6PC1). Specifically, the movement of S5PC1 and S6a coincides with that of VSDIII, whereas the pore domains of PC2 remain largely unchanged in all three structures (Supplementary Fig. 4b,c). These concomitant motions are likely attributed to the interaction between S4III and S5PC1. Our structures suggest that the motion is transmitted from VSDIII to the pore domain of PC1 (PDPC1), which may regulate ion channel activity.

Phospholipids in the pore of the PC1-PC2 channel

The EM maps of the pore domains, resolved at local resolutions of 2.3-3.0 Å, allow identification of, to the best of our knowledge, previously unrecognized features. The PC1 pore loop, which connects S5PC1 and S6a but was unassigned due to inadequate map quality in our earlier study35, has been resolved in WT-Classes I and II (Fig. 2d, e, Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). The pore loop, supported by S6a, is positioned opposite to the PC2 pore helices (PHs). Intriguingly, unlike WT-Classes I and II, the pore loop of PC1 is disordered in WT-Class III (Fig. 2f).

Most notably, lipid molecules are present in the center of the pore domains in WT-Classes I and II (Fig. 2d, e), but not WT-Class III (Fig. 2f). Through analysis of the EM density features and the surrounding charge distribution, we have tentatively assigned the densities to phosphatidylglycerol (PG) and phosphatidic acid (PA) in WT-Class I and II, respectively. Although these phospholipids fit well into the EM densities (Fig. 2a, b), we cannot rule out other possibilities, such as an atypical lipid.

Each of the two phospholipids infiltrates the pore domain laterally, occupying the cavity that is delineated by S6a, S6b, S6III, and PH1III (Fig. 2d, e). The head of the phospholipid is buried in the central pore, whereas the tail extends outward. The phospholipid interacts extensively with residues in the central pore (Fig. 2d, e). In WT-Class I (Fig. 2d), the phosphate and a glycerol hydroxy group of PG may accept two hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) from Arg4094 of S6a, whereas the other hydroxyl group of PG likely donates a H-bond to the carbonyl group of Ile640 in PH1III. In addition, Trp4096 and Phe669 help coordinate PG through van der Waals contacts.

PA is recognized differently in WT-Class II (Fig. 2e). The phosphate group is mainly anchored by Asn674 in S6III through a H-bond, and the neck and tails of PA are stabilized by Trp4096 and Leu4095 via van der Waals interactions.

In contrast to WT-Classes I and II, there is no lipid-like density in the EM reconstruction for WT-Class III (Fig. 2c). The rotation of TOPIII and VSDIII likely triggers a conformational change in the pore domain of PC1, leading to the release of the phospholipids from the central pore (Fig. 2d–f, Supplementary Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Movies 1,2). The phospholipid-free conformation of WT-Class III sets it apart from the phospholipid-bound structures (Fig. 2d–f).

Despite these differences, the pore in all three structures has an identical feature: Arg4100 mediates two H-bonds to Asn674 in S6I and S6II, stabilizing the local conformation (Fig. 2d–f).

An ion permeation pathway

A potential ion permeation pathway is clearly present in WT-Class III as calculated using the HOLE software43 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Analysis of the pore radius along the pathway reveals four constriction sites (Supplementary Fig. 6b): Leu641/Gly642/Asp643 in PC2 and Arg4094 in PC1; Arg4100 in PC1 and Leu673/Asn674 in PC2; Ala4103 in PC1 and Leu677 in PC2; Arg4107 in PC1 and Asn681 in PC2. The radius of the narrowest constriction site is smaller than that of a dehydrated Na+ ion (0.95 Å)44 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Therefore, WT-Class III is still in a non-conductive state, despite dissociation of the phospholipid.

Potentiation of ion channel activity through phospholipid dissociation

The phospholipids in the central pores of WT-Classes I/II may inactivate the channel by blocking ion permeation. To scrutinize this hypothesis, we individually replaced the phospholipid-binding residues with aspartic acid (Fig. 2g). These mutations are expected to repel the anionic head and hydrophobic tail of the phospholipid and potentially abolish phospholipid binding in the pore.

To evaluate the functional consequence of these mutations, we examined the ion channel activity of the PC1-PC2 complex expressed in Xenopus Laevis oocytes with two-electrode voltage clamp (TEVC) (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 7). The surface biotinylation experiments indicated that every mutant PC1-PC2 complex traffics efficiently to the surface and exhibits expression levels comparable to those of the wild-type complex (Supplementary Fig. 7a). While WT channel-expressing oocytes exhibit background-level of currents comparable to un-injected oocytes in the presence or absence of extracellular Ca2+, each of four mutant channels (R4094D, L4095D, W4096D, or R4100D), affecting a phospholipid-binding residue in WT-Classes I and II, displays prominent inward and outward ion currents, suggesting a Ca²⁺-independent GOF effect (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 7b). In contrast, the mutations C4087D and W4091D in PC1, located far from the phospholipids, yield substantially smaller currents that are comparable to the WT and un-injected groups (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 7b). It is worth noting that, although the electrophysiology data strongly suggest that disrupting the lipid interaction underlies the observed gain-of-function effects, we cannot completely rule out that additional negative charge or localized conformational changes introduced by certain pore mutations may also contribute to the enhanced channel activity.

Stepwise opening of the PC1-PC2 channel

None of the channel conformations we have described so far is in the fully open state. However, elucidation of the gating mechanism requires structural information on an open conformation. To this end, we sought to resolve the structure of the AA-channel22. Following standard protocol, we obtained the EM reconstructions of four distinct conformations, denoted as AA-Class I, AA-Class II, AA-Class III, and AA-Class IV, at average resolutions of 3.3 Å, 3.7 Å, 3.4 Å, and 3.5 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8,9 and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, each of the four conformations is represented by a significant percentage of the particles.

Compared to that in AA-Class I, TOPIII in AA-Classes II, III, and IV is rotated by approximately 15°, 60°, and 120°, respectively, moving stepwise away from TOPII (Fig. 3a). These features are reminiscent of those for the WT-channel (Fig. 2a). Concurrent with the movement of TOPIII, VSDIII rotates in the same direction (Supplementary Fig. 10a). In particular, VSDIII in AA-Class IV undergoes a rotation of ~120°, bringing it into close proximity of VSDPC1.

In all four AA-channel conformations, Leu4099 of PC1 and Phe676 of PC2 block the phospholipid-binding cavity (Supplementary Fig. 10b), ensuring a phospholipid-free state in the pore domains. In addition to preventing phospholipid association, the AA mutations also affect the pore radius. At constriction site 2, the pore radius in AA-Class IV is markedly larger than that in WT-Class III. Further analysis reveals gradual enlargement in pore size from AA-Class I to IV (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 10c, Supplementary Movie 3). The narrowest radius for the ion-conducting pore in AA-Class IV is ~1.7 Å (Fig. 3b), which is sufficient for permeation of partially hydrated cations45. This analysis strongly suggests that AA-Class IV is in an ion-conducting, open state.

Our structural analysis also suggests that constriction site 2 may play a key role in defining ion permeation. To further corroborate the structural finding, we generated six AA-channel variants, each carrying a missense mutation in PC2 (N674A, N674C, or N674D) or in PC1 (R4100A, R4100C, or R4100D) that targets a key residue in constriction site 2. We examined the ion permeability of these mutant channels (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 11). The NMDG⁺ was used as reference ions here for accessing the overall enhancement of cation permeation relative to a weakly permeant large cation. Compared to the AA-channel, the calculated permeability ratios of the several test cations over the weakly permeable NMDG+ (PX/PNMDG) are markedly smaller for the variants N674A and N674C (Fig. 3c). Similarly, mutations at R4100 also produce significant, though variable, effects on ion permeability. PX/PNMDG decreases in R4100A but increases in R4100D, with overall changes less pronounced than those observed for N674A and N674C (Fig. 3d). This difference is understandable, as the complex contains only one PC1 subunit compared to three PC2 subunits. More modest yet statistically significant changes are also observed when Na⁺ is used as the reference ion (PX/PNa) for both N674 and R4100 mutants (Supplementary Fig. 11e, f). Although constriction site 2 may not be the sole determinant of ion selectivity as multiple constriction sites likely act in concert to define the channel’s selectivity profile, these results suggest that constriction site 2 plays an important role in regulating ion permeation.

The 7β,27-DHC binding cavity

To gain mechanistic insights into 7β,27-DHC action on PC1-PC2 channel activation, we resolved three conformations of the PC1-PC2 channel in the presence of 7β,27-DHC, with average resolutions of 3.1 Å, 3.1 Å, and 3.4 Å (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Table 3). In two conformations, the central pore is occluded by either PG or PA (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b). These two conformations are nearly identical to WT-Classes I and II, with root-mean-squared deviations of 0.34 Å and 0.38 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13c). The third conformation is, to the best of our knowledge, previously unrecognized, denoted as WT-Class IV. Although we used the WT-Class III map as the initial model, we were unable to identify a distinct population of WT-Class III particles in the 7β,27-DHC dataset. Compared to the other conformations, WT-Class IV displays a more pronounced rotation in TOPIII/VSDIII (Supplementary Fig. 13d).

Scrutiny of the EM map of WT-Class IV reveals a cholesterol-like density sequestered within a cavity formed by S1III/S4III, S5PC1/S6bPC1, S5III/S6III, and S4-S5III (S4-S5 linker of PC2III) (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 13e). This density, absent in the EM maps for samples prepared in the absence of 7β,27-DHC, was tentatively assigned as 7β,27-DHC. 7β,27-DHC is embedded in a cavity that is buttressed by non-polar residues, including Leu4031/Gly4028/Pro4024 in S5PC1, Leu224/Leu228 in S1III, and Phe579 in S4-S5III (Fig. 4b). Additionally, 7β,27-DHC is enclosed by side chains of Glu4025 in S5PC1 and Lys688 in S6III, which are further stabilized by Arg4109 and Trp4108 in S6bPC1 through H-bonds and cation-π interaction, respectively.

To corroborate the structural observation, we first performed electrophysiological analyses. However, despite extensive efforts, we achieved only a low success rate in detecting 7β,27-DHC-induced activation of the PC1-PC2 channel expressed HEK293 cells, which precluded further mutagenesis-based functional validation (see Discussion for details). We therefore examined the amount of 7β,27-DHC bound to the recombinant PC1-PC2 channel using mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 14). Compared to the control, incubation with 7β,27-DHC results in a 5-fold increase for the amount of 7β,27-DHC in the WT PC1-PC2 channel (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 14e, f). In contrast to the WT PC1-PC2 channel, the variant carrying the mutation G4028W or E4025R, contains a markedly reduced amount of 7β,27-DHC despite prior incubation (Fig. 4c). G4028W or E4025R in PC1 is expected to disrupt the binding by 7β,27-DHC. These biochemical data confirm the specific binding of 7β,27-DHC in the cavity as observed in the WT-Class IV structure.

Allosteric regulation of the PC1-PC2 channel by 7β,27-DHC

The WT-Class III conformation is more open compared to WT-Classes I and II (Fig. 2a-c). However, in the presence of 7β,27-DHC, the WT-Class III conformation is no longer observable, and a distinct conformation (WT-Class IV) emerges. This observation suggests that the binding of 7β,27-DHC may lead to further switching the conformation from WT-Class III to WT-Class IV. Compared to WT-Class III, TOPIII/VSDIII in WT-Class IV are rotated by ~120° toward TOPPC1/VSDPC1 (Fig. 4d, left and Supplementary Movie 4). A closer inspection shows that 7β,27-DHC binds to S5PC1, disrupting the interaction between S5PC1 and S4-S5III. This leads to a lateral translation of S4-S5III by ~4 Å, and a concomitant translation of S6III towards the rotation axis by ~2 Å (Fig. 4d, right).

7β,27-DHC binding causes pronounced dilation at constriction site 4, with the pore radius increasing from ~0.6 Å (WT-Class III) to ~2 Å (WT-Class IV) (Fig. 4e, left). Such dilation is a result of side chain rearrangement of Arg4107 in PC1 and Asn681 in PC2 upon the binding of 7β,27-DHC to the cavity (Fig. 4e, right). Despite these changes, the pore radius at constriction site 2 in the WT-Class IV is still smaller than the dehydrated sodium radius of 0.95 Å, indicating that WT-Class IV remains in a non-conductive state.

The “Latch and Wedge” model of PC1-PC2 channel in distinct cellular region

With all the structures resolved in this study, we proposed a “Latch and Wedge” model for the gating of the PC1-PC2 channel. On the ER or plasma membrane, for WT-Classes I/II, the endogenous phospholipid, PG or PA, occludes the ion permeation pathway and locks the channels in an inactivated state (Fig. 5a). In contrast, in WT-Class III, the phospholipids are dissociated, concurrent with the rotation of TOPIII/VSDIII (Fig. 5b). In particular, in the S6III, the rotation of F676 obstructs lipid binding of PG or PA. In this conformation, the phospholipid can be compared to a door latch, and TOPIII/VSDIII resembles a rotary door. The release of the phospholipid (“Latch”) may allow the rotation of TOPIII/VSDIII (“Door”), making the channel in a pre-open state. Consistent with this model, the GOF AA-channel is devoid of phospholipids (“Latch”) in the central pore; its TOPIII/VSDIII (“Door”) exhibits stepwise movements during gating (Fig. 3a and Fig. 5c).

TOPIII undergoes additional movement to open up further. The figure was created in BioRender. Chen, M. (2026) https://BioRender.com/q38r840.

On the cilia membrane, in the presence of 7β,27-DHC, TOPIII/VSDIII further rotates outward (Fig. 5d). 7β,27-DHC functions as a wedge to secure the position of TOPIII/VSDIII (“Door”), leading to an open state. In this “Latch and Wedge” model, PG or PA serves as the door latch, whereas 7β,27-DHC acts as the internal door wedge, respectively, stabilizing the channel in a more open state.

Discussion

In this study, we report ten distinct conformations of the PC1-PC2 channel at atomic resolutions, including three conformations of the WT-channel, three conformations of the WT-channel in the presence of 7β,27-DHC, and four conformations of the AA-channel. The improved resolution of the PC1-PC2 complex enables the assignment of an important but previously vague pore domain of PC1 and the identification of phospholipids that bind to this region. Moreover, the structure-guided electrophysiology analyses suggest a distinct roles of lipids on the gating for the PC1-PC2 channel, revealing the functional importance of lipid-interacted residues in the pore domain of PC1, especially for those positive charged residues (such as R4094 and R4100). Accordingly, a recent study has functionally validated GOF effects resulting from mutations of positive charged residues in the PC1 ion permeation pathway when these mutations are combined with the GOF PC2_AA mutant37. In addition, distinct conformations of WT and GOF PC1-PC2 complex further indicate an unrecognized allosteric activation mechanism modulated by membrane lipids. Built upon our prior work35,46,47, this study markedly advances our understanding on the working mechanisms of the polycystin complex channel.

WT-channel and AA-channel both exhibit the same correlation between the conformations of TOPIII/VSDIII and PDPC1, implying a potential gating mechanism whereby the movement of TOPIII/VSDIII is transduced to PDPC1. The relatively fragile interface between TOPPC1 and TOPIII35 provides more conformational dynamics for TOPIII compared to other TOP domains. Recently, the PC1-NTF was proposed to act as an intrinsic agonist for the activation of the PC1-PC2 channel through direct interaction with the TOP domains of PC248. It is possible that PC1-NTF may do so by directly modulating the conformation of TOPIII.

The flexibility of TOPIII within the PC1-PC2 channel underscores a potential activation mechanism involving subunit-specific dynamics in PKD channels, particularly within a pseudo-symmetric 1:3 heterotetrameric architecture, exemplified by the PC1-PC2 channel and the PKD1L3-TRPP3 channel. In our previous investigation of the PKD1L3-TRPP3 channel, we identified a distinct Ca²⁺ binding site specifically located in the VSDIII of PKD2L147. These observations suggest that subunit-specific conformational changes may serve as a critical initiation step for channel activation. The rational design of small proteins or peptides targeting this specific subunit could offer a strategic approach to modulate PKD channel activity.

A similar lipid-mediated inactivation mechanism has been reported for several mechanosensitive potassium channels, exemplified by TRAAK, TWIK1, and TREK149–51. Specifically, the lipid acyl chain or the hydrophilic portion of the lipid molecule occupies the channel pore, blocking ion conduction. Mechanical forces induce membrane tension-dependent conformational changes in these channels, leading to lipid dissociation from the pore. Compelling evidence suggests a crucial role in mechanosensation by the PC1-PC2 channel or other related polycystin complexes on the cilia12,15,16, despite some contention in the field52. It remains to be seen whether mechanical force directly changes the TOPIII/VSDIII conformation in the ciliary PC1-PC2 complexes, thereby facilitating the dissociation of the inactivating phospholipid (“Latch”).

Our electrophysiological data are consistent with and support the conclusions drawn from the structural analyses regarding lipid-mediated modulation of PC1-PC2 channel activity. However, although compelling, these findings remain indirect. More direct experimental approaches that enable precise control of membrane composition would further strengthen this conclusion. Such approaches may include reconstitution of the PC1-PC2 complex into artificial lipid bilayers to directly assess the effects of specific phospholipids, such as PA and PG, on channel function, enabling a more controlled and quantitative evaluation of lipid–channel interactions. We consider these directions important for future investigation and plan to pursue them in subsequent studies.

The modulation of membrane lipid on PC1-PC2 channel gating reminds us of the critical role of membrane lipids in regulating the function of homomeric PC2 channel or other TRP channels. Growing number of structural evidence for homogenous PC2 complexes, coupled with molecular dynamics (MD) simulation analyses, has demonstrated that membrane lipids can bind to PC2 and regulate the channel gating via distinct lipid-protein interaction mode, the effect is different dependent on lipid type and binding location53,54. These distinct lipid-protein interaction modes may facilitate our understanding of the functional heterogeneity of PC2 across different cell types and subcellular locations, and further underscore the potential of lipid-targeted therapeutic strategies for PC2-related disease.

The discovery of activators and inhibitors of the PC1-PC2 channel is crucial for understanding the ciliary ion channel physiology and the pathogenesis of ADPKD. In this study, we reveal the binding sites for the inactivating phospholipid PG/PA and the potentiator 7β,27-DHC. Notably, with molecular dynamics simulations, a recent study suggested that 7β,27-DHC may bind within a cytoplasmic region in homomeric PC2 channel33, which is distinct from the binding site of 7β,27-DHC identified here in PC1-PC2 channel. However, the PC1-PC2 structure we resolved remains in a non-conductive state even in the presence of 7β,27-DHC.

This observation is consistent with our low success rate in recording 7β,27-DHC-indcued activation of the PC1-PC2 channel. To test the 7β,27-DHC activation, we performed whole-cell recording in HEK293 cells overexpressing PC1 and PC2. We evaluated multiple PC1 and PC2 constructs using different expression approaches. Overall, the best results were obtained when we followed methods similar to those described in previous studies, which employed tetracycline-inducible expression for PC1 and PC233,55. Using these methods, we were able to detect 7β,27-DHC–induced currents that were comparable to those previously reported (Supplementary Fig. 15). However, a major limitation in our recordings was the low success rate of current-positive cells following 7β,27-DHC application (3 out of 54 tested). This low yield precluded reliable and reproducible functional characterization and rendered systematic mutagenesis studies infeasible.

We cannot roll out the possibility that technical factors contribute to the low success rate in our recordings. However, if the low activation efficiency is real, then together with our structural findings that the PC1-PC2 complex remains non-conductive even in the presence of 7β,27-DHC, it suggests that additional cofactors or specific cellular conditions may be required, which are not fully recapitulated in our experimental systems. These results indicate that 7β,27-DHC alone may be insufficient to fully activate the channel and that additional regulatory elements are likely necessary. The 7β,27-DHC binding pocket identified in our structure may represent a potential activation site, although further investigation will be required to establish its functional role. Nevertheless, this structural insight may be leveraged to facilitate the design of specific activators targeting the PC1-PC2 channel, ultimately contributing to the development of potential therapeutics for ADPKD.

Methods

Ethical statement

Care and oocyte isolation of Xenopus laevis frogs (wild-type) were conducted in accordance with a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at St. John’s University (Protocol #2061).

Optimization of recombinant expression for the PC1-PC2 complex

Built upon our previous study35, we optimized recombinant expression of the PC1-PC2 complex through five key modifications. First, we selected the constructs of wild-type (WT) human PC1-CTF (residues 3052-4303; UniProt ID: P98161) and full-length human PC2 (residues 1-968; UniProt ID: Q13563) for recombinant expression. The N-terminus of PC1-CTF was fused to a synthetic signal peptide (MDMRVPAQLLGLLLLWLSGARC)56 followed by a FLAG tag (MDYKDDDDK) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). This signal peptide is expected to facilitate trafficking of the PC1-PC2 complex to the plasma membrane and enhance its maturation22,57. Second, we upgraded the 25-kDa linear polyethyleneimines (PEIs) (Polysciences) with 40-kDa linear PEIs (Yeasen) to improve transfection efficiency58. Third, we added SMS293-SUPI (SinoBiological Inc) 24 hours after transfection to boost protein expression. Fourth, we replaced the initial HEK293F cells (Invitrogen) with Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to improve protein expression. Fifth and most importantly, we engineered a distinct plasmid, named pSQ after its designer’s initials, based on the vector pCAG59. As detailed below, pSQ has a number of changes over pCAG that are designed to enhance protein expression (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

In the pSQ plasmid, the CAG promoter is replaced by the CAM promoter to enhance transcription efficiency60. A synthetic 5’-UTR is incorporated to enhance translation efficiency61. A 3’-UTR, specifically the woodchuck hepatitis virus posttranscriptional regulatory element (WPRE), is integrated to reduce readthrough transcription62,63. Finally, the TAR/tat system was introduced to boost transcription64. The plasmid was constructed using the ClonExpress kit (Vazyme, C115-01/02) and verified through sequencing (Tsingke Biological Technology). The final yield of the PC1-PC2 complex using pSQ is approximately 5-fold higher compared to that using pCAG in three independent replicates (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

Purification of the PC1-PC2 complex

The cloning, expression, and purification of WT or GOF PC1-PC2 complex were performed largely as described47. Briefly, the cDNAs of human PC1-CTF and full-length PC2 were individually cloned into the pSQ vector. One liter Expi293F cells were transiently transfected with 1.5 mg of each plasmid using 40-kDa linear PEIs at a cell density of 3.0×106 cells per mL. 24 h after transfection, 20 ml SMS-293SUPI was added. The transfected cells were cultured for additional 36 h at 37 °C supplemented with 5% CO2 in a Multitron-Pro shaker (Infors, 130 r.p.m.).

Cells expressing PC1-PC2 complex were harvested via centrifugation and resuspended in the lysis buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% (w/v) glycerol, and Amresco protease inhibitor cocktails (2 μg/ml aprotinin, 2 μg/ml leupeptin, 2 μg/ml pepstatin). The cell suspension was then solubilized with 1% DDM (Anatrace), 0.25% soybean lipids (Sigma), and 0.2% CHS (Anatrace) at 4 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation at 13,000×g for 1 h, the supernatant was applied to anti-Flag M2 affinity resin (MilliporeSigma). The resin was rinsed with the wash buffer (lysis buffer supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) GDN). The target protein was eluted with the wash buffer plus 400 μg/ml FLAG peptide. The eluent was applied to Strep-Tactin resin (IBA). The proteins were eluted using the wash buffer plus 2.5 mM D-Desthiobiotin. The eluate was concentrated and further purified using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC, Superose® 6 Increase, 10/300 GL, GE Healthcare) in the buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.01% GDN (fig. S1D). For the preparation of the PC1-PC2 complex with 7β,27-DHC, 200 mM 7β,27-DHC was added prior to SEC. Peak fractions containing the PC1-PC2 complex were pooled and concentrated to approximately 5 mg/ml for cryo-EM grid preparation.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection were performed as described42. Briefly, holey carbon grids (Quantifoil, Au, 200-mesh, R1.2/1.3) were subjected to glow-discharge using the Plasma Cleaner PDC-32G-2 (HARRICK PLASMA Company) with mid force for 30 s. Then, 4-μl aliquots of the PC1-PC2 complex were applied to the grids, and subsequently vitrified via plunge-freezing in liquid ethane using Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher). The grids were transferred to a Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a Gatan K3 Summit detector and a GIF Quantum energy filter with a slit width of 20 eV. Micrographs were automatically collected using EPU (Thermo Scientific). The stacks were subsequently motion-corrected using MotionCorr265 and the defocus values were estimated using Gctf66.

Cryo-EM data processing

For the dataset of the WT-complex, a total of 2,814,570 particles were picked and extracted from 4756 micrographs using RELION 3.167. These particles were subjected to 2D classification. 1,494,572 good particles were selected and subjected to global search 3D classification using the Class number of 6 and the step size of 7.5°. The global search 3D classification resulted in two good classes with distinct conformations of the extracellular domain. Then, the particles of each class were subjected to a local search 3D classification. The good particles of each class were selected and subjected to 3D auto-refinement, yielding two EM reconstructions at average resolutions of 2.84 Å (379,811 particles) and 3.52 Å (113,707 particles). To improve the local map quality, a small mask was applied to the dataset of 379,811 particles during the “skip alignment” 3D classification, resulting in two classes with obviously distinct local conformations. Through further 3D auto-refinement, two EM maps of WT-Class I (162,204 particles) and WT-Class II (146,525 particles) were obtained, with improved map quality in the pore domain at average resolutions of 2.9 Å and 3.0 Å, respectively. The particles in each class were imported into CryoSPARC68 and subjected to one round of local refinement. Together with particle polishing, two final EM reconstructions were obtained at average resolutions of 2.3 Å and 2.8 Å. In parallel, the dataset of 113,707 particles was similarly processed using CryoSPARC68 and yielded a final EM reconstruction (WT-Class III) at an average resolution of 3.21 Å.

For the dataset of WT-complex in the presence of 7β,27-DHC, 8,686,216 particles were picked from 14,708 micrographs using Gautomatch69. Three rounds of 2D classification resulted in 5,063,876 good particles. A global angular search 3D classification was performed using Class number of 6 and step size of 7.5°. The good particles, 2,971,330 particles and 915,045 particles, were selected for two classes and separately subjected to 3D auto-refinement, resulting in average resolutions of 3.34 Å and 3.57 Å, respectively. Furthermore, a “skip alignment” 3D classification using a small soft mask, was performed on the dataset of 2,971,330 particles, resulting in two classes, which are similar to WT-Classes I and II of the WT-complex datasets. The particles belonging to these two classes were subjected to 3D auto-refinement, yielding a final resolution of 3.06 Å for each class. In addition, the dataset of 915,045 particles was imported into CryoSPARC68 and subjected to local refinement, yielding a final resolution of 3.38 Å, denoted as WT-Class IV.

For the dataset of the AA-complex, a total of 2,169,255 particles were picked and extracted from 4,275 micrographs using RELION 3.167. Subsequently, three rounds of 2D classification were performed, result in 1,232,237 good particles. These particles were then subjected to global search 3D classification with the Class number of 1 and the step size of 7.5°. The previously deposited PC1-PC2 EM map (EMDB code: 6991), lowpass-filtered to 10 Å, was used as the initial model. Following that, local search 3D classification was performed, resulting in two good classes with distinct conformations of the extracellular domain. These two good maps, together with three bad maps, were used as references for a new round of global search 3D classification followed by a local search 3D classification. This process yielded four good classes: AA-Class I, AA-Class II, AA-Class III and AA-Class IV (192,205 particles). The particles in each class were subsequently subjected to 3D auto-refinement, resulting in four EM reconstructions at resolutions of 3.34 Å, 4.3 Å, 3.34 Å, and 3.4 Å, respectively. To improve the resolution, the dataset of AA-Class II was imported into CryoSPARC68 and subjected to one round of local refinement, resulting in an EM reconstruction at 3.69 Å resolution. To improve the map quality of AA-Classes I and IV, an overall mask was applied during the “skip alignment” 3D classification, yielding EM maps with improved quality but at the same resolution.

Model building and refinement

The atomic coordinates of the truncated human PC1-PC2 complex (PDB code: 6A7035) were used as the initial model and docked into the corresponding map in UCSF ChimeraX70. Manual adjustment was performed using COOT71. All structural refinements were carried out using the phenix.real_space_refine within PHENIX, with secondary structure and geometry restraints applied72. To avoid overfitting, the model was refined against one of the two independent half-maps from the gold standard refinement approach and tested the refined model against the other map73.

Identification and quantification of 7β,27-DHC by LC-MS/MS

The identification and quantification of 7β,27-DHC are conducted either by Agilent 6545 Q/TOF mass spectrometer or SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer.

The protein solution was reduced using 5 mM DTT (dithiothreitol) and alkylated using 20 mM idoacetamide at 25 °C for 30 minutes. Trypsin (Promega) was subsequently added at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50 (w/w) and the mixture was digested overnight at 37 °C. The digested solution was extracted using MTBE (methyl tert-butyl ether) followed by centrifugation. Supernatants were collected, lyophilized under vacuum, and the extracted lipids were dissolved in 100 μL CHCl3/methanol (2:1) for LC-MS/MS analysis.

For LC-MS/MS analysis of 7β,27-DHC in standard sample and different group using an Agilent 6545 Q/TOF mass spectrometer connected with Agilent 1290 LC system. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) at 50 °C. The mobile phase A consists of 10 mM ammonium acetate, 0.2 mM ammonium fluoride in a 9:1 water/methanol ratio. The mobile phase B contains 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.2 mM ammonium fluoride in a 2:3:5 acetonitrile/methanol/isopropanol ratio. The flow rate was set at 0.3 ml/min. The gradient of mobile phase B was 70% in 1 min, 70 to 86% in 2.5 min, held at 86% for 6.5 min, then 86% to 100% in 1 min, held at 100% for 6 min, then 100% to 70% in 0.1 min, held at 70% for 1.9 min. A sample volume of 3 μL was injected for analysis. Detection was carried out using an Agilent 6545 Q/TOF-MS system in ESI positive modes with the following parameters: capillary voltage, 3500 V; Nozzle voltage, 120 V; nebulizer gas, 50 psi; gas flow rate,10 L/min; and gas temperature, 200 °C. The mass spectrometer was operated in the MS1 scan mode and MS/MS scan mode with a scan range spanning 50 to 1100 Da. MassHuter Workstation software Data Acquisition and Qualitative Analysis 10.0 (Agilent, Santa, CA, USA) was used for data acquisition and data analysis.

For LC-MS/MS analysis of 7β,27-DHC content in wildtype and mutant samples using SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer connected with Exion LC system. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Accucore C8 column (100 mm×2.1 mm, 2.6 μm) at 50 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 10 mM ammonium acetate, 0.1% formic acid in 95:5 water/methanol (A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (B) at a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min. The gradient of mobile phase B held at 20% for 1 min, 20% to 80% in 2 min, then 80 to 100% in 7 min, held at 100% for 8 min. The sample volume injected was 3 μL. Mass spectrometer operating in positive ion mode using the following settings: Curtain Gas 40 psi, IonSpray Voltage 4.5 kV, source temperature 550 °C, ion source gas 160 psi, ion source gas 255 psi. Compounds were measured by multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with optimized cone voltage and collision energy. The quantifier MRM transitions (m/z) for 7β,27-DHC was m/z 383.1/159.1. SCIEX Analyst Software v1.7 (AB SCIEX, USA) was used for data acquisition, SCIEX OS 1.4 (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) was used for data analysis.

Especially, quantitation was performed based on the integrated peak areas of the MRM signals. Relative levels of 7β,27-DHC were quantified by comparing peak areas across samples. The purified PKD complexes from four biological replicates of WT, six biological replicates of G4028W, and six biological replicates of E4025R were analyzed in parallel. Each purified sample was equally divided into two conditions, with or without incubation with 7β,27-DHC. The fold change of 7β,27-DHC in each sample was calculated as the ratio of the MRM signal in the presence versus absence of 7β,27-DHC incubation. More details can be found in Supplementary Data 1. Data was processed by GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and statistical significance was assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, using the corresponding WT samples as controls.

Xenopus oocytes electrophysiology: constructs and oocyte injection

The CTF fragment (residues 3049-4303) of human PC1 cDNA (NCBI accession No. L33243.1) and full-length human PC2 cDNA (NCBI accession No. U50928) were cloned into a modified pGEMHE vector for in vitro transcription. An Ig k-chain leader sequence from the vector pDisplay (Invitrogen) was fused to the N-terminus of PC1-CTF, followed by an HA tag. Similarly, an N-terminal HA tag was incorporated into PC2 for this study. All mutations were introduced using PCR and confirmed through double-strand DNA sequencing.

In vitro synthesized RNAs were then injected into follicle-membrane-free Xenopus oocytes. For each oocyte, 96 ng of PC1-CTF and 27 ng of PC2 were injected. The molar ratio of injected PC1 RNA to PC2 RNA is approximately 2.7:1. Excess PC1 was designed to reduce formation of the homomeric PC2 complex. After injection, these oocytes were incubated at 18 °C for 3–-6 days before whole-oocyte currents recording.

Animal use statement

Frogs were housed in the St. John’s University Animal Care Center under standard conditions and maintained for 6 months to 1 year. For each batch of oocyte injection experiments, oocytes were isolated from one female Xenopus laevis frog. Only female Xenopus laevis frogs were used because mature oocytes are required for the experimental procedures. Comparisons based on sex were not applicable in this study.

Xenopus oocytes electrophysiology: solutions

Unless otherwise stated, oocytes were recorded at room temperature in standard bath solution containing 2 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 2 mM CaCl2. In divalent‑free conditions, oocytes were recorded in bath solutions containing 100 mM NaCl or 100 mM NMDG-Cl, with 2 mM HEPES adjusted to pH 7.5. For the ion permeability assay, 100 mM Na+ in the standard bath solution was replaced with 100 mM Li+, guanidinium, tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane (Tris), dimethylamine (DMA), or N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG). The pH of Li+, Gua+, Tris+ and NMDG+ solutions was adjusted to 7.5 using HCl, whereas the DMA+ solution was adjusted to 7.5 using NMDG. Before application, 1 mM niflumic acid was added to the ion permeability assay solutions to inhibit unknown channel activity induced by these ions. To further minimize leak currents from endogenous channels, particularly under divalent-free conditions, we adopted a similar strategy as previously described: co-injecting 3 ng antisense phosphorothioate oligomer to suppress endogenous connexin 38 hemichannel activity. This approach substantially reduced basal currents in oocytes74.

Xenopus oocytes electrophysiology: protocol and relative ion permeability ratio calculation

Whole-oocyte currents of Xenopus laevis oocytes were recorded using the two-electrode voltage clamp (TEVC) technique. TEVC data were acquired using an Oocyte Clamp OC-725C amplifier (Warner Instruments) and digitized using a Digidata 1440 A digitizer and pClamp10 software (Molecular Devices). A standard TEVC protocol includes holding oocytes at 0 mV and measuring the current-voltage (I-V) relationships by applying an I-V protocol that runs 80-ms voltage steps ranging from -100 to +100 mV (or as indicated) in 10-mV increments.

For experiments determining ion permeability, oocytes were perfused using 100 mM Na+ bath solution first. The I–V curve and the reversal potential of Na+ were extracted using the voltage step protocol. Oocytes were then washed using solutions containing 100 mM of the other tested ions. The I-V was monitored using a voltage ramp protocol (from −80 to +60 mV in 170 ms) until the reversal potential shift stopped (usually takes about 20 seconds). Another voltage step protocol was then applied to extract the I-V curve and reversal potential for the tested ion. The relative permeability of the tested ions was calculated based on the difference between the reversal potentials of the tested ion and the reference ion (NMDG+). The permeability ratios between tested ions (x+) and the reference ion (NMDG+), Px/PRef, were calculated using the following modified Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) equation75: PX/Pref = e∆ErevF/RT, where Erev represents the reversal potential, and ΔErev is the difference between the reversal potentials of ion x+ and the reference ion (NMDG+). ΔErev = Erev,X - Erev,ref. F is the Faraday constant, R is the universal gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature.

All recordings were repeated using at least two batches of oocytes, with the majority repeated three times or more. Channel current data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA), and the statistical significance was assessed using either one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test as indicated in figure legends. Results with P < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant. Results were presented as means ± SD.

HEK293 cell electrophysiology: whole-cell patch clamp

HEK cells stably expressing both human PC1 and PC2, or human PC2 alone under a doxycycline-inducible promoter, were kindly provided by Dr. M. Delling55. Both cell lines were used in our recording. For experiments using the PC2-only stable line, PC1 in the pTRE vector was transiently transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Expression of the polycystins was induced in HEK-293 cells with 2 μg/ml doxycycline in complete medium for 48 h, prior to recording.

Whole-cell patch clamp recordings were performed using Multiclamp 700B amplifier and Digidata 1550B digitizer controled by pClamp 11 software (Molecular Devices). Currents was filtered at 1 kHz and sampled at 10 kHz. Cells were held at -60 mV and 150-ms voltage steps were applied from -100 mV to +180 mV in 20 mV increments. The extracellular solution contained (mM): 145 Na-gluconate, 5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 5 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, and adjusted to pH 7.4 using NaOH. The intracellular (pipette) solution contained (mM): 90 NaMES, 10 NaCl, 5 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 5 EGTA, 100 nM free Ca2+ adjusted by CaCl2, and adjusted to pH 7.4 using NaOH. Data were analyzed using Clampfit 11 software (Molecular Devices).
