Programmable, target-induced fluorogenic CRISPR–tDeg platform for live-cell RNA visualization
Authors: Huimin Zhong, Jiayan Zhou, Fujun Qin, Xian-En Zhang, Minghai Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA molecules display remarkable heterogeneity in structure, dynamics, and function, yet methods for their precise visualization in living cells remain limited. While CRISPR-based RNA imaging holds great potential, existing systems often suffer from high background fluorescence due to constitutive signal emission or non-specific binding. To overcome these challenges, we developed CtDeg (CRISPR–dCas13–tDeg), a modular RNA imaging platform that links fluorescence activation directly to target RNA recognition while leveraging degron-mediated degradation to suppress background signals. By engineering the crRNA scaffold to embed the Pepper RNA motif, CtDeg ensures that fluorescence is present only upon binding to the native RNA target. We systematically optimized C-terminal tDeg variants to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio and demonstrated that CtDeg achieves substantially lower background and higher specificity than conventional fluorescent protein–CRISPR-based RNA imaging approaches. Using CtDeg, we captured real-time paraspeckle assembly dynamics and visualized early-stage SARS-CoV-2 genomic RNA transport. Remarkably, CtDeg provided the first direct imaging evidence of virus-induced NEAT1_2 lncRNA accumulation, revealing a host-virus regulatory interaction. Beyond these applications, CtDeg is compatible with multiple Cas13 orthologs and fluorescent proteins, establishing a versatile, target-induced platform for probing RNA localization, dynamics, and function in living cells, with broad applications in synthetic biology and RNA biology.

Introduction

RNA molecules, including messenger RNAs (mRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and exogenous pathogenic RNAs, play central roles in gene regulation and cellular homeostasis [1]. Their abundance, localization, and dynamics are tightly regulated, and dysregulation of these processes is linked to diverse diseases [2–4]. Visualizing RNAs in living cells is therefore essential for elucidating their interactions with biomolecules, spatial organization, transport mechanisms, and stimulus-responsive dynamics [5–8]. Such insights are critical for deciphering RNA regulatory networks and identifying therapeutic targets in RNA-associated pathologies [9, 10].

Current strategies for live-cell RNA imaging include fluorescent RNA aptamers and the MS2–MCP tagging system, which have advanced our understanding of RNA behavior [11–13]. However, these methods often require exogenous dyes or extensive RNA engineering, which may perturb native RNA function or introduce background noise [14, 15]. CRISPR-based RNA imaging systems, such as those employing catalytically dead Cas13 (dCas13), offer a modular alternative by enabling sequence-specific labeling of endogenous RNAs without genetic modification [16–18].

To improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), recent efforts have focused on enhancing specificity and reducing background fluorescence [4]. For example, systems coupling fluorescent proteins with Tat–Degron domains (tDeg) can selectively stabilize fluorescence upon binding to engineered RNA scaffolds, such as the optimized TAR RNA variant Pepper [19]. This principle of conditional stability has also been successfully applied to classic RNA tagging systems; for instance, degron-masked variants of the MS2 coat protein (dMCP) enable sensitive, low-background imaging of single mRNAs by stabilizing only upon RNA binding [20]. While effective, these scaffold- or tag-dependent strategies generally require modification of the target RNA, thereby limiting their application to native transcripts [21]. Building on the tDeg concept, fCRISPR was proposed for DNA imaging by incorporating the Pepper motif into the sgRNA [22]; however, it suffers from nonspecific fluorescence due to spontaneous tDeg–Pepper interactions. Another advance, smLiveFISH, employs CRISPR–Csm complexes with up to 48 guide RNAs to visualize unmodified endogenous RNAs at single-molecule resolution [23]. Although highly sensitive, this method relies on stitching long RNA regions and repetitive crRNA arrays, which may constrain its applicability for many physiological transcripts.

Here, we introduce CtDeg (CRISPR–dCas13–tDeg), a genetically encoded RNA imaging platform that integrates programmable RNA targeting with degron-mediated background suppression, enabling high-specificity, target-induced visualization of unmodified RNAs. By embedding the Pepper motif within the crRNA scaffold, CtDeg ensures fluorescence is activated only upon target binding, while unbound proteins are selectively degraded. This target-induced activation enables high-specificity imaging using a single crRNA. Using CtDeg, we demonstrate high-specificity, target-induced RNA imaging in living cells, capable of capturing dynamic behaviors of both nuclear and viral RNAs. Its modular design, minimal perturbation, and broad adaptability make it a powerful platform for studying RNA localization, dynamics, and function.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK 293T, ATCC CRL-3216), Vero E6 cells (ATCC CRL-1586), and Caco-2-N cells [24] were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C with 5% CO₂.

For transfection, HEK 293T cells (4 × 10⁵ cells per well) or Vero E6 cells (2 × 10⁵ cells per well) were seeded in 12-well plates (Corning) or 35-mm glass-bottom dishes (Cellvis). Cells were transfected at ~80%–85% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Plasmid construction

Fluorescent protein–dCas13 fusion constructs were generated in the pcDNA3.1(+) backbone under the control of CMV or MiniCMV promoters. These constructs contained fluorescent reporters (n*GFP, Venus, or mCherry), Cas13 variants (dLwaCas13a, dPspCas13b, or dRfxCas13d), nuclear localization signals (NLS), and degron or linker elements as schematized in Supplementary Fig. S1A–D.

crRNA expression vectors were based on the pAV-U6+27 backbone and utilized the U6 promoter. They encoded either targeting or non-targeting crRNA sequences and, where applicable, included an internal reference cassette (Supplementary Fig. S1E–J). For other components, MCP–3×BFP was expressed from a lentiviral vector (pHAGE-EFS), while multi-crRNA arrays were cloned into a mammalian expression vector (pCAGGS) (Supplementary Fig. S1K and L). All construct architectures are detailed in Supplementary Fig. S1, with complete sequences provided in Supplementary Tables.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA Extraction Kit (Magen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed using a One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (Vazyme Biotech) with the following cycling conditions: 50°C for 5 min, 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was conducted after amplification to verify primer specificity.

Relative RNA levels were normalized to GAPDH or 18S rRNA, as indicated for each experiment. Data were acquired using a Bio-Rad CFX system and analyzed with GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1. Primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Live-cell imaging

To visualize RNA and protein signals labeled by the CtDeg system, cells were cultured on 35-mm glass-bottom imaging dishes (Cellvis) and imaged live without fixation. When indicated, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime) at a final concentration of 5 µg/ml for 20 min prior to imaging.

Live-cell confocal imaging was performed using a Nikon AX laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 60× oil-immersion objective (NA 1.42). Fluorescent proteins (e.g. GFP, Venus, and mCherry) and fluorescent dyes were excited using appropriate laser lines (405, 488, 561, or 640 nm), and emission signals were collected through their corresponding spectral detection channels. Laser power, detector gain, and scanning parameters were adjusted to achieve sufficient SNR while minimizing photobleaching and phototoxicity.

For comparative analyses, imaging parameters were kept identical between experimental and control conditions within each independent experiment. Cells were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO₂ using an on-stage incubator during imaging for all experiments requiring extended observation. For short-term live-cell imaging experiments, cells were imaged under ambient conditions. In both cases, experimental and control samples were acquired using the same imaging settings.

For time-lapse imaging experiments, including paraspeckle dynamics and viral particle tracking, the imaging system was selected based on the specific experimental requirements of each biological process. Paraspeckle dynamics were imaged using a Nikon CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal system with a 1-min acquisition interval, whereas viral particle tracking was performed using a Nikon AX laser scanning confocal microscope with acquisition intervals of 1 or 3 min, as indicated. For each experiment, imaging parameters were kept constant across all compared conditions.

Flow cytometry

Cells subjected to treatments were washed twice with PBS and detached using 0.05% trypsin. Cells were resuspended in PBS for flow cytometry analysis. Data acquisition was performed using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman). Fluorescence signals were collected using appropriate detection channels according to the spectral properties of the fluorescent proteins. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo version 10.8.1.

Stable cell line generation and cell sorting

The target gene was cloned into a lentiviral vector carrying a puromycin resistance marker. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK 293T cells by co-transfection with packaging plasmids and used to transduce Vero E6 or HEK 293T cells. Transduced cells were selected with puromycin 48 h post-infection.

For experiments requiring homogeneous reporter expression, the polyclonal population was subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Cells were harvested, resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS, and filtered through a 70-μm strainer. Sorting was performed using a calibrated FACSAria III sorter (BD Biosciences), and target cells were collected into pre-warmed medium. Stable expression was confirmed by western blotting (WB).

Western blotting

Following transfection, cells were lysed on ice for 15 min using WB lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation at 14 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, protein concentrations in the supernatants were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Bio-Rad Mini PROTEAN system) and transferred to PVDF membranes using a semi-dry transfer apparatus (Bio-Rad).

Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-sfGFP (HUABIO, 1:1000), rabbit anti-N protein (Sino Biological, 1:1000), and mouse anti-β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:3000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated species-specific secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate on a chemiluminescent imager (Analytic Jena). Band intensities were quantified using FIJI and Prism (Version 10.3.1) software.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded on glass-bottom dishes and transfected. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime; 15 min, RT), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Solarbio; 5 min), and blocked with 5% BSA (Fdbio; 1 h). Subsequently, cells were incubated with rabbit anti-NONO antibody (Affinity; 1:100) overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam; 1:500; 1 h, RT). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime; 10 µg/ml; 10 min). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon AX microscope and analyzed with FIJI.

CRISPR–Cas9–mediated disruption of a NEAT1 genomic region

To generate a cellular model with disrupted NEAT1 lncRNA expression, we employed CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing targeting a defined region within the NEAT1 locus. The PX459 plasmid (Addgene #62988), which expresses SpCas9 and a puromycin resistance gene, was used as the backbone. sgRNAs were designed using the CHOPCHOP web tool and cloned into the plasmid.

Cells were transfected with the sgRNA–PX459 constructs using Lipofectamine 3000, followed by puromycin selection to enrich transfected populations. To obtain isolated clones, limiting dilution was performed. Successful disruption of the targeted genomic region was screened by PCR. The sgRNA target sequences and the corresponding genomic regions are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization

Fluorescently labeled DNA oligonucleotide probes targeting specific transcripts were designed and synthesized commercially (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). All steps were performed using an RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) kit (Beyotime) unless otherwise specified, following the manufacturer’s protocol noted below.

Cells grown on glass-bottom dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (10 min, RT), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (10 min), and washed with PBS. Hybridization was carried out with 100 nM of each probe in the provided hybridization buffer at 40°C overnight (∼16 h). After hybridization, slides were washed stringently according to the kit protocol to remove nonspecifically bound probes. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (10 μg/ml, 10 min). Images were acquired on a Nikon AX confocal microscope using identical settings across all samples and controls. Colocalization between RNA-FISH signals and CtDeg fluorescence was quantified on a per-cell basis using FIJI, as described in the corresponding figure legends.

The sequences of all Cy3-labeled RNA-FISH probes used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Cellular viability, apoptosis, and RNA stability assays

Cellular compatibility of the CtDeg system was evaluated using cell viability, apoptosis, and transcription inhibition assays.

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Apexbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HEK 293T cells (2 × 104 per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and transfected with the indicated RNA imaging constructs. At 24 h post-transfection, fresh culture medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent was added, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 h using a microplate reader (Agilent).

Apoptosis was analyzed 24 h post-transfection by Annexin V and propidium iodide staining (Elabscience, Cat# E-CK-A239) followed by flow cytometry. Actinomycin D (ActD; 3 μg/ml) was added at 20 h post-transfection as a positive control. Samples were analyzed using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and 20 000 events were acquired per sample. Total apoptosis was defined as the sum of Annexin V⁺/PI− and Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ populations.

To assess whether CtDeg imaging perturbs endogenous RNA stability, transcription inhibition assays were performed using ActD (Invivochem). Cells were transfected and incubated for 18 h, followed by ActD treatment at a final concentration of 5 μg/ml to inhibit transcription. Total RNA was harvested at the indicated time points and analyzed by RT-qPCR as described earlier.

Virus production, concentration, and fluorescent labeling

The SARS-CoV-2 transcription- and replication-competent virus-like particle (trVLP) system, a BSL-2–compatible model that mimics the viral life cycle, was used as previously described [25]. trVLPs were propagated in Caco-2-N cells cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Upon reaching 90%–95% confluence, cells were inoculated with the virus. Supernatants containing viral particles were harvested at 48 and 96 h post-infection, clarified by centrifugation (500 × g, 5 min), and filtered through a 0.45-µm membrane.

For concentration, clarified supernatant was mixed with PEG6000 [final 10% (w/v)] and NaCl (0.5 M), incubated overnight at 4°C, and centrifuged (10 000 × g, 30 min, 4°C). The viral pellet was resuspended in a small volume of Opti-MEM and stored at −80°C.

For fluorescent labeling, concentrated virus was incubated with SYTO Deep Red (2 µM final; Thermo Fisher) at 37°C for 1 h. Unincorporated dye was removed using a PD MiniTrap G-25 desalting column (Cytiva) per the manufacturer’s protocol. The labeled virus preparation was stored at 4°C and used for imaging within 48 h.

Structural modeling and docking

Structural modeling of the circular Pepper–crRNA was performed using AlphaFold3 [26]. Predicted structural models were used for subsequent structural visualization and docking analyses.

Molecular docking between the tDeg module and the circular Pepper–crRNA scaffold was performed using HADDOCK 2.4 [27, 28]. The tDeg domain was defined as Molecule 1 and the Pepper–crRNA structure as Molecule 2. Docking calculations were carried out using default HADDOCK parameters. Docking solutions were clustered based on HADDOCK scores, and representative models from dominant clusters were selected for analysis. Energetic and interface-related parameters, including van der Waals energy, electrostatic energy, desolvation energy, restraint violation energy, and buried surface area, were extracted for evaluation of the predicted interaction interfaces.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests. P-values < .05 were considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. For single-cell–based analyses, each data point represents one cell, and the number of cells analyzed (n) as well as the number of independent experiments (N) are specified in the corresponding figure legends. Detailed information on statistical tests and sample sizes for each analysis is provided in the figure legends.

Results

Design of a target-activated CtDeg RNA imaging system

To achieve precise and low-background RNA imaging, we developed CtDeg (CRISPR–dCas13–tDeg), a CRISPR–dCas13-based platform that couples RNA recognition with conditional stabilization of a fluorescent reporter. CtDeg integrates a fluorescent protein–tDeg reporter module regulated by the Pepper aptamer, a TAR-derived RNA that binds the Tat peptide. Without Pepper–Tat interaction, tDeg directs fused fluorescent protein for rapid proteasomal degradation, minimizing background fluorescence. Upon specific binding to the correctly folded Pepper aptamer, degradation is inhibited and fluorescence is restored (Fig. 1A).

To tightly link fluorescence to target RNA recognition, we engineered inactive circular Pepper–crRNA and target-activated circular Pepper–crRNA states (relevant sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S4). In the absence of target RNA, Pepper misfolding prevents Tat binding and maintains the reporter in an unstable state. Target binding induces Pepper refolding, enabling Tat interaction and reporter stabilization, thus generating fluorescence (Fig. 1A). Through this mechanism, CtDeg functions as a target-activated RNA imaging platform with minimal nonspecific background, yielding fluorescence in a target-dependent manner.

Target-dependent activation of CtDeg in living cells

To directly test whether CtDeg fluorescence is strictly dependent on RNA binding and not influenced by pre-existing nuclear structures, we selected the endogenous long non-coding RNA NEAT1_1 as our initial model target. Unlike the long NEAT1_2 isoform, which serves as an essential scaffold for paraspeckle nuclear bodies, NEAT1_1 does not participate in paraspeckle assembly and exhibits a diffuse cellular distribution. This non-canonical localization makes NEAT1_1 a challenging and informative target for evaluating CtDeg performance in the absence of prominent RNA condensates. Unlike the largely nuclear NEAT1_2, NEAT1_1 can distribute outside the nucleus, making it amenable to cytoplasmic RNA imaging.

Fluorescent reporters in the CtDeg system employ a split-GFP (n*GFP) reconstitution strategy (sfGFP-10×GFP11 and GFP1–10) fused with dLwaCas13a and tDeg (amino acid sequences provided in Supplementary Table S5), hereafter referred to as n*GFP–dLwaCas13a–tDeg, enabling robust, target-induced cytoplasmic fluorescence while maintaining minimal background signal. Using PX459–Cas9-mediated non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) [29], we generated NEAT1_1-depleted HEK 293T cells by introducing fragment deletions within the NEAT1_1 locus (Supplementary Fig. S2A). PCR genotyping (Supplementary Fig. S2B) and RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. S2C) showed that NEAT1_1 transcript levels were reduced to ~30% of wild-type (WT) cells; residual transcript fragments remained detectable, and this system is therefore interpreted as a depletion rather than a complete knockout model.

Two circular Pepper–crRNAs targeting distinct regions of NEAT1_1 (T3 and T5) were evaluated. In WT cells, both crRNAs produced strong cytoplasmic fluorescence, whereas CtDeg signal intensity was markedly reduced in NEAT1_1-depleted cells. Live-cell imaging and flow cytometry quantification revealed a reproducible reduction in fluorescence intensity in NEAT1_1-depleted cells, consistent with partial transcript depletion–dependent modulation of CtDeg signal (Fig. 1B and C and Supplementary Fig. S2D and E). Non-targeting crRNA controls in both WT and NEAT1_1-depleted cells exhibited minimal fluorescence, confirming low background activation of the CtDeg system.

As an orthogonal reference, a multi-probe RNA-FISH assay targeting NEAT1_1 was performed to support the correlation between RNA-derived CtDeg fluorescence and the intended target gene (Supplementary Fig. S2F). Because partial transcript depletion can lead to heterogeneous responses at the single-cell level, the NEAT1_1 depletion model was used to assess RNA-dependent modulation of CtDeg signal intensity, rather than to define absolute background fluorescence. To establish a stringent single-cell negative control in the complete absence of target RNA, we next employed an exogenous RNA system with genetically defined target presence or absence.

We generated HEK 293T cells stably expressing the SARS-CoV-2 N RNA and compared them with matched parental cells lacking the transcript (Supplementary Fig. S2G). Circular Pepper–crRNAs targeting the N RNA produced robust cytoplasmic fluorescence exclusively in N RNA–expressing cells, whereas parental cells exhibited signal near background levels (Fig. 1D and E), providing an unambiguous negative control at single-cell resolution.

To further assess CtDeg performance across different expression contexts, we also imaged transiently expressed SARS-CoV-2 N and NSP1 transcripts. Strong fluorescence was observed only in cells expressing the corresponding target RNAs, while non-expressing cells showed minimal signal (Supplementary Fig. S2H–L), demonstrating that CtDeg supports imaging of both stably and dynamically expressed exogenous RNAs.

Finally, comparison of inactive and constitutively activated circular Pepper–crRNAs in cells lacking target RNA confirmed that fluorescence activation arises from the designed target-dependent mechanism rather than passive reporter stabilization (Fig. 1F and G and Supplementary Fig. S2M and N). Collectively, these results establish that CtDeg exhibits minimal basal activity and robust, target-induced activation across endogenous, stably expressed, and transiently expressed RNA contexts.

Circularization of Pepper–crRNA enhances CtDeg activation stability

Before exploring degron engineering, we evaluated the effect of crRNA scaffold architecture on CtDeg performance. Circular Pepper–crRNAs consistently produced stronger and more sustained target-dependent fluorescence than linear counterparts (Supplementary Fig. S3A–E). In contrast, linear Pepper–crRNAs failed to sustain reporter stabilization over time, leading to reduced steady-state fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. S3F and G). These results indicate that circularization stabilizes the Pepper–crRNA scaffold and supports sustained Tat-mediated protection of the reporter, whereas linear scaffolds are insufficient to maintain CtDeg activation. Accordingly, circular Pepper–crRNAs were used for all subsequent experiments.

Engineering the tDeg module to improve SNR

Having established the specificity and fluorescence performance of CtDeg, we next sought to expand its dynamic range by lowering background while enhancing target-induced activation. Our optimization focused on the tDeg module, reasoning that engineering the Tat peptide, in combination with different degron motifs, could improve its interaction with the Pepper aptamer and thereby refine control of fluorescent reporter protein degradation. Previous work demonstrated that replacing the C-terminal residues of bovine immunodeficiency virus (BIV) Tat with the “KIHY” motif from Jembrana disease virus (JDV) Tat increases binding affinity for structured Pepper RNA [30]. Within Tat peptide, tyrosine (Y) and histidine (H)—particularly Y—are critical for RNA recognition, while isoleucine (I) contributes to TAR RNA recognition and peptide folding [31]. Guided by these insights, we constructed four mutation series—Y, I, G, and g (Supplementary Fig. S4). The Y and I series were generated by replacing the native C terminus of BIV Tat (–RIRR) with the –KIHY motif, combined with different degron fusions, whereas the G and g series introduced an N-terminal glycine (G) degron fused to Tat [32, 33]. All variants preserved the essential isoleucine, with selected constructs incorporating additional substitutions. Predicted structural models of tDeg–Pepper complexes were generated using AlphaFold3 (Supplementary Fig. S5).

WB analysis of the WT tDeg construct further supported crRNA-dependent modulation of CtDeg reporter protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S6A and B). To functionally evaluate the variants, we compared their cytoplasmic expression based on the n*GFP–dLwaCas13a fusion backbone relative to the original tDeg. Each construct was co-transfected with circular Pepper–crRNA targeting NEAT1_1, and fluorescence outputs were measured by flow cytometry in the presence or absence of crRNA, corresponding to target-induced signals and background, respectively. The activation ratio of WT tDeg was ∼4-fold (Supplementary Fig. S6I). Fluorescence intensities were normalized, and activation ratios (target-induced/background fluorescence) were calculated to quantify performance. Dual-threshold analysis (|log₂ fold change| > 0.5, P < .05), visualized by volcano plots (Supplementary Fig. S6C and D), identified three variants with significantly increased activation ratios (I1, G2, G3) and one with reduced performance (g7). Among these, G3 exhibited the largest fold change, combining maximal target-specific enhancement with minimal background, thereby representing the optimal variant for fluorescence contrast (Supplementary Fig. S6E).

Confocal imaging further corroborated the superior performance of G3. Cells co-transfected with G3 and Pepper–crRNA displayed bright and uniform cytoplasmic fluorescence, whereas cells lacking crRNA exhibited minimal background signals (Supplementary Fig. S6F and G). Compared with the original degron, G3 produced a markedly higher contrast between target-dependent activation and basal fluorescence, consistent with the flow cytometry results (Supplementary Fig. S6H). Quantitative analysis confirmed that G3 increased the activation ratio from ∼4-fold in the original construct to ∼9-fold, significantly enhancing signal while effectively suppressing background (Supplementary Fig. S6I). The G3 variant was therefore selected as the optimized tDeg module and used in all subsequent experiments unless otherwise specified.

Structural modeling supports target-dependent CtDeg activation

To provide structural support for target-dependent CtDeg activation, we performed exploratory structural modeling and docking analyses. Structural models of the circular Pepper–crRNA scaffold were generated using AlphaFold3 in the absence and presence of target RNA, corresponding to two predicted conformational states (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Predicted models of dCas13 bound to circular Pepper–crRNA did not reveal obvious steric clashes, indicating that incorporation of the Pepper aptamer into the crRNA scaffold is structurally compatible with Cas13 binding (Supplementary Fig. S7B).

Molecular docking between the tDeg module and Pepper–crRNA was performed using HADDOCK 2.4. Docking solutions formed distinct clusters in interface RMSD and contact fraction space (Supplementary Fig. S7C), with dominant clusters further supported by Z-score analysis (Supplementary Fig. S7D). Representative docking models showed that the tDeg module can approach the Pepper aptamer and form plausible contact interfaces without apparent steric conflicts. Energetic and interface-related parameters of dominant clusters were within ranges commonly observed for RNA–protein interactions, with electrostatic interactions contributing substantially to the predicted interfaces (Supplementary Fig. S7E–G). The buried surface area of representative docking models was consistent with typical RNA–protein interaction interfaces (Supplementary Fig. S7H).

Together, these analyses support the structural feasibility of interactions between the Pepper–crRNA scaffold and the tDeg module, consistent with the experimentally observed target-dependent activation of CtDeg fluorescence.

Specific imaging of endogenous nuclear RNAs

To evaluate the specificity of CtDeg (G3) for nuclear RNA imaging, we examined its ability to visualize NEAT1_2, the long isoform of the nuclear long non-coding RNA NEAT1 that is essential for paraspeckle assembly [34]. For nuclear imaging, an NLS-tagged n*GFP–dLwaCas13a–G3 fusion protein was transiently co-expressed with circular Pepper–crRNAs targeting NEAT1_2. CtDeg fluorescence formed distinct nuclear puncta that overlapped with the paraspeckle marker NONO (Fig. 2A and B), indicating that CtDeg localizes to nuclear regions enriched in paraspeckle-associated proteins. However, colocalization with paraspeckle markers reflects live-cell localization context and does not by itself establish RNA identity [35]. Notably, the prominent large nuclear accumulations observed in Fig. 2A are larger than canonical paraspeckles and localize to DAPI-depleted regions (Supplementary Fig. S8A and C); these structures were therefore not interpreted as typical paraspeckles and were not used to infer RNA identity [36, 37].

To directly determine the RNA identity associated with CtDeg-labeled nuclear structures, we performed RNA-FISH targeting endogenous NEAT1_2. RNA-FISH revealed that CtDeg-positive nuclear puncta consistently contain endogenous NEAT1_2 RNA (Fig. 2C). Quantitative colocalization analysis using Pearson’s correlation coefficient and thresholded Manders overlap coefficients demonstrated robust spatial overlap between CtDeg and NEAT1_2 RNA in the targeting condition, but not in non-targeting controls (Fig. 2D–F). Together, these results distinguish live-cell localization context from RNA identity validation and demonstrate that CtDeg labels nuclear structures containing endogenous NEAT1_2 RNA.

Validation of cytoplasmic RNA imaging using an MS2-based system

To independently validate cytoplasmic RNA localization by CtDeg, we compared it with an established MS2-based tagging system. Three MS2 binding sites were fused to the SARS-CoV-2 N RNA, and circular Pepper–crRNAs targeting the N RNA were co-expressed with a cytoplasmic n*GFP–dLwaCas13a–G3 construct lacking the NLS. In parallel, the same N RNA was visualized using the 3×BFP-MCP/3×MS2 system. Fluorescent puncta generated by CtDeg overlapped with MCP/MS2 signals in the cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. S8B and D), indicating that CtDeg enables specific and reliable visualization of cytoplasmic RNA comparable to the MS2 system.

Sensitivity of CtDeg for weakly expressed endogenous transcripts

Having established the specificity of the CtDeg system, we next evaluated its operational sensitivity for imaging endogenous transcripts in living cells. To this end, we selected FOS and MYC, two representative endogenous RNAs that are not highly expressed under our experimental conditions and span a range of moderate to low expression levels [38].

Under identical imaging and acquisition settings, fluorescence signals generated by CtDeg targeting FOS or MYC were quantitatively compared with those obtained using a non-targeting control crRNA. As shown in Fig. 2G and H, both targets produced reproducible fluorescence signals above background. Targeting FOS, which is detected at the lower end of the expression range, resulted in an approximately two-fold increase in signal intensity relative to the non-targeting control, demonstrating robust target-dependent detection despite its low expression. Notably, CtDeg targeting MYC consistently yielded a higher fluorescence signal than targeting FOS, indicating that the system can resolve relative differences in expression levels across this moderate-to-low expression regime.

Flow cytometry analysis provided an independent, population-level validation of these observations, confirming significant fluorescence enhancement for both FOS- and MYC-targeting crRNAs compared with non-targeting controls (Supplementary Fig. S8E).

To provide expression context for the selected targets, RT-qPCR analysis was performed. These measurements confirmed that MYC exhibits moderate expression relative to housekeeping genes, whereas FOS is consistently detected at substantially lower relative levels under the experimental conditions used in this study (Fig. 2I). Importantly, RT-qPCR was utilized here solely to establish relative expression context between targets and was not intended for absolute transcript quantification.

Together, these results demonstrate that CtDeg enables reproducible, target-dependent fluorescence detection of endogenous transcripts across a moderate-to-low expression range in living cells, thereby complementing its high specificity.

Generalizability of CtDeg across Cas13 orthologs and reporters

To evaluate the versatility of CtDeg, we implemented the optimized CtDeg architecture with two additional Cas13 orthologs, dPspCas13b and dRfxCas13d, alongside dLwaCas13a, and paired each with n*GFP, Venus, or mCherry reporters [39–41]. Using circular Pepper–crRNAs targeting an exogenous SARS-CoV-2 N RNA, CtDeg exhibited robust target-dependent fluorescence activation with minimal background across compatible Cas13–reporter combinations, as assessed by confocal imaging and single-cell quantification (Fig. 3A and B and Supplementary Fig. S9A). Consistent results were observed by flow cytometry, supporting the imaging-based measurements (Supplementary Fig. S9B and C).

Biocompatibility and impact of CtDeg on cellular physiology

To evaluate the biocompatibility of CtDeg, we next assessed its effects on cellular viability and endogenous RNA behavior using the n*GFP–dLwaCas13a–G3 construct as a representative CtDeg configuration. Cell viability was monitored using CCK-8 assays following CtDeg expression. CtDeg-expressing cells maintained viability comparable to, or slightly higher than, control groups across the examined time points, with no evidence of compromised cell viability or cytotoxic effects under imaging conditions (Fig. 3C).

A critical requirement for live-cell RNA imaging is that the detection system does not perturb the native dynamics of its target transcripts. To address this, we examined the decay dynamics of the short-lived endogenous transcript MYC [42]. Time-course analysis revealed rapid induction followed by a decay phase beginning ~30 min after induction under targeting, non-targeting, and vector control conditions (Fig. 3D). Importantly, the overall decay behavior of MYC was comparable across all conditions, indicating that CtDeg-mediated imaging does not cause overt stabilization or depletion of endogenous RNA.

As an additional assessment of cellular safety, we examined apoptosis in HEK 293T cells expressing CtDeg constructs. Flow cytometric analysis showed that early, late, and total apoptosis rates in CtDeg-expressing cells were comparable to those observed in empty vector or non-crRNA controls, whereas treatment with actinomycin D resulted in a marked increase in apoptosis, confirming assay sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. S10). Together, these results demonstrate that CtDeg is biocompatible and preserves both cellular viability and endogenous RNA dynamics.

Comparison with established RNA imaging systems

To benchmark CtDeg performance against established RNA imaging approaches, we engineered an exogenous SARS-CoV-2 N transcript tagged with 3×MS2 stem-loops, enabling direct comparison with the MS2–MCP system using MCP-tagged 3×BFP (Fig. 4A). Quantitative imaging analysis showed that CtDeg achieved higher signal-to-noise ratios (SNR; Supplementary Equation 1) and lower background fluorescence than conventional CRISPR–fluorescent protein–based systems under the tested conditions, enabling more precise detection of RNA molecules in live cells (Fig. 4A and B). Co-localization analysis further confirmed that CtDeg and MS2–MCP signals localized to identical subcellular compartments, validating accurate RNA targeting (Fig. 4C).

In the absence of guide RNAs, CtDeg exhibited markedly lower single-cell background fluorescence than the CRISPR–Csm system across the tested Cas13 variants (Fig. 4D and E and Supplementary Fig. S11). Upon crRNA targeting, CtDeg achieved higher SNR under the tested conditions while requiring fewer crRNA units to enable detectable imaging compared with the CRISPR–Csm system, which has been reported to employ larger crRNA arrays for visualization of the same transcript. Together, these results indicate that CtDeg provides improved background suppression and efficient target-dependent signal activation under the tested configurations, highlighting its potential advantages for low-background RNA imaging applications.

Live-cell tracking of RNA dynamics using CtDeg

Leveraging the low-background and high-specificity properties of CtDeg, we visualized nuclear paraspeckle dynamics in living cells. A stable Vero E6 cell line expressing n*GFP–dLwaCas13a–G3 was generated and validated by WB (Supplementary Fig. S12A and B). Live-cell imaging revealed NEAT1_2-labeled paraspeckles undergoing pairwise, triple, or quadruple “kiss” events, as well as existing in isolation. Time-lapse imaging at 1-min intervals captured dynamic fusion and fission behaviors (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Movie S1). Quantitative 3D surface reconstruction (Fig. 5A) and fluorescence profiling (Supplementary Fig. S12C and D) indicated rapid initial contact, an extended ∼8-min fusion phase, subsequent fission into distinct structures, and occasional re-fusion, supporting a “fusion/kiss-and-run” model (Fig. 5B) and revealing heterogeneous movement patterns of individual paraspeckles (Fig. 5C and D).

We further applied CtDeg to track cytoplasmic SARS-CoV-2 genomic RNA. Vero E6 cells stably expressing n*GFP–dLwaCas13a–G3 were infected with SARS-CoV-2 trVLPs pre-labeled with SYTO Deep Red dye, enabling dual-color co-localization with CtDeg (Fig. 5E). Time-lapse imaging at 1-min intervals over a 30-min period captured intracellular viral RNA movement, which co-localized with CtDeg fluorescence and maintained stable dual labeling across frames (Fig. 5F and G and Supplementary Fig. S13A and B and Supplementary Movie S2). Imaging at lower temporal resolution (3-min intervals) similarly captured viral RNA trajectories (Supplementary Fig. S13C and D and Supplementary Movie S3). Trajectory and speed analyses revealed both random and directed motion within the cytoplasm, with net movement away from the nucleus and mean velocity of 0.0273 μm/s (Fig. 5H and I and Supplementary Fig. S13B). Interestingly, exposure to transcription- and replication-competent SARS-CoV-2 trVLPs for 4 h led to an increase in paraspeckle number and the frequency of multivalent “kiss” events, suggesting that viral infection perturbs NEAT1_2/paraspeckle dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S14). These results demonstrate that CtDeg enables high-specificity, low-background tracking of both nuclear and cytoplasmic RNAs, providing a versatile platform for real-time visualization of RNA dynamics in living cells.

Discussion

Understanding the spatiotemporal dynamics of RNA is fundamental for elucidating its diverse roles in cellular physiology [7]. Recent advancements in live-cell RNA imaging approaches have significantly improved our ability to visualize RNA dynamics [43]; however, achieving high specificity and low background fluorescence without genetic manipulation of target RNAs remains a major challenge. Current RNA imaging technologies often face the difficulty of robust, target-specific fluorescence due to non-specific binding or intrinsic cellular background signals, hindering the study of RNA dynamics in living cells, especially for structurally complex or low-abundance RNAs. Here, we developed CtDeg, a modular RNA imaging system that combines target RNA activation with tDeg-mediated fluorescence stabilization, enabling robust, target-induced RNA visualization without transcript tagging while maintaining excellent biocompatibility, with negligible cytotoxicity and minimal perturbation of endogenous RNA function.

The CtDeg system suppresses premature Tat–Pepper binding by embedding the “Pepper” RNA motif into the crRNA structure, such that fluorescence is restored only upon target RNA hybridization. Unlike Tat–Pepper-based imaging systems, where spontaneous interaction generates high background fluorescence [44], this structural modification ensures that Tat binding and fluorescence activation occur strictly in a target-dependent manner, thereby reducing background and enhancing specificity. Building on this framework, we systematically engineered and, for the first time, optimized the original tDeg short peptide [19], which enables dual regulation of protein stability by promoting degradation in the absence of Pepper binding while stabilizing proteins when bound. Through the design of multiple Tat peptide–C-terminal degron fusions, we identified variants that more effectively degrade unbound fluorescent proteins and improve target-induced fluorescence output, establishing a foundation for future multiplexed tDeg-based applications.

The flexibility of the CRISPR system underpins its potential for RNA tracking [15]. Although live-cell RNA imaging using dCas13 was first demonstrated in 2017 [17], applications have lagged behind CRISPR-based DNA imaging, partly due to challenges in achieving robust, target-specific fluorescence. Recent studies have expanded the Cas13 family to include orthologs such as LwaCas13a, PspCas13b, and RfxCas13d, offering new possibilities for RNA visualization [40, 45, 46]. Our results demonstrate that CtDeg is compatible not only with different Cas13 orthologs, including dPspCas13b and dRfxCas13d, but also with multiple fluorescent proteins, thereby enabling robust and target-induced imaging across diverse configurations. This dual compatibility underscores the system’s versatility and provides a foundation for extending CtDeg to more complex multiplexed RNA imaging applications. Future efforts to systematically evaluate CtDeg across a broader set of Cas13 variants and reporter proteins could further expand its utility and performance.

Compared to existing CRISPR-based RNA imaging strategies, CtDeg offers a clear conceptual advance by overcoming two long-standing obstacles: high background and scalability limitations. Traditional dCas13–fluorescent protein fusions often accumulate nonspecifically, leading to poor imaging contrast, while array-based systems such as CRISPR–Csm smLiveFISH require dozens of crRNAs to achieve sufficient signal [23]. The single-molecule detection has been reported with arrays of up to 48 crRNAs, but the construction of such repetitive cassettes is technically challenging, often unstable, and difficult to generalize across targets. By contrast, CtDeg achieves robust and specific visualization with a single guide RNA, thereby simplifying system design while maintaining higher SNR than 12× array-based approaches. Such a streamlined architecture expands the accessibility of RNA imaging to a wider range of targets, including moderately sized endogenous transcripts, without the need for extensive engineering.

Tracking the dynamics of large viral RNAs, such as the ∼30 kb SARS-CoV-2 genome, has long been challenging, as their extensive genomes are inherently difficult to modify and highly susceptible to perturbation, making conventional tagging strategies impractical [47]. Using a trVLPs-based infection model [25], we demonstrated that CtDeg enables real-time visualization of SARS-CoV-2 genomic RNA transport immediately after viral entry, thereby providing a non-invasive means to probe the earliest stages of infection. This capability not only overcomes the intrinsic limitations of genome editing but also offers a unique window into virus–host interactions at previously inaccessible time points. Extending beyond viral genomes, CtDeg also proved effective for nuclear long non-coding RNAs, exemplified by the live tracking of NEAT1_2 during paraspeckle organization. The system captured minute-scale fusion and fission events and revealed infection-induced enhanced accumulation and assembly of NEAT1_2, linking RNA dynamics to host responses. Collectively, these applications highlight CtDeg as a versatile live-cell imaging platform, enabling both mechanistic dissection of viral pathogenesis and broader investigations into RNA-mediated regulation of cellular physiology.

Despite its advantages, CtDeg still has aspects that warrant further improvement. While most previous efforts have focused on N-terminal degrons [48, 49], our study addressed the underexplored C-terminal modules, yet the potential of fusing tDeg with N-terminal degrons remains to be systematically investigated. In addition, our design primarily relied on the BIV-derived Tat peptide, whereas diverse Tat variants exist across viruses and may provide opportunities to optimize the balance between degradation efficiency and conditional stabilization [33]. Future directions include extending CtDeg toward multiplexed or single-molecule imaging and performing broader, targeted evaluations across Cas13 orthologs to further enhance its applicability. In summary, CtDeg represents a significant advance in RNA imaging, integrating RNA activation with inducible stabilization to achieve high-fidelity visualization of endogenous transcripts. Its modular design, minimal perturbation, and broad adaptability position it as a promising platform for dissecting RNA localization, dynamics, and function in live cells.
