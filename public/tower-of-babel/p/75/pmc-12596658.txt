Immunological associations in post-infective fatigue syndromes including Long COVID—a systematic review and meta-analysis

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe pathophysiology of post-infective fatigue syndromes (PIFS), including Long COVID, is unknown. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to investigate if PIFS is associated with persistent immune activation.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science were searched for terms related to infection, fatigue, persistent symptoms, and immunological markers. Population: adults and adolescents; Exposure: documented acute infection; Comparator: those who developed PIFS vs. recovered controls from the same exposure; and Outcomes: immunological biomarkers. Studies which documented acute infection, applied diagnostic criteria for PIFS, and assayed circulating immunologic markers were eligible.FindingsFrom 14,985 studies screened, 30 articles were included (n = 5102 participants; 833 PIFS/PIFS-like cases, n = 4269 recovered control participants) with many studies excluded by inadequate quality in eligibility criteria. The meta-analysis (11 studies; n = 413 PIFS cases, analysed with random-effects models) showed PIFS cases had increased: white cell counts at 3–6 months (Cohen's d: 0.41, 95% CI 0.09–0.74); and circulating levels of RANTES and TNFα at 6–12 months (Cohen's d: 0.45 [95% CI 0.16–0.73] and 0.30 [95% CI 0.04–0.57], respectively) compared to controls recovered from the same exposure.InterpretationThese findings provide cautious support for persistent immune activation in PIFS, but warrant further replication. Research in context

Evidence before this study

The pathophysiology of post-infective conditions, including post-infective fatigue syndrome (PIFS) and Long COVID, is poorly understood. The infective onset with the stereotyped inflammation-driven acute sickness response, and the subsequent persistence of these symptoms suggest that an ongoing immunologic component may play a role. Many studies have investigated this immunologic component, but results have often been inconclusive or even contradictory. The recent increase in PIFS research due to Long COVID provides an opportunity for better insights into the pathophysiology of the condition.

This review and meta-analysis aimed to investigate if PIFS is associated with persistent immune activation. PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science were searched (until 10/09/2024) for terms related to infection, fatigue, persistent symptoms, and immunological markers. Studies were eligible if they: reliably diagnosed the acute infection, formally applied criteria for subsequent carefully characterised PIFS, and investigated circulating immunologic markers up to 12-months post-infection. Studies were included if they were written in English, Norwegian, Swedish, Spanish, German, or Dutch. Each study was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute tool to appraise study design, and the BIOCROSS tool to evaluate laboratory methodology.

Added value of this study

Unique to this review, to ensure adequate case characterisation, we designated levels of confidence in the reliability of the diagnosis of the acute infection (confirmed or probable) and the syndromal diagnosis of PIFS (PIFS or PIFS-like) for each study. The systematic review included 30 high-quality papers of 14,985 screened–indicative of many studies being excluded by inadequate quality in eligibility criteria. The included studies measured a broad range of immunologic markers, including cytokines, leucocyte gene expression, clinical inflammatory markers, immune cell subsets and activation status, immunoglobulins and autoantibodies, and pathogen-specific antibody and T-cell responses. The meta-analyses found that only the white blood cell (WBC) count, as well as circulating levels of RANTES and tumour necrosis factor (TNF)α were significantly increased in PIFS cases compared to recovered control participants, but only in a small number of studies and at selected timepoints.

Implications of all the available evidence

This review identified biomarkers of interest which warrant further exploration in relation to post-infective sequalae (WBC count, RANTES, TNFα). This review highlighted the need to strengthen the methodology to ensure high-quality PIFS studies, including development of an operationalised PIFS case definition ensuring documentation of the acute infection and characterisation of PIFS caseness with medical, psychiatric, and laboratory assessments.

Introduction

Persistent symptoms following the febrile phase of acute infectious diseases are common and associated with substantial morbidity and health care burden.1 In a subset of infected patients, post-infective symptoms such as fatigue can last 6 months or longer, and remain disabling. If the symptom complex cannot be explained by thorough clinical assessment and laboratory investigations, a diagnosis of a post-infective fatigue syndrome (PIFS) may be made.2 Data from prospective cohorts indicate that the proportion of patients who develop PIFS varies by pathogen, with a relatively high six month point prevalence following Epstein–Barr virus (infectious mononucleosis) and SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) infections of over 10%, and a lower prevalence after minor upper respiratory tract infections.2, 3, 4, 5 The severity of the acute infective illness is a risk factor for subsequent PIFS.6, 7, 8, 9, 10

The pathophysiology of PIFS remains elusive, and the diagnosis relies on the application of syndromal case criteria rather than objective measures such as biomarkers. To diagnose PIFS, the recommended approach has been to document the acute infection and apply one of the diagnostic criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS)2,11, 12, 13, 14, 15 or a similar syndrome (e.g. Post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome)16,17 following the acute infection (Supplementary Table S1). Post Q-fever fatigue syndrome18 and Post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome16 have closely comparable diagnostic criteria, but also require exclusion of ongoing infection (e.g. following an adequate course of appropriate antibiotic treatment).1 With the recognition that SARS-CoV-2 infection also acts as a trigger for PIFS,4 constituting the major proportion of those diagnosed with Long COVID,19,20 there has been growing interest in the opportunity provided by the COVID-19 pandemic to investigate the pathophysiology of PIFS. In combination, these studies may inform our understanding of the likely heterogeneous condition of ME/CFS.5

The symptoms of acute infectious diseases arise from two processes: firstly, the acute sickness response; and secondly, the consequences of tissue invasion by the pathogen and associated local host response. The physiological (e.g. fever), behavioural (e.g. excessive sleep, avoidance of exertion), and psychological (e.g. cognitive impairment, mood disturbance) manifestations of the acute sickness response are driven by the host immune response to the pathogen. These manifestations are stereotyped across many viral and non-viral infections,21,22 and are highly conserved across species.22 In contrast, the consequences of tissue invasion by the pathogen are often specific for each pathogen, based on the tissue site(s) infected by the pathogen and the associated pattern of inflammation; for example, the sore throat and tender lymph nodes reflecting tonsillar replication of the virus and local inflammation in acute Epstein–Barr virus infection, or cough and dyspnoea reflecting respiratory tract infection and inflammation triggered by SARS-CoV-2. The acute sickness response serves a fundamental evolutionary purpose in that the physiological and behavioural changes create a favourable environment for clearance of the pathogen (e.g. elevated body temperature to inhibit pathogen replication).21,23 Production of key inflammatory mediators such as the interferons (IFN), interleukin (IL)-1β, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)α, and IL-6 have been associated with the symptom pattern (e.g. fatigue, pain, mood disturbance) and the overall severity of the acute sickness response.7,23

Persistent infection with the replicating pathogen has been convincingly ruled out in PIFS,1 although non-viable pathogen remnants (antigens or nucleic acids) which may drive ongoing immune activation have been found in some PIFS disorders, including of Coxiella burnetii in some studies of Q-fever fatigue syndrome,24, 25, 26, 27 and of SARS-CoV-2 following COVID-19.28,29 Following primary Epstein–Barr virus infection, where natural persistence via establishment of latency and intermittent reactivation is the norm, no association has been found between viral replication and ongoing symptoms.27

By contrast, continuation of the cardinal symptoms of the acute sickness response after the replicating pathogen has been cleared suggests that PIFS could arise from persistent immune activation.5 This notion has been examined in numerous case–control studies seeking associations with circulating biomarkers indicative of inflammation, but with generally small sample sizes and inconsistent or inconclusive findings. Accordingly, this systematic review and meta-analysis collated and synthesised the published evidence investigating the hypothesis that analogous to the acute sickness response, PIFS is a largely stereotyped condition independent of the specific initiating infection, associated with persistent immune activation.

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines and the Synthesis without meta-analysis (SWiM) in systematic reviews reporting guidelines were utilised for this systematic review and meta-analysis.30,31 The protocol was registered in PROSPERO, the international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42021281161).

Eligibility

Eligibility was formulated using the PECO framework32– Population: adults and adolescents; Exposure: documented acute infection; Comparator: people who developed PIFS compared to recovered controls from the same exposure; and Outcomes: immunological biomarkers up to 12 months post-exposure. Human observational (e.g. cohort, case–control, cross-sectional) studies on PIFS were eligible if the study: i) documented the acute infection according to pre-defined criteria (Table 1), ii) applied PIFS caseness 6–12 months after acute infection according to diagnostic criteria (Table 1), iii) measured immunological markers (outcomes of interest) up to 12 months post infection onset, iv) measured fatigue using a validated multi-item (at least three) questionnaire, or by interview (e.g. comprehensive clinical assessment), captured between 3 and 12 months post infection onset, v) had a control group (i.e. healthy, recovered, or positive controls—those with persisting symptoms not attributable to a PIFS or PIFS-like illness), vi) had a minimum sample size of 5 participants, and vii) were written in English, Norwegian, Swedish, Spanish, German, or Dutch. Several criteria sets were accepted for PIFS diagnoses including the Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) criteria for Post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome, the Dutch Guideline for post Q-fever fatigue syndrome, and various ME/CFS criteria including Fukuda or CDC94, Canadian Consensus, and Institute of Medicine criteria11, 12, 13, 14,16,18 (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1). The level of confidence in the acute infection diagnosis was categorised as level 1 (i.e. confirmed) or level 2 (i.e. probable acute infection). Confidence in the PIFS diagnosis was classified as level 1 (i.e. PIFS — chronic fatigue state meeting ME/CFS or relevant PIFS diagnostic criteria, including with: history taking, assessment of mental health, physical examination, and recommended laboratory investigations) or level 2 (i.e. PIFS-like – chronic fatigue state meeting ME/CFS or relevant PIFS diagnostic criteria, with one or more of the following missing: history taking, assessment of mental health, physical examination, or recommended laboratory investigations) (Table 1). Long COVID was included as PIFS or PIFS-like within the framework described above. Studies were excluded if they enrolled participants with chronic infection (e.g. persistent hepatitis C viraemia for ≥6 months) or were animal or in vitro studies. Narrative and systematic reviews, and opinion papers were excluded. Search strategy

PubMed (Medline), EMBASE, Web of Science were searched using terms related to infection, fatigue, persistent symptoms, and immunological markers until 10/09/2024. The search strategy was developed with assistance from a scientific librarian (Supplementary Table S3). Where available, database-specific filters were used to identify publications in humans.

Results from the database searches were imported into EndNote (×9) and duplicates were removed.33 The remaining articles were imported into Covidence34 and screened by two independent reviewers for eligibility by title and abstract, followed by full-text review. Any discrepancies were resolved by consultation with other authors.

Quality appraisal

Eligible articles were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality appraisal tools specific to each study design.35 To assess the quality of the study design in relation to the biomarker data, the BIOCROSS quality appraisal tool was applied with both the original (possible range 0–20) and a modified version (to better fit the purposes of this review, possible range 0–28).36 For the modified version, four items were added to the BIOCROSS: item 11: sample handling and preparation, item 12: assay methods and data reporting, item 13: design and analysis of biomarker measurement, and item 14: biomarker measurement in the control group—to comprehensively evaluate longitudinal biomarker assays and data analysis approaches (Supplementary Table S4). A threshold score of 70% was used for the BIOCROSS to identify high-quality studies.37 Two reviewers conducted the quality appraisal independently, the results were compared, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer.

Data extraction and statistical analysis

The following data were extracted from the included studies: country of origin, study design, sample size, sample information (e.g. age, infective aetiology and means of acute infection diagnosis, PIFS diagnosis), time point(s) of measurement since infection, control or comparison group, fatigue and functional impairment assessment, study aims, biomarker assays, outcomes or results, and statistical analysis methods. Authors of studies with missing data were contacted to obtain additional information. Each article was ultimately classified according to both the characterisation of the acute infection as confirmed (Level 1 confidence) or probable (Level 2 confidence); and 2) the application of the PIFS diagnostic criteria, as PIFS (Level 1 confidence); or PIFS-like (Level 2 confidence) (Table 1). For the analysis, studies were grouped by biomarker type, regardless of level of confidence in acute infection or diagnosis criteria, including six biomarker types: cytokines and chemokines, leukocyte gene expression, clinical inflammatory markers, immune cell subsets and activation status, immunoglobulins and autoantibodies, and pathogen-specific antibody and T-cell responses.

The following data were extracted for the meta-analysis: infective aetiology, characteristics of the study population, quality appraisal information (see Quality appraisal–above), and the biomarker data at pre-determined time windows following infection: 0–1 month (termed here baseline or acute infection phase), 1–3 months (early post-infective phase), 3–6 months (PIFS caseness phase), and 6–12 months (chronic phase). The biomarker data were unadjusted when available, or from multivariable-adjusted analyses if not.

If the biomarker concentrations and their variance were only reported in a figure, measures of central tendency (e.g. mean, median) and the measure of dispersion (e.g. standard deviation, interquartile range), were estimated from the y-axis of the graph, wherever possible, with WebPlotDigitizer V4 software (https://automeris.io). Data reported as relative concentrations or fold changes were only considered for narrative descriptions.

In order to reduce heterogeneity in the meta-analyses to identify biomarkers associated with PIFS, only those studies that reported similar biomarkers (outcomes), measured at similar timepoints, and compared with a recovered control group were included. No distinction was made in relation to the triggering acute infection leading to PIFS. Data from different biomarker studies from the same cohort were not analysed together at overlapping timepoints. For the meta-analyses, median and interquartile ranges were converted to mean and standard deviation using an approximation method described by Wan et al.38 Standard error of the mean and confidence intervals of the mean were converted to standard deviation using methods described in the Cochrane handbook.39

Meta-analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0.1.1, using an inverse variance-weighted random-effects model. P-values ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. The difference between means was measured with Cohen's d and results were interpreted based on the effect size (i.e. 0.2 low effect, 0.5 medium effect, and 0.8 high effect). Heterogeneity was assessed with I2 statistic, using ≥70% as a cut-off score for high heterogeneity.39 Where possible, sensitivity analyses were performed based on age and sex distribution. Publication bias of statistically significant results was assessed with Egger's regression test and visualised with funnel plots.

Role of funders

Funders had no role in the design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, or writing of the study.

Results

Study selection

Database searches identified 25,291 articles, and after removing duplicates (10,306), and screening of title and abstracts (14,985), 490 full-text articles were reviewed (Fig. 1). Of these 490 articles, 460 (94%) had inadequate PIFS or PIFS-like case characterisation, including 25 which also had inadequately confirmed acute infection (Fig. 1). The original cohort recruited a total of 4501 participants of whom n = 238 developed an infection.

Quality appraisal

The JBI quality appraisal checklist outcomes for each study are detailed in Supplementary Table S5. The limitations in study quality were mainly related to adequacy of follow-up data for cohort studies, and identifying and dealing with confounding factors for case–control studies. The original BIOCROSS quality appraisal tool scores ranged from 8 to 16 (possible 0–20), and the modified BIOCROSS from 10 to 23 (range 0–28) (Supplementary Table S4). According to this tool, studies included in this review were assessed as being of ‘acceptable’ to ‘good’ quality, as n = 17 and n = 14 studies scored ≥70% on the original and the modified BIOCROSS, respectively.37 Studies were not excluded based on either of the quality appraisal tools; however, quality scores were taken into consideration in the interpretation of the findings.

Diagnosis of acute infection

Most studies (n = 22) were classified as having confirmed acute infection (Level 1) (acute and convalescent pathogen-specific serology (n = 12),15,26,27,48, 49, 50, 51, 52,54,59,60,64 polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection of the pathogen at acute infection (n = 8),10,44,46,47,58,61, 62, 63 or acute pathogen-specific serology (n = 2).43,57 The remaining eight studies were designated as Level 2 probable (e.g. Monospot positive, late pathogen-specific serology) acute infection (Table 2).4,40, 41, 42,45,53,55,56

Diagnosis of PIFS

Most studies (n = 21) reported evidence to support having reliably classified cases as PIFS (Level 1—confirmed).4,10,26,27,40, 41, 42,44,46,47,50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56,59, 60, 61,63 A total of nine studies only classified cases as PIFS-like (Level 2–probable), as no clinical or laboratory assessments were undertaken to exclude alternative explanations for the ongoing symptoms.15,43,45,48,49,57,58,62,64

Cytokines and chemokines

There were 16 studies which investigated levels of circulating cytokines and chemokines in plasma or serum,4,10,41,42,45,46,48,52,53,55,58,60, 61, 62, 63, 64 of which eight used a multiplex immunoassay panel, six used ELISAs and one study did not describe the assay used45 (Tables 2 and 3). Data from eight of these studies were included in a meta-analysis (Table 4).10,42,46,52,58,61, 62, 63

In the meta-analysis, circulating levels of both the chemokine, RANTES (also known as CCL5) and the pro-inflammatory cytokine, TNFα were found to be significantly increased in cases with PIFS compared to recovered control participants in samples collected at 6–12 months (Fig. 2A and B).46,52,62 For RANTES, the difference (Cohen's d 0.45 [95% Confidence Interval (CI) 0.16–0.73, P < 0.05, I2 = 0.31), was based on two studies (post-Epstein-Barr virus52 and post-SARS-CoV-2,46 each with modified BIOCROSS scores of 64%) with PIFS cases (n = 58) and recovered control participants (n = 324), and with a low risk of publication bias (Egger's test intercept 0.26, P = 0.44). For TNFα the difference (Cohen's d 0.30 [95% CI 0.04–0.57, P = 0.02, I2 = 0.58) was based on two studies (both post-SARS-CoV-2) (modified BIOCROSS scores 50%62 and 64%46) with PIFS (n = 83) and recovered control participants (n = 339), and with a low risk of publication bias (Egger's test intercept −0.00, P = 0.99) (Table 5, Fig. 2A and B, and Supplementary Figures S1C and S6).46,52,62 No significant differences were identified in data from samples collected at 0–1 month or 3–6 months (Table 5 and Supplementary Figure S1B). Leucocyte gene expression

There were five studies that investigated gene expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC)s.47,49,51,54,59 Of those studies, two assayed transcripts using RNA sequencing (RNAseq)47,49 and three used microarrays51,54,59 (Tables 2 and 3). There were no data suitable for meta-analysis.

The systematic review showed no consistent significant findings were described when comparing cases with PIFS to recovered control participants (Table 3).47,49,51,59

Clinical inflammatory markers

There were 11 studies that investigated routine clinical inflammatory markers including high-sensitivity (hs) C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), or white blood cell (WBC) count with a leucocyte differential (Tables 2 and 3).4,10,15,40,43, 44, 45, 46,48,57,58,63

Seven of these studies were included in a meta-analysis (Table 4).10,15,40,46,57,58,63 In the meta-analysis, WBC count was found to be significantly increased in PIFS cases compared to recovered control participants in samples collected at 3–6 months (Cohen's d 0.42 [95% CI 0.09–0.73, P = 0.01, I2 = 0.36], based on three studies (all post-Epstein-Barr virus15,40,57) (modified BIOCROSS scores–75%–79%) with PIFS cases (n = 48) and recovered control participants (n = 237), with low risk of publication bias (Egger's test intercept 0.70, P = 0.57) (Table 5, Fig. 2C, and Supplementary Figures 2C, S5, and S6).15,40,46,57 No significant differences in WBC count were identified in samples collected at 0–1 month (Table 5 and Supplementary Figure S2A). Sensitivity analyses for age and sex distribution yielded similar results (Supplementary Figure S5).

The systematic review revealed that two studies found higher neutrophil counts in PIFS cases vs. recovered control participants at 12–24 months.40,63 This was observed following Epstein–Barr virus (n = 13 PIFS cases compared to n = 12 recovered control participants at 24 months, modified BIOCROSS score–79%) (6.13 vs. 5.45, P = 0.02).40 Similarly in SARS-CoV-2 infection (n = 13 PIFS cases compared to n = 17 recovered control participants at 12 months, modified BIOCROSS score–50%; heterogeneity in timepoint and sex distribution) (4.10 vs. 2.90, P = 0.02).63 There were no differences in either study at earlier timepoints (Tables 2 and 3, and Supplementary Table S4).40,63 Three studies (number of PIFS cases—n = 10–53, modified BIOCROSS score >70%–1 of 3 studies,15 heterogeneity in age and sex distribution) found no differences in total WBC or differential counts between cases with PIFS and recovered control participants (Tables 2 and 3, and Supplementary Table S4).15,46,58 No other consistent significant findings in leucocyte differential counts were described when comparing cases with PIFS to recovered control participants or healthy control participants (Table 3).4,10,43,45,48

Immune cell subsets and activation status

There were eight studies that investigated immune cell subsets, with or without assessments of functionality/activation, by flow cytometry (Tables 2 and 3).15,26,27,43,53,56,57,63

Two of these studies were included in a meta-analysis (Table 4).15,57 No significant differences between PIFS cases and recovered control participants were identified for cluster of differentiation (CD)4 and CD8 lymphocyte subsets at 3–6 months (Table 5 and Supplementary Figure S3).

The systematic review also revealed three studies (number of PIFS cases—n = 6–35, modified BIOCROSS score >70%–2 of 3 studies,15,27 heterogeneity in sex distribution) which found no differences in frequencies of lymphocyte subsets including natural killer (NK)-cells or their activation status between cases with PIFS and recovered control participants or healthy control participants (Tables 2 and 3, and Supplementary Table S4).15,26,27 No consistent significant findings were described when comparing cases with PIFS to recovered control participants (Table 3).43,56,63

Immunoglobulins and autoantibodies

There were nine studies that investigated immunoglobulin levels as well as autoantibodies as predictors of PIFS (Tables 2 and 3).4,10,43, 44, 45,53,57,62,63 There were no data suitable for meta-analysis.

The systematic review revealed no consistent findings when comparing cases with PIFS to recovered control participants or healthy control participants (Table 3).4,10,43, 44, 45,53,62,63

Pathogen-specific antibody and T-cell responses

There were 10 studies that investigated responses in pathogen-specific serology, including Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISA), or Enzyme-Linked Immunospot Assays (ELISpot) as well as Monospot and Interferon-Gamma Release Assays (IGRAs) (Tables 2 and 3).10,15,26,27,43,46,50,57,63,64

Two of these studies were included in a meta-analysis (Table 4).15,57 No significant differences between PIFS cases and recovered control participants were identified for pathogen-specific serology at 3–6 months (Table 5 and Supplementary Figure S4).

The systematic review identified five studies (number of PIFS cases–n = 6–107, modified BIOCROSS score >70%–2 of 5 studies,27,43 heterogeneity in age and sex distribution) which found no differences in pathogen-specific antibody responses between cases with PIFS and recovered control participants (Tables 2 and 3, and Supplementary Table S4).26,27,43,46,64 No consistent significant findings were described when comparing cases with PIFS to recovered control participants (Table 3).15,27,63

Discussion

The systematic review included 30 high-quality papers measuring a broad range of immunologic markers, including cytokines and chemokines, leucocyte gene expression, clinical inflammatory markers, immune cell subsets and activation status, immunoglobulins and autoantibodies, and pathogen-specific antibody and T-cell responses. The systematic review of these studies revealed largely inconsistent or negative findings between groups and with heterogeneity in the age and sex distribution of participants. The meta-analyses found that only the WBC count, as well as circulating levels of RANTES and TNFα were significantly increased in PIFS cases compared to recovered control participants but only in a small number of studies and at selected timepoints. These findings generally support our hypothesis that PIFS is associated with persistent immune activation.

There was a very high proportion of potentially eligible studies (94%) which were considered ineligible on the basis of inadequate study methodology, including both documentation of the acute infection and characterisation of PIFS or PIFS-like illness caseness. It is perplexing why studies seeking biomarkers associated with post-infective sequelae would fail to document the acute infective trigger. It should be noted however that the majority of the 25 studies excluded on this basis (19, 76%) were cross-sectional case–control studies conducted in the post-infective window and so were likely reliant on non-protocolised data from clinical records for diagnostic laboratory tests conducted during the acute illness. It is disappointing that so many potentially eligible studies made no protocolised effort to assign PIFS or PIFS-like caseness, with the latter only requiring application of validated questionnaires for the key symptom domains such as fatigue, neurocognitive difficulties (‘brain fog’), sleep disturbance, and mood disturbance as well as a measure of disability. In relation to post-SARS CoV-2 studies this outcome partly reflects the slowly emerging recognition that Long COVID is a variant of PIFS, and partly a consequence of the use of the WHO Post COVID-19 condition (Long COVID) clinical case definition,67 which is yet to be operationalised for research purposes, although recommended instrumentation has been published.5,68

The biomarkers identified in the meta-analyses were modest in effect size and consistency across studies, arose from multiple comparisons, and should therefore be carefully interpreted. Each of the three biomarkers (WBC count, circulating levels of RANTES and TNFα) were elevated in participants with PIFS in comparison to participants who had recovered uneventfully from the same triggering acute infection. The key caveats to these data are firstly that although the symptom set in PIFS is typically very stable in pattern over time (e.g. predominant fatigue, brain fog),7 the biomarker associations were not found at each time window in the longitudinal post-infective illness course up until 12 months following infection. Studies assessing biomarkers later in the follow-up period may be of interest. Although, it should be noted that inflammation and immune changes can also be influenced due to lifestyle factors (e.g., sleep disturbance, psychological distress, reduced physical activity). Secondly, as the included studies spanned from 2001 to 2024 and were performed by different research groups, the laboratory methods varied in details of the assay system and the likely sensitivity (e.g. microarray vs. RNA sequencing technologies for gene expression, but also within the cytokine and chemokine, and serology assessments). Thirdly, due to a low number of studies per biomarker and timepoint, a meta-regression or sensitivity analysis of highest-quality studies could not be performed, a sensitivity analysis for age and sex distribution was only possible for a subset of biomarkers and timepoints (Supplementary Figure S5), noting that participants were predominantly female, consistent with literature from other chronic fatigue states, and that the studies covered adolescents through to adults. Additionally, it should also be noted that not all studies included post-exertional malaise as a core symptom criterion, which some regard as an essential component of the syndromal diagnosis.14,69

Further avenues for systematic investigation of biomarkers in PIFS may be evident among the Long COVID papers deemed ineligible for inclusion here, as no formal PIFS or even a PIFS-like case definition was applied. These studies did include several which applied the WHO Long COVID clinical case definition, utilised validated questionnaires as well as clinical assessments and laboratory investigation to exclude alternative explanatory conditions, and undertook immunological biomarker investigations.70, 71, 72, 73 These studies did not identify the same biomarkers associated with Long COVID as those reported in the meta-analysis (WBC, neutrophil count, circulating levels of RANTES and TNFα), but did report increases in frequencies of SARS CoV-2 specific CD4 and CD8 T cells in those with Long COVID in some but not all studies.70, 71, 72, 73 A separate systematic review on biomarkers in Long COVID, with little mention of Long COVID caseness, also found TNFα to be increased compared to recovered controls.74

While most immunological research on PIFS has focused on the convenient samples of circulating serum, plasma or peripheral blood mononuclear cells, alternative sites of inflammation such as in skeletal muscle, or the central nervous system may play a role and may not be reflected in circulating markers.75,76 In this regard, the modest elevation in circulating levels of RANTES and TNFα may be consistent with neuroinflammation. These factors are also recognised to induce metabolic dysfunction and endothelial changes which are proposed mechanisms in Long COVID.77 Further, recent findings by independent research groups studying other biomarkers in Long COVID include reports of differences in tryptophan breakdown products such as kynurenine in a possible neuropathogenesis, possibly via overexpression of inflammation induced indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO)2.78,79 Complement dysregulation was also found to be related to Long COVID by independent groups.80,81

The strength of this review lies in the inclusion of high-quality PIFS studies with appropriate control groups, robust study designs, and reliable laboratory methods. There was a good representation of negative findings in the systematic review and publication bias was deemed low with Egger's regression tests in the meta-analysis synthesis. Given the presumed pathophysiological link with the host response in acute infection, carefully considered levels of confidence in the reliability of the diagnosis of the acute infection and the syndromal diagnosis of PIFS (confirmed or probable) were applied. Recognising that inflammation and immune activation after acute infection are likely to be dynamic, measurements were clustered into well-matched time windows post-infection for meta-analysis. Nevertheless, it should be noted that selected research groups including those of the authors were over-represented in this systematic review and meta-analysis. This stringent approach resulted in small group sizes for analysis of individual biomarkers (ranging from 10 to 53 participants), and revealed residual heterogeneity in available markers and timepoints, ultimately limiting availability of data for meta-analysis.

This review highlights the recommendation that to increase the amount of high-quality PIFS studies, a uniform PIFS case definition should be developed that includes criteria for both adequate documentation of the diagnosis the acute infection and characterisation of PIFS caseness via protocolised clinical and laboratory assessments. Immunologic measurements should be completed longitudinally with careful attention to methodological rigour in timepoint selection, sample collection and handling, as well as assay platforms. The inclusion of a comparison group who have recovered from the same infection, and ideally an additional matched healthy control group, is strongly recommended.82 The international Collaborative On Fatigue and related symptoms Following Infection (COFFI) (www.coffi-collaborative.com) is developing research diagnostic criteria for PIFS to support this process.2

Conclusion

The immunologic hypothesis for PIFS has gained cautious support from this metanalysis. PIFS may also be driven by other disease mechanisms, such as metabolic processes and/or functional brain alterations.7,83 The current surge in PIFS research due to Long COVID holds potential to finally understand the pathophysiology of PIFS. Future studies should adhere to the high-quality standards for which this paper provides a benchmark, including careful documentation of the acute infection and the strict application of at least one PIFS research case definition.
