Successful long-term treatment of tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis with mycophenolate
Authors: Liam Qi, Shaun Chandler, Prianka Puri, Kirsten Hepburn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis (TINU) syndrome is characterised by bilateral anterior uveitis and acute kidney injury secondary to interstitial nephritis. Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of therapy. There is little data around the use of steroid-sparing agents.We present a female in her sixth decade of life of Caucasian ethnicity who presented with acute kidney failure requiring dialysis from TINU. Systemic corticosteroids were commenced; however, she developed a cushingoid appearance, proximal myopathy and hyperglycaemia. Mycophenolate mofetil was initiated as a steroid-sparing agent with significant improvement in kidney function. She remains well 2.5 years later. Incidentally, she was noted to have a distant history of vulval sarcoidosis without any other evidence of active disease on imaging and a normal serum angiotensin-converting enzyme.This case demonstrates successful long-term outcomes of mycophenolate in the management of TINU.

Background

Tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis (TINU) syndrome was described in 1975 by Dobrin et al and has become increasingly recognised clinically and in the literature.1 It is characterised by acute interstitial nephritis with bilateral uveitis that is immune-mediated, which may be idiopathic or precipitated by infection or drugs.2 The prevalence of TINU is <0.1%–2% in all age populations, with up to 2.3% in the paediatric population.3 Diagnosis may be difficult as nephritis and uveitis only occur concurrently in 15%, with ocular symptoms following the onset of nephritis in most patients (65%), with the remaining having ocular symptoms preceding nephritis.4 Treatment for TINU consists of high-dose corticosteroids. The use of steroid-sparing agents is limited to case reports.5

Mycophenolate inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase to limit purine metabolism, inhibiting lymphocyte proliferation, T- and B-cell maturation, antigen presentation and impairing innate immune system recruitment.6 TINU syndrome is hypothesised to be a T-cell-mediated reaction, with CD8+ and predominantly CD4+T cells infiltrating kidney tubular epithelial cells present on kidney biopsies.4 7 Uveitis is hypothesised to occur as kidney tubular and ciliary body epithelia share several functions and may share antigens.4 This case describes the successful long-term outcomes of using mycophenolate mofetil as a steroid-sparing agent for TINU in an adult patient.

Case presentation

A female in her sixth decade of life and of Caucasian ethnicity presented with oliguric severe acute kidney injury, following successful treatment of right eye anterior uveitis 2 months prior with topical atropine 1% and dexamethasone 0.1% eye drops. Her primary symptoms were progressive generalised weakness, fatigue, exercise intolerance and 4 kg of unintentional weight loss over 2 months due to nausea, early satiety and decreased appetite. She did not experience any arthralgias, rash, fevers, haematuria or pulmonary symptoms. She declined further investigations at the time of uveitis presentation; however, she had been noted to be human leukocyte antigen B27 positive as part of her uveitis workup.

Regular medications included calcium supplementation and fish oil daily. She had no known allergies and did not use other non-prescribed medications or complementary medications. She had three COVID-19 vaccinations before her presentation (2× AstraZeneca and 1× Pfizer). There was no history of smoking, significant alcohol consumption, recreational drug use or notable occupational exposures.

She was haemodynamically stable, afebrile, and her clinical examination was unremarkable, besides an indwelling urinary catheter inserted to monitor urine output.

Investigations

Initial biochemistry revealed urea of 56.3 mmol/L, creatinine 1586 µmol/L, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 2 mL/min/1.73 m2, metabolic acidosis with a bicarbonate of 12 mmol/L and a corrected calcium of 2.43 mmol/L. She had normal kidney function 2 years (creatinine 73 µmol/L and eGFR 73 mL/min/1.73 m2) and 2 months before presentation (creatinine 81 µmol/L and eGFR 65 mL/min/1.73 m2). At presentation, urinalysis showed mild leucocytosis with subnephrotic range proteinuria, no haematuria and no monoclonal immunoglobulins or Bence Jones proteins (table 1).

CT of the abdomen and pelvis showed no kidney calculi, hydronephrosis or kidney lesions. A full glomerulonephritis and autoimmune screen were unremarkable. Incidentally, she was found to be deficient in vitamin B12 and was commenced on replacement.

An echocardiogram revealed normal left ventricular size and function with an estimated ejection fraction of 58%. Right ventricular size and function were normal, and atrial dimensions were normal. There were no significant valvular abnormalities.

A kidney biopsy was performed, and light microscopy showed 35 glomeruli per slide with 17 (49%) globally sclerosed (figure 1). Some of the non-sclerosed glomeruli appeared hypoperfused with tuft shrinkage and wrinkling of the glomerular capillary walls. There was no segmental sclerosis, endocapillary or mesangial hypercellularity, crescents, necrotising lesions, vasculitis or thrombotic microangiopathy identified in the sample. There was approximately 30% tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis. Prominent interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration was present, which consisted mostly of lymphocytes with a small number of plasma cells, and eosinophils were also present. Granulomata were not identified, and there were extensive features of acute tubular injury.

There was no reactivity on immunofluorescence in the glomeruli or the tubular basement membranes for IgG, IgA, IgG, C3, C1q, kappa light chain and lambda light chain. Electron microscopy was not performed.

In summary, the kidney biopsy was consistent with interstitial nephritis with acute tubular injury, and given the clinical history of uveitis, could represent tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis (TINU). The degree of global sclerosis and tubular atrophy was surprising and was thought to represent old vascular scarring.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnoses for both nephritis and uveitis were broad, including TINU, sarcoidosis, Sjögren syndrome, Beçhet’s disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, granulomatosis with polyangiitis or infectious diseases such as tuberculosis, brucellosis, toxoplasmosis and histoplasmosis. Given that antineutrophil antibody, extractable nuclear antigens and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies were negative, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren syndrome and granulomatosis with polyangiitis were unlikely. QuantiFERON testing excluded tuberculosis, and there were no infectious symptoms or travel history to suggest another infectious aetiology. There were no oral aphthae to suggest Beçhet’s disease, leaving TINU and sarcoidosis as possible diagnoses.

Sarcoidosis typically presents with elevated immunoglobulin levels, non-caseating granulomata and usually elevated angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE).8 Ocular involvement with sarcoidosis generally presents with bilateral granulomatous anterior uveitis. Kidney involvement in sarcoidosis can involve granulomatous interstitial nephritis, kidney calculi or nephrocalcinosis secondary to hypercalcaemia.8 9 These features of granulomatous inflammation and hypercalcaemia were absent in our patient, making TINU more likely.

Treatment

Acute haemodialysis was commenced on the fourth day of admission due to severe acidosis and oliguria not responding to pharmacological management. Further symptoms of acute uveitis recurred, and following ophthalmology consultation, 0.1% topical dexamethasone was commenced. Intravenous methylprednisolone 1.5 g in total was given daily over 3 days, and transitioned to oral prednisolone at 1 mg/kg. She remained dialysis dependent for a total of 14 days. She was discharged with an improving urine output and kidney indices (creatinine 422 µmol/L and eGFR 9 mL/min/1.73 m2).

Unfortunately, steroid complications developed after 2.5 months of treatment, including cushingoid appearance, proximal myopathy and hyperglycaemia. Mycophenolate was commenced as a steroid-sparing agent and uptitrated to 1 g two times per day, while prednisolone was weaned until it was ceased over 4 months for a total duration of 6.5 months. Despite immunosuppression for 6 months, her kidney impairment persisted (creatinine 162 µmol/L and eGFR of 28 mL/min/1.73 m2). A repeat kidney biopsy was performed to understand if there was ongoing interstitial nephritis or established fibrosis and guide immunosuppression. The second biopsy had only six glomeruli but was adequate to show persisting tubulointerstitial nephritis with lymphocytic inflammation and tubulitis, without interstitial fibrosis or tubular atrophy (IFTA 0%; figure 2). It was noted that the first kidney biopsy had more IFTA compared with the second. The opinion from the kidney histopathologist was that the pattern of sclerosis and tubular atrophy in the original biopsy was restricted to a confluent zone and therefore represented old vascular scarring. Immunosuppression was continued and mycophenolate was further increased to 3 g daily, but gastrointestinal side effects prompted a transition to mycophenolate sodium 720 mg three times a day. Kidney function improved significantly by 18 months (figure 3). At this time, a history of a vulval lesion with granulomatous changes consistent with vulval sarcoidosis was revealed, which was treated with diprosone ointment 5 years earlier. She was reluctant to pursue further investigation. Given that she had no lymphadenopathy within the limits of a non-contrast scan, a chest X-ray revealing no granulomata with no other clinical manifestations of sarcoidosis, the diagnosis of TINU was maintained.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient’s kidney function had improved 2.5 years postdiagnosis with a creatinine of 100 µmol/L and eGFR 49 mL/min/1.73 m2, and mycophenolate has been weaned to 360 mg mane and 180 mg nocte (figure 3).

Discussion

This case describes the successful long-term treatment of TINU with a previous history of vulval sarcoidosis with mycophenolate due to corticosteroid complications, highlighting both diagnostic and treatment challenges.

Ocular inflammation of TINU responds promptly to topical corticosteroids and kidney disease usually responds to systemic corticosteroids.4 Long-term kidney prognosis is favourable for patients with TINU treated with systemic corticosteroids and recurrence is uncommon, with approximately 60% of patients having a normal eGFR at follow-up and around 15% having permanent kidney dysfunction.45 1012 In contrast, ocular disease recurs in up to 50% of patients after topical corticosteroid treatment.13 For kidney disease, corticosteroids are typically used for 3–6 months; however, previous studies show this is highly variable (median duration of 8 months, ranging between 0.2 and 39 months).5 14 Recurrence of tubulointerstitial nephritis is uncommon.

Synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs are utilised for recurrence of TINU or when there is a poor response to systemic corticosteroids.15 These therapies have included azathioprine, mycophenolate, chloroquine, T-cell calcineurin inhibitors, methotrexate, cyclophosphamide and tissue necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) agents. Published cases are limited to case reports, with durations of therapy varying between 3 and 52 months.5

Mycophenolate has successfully been used with corticosteroids for the treatment of TINU in the paediatric population and acute interstitial nephritis without uveitis in the adult population.16 17 One case report showed a favourable long-term prognosis 8 years post-treatment in a 7-year-old Chinese female with TINU treated with concurrent prednisolone and mycophenolate mofetil.18 There has been only one case when mycophenolate mofetil was added to corticosteroid treatment in an adult patient.19 Mycophenolate was withdrawn after 28 months; however, the dose and rationale were not described. The patient improved but still had persistent kidney impairment after 52 weeks of follow-up.19

It is unclear whether vulval sarcoidosis was part of her syndrome, as she declined further testing and ACE levels were negative at the time; however, only 55% of patients with sarcoidosis have a raised serum ACE level.20 TINU and sarcoidosis may not be distinct syndromes but could exist on a spectrum as they share clinical and immunological similarities.21 Treatment of sarcoidosis also involves corticosteroids, with resistance or intolerance treated with azathioprine, mycophenolate or a TNFα inhibitor.9

To our knowledge, there has been only one case report that describes concomitant sarcoidosis and TINU in a paediatric patient by Dawson et al.21 In this case report, a 7-year-old girl presented with bilateral conjunctival injection, fatigue, unintentional weight loss and acute kidney injury with elevated inflammatory markers. She underwent a kidney biopsy that revealed marked tubulointerstitial nephritis with eosinophils. A CT chest revealed pulmonary nodules, and a pulmonary biopsy revealed non-necrotising granulomatous inflammation with negative staining for acid-fast bacilli and fungal organisms. ACE level was negative. She was treated with oral prednisolone (2 mg/kg/day), methotrexate and infliximab with good response.21

Tubulointerstitial nephritis has been rarely associated with COVID-19 messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) vaccination, but not Ad5-vectored COVID-19 vaccines.22 TINU associated with COVID-19 vaccination has been reported once, in a paediatric patient who responded promptly to systemic steroid therapy.23 It was thought that COVID-vaccination-related tubulointerstitial nephritis was unlikely for our patient, given her clinical stability following booster vaccination in 2024.

IgG4-related disease has been associated with uveitis and tubulointerstitial nephritis separately but not typically with TINU.2426 IgG4-related kidney disease is characterised by abundant immunostaining for IgG4-positive plasma cells with a ratio of IgG4/IgG-positive plasma cells usually over 40%.25 Given there was no reactivity for IgG on immunostaining on kidney biopsy, with normal serum IgG4 levels, IgG4-related disease was thought to be unlikely in our patient.

This was a complex case of acute kidney failure secondary to TINU syndrome, where treatment had to pivot away from corticosteroids due to complications, and mycophenolate was used. TINU is rare but increasingly recognised, and cases like this add to the paucity of evidence that is currently available. This is the first described adult case of TINU with long-term follow-up that showed successful treatment with mycophenolate. More research is required to improve the diagnosis of TINU and the long-term management of this condition.
