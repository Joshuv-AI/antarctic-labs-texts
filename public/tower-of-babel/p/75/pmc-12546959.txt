Robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery-assisted aberrant right subclavian artery transposition for dysphagia lusoria
Authors: Sean M Lazinger, Michael Gibson, Anthony N Grieff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysphagia lusoria is a rare esophageal compression syndrome related to aberrant right subclavian anatomy. Traditional surgical management often requires thoracotomy or sternotomy, whereas more contemporary approaches involve a hybrid approach with thoracic endovascular aortic repair and subclavian bypass or transposition. We present the case of a 44-year-old woman with hostile endovascular anatomy and progressive dysphagia to solids and liquids who underwent robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery ligation of the proximal aberrant right subclavian artery with retro-esophageal pull-through and transposition to the right common carotid artery.Keywords: Dysphagia lusoria, Robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, Aberrant right subclavian artery, Kommerral diverticulum, Vascular surgery

In 1794, David Bayford first described dysphagia lusoria as a progressive dysphagia of solids and liquids related to esophageal compression by an aberrant right subclavian artery.1 In patients who have an aberrant right subclavian artery, there is a prevalence of 2% who have dysphagia lusoria.2 Symptoms typically manifest in the fourth or fifth decades owing to atherosclerotic changes and decreased esophageal mobility.3 Approximately 20% of the population has an aortic arch variant, with aberrant subclavian arteries occurring in less than 1% of the population.4 The percentage of the population with an aberrant subclavian artery is higher in patients diagnosed with Down, DiGeorge, and Edwards syndromes.5 This aberrant artery is caused by an abnormal persistence of the dorsal right aorta between the fourth arch and the seventh right intersegmental artery.6

Aberrant right subclavian arteries are suspected to be symptomatic in as many as 30% of patients owing to esophageal compression secondary to the retroesophageal arterial course.7 When this happens, patients can develop cough, dysphagia, and dyspnea owing to compression of the esophagus or trachea.4 One type of aortic arch abnormality that should be mentioned is a Kommerral diverticulum. Kommerral diverticulum’s are extremely rare occurring in between 0.05 and 0.1% of the population, but are important to recognize owing to their rupture and dissection rates, which reach up to 53%.8 Although esophageal compression can be seen on barium swallow, diagnosis of dysphagia lusoria is typically confirmed with computed tomography angiography or endoscopic ultrasound examination.9

Initial treatment is conservative with lifestyle and diet changes, including eating smaller portions, eating slower, and avoiding trigger foods.10 Proton pump inhibitors alone or in combination with promotility medications has also shown to be effective.10 First-line medical management before surgical evaluation is essential as most cases of aberrant subclavian artery disease may be an incidental finding in the setting of other pathologies.11 Patients who fail medical management are referred to surgery, but surgical methods remain variable and dependent on surgeon preference and specific patient anatomy.12 Historically, surgical treatment consisted of performing either a thoracotomy or sternotomy but contemporarily is often managed with hybrid endovascular procedures to minimize surgical morbidity.13 Hybrid repairs often include bypass or transposition of the subclavian artery through a supraclavicular incision coupled with endovascular coverage or embolization of the aberrant subclavian ostium. For these cases, patients require adequate landing zones for stent coverage. In this particular case, the patient lacked an adequate landing zone secondary to carotid proximity and a left vertebral origin from the aortic arch. For that reason, we employed a multidisciplinary surgical approach using robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) and transposition to avoid the morbidity of a traditional open repair in young woman. The patient consented and gave their agreement with the sharing of their case details and images.

Case report

A 44-year-old woman initially presented to the office for evaluation of esophageal compression noted in the setting of aberrant subclavian artery by both esophagram and axial imaging. She had been having symptoms of dysphagia that had progressively worsened over several years to the point that soft foods got stuck when swallowing. She had no other relevant past medical or family history.

Computed tomography angiography of the head and neck determined that endovascular repair was not a good option owing to her left subclavian and vertebral anatomy, which would require a separate incision and debranching (Fig 1). The patient was offered traditional open approaches; however, given the patient's desire to avoid morbidity associated with open repair, we used a collaborative approach with cardiothoracic surgery undertaking RATS ligation of the proximal aberrant right subclavian artery off of the aortic arch to allow for artery pull through and transposition to the right common carotid artery. Standard robotic port placement was obtained with the camera at the midaxillary line at the eighth intercostal space with two 8-mm posterior ports and one 12-mm anterior port. An air seal port was placed in the ninth interspace once single lung ventilation was completed. The lung was retracted posteriorly to identify the subclavian artery superior to the azygos vein. The subclavian artery was then dissected circumferentially to mobilize the esophagus.

Computed tomography angiogram coronal images of patient aortic arch anatomy. The aberrant right subclavian (solid line) is the most distal branch vessel and passes posterior to the esophagus. The left subclavian artery (dashed line) originates approximately 6 mm proximal to the right subclavian providing an insufficient landing zone for thoracic endovascular aortic repair. A direct aortic origin of the left vertebral artery (dotted line) is within 1 mm of the left subclavian origin.

A vessel loop was then placed around the artery to elevate it and provide tension, and an Endo GIA vascular load was used to divide the artery (Fig 2). Once the artery was divided, the remainder of the artery was mobilized inside the visceral pleura up to the thoracic outlet. We then made a right supraclavicular incision and dissected out the common carotid artery while protecting the vagus nerve deeply. The subclavian artery was then located just anterior to the vertebral body of the spine and dissected free until it could be pulled through the retroesophageal space in its entirety (Fig 3). An end-to-side anastomosis was then performed to the right common carotid resulting in a palpable radial. The patient was seen for a follow-up after surgery to complete the resolution of her dysphagia and patent bypass.

Discussion

Repair of aberrant subclavian arteries with traditional open techniques is associated with increased procedural and postoperative complications leading, to higher mortality compared with endovascular treatment.14 In particular, open approaches have higher mortality rates owing to increased risks of neurological events.15 In this patient, endovascular hybrid techniques were not feasible because of hostile endovascular anatomy; therefore, we used a RATS approach with transposition to the carotid artery with the intent of minimizing patient recovery and morbidity.

Few data exist regarding outcomes of hybrid RATS procedures for the treatment of aberrant subclavian arteries. Although this patient was not amenable to endovascular repair, anatomy was favorable for RATS for several reasons, including the lack of subclavian ostial calcification and a virgin chest. This procedure also posed a low risk of injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve because its course is often nonrecurrent in aberrant anatomy, allowing for extensive mobilization of the aberrant subclavian. The other major benefit of a RATS approach compared with a hybrid endovascular procedure was that it allows for the subclavian artery to be pulled through and not simply disconnected. We favor this approach specifically in this patient population with symptoms in the absence of a Kommerall aneurysm because it completely removes the compressive artery, as opposed to simply disconnecting it from the arterial system, where the thrombosed remnant may still cause symptoms. This technique was a good option for our patient who had hostile endovascular anatomy that included short landing zones.

We have presented a case with a relatively new treatment of an aberrant subclavian artery in a patient with dysphagia lusoria that failed medical management. This case demonstrates another option to treat aberrant subclavian arteries and provides clinical awareness of the use of RATS in place of sternotomy or thoracotomy. This procedure has the potential to decrease complications associated with sternotomy or thoracotomy. To our knowledge, this report presents one of a few technically successful cases of using RATS ligation of the proximal aberrant right subclavian artery off of the aortic arch with the removal of the artery from the esophagus and then right subclavian to right carotid artery transposition to treat dysphagia lusoria.
