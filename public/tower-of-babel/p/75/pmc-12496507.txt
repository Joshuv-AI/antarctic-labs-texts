Long-term implant success after treatment of a giant mandibular dentigerous cyst: A case report
Authors: Yi Li, Lijia He, Zhencun Tang, Limei Shao, Wenyun Zhang, Jun Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceDentigerous cysts are among the most common odontogenic cysts, with a postoperative recurrence rate of approximately 5 %–15 %. Large dentigerous cysts present significant clinical challenges due to extensive lesions and severe bone destruction, often resulting in inadequate bone volume and poor soft-tissue conditions for subsequent implant restoration.Case presentationA 71-year-old male presented with a history of a giant right mandibular dentigerous cyst diagnosed 12 years prior, treated by affected tooth extraction and cyst marsupialization (windowing drainage). The subsequent edentulous site underwent root canal therapy for the involved tooth, followed by extraction due to vertical root fracture, and finally dental implant restoration, achieving stable occlusion. A 7-year follow-up confirmed no cyst recurrence and satisfactory implant stability.DiscussionSuccessful dental implantation in cured cyst sites requires reconstructing sufficient bone tissue with minimal trauma. Key considerations include lesion size/characteristics, surgical history, and patient age to mitigate complications like osseointegration failure, inflammation, or infection.ConclusionFor jaw cysts with significant bone defects, dental implantation remains the optimal treatment for missing teeth when the lesion is resolved, favorable periodontal conditions and adequate bone regeneration are achieved, and guidelines are followed after thorough individual risk-benefit assessment.

Introduction

Dentigerous cysts, also known as follicular cysts, are common oral diseases [1,2]. Large dentigerous cysts can cause significant jawbone resorption and destruction, potentially affecting adjacent permanent teeth. In severe cases, extraction of the involved teeth is required, which adversely impacts patients' oral function and facial aesthetics. Dental implants, with their advantages of preserving adjacent teeth, providing stability, achieving favorable aesthetics, and offering long-term durability, have become the preferred treatment for permanent tooth loss. However, for patients with a history of large dentigerous cysts, residual bone defects after cyst resolution often heal slowly and rarely restore the original bone structure completely. These conditions pose challenges to successful implant placement and make dental rehabilitation in such patients a focus of clinical concern.

Dental rehabilitation following bone defect reconstruction is a crucial step in restoring oral function and improving quality of life. This is particularly important for patients who have recovered from large dentigerous cysts, as their residual bone conditions are complex, and achieving effective repair remains a key area of clinical research. This case report presents a patient with a successfully placed dental implant after the resolution of a large mandibular dentigerous cyst, providing a reference for the treatment of similar cases.

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE Guidelines 2025 criteria [3].

Case presentation

In February 2013, a 61-year-old male patient presented to the Department of Stomatology at the Hospital. He reported progressive swelling and bulging in the right lower facial area, which had persisted for over two years.

Clinical examination revealed a slight swelling and prominence of the right mandibular region without obvious tenderness. The posterior portion of the right mandibular vestibular sulcus appeared bulged, with normal overlying mucosa; however, no apparent fluctuation or “ping-pong ball” sensation was detected. Panoramic radiography demonstrated an inverted, low-position impaction of the right mandibular third molar. A well-defined, oval radiolucent lesion measuring approximately 46.6 × 12.5 mm was observed from the right mandibular first to third molar region, enclosing the third molar (Fig. 1a). The patient immediately underwent extraction of the right mandibular third molar, cyst curettage, and marsupialization with decompression. After routine disinfection and draping, local anesthesia was achieved using lidocaine via inferior alveolar nerve block combined with local infiltration around the third molar surgical site. The third molar was removed with a powered system, and a periosteal elevator was used to carefully separate part of the cyst wall from the alveolar bone. A portion of the cyst wall was sent for histopathologic examination, which confirmed the diagnosis of a dentigerous cyst (Fig. 1b). A rubber drain strip was placed in the extraction socket, and marsupialization with decompression was performed.

Additionally, the right mandibular first molar had undergone root canal therapy at an outside hospital two weeks earlier. We recommended that the patient complete the endodontic treatment to optimize future dental rehabilitation.

The patient returned for follow-up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Panoramic radiographs showed that the radiolucent area in the right mandible gradually decreased in size, while the surrounding bone exhibited progressive reparative thickening and increased density (Figs. 2a–d). By 12 months postoperatively, the cystic area had markedly diminished, with increased bone density and clearly defined, continuous trabecular architecture. After clinical evaluation, a secondary cyst curettage was performed under local anesthesia. During surgery, the drainage tube was removed, and the residual cyst wall was carefully curetted. However, the panoramic radiograph at that time revealed that the previously root-canal-treated right mandibular first molar showed decreased radiopacity in the root canal, suggesting possible absorption of the filling material (Fig. 3a). Immediate clinical examination of the tooth demonstrated only mild percussion sensitivity. At 6 months after the cyst curettage (18 months after the initial decompression surgery), the patient returned for another follow-up visit, reporting occlusal pain in the right mandibular first molar. CBCT imaging demonstrated a further reduction in the cyst area, accompanied by denser cortical and cancellous bone. However, a new CBCT scan indicated a vertical root fracture in the mesial root of the right lower first molar (Fig. 3b). Following the 2007 International Association of Dental Traumatology guidelines and after multidisciplinary consultation, it was decided to extract the right lower first molar [4].

Six months post-extraction, a panoramic X-ray assessment revealed that the bone quality at the extraction site was insufficient for dental implant placement. Consequently, we recommended a removable denture restoration to restore the occlusal relationship. By thirty-six months post-extraction, the patient inquired about dental implant treatment. CBCT imaging demonstrated adequate buccolingual and vertical dimensions of the alveolar bone at the site of the right lower first molar, measuring 12.1 mm and 15.3 mm, respectively, with a mesiodistal width of 15.7 mm. The bone quality in this area had finally reached the necessary standards for dental implant placement, leading us to recommend this treatment for the patient (Figs. 4c-d).

Five years after the initial treatment for the dentigerous cyst (three years post-extraction of the affected tooth), a 5.0 × 10 mm Andokan implant was installed in the affected area. The patient was fitted with an implant-supported denture and attended follow-up appointments one, two, five and seven years after the surgery. The implant in the right lower first molar area displayed no mobility, and the surrounding gingiva showed no signs of redness or swelling. Radiographic examinations confirmed the absence of significant radiolucency around the implant, no angular resorption, normal bone density, and proper adaptation between the crown and the abutment (Fig. 5a). Discussion

Dentigerous cysts, common odontogenic cysts of the jaw, typically involve the lower third molars [5]. Most dentigerous cysts are asymptomatic, leading to prolonged periods without symptoms that can result in the absorption and destruction of adjacent bone, displacement of neighboring teeth, and resorption of tooth roots [6,7]. Standard treatment for dentigerous cysts includes the removal of the cyst wall and extraction of the affected teeth, often accompanied by decompression to reduce cyst volume and stimulate bone healing [8,9]. For larger cystic lesions in the mandible, decompression serves as an effective treatment method that minimizes damage and complications. This approach reduces lesion volume while preserving the vitality of the pulp in surrounding teeth, making it a widely used minimally invasive technique for treating large jaw cysts [[10], [11], [12]]. In this case, the patient underwent decompression surgery for a large dentigerous cyst in the mandible, avoiding a pathological fracture while maintaining jaw integrity and masticatory function, which led to optimal treatment outcomes.

Existing literature defines large cystic lesions in the mandible as those with a diameter greater than 35 mm, commonly including odontogenic keratocysts, ameloblastomas, and dentigerous cysts encountered in clinical practice [13]. Reports of giant dentigerous cysts are rare, both domestically and internationally, with only four documented cases of dentigerous cysts exceeding 40 mm in diameter [14]. This case details a giant dentigerous cyst in the right lower mandible that affected the first molar, which ultimately had to be extracted as it could not be preserved. Under normal circumstances, ideal conditions for mandibular implant placement primarily depend on both the quality and quantity of the alveolar bone. Optimal parameters include appropriate bone density (preferably D1-D2 type bone, characterized by a high proportion of cortical bone and greater density), sufficient bone volume (width ≥ 6 mm, height ≥ 8 mm), and intact cortical plates. In this case, however, during the two-year follow-up period, the mandibular bone in the affected region did not meet these requirements. Therefore, a mixed-support removable partial denture was provided as an interim restoration to reestablish occlusal function. However, the long-term use of removable dentures can lead to ongoing damage to oral mucosa and bones, such as mucosal ulcers due to prolonged compression and varying degrees of bone resorption. Compared to removable denture restorations, implant restorations provide higher masticatory efficiency and greater comfort with intraoral prosthetics [15]. Therefore, if the defect area demonstrates sufficient bone regeneration, implants remain the best option for replacing missing teeth. In this case, five years after treating the dentigerous cyst, we pursued implant restoration of the missing right lower first molar following careful evaluation.

Bone defects resulting from the removal of jaw cysts, particularly those with a diameter exceeding 3–5 mm not only constitute a major factor affecting postoperative wound healing but also represent a common cause of maxillofacial bone deficiency, posing significant challenges for subsequent prosthetic rehabilitation [16,17]. If a cyst is not accurately diagnosed and appropriately treated before implantation, there is an increased risk of recurrence and enlargement, potentially resulting in implant failure years later. Consequently, reconstructing sufficient bone tissue in the defect area is essential for successful treatment. Existing research indicates that bone defects caused by cyst removal do not heal completely on their own; large cyst bone defects often exhibit significant deficiencies 12 months postoperatively, which may necessitate additional treatments, such as bone grafting, to promote ongoing repair of the bone defect [18]. In this case, the bone defect was completely replaced by newly formed bone after the initial treatment, achieving sufficient soft- and hard-tissue healing along with satisfactory oral hygiene, thereby meeting the criteria for implant placement. Moreover, during a 7-year follow-up after implant restoration, no cyst recurrence was observed, and the implant remained stable with excellent functional outcomes.

It is important to note that dentigerous cysts carry an inherent risk of recurrence, which may lead to renewed local bone destruction and compromise both the available bone volume and the stability required for implant placement. Therefore, careful evaluation of the likelihood of recurrence is essential when formulating an implant treatment plan. When necessary, the risk can be mitigated by prolonging the observation period and performing regular radiographic monitoring. Bone regeneration after cyst enucleation follows a characteristic timeline: the initial bone repair phase generally occurs within 3–6 months, followed by a maturation and stabilization phase at 6–12 months postoperatively. Clinically, bone grafting with autologous bone or synthetic substitutes may be considered to accelerate the repair of defect areas. The optimal timing for implant placement should be determined based on the patient's bone regeneration status. For patients with adequate bone recovery, implant placement can typically be performed around 6 months after surgery, whereas delayed implantation is advisable in cases of slower healing. Precisely timing implant surgery creates favorable conditions for osseointegration and long-term stability. In summary, implant rehabilitation in patients with a history of jaw cyst surgery is highly challenging and requires a comprehensive assessment of lesion size and characteristics, surgical site, and patient age. Equal attention must be given to recurrence risk, bone regeneration, and the timing of implant placement to minimize complications such as poor osseointegration, inflammation, and infection, ultimately achieving an optimal restorative outcome [19,20].

Currently, there are no reports in the literature detailing successful implant treatments following the resolution of giant dentigerous cysts. This case report illustrates the feasibility of successfully reconstructing bone tissue and placing dental implants after decompression surgery for a giant dentigerous cyst, yielding satisfactory results in both aesthetics and functional restoration. The patient's quality of life and psychological satisfaction were significantly enhanced. This case provides a valuable clinical reference for implant rehabilitation following the treatment of large mandibular cysts and offers new insights and perspectives for oral clinicians in managing extensive jaw cystic lesions.

Conclusion

In this case, a 71-year-old male patient with a large mandibular dentigerous cyst achieved clinical resolution after treatment. A 7-year follow-up confirmed that, once sufficient soft- and hard-tissue healing and adequate bone regeneration were achieved at the lesion site, implant-supported restoration resulted in long-term stable outcomes. This case provides practical evidence for the rehabilitation of similar post-cyst edentulous cases, demonstrating that even in patients with a history of extensive bone destruction, implant restoration remains a feasible and effective option when carefully evaluated and performed according to standardized protocols. These findings enrich the clinical strategies for post-odontogenic cyst implant rehabilitation.

However, it should be noted that this report represents a single case with limited follow-up duration and sample size, and its generalizability requires validation through larger, long-term studies. In future clinical practice, implant rehabilitation following the resolution of large dentigerous cysts should include stringent preoperative assessment of soft- and hard-tissue conditions, development of individualized treatment plans, and extended follow-up to further improve treatment success rates.
