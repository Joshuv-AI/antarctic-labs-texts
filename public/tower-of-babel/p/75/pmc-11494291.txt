Revealing OH species in situ generated on low-valence Cu sites for selective carbonyl oxidation
Authors: Yang Cao, Qiaozhi Zhang, Iris K. M. Yu, Daniel C. W. Tsang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceIn situ *OH species can be formed on low-valence Cu active sites when water and air are used as the solvent and oxidant, respectively. Compelling experimental evidence is presented here on the critical roles of the surface *OH species in achieving selective oxidation at the glucose carbonyl group. These findings expand our knowledge base in catalytic oxidation, in which lattice oxygen transfer through the Mars–van Krevelen mechanism has been widely observed. Moreover, the way heterogeneous catalysis impacts product selectivity via the surface *OH inspires a unique perspective to tailor active sites. We also highlight the participation of water in *OH formation and its impact on the kinetics, of which the generic understanding can be transferred to other solvent-assisted oxidation reactions.

Catalytic oxidation is a significant process in the chemical industry (1, 2). While H2O2 has been widely studied as an effective oxidant that promotes selective oxidation route (3, 4), the use of the abundant, low-cost molecular oxygen (O2) and even air as greener alternatives is drawing attention (5–7). In addition, the development of nonprecious metal-based catalysts, such as Cu (8), Mn (9), and Fe (10), is motivated in the context of sustainable development. Their performance in terms of product yield and selectivity, however, often falls behind those of noble metals (Pd and Au) (11, 12). The challenge persists in the knowledge-guided design of the catalyst surface to activate the oxidant and substrate for a high reactivity and selectivity.

The selective oxidation of biomass-derived aldehydes is of interest as organic acid products have wide applications in cosmetics, polymers, food additives, etc (13, 14). We selected glucose as the target substrate in this study considering that it is the most abundant sugar on earth. The oxidation at the aldehyde group (R-C1H=O) at the C1 position of glucose gives rise to gluconic acid (R-C1OOH), which is widely used in food and pharmaceutical industries. From the scientific viewpoint, glucose has a hydroxyl group bonded to every carbon, which makes oxidant attack possible at multiple positions on the backbone in addition to the aldehyde group at C1. The catalytic glucose oxidation mechanism is hence more complex when compared with the reactions of petrochemicals with low oxygen contents reported in the literature (15, 16).

Although the low-cost Cu-based catalysts (CuO, biochar-supported Cu) have been explored for glucose oxidation (8, 17, 18), the mechanism has not been sufficiently understood, and, in particular, the role of Cu oxidation states (Cu/Cu+/Cu2+) remains obscure. Our previous work synthesized Cu-biochar catalysts containing a mix of Cu, CuO, and Cu2O, which were capable of H2O2 activation to oxidize glucose into gluconic acid (30.7%) and glucuronic acid (39%) (18). The question remains open regarding how the Cu chemical state influences the catalytic mechanism and product selectivity. It was suggested that the surface lattice oxygen of CuO nanoleaves can activate the C1–H bond in glucose and be transferred to the glucose molecule to form gluconic acid (17). Another study reported glucose oxidation on CuO catalyst in a high-frequency ultrasound system (8). While the latter facilitated water sonolysis to generate radicals: H2O → •H + •OH, the surface lattice oxygen in CuO reportedly trapped the •H radicals, leaving the •OH radicals available for oxidizing glucose on the catalyst surface (8). Although both studies highlighted the contribution of CuO, their suggested oxidation paths differed in terms of the form of O species added to the reactant—lattice O from CuO or •OH radicals from water (8, 17).

In this study, we hypothesize that reactive oxygen species (ROS) could emerge from O2 activation in water in an O-deficient Cu environment (i.e., low-valence Cu). The O2-derived species responsible for oxidation need to be identified. It is widely agreed that oxidants can be activated to generate •OH, singlet oxygen (1O2), and superoxide anions (O2•−), which then attack the substrates (5, 7). While their concentrations are critical to achieving high conversion, excessive radicals lead to nonselective oxidation and undesirable mineralization (19, 20). To well control oxidant activation and oxidative process, our improved understanding of radical formation and its transfer to reactant is important for guiding the future design of nonprecious metal catalysts. As for the mechanisms, while previous theoretical calculations proposed C1–H activation as the critical step in glucose oxidation (12, 17), there is limited experimental evidence of the kinetically relevant step that controls the overall oxidation rate. Hence, this study aims to reveal the chemistry between Cu species, O2, and water medium in the context of glucose oxidation.

Here, we investigated the oxidation of glucose to gluconic acid in pressurized air (20% O2) over Cu-based catalysts. To understand the active Cu species, we compared Cu catalysts supported on unmodified carbon (Cu/C) and nitrogen-doped carbon (Cu/C-N). The latter is hypothesized to enrich the electron density of metal sites (21, 22), which enables the investigation of the impact of Cu valence on the catalytic mechanisms. To study O2 activation and the formation of ROS as well as their contribution to glucose oxidation, kinetic isotope effect (KIE) measurements, in situ Raman experiments, and DFT calculations were conducted. It is evident that hydroxyl radicals can be generated in situ on the catalyst surface, i.e., in the form of adsorbates, which are denoted as *OH in our discussion. Such localized radicals outperformed the free hydroxyl radials in bulk solution (denoted as •OHfree; produced from noncatalytic, thermal degradation of H2O2) in terms of product selectivity. Our knowledge can help rational design of nonprecious metal catalysts for oxidation of biomass-derived sugars beyond glucose in sustainable air-driven systems, expediting the establishment of circular bioeconomies.

Results

Catalyst Characteristics.

A soft urea strategy was employed to introduce N heteroatoms and further stabilize Cu species (Fig. 1A) (23, 24). Based on ICP-OES analysis, 2.99% and 2.57% of Cu are found in Cu/C-N 800 and Cu/C 800 catalysts, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S1; “800” in catalyst labels—fabrication temperature 800 °C). Both appeared as particles at 50 to 100 µm with smooth surfaces (SEM-EDX in SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and presented a typical type IV isotherm for mesoporous materials (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The TEM images reveal highly dispersed Cu species on wrinkled carbon supports (Fig. 1B). The HRTEM image reveals that the Cu/C-N 800 catalyst carries nano-sized crystal Cu particles measuring 6 to 12 nm (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Additionally, lattice stripes corresponding to the Cu(111) plane with a lattice spacing of 0.205 nm are observed (25, 26). In contrast, larger Cu particles (15 to 20 nm) are randomly dispersed on the surface of the Cu/C 800 catalyst with more exposed lattice spacing of 0.250 nm that corresponds to Cu2O(111) planes (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4) (27). The Cu/C 800 may also carry metallic Cu crystals according to its XRD pattern showing Cu0 diffraction peaks (crystallite size estimated to be 26.2 ± 0.5 nm using the Scherrer equation; Fig. 2A). The absence of Cu0 diffraction peaks in Cu/C-N 800 can be attributed to nano-sized Cu particles and amorphous metal nitride formed in soft urea synthesis that are undetectable by XRD (Fig. 2A) (23). These results demonstrate that N doping favored Cu dispersion.

The full XPS spectrum indicates the successful incorporation of Cu and N into Cu/C-N 800 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). The peak of Cu 2p3/2 was deconvoluted, in which the binding energies of 932.8 and 934.2 eV can be assigned to Cu0/Cu+ and Cu2+ species, respectively (Fig. 2B) (28). The low-valence species (Cu0/Cu+) in Cu/C-N 800 accounts for 64.5% of the total surface Cu, higher than that in Cu/C 800 (56.6%). Moreover, a higher Cu0 signal is found in the AES spectrum of Cu-LMM of Cu/C-N 800 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Given that XPS and AES offer a lower penetration depth of 2 to 10 nm (29) than TEM (in μm), a combination of these characterizations points to the presence of Cu nanoparticles with the Cu0/Cu+-rich surface and Cu(0) underneath in Cu/C-N 800. In comparison, without N doping, Cu nanoparticles on Cu/C 800 exhibit a higher oxidation state both on the surface and in the core. These results suggest on average a lower valence of Cu on the N-doped carbon support, from which electrons possibly transferred to the metal sites (1, 30). The subpeak assigned to Cu-coordinated N (398.8 eV) (31, 32) is notable in the N 1 s spectra of Cu/C-N 800 (13.8% of total surface N; Fig. 2C), underscoring the presence of strong Cu-N interactions. Other N species are found to be pyridinic-N, pyrrolic-N, graphitic-N, and NOx species (398.3, 399.9, 400.9, and 402 eV, respectively) (31, 32). They may appear as defects accounting for a higher ID/IG ratio of Cu/C-N 800 than that of Cu/C 800 in the Raman spectra (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A) (33), given that surface oxygen-containing functional groups largely disappeared after the pyrolytic fabrication of catalysts (FTIR spectra in SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). It has been suggested that atomically dispersed metals can be anchored onto carbon materials by preferential coordination with N atoms, inducing the electron modulation of Cu active sites and hence, modifying catalytic activity and stability (28, 31). It is noted that NH3 from the decomposition of urea, the N precursor, during catalyst fabrication (DTG peak at 120 to 280 °C; SI Appendix, Fig. S7) may contribute to Cu reduction. A simplified expression is NH3 + CuOx → Cu + H2O + N2. The actual reaction scheme can be more complex, where Cu exists in various oxidation states (CunOm+) and reacts with NH3 to different extents, as observed in the field of catalysis for emission control (34).

The Cu electronic characteristics were further investigated by synchrotron radiation X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurements. Fig. 2D shows the normalized Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) spectra of Cu/C-N, Cu/C 800, and Cu/C 600 catalysts along with Cu foil, Cu2O, and CuO references. All catalysts have the absorption threshold positions located between Cu2O and Cu foil, suggesting that the valence of bulk Cu particles was between Cu0 and Cu+, in the ascending order: 0 < Cu/C-N 800 < Cu/C 800 < Cu/C 600 < 1, demonstrating the impact of N doping and pyrolysis temperature on Cu chemical states. As shown in the Fourier transform (FT) k3-weighted EXAFS spectra (Fig. 2E), the prominent peak of Cu/C-N 800 is located at 1.85 Å stemming from the Cu-C/N scattering path, which provides further evidence that most Cu atoms are highly dispersed on the C-N support. The Cu/C 800 exhibits a prominent peak of Cu-Cu and an additional small peak at 1.56 Å of Cu-O. The structural parameters of Cu atoms obtained from quantitative EXAFS curve-fitting can be found in SI Appendix, Fig. S8 and Table S2. Wavelet transform (WT) was also conducted to provide powerful resolution in both R and k spaces and discriminate the backscattering atoms (Fig. 2F). In line with the FT-EXAFS analysis, a WT maximum at 5.20 Å−1 assigned to Cu-C/N bond is visible in the Cu/C-N 800 sample, and the presence of a metallic crystal structure is not pronounced. For comparison, the Cu/C 800 catalyst exhibits a distinct feature at 6 to 8 Å−1 besides the WT maximum at 3 to 6 Å−1, which represents the coexistence of Cu-Cu and Cu-O coordination. The latter is even more evident in Cu/C 600, suggesting that Cu-O is favored in low-temperature fabrication. It should be noted that XAFS offers information about bulk Cu particles rather than surface only. The results are consistent with the TEM and XRD for Cu/C 800, which jointly suggest the coexistence of Cu2O (i.e., Cu+) in 15 to 20 nm and Cu0-dominating crystallites (26.2 nm) (Figs. 1 D and E and 2A). We remark that such Cu2O particles carry Cu2+ species on the surface, as suggested by the Cu 2p3/2 peak analysis (Fig. 2B). For Cu/C-N 800, although the metallic Cu nanoparticles in 6 to 12 nm (Fig. 1 B and C) with oxidized surface (Fig. 2B) may not be noticeable in XAFS, it substantiates the presence of Cu-N interaction, which has been indicated by the deconvoluted N 1 s peak (Fig. 2C).

Cu Active Sites for Glucose Oxidation.

The catalytic experiments showed that all the Cu-based catalysts were active for the conversion of glucose to gluconic acid in 20 bar air (160 °C, 30 min; Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Table S3). The Cu/C-N 800 catalyst exhibited the highest activity, with a glucose conversion of 71.3% and a gluconic acid yield of 64.9%, which was higher than that achieved on the catalyst without N doping (Cu/C 800, 32.3% gluconic acid yield). The same trend was observed for the catalysts prepared at 600 °C, i.e., the gluconic acid yield was 61.8% on Cu/C-N 600 vs. 20.1% on Cu/C 600. These results pointed to the improved catalytic activity by N doping. It is noteworthy that N doping merely altered the total Cu content according to the ICP-OES analysis (2.99 wt% in Cu/C-N 800 and 2.57 wt% in Cu/C 800; SI Appendix, Table S1). Given the similar absolute Cu loading, it is hypothesized that the activity discrepancy was related to the difference in Cu speciation on the catalyst surfaces.

As discussed in the catalyst characterization section above, the XPS and XANES analyses suggested Cu species with a lower oxidation state on the N-doped support, indicating the presence of more electron-rich Cu sites on Cu/C-N 800 than on Cu/C 800 (Fig. 2 B and D). Furthermore, the TEM images and FFT analysis revealed Cu(111) facets in Cu/C-N 800 while Cu2O(111) facets were observed in the Cu/C 800 catalyst (SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4). A linear correlation can be obtained between the glucose conversion (or gluconic acid yield) and the Cu0-Cu+ to Cu2+ ratio (Fig. 3B), highlighting the strong dependence of catalytic activity on the Cu valence. The XPS of spent catalysts shows that after reaction, the Cu2+ distribution increased to 56.7% from 35.5% in the pristine Cu/C-N 800 catalyst, suggesting that the low-valence Cu was involved in the glucose reaction by donating electrons and becoming Cu2+ (Fig. 3D). Combining these results, it is believed that the electron-rich, low-valence Cu species (Cu0/Cu+) served as the primary active sites for glucose oxidation. One may expect that the catalytic activity is highly associated with the abundance of Cu0 sites in particular. We emphasize that it is the Cu0/Cu+ sites on well-dispersed nano-sized Cu particles that contribute to the catalysis, considering that Cu/C 800 carries notable metallic Cu in large crystallites (26.2 nm) but was outperformed by Cu/C-N 800 (Fig. 3A).

ROS Generation.

The turnover frequency (TOFs) of glucose oxidation on Cu/C-N 800 at 20 bar air can reach as high as 821 h−1, while maintaining a carbon balance of at least 95% (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). When the reaction atmosphere was changed from air to N2, only 9.1% conversion of glucose was obtained (Fig. 3C), indicating the significance of O2 in oxidation reactions. That leads to the next scientific question about the active species derived from molecular O2 activation in the catalytic system. In the aqueous phase, radicals including •OH, 1O2, and O2•− are possibly generated when O2 is activated on the catalyst surface (35, 36). Starting from the thermodynamic perspective, we calculated the Gibbs free energies (ΔGo) of the formation of different radicals through the redox reactions between O2 and Cu0 using the standard cell potentials (Fig. 4A). The results showed that the activation of O2 to O2•− reaction is 68 kJ/mol and 50 kJ/mol along with Cu0 oxidation to Cu+ and to Cu2+, respectively (i.e., from initial stage to stage A and to stage B in Fig. 4A). The positive signs indicate that O2•− formation is not spontaneous. In comparison, the ΔGo of O2 activation to H2O2 (stage D) and then to •OH (stage E) is approximately −4 kJ/mol and −5 kJ/mol, respectively, along with Cu0 oxidation to Cu2+. The negative values suggest thermodynamically favorable processes. Based on the theoretical analysis, we conjecture that •OH radicals, plausibly in the form of *OH adsorbed on active sites of Cu/C-N 800, were the major ROS for glucose oxidation.

To experimentally evidence the ROS, KIE was studied by comparing catalysis in D2O and H2O. It has been suggested that D2O extends the 1O2 lifetime by up to 10 times that in H2O (39–41). Our experimental results showed that glucose conversion decreased to 39.8% in D2O, inferring the minor contribution of 1O2 in this study (Fig. 4B). Instead, the results suggested that the rate-determining step involved water or its derivatives, i.e., H-containing species (or D-containing in D2O condition). Considering the significance of O2 (Fig. 3C), the ROS is speculated to be OH species originated from the activation of O2 in H2O. To verify, radical quenching experiments were conducted, which show that glucose conversion significantly decreased from 71.3 to 23% in the presence of isopropanol (IPA) as the •OH radical scavenger (42) (Fig. 4C), whereas the conversion in the test with benzoquinone (BQ; O2•− scavenger) only dropped to 58.2% (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), indicating the more significant contribution of •OH radicals. The XPS analysis revealed similar Cu chemical states after reaction regardless of the IPA addition, i.e., Cu0/Cu+ content ~44% (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S11), which is lower than that of the pristine catalyst (64.5%; Fig. 2B). These results uphold our speculation that O2 and H2O are activated on low-valence Cu sites, generating *OH radicals for reactions.

In conventional systems using exogenous H2O2 as the oxidant, the latter was reported to deliberate •OH radicals to facilitate oxidation reactions (18, 19). This was confirmed by electron spin resonance (ESR) results indicating •OH as the main ROS from H2O2 decomposition (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). If OH radicals prevail as the major ROS in a pressurized air system, we may expect that oxidation performance in air resembles that in H2O2. To test such a hypothesis, glucose oxidation in H2O2 in N2 was performed for comparison with the air system. Our results showed that at a similar glucose conversion of ~43% over Cu/C-N 800, H2O2 (1 equiv.) and O2 (5 bar) led to comparable gluconic acid yield (31.3 to 34.2%; 160 °C, 30 min; Fig. 4D), hinting the same pathway with adsorbed *OH in both systems regardless of the oxidant sources. In comparison, the product yield dropped to 16.2% in the noncatalytic H2O2 system (i.e., without Cu/C-N 800). In the latter scenario, heating alone triggered the decomposition of H2O2 into free •OH radicals (•OHfree) promoting similar glucose conversion at 41% in N2 condition and, yet, resulting in only half the gluconic acid yield on Cu/C-N 800, in company with the formation of overoxidized products, such as 10.2% of formic acid (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). This underscores the significant contribution of the heterogeneous catalyst to the selective production of gluconic acid. We speculate that the adsorbates (i.e., in situ generated *OH and *glucose) could enable more selective attack at the carbonyl group of the substrate compared to the noncatalytic system, where the low selectivity might be associated with the random collision of •OHfree radicals and glucose molecules in bulk solution. Therefore, regulating the interfacial reactions for a controlled formation of *OH on the catalyst surface may be a promising strategy to improve the production of target products. We highlight that the surface Cu2+ on Cu/C-N 800 significantly increased from 35.5 to 56.7% after glucose reaction in air, while being relatively stable after the glucose-free control run (37.5%; Figs. 2B and 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). The findings substantiate the formation of *OH radicals via the redox reaction between Cu0/Cu+ and O2. Glucose consumed *OH, propelling the O2-to-*OH reduction along with Cu0/Cu+-to-Cu2+ oxidation; otherwise *OH accumulated on the catalyst surface impeding O2 activation and so the Cu0/Cu+ transformation.

To substantiate the speculations above, in situ Raman experiments were conducted to monitor the surface-adsorbed intermediates and their relative abundance on Cu/C-N 800 under different reaction conditions. At 160 °C with O2 flow in a water-free environment, the Raman spectra show three broad bands at 350, 601, and 1,068 cm−1 attributed to surface CuOx, Cu-Oad, and O2•− species, respectively (Fig. 4E) (43–46). To simulate the hydrothermal conditions for catalysis, both O2 and H2O vapor were introduced into the in situ Raman system, which resulted in two sharp bands that are distinctive from those in O2 only (Fig. 4F). The band at 640 cm−1 is assigned to Cu-OH bending model according to the previous DFT calculation (659 cm−1) and observed position (500 to 700 cm−1) (46–49) (20 cm−1 deviation from theoretical values is generally acceptable). Another sharp band at 560 cm−1 can be assigned to the stretching mode of the top site-adsorbed OH that interacts with another OH through hydrogen bonding (Cu-OH⋯OH) (46, 48). These bands grow as the dominating signals with increasing time and increasing H2O vapor loading (Fig. 4 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S14A). Considering the aqueous-phase catalysis (H2O-to-O2 ratio > 100:1), one may expect more pronounced formation of OH species acting as major ROS for glucose conversion. It is worth noting that O2•− was not detected when H2O was present in the in situ Raman tests, giving no evidence on O2•− as the ROS for aqueous-phase glucose oxidation. When mixed N2-H2O feed gas was used, no surface ROS can be detected by in situ Raman (SI Appendix, Fig. S14B), which aligns with the observation of negligible catalytic activity in N2 (Fig. 3C).

Theoretical Calculations.

We performed DFT calculations to further investigate the electronic structures of active sites and possible reaction pathways. Two models were considered, i.e., N-doped (25% pyridinic N, 25% pyrrolic N, 50% graphitic N; set based on XPS results in Fig. 2C) and undoped carbon supports, each with Cu clusters sitting on top. The charge-density difference diagram unveils an overall charge transfer of 0.39 e− from Cu atoms to the N-doped carbon support in the case of Cu/C-N, which is higher than 0.31 e− observed for Cu/C as the undoped system, indicating the stronger interaction between Cu and N-doped support (Fig. 5A). Delving into different segments of Cu clusters, it can be found that the Cu atoms close to the support tend to lose electrons, whereas electrons notably accumulate within the upper two layers of Cu clusters (0.30 e−) in Cu/C-N, and to a greater extent compared to Cu/C (0.23 e−) (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Table S4). Such results mostly align with the catalyst characterizations which reveal more electron-rich, low-valence Cu sites on Cu/C-N catalyst. Projected density of states (PDOS) calculations indicate that the 3d orbital of Cu atoms and 2p orbital of C/N atoms overlapped near the Fermi level (EF; Fig. 5B). In particular, N doping induces a downshift of Cu d-band center (εd) from −2.289 eV in Cu/C to −2.338 eV in Cu/C-N (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). These evidence the capacity of N doping to redistribute charges within metal clusters and to modify d-band structure for mediating the binding of reacting adsorbates (39, 50).

DFT calculations elucidated O2 activation over Cu/C-N and Cu/C catalysts, which considered the binding of O2 on the metal sites in both “end-on” (Pauling model) and “side-on” (Yeager model) configurations (51). The energy pathways and all optimized intermediate structures are illustrated in Fig. 5C and SI Appendix, Figs. S16 and S17. It is noted that both Pauling and Yeager adsorption models enable the formation of *OOH—a peroxo intermediate featuring O-O bond—possibly via a proton-coupled electron transfer (Fig. 5C). This agrees very much with the in situ Raman spectra, where a subtle band was detected at approximately 900 cm−1 on Cu/C-N catalyst, corresponding to the typical peroxo O-O stretching (700 to 900 cm−1) (Fig. 4 F and G). Combining the multifaceted experimental and computational evidence discussed above, we conclude that the Cu/C-N catalysts facilitate the activation of O2 molecules to *OH through a three-electron transfer pathway, which involves *OOH as the intermediate (*O2→*OOH→*OH). The whole process is exothermic and thus thermodynamically favorable (SI Appendix, Table S5). Despite a similar O2 activation pathway, the binding of molecular O2 on Cu/C (i.e., step O2→*O2) tends to be less thermodynamically favored than on Cu/C-N, which may be associated with the difference in the surface electron density of the Cu clusters.

The thermodynamics of •OHfree radicals in aqueous bulk solution and *OH localized on the catalyst surface were also investigated to better understand the interfacial reactions involving OH. We compared the energetics of a *OH localized on a hydrated Cu surface (Cu coordinated with one H2O molecule) and a •OH radical in a network of water molecules representing bulk water. The results show more favorable *OH-Cu-H2O coordination (−1.26 eV) on Cu/C-N compared with H2O-•OH binding in water (−0.52 eV) (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Table S6), which indicates that OH tends to be localized on Cu surface, i.e., in form of *OH admolecule on the catalyst surface. Such localized radicals are thus regarded as the particular species that attack glucose during the catalytic oxidation reaction. It is noted that *OH exhibits stronger adsorption on Cu/C (−1.36 eV) than on Cu/C-N (−1.26 eV) (Fig. 5D). Moreover, the binding of OH from bulk water to a nonhydrated Cu surface is thermodynamically feasible for Cu/C catalyst only and not for Cu/C-N, reaffirming the stronger affinity of OH species for Cu/C (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). This may induce excessive OH accumulation on Cu sites, resulting in catalyst poisoning and hence sluggish reaction kinetics (52, 53). These findings suggest the importance of moderate adsorption strength of OH species in enabling their participation in oxidizing the reactants (54).

Kinetic Insights into Glucose Oxidation.

The recent studies of sugar oxidation over non-noble metal catalysts shed light on the mechanism with the aid of DFT calculations (8, 17). Herein, we propose a series of elementary steps to examine the rate-determining step, based on the experimental KIE and glucose conversion results in this study (Fig. 6A). Briefly, O2 is activated at Cu0 to generate in situ *OH (step 1) while the carbonyl group of glucose, i.e., R-C1H=O, is adsorbed (step 2). The oxidation of the adsorbed glucose to gluconic acid takes place via C1-H activation (step 3) and then *OH addition to R-C1=O (step 4). Based on the pressure drop in the catalytic system with IPA (•OH scavenger) in the absence of glucose reactant, we calculated the consumption rate of O2 for determining the TOF of *OH formation, which reached a value of up to 5,864 h−1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). Notably, the TOF value of step 1 surpassed that of glucose oxidation (821 h−1), indicating that step 1 is not rate-determining. The reaction order in glucose is one within the concentration range of 27 to 111 mM, beyond which the zeroth reaction order applies and TOF remains constant (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9D). Most of the experiments in this study adopted 111 mM glucose, where the zeroth-order regime starts to manifest. Hence, we presume step 2 to be quasi-equilibrated. To evaluate the kinetic relevance of step 3, KIE was studied using glucose substrate deuterated at the C1 position (D-glucose-d1), which resulted in 59% gluconic acid that was similar to that from nonlabeled glucose (64.9%; Fig. 6C). The result indicates that C1-H activation is not rate-limiting. Combining the above analyses, we deduce that the *OH addition to R-C1=O (step 4) was the rate-determining step.

While the literature (11, 12, 19, 36) rarely reported an air-promoted Cu-based catalytic system for glucose oxidation as in our current work, we discuss the following findings from broader oxidation studies to extract mechanistic insights. Zhao et al. investigated the oxidation of hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) on vanadium carbon catalysts in molecular O2 (7). When using HMF deuterated at the C atom of hydroxymethyl group (−CD2OH), the apparent rate constant notably decreased from 2.29 × 10−2 min−1 to 0.37 × 10−2 min−1, suggesting C-H bond activation as the rate-determining step. A similar observation was reported by Yan et al., where the rate-determining activation of the C-H bond in glycerol was evidenced by DFT and KIE (single-atom Pt catalyst, O2) (50). Moreover, it is widely agreed that hydride transfer from the benzylic carbon to catalyst (relevant to C-H activation) is rate-determining during the catalytic oxidation of benzyl alcohol (55, 56). It is noted that most literature findings above apply in the oxidation of alcohol to aldehyde (7, 55, 56). In the DFT analysis for toluene (Ph-CH3) oxidation to benzoic acid (Ph-COOH) on Pd (111), the OH addition to Ph-COH intermediate had an activation energy of 0.79 eV, which was higher than that for C-H activation (0.17 eV) (57). This resembles our conclusion that step 4 is kinetically relevant in this work producing an acid as the end product (Fig. 6A). Nonetheless, parameters that decide the rate-limiting step will necessitate more evidence-based understanding through vigorous investigation of an array of catalytic systems.

Different Lignins as Carbon Support Precursors.

The experiments discussed above used the Cu-based catalysts prepared from enzymatic corn cob lignin. Considering that lignin chemical structures and properties vary with the biomass sources and fractionation methods, it is of great interest to study how such variation affects the Cu speciation and thus, the catalytic activity. Herein, we explored three other lignins, which were kraft lignin from wood and organosolv lignin from bamboo and poplar, respectively. They were individually used in catalyst fabrication following the same protocol under the same conditions as for the Cu/C-N 800 catalyst (i.e., lignin precursor as the only variable). Then, the catalytic activity toward glucose oxidation was evaluated (160 °C, 30 min, 20 bar air). The results show that these catalysts perform similarly as comparable glucose conversion (66.4 to 71.3%) and gluconic acid yields (54.2 to 64.9%) were observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S25). This implies that the catalytic activity was not sensitive to the choices of lignin precursors.

We note that the four lignin precursors in this study exhibit different characteristics. While the enzymatic lignin and organosolv lignin samples have the lower average molecular weight (Mw) ranging from 899 to 1,581 Da, kraft lignin has much higher Mw (≥10,000 Da) due to the formation of condensed structures under the harsh extraction conditions (SI Appendix, Table S8). Furthermore, both organosolv lignin samples exhibit a broader range of molecular weight than the enzymatic lignin as indicated by the higher polydispersity index (PDI; 1.53 vs. 1.18). The 2D heteronuclear single quantum correlation nuclear magnetic resonance analysis shows that enzymatic and organosolv lignin samples exhibit a high ratio of β-O-4 linkages indicative of the preserved native structures, whereas the kraft lignin has a condensed structure without syringyl units, underscoring compositional differences among various lignin sources (SI Appendix, Table S9). The detailed discussion of lignin characterizations can be found in SI Appendix, Note S1. The key highlight of these results is that the structural discrepancies among the lignin precursors exerted only trivial impacts on the catalytic performance. We speculate that the catalyst preparation at 800 °C achieved deep lignin carbonization, to the stage where the carbonized structure may not vary markedly within the category of lignin. This aligns with the TGA results and Raman spectra, showing comparable mass loss% (55 to 67.8%; 900 °C) of the lignin precursors and a narrow range of ID/IG ratio (0.88 to 0.91) for the corresponding Cu catalysts prepared at 800 °C, respectively (SI Appendix, Figs. S20 and S21). While future studies may delve into the carbonization pathways of diverse lignins and their interactions with Cu species, the current results suggest a good chance in securing reproducible catalysis when changing from one type of lignin precursor to another. Our finding supports the evidence-based practice of using lignin, a sustainable biomass-derived material, as a carbon support precursor in wider catalytic applications.

Discussion

We present multifaceted evidence on the catalytic function of low-valence Cu sites and the *OH-involving rate-determining step in air-promoted glucose oxidation, which is in good agreement with the recent biomedical studies using low-valence Cu to convert O2 to •OH radicals for advanced tumor therapy (58, 59). Such an O2 activation pathway has not been experimentally proved in the context of Cu-catalyzed glucose oxidation, of which most studies were performed in the absence of air or O2 with the emphasis on CuO participation. The latter resembles the well-known Mars–van Krevelen mechanism where lattice oxygen is transferred from metal oxide to reactant. For instance, Amaniampong et al. suggested that the surface lattice oxygen of CuO nanoleaves assisted C-H activation in glucose and was then incorporated into it to form gluconic acid based on DFT calculations (17). When CuO and high-frequency ultrasound were applied simultaneously, the DFT results suggested the formation of •OH and •H radicals, and the latter was trapped by CuO, leaving the •OH available for glucose oxidation to glucuronic acid (8). On top of these suggested models, our experimental findings and theoretical calculations herein reveal a unique pathway for aqueous-phase glucose oxidation over low-valence Cu catalysts in O2 gas, featuring the rate-limiting addition of *OH admolecule to the activated glucose. Moreover, the 0th reaction order with respect to glucose concentrations (≥111 mM) suggested that the Cu surface was fully saturated with adsorbed glucose, whereas the reaction order was 0.46 in varying air pressure, pointing to a catalyst surface partially covered by *OH (Fig. 6 B and D) (60). The critical design parameters of Cu sites deserve further investigation to manipulate adsorbates for improving the oxidation rate. It is noteworthy that the glucose conversion and gluconic acid yield of this work are comparable with or even superior to those reported in the literature (SI Appendix, Table S7).

In conclusion, the Cu/C-N catalysts outperformed Cu/C catalysts in the O2-promoted oxidation of glucose to gluconic acid. The former catalysts had a higher proportion of Cu species with zero or low valence (Cu0, Cu+) because the N-doped carbon support favored the formation of electron-rich metal sites. The DFT calculations, in situ Raman analysis, quenching experiments, and KIE studies suggest *OH generated in situ on the catalyst surface as the major radical responsible for glucose oxidation. The evidence also reveal the activation of O2 to *OH through the pathway: *O2→*OOH→*OH, regardless of the O2 adsorption configuration. Furthermore, the localized *OH on the catalyst surface was more selective toward glucose oxidation at C1HO, yielding gluconic acid of higher selectivity, compared to •OHfree radicals in bulk solution that arise from thermal degradation of exogenous H2O2 under noncatalytic conditions. With further kinetic analysis, the addition of *OH to R-C1=O of glucose, instead of C1-H activation, was concluded as the rate-determining step. Another exciting finding is that the performances of catalysts were insensitive to the type of lignin precursor (as catalyst support), of which the reproducibility deserves vigorous verification in the future.

Our mechanistic understanding is illustrated in Fig. 7, highlighting the electron transfer between Cu clusters and N-doped carbon support as well as the participation of *OH in the reaction. We envision that the tuning of *OH coverage and its activity by nanoengineering metal surface is a promising strategy to achieve selective oxidation of bio-based molecules. Our study sheds light on the oxidation mechanisms and characteristics of the key players in the low-cost, green system comprising Cu catalysts and pressurized air. The understanding could assist development in catalytic oxidation of diverse carbonyl compounds and even polysaccharides. The oxidation of starch, pectin, cellulose, etc. to simple organic acids will require systems capable of both breaking glycosidic bonds and oxidizing carbonyl groups. Further advances in catalyst design could foster innovations for effectively utilizing the widely available bioresources, creating circular bioeconomies in broad industries.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of the Cu/C-N and Cu/C Catalysts.

Typical experiments used catalysts that were synthesized from enzymatic corn cob lignin (Shandong Longli Biotechnology) as the carbon precursor. The lignin was suspended in ethanol and sonicated for 1 h (images of suspensions in SI Appendix, Fig. S24). One gram of urea and a designated amount of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O were dissolved in DI water. The latter solution was added dropwise into the lignin suspension (30 min), and then, the mixture was stirred at 80 °C to evaporate the solvent. The obtained gel-like product was pyrolyzed at 800 °C for 1 h under an inert N2 atmosphere. The resulting sample was denoted as Cu/C-N 800, whereas Cu/C-N 600 represents the counterpart pyrolyzed at 600 °C. Cu/C catalysts were prepared following a similar procedure except without using urea as a soft template.

Three additional lignin precursors were studied for the preparation of Cu catalysts, including kraft lignin (Sigma-Aldrich) and organosolv lignins from bamboo and poplar, respectively. Detailed information and characterizations of the lignin samples can be found in SI Appendix, Note S1.

Material Characterization.

The morphology of the catalysts was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX, TESCON VEGA 3). The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using a Micromeritics 3Flex. The pore size distribution and specific surface area were determined by the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method and the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, respectively. The chemical states of the catalysts were revealed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific Nexsa) and Auger electron spectroscopy (AES, PHI710). Cu K-edge X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (XAFS, BL11B beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility, China) was carried out to investigate the Cu coordination environment in the carbon matrix. A Raman spectrometer (Renishaw, UK) was employed to investigate the graphitization degree of the catalysts. The crystal structure was examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8A, Germany). The metal content was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out using a TGA 8000 PerkinElmer (a heating rate of 10 °C/min to reach 900 °C in N2). In situ Raman was conducted at 160 °C using Horiba HR Evolution equipped with a 532 nm laser source. The catalysts were exposed to a mixture of O2 and H2O vapor at different molar ratios (1:1, 1:5, and 1:10) for spectra collection. Additional comparison experiments with O2 flow or with a mixture of N2 and H2O vapor (1:1) were conducted at 160 °C. The ROS was observed by electron spin resonance (ESR) using a Bruker EMXnano.

Catalytic Test and Product Analysis.

Typically, the vessel was loaded with 0.1 g glucose, 0.025 g catalyst, and 5 mL water and was pressurized with air to 20 bar (or varying pressure at 5, 10, 20, or 30 bar). The sealed vessel was then heated to the desired temperature (160 °C) under stirring (SynthWAVE reactor). After the reaction, the liquid and solid phases were separated by filtration for further analysis. For the H2O2 system, 1 equivalent of H2O2 relative to glucose was introduced to the vessel without charging air, and then, the reaction was performed under autogenous pressure in the sealed vessel. In radical quenching tests, the scavenger was loaded to the vessel before the reaction. Isopropanol (IPA 0.2 mL) was selected as the scavenger of •OH radicals as previously reported (42). Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

The liquid products were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Hitachi Chromaster) equipped with both refractive index and ultraviolet-visible detectors. The mobile phase was 5 mmol/L H2SO4 aqueous solution at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min coupled with an ion-exclusion column (Aminex HPX-87H column, Bio-Rad) at 65 °C. The glucose conversion, product yield, and turnover frequency (TOF) were calculated according to the following equations:Glucose converison%=Cmoles, reactantinitial-Cmoles, reactantfinalCmoles, reactantinitial×100%, Product yield%=Cmoles, productfinalCmoles, reactantinitial×100%, TOF=moles of gluconic acid (mol)cat.(g)×Cu loading (wt%)×Cu dispersion (%)÷Mcu (g/mol)×time (h).

TOF was calculated as moles of gluconic acid produced per mole of surface atoms per hour. Cu loading is 2.99 wt% and Cu dispersion is determined to be 7.52% by H2 chemisorption.

Computational Methods.

DFT calculations were performed by Vienna ab initio simulation package utilizing the projector augmented wave potentials with a planewave cutoff energy of 450 eV (61, 62). The generalized gradient approximation functional of Perdew et al. was applied as the exchange-correlation functional with grimme’s semiempirical DFT-D3 dispersion correction to describe the van der Waals interactions (63). To prevent the interaction between two adjacent layers, a vacuum layer of 15 Å were applied for all of the surface model. We applied a gamma-centered k-point grid of 3×3×1 to sample the Brillouin zones. The convergence criteria of electronic energies were 10−5 eV, and the atomic forces was 0.03 eV/Å for all calculations.

To construct the models of Cu-Graphene (denoted as Cu/C) and Cu-N-Graphene (denoted as Cu/C-N), 13 Cu atoms were chosen to form a Cu cluster. We used the computational hydrogen electrode model proposed by Nørskov to calculate the Gibbs free energy of the intermediates (64). For each intermediate, the Gibbs free energy ΔG can be calculated using:ΔG=ΔE+ΔZPE-TΔS+Ue+ΔGpH,

where ΔE is the reaction energy difference between the reactants and the products, ΔZPE is the zero-point energy, ΔS is the entropy change, T represents the finite temperature, e is the charge transferred, U is the applied potential, and ΔGpH is considered as the correction free energy of H+. The zero-point energy and the entropy change were calculated from vibrational frequencies.

The calculated adsorption energy (Eads) was evaluated based on the following equation:ΔEads=Esur+ads – Esur – Eads,

where Esur+ads, Esur, and Eads are the obtained energies for the slab system containing the OH, the energy of the slab, and the energy of the adsorbate in a vacuum, respectively. Here, the adsorbate refers to chemisorbed OH.
