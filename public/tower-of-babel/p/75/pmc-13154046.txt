Ultrasound-Guided Transmesenteric Retrograde Recanalization of Ostial Superior Mesenteric Artery Occlusion
Authors: Yulong Huang, Min Zhou, Xinsheng Xie, Xiang Hong, Lixin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic mesenteric ischemia caused by superior mesenteric artery (SMA) occlusion is treated with endovascular or open revascularization. However, complete ostial SMA occlusion without a stump or collaterals poses significant technical challenges, particularly in high-risk patients.Case SummaryA 64-year-old woman with multiple autoimmune disorders presented with postprandial abdominal pain and occult gastrointestinal bleeding. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated complete ostial SMA occlusion without a residual stump and absence of the inferior mesenteric artery. Antegrade and collateral retrograde recanalization attempts failed. Modified retrograde open mesenteric stenting (ROMS) was performed via minilaparotomy with ultrasound-guided distal SMA puncture. The patient’s symptoms resolved with weight gain and improved nutrition. Stent patency was maintained for 2 years; restenosis at 36 months was treated with drug-coated balloon angioplasty, with 4-year patency.DiscussionThis case highlights an invasive ROMS modification that may provide a salvage option for high-risk patients with complex SMA occlusion unsuitable for standard approaches.Take-Home MessageUltrasound-guided transmesenteric revascularization via minilaparotomy is a salvage option for patients with high-risk SMA ostial occlusion refractory to endovascular therapy and prohibitive risk profiles for conventional ROMS.

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old woman with multiple autoimmune disorders (vasculitis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren syndrome, and overlap syndrome) presented with a 6-month history of recurrent postprandial epigastric pain, which could be alleviated by rest, accompanied by persistent occult intestinal bleeding requiring intermittent hospitalization for blood transfusion.Take-Home Message•Ultrasound-guided transmesenteric revascularization via minilaparotomy is a salvage option for patients with high-risk SMA ostial occlusion refractory to endovascular therapy and prohibitive risk profiles for conventional ROMS.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of cerebral infarction; multiple autoimmune disorders, including overlap syndrome, Sjögren syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, and vasculitis treated with oral prednisone (2 tablets daily) and thalidomide capsules (2 capsules nightly); osteoporosis treated with oral calcitriol (1 tablet nightly); hypertension for 8 years for which she had intermittently taken valsartan (1 tablet daily) and nifedipine controlled-release tablets (one tablet daily); and coronary stent implantation and thoracic endovascular repair for thoracic aortic arch aneurysm.

Differential Diagnosis

Chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI) must be differentiated from peptic ulcer disease, which manifests with chronic, periodic, rhythmic epigastric pain, often accompanied by heartburn or acid regurgitation. Peptic ulcer disease is confirmed via endoscopy or barium meal examination. Gastrointestinal malignancy, such as gastric cancer, should be considered in cases of persistent pain, vomiting of undigested food, and significant weight loss, with advanced stages showing cachexia or lymphadenopathy; a diagnosis requires endoscopic biopsy. Chronic cholecystitis is characterized by pain triggered by fatty food intake, whereas chronic appendicitis typically manifests with fixed right lower quadrant tenderness. Chronic pancreatitis is associated with pancreatic enzyme abnormalities or morphologic changes on imaging studies such as ultrasound or computed tomography. Chronic gastrointestinal inflammation, which is distinguished by diarrhea or mucus stools, requires endoscopic and pathologic evaluation for confirmation. Angiography remains the key diagnostic modality for detecting mesenteric artery stenosis, occlusion, or collateral circulation to confirm CMI.

Investigations (Supplementary Diagnostic Tests)

The physical examination revealed cachexia (body mass index 17.3 kg/m2) and pallor. Abdominal assessment revealed a soft abdomen without tenderness, rebound pain, or guarding. Laboratory findings revealed pancytopenia (white blood cells 3.25 × 109/L; platelet count 67 × 109/L; hemoglobin level 95 g/L) with an elevated D-dimer level (4.57 mg/L). Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed complete occlusion of the proximal superior mesenteric artery (SMA) without a residual stump and total absence of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) (Figures 1A to 1C).Figure 1Preoperative Computed Tomography Angiography(A and B) Occlusion at the superior mesenteric artery ostium. Management (Medical/Interventions)

Under local anesthesia, right brachial artery access was established using a 5-F sheath (Merit Medical Systems, Inc). A 0.035-inch Terumo guidewire (Terumo Corporation) and 4-F pigtail catheter (Cordis Corporation) were advanced into the abdominal aorta. Angiography revealed proximal SMA occlusion with distal collateral filling (Figure 2A). Multiple attempts to engage the SMA ostium failed. Selective hepatic and splenic artery angiography via a 4-F C2 pigtail catheter (Cordis Corporation) revealed tortuous microcollaterals (Figures 2B and 2C). Retrograde SMA recanalization through the collaterals was unsuccessful, thereby precluding antegrade/retrograde revascularization. The patient was a poor surgical candidate for open repair or conventional retrograde open mesenteric stenting (ROMS); therefore, a modified ROMS approach was implemented.Figure 2Initial Digital Subtraction AngiographyDistal superior mesenteric artery opacified via collateral circulation (A), via the pancreaticoduodenal arcade (B), and via splenic artery branches (C).

Under general anesthesia, a midline infraumbilical incision was made to expose the mesentery. Ultrasound guidance (using a hockey-stick probe) detected the distal SMA. A 21-gauge micropuncture needle (Merit Medical Systems, Inc) achieved transmesenteric access, followed by advancing a V-18 guidewire (Boston Scientific) and inverting a catheter (CXI support catheter, Cook, Inc) (Figures 3A and 3B). The V-18 guidewire traversed the occlusion into the aortic true lumen. From a brachial approach, an MPA catheter (Cordis Corp) was positioned at the right subclavian artery (Figure 3D). A retrograde V-18 guidewire was snared to establish a through-and-through traction system. A 6-F, 90-cm sheath (Cook Medical) was positioned suprarenally, followed by the MPA catheter (Figure 3E), and a Supra Core guidewire (Abbott Vascular) was advanced distal to the SMA.

After using a 4 mm × 40 mm balloon (ev3 Inc) for predilation, a 6 mm × 60 mm self-expanding bare-metal stent (Cordis Corporation) was deployed to achieve full lesion coverage (Figures 3F and 3G). Poststent angiography confirmed optimal apposition, restored mesenteric perfusion, and the absence of contrast extravasation (Figure 3H). No other vascular complications such as dissection, rupture, or distal embolization occurred.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery. Enteral feeding with a liquid diet was started on postoperative day 2, advancing to an unrestricted diet by postoperative day 5, without recurrence of abdominal symptoms. Pharmacotherapy, including dual antiplatelet therapy, statin therapy, and corticosteroid supplementation, was maintained throughout the hospitalization. Complete clinical resolution was confirmed before discharge on postoperative day 9.

Serial follow-up examinations demonstrated sustained therapeutic efficacy, with a clinically significant 5-kg weight gain recorded at the 3-month postoperative evaluation. Serial follow-up CTA at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively revealed preserved stent morphology, optimal positioning, and patent lumen (Figures 4A to 4C). At the 3-year follow-up, CTA revealed focal in-stent intimal hyperplasia with hemodynamic luminal stenosis (Figure 4D), which was successfully managed using drug-coated balloon angioplasty. The 4-year follow-up CTA confirmed durable stent patency and restored perfusion (Figure 4E).Figure 4Follow-Up(A-C) At 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years, the stent remains patent. Discussion

The blood supply to the abdominal viscera is primarily derived from the celiac trunk, SMA, and IMA. SMA occlusion is the most common etiology of mesenteric ischemia, whereas CMI, a relatively rare condition, is primarily caused by atherosclerosis.1 CMI classically manifests as postprandial abdominal pain, anorexia, and weight loss, collectively termed the ischemic triad.2 The primary treatment goal for CMI is symptom relief, achieved through conservative management, endovascular therapy, open surgery, or hybrid procedures.3 Since Shaw and Maynard4 first performed SMA endarterectomy in 1958, open surgical revascularization has been the gold standard and appears to provide the most durable long-term symptom relief in cohort studies.5

Endovascular therapy has emerged as the first-line treatment for mesenteric artery occlusion owing to its minimally invasive nature and low perioperative morbidity.6 However, complex SMA lesions, such as ostial atherosclerotic lesions without residual stumps, long-segment occlusions, or heavily calcified lesions, remain a challenge for conventional endovascular approaches.7 To address these limitations, Milner et al8 introduced ROMS in 2004, which combines direct arterial access with endovascular techniques. Traditional ROMS requires laparotomy and proximal SMA exposure, which may increase the risk of surgical trauma and complications.9 Modified approaches such as retrograde SMA recanalization via the jejunal artery, as described by Gilmore and Fang,10 have demonstrated reduced invasiveness while maintaining efficacy.

In this patient, neither the SMA ostium nor the collateral pathways were identifiable, thereby precluding antegrade or collateral-based retrograde recanalization. Given the patient's poor nutritional status, frailty, and multiple comorbidities, traditional ROMS was considered excessively invasive. Our modified ROMS technique (Figure 5) offers the following advantages: 1) the minilaparotomy requires only partial mesenteric exposure and ultrasound-guided distal SMA puncture; 2) mesenteric dissection is avoided, thereby preserving the collateral vasculature and minimizing visceral manipulation, which is critical for malnourished patients with compromised wound healing; and 3) postprocedural SMA or mesenteric suturing is unnecessary—a micropuncture needle establishes distal SMA access while a transbrachial long sheath deploys the stent. The distal SMA puncture site requires only a 15-minute compression, which aligns with recent trends toward minimizing vascular closure–related complications.Figure 5Schematic of Modified Technique(A) Ostial superior mesenteric artery occlusion. Conclusions

For patients with atherosclerotic lesions of the SMA characterized by complete ostial occlusion and the absence of viable collateral vessels, who are unsuitable for conventional endovascular intervention because of poor baseline conditions, the modified ROMS technique performed through a small abdominal incision with retrograde arterial puncture while omitting the mesenteric incision may serve as a viable alternative therapeutic option. Nevertheless, this conclusion is based on a single case, and the generalizability of the technique remains uncertain. Further studies with larger patient cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to validate its safety, reproducibility, and long-term outcomes.
