Implementation and economic analysis of a perioperative brain healthcare bundle: a mixed-methods study

AbstractStudy typeMixed-methods observational implementation and economic evaluation study.BackgroundPerioperative care bundles have the potential to improve patient outcomes but their successful implementation depends on staff engagement and the resources required. Introduction

Hospitals face growing pressure to deliver high-quality patient care amid rising surgical volumes and resource constraints. With an ageing population and increasing disease burden, surgical procedures are projected to double between 2015 and 2030.1 In this context, optimising perioperative care is critical for improving outcomes and ensuring the efficient use of resources.2

Modern strategies emphasise multidisciplinary care bundles—coordinated, evidence-based interventions across the surgical continuum. These pathways can reduce hospital stays, prevent postoperative complications and lower critical-care utilisation through components such as prehabilitation, enhanced recovery protocols and cognitive protection measures.1 Among these, brain-focused protocols targeting cognitive outcomes have attracted attention, with studies showing reduced postoperative cognitive dysfunction.35

Despite their promise, quality improvement (QI) initiatives often face resistance from staff due to added complexity, intervention fatigue and misalignment with workflows.6 7 Successful implementation requires leadership engagement and strategic resource planning.

The Safe Brain Initiative (SBI) exemplifies a surgical QI bundle, integrating 18 evidence-based interventions—from depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring to circadian rhythm optimisation—primarily to reduce postoperative delirium (POD) in the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU).35 810 By incorporating patient-reported outcomes, the SBI promotes data-driven care and exposes systemic gaps across perioperative care.35 810

POD is associated with adverse clinical and economic outcomes, including prolonged hospital length of stay (LOS), increased complications and higher healthcare costs.1114 Evidence from large observational studies and reviews indicates that POD is an independent predictor of extended hospitalisation.13 14 Consequently, interventions that reduce the incidence of POD may also reduce LOS and associated costs. This relationship underpins a key assumption of the economic model used in the present study.

While the clinical effectiveness of the SBI has been established,810 the present study provides novel insights into the economic sustainability and staff perceptions of the implementation, both of which are critical for scalability. Questions remain about the real-world cost, resource demands and organisational impact of such programmes. Understanding staff perspectives is equally vital for a sustainable adoption. As healthcare shifts towards value-based models, assessing these operational and human factors becomes essential.

The aim of this study was to assess the operational, economic and organisational impact of implementing a perioperative brain healthcare bundle, adopted from the SBI, at a Swiss hospital. The mixed-methods approach combined quantitative health-economic modelling with qualitative staff feedback.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective mixed-methods case study examined the operational and economic impact of a prospectively implemented perioperative brain healthcare bundle for 6918 surgical patients at a Swiss hospital. A convergent mixed-methods design was employed, where qualitative (semi-structured staff interviews) and quantitative data (health-economic analysis) were assessed. For the qualitative component, the unit of analysis was the individual staff member (anaesthesiologist or nurse). For the quantitative economic analysis, the unit of analysis was the hospital level, with costs and outcomes aggregated across all eligible surgical patients. The quantitative analysis and data inputs for the economic model were drawn from the implementation period spanning January 2023 to January 2024. Integration occurred during the interpretation phase, using a narrative weaving approach to compare qualitative themes with quantitative resource use and cost findings. A pragmatist paradigm was adopted to combine qualitative and quantitative evidence in addressing practical implementation questions. Details on the study population and clinical outcomes can be taken from results published separately.5 9

Health-economic reporting adhered to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022 guidelines (online supplemental table S1)15 and qualitative reporting followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) 2014 (online supplemental table S2).16

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the total implementation cost after 12 months—reported in aggregate and per patient. Secondary outcomes included staff time, the incidence of PACU-detected POD and associated reductions in hospital stay and qualitative insights into implementation challenges and successes. Staff-time estimates were derived from interview-based recall and validated across roles.

The perioperative brain healthcare bundle

The perioperative brain healthcare bundle was adopted from the SBI, which comprises 18 evidence-based perioperative concepts aimed at reducing POD in the PACU.35 810 The implementation process and clinical protocols of the perioperative brain health bundle at our institution have been described in detail elsewhere.9 Briefly, the intervention consisted of a multicomponent bundle targeting high-risk surgical patients. To support reproducibility of this economic evaluation, we have included a visual summary of the intervention workflow in figure 1, which outlines the key tasks at each perioperative stage. The bundle was implemented at the Salem-Spital, Bern, Switzerland, over 12 months (6 January 2023–6 January 2024) under the guidance of the implementation coordinator (JB-E, co-author) and outcomes were published separately.5 9

Collected data included patient and surgical characteristics, surgery duration, BIS-derived burst suppression time, patient-reported outcomes (patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs); pain, thirst, stress, well-being, anxiety, nausea, vomiting), assistive device use (hearing aids, dentures, visual aids), cognitive status and delirium incidence using the Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (Nu-DESC).17 18 Patients screening positive for POD (eg, Nu-DESC score ≥2) in the PACU were managed according to a standardised institutional protocol (online supplemental table S3). No changes to clinical protocols were required beyond completing the questionnaire. Fidelity to questionnaire completion was monitored weekly through coordinator review and deviations were documented.

Interviews

Semi-structured interviews were conducted retrospectively to assess the impact of the perioperative brain healthcare bundle on workflow and resource use. Anaesthesiologists and nurses involved in data collection during the implementation at Salem-Spital were recruited via the implementation coordinator who is an anaesthesiologist. Participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure variation across professional groups and experience with the implementation process. In line with the exploratory and descriptive design, sampling prioritised maximum variation and information power rather than formal data saturation.19 All interviewees were directly involved in the implementation of the perioperative brain health bundle and were therefore considered information-rich participants. The final sample comprised five nurses and five anaesthesiologists, representing approximately one-fifth of nurses and one-third of physicians in the department.

Interviews lasted 30–45 min and were conducted via Microsoft Teams in either English or German by a lead interviewer (co-authors KS or SS) and a note-taker. Sessions were audio-recorded, transcribed and anonymised for analysis. The interview guide is provided in online supplemental table S4.

Questions explored perceived workload, stress and challenges and successes related to implementation. Using a context analysis of recorded transcripts, themes and subthemes were identified using an inductive method without the use of dedicated software. The number and roles of interviewees contributing to each theme were documented. A second reviewer independently verified theme extraction to ensure reliability; no discrepancies were identified. A consultancy with no financial or employment ties to the Salem-Spital or the interviewees conducted interviews and analysis. Supporting quotes were extracted in their original language and are available on request.

Economic analysis

An implementation model was developed to provide a retrospective quantitative estimate of the costs and consequences of the perioperative brain healthcare bundle. An implementation model was developed to provide a retrospective, quantitative estimate of the bundle’s costs and consequences. Given the retrospective nature of the study, a pragmatic costing approach was used, focusing on the primary resource drivers—staff time and equipment—identified through staff interviews. While more granular techniques like activity-based costing can provide higher precision in prospective designs, our choice of model was guided by the feasibility of capturing retrospective data from a completed implementation.

Clinical setting

The model simulated the implementation across eight operating rooms at the Salem-Spital in Bern over 12 months. This time horizon aligns with the hospital’s annual budgeting and resource allocation cycles. Based on 2023 records, 6918 elective inpatient surgeries were covered, including general surgery, orthopaedics, spine, urology, gynaecology and obstetrics. Implementation covered the perioperative phase, starting from pre-induction preparation to PACU discharge.

Intervention and comparison

To estimate implementation costs, the additional staff time required for bundle activities was compared with the standard care period of the preceding year (2022), based on staff recall via semi-structured interviews. PACU-detected POD incidence was compared between the first 6 months (January–June 2023) and the second 6 months (July–December 2023) of the implementation period (methods in online supplemental table S5). Patient questionnaire answers were not evaluated for this work, but monthly POD incidences were published previously.5 9 Because no PACU delirium screening existed before implementation, a before-and-after comparator using the first versus the last 6 months of the implementation period was used as a pragmatic alternative. Limitations of this approach are acknowledged.

Model setup and structure

The model was built in Microsoft Excel (Redmond, USA) with a 12-month time horizon, reflecting typical hospital investment cycles. A hospital perspective was adopted as the party most economically impacted, and costs were reported in 2024 Swiss Francs (CHF) without discounting.

A core assumption of the economic model was that preventing POD reduces total hospital LOS. This assumption is supported by existing literature demonstrating that POD is independently associated with prolonged hospitalisation. In the model, an average reduction of 18 hours in PACU LOS per avoided case of POD was applied, based on published evidence.8

Resource use followed the pathway in figure 1, beginning post-QI approval and with a trained implementation coordinator in place. Costs included staff time and equipment acquisition, offset by savings from reduced hospital stays due to avoided POD in the PACU.

Model inputs

Inputs for the economic model were derived from multiple sources. Implementation-related resource use (eg, time per phase, meeting frequency and staff involvement) was estimated through semi-structured staff interviews, which included both pre-implementation and post implementation assessments. Mean values were used for the base-case analysis, while reported ranges informed the sensitivity analyses. Additional cost inputs, including equipment and materials, were obtained from published literature and manufacturer list prices.

Clinical inputs, including PACU-detected POD incidence and patient characteristics, were obtained from previously published data on the implementation of the SBI at our institution.9 These data were accessed via the original study database and corresponding publication and were used to inform the economic model, including estimates of potential reductions in hospital LOS. POD was defined as a Nu-DESC score ≥2 at PACU transfer or discharge, using the highest recorded score. Model inputs are given in online supplemental table S6.

Scenario analysis

A scenario analysis evaluated the amortisation of equipment costs (eg, BIS monitors), assuming a 5-year lifespan. Costs for equipment monitoring or replacement were not considered.

Model uncertainty

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was based on 2000 simulation runs. Model results are presented as the base-case mean alongside the 95% credible intervals (CrI) as the result of the PSA. CrIs represent the range within which 95% of outcomes fall; intervals crossing zero were deemed non-significant.

Each simulation drew input values from distributions reflecting uncertainty:

Selection of distributions (normal, beta, log-normal and gamma) followed established recommendations for early-stage health-economic modelling. A deterministic sensitivity analysis identified key drivers of model outcomes by varying each input by ±20%. This range reflects standard practice for early-phase implementation models where high uncertainty is expected. Changes in costs were visualised using a tornado plot.

Results

Qualitative interview outcomes

Ten healthcare professionals, five anaesthesiologists and five nurses, participated in semi-structured interviews. Findings are summarised by theme in online supplemental table S7.

Staff generally viewed the perioperative brain healthcare bundle as scientifically sound and beneficial, reporting improved patient interactions and heightened awareness of cognitive outcomes. While initial concerns about time investment were noted, most found the bundle manageable after a brief learning curve. One nurse cautioned that the questionnaire occasionally dominated patient interactions, potentially limiting responsiveness to patient cues.

Most interviewees (9/10) perceived a positive impact on patient outcomes, citing fewer POD cases in the PACU and improved PROMs such as thirst and anxiety. Staff also noted a decline in postoperative complaints. However, some patients appeared confused when the questionnaire was administered shortly after anaesthesia, likely due to insufficient prior explanation.

Most interviewees (6/10) reported long-term benefits, including increased awareness of POD prevention, greater attention to patient well-being and the successful integration of depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring for individualised care.

Regarding workload, interviewees (7/10) felt the bundle did not significantly increase workload demands, while three described mixed experiences. Completing paper-based questionnaires during PACU transfers was considered suboptimal, particularly when preparing for subsequent cases. Manual digitisation of paper forms added a clerical burden.

Suggestions for improvement included establishing external support teams for hospitals with limited capacity, appointing a dedicated nurse to oversee data collection and digitalisation of the data-collection process to reduce errors and eliminate transcription.

Implementation model outcomes

The estimated time investment for implementing the perioperative brain healthcare bundle was 803 hours (95% CrI 695 to 912) over 12 months for 6918 patients, averaging 6.96 min (95% CrI 6.03 to 7.91) per patient (table 1). Of this, 161 hours (95% CrI 119 to 203) were one-off initiation time and 642 hours (95% CrI 576 to 709) were recurrent time investments. The implementation coordinator contributed 39 hours during initiation and 284 hours during the running phase, primarily due to digitising data-collection forms.

Implementation costs were CHF 326 612 (275 370 to 374 781) with 29.6% allocated to the initiation phase (table 1). The average cost per patient was CHF 47.2 (39.8 to 54.2). Equipment accounted for 85.4% of the initiation costs and 69.1% of recurrent costs.

By comparing rates of PACU-detected POD between the initial 6 months and the final 6 months, the model estimated a reduction of 300 events (95% CrI 270 to 330), representing a 19% decrease. The model also estimated a potential reduction of 421 hospital days (95% CI 341 to 509). Accounting for savings from reduced hospital stays, the projected net cost savings were CHF 389 523 (95% CI 159 209 to 688 988).

Scenario and deterministic analysis

To assess long-term impact, a scenario analysis extended the implementation period to 5 years. Assuming no equipment repurchase, the model projected cumulative cost savings of CHF 2 334 736 (95% CrI 1 209 089 to 3 823 969) (figure 2). Incremental costs per year are given in online supplemental table S6. Even accounting for a one-time equipment cost of CHF 82 668 (table 1) and unmodelled training time for new staff, potential savings remained substantial. The bundle reached financial breakeven within 2 months.

Cumulative costs and savings related to the brain health care bundle. CHF, Swiss Francs (2024 values).

Deterministic sensitivity analysis determined the three equally major contributors to model outcomes as follows: (1) the percentage difference of PACU-detected POD, (2) cost per day in hospital, (3) reduction of hospital LOS that is associated with PACU-detected POD (online supplemental figure S1). Each of these inputs is directly related to the cost-saving potential of the perioperative brain healthcare bundle rather than actual implementation costs. These inputs have a substantial and equal impact on potential cost savings because they are linked: POD events avoided are multiplied by the hospital LOS decrease directly associated with avoiding POD (in days), multiplied by the cost per day in hospital. The worst-case scenario would be that there is no decrease in POD or that patients who avoided having POD did not spend less time in hospital. In this instance, there would be no cost saving and only implementation costs. The best-case scenario would be having a high baseline rate of POD and through implementing the perioperative brain healthcare bundle, reducing POD events a minimum number. In other words, the best-case scenario would be a hospital with a high rate of POD before implementation that saves at least 1 day in hospital per POD event avoided—with a significant and substantial decrease in POD events attributed to the implementation of the care bundle. In this work, we had a decrease in POD events from 22.45% to 18.11% when comparing the first and last 6 months. This is a more conservative reduction (likely due to the calculation) in comparison with previous implementations of the SBI, where one hospital decreased their POD incidence from 18.56% to 10.46% and another from 18.7% to 9.01% in the first and last 3 months of implementation, respectively.8 In contrast, the two other hospitals only had a very low baseline rate of POD of around 3% where POD reductions were negligible.8

The key model drivers for implementation costs were EEG sensor and monitor costs and the costs for the implementation monitor. These factors had a comparatively minor impact on total implementation costs (online supplemental figure S1).

Discussion

This mixed-methods implementation study provides a comprehensive evaluation of a perioperative brain healthcare bundle in a Swiss hospital. By integrating qualitative staff interviews with a hospital-level health-economic model, we found that the care bundle was associated with an estimated reduction in POD incidence in the PACU and with substantial projected net cost savings—approximately CHF 390 000 in the first year. At the same time, staff generally perceived the intervention as clinically meaningful and manageable after a short adaptation period.

Although the initial investment was estimated at 803 staff hours and CHF 326 612, these costs were offset by an estimated CHF 716 135 in savings, primarily from 421 fewer hospital days associated with reduced POD events in the PACU. The model projected a 19% reduction in POD (resulting in 300 fewer cases) among 6918 patients, based on Nu-DESC screening during implementation.9 Prior studies have shown that non-pharmacological, multicomponent interventions, particularly structured care bundles, can reduce POD in both randomised trials and real-world settings,82025 and that patients with POD typically experience substantially longer hospital stays.8 20 26 27 While causality cannot be definitively established in our design, our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that preventing POD in the PACU contributes to shorter hospital stays and lower costs.

From a health-economic perspective, the time and cost required for implementation compare favourably with those of other complex perioperative QI initiatives. For example, a rapid response system introduced to a surgical ward required 805 hours of training alone.28 In our model, the bundle required an average of approximately 7 min of staff time per patient and remained cost saving at the hospital level. A scenario analysis, which extended the amortisation period to 5 years, further improved estimated cost efficiency, with the bundle reaching financial breakeven within 2 months. A systematic review reported inpatient intervention costs for delirium prevention ranging from US$386 to US$553 per patient,29 whereas our estimated per-patient implementation cost was considerably lower at CHF 47 (95% CrI 40 to 54), approximately US$59 (November 2025 exchange rates). This discrepancy likely reflects differences in scope and intensity, as the reviewed interventions also included pharmacological and mental health programmes, whereas our care bundle focused on perioperative monitoring, non-pharmacological prevention strategies and structured assessment.

The mixed-methods approach adds important context to the quantitative findings. Staff interviews revealed strong support for the initiative: most anaesthesiologists and nurses viewed the perioperative brain healthcare bundle as scientifically sound, clinically relevant and aligned with patient-centred care. Interviewees perceived improvements in patient comfort (eg, thirst, anxiety, temperature) and fewer POD events in the PACU, which aligns with the modelled reductions in POD and LOS. Patient complaints reportedly fell from one per month to zero during implementation. Initial concerns about additional workload diminished once the bundle was integrated into routine workflows, suggesting that the learning curve is manageable with adequate support and clear communication. At the same time, staff highlighted that completing paper questionnaires during PACU transfer could be stressful, and it shifted their attention from the patient to the form, underlining the importance of tailoring implementation strategies and timing to existing workflows.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the study was conducted in a single Swiss hospital, and cost estimates are specific to local wage structures and reimbursement mechanisms, which may limit generalisability. Because our analysis relied on retrospective staff recall to estimate time investments, the results reflect the specific organisational context of a private Swiss institution. While advanced techniques like activity-based costing might offer more refined insights in other settings, we have provided time-based resource estimates to allow other healthcare systems to adapt the model using their own local salary and administrative data. However, time-based estimates are reported to facilitate adaptation using site-specific salary data. Second, the health-economic analysis relied on post hoc modelling, including staff recall for some pre-implementation parameters, which introduces the potential for recollection bias. Prospective designs with clearly defined pre-implementation baselines would strengthen causal inference. Third, the qualitative component included a small convenience sample of 10 out of 77 professionals involved in project implementation (13%) and not all interviewees were directly involved in day-to-day data collection. This raises the possibility of selection bias and may over-represent those who were more engaged or favourable towards the intervention. Fourth, the qualitative component also has limitations. Only 10 semi-structured interviews were conducted and formal thematic saturation cannot be assumed. However, the study was designed as an exploratory assessment of implementation experiences and sampling was guided by information power and maximum variation rather than saturation.19 Moreover, the interview sample comprised a substantial proportion of the local clinical staff involved in the intervention. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted as context-specific and exploratory. In addition, several model inputs were based on interview-derived estimates and assumptions, and equipment replacement or training costs for new staff were not explicitly modelled. Although extensive probabilistic and deterministic sensitivity analyses were performed, residual uncertainty remains, particularly around the magnitude of POD reduction, the associated LOS effect and the cost per hospital day. Fifth, data collection relied on paper forms digitised by the implementation coordinator. In routine practice, such tasks would typically be delegated to nursing staff or medical students, potentially reducing coordinator time and overall costs. Full digitalisation—integrating the bundle into electronic patient records and dashboards—could streamline data entry, minimise transcription errors and provide real-time feedback to perioperative teams.

Another limitation is the comparison of outcomes between two periods within the implementation phase rather than against a pre-intervention baseline. This approach may be subject to temporal confounding, as seasonal variation in surgical case mix or patient characteristics was not adjusted for and may have influenced POD incidence. In addition, the first 6 months likely represent an early implementation phase, during which the intervention was still being adopted. Improvements observed in the later period may therefore reflect both the effects of the intervention and increasing staff familiarity and learning over time. As a result, the observed differences should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the first 6 months (the ‘early phase’) already incorporated the rollout of the SBI; therefore, any clinical improvement observed in the subsequent 6 months likely represents an incremental gain due to staff learning curves and process stabilisation. Consequently, our results may represent a conservative estimate of the initiative’s total effectiveness compared with a true ‘standard care’ baseline.

Finally, POD was defined using Nu-DESC screening in the PACU, rather than formal clinical diagnoses assessed over the first postoperative week.30 As a result, late-onset delirium episodes and ward-based delirium may have been missed, and our incidence and impact estimates relate specifically to PACU-detected POD. Nevertheless, the hospital-stay reduction parameters in the model were drawn from the literature, using the same PACU-based Nu-DESC definition,8 9 thereby ensuring internal consistency in the underlying assumptions.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It evaluates a complex, department-wide QI intervention in a real-world perioperative setting, using a large cohort of nearly 7000 surgical patients. The convergent mixed-methods design provides both quantitative estimates of costs and effects and qualitative insights into implementation feasibility, acceptability and perceived clinical value. The use of probabilistic and deterministic sensitivity analyses further enhances the transparency and robustness of the health-economic analysis.

In summary, our findings suggest that structured implementation of a perioperative brain healthcare bundle can reduce PACU-detected POD, improve patient-centred outcomes and generate net cost savings from a hospital perspective, with modest time requirements per patient. Future research should validate these results in multicentre, prospective and preferably controlled designs, incorporate digital implementation strategies and extend outcome assessment beyond the PACU to include ward-based delirium and long-term cognitive and functional outcomes. If confirmed, perioperative brain healthcare bundles could represent a high-value, scalable strategy for improving perioperative brain health while supporting more efficient use of healthcare resources.
