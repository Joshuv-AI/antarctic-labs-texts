Lightning-induced keraunographic injury with otologic involvement in skin of color
Authors: Marco A Montoya-Flores, Kyle Mueller, Andrew S Williams, John Trinidad
Published: 2026

Case description

A 60-year-old man with darker skin presented to a rural clinic in the Peruvian Andes 48 hours after surviving a lightning strike while walking during a thunderstorm. He reported pain but remained ambulatory, with no loss of consciousness, cardiac symptoms, altered mental status, or weakness on evaluation. Cutaneous examination revealed linear and curvilinear erythematous-violaceous lesions extending from the right postauricular and supraclavicular region across the anterior chest to the left lower abdomen (Fig 1, A and B), with focal superficial erosions near the left inguinal region (Fig 2). Dried blood near the right external auditory canal suggested otologic injury (Fig 3). No classic Lichtenberg figures were identified on examination. The patient was treated with oral analgesics and topical silver sulfadiazine. At follow-up more than 3 years later, lesions had resolved without scarring, with residual postinflammatory dyspigmentation along the original keraunographic distribution, including the anterior chest and left lateral thigh (Fig 4, A-C). Weber testing lateralized to the left, and Rinne testing was positive bilaterally; findings clinically consistent with right-sided sensorineural hearing loss. Audiometry was unavailable. The patient remained independent but reported persistent right-sided deafness.

Discussion

Keraunographic injury results from a flashover—a high-voltage discharge traveling across the body's external surface—producing linear, curvilinear, or branching erythematous-violaceous lesions without the deep thermal destruction of conducted current.1 Resolution without scarring, as documented in our patient, is consistent with the superficial nature of the flashover mechanism and distinguishes it from conducted electrical burns.

Lichtenberg figures—the fernlike arborescent patterns considered pathognomonic for lightning strike—represent a distinct variant of keraunographic findings.2 Histologic examination demonstrates normal epidermis and dermis with focal extravasation of red blood cells in the subcutaneous fat, supporting classification as a physical rather than thermal phenomenon.3 Importantly, Lichtenberg figures are highly evanescent, typically resolving within 48 hours of the strike, and their absence at clinical evaluation should not exclude lightning injury.1,2,4 In resource-limited settings without dermoscopy, lesion distribution, thunderstorm exposure, and associated findings anchor diagnosis.

In patients with darker skin (Fitzpatrick phototypes IV-VI), erythematous and violaceous changes exhibit reduced chromatic contrast, limiting early visual detection of Lichtenberg branching patterns and rendering residual dyspigmentation subtle at follow-up. These limitations highlight the necessity of images depicting skin of color in dermatology teaching materials to improve diagnostic familiarity.

This case also illustrates that lightning-related otologic injury may extend beyond tympanic membrane rupture and can be clinically associated with sensorineural hearing loss through acoustic trauma, barotrauma, and/or direct electrical effects.4,5 When audiometry is unavailable, tuning fork testing may help localize sensorineural hearing loss as a persistent sequela of lightning-related injury and support referral for otologic evaluation.

In summary, this case underscores that lightning injury should be recognized through a composite clinical pattern rather than a single classic sign.5 Classic Lichtenberg figures were not identified, yet the keraunographic distribution, otologic findings, and nonscarring resolution with persistent dyspigmentation supported a superficial flashover mechanism. Recognizing this pattern may help clinicians attribute delayed cutaneous and otologic findings to lightning injury even after pathognomonic surface-discharge patterns have resolved, particularly in darker phototypes and resource-limited settings.
