Neutrophils and neutrophil extracellular traps: double-edged swords in cancer pathophysiology and therapy resistance
Authors: Yao Shen, Huijie Fang, Huiqi Xie, Siqi Zhu, Xiaoqi Wang, Shuang Liu, Jiayu Sui, Yage Xu, Yunfei Bo, Junjie Zhao, Jiaqi Jin, Jingwen Du, Peng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Among white blood cells (WBCs), neutrophils are the most abundant and multifunctional immune cells in the blood, which are also involved in diverse physiological and pathological processes, particularly affecting the deterioration of the tumor. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), composed of chromatin DNA studded with granule proteins, play a crucial role in reactive sterile inflammation. Recently, neutrophils have garnered focus in cancer research due to their participation in cancer initiation, proliferation and metastasis. As important players in the tumor microenvironment (TME), tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) are viewed as “a double-edged sword” in cancer pathophysiology.In this review, we introduce the NETs formation and associated signaling pathways. Meanwhile, recent studies on the mechanisms through by which neutrophils could promote tumors development will be described. Additionally, we outline the complex complicated molecular mechanisms underlying cancer therapy resistances and associated preface tumor treatment. Finally, we highlight latest advances in NETs-targeting treatment strategies designed to improve the efficacy of cancer therapies.Keywords: Neutrophil, Tumor-associated neutrophils, Neutrophil extracellular traps, Metastasis, Cancer, Therapy resistances

Introduction

Neutrophil extracellular traps, which consist of extracellular DNA-integrated chromatin decorated with azurophilic granular, play a critical role in fighting against microbial pathogens and have been considered as essential pathological mediators in sterile inflammation, such as autoimmune diseases and cancer [1]. Neutrophils contain abundant strategies to eliminate invading pathogens, including phagocytosis, degranulation and the release of nuclear material in the form of NETs [2]. NETs could capture and kill a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria, fungi and viruses, as well as capture macromolecules and immune complexes, thereby preventing infection from spreading [1]. Stimulation by pathogens such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, or agents like lipopolysaccharide (LPS), phorbol myristate acetate (PMA), antibodies, immune complexes, chemokines, and other physiological stimuli could induce neutrophils to extrude decondensed chromatin together with granule proteins, forming NETs [3].In addition, proteomic analysis reveals that NETs induced by different stimuli exhibit heterogeneity in protein composition and post-translational modifications. This finding adds another layer of complexity to the NETs phenomenon, suggesting that NETs formed under distinct conditions or pathways may exert different biological effects [4]. Specifically, while LPS-induced and spontaneous NETs display distinct protein compositions, PMA-and A23187-induced NETs are more homologous in this regard [4].TANs have become a key component of the tumor microenvironment.TANs promote the cancer progression by driving angiogenesis, remodeling extracellular matrix, facilitating metastasis and promoting therapy resistance [5]. In most, but not all, human tumor, high infiltration of TANs is closely associated with poor prognosis [6].Additionally, a high neutrophil-to-lymphocyte (NLR) ratio has been identified as an unfavorable prognostic factor in various cancers [7].Neutrophils also significantly contribute to cancer-related inflammation and antitumor therapies resistance [8, 9]. Chronic inflammatory stimulation is a major factor in the development of most gastrointestinal tumors. Tumor cells interact with immune cells and various factors produced by them in the inflammatory microenvironment, further transforming the inflammatory microenvironment into the tumor microenvironment which is favorable for its development [10]. Immunotherapy has emerged as a breakthrough approach in cancer treatment.

Here, we confirm the underlying trigger mechanisms and related signaling pathways that induce the formation of NETs in the context of tumors. Subsequently, emerging evidence emphasis that NETs play a critical role in the tumor microenvironment, including in tumor initiation, progression, metastasis and therapy resistance [3, 11–13]. The effects of NETs-associated components appear interrelated and complex. We highlight NETs potential mechanisms as a cancer therapy strategy, particularly focusing on their role in immune evasion. Considering the functions of NETs mentioned above, NETs or their components could serve as potential prognostic biomarkers and represent promising targets for breakthroughs in cancer therapy (Fig. 1).

Formation of NETs and their interaction with tumor cells

The mechanism of different NETs formation

As the most abundant leukocytes in humans, neutrophils are typically the first responders to sites of infection and injury. Therefore, NETs formation represents a protective mechanism against foreign invasion.However, its underlying molecular pathways remain incompletely elucidated.

To date, three distinct forms of NETs release have been characterized: One form, the lytic suicide mechanism needs the rupture of the plasma membranes. Another form, vital NETs that maintains the activity and function of neutrophil does not demand the plasma membrane rupture, which can be further divided into nuclear and mitochondrial NETs formation [14, 15] (Fig. 2).

Three mechanisms of NETs formation will be described: the lytic, viable and mitochondrial pathways, which differ in their initiating stimulus, membrane rupture dynamics and ROS dependence

Lytic NETs formation

In 2004, Brinkmann and colleagues found that when neutrophils were stimulated by LPS, a network of DNA coated with histones, NE, MPO and Cathepsin G could trap and kill invading microorganisms [16]. The process by which neutrophil is activated to form NETs is called NETs-trapping death, which is a cell death program distinct from apoptosis and necrosis, firstly describe the generation of NETs prior to cell death or release of lytic NETs [17].PMA activates neutrophils by engaging the PKC and RAF-MEK-ERK signaling pathways, leading to NADPH oxidase activation and ROS production. Concurrently, activated PAD4 converts arginine residues on the histones into citrulline, causing chromatin condensation. MPO and NE translocate to the nucleus, facilitating chromatin remodeling crucial for NETs formation [18].Given the established ROS dependence of NETosis, the researchers using the NADPH oxidase inhibitor diphenyleneiodonium chloride (DPI) confirmed its suppressive effect on NETs generation [19]. Furthermore, NE also could active Caspase11,which bind to GSDMD on the membrane and rapidly cleave GSDMD into its active form (GSDMD-NT), creating pores in the plasma membrane and granule membranes, triggering cell swelling and the loss of plasma membrane integrity, leading to the release of NETs into the extracellular space, exposing the cell contents and aggravating the inflammatory response [20–22].Recently, Wu et al. recently described 6,7-dichloro-2-methylsulfonyl-3-N-tert-butylaminoquinoxaline(DMB), as a direct and selective GSDMD agonist, suppresses tumor growth while preserving GSDMD-expressing immune cells by pulsed and low-level sagging. In addition, anti-PD-1 combined with DMB could significantly reduce tumor volume compared to the use of a single drug [23].

The mechanism of NETs formation may differ depending on the initial stimulus activating neutrophils, as studies have shown [4]. Interestingly, NETs formation was suppressed in the absence of NADPH oxidase in patients with chronic granulomatous disease and in gene-knockout mice stimulated with Aspergillus fumigatus or PMA [24, 25]. However, NETs formation stimulated by Staphylococcus aureus was not associated with oxidant production [26]. Subsequently, it was shown that Candida albicans, ionomycin and nicotine also induce NETs release, a process independent of NADPH oxidase production [27–30]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which nicotine and ionomycin stimulate NETs production depend on AKT signaling and calcium ion-mediated mitochondrial ROS release (oxidants are required, but the mechanisms of acquisition are different) [29–31]. In addition to ROS, NETs formation also requires citrullination of histones, which is dependent on the function of PAD4 protein. PAD4 inhibitor can inhibit NETs formation in vitro [32].Numerous studies have confirmed that pharmacological or genetic inhibition of MPO and PAD4 significantly suppresses NETs release [33–35]. Conversely, NETs formation induced by Pseudomonas aeruginosa operates independently of MPO activity, while pathogens such as Candida albicans, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and cholesterol crystals trigger NETosis via PAD4-independent mechanisms [28, 36–38]. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that histone citrullination is insufficient to drive chromatin decondensation [39]. Critically, NE inhibition abolishes decondensation without affecting histone citrullination suggesting a decoupling of these processes [40].In summary, the mechanism of NETs formation depends on the original stimulus [4].

Viable NETs formation

In 2012, Yipp et al. showed that the neutrophils also could release NETs without rupturing cytoplasmic membranes, fully retaining their ability to migrate and phagocytose invading bacteria [41]. Notably, this process occurs rapidly(5–60 min). Mechanistically, NADPH oxidase is not required for Staphylococcus aureus-induced non-lytic NETs formation, and neutrophils maintain migratory and phagocytic capacity post-release [41, 42].In addition, this NETs formation requires the activation of specific receptors, including TLRs (Toll-like receptors), as well as complement receptors during infection with Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans.In vivo-formed NETs appear within minutes and measure several micrometers in diameter, contrasting in vitro-generated lytic NETs that emerge hours post-stimulation with strand diameter of 15–100 μm [17, 18, 41, 43]. When E. coli was injected in vivo, platelets immediately bound to neutrophils and induced NETs production, but no neutrophil-mediated lysis was observed [44]. Indeed, in vivo and in vitro experiments, neutrophil bound to lipopolysaccharide-activated platelets produce NETs within minutes but remain unstained by SYTOX Green, a cell-impermeable dye. In 2019, Paul Kubes et al. described that neutrophils bound to TLR4-activated platelets and then released NETs in a non-lytic manner during human sepsis [31]. During this process, NE translocates to the nucleus, PAD4 activates, and chromatin decondenses [45].Crucially, the release of NETs preserves plasma membrane integrity by secreting protein-coated chromatin through vesicles, allowing the neutrophils to survive to perform further functions [41].

Mitochondrial NETs formation

A third mechanism describes NETs formation primarily containing mitochondrial DNA rather than nuclear DNA [18].Studied indicate mtDNA release occurs under pathological conditions including oxidative stress, genotoxic stress, elevated proinflammatory factors and viral infection [46]. This process is triggered by stimuli such as cytosolic calcium influx or TLR ligation, which induce mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) overproduction [47]. Subsequent mitochondrial membrane permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening enables extrusion of oxidized mitochondrial DNA-protein complexes into the extracellular space [48]. Critically, mtNET formation occurs independently of nuclear DNA decondensation, NE and PAD4 [49, 50]. In the meantime, the neutrophils remain physiologically active and the expelled DNA could not detect nuclear proteins. In SLE, mtNETs release oxidized mtDNA into the extracellular environment that induces anti-oxidizing mtDNA autoantibodies production, triggering autoimmune pathogenesis [49].Within the TME, hypoxia is an important feature, which is associated with tumorigenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), tumor progression, tumor metastasis, tumor invasion and therapy resistance. So it is closely related to poor patient prognosis [51]. However, mitochondrial NETs enhance glycolysis and mitochondrial ATP synthesis, thereby accelerating cancer progression [52].

Lytic vs. viable vs. mitochondrial NETosis - they play different roles in cancer progression

During cancer progression, the formation mechanisms and biological effects of lytic NETs, viable NETs and mitochondrial NETs may indeed influence tumor development in different ways. Here are the differences the three and their potential roles:

Lytic NETs

Lytic NETs release large amounts of proteases (such as MMP-9) and histones, directly destroying the extracellular matrix and promoting tumor cells invasion [53].Histones (such as H3, H4) released from lytic NETs could act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activate NF-κB signaling via TLR2/4/9, and promote tumor metastasis [54].NETs released after this type of cell death may trigger a strong local inflammatory response, activate macrophages to polarize toward the pro-tumor M2 type, forming an immunosuppress microenvironment-as seen in pancreatic cancer-in synergy with the fibrotic barrier [53, 55]. Excessive NETs may also block T-cell infiltration by forming a physical barrier [56].Additionally, chemotherapy/radiotherapy-induced survival signals: NETs released after chemotherapy activate the PI3K/AKT pathway through cathepsin G, promoting tumor cell survival, as shown in studies in leukemia and breast cancer [57].

Viable NETs

Viable NETs contribute to tumor cell survival by capturing circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and forming “pre-metastatic niches” [56]. The sustained release of viable NETs maintains a chronic inflammatory environment through inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and promotes EMT, a process observed in pancreatic cancer [53, 55]. They could directly inhibit T cell function by upregulating PD-L1 expression or releasing arginase-1 (Arg-1), thereby inducing immune tolerance [56]. Additionally, it could also promote regulatory T cell (Treg) recruitment and suppress antitumor immune responses, which is a mechanism studied in lung and colorectal cancers [53, 58].Moreover, viable NETs promote fibrosis through TGF-β signaling, forming a mechanical barrier that limits drug delivery in pancreatic cancer [55].

Mitochondrial NETs

Mitochondrial DNA, which is rich in unmethylated CpG motifs, can activate stronger inflammatory responses through TLR9 and cGAS-STING pathways, and promote tumor cell migration and metastasis [59]. Mitochondria-specific enzymes released with mitochondrial NETs, such as Caspase-1, activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) in the tumor microenvironment, thereby accelerating extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation [60].Notably, the unique role of mitochondrial NETs lies in inducing the secretion of type I interferon (IFN-β) through the cGAS-STING-IRF3 axis, which may activate antiviral immunity, but in cancer it may be hijacked by tumor cells to promote immune evasion [61, 62].Studies have shown the levels of mtDNA and PD-L1 in EVs from patients with oral cancer function as a potential diagnostic biomarker for anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy [63]. Mitochondrial-derived oxidative products (such as mitochondrial ROS) can directly impair the function of CD8 + T cells. Mitochondrial DNA in mitochondrial NETs may induce autophagy in tumor cells and enhance chemoresistance by the STING pathway [61]. Additionally, mitochondrial ROS can also promote tumor cell survival via NF-κB signaling [62].

Three different mechanisms may drive cancer progression through different molecular mechanisms, such as DAMPs release, immunosuppression or matrix remodeling, and roles may vary depending on tumor types and therapeutic interventions. Future studies are warranted to clarify the spatiotemporal distribution and synergistic effects of the three NETs in tumors design combinatorial therapies for precision targeting.

The oncogenic effect of neutrophils

Neutrophils promote inflammatory pathology to drive carcinogenesis

A classic metaphor:“Cancer is a wound that never heals, and inflammation is its accomplice.” Inflammation critically fuels cancer progression by inducing tissue damage, with neutrophils serving as key mediators of this process [64].Recent evidence indicates that factors secreted by cancer cells trigger NETs formation, which in turn promotes cancer metastasis by inducing a pro-inflammatory response in tumor cells [65–67].Thus, neutrophils functionally link inflammation and cancer. In the HRAS G12V -driven melanoma zebrafish model, neutrophils are attracted to the inflammatory site of postoperative trauma, where they interact with the premalignant cells and create conditions conductive to their proliferation, thereby increasing cancer formation [68]. Using an anti-Ly6G antibody to deplete the entire neutrophil population damaged the carcinogenic effects in chemically induced and spontaneous cancer models. Similarly, Yang et al. also reported that NETs triggered an aggressive inflammatory response characterized by COX2 upregulation in the captured HCC cells, which promote HCC metastasis. Pretreating HCC cells with the celecoxib (COX2 inhibitor) increased cell death post-NETs exposure and significantly reduced metastatic burden [65].

Tumor associated neutrophils induce DNA damage

Neutrophils play a critical role in cancer occurrence and progression, which could produce and release genotoxic DNA materials to increase DNA instability [69]. They cause DNA damage by releasing ROS, which induces cancer cell proliferation and promote metastasis [70]. However, a new mechanism inducing DNA damage had been reported. Neutrophils could release granules carrying pro-inflammatory microRNAs, including miR-23a and miR-155, which cause replication fork collapse and inhibit homologous recombination, increasing DNA double-strand breaks and genomic instability, thereby causing DNA damage [71].Thus, miR-23a could also promote angiogenesis in hypoxic lung cancer cells, increase vascular permeability and enhance the ability of tumor cells to migrate across endothelial cells [72]. Shang et al. found that the HP1BP3/miR-23a axis played an important role in promoting esophageal cancer growth and metastasis. Targeting this axis significantly inhibited tumor growth and metastasis in vivo, highlighting its role in cell proliferation and invasion [73]. Meanwhile, Zhou et al. showed that miR-155 was released from melanoma cells via exosome, which induced the activation of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and promoted the expression of VEGFA, FGF2 and MMP9 through CAFs [74]. Upregulation of PD-L1 expression in lymphoma cells by miR-155 was found to suppress CD8 + T cell function by the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, enhancing immune tolerance of tumor cells [75]. Therefore, as a key regulator of cancer, taking effective measures to interfere with its expression may be a potential anti-cancer therapeutic strategy.

How the different composition of TME, including microbiome, impacts NETs composition and anti/pro-tumor properties?

The different components of TME, including microbiota, immune cells, stromal cells, and metabolites, can influence NETs formation function through various mechanisms, thereby regulating their antitumor or protumor properties. Herein, we summarize the regulatory roles of each component on NETs and their effects on cancer progression.

Microbiota

More and more evidence implicates the tumor microbiota as a factor that can influence cancer progression [76, 77].Viruses have been reported to trigger NETs release through direct interaction with Toll-like receptors (TLRs) TLR7 and TLR8 on neutrophils [78].Study had revealed that in EBV-associated epithelial cancers, neutrophils were attracted and induced to extrude NETs, thus interfering with CD8 + T cells and NK cells cytotoxicity, forming a physical barrier to block immune cell infiltration and promote tumor progression. This process was verified to be stimulated by the CXCL10-CXCR3 axis through a positive feed-back loop. The results showed that patients with higher NETs levels had shorter overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in EBV-associated epithelial patients [79].In the HCC models, dysregulation of gut microbiota drives the release of VEGF and MMP-9 from NETs, degrades the ECM and promotes neovascularization, accelerating intrahepatic metastasis [80].Mounting evidence indicates that bacteria and cancer cells can co-exist in patients and may even collaboratively facilitate metastasis [81, 82]. This understanding holds significant implications for clinical strategies aimed at preventing metastasis in cancer patients. In the present study, we reveal the detrimental impact of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infection-induced pneumonia on cancer metastasis, which is mediated through the formation of NETs. NETosis has been recognized as a key defense mechanism through which neutrophils counteract pathogenic bacterial infections [83]. During infection, NETs entrap and degrade bacteria, thereby curbing pathogen proliferation and minimizing host damage [84]. However, residual NETs can also perpetuate inflammatory responses that adversely affect healthy tissues, particularly in individuals with cancer [85]. This finding indicates that lung metastasis of breast cancer alters immune cell composition, especially neutrophils in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), which plays a vital role in metastatic progression. Stimulation with Staphylococcus aureus recruit neutrophils into pulmonary tissues and induces neutrophil death via NETs formation, both in vitro and in vivo. Researchers further establish that NETs structures are critical for facilitating cancer dissemination [86]. Importantly, NETs induced by pulmonary bacterial infection act as a negative regulator in cancer recurrence and metastasis, a process modulated by autophagy [87].In conclusion, targeting NETosis represents a promising therapeutic avenue for suppressing metastatic spread. Key future directions include elucidating how autophagy modulates the transcriptional regulation of NETosis and defining the functional contribution of autophagic processes to cancer recurrence and metastasis.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs)

CAFs have emerged as key players in the TME, exerting multifaceted influences on tumor progression, immune modulation, angiogenesis, and therapeutic response. It is reported that CAFs could secrete amyloid β to stimulate the NETs formation through a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated pathway, thereby promoting tumor progression [88].As is well known, fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19) functions as a tumor-promoting gene in multiple cancers [89, 90]. This research demonstrates that FGF19 serves a critical function in colorectal cancer liver metastasis. Elevated expression and secretion of FGF19 are strongly associated with hepatic metastasis and reduced overall survival among CRC patients. Using an in vivo model, it was observed that overexpression of FGF19 enhances the ability of CRC cells to metastasize to the liver. At the mechanistic level, FGF19 modulates immune responses to establish a pro-metastatic environment within CRLM. It drives the transdifferentiation of hepatic stellate cells into inflammatory cancer-associated fibroblasts (iCAFs) through FGFR4-JAK2-STAT3 pathway-dependent activation of IL-1α autocrine signaling. These iCAFs, stimulated by FGF19, enhance neutrophil recruitment and facilitate the generation of NETs in metastatic hepatic niches by secreting complement C5a and IL-1β, thereby promoting the colonization of CRC cells in the liver. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of FGF19 signaling with fisogatinib markedly reduces FGF19-driven liver metastasis in mice [91].Study has shown that HCC-derived CAFs (HCC-CAFs) secrete CXCL12, which recruits neutrophils and also upregulates their PD-L1 expression via a IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway. These activated PD-L1 + neutrophils exert a tumor-promoting effect by inhibiting T-cell proliferation and IFN-γ production [92].Beyond producing CXCL12, CAFs can also produce amyloid β, promoting NETs formation through a mechanism involving ROS, thereby contributing to tumor advancement [88]. Moreover, in pancreatic cancer, malignant cells are capable of inducing NET formation, which then promotes the transformation of hepatic stellate cells into CAFs [93].

In conclusion, these findings suggest that the stromal components in the TME play synergistic roles in enhancing the invasiveness of gastric cells and promoting metastatic dissemination. Strategies against CAFs-NETs interactions should provide a new therapeutic avenue for inflammation-associated cancers.

Lactic acid accumulation in cancer metabolic reprogramming

Studies show that lactic acid accumulation is a metabolic danger signal triggering neutrophil extracellular trap release (NETosis), enhancing the immune escape, reducing immunotherapy efficacy and driving the formation of immunosuppressive TME [94, 95].Evidence suggests that extracellular acidosis caused by the lactic acid secretion from cancer cells is a key regulator of neutrophil apoptosis and function [96]. As observed by flow cytometry and Wright staining, neutrophil apoptosis is delayed when extracellular acidosis reduces intracellular pH [96, 97].In this context, activation of the apoptotic key regulator caspase 3 is also suppressed [96].Acidic TME-induced decreases in intracellular pH alter the activity of various intracellular enzymes. Cao et al. found that an acidic environment promotes another functional feature of neutrophils, characterized by suppressed reactive oxygen species production and poor phagocytic capacity [96]. Importantly, lactic acid in the acidic TME could facilitate the neutrophils differentiation into its N2 phenotype(tumor-promoting type) [98].Extracellular acidosis is a symbol of human malignant tumors [99–101].TANs in an acidic TME express high levels of β2 integrin and CD11b/CD18, which promote cancer progression through various mechanisms, including T-cell suppression, angiogenic factors production, and proteases secretion such as MMP-9 and elastase [98]. Although the impact of lactic acid on neutrophils and the exact mechanisms of action have yet to be fully characterized, it is evident that TME acidity significantly enhances tumor-promoting functions of TANs.

Neutrophils and NETs in tumor metastasis

Neutrophils, the most abundant inflammatory cells in the body, derive from bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells and function in pathogen removal and tissue repair. They could affect the early stages of the metastatic cascade by enabling cancer cells to leave the primary tumor sites and migrate through NETs release [14].However, in tumor contexts, the tumour-infiltrating neutrophils (TINs) could mediate potent immunosuppression by expressing PD-L1, releasing VEGF to promote angiogenesis and releasing MMPs to induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition, which collectively accelerating tumor progression and impeding treatment [102–104]. Study has shown that TINs are highly expressed in renal cell carcinoma and could release elastase within NETs, which disseminate tumor cells and constitute a major obstacle to anti-tumor therapy [105]. Xia et al. reported that the abdominal infectious complication (AIC) after the postoperative gastric cancer could stimulate the neutrophils to release the higher NETs than in patients without postoperative infection, which facilitate GC metastasis in vitro and in vivo [106]. In colorectal cancer, NETs could promote the tumor cells proliferation, invasion and migration. Guo et al. similarly demonstrated neutrophils promote the pancreatic metastatic microenvironment development along with tumor cells proliferation and metastasis [107]. Clinical evidence indicates that NETs could independently predict disease progression, correlating with overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and disease-specific survival (DSS). Zhang et al. found that the NETs scores strongly prognosticate outcomes in multiple cancers, including Kidney clear cell carcinoma, Colorectal cancer and Lung adenocarcinoma [108]. In addition, Park et al. identified NETs in breast tumor tissue by immunofluorescence staining. Notably, the NETs levers were the highest for lung metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer [109]. Therefore, Neutrophils and NETs have become one of the research hotspots of acting as chaperones to distant metastatic sites [105]. Numerous studies indicate that NETs facilitate metastasis, as detailed below.

Neutrophils and NETs awaken dormant tumor cells and regulate metastatic proliferation

CTCs detach from the primary tumor and disseminate via blood flow to establish metastatic lesions in distant organs. Disseminated tumor cells in a metastatic organ could exist in three distinct states: dissemination, dormancy, and colonization [110].The latest research shows the tumor cells could remain dormant for extended periods within target organ stroma, allowing them to resist treatment, evade immune surveillance, and persist quiescently. NETs awaken disseminated dormant cancer cells and promote primary tumor progression and metastatic recurrence. Mikala Egeblad et al. reported that sustained lung inflammation also caused the formation of NETs [111]. Two NETs-associated proteases, neutrophil elastase and matrix metalloproteinase, sequentially cleaved laminin. The proteolytically remodeled laminin triggered dormant cancer cells proliferation through integrin a3b1 signaling and FAK/ERK/MLCK/YAP signaling [112]. Based on these results, an inhibitory antibody targeting NET-remodeled laminin was developed to mitigate the nicotine or LPS induced lung inflammation, preventing the dormant cancer cells from awakening and proliferating [52, 112]. Meanwhile, researchers found that Paclitaxel‌ could damage stromal cells, leading to the release of cytokines like IL-6 and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), which induce neutrophils infiltration, modulate effector T cell function, awaken dormant tumor cells and promote the proliferation of dormant cancer cells. Treating mice with antibodies that neutralizing IL-6 and/or G-CSF, along with Selumetinib, could significantly inhibit awakening of chemotherapy-induced dormant cancer cells [113]. This discovery is crucial for advancing novel anti-cancer treatments, providing a preventive strategy to avoid dormant tumor cells from proliferating to the metastatic tumor (Fig. 3).

Tumor-associated therapies induce cell cycle arrest in tumor cells, which maintain dormancy and remain undetectable as disseminated cells. Then, tumor cells adapt to the new microenvironment by acquiring mutations and epigenetic modifications, thereby developing resistance to anti-tumor therapies and immune surveillance. NETs could awaken dormant cancer cells and promote tumor cells proliferation, leading to relapse and metastasis. Research has found a preventive strategy to avoid dormant tumor cells from being awakened

Neutrophils and NETs protect cancer cells to promote metastasis

NETs could form the physical shields to avoid the infiltration of CD8་T cells into tumor sites. Encapsulation of tumor cells by NETs impairs the CD8་T cells cytotoxicity, rendering the tumor cells unable to make direct contact with Cytotoxic T Lymphocyte(CTL) [114]. Teijeira Á et al. first injected intrasplenically fluorescent LLC cells in mice which have been established subcutaneous tumor cells and injected fluorescent substrate after six hours later. Intravital microscopy (IVM) observation of the liver was then performed. It was found that the hepatic sinusoids of tumor-bearing mice were enriched with NETs, which were around tumor cells. The author counted the number of tumor cells that come into contact with NK cells and CD8 + T cells. They found that the number of tumor cells come into contact with immune-killing cells is greatly reduced when NETs are present [115]. Meanwhile, the immune response of CD8་T cells and NK cells will be inhibited, which promote the tumor metastasis. William et al.also reported the TNBC-secreted cytokine CHI3L1 could recruit neutrophils into the TME and induce “Suicidal” formation of NETs that prevented CD8 + T cells from infiltrating the tumor stroma to kill cancer cells at the tumor site [116]. Using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry, CCDC25 was identified as a receptor in cancer cells that recognized the specific molecular structures of extracellular DNA. Studies found that NETs could trap colorectal cancer cells to promote their spread in the liver by CCDC25, leading to colorectal cancer liver metastasis [117].Using a murine model of intra-abdominal sepsis to simulate the postoperative inflammatory, the researchers found that upregulated expression of β1-integrin enhanced CTCs adhesion to NETs in vivo [118].It is also shown that NETs could trap and protect CTCs and promote tumor metastasis [119, 120].

Neutrophils and NETs promoting the formation of pre-metastatic niche

In recent years, our understanding of tumor metastasis mechanisms has become increasingly clear. The pre-metastatic niche is a unique microenvironment influenced by the primary tumor and host factors. The seed and soil hypothesis are a vivid metaphor for this process. Neutrophils significantly contribute to the colonization of cancer cells at distant metastatic sites for growth [121]. In fact, neutrophils significantly contribute to the colonization and metastasis of tumor cells by modulating the pre-metastatic niche [122–124].It was observed in experiments that neutrophils aggregate in the lungs prior to metastasis in breast cancer and melanoma, promoting lung metastasis from primary tumors [125–127]. At the same time, Bellomo reported that neutrophils infiltrate the metastatic sites in advance and interact with cancer cells, thereby promoting pancreatic cancer proliferation and metastasis [128]. Neutrophils exhibiting this phenomenon have also been observed in ovarian cancer [123]. As the important component of pre-metastatic niche, neutrophils could also increase the angiogenic milieu in tumors by secreting various cytokines (such as MMPS, VEGF, TGF-β and IL-17), which aggravate immunosuppression, promote inflammation, induce angiogenesis and remodel extracellular matrix [129].With deeper understanding of neutrophils’ role in the pre-metastatic niche, the potential therapeutic implications of targeting neutrophils interest researchers increasingly. Tyagi reported that nicotine activated the STAT3 signaling pathway to recruit N2 neutrophils, facilitating the pre-metastatic niche and inducing the mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET) of tumor cells [130]. And using the inhibition (Salidroside) could effectively impede the nicotine induced N2 neutrophils polarization and lung metastasis.

Neutrophils and NETs injure vascular integrity and facilitate EMT

Tumor metastasis reflects not only the activity of cancer cells themselves but also the integrity of the vascular structure [131].The current studies demonstrate that NETs released by tumor infiltrating neutrophils facilitate both processes by up-regulating the tumor cells migration ability and at the same time down-regulating VE-cad expression on endothelial cells [132].In peritumor stroma of human HCC, activated monocytes can induce the expansion of IL17A-producing T cells and subsequent neutrophils recruitment, which then promote tumor angiogenesis by releasing MMP9-mediated ECM degradation [133, 134].Jiang et al. also demonstrated that neutrophils might reduce the tight junction between endothelial cells and disrupt vascular integrity. Its magnitude was sufficient to promote tumor intravasation by the release of NETs [135].Meanwhile, Lu et al. showed that NETs could enhance epithelial-mesenchymal transition and lung cancer cells invasion. In vitro, NETs form a network around tumor cells, which may promote distant metastasis [136]. Inhibition of NETs production by DNase 1 effectively eliminated NETs causing cancer growth and metastasis. NETs could induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition to endow epithelial-derived cells with invasive properties. Intriguingly, using DNase 1 treatment minimally impacts on the invasiveness and migration of cancer cells [137]. However, inhibition of NET-associated elastase could degrade EMT induction [138].

NETs release certain substances that promote tumor metastasis

The previous studies emphasized the strong association between metastatic cancer cells, neutrophil recruitment, and NETs formation. Increasing evidence suggests that tumor cells and stroma could trigger neutrophils to release NETs. However, the granular protein released by the NETs could result in tumor cells recruitment and migration. Lennard Marten Weide et al. indicated metastasis size could be highly interrelated with neutrophil and NETs invasion and necrosis in human melanoma metastasis [139].It is ultimately concluded that the neutrophils are recruited to necrotic tumor sites, leading to increased necrosis via NETs-induced. Necroptotic cells release DAMPs, which in turn will recruit neutrophils to form a positive feedback loop [140].Increasing studies show that Necroptosis-associated DAMPs release have a twofold function in cancer [141].They can activate inherent and adaptive immune responses to enhance anti-tumor immune responses by releasing antigens and specific cytokines such as interleukin 1 A (IL-1 A) into the tumor microenvironment. However, DAMPs play complex mechanisms in promoting aging-related cancers progress, including inducing immune cell senescence, altering the tumor immune microenvironment, driving chronic inflammation and boosting cancer cells mutation and metastasis. High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is the most studied nuclear DAMP, with its role varying depending on the cellular context [142]. For instance, under hypoxic conditions, human HuH7 and mouse Hepa1–6 hepatocellular carcinoma cells release HMGB1 and mtDNA into the cytoplasm, where their interaction activates the TLR9 signaling pathway, advancing cancer progression [143]. So, identifying specific DAMPs that influence neutrophil function provides a promising strategy for precision prevention and treatment of cancer.

Neutrophil could closely interact with cancer cells through various mechanisms, such as awaking dormant tumor cells, secreting cytokine, releasing protease and forming neutrophil extracellular traps. These interactions cancer tumor proliferation, invasion and metastasis. Thus, inhibiting cancer cells -neutrophils interaction could enhance the survival of cancer patients (Fig. 4).

Tumor cells could induce NETs formation by producing chemokine receptor agonists of CXCR1 and CXCR2, which protect cancer cells and promote metastasis. NETs-DNA combined with the CCDC25 receptor promotes colorectal cancer liver metastasis. NETs could promote angiogenesis, ECM remodeling, TME and pre-metastatic niche formation, immune cells exhaustion and epithelial-mesenchymal transition

Neutrophils and NETs in cancer therapy resistance

So far, the cancer treatment remains a persistent challenge. However, available treatment methods like chemotherapy, radiation therapy and immunotherapy present major challenges. Increasing evidence indicates that neutrophils, as the main core components of the TME, play critical roles in cancer therapy resistance. Meanwhile, the cancer cells could also recruit the neutrophils to aggregate at tumor sites and initiate signaling pathways linked to the premetastatic niche formation. Teijeira Á et al. reported that CXCR1 and CXCR2 chemokine receptor agonists produced by tumors induce NETs formation [115]. However, neutrophils are now recognized as a diverse cell population with seemingly contradictory roles in both promoting and fighting cancer. Notably, this diversity extends to NETs, which have been shown to both impede and improve the efficacy of anticancer treatment. A significant challenge in understanding varying outcomes is identifying influencing factors, such as the cancer type, the mutational spectrum of targeted cells, the cancer’s location in the body, and whether the tumor is primary or metastatic. Based on these findings, the mechanism of NETs in cancer therapy resistance is highly complex.

Neutrophils and NETs in immunotherapy resistance

In recent years, the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy has gained significant attention. Indeed, most patients have shown resistance or relapse after immune checkpoint blockade. Studies have shown that higher levels of neutrophils infiltration were closely associated with shorter survival and result in the resistance to ICIs [144]. Therefore, the mechanisms by which interactions and competitions between different cell populations in the microenvironment drive and/or reshape resistance to immunotherapy remain to be further elucidated. Here we focus specifically on the emerging role of neutrophils and NETs in the efficacy of immunotherapy.

NETs directly stimulate T cell exhaustion, impairing antitumor immunity

A potential way to evade the tumor immune system is by inducing exhaustion of T cells, particularly CD8 + T cells. PD-1 blockade could reinvigorate exhausted CD8 + T cells with favorable therapeutic effects [145]. The CT10 regulator of Kinase-like gene (CRKL), locating on chromosome 22q11.21 in humans, is known as an adaptor protein composed of an Src homology 2 (SH2) domain followed by two SH3 domains. CRKL is overexpressed in a variety of cancers and CRKL amplification drives carcinogenesis and resistance to epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors [146–148].Using an HCC model, Xie et al. demonstrated that CRKL overexpression recruited TANs recruitment to promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, resulting in the cytotoxic CD8 + T cells exhaustion and a remarkable increase in PD-L1 + TANs within tumors. Combining CRKL inhibitors with anti-PD-1 could effectively treat HCC [149].As we all know that CD276 (B7-H3), a pivotal immune checkpoint, mediates multifaceted tumor immune evasion mechanisms, including reduced CD8་ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells numbers and suppressed T helper type 1-mediated immune responses [150, 151].Xiong et al. reported that CD276 overexpression enhances tumorigenesis in ESCC by facilitating the NETs formation and decreasing NK cell numbers. CXCR2 is notably overexpressed in neutrophils and upregulated CXCR2 facilitates the TANs recruitment, promoting distant cancer metastasis. Importantly, inhibition of CD276 significantly decreased CXCL1 expression, thereby reducing NETs formation via the CXCL1-CXCR2 axis and increasing NK cells infiltration [152]. Recent studies report that NET-DNA binds to the transmembrane and coiled-coil domains 6(TMCO6) on CD8་T cells to enhance the immunotherapy resistance and accelerate the HCC progression [153].Importantly, targeting the axis effectively suppresses the HCC progression. This may introduce novel approaches for cancer treatment. Collectively, these studies reveal a fundamental mechanism by which NETs contribute to tumor immune escape through exhausting T cells and reducing the NK cells. Of note, the connection between chemokines and cancer progression also provides further insights into novel immunotherapy targets and possible synergistic treatment regimens [154](Fig. 5).

Neutrophils and NETs in immunotherapy resistance. The combination of targeting TANs PD-L1 + or NETs formation and using ICI could improve immunotherapy efficacy and lead to better prognosis. (1) CRKL inhibitors combined with anti-PD-1 could be more effective for HCC treatment. (2) The potential value of targeting the B7-H3 checkpoint together with PD-1 to serves as a new immunotherapy against cancers. (3) The NET-DNA binds to the transmembrane and coiled-coil domains 6(TMCO6) on CD8་T cells, resulting in immunotherapy resistance

Tumor cells mediate the conversion of neutrophils into immunosuppressive phenotype

The neutrophils are transported from bone marrow into peripheral blood and then infiltrate into the inflammatory tumor microenvironment, resulting in spatiotemporal heterogeneity. Therefore, the regulation of neutrophil plasticity is closely linked to the microenvironment they inhabit. Opportunistic cancer cells often exploit the remarkable plasticity of neutrophils to establish a microenvironment conducive to cancer cell survival. TANs play a crucial role in tumor progression and exhibit prolonged survival. In laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC), Zhu et al. reported that tumor-derived G-CSF and GM-CSF upregulate anti-apoptotic Mcl-1 expression and downregulate Caspase-3 levels through activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Long-term survival of TANs leads to the accumulation of aged CXCR4 + neutrophils with potent immunosuppressive properties, which are associated with poor patient prognosis. Additionally, prolonged survival enhances the immunosuppressive function of TANs toward CD8 + T cells, thereby promoting in vitro and in vivo progression and growth of human LSCC tumors [155]. Meanwhile, Meng et al. also discovered that the hepatoma cell-derived NAMPT-NTRK1 axis reprograms CD10 + ALPL + neutrophils, maintaining them in an immature state and inhibiting their maturation and activation. These neutrophils exhibit strong immunosuppressive activity by inducing an apparent “irreversible” exhaustion of T cells in terms of cell number, frequency, and gene expression profile, which affects the awakening of immune surveillance and consequently triggers resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy [156].

However, neutrophils play different roles in ICIs’ efficacy, as they were shown to either impair the immunotherapy response or eliminate tumor cells following ICIs. Recently, Mikael J. Pittet et al. found that successful cancer immunotherapy acutely expanded tumor neutrophil numbers. These neutrophils showed a unique immune phenotype to inhibit the tumor progression that the researchers called a Sellhi state. Therapy-elicited neutrophils are associated with interferon regulatory factor 1(IRF1),which is effectively modulated by a CD40 agonist or a PD-1 inhibitor [157]. Simultaneously, Taha and colleagues found that T cells also activate a population of neutrophils with unique genotypes and phenotypes that commonly attack tumor cells [158].Therefore, we need to further understand how the interplay between highly heterogeneous neutrophils and T cells drives the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and resistance to immunotherapy.

Neutrophils and NETs in chemotherapy resistance

The chemotherapy resistance is a common phenomenon in human tumor therapy, especially in advanced metastatic disease. When effective, chemotherapy causes cell damage and death, which strongly affects the TME and recruits inflammatory cells. It is reported that neutrophils in chemotherapy-resistant breast cancer are highly sensitive to ferroptosis [159]. Furthermore, a specific subtype of IL1β + CXCL3 + CD4+ (Fer-CD4) T cells, which are abundant in chemotherapy-resistant tumors, showed a strong association with neutrophil ferroptosis. These Fer-CD4 T cells regulated ferroptosis in neutrophils by controlling MOAT1 expression through the IL1β/IL1R1/NF-κB pathway. They also released CXCL3, IL8, and S100A9 to sustain neutrophil populations within the tumor microenvironment. In a feedback loop, ferroptotic neutrophils further promoted the differentiation of Fer-CD4 T cells. In spontaneous murine cancer models, disrupting the interaction by targeting either IL1β + CD4 + T cells or IL1R1 + neutrophils inhibited neutrophil ferroptosis, strengthened antitumor immune responses, and reversed chemoresistance. Collectively, these results reveal how ferroptotic neutrophils influence the immune milieu and identify promising therapeutic strategies for reestablishing immune surveillance and chemotherapy response in breast cancer [159–161].According to related reports, NETs, as an independent risk factor, is related with poorer survival in cancer patients and chemotherapy resistance. The studies found that chemotherapy drugs such as cisplatin, paclitaxel and fluorouracil could directly stimulate the neutrophils to release NETs, which reduce the effect of chemotherapy on lung metastasis of breast cancer. The NETs caused by chemotherapy drugs could alter the tumor microenvironment, and further affect the chemotherapy sensitivity of tumor cells. For example, Alexandra Mousset et al. reported that chemotherapy-treated cancer cells secrete IL-1β, which in turn triggers NETs formation. Two proteins associated with NETs are required to reduce treatment resistance: integrin- αvβ1, which captures latent TGF-β, and matrix metalloproteinase 9, which cleaves and activates the trapped latent TGF-β. TGF-β activation could induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition in cancer cells and endow the treatment resistance. In addition, an MMP9 inhibitor could eliminate NET-induced TGF-B activation, chemoresistance and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. NETs regulate activities of neighboring cells by capturing and activating cytokines and suggest targeting the IL-1β-NETs-TGF-β axis could improve chemotherapy resistance, which may prompt further treatment strategies [162]. Studies show that NETs levels were closely related with chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer blood samples and NETs may sever as a prognostic biomarker in breast cancer patients treated with chemotherapy. As is well known, chemotherapy can cause numerous complications. It has been reported that NET-associated histones can cause acute kidney injury (AKI) through TLR2/4 pathways in cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity and the NETs formation in the kidney after Cisplatin treatment occurs independent of tumor burden [163]. NETs have been targeted for the treatment of non-infectious diseases such as cancer. Cancer cells could secrete certain cytokines that interlinked with NETs in the process of chemotherapy, sometimes even forming a positive feedback loop. Therefore, further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying NETs formation.

Neutrophils and NETs in radiotherapy resistance

Radiotherapy (RT) is a cancer treatment that uses the high-energy radiation to destroy the cancer cells, preventing their proliferation and causing their death. RT, as a routine treatment, has been used for various types of cancer. Nevertheless, a substantial number of patients remain radiotherapy resistance. Thus, it may not be sufficient to effectively inhibit the local progression of cancer. In a Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) model, radiotherapy could stimulate neutrophils recruitment and polarize them into an anti-tumor phenotype. Neutrophils, characterized by elevated ROS levels, are crucial in reversing epithelial-mesenchymal transition by inhibiting phosphatidylinositol-3 Kinase (PI3K) and protein Kinase B (AKT) signaling in LLC cells [164]. Furthermore, although radiotherapy led to CD8་T cells infiltration of the tumor, this phenomenon was counterbalanced by neutrophils depletion. Recently, Teijeira Á et al. also reported that low-dose ionizing irradiation could induce NETs in a neutrophil-intrinsic manner, thereby hindering immune-mediated cytotoxicity. Radiation-induced NETs could cause the cancer therapy resistance and bring a poor prognosis [165].In 2016, Takeshima et al. found that the combined applications of G-CSF improved RT-mediated antitumor activity by activating RT-NS [166]. In a lymphoma mouse model, RT combined with targeted complement inhibition enhanced the therapeutic effect by promoting tumor cell apoptosis, inflammation and early neutrophils infiltration. Neutrophils depletion completely abrogated the augmentation of RT efficacy by complement inhibition [167]. Moreover, studies reported that radiation could damage healthy lung tissue, cause sterile inflammation and pre-activate the infiltrating neutrophils, which further modifies the lung microenvironment for the transplantation of tumor cells. Their team delved into RNA-Seq data from lung epithelial cells and found Notch signaling in lung epithelial cells was activated by pre-activated neutrophils. The process may be mediated by the release of granular proteins from activated neutrophils. Radiation-induced cancer metastasis is significantly counteracted by blocking degranulation and inhibiting the neutrophil-mediated Notch pathway [168].Another related study identified a role of NETs in local recurrence (also known as locoregional recurrence or failure, LRF) following breast cancer radiotherapy. A model of LRF after surgery and radiotherapy showed that high expression of ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (Enpp1 or CD203a) in circulating tumor cells enhanced the expression of haptoglobin (hp), resulting in neutrophils infiltration, NETs formation, and tumor recurrence. Accordingly, Enpp1 and inhibition of NETs improved the LRF-free survival rate, suggesting that blocking NETs formation has a significant impact on relapse-free survival [169].The clinical relevance of this study is also consistent with the role for NETs in promoting distant metastatic relapse in cancer patients in an inflammatory milieu [170].Focusing on the emerging role of NETs in cancer, several studies have described how NETs contribute to RT resistance. In response to radiotherapy, neutrophils are recruited into bladder tumors and form NETs, leading to resistance to RT. The cancer cells release high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in response to RT,, which promotes the formation of NETs through mediating toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling. Using NE inhibitors (NEI) and DNase 1 could enhance the effectiveness of radiation therapy, indicating their potential as adjuncts to overcome therapy resistance. Meanwhile, in clinical, NETs were also found persistent presence in bladder tumor patients who did not respond to radiotherapy [171].

In conclusion, these studies identified that NET-targeting strategies could be used as potential therapeutic targets to improve radiotherapy resistance. However, recent studies have shown that off-target exposure to radiation may injure health tissue, which promote the formation of a premetastatic niche by neutrophils. This inadvertently creates a favorable environment for metastatic cancer cells. RT remains a powerful tool for controlling cancer, and we have described the involvement of neutrophils in radiation resistance, with the goal of further enhancing the efficacy of this cancer treatment. We need a better understanding of why cancer spreads, what conditions promote metastasis of cancer cells, and how to prevent it [168].

NETs components have contradictory effects on the development of cancer

Neutrophil elastase (NE)

NE is one of a series of proteolytic enzymes released by the neutrophils, being released into the extracellular space after degranulation. It translocates to the nucleus and hydrolyzes nuclear histones, causing chromatin to disperse and promoting NETs formation [33, 172].NE facilitates neutrophils migration and tissue repair by cleaving various ECM proteins, including collagen and elastin [173, 174]. Furthermore, it contributes to immune and inflammatory responses through its ability to activate and modulate various cytokines and chemical mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor and interleukins [175, 176].Importantly, it could enhance tumor proliferation, migration, and vascularization, induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition and alter TME [177]. Prior research indicates that NE could lead to adverse effect in breast cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer and colorectal cancer [178–182].Researchers have proposed several mechanisms to explain how neutrophil elastase drives the pro-tumorigenic activity. First, NE could directly activate proliferative signaling via extracellular transactivation of membrane receptors (EGFR and TLR4), triggering MAPK cascade activation. In breast and prostate cancer models, NE induces ERK phosphorylation and upregulates ERK-dependent genes (FOS) through this pathway. Critically, MEK inhibitor pretreatment completely blocks NE-driven proliferation by suppressing ERK activation [179, 182].NE could enter tumor cells through endocytosis, degrade insulin receptor substrate (IRS-1), activate PI3K signaling pathway and promote cancer progression. Studies have shown NE can promote recurrence and regeneration after esophageal cancer surgery. NE cleaves and releases transforming growth factor-α (TGF-α) on the cell membrane, activates epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), and triggers extracellular regulated protein kinases 1 and 2 (ERK 1/2) signaling pathway [183–185].Notably, pretreatment with TGFα-neutralizing antibodies completely abrogates neutrophil elastase (NE)-induced proliferation in keratinocytes by blocking downstream signaling [186].Beyond activating oncogenic signaling, NE promotes tumor progression by inactivating tumor suppressors-a pivotal step in carcinogenesis. This is exemplified by NE-mediated cleavage of EMILIN1, which abolishes its tumor-suppressive function: whereas intact EMILIN1 inhibits leiomyosarcoma proliferation via α4/β1 integrin engagement, NE-cleaved EMILIN1 loses this capacity [187, 188]. Additionally, NE inactivates thrombospondin-1 (Tsp-1), thereby enhancing lung tumor growth and pulmonary melanoma metastasis through loss of anti-tumorigenic restraint [189].In vivo experiments with nude mice (KSN/SLC) revealed that inhibiting NE could suppress the liver metastasis in CRC cells. Sivelestat is a highly selective NE-specific inhibitor that could be effective by competitively inhibiting the activity of NE [190, 191]. Clinical studies also have suggested that NE inhibitor has been approved for the treatment, which could effectively reduce lung injury score and improve lung function in patients with ARDS [192]. However, NE also has great potential to promote the development of cancer treatment strategies. It is reported that inhibiting NE reduces the formation of NETs, leading to decreased liver metastasis of CRC cells [193]. Similarly, NE inhibitor could also inhibit NETs formation in mice with lung carcinoma, which prevent the cancer progression [194]. Another therapeutic target associated with NE inhibition is GSDMD. Study have shown that inhibiting GSDMD through disulfiram or genic deletion could suppress NETs formation, reduce multiple organ dysfunction and sepsis lethality [195]. Therefore, inhibition of NE activity may be regarded as a novel drug therapeutic strategy for cancer and inflammatory diseases. And we hope to develop more effective treatment by further understanding the role of NE’s role in tumor [196].

However, NE have also been shown anti-tumor effects. After uptake by cancer cells, it cleaves the cancer cell surface receptor CD95 through its protease activity, releasing a fragment containing the death domain (DD). This fragment further binds to the highly expressed histone H1 isoform in the cell, inhibiting cell survival signaling and activating the apoptotic pathway. Remarkably this mechanism exhibits specificity for the selective killing of cancer cells [197].This study deepens the complexity of neutrophil proteolytic specificity in tumorigenesis and immunomodulation [198].

Matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9)

MMP9 is another key neutrophil serine protease that is released into the extracellular space through degranulation and its binding to NETs [199].MMP9 is another key neutrophil serine protease that is released into the extracellular space through degranulation and its binding to NETs [199].MMP9 degrades collagen and the basement membrane, promoting neovascularization, invasion, and metastasis, while also promoting tumor progression by inducing an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [200].The gain of function (GOF) CTNNB1 mutations (CTNNB1 GOF) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) result in the suppressive tumor immune microenvironment and the lower remission rete of immunosuppressive therapy. Mechanistically, CTNNB1 GOF downregulated sirtuin 2 (SIRT2), leading to promotion of β-catenin/lysine demethylase 4D (KDM4D) complex formation, which fostered the transcriptional activation of MMP9. And MMP9 could inhibit the G protein-coupled receptors signaling mediated by the chemokine receptor C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3) by cleaving the slingshot protein phosphatase 1 (SSH1) the surface of CD8 + T cells, thereby impeding the activation and migration of CD8 + T cells. Notably, researchers found that the combination of anti-MMP9 antibody and PD-1 inhibitor treatment had a better effect on tumor regression and survival period, enhanced the toxicity and infiltration of T cells, and no obvious toxicity was observed [201].Additional, studies also demonstrate that Dysadherin/MMP9 axis could promotes tumor metastasis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition deposition in colorecta cancer. And targeting this axis can reverse these adverse effects [202].

Proteinase 3 (PR3)

PR3(encoded by Prtn3) was originally termed myeloblastin due to its ability to induce proliferation and inhibit differentiation of HL-60 cells, a promyelocytic leukemia cell line [203]. As a member of neutrophil serine proteases, PR3’s role have been extensively described in bacterial killing and post-translational modification of cytokines [204, 205].Lung metastasis is the major cause of breast cancer-related mortality [206, 207].Studies have shown that tumor-secreted protease cathepsin C (CTSC) promotes breast-to-lung metastasis by regulating recruitment of neutrophils and formation of NETs. Mechanistically, CTSC enzymatically activates neutrophil membrane-bound PR3 to facilitate interleukin-1β processing and nuclear factor κB activation, thus upregulating IL-6 and CCL3 for neutrophils recruitment. Furthermore, the CTSC-PR3-IL-1β axis induces neutrophil reactive oxygen species production and formation of NETs, which degrade thrombospondin-1 and support metastatic growth of cancer cells in the lungs [86]. This finding provides potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for breast cancer intervention.

Cathepsin G

CG is a potent serine protease belonging to chymotrypsin-like produced and released by neutrophils and devotes to killing microbes as well as processing host proteins, including extracellular matrix and cell surface receptors [208, 209]. CG can be expressed in cells and is capable of degrading many extracellular matrix precursor proteins, such as collagen, elastin, laminin. It participates in regulating the secretion and deposition of matrix precursor proteins in both normal and cancerous environments [210]. The hydrolytic activity of CG influenced by DNA derived from neutrophils, which can block the protective activity of endogenous protease inhibitors in the tissue, thereby promoting the hydrolysis of CG. Furthermore, studies found CG of NETs-originated could promote HCC cell invasion by decreasing the levels of adhesion molecules and elevating the levels of inflammatory mediators in vitro [211].

When CG works in a non-hydrolytic way, it is able to activate platelets, and as platelets cover tumor and transfer their major histocompatibility complex (MHC) to tumor cells, the level of MHC in tumor cells is elevated, activating natural killer cells (NK and avoiding the immune evasion of tumor cells, achieving the suppression of tumor cells [212, 213]. Other studies have shown that CG may be a tumor suppressor gene for breast, bladder cancer and colorectal cancer [214–216]. As the arginine residues in the CG sequence leads to working in a hydrolytic or non-hydrolytic way, its promotion or suppression of cancer metastasis also depends on its hydrolysis mode. In summary, the role CG in cancer metastasis is worth further investigation.

Strategies for inhibiting tans formation and disrupting tumor-neutrophils interactions

Neutrophils release NETs, which are a special network of DNA wrapped with citrullinated histones (Cit-H3, a NETs marker) and microbicidal proteases including PR3, NE and MPO. Based on the cancer-promoting effect of NETs, targeting NETs component factors is considered a promising strategy to prevent or inhibit cancer progression and metastasis. This section outlines prevalent and extensively researched strategies for inhibiting TANs formation and mitigating interactions between cancer cells and neutrophils [117].

PAD4 is an enzyme responsible for post-translational protein modification by catalyzing the conversion of arginine or mono-methylarginine residues to citrulline. In neutrophils, extensive citrullination driven by PAD4 triggers the release of nuclear chromatin, leading to the formation of web-like structures known as NETs [217].PAD4 overexpression has been documented in multiple tumor types [218, 219].Moreover, in colorectal cancer, tumor cell-derived PAD4 promotes hepatic metastasis by mediating citrullination of type I collagen, a key extracellular matrix component [220].PAD4 could also catalyze the deamination of histone arginine residues to citrulline under hypoxic conditions, which prompt breast cancer progression and liver tumor growth and angiogenesis [221].Recently, study demonstrates that neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) cells secrete 5-HT to communicate with neutrophils, inducing neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation in the liver. This process recruits disseminated tumor cells and promotes hepatic metastasis. Mechanistically, 5-HT triggers histone serotonylation (H3Q5ser) and coordinates histone citrullination (H3cit) in neutrophils, orchestrating chromatin decondensation for NETs generation [222].Additionally, a compound(inhibition of PAD4 enzyme activity) could block the PAD4-H3cit-NET signaling pathway and inhibit the growth of solid tumors and pulmonary metastatic nodules in the 4T1 in situ mouse model, in which the tumor microenvironment changed form an inhibitory state to anti-tumor environment by regulating the proportion of immune cells and reshaping the neutrophil phenotypes and functions [223]. Marina et al. also demonstrate that administration of a novel specific small molecule BMS-P5(a target of PAD4) with to multiple myeloma (MM)-bearing mice delays the appearance of symptoms and disease progression [32].In addition, administration of anti-PAD4 drugs combined with PD-1 antibody treatment after cryoablation increased CD8 + T cell infiltration, which enhanced the effect of PD-1 antibodies and improved patient prognosis in colorectal cancer patients with liver metastasis [224].Recently, several compounds inhibiting PAD4 activity have been reported. These include CI-amidine, a non-selective irreversible PAD4 inhibitor, and GSK-484, a reversible selective PAD4 inhibitor [225–227]. The mechanisms through which PADI4 regulates primary tumor growth remain to be fully elucidated. Given its critical role in facilitating tumor progression and metastasis, PAD4 represents a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of highly metastatic cancers involving PAD4-driven mechanisms.

Chemokines such as CXCL1, CXCL8, IL17, IL8, or CCL2 promote the recruitment and activation of neutrophils in the TME and blood circulation. Blocking these chemokines or inhibiting their receptors could reduce the neutrophils recruitment and NETs formation, which are promising antitumor effects [228].NETs have become key factors in the pathogenesis of malignant hematological diseases. Research on lymphoma and NETs has shown that their elevation in plasma and tumor tissue is associated with a poor prognosis in patients. The main mechanism involves the interaction between IL-8 derived from Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma (DLBCL) and its receptor (CXCR2) on neutrophils, leading to NETs formation through signaling pathways, including Src, p38, and ERK. The newly formed NETs directly upregulate TLR9 in DLBCL, subsequently activating the NFκB and STAT3 pathways and ultimately promoting tumor progression [229].CXCR2 is crucial for recruiting MDSCs to the TME and promotes immunosuppression.Anti-CXCR2 antibodies could block MDSCs recruitment, improving patients response and counteracting immunotherapy resistance, which demonstrated that CXCR2 blockade could regulate the tumor immune microenvironment [230, 231]. Gulhati P et al. also reported that the combination of immune checkpoint therapies and a CXCR1/2 inhibitor has significant effect on the tumor immune microenvironment [232]. Similarly, the use of CCR2i and CXCR2i could strengthen antitumor immunity and improve the FOLFIRINOX chemotherapy effectiveness in PDAC [233].The study has showed that the expression level of CXCR2 is significantly related to the prognosis and survival of lung cancer patients. Targeting CXCR2 could significantly inhibit tumor growth, reduce TANs infiltration, enhance T cell response, and increase the sensitivity to the chemotherapeutic drug cisplatin [234].In addition, Pant et al. showed that co-inhibiting CCR2 and CCR5 decreased the M-MDSCs infiltration in brain tumor while enhancing the number of tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells and cytokine secretion, effectively synergizing with anti-PD-1 therapy in glioma [235]. In terms of metastasis, Chao et al. have shown that the CXCR2/CXCL1 axis plays a key role in osteosarcoma lung metastasis via paracrine signaling. The CXCL1 is highly secreted in lung blood vessels and the CXCR2 receptor is express in osteosarcoma cells, which promotes metastasis upregulating VCAM-1 expression [236].Therefore, targeting the CXCR2/CXCL1 axis is worth exploring in treatment. Li et al. also have reported that most HCC patients present with RIP3 deficiency, which promotes CXCL1/CXCR2-mediated MDSCs chemotaxis, immune evasion and HCC progression. In RIP3 knockout mice, CXCR2 antagonists inhibit MDSCs infiltration and lead to delayed HCC progression [237].

TGF-β is a potent immunosuppressive and differentiation factor that can promote tumor growth [238].Treatment with a small molecule inhibitor of TGF-β receptor signaling significantly reduced the growth of mouse mesothelioma tumors, accompanied by an increase in CD8 + T cells [239].Another factor also involves that TGF-β has a significant effect on TANs polarization in the TME [5, 240]. This study indicated that TGF-β is able to induce TANs differentiation into two phenotypes with different functions: type N1 and type N2. N1 TANs show anti-tumor activity with high cytotoxicity and proinflammatory cytokine expression levels, while N2 TANs support tumor growth and have immunosuppressive effects. The TGF-β receptor complex can engage both Smad-dependent canonical signaling and Smad-independent non-canonical signaling [241, 242]. Studies show that smad3 activation is related to a poor outcome in patients with non-small-cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) because TANs is predominantly polarized to the N2 pro-tumor state. In this model, Smad3-KO mice show increased neutrophils infiltration and mainly conversion into the N1 antitumor state, which exhibits enhanced killing of tumor cells in vitro [243].Thus, SMAD3 inhibitors are identified as a therapeutic strategy to prevent the progression of cancer by reversing the tumor-promoting into anti-cancer microenvironment in vivo [244]. As important inflammatory cells in the TME, TANs contribute to cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, angiogenesis and immune regulation. Therefore, we could achieve the goal of treating cancer by converting N2-type TANs (pro-tumor cells) into N1-type TANs (anti-tumor cells). Studies indicate that TGF-β inhibition promotes the polarization of N1-type TANs (anti-tumor cells), enhancing cancer immunotherapy and chemotherapy sensitivity [245, 246]. Effective leveraging the dual nature of TANs will pose a significant future challenge. It will also provide us with new insights for treating cancer.

NETs are present at high levels in a variety of malignant tumors, and the close relationship between NETs and tumor-associated thrombosis has been reported [171].Studies have shown that NETs induce a prethrombotic state and thrombosis through various mechanisms. NETs lead to the release of a large number of histones and proteases from neutrophils into the blood. NETs act as mesh scaffolds to physically capture platelets or interact with them. Histones increasingly activate platelets and enhance their adhesion, aggregation and release functions. These changes ultimately lead to fibrin deposition and red blood cell capture, thereby accelerating the clotting process [247].Venous and arterial thrombosis were increased in tumor-bearing mice compared with tumor-free mice and citrullinated histone H3 was directly detected in the thrombus [248].The injection of endotoxin into the peritoneum of tumor-bearing mice induces the formation of NETs, which in turn induces a hypercoagulable state and coagulation dysfunction [249]. Studies have shown that lPS-activated neutrophils adhere to tumor cells through the Mac-1/ICAM-1-mediated pathway [250].Neutrophils may then act as “escorters” in peripheral sites [251]. This might explain the aberrant sites of metastasis in renal cell carcinoma (RCC), such as the skin and lung [252].The interaction between targeting NETs and thrombosis has important clinical implications for effectively intervening in the formation of tumor-related complications.

These approaches enhance the effectiveness of conventional cancer therapies and reduce drug resistance through synergistic effects. However, due to their limitations and complexity, further research involving preclinical models and clinical trials is necessary to assess their feasibility and effectiveness in cancer therapy(Fig. 6).

Different strategies for inhibiting TANs formation will be described: PAD4 inhibitors, CXCR1/2 inhibitors, promoting TAN N1 maturation, NE inhibitors, DNase 1 therapy.Therefore, targeting the formation of TANs will better improve patients’ survival and overcome tumor therapeutic resistance

Concluding remarks

With the role of neutrophils and NETs in the efficacy of anticancer therapy continuing to be revealed, various strategies targeting neutrophils and NETs formation are being combined with established anticancer treatments for clinical evaluation. However, neutrophils remain heterogeneous and different neutrophil subpopulations may have unique and opposing effects on specific anticancer treatment outcomes. This diversity extends to NETs, which show both protumor and antitumor activities. This heterogeneity further complicates our understanding of their role in responses to anti-cancer therapy, as identifying the factors that drive these different outcomes remains a major challenge. In this review, the role of the NETs formation regulated by different signaling pathways in tumor progression and metastasis is described. Therefore, a deeper understanding of NETs and their effector cells and the upstream and downstream signaling pathways involved is needed. To overcome resistance to traditional drug treatments in tumors, the complex regulatory mechanisms and multiple functions of neutrophils and NETs within the TME must be considered. Despite existing limitations, the growing interest in NETs as potential cancer therapies presents a very worthwhile opportunity to explore and possibly improve the poor prognosis of cancer patients.

Outstanding questions

How to induce neutrophils to form an antitumor phenotype and block the bidirectional action between cancer cells and NETs to optimize the cancer treatment outcomes?

Are there any biomarkers in neutrophils and NETs that can be used to predict tumor treatment response and precisely assess the severity and prognosis of patients?

What are the different cellular and molecular mechanisms by which NETs interfere with anti-tumor immunity?

Identifying these precise mechanisms will greatly improve therapeutic resistance in cancer.
