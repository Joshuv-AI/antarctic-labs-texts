Insight into Natural History and Phenotype in Untreated Adults with X-Linked Hypophosphatemia
Authors: Giampiero I. Baroncelli, Filomena Cetani, Benedetta Toschi, Alessandra Bulleri, Alessandro Isola, Maria Rita Giuca, Elisabetta Carli, Maria Rita Sessa, Caterina Pelosini, Angela Michelucci, Pasquale Comberiati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
X-linked hypophosphatemia (XLH) is a rare genetic rachitic/osteomalacic and dental disorder caused by pathogenic variants in PHEX gene resulting in fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) excess leading to hypophosphatemia by renal phosphate wasting and decreased 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D production. The aim of the study was to provide insight into natural history and phenotype in untreated adults with XLH (no phosphate supplements and active vitamin D metabolites or burosumab). Clinical, biochemical, skeletal features, co-morbidities, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were examined in 52 patients (51.5 ± 12.2 years; 18 men and 34 women). Mean height Z-score and mean leg length Z-score were lower than normal (P < 0.0001) and were lower (P < 0.01) in men (–3.8 ± 1.2 and –4.2 ± 1.2, respectively) than in women (–2.9 ± 0.8 and –3.4 ± 0.9, respectively). BMI was in the range of overweight in 30 patients (57.7%) and in the range of obesity in 15 patients (28.8%). Most of patients had severe skeletal deformities and dental-periodontal abnormalities. Pseudofractures were documented in 17 patients (33%). Mean concentration of intact FGF23, osteocalcin, PINP, CTX, and BALP was higher in men compared to women (P < 0.05–P < 0.001). PROs ranged from moderate to severe scores, with no difference (P = NS) between sexes. The phenotype in adult individuals with XLH was characterized by severe and disproportionate short stature, overweight/obesity, severe skeletal deformities with difficulty walking, osteoarticular pain, poor dental health, and reduced quality of life. Introduction

X-linked hypophosphatemia (XLH, MIM 307800) is a rare inherited and progressive skeletal disorder with an estimated prevalence of 1:20,000–60,000 [1]. XLH is caused by a PHEX (Phosphate Regulating Gene with Homologies to Endopeptidases on the X chromosome) gene pathogenic variant that results in an excessive production of fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) leading to excessive urinary phosphate wasting and impaired vitamin D metabolism by stimulating renal 24-hydroxylase activity and reducing renal 1α-hydroxylase activity with insufficient production of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25(OH)2D) [2]. In addition to hypophosphatemia, locally accumulating components of the extracellular matrix, including phospho-ASARM peptides and osteopontin, resulting from decreased PHEX function independently of FGF23, contribute to impaired matrix mineralisation [3, 4] and dentin formation [5].

Typical phenotype in children with XLH is the association of rachitic skeletal deformities, stunted growth with body disproportion, and peculiar dental-periondontal lesions [1, 4]. In adults with XLH, chronic hypophosphatemia leads to impaired skeletal remodeling and accumulation of unmineralized osteoid, histologically manifested as osteomalacia [6]. Short stature, overweight, severe dental-periondontal lesions, pseudofractures, enthesopathy, osteoarthritis, and skeletal deformities are main features observed in adults with XLH [7–13]. Patients with XLH also experience musculoskeletal pain, stiffness, and impaired physical function that severely impact on health-related quality of life (HRQL) and place limitation on daily activities [10–18].

The lifelong impact of XLH is influenced by age at diagnosis, severity of disease, and compliance and effectiveness of treatment. In most of children with XLH, treatment with oral phosphate supplements combined with active vitamin D metabolites (Pi/D) started in early infancy resulted in improved outcome even though it did not completely normalize skeletal development and linear growth [1, 4, 19]. In symptomatic adults, Pi/D treatment improved musculoskeletal pain, osteomalacia, and oral health, but did not decrease or prevent the development of osteoarthritis, enthesopathies or hearing loss [13, 20–23]. However, most adults with XLH had poor tolerance with oral Pi and severe complications, such as secondary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism and nephrocalcinosis, frequently developed [4, 13, 21, 23, 24].

Phenotypic examination in adults with XLH who had not received Pi/D treatment may be useful for assessing the natural course of disease and the progression of its during life. Nevertheless, few data on the phenotype evolution in never-treated patients with XLH across the lifespan are available. Javaid et al. [16] in adults with XLH who currently received (40%) or had received within the past 2 years (54%) Pi/D treatment showed that multiple musculoskeletal features beginning as early as in their 20 s, and progressively accumulating with age.

The aim of this study was to examine clinical, biochemical and skeletal features, co-morbidities, and HRQL in adults with XLH who did not receive any treatment or had received Pi/D for very short periods of time during childhood and/or adulthood in order to provide insight into the natural history and phenotypic expression of the disease.

Methods

Patients and Study Design

This is a retrospective study that included patients with XLH born between 1929 and 1992 who had never received (never-treated) or had received short and discontinuous treatment with Pi/D alone or in combination (minimally-treated) during childhood and/or adulthood. None of the patients had received burosumab. All the patients were diagnosed and followed at the Pediatric Endocrine Unit of the University Hospital of Pisa, Italy. A total of 52 Caucasian patients, 18 men and 34 women (male-to-female ratio 1:1.9) ranging in age from 31 to 83 years (51.5 ± 12.2 years; median 50.9 years) were enroled in the study. All patients were examined from June 2024 to March 2025. In all patients, the diagnosis of XLH was confirmed by the evidence of a PHEX gene pathogenic variant (Supplemental Table S1 and Table S2 for men and women, respectively).

Anthropometric data were measured at time of enrolment in the study. Medical data were obtained from the patient’s medical files and by interviews or visits as outpatients. A detailed analysis was performed on the treatment regimens administered to patients from the time of diagnosis. In all patients, co-morbidities, skeletal complications, number of corrective orthopedic surgeries, dental-periodontal health, and HRQL were recorded and analyzed.

In all patients, serum concentration of calcium, phosphate, creatinine, bone specific alkaline phosphatase (BALP), osteocalcin, procollagen type I N-propeptide (PINP), collagen type I C-telopeptide (CTX), intact parathyroid hormone (PTH), 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25OHD) and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25(OH)2D), and urinary phosphate and creatinine were measured at time of enrolment. Intact FGF23 plasma concentration was also assessed in 25 patients (9 men and 16 women).

At time of enrolment, patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were investigated by using Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) questionnaire designed for the assessment of lower-extremity pain, Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form (BPI-SF), Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) for depression assessment.

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee for human investigation of the Department of Clinical and Experimental Medicine, University Hospital of Pisa. All patients provided informed consent to be enroled in the study and re-examined. The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki II and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Anthropometric and Dental-Periodontal Examination

Standing height and leg length were measured by a calibrated wall-mounted Harpenden stadiometer and by a calibrated Harpenden sitting height table, respectively. Standing height and leg length were expressed as Z-score according to the average age of 23 years and sex (standing height: men, median cm 178.23; women, median cm 163.82; leg length: men, median cm 85.109; women, median cm 77.811) by using LMS growth curves of Freeman et al. [25]. Sitting height/height ratio was assessed according to the average age of 21 years and sex (men, median 0.513; women, median 0.526) by using the LMS growth curves of Fredriks et al. [26]. Weight was measured with a standard clinical balance wearing only underwear. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the formula weight (kg)/height (m2).

In all patients, history of periapical abscesses with fistulae, and the decayed, missing, or filled teeth (DMFT) index for permanent teeth were evaluated.

Biochemical Blood and Urine Analyses

Blood and urine samples were drawn between 8 and 10 am after an overnight fast. Maximum rate of renal tubular reabsorption of phosphate normalized to the glomerular filtration rate (TmP/GFR) was calculated by the following equation: Pp - (Up x PCr/UCr), where GFR was measured as creatinine clearance and Pp, Up, PCr, and UCr refer to the serum and urinary concentrations of phosphate and creatinine, respectively, as suggested by Stark et al. [27].

Intact FGF23 plasma concentration and intact PTH 1–84 (third generation) serum concentration were assessed by immunochemiluminescent sandwich assay (DiaSorin, Saluggia, Italy) on the Liaison® XL platform. A value of FGF23 greater than 40 pg/mL was considered the threshold for the diagnosis of FGF23-related hypophosphatemia [28].

Serum osteocalcin, PINP, CTX (CrossLaps®), and BALP (Ostase®) concentration was performed by an automated chemiluminescence immunoassay (IDS-iSYS, Boldon, UK) that detects both N-midfragment and intact osteocalcin, intact PINP, CTX, and BALP, respectively.

Serum 25OHD concentration was assessed by a chemiluminescent immunoassay (Lumipulse G, Fujirebio, Gent, Belgium). A value of serum 25OHD < 12 ng/mL and between 12 ng/mL and 20 ng/mL was defined as vitamin D deficiency and vitamin D insufficiency, respectively. A value of 25OHD > 20 ng/mL was defined as normal.

Serum 1,25(OH)2D concentration was measured by a chemiluminescent immunoassay (DiaSorin, Saluggia, Italy) on the Liaison® XL platform.

For all the biochemical measurements, inter-assay variability was less than 9% and intra-assay variability was less than 7%.

PROs

WOMAC index is the result of three subscales, such as pain, stiffness, and physical function that include 5, 2, and 17 items, respectively. Each scale uses the following descriptors for all items: none, mild moderate, severe, and extreme. These correspond to an ordinal scale of 0–4. The scores are summed for items in each subscale, with possible ranges as follows: pain = 0–20, stiffness = 0–8, and physical function = 0–68. A total WOMAC index is created by summing the items for all three subscales (0–96). Then, raw scores were normalized by multiplying each score by 100/96 and transformed the final score into a 0–100 scale. WOMAC index is a well-validated and reliable (Cronbach’s α: pain subscale = 0.89; physical function subscale = 0.97) instrument and it has been validated in adults with XLH [29].

BPI-SF is a multidimensional scale evaluating the intensity and the interference of pain in general activities, mood, locomotion ability (walking or using a wheelchair), work, relations with others, sleep, and enjoyment of life [29, 30]. BPI-SF has been applied in various pain conditions such as visceral, musculoskeletal, and neuropathic origin [30], as well as in adults with XLH [29]. BPI-SF is based on 9 items self-administered questionnaire; 4 items to assess pain severity score and 7 items to detect pain interference score. Each questionnaire item ranges from 0 (no pain/interference) to 10 (maximum pain/interference). For each patient, BPI-SF was expressed as total pain experience score. BPI-SF is a well-validated and reliable (Cronbach’s α: pain intensity dimension = 0.91; pain interference dimension = 0.87) instrument [30].

PCS is based on a 5-point Likert scale, from 0 (not at all) to 4 (always). Patients were asked to rate how often they experience the mentioned thoughts and feelings when they are in pain. Along with three subscale scores evaluating rumination (4 items), magnification (3 items), and helplessness (6 items), the overall score has a range of 0–52. Higher scores indicate a greater degree of pain catastrophizing; a total score above 30 represents a clinically significant level of pain catastrophizing. PCS is a reliable and valid measure of catastrophizing [17, 31].

PHQ-9 contains the 9 items from the DSM-IV used in the diagnosis of depression. Briefly, each item is evaluated on a severity scale ranging from 0 to 3 where the respondent is asked to rate how often each symptom occurred over the last 2 weeks (0, not at all; 1, several days; 2, more than half of the days or 3, nearly every day), yielding a total score ranging from 0–27. Results are scored as follows: 1–4 minimal depression; 5–9 mild depression; 10–14 moderate depression; 15–19 moderately severe depression; and 20–27 severe depression [32].

Statistical Analysis

The results are expressed as means ± SD. Comparison of clinical and biochemical data between men and women was determined by non-parametric Wilcoxon’s (Mann–Whitney) rank sum test, and the comparison of proportions by Fisher exact test. Simple linear regression analysis by Pearson's formula and multiple regression analysis corrected by the Bonferroni method were used to determine the associations among anthropometric data, biochemical measurements, and PROs. In addition, partial correlation analysis between age at diagnosis, height Z-score, and number of pseudofractures, controlling for chronologic age, was applied. To examine the relationship between chronologic age and number of pseudofractures proportional-retrospective odds logistic regression model, including covariares, was performed.

All statistical analyses were carried out using the SPSS (Statistical Package of Social Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA). A P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Mean height Z-score and leg length Z-score was lower than normal (P < 0.0001) and was significantly lower in men than in women (P < 0.01). Only one man and two women had a height Z-score > −2. Mean sitting height/height ratio did not differ between men and women (P = 0.99).

Thirty patients (57.7%) had a BMI value in the range of overweight (25 to 29.9 kg/m2); 15 patients (28.8%) showed a BMI value in the range of obesity (≥ 30 kg/m2); there was no difference between men and women (P = 0.56 and P = 0.75, respectively).

History of periapical abscesses with fistulae without trauma or tooth decay was documented in 83% of patients, without difference between sexes (P = 0.14). Complete edentulism was observed in six patients ranging in age from 50 to 63 years. DMFT index did not differ between men and women (P = 0.59).

Almost all patients showed skeletal deformities, including coxa vara, genu-varum/valgum or combined, femoral/tibial/fibular curvature, leg length discrepancy, pigeon chest, and abnormal gait. Sixteen patients (31%; 6 men and 10 women) regularly used mobility aids, and three men used wheelchairs for transportation. Scoliosis and craniosynostosis were documented in 60% and 54% of patients, respectively.

History of pseudofractures was documented in 17 patients (33%). Men had a higher number of pseudofractures than women (P < 0.05); nine patients (6 men and 3 women) had history of more than one pseudofracture, and one man had history of three pseudofractures. Mean age at first pseudofracture was 45.8 ± 5.4 years, and mean age at last pseudofracture was 50.4 ± 4.9 years; there was no difference between sexes (P = 0.12 and P = 0.15, respectively).

Almost all patients showed enthesopathic disease and osteophytes/osteoathritis, and 19% had spinal canal stenosis. Enthesopathies occurred in multiple skeletal sites in the majority of patients (92%); four patients (8%) showed enthesopathies only at pelvis.

Men experienced a higher incidence of hearing loss compared to women (P = 0.02). Hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg) was found in three patients (6%).

Three patients had developed secondary hyperparathyroidism in childhood during Pi/D that resolving with the suspension of treatment. No patient developed tertiary hyperparathyroidism or nephrocalcinosis assessed by renal ultrasound.

Almost all patients had undergone one or more osteotomies, with a mean of 8.3 ± 4.6 orthopedic surgeries. Only two women did not undergo orthopedic surgeries. Hip replacement surgery was performed in six patients (12%); in three out of them hip replacement was bilateral. Knee replacement surgery was performed in nine patients (17%); bilateral knee replacement was performed in five out of them. Two patients underwent laminectomy for two times. After the end of growth, five patients had undergone lengthening of lower limbs combined with the correction of skeletal deformities, with a height gain of 6.56 ± 0.94 cm. Surgical correction of craniosynostosis was performed in two men.

Most of patients (n = 41, 79%) were never-treated; only 11 patients (21%) were minimally-treated with Pi/D alone or in combination for up to approximately 4 years during childhood. Only three patients had received discontinuous Pi/D treatment during adulthood approximately for 1.5 years, but they did not receive any treatment during childhood. At time of enrolment none of them were receiving Pi/D treatment.

Overall, 25 patients (48%) currently used pain medications, primarily nonsteroideal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). The percentage of patients taking NSAIDs was higher (P < 0.02) in older (> 50.9 years) than in younger (< 50.9 years) individuals (69%, n = 18, 9 men and 9 women, and 31%, n = 8, 1 man and 7 women, respectively). In addition, opioids were prescribed in seven patients (13%); men used opioids more than women (P < 0.05).

Discussion

The present study provides data on the long-term effects of XLH in a cohort of most never-treated adults. Stature and leg length were very compromised, especially in men. Approximately 60% of patients showed a value of BMI in the range of overweight and one-third of them were frankly obese. Dental-periodontal abnormalities affected the majority of patients and some of them were completely edentulous, confirming previous data in XLH adults born before 1970 who had received vitamin D or no treatment during childhood [33].

A variable prevalence of skeletal abnormalities has been reported in mixed populations of adults with XLH who had received Pi/D or no treatment [7, 9, 12, 16, 34–38]. In our study, almost all patients showed skeletal deformities, enthesophytes, and osteoarthritis. Osteophytes occurred at spine and at synovial joints, with predominance at weight-bearing joints, as found by X-ray [37] or ultrasound [38]. The mechanisms causing the progression of enthesopathies and age-related skeletal abnormalities are largely unknown [12, 35, 38, 39]. Connor et al. [22] suggested an association between enthesopathies and age, BMI, and PTH concentration. Herrou et al. [39] argued that mechanical factors/stress could be a main cause for the development of enthesophytes. Furthermore, Meaux et al. [40] hypothesized that inflammation may have a crucial role in the development of obesity and extraskeletal complications in patients with XLH. Likely, the pathogenesis of enthesopathies is multifactorial in patients with XLH and it seems to be independent from Pi/D treatment.

Pseudofractures have been reported in 18–58% of adults with XLH [6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 16, 35, 36]. Javaid et al. [16] showed that more than 25% of patients with XLH aged 18–29 years had experienced a fracture, increasing to over 65% in those older than 60. In our cohort, pseudofractures were documented in one-third of patients; the mean age at first fracture was 46, whereas Skrinar et al. [9] reported a mean age of 26 years. In our patients who had history of more than one pseudofracture the last one occurred near 50. In our cohort of patients, men had higher rate of pseudofractures compared to women, as reported by Hardy et al. [35].

Height Z-score was lower, whereas BMI, number of orthopedic surgeries, intact FGF23 plasma concentration, WOMAC index and PHQ-9 were higher in patients with history of pseudofractures than in those with no history of pseudofractures. Moreover, the number of pseudofractures was positively correlated with age, serum BALP concentration, and number of orthopedic surgeries, and inversely correlated with height Z-score and TmP/GFR values, suggesting that the occurrence of pseudofractures could be associated with the severity of disease that progresses with age.

Pseudofractures represent a main clinical expression of osteomalacia and may contribute to bone pain and difficulty walking, even though the absence of pseudofractures does not exclude osteomalacia. Moderate to severe osteomalacia has been detected by histomorphometry in asymptomatic adults with XLH [20].

Craniosynostosis was reported approximately up to 60% of patients with XLH [41], but most of them were asymptomatic. In our cohort, only two patients (4%) underwent surgery for the correction of craniosynostosis.

Hearing loss has been reported more frequently in adults than in children with XLH (48–82% and 9%, respectively) [8]. Hearing loss was found in 37% in our patients, especially in men. Likely, hearing loss was closely related to the progression of disease itself and contributed to clinical burden.

The prevalence of hypertension was low in our cohort, as found by Bouzemane et al. [42], suggesting that adults with XLH do not have an additional risk of developing hypertension or left ventricular hypertrophy.

Secondary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism, nephrocalcinosis, and nephrolithiasis are generally related to Pi/D treatment and should be considered a main reason for stopping therapy after growth completion [13, 21, 23]. None of our patients developed nephrocalcinosis likely due to the absence of Pi/D treatment; nephrocalcinosis did not occur in three minimally-treated patients who showed transient secondary hyperparathyroidism.

Our patients showed raised mean serum concentration of PINP and CTX, suggesting remodeling imbalance between osteoblast osteoid formation and osteoclast activity, respectively; these data were in keeping with histomorphometric investigations showing impaired rates of bone formation and resorption in untreated adults with XLH [20]. Hypophosphatemia, reduced serum concentration of 1,25(OH)2D, and increased serum concentration of PTH could be main factors affecting the coupling of bone formation and bone resorption. Elevated CTX levels have been reported in patients with XLH receiving Pi/D in comparison with untreated patients [43–45], supporting the hypothesis that Pi/D treatment may increase bone resorption. McKenna et al. [44] showed that the frequency of abnormal elevation in biochemical bone markers was BALP 96%, PINP 52%, osteocalcin 28%, CTX 72%, and uNTX 48%. Nagata et al. [46] did not find increased serum concentration of BALP, osteocalcin, and PINP in adults with XLH compared with healthy controls. Conversely, Ros et al. [47] did find reduced serum osteocalcin levels. These discrepancies could be related to different assay methods and range of normality, degree of osteomalacia, Pi/D treatment, vitamin D status, or circulating concentration of PTH and FGF23.

The evidence that men had higher intact FGF23 and bone markers concentrations, prevalence of hearing loss, and number of pseudofractures, and lower height Z-score and leg length Z-score, compared to women could suggest a lyonization mechanism. Beck-Nielsen et al. [7] showed that males tended to be skeletally more severely affected than females but without differences in anthropometric and biochemical findings. Nevertheless, other studies failed to show sex-dependent differences in disease severity [48, 49]. It has been suggested that gender differences of skeletal disease in XLH patients could be due to sex hormones or physical activities rather than gene dosage effect [49]. Potential selection bias, heterogenous retrospective assessment, and broad intrafamilial phenotypic variability may be additional factors influencing gender difference in XLH patients.

In our cohort, clinical co-morbidities had considerable impact on mobility, physical functioning, ability to perform daily activities, emotional wellbeing, and social/family/work life, as found in other studies [9, 12, 15, 16]. Skeletal deformities, and especially enthesopathies, were usually associated with a worse HRQL in adults with XLH, that was close to that observed in patients with chronic diseases [14]. Our patients showed higher values of WOMAC index (mean > 70) compared with the reported population normative score of 20.1 [9], indicating that osteoarthritis/osteoarthrosis can greatly contribute to pain-related diminished HRQL.

Studies in adults with XLH have reported high BPI-SF score [9, 17, 18, 50], consistent with our results. Comparison of BPI-SF results found in our patients (mean > 5) with the values observed in controls (near 0) by delCastillo et al. [17] provide evidence that pain intensity and interference markedly impact multiple indicators of HRQL in adults with XLH.

Mean PCS and mean PHQ-9 scores were in the high range and moderate range, respectively, showing that pain and its effect on HRQL impacted mental health in adults with XLH. In our patients, mean PCS and mean PHQ-9 scores were higher than the values found in adults with XLH who had a history of Pi/D treatment and in healthy controls [17]. These data suggest that Pi/D may improve the quantification of a patient's pain experience and the depressive symptoms. Similar data were reported by using SF36-mental health score in adults with XLH [14].

About half of our patients were taking analgesic medications for pain relief, as reported in other studies [9, 12, 17, 18]. Both NSAIDs and opioids were increasingly used with advancing age indicating that disease progression and pain severity were main factors in affecting HRQL.

In our cohort, all the assessed PROs did not differ between sexes. Nevertheless, Che et al. [14] showed worse HRQL in women than in men by RAPID3 and SF36-PCS questionnaires. In our patients, no correlation was found between PROs and anthropometric data, biochemical parameters, number of pseudofractures, or number of orthopedic surgeries, as observed in other studies [16, 50].

Continuation of Pi/D in adulthood is debated as the clinical benefit is limited and poor tolerance and severe complications, such as hyperparathyroidism and nephrocalcinosis, are common [13, 21, 23]. A real-world study in adults with XLH showed that burosumab treatment was associated with improved biochemical parameters, pain, physical function, and mobility compared to Pi/D [51]. However, additional data on the long-term effects of burosumab are needed.

There were some study limitations. All patients were followed at a single tertiary center and the cohort spanned a wide birth range introducing heterogeneity in diagnostic practice and treatment. Moreover, the retrospective design of our study could have influenced the estimation of pseudofractures progression over time. PROs results were not compared with sex-matched healthy controls. However, the PROs we used have been validated in adults with XLH, and the results were consistent with the clinical symptoms and literature data. Potential selection bias could be related to measurement of intact FGF23 only in a subgroup of patients; however, intact FGF23 was measured in half of our patients for both genders, and patients with or without history of pseudofractures were equally represented. Finally, bone biopsies were not available which precluded a direct comparison of biochemical markers and histomorphometric remodeling parameters.

In conclusion, we showed that severe and disproportionate short stature, overweight/obesity, severe skeletal deformities with impaired mobility, osteoarticular pain, poor dental health, and reduced HRQL represent the main phenotypic features of never-treated or minimally-treated adults with XLH. Men showed higher prevalence of pseudofractures and hearing loss, higher concentration of intact FGF23 and biochemical markers of bone turnover, and lower final stature than women.
