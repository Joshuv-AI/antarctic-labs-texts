Continuum topological derivative - A novel application tool for segmentation of CT and MRI images
Authors: Viswanath Muthukrishnan, Sandeep Jaipurkar, Nedumaran Damodaran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionComputed Tomography (CT) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) are essential tools for unraveling anatomical and tissue properties, particularly in the head and brain. CT provides high-contrast images, particularly valuable in cases such as cerebral bleeds, and also aids in estimating cranial deformities and organ shape deviations. MRI, on the other hand, offers excellent imaging of cerebral artery regions, allowing analysis of various cerebral pathologies through different sequences. Beyond detecting common head and brain disorders, these modalities play a crucial role in identifying abnormalities in orbits, middle cerebral artery territories, brain ventricles, soft tissues, and bones. A unique aspect of brain MRI is its ability to produce multiplanar brain assessments. Both head/brain CT and MRI are invaluable for studying haemorrhage cases, with segmentation of affected areas providing detailed images for further analysis. This study explores the application of a novel mathematical technique, continuum topological derivative (CTD), for CT and MR image segmentation.MethodsThe initial stage of Continuum Topological Derivative (CTD) segmentation involves preprocessing CT and MR images due to their susceptibility to inherent noises, such as quantum mottle, and Gaussian and Rayleigh noises, respectively. In this study, we have implemented the CTD denoising algorithm to produce denoised CT/MR images, serving as ground truth for subsequent segmentation steps. Validation of the denoised CTD CT/MR images was conducted through minimal residual value computation across all case studies. Following this, segmentation of the region of interest was performed using the CTD technique, with comparisons made against Discrete Topological Derivatives (DTD), k-mean clustering and Adaptive Threshold methods. Evaluation of the proposed CTD algorithm's effectiveness in segmentation involved calculating performance metrics such as Jaccard and dice indices to assess spatial overlap of segmented images.ResultsThe CTD technique yields excellent segmentation results, not only for the delineated region of interest but also for volume-based cerebral blood areas and anomalies in the middle cerebral artery (MCA) and its territorial areas, which are substantiated through performance metrics and visual inspection by trained radiologist. This aids in determining the severity of stroke in affected patients. Additionally, a unique attempt is made to apply CTD to Electrical Impedance Tomography (EIT) images of the lungs for precise estimation of the breathing cycle. CTD successfully generates standardized images, demonstrating attenuation and density characteristics for cerebral cisterns, arteries, and ventricles.DiscussionThe denoised images obtained through CTD facilitate thorough analysis of both normal and pathological conditions, providing radiologists with enhanced capabilities to identify subtle details, particularly in areas such as abnormal cerebral artery territories, haemorrhage cases, cisterns, ventricles and arteries. Results clearly demonstrate that the combination of CTD denoising and segmentation outperforms the other three established methods in terms of both efficiency and accuracy in delineating diseased or affected areas, as evidenced by the various case studies conducted in this research. In summary, the proposed CTD method aims to delineate boundaries and contours of the region of interest, facilitating precise estimation of size and shape for accurate detection of the extent of diseased or affected areas.

Introduction

Medical imaging forms the best non-invasive technology for visual inspection of anatomical and physiological functions. Modalities like Computer-Tomography (CT), High Resolution CT (HRCT) images reveal the anatomical information, whereas the Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) provides physiological details. Segmentation of the organs, arteries, veins and disease affected areas are the important tasks in diagnosis, treatment procedures and clinical studies. Automation of segmentation using state-of-the art techniques provides efficient diagnostic benefits such as, large volume of cases with same accuracy, speed, and quick consultation with the specialist (Sharma and Aggarwal, 2010). This research work aims to probe a reliable algorithm for the CT and MR images. The fundamental radiological characteristics of CT and MR images—density, attenuation, and intensity—play crucial roles in identifying Regions of Interest (ROIs) for patient prognosis and diagnosis. Despite their significance, there remains room for enhanced analysis, particularly through refined segmentation techniques applied to CT and MR images. This research specifically tackles this issue, employing Continuous Topological Derivative (CTD) Segmentation and Discrete Topological Derivative (DTD) techniques. The primary impetus behind this study lies in the imperative need for precise identification of affected areas, which is pivotal for detecting brain or head-related diseases and disorders via CT/MRI scans.

This research endeavours to delineate ROIs encompassing ventricles, cisterns, arteries, neuro-tissues, orbits, and other pertinent areas within brain and head CT/MRI scans. Although the initial results are promising, achieving modest success in segmentation, the segmented regions shed light on density, attenuation, and intensity variations within the brain. This illumination, in turn, facilitates further analysis of diseased regions, paving the way for more comprehensive understanding and diagnosis of neurological conditions.

Segmentation

Segmentation primarily relies on the specific characteristics of the images, the modalities of imaging used, and the anatomical regions under examination. Furthermore, the similarity and discontinuity in intensity values within the images form the fundamental basis for segmentation. Within similarity-based segmentation, algorithms operate on the premise of similarities within Regions of Interest (ROI), employing techniques such as region splitting, thresholding, and region growing. Conversely, discontinuity-based segmentation hinges on the detection of edges and boundaries.

Most segmentation algorithms are engineered with the aim of precisely delineating the ROI from surrounding regions. Segmentation can be broadly categorized into two main types: global segmentation, where the entire image is segmented as a whole, and local segmentation, where the image is partitioned into smaller windows. This research focuses on ROI segmentation, which involves classifying connected pixels with unique properties.

Furthermore, boundary and edge segmentation techniques concentrate on delineating boundary and edge regions respectively, with edge techniques specifically targeting precise edge delineation that contributes to boundary determination. Representation and feature selection, along with feature recognition, are crucial for identifying ROI in segmented outputs (S et al., 2000).

In the realm of medical image segmentation, various tools such as soft and hard segmentation, thresholding, classifiers, and deformable models are utilized (Pal and Pal, 1993). While numerous segmentation methods exist, the distinguishing factor lies in their ability to quantitatively analyze key features of interest. For instance, edge detection is well-suited for segmenting chest X-rays, whereas clustering techniques are preferred for cell-type segmentation (Fu and Mui, 1981).

Essential attributes of medical image segmentation include accuracy, repeatability, reliability, robustness, and minimizing operator dependency (Sharma and Aggarwal, 2010). The automation of medical image segmentation enhances prognosis and disease diagnosis, surpassing traditional techniques in speed and accuracy (Guo et al., 2021).

Methodology

Segmentation plays a crucial role in delineating anatomical structures or diseased organs, facilitating detailed classification of abnormalities within medical images. Various segmentation approaches address a range of issues including partial volume correction (Niesporek et al., 2015), tissue volume quantification (Axel, 1980; Hu et al., 2016), extraction of diagnostic information (Aisen et al., 1986), detection of pathology (Gilbert et al., 2012; Theodore et al., 1990), correction of intensity inhomogeneity (Zhang et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2020; Trinh et al., 2023), and adjustment of grey levels (Hasan et al., 2016; Hasan and Meziane, 2016).

Popular segmentation methods encompass a spectrum of techniques such as dimensionality reduction (Babu et al., 2021; Arai et al., 2018; Kaya et al., 2017; Modiya and Vahora, 2022), rough set theory (Senthilkumaran and Rajesh, 2009; Huang et al., 2019a; Li, 2010; Hirano and Tsumoto, 2002; Kishore and Suresh, 2022; Toufiq et al.), threshold operations (Toufiq et al.; Laddha and Ladhake, 2014; Lee et al., 2015; Tra et al., 2016; Natarajan et al., 2012; Hofmann et al., 2011; Meissen et al., 2022), atlas-based approaches (Arabi and Zaidi, 2017; Bach et al., 2015; Balafar et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2022a; Zhu et al., 2022a), clustering methods (Kaur et al., 2020; Ramesh et al., 2021; Bi et al., 2021; Zhu and Ma, 2022; Devi et al., 2022; Habib et al., 2022), and neural networks (Malhotra et al., 2022; Islam et al., 2022; Awang Mustapa et al., 2022; Soomro et al., 2022; Ilesanmi et al., 2022). In this research work, the segmentation of medical images based on threshold, class values and clustering was attempted for knowing the best way of distinguishing the useful portions from the remaining parts of the images. Here, threshold adaptive thresholding, Discrete Topological Derivative (DTD), and Continuum Topological Derivative (CTD) were chosen for segmentation based on class values, whereas K means clustering was chosen for the cluster based segmentation. In K means clustering segmentation, split and merge concept, nearest neighbor (NN) and image features play the decisive role. Grouping and Euclidean distance are inherent property of K means clustering which aids in segmenting the medical image. Similar to labeling and depth factor in K means, DTD segmentation algorithm estimates the result using class and normalizing both the class as well as minimum and maximum values of the medical image. Here, the central part of segmentation depends on computing the cost function, but the most pivotal step lies in adding the weight to the cost function. In accordance with NN, the variance of the image and class values help in categorizing the required value for segmenting the image. One big disadvantage of the DTD segmentation is the number of iterations that decides the quality of the segmented image. Under certain circumstances, DTD cannot segment the image, which is evident from the never ending loop in estimating the segmentation. Threshold segmentation basically involves segmenting the background from the foreground section of the images. It is an efficient and an age old technique for segmenting images. In this work, we have used adaptive thresholding method based on variable thresholding technique. Adaptive threshold is a statistical segmentation approach where the value of the threshold is varied over the image and is a neighborhood operation. Here, image foreground has given due importance with the aid of sensitivity factor. The performance of the segmentation methods were evaluated with the help of quality metrics namely intersection and similarity index. Precision, accuracy and efficiency are the most widely evaluated metrics for estimating the performance of the image segmentation (Udupa et al., 2006; Fenster and Chiu, 2006). Continuum topological derivative (CTD)

For solving topological asymptotic expansion-based segmentation applications, perturbation based topological derivative DT(xˆ) can be conveniently expressed as(4)DT(xˆ)=limε→∞1f′(ε)∂∂τΨ(Ωτ)|τ=0

Equation (4) holds for f(ε)=0<|DT(xˆ)|<∞, where ε is the domain's radius f(ε) is the monotone function, Ψ(Ωτ) is the cost function defined for the above condition and τ∈R+ parameterizes the domain. The concept of topological derivative has been effectively utilized in the restoration of CT and MRI images, aiming at obtaining ground truth images through CTD denoising, as well as facilitating CT, MRI, and EIT segmentation of Regions of Interest (ROIs) (Viswanath et al., 2017). Various techniques such as Discrete Filter Bank (DFB), Contourlet Transform (CLT), wavelet transform, and interpolation methods have been explored on medical images to enhance contours, boundaries, and edges (Viswanath et al., 2018a, 2018b). Pyramidal Directional Filter Bank (PDFB) in conjunction with CLT has demonstrated efficacy in sub-band level decomposition (Viswanath et al., 2019a). Moreover, diffusion-based methods have shown promise in producing enhanced-segmented images in EIT (Viswanath et al., 2019b, 2019c). Functional analysis-based approaches have been effective in highlighting delineation characteristics in medical images (Viswanath et al., 2019d).

In the realm of continuum topological derivative (CTD), shape functionals' sensitivity, asymptotic expansion, boundary with edge functionals, and their asymptotic treatment using various mathematical techniques play a crucial role in deriving ground truth and segmented CT and MRI images (Viswanath et al., 2018b). The application of topological derivative extends to solving sensitivity of cost functions, addressing directional problems while considering anisotropy, and efficiently resolving computational challenges (Giusti et al., 2016; Bonnet and Delgado, 2013). This continuous approach of topological derivative holds promise for enhancing medical images (Larrabide et al., 2005b) and improving segmentation for both recognition and visualization of abnormalities within the human body's organs (Viswanath et al., 2019a). Furthermore, topological derivatives aid in optimizing geometrical domains with unique shapes (Yaghmaei et al., 2020). The perturbed domain can be expressed in equation (6) as(6)Ψ[Ωε]=Ψ[Ω]/Dεwhere Ψ[Ω], Ψ[Ωε] is the original and perturbed domain's state equation, respectively, and Dε is the shape of the perturbed domain. This is expressed as(7)[xˆ,yˆ]↦DCT(xˆ)

Equation (7) maps the domain and the state equation as Ω↦Ψ[Ω] which is a result of perturbation. Taking account of the entire original domain Ψ[Ω], the perturbation effect of the entire original domain can be approximated by extending as the state of the perturbed domain. Thus, the state equation helps to represent CTD as shown in equation (8)(8)Ψ[Ωεn]≈Ψ[Ω]+fεnDCT(xˆ)

Uniqueness of equation (8) is that it represents perturbation series for various levels of ε represented by εn. For each and every perturbation series, a constant has to be determined from the boundary conditions. In most cases, the constant can be found, but in few cases, one encounters difficulty in determining the constants. This can be overcome by looking into the solution that is in existence in the neighbouring perturbation series and then solving the constants.

Matched asymptotic expansion helps in handling situation of non-uniformity in the incision of Dε. Non-uniformity will be present in the segmented region of the human organs, especially in the diseased part of the human organs, which required tools like matched asymptotic expansions. Matched asymptotic expansion helps in handy for the study of turbulence in fluid physics (Lundgren, 2003) and viscous-in-viscid interaction in aerodynamics (Veldman, 2001). Incorporating small terms, which are exponential in nature and subsequently paves the way for the introduction of transcendental terms with the aid of matched asymptotic analysis having a numerical approach to the problem (MacGillivray, 1997). Non-uniformity arises in perturbation series in addition to the singular perturbation and this is jointly handled by the techniques of topological derivative and that of matched asymptotic expansion. Thus, DCT(xˆ) is a differential operator that is dependent on the perturbation factor and aids in characterization of class function that plays an important role in both the segmentation and recognition of disease in the medical images. Class values thus obtained assists in assigning the merit of the medical image that recognises the normal region or disease part in the medical image. Particular value earmarked as a member of class will be Homomorphic with respect to the given domain. This gives yet another relationship between the perturbed and the unperturbed domain. Also, the perturbed domain lays a condition for the variation of cost function which is calculated with the aid of topological derivative. Cost functional values are reflected in this medical image problem in such a way that it pronounces the optimal values. These optimal values are used in such a manner which decides the articulated regions that in turn represent the regions of segmentation. In this way, medical images are represented with finer details from the original image. This optimal class assigned output image results in both the values and the segmented image, which is indicative of regions of interest. All these address the boundary variation problems inherent in segmentation. Further, adjoint variable technique and boundary element method play a key role in shape sensitivity study that happens as a consequence of boundary variation in medical images. Medical images are treated in the state equation as geometrical domain of integration. From the functional parameter of the perturbation as series of ε, the sensitivity of the boundary variation can be obtained in terms of first order. It happens because, the DCT(xˆ) is of first order in this problem. Its accuracy and the numerical treatment are also possible (Burczyński et al., 1995). Elliptic boundary value is a special case of study for boundary value problems. Study of singularities, iterative schemes along with finite volume method aids in elliptical boundary problems and it can be solved for edges of varying degrees present along the boundary of the domain (Costabel and Dauge, 1993; Chang et al., 1993; Bramble et al., 1993; Chen et al., 2012; Plamenevskij, 1997). Also, the elliptical boundary problem Ψ[Ω] is based on the solution of the state function Ψ, i.e., the shape functional. In this situation, the matched asymptotic expansion in Holder space is a viable solution (Nazarov and Sokołowski, 2003). CT and MRI specification

For testing and validation of the proposed CTD algorithm, HRCT thoracic cavity images were acquired from Vijaya Health Centre, Vadapalani, Chennai. The images were taken in SOMATOM dual-source CT. All the scans were taken at 1.00 mm thick, 40 mm beam length, and 400 mm beam width (or Window Width). The images were acquired in 0∘ angle tilt or gantry tilt and 30.3 cm Display Field of View (DFOV). The kernel used for soft tissue reconstruction is Br40f/3. The resolution of the images acquired is 480 × 340 (width x height) with a bit depth of 32 bits. Brain HRCT images were also collected from the same scan centre in spiral (SPI) mode using GE Healthcare SIGNA™ Explorer machine operating at 1.5 T magnetic fields, which is available at Vijaya Health Centre, Vadapalani, Chennai. Both the CT and MRI images are acquired in the Digital imaging and communication system (DICOM) format, which is usually recommended for the PACS system. The algorithms were developed and tested in MATLAB 2018 environment using a HP laptop with the configuration of 86FD motherboard, AMD Ryzen 5 3500U processor, Radeon Vega mobile Gfx graphics card, a 64-bit operating system with a clock speed of 2.10GHz, and 8GB RAM.

Validation

To validate the performance of the algorithm, ocular globe of thoracic cavity, haemorrhage cases, superior cistern along with grey and white matter belonging to brain CT images, Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) of PROP T2 mode 1, 2 and FLAIR T2 MRI brain images, and EIT lung images were tested with the proposed and traditional segmentation algorithms. In all these studies, characteristic functions ηε (Viswanath et al., 2018a) its value 1Ω→{0or1} (Viswanath et al., 2018a), ψ(ςε(xˆ)) (Viswanath et al., 2018b), E(dydx,ε) (Viswanath et al., 2018b) and Γb (in equation (5)) play a decisive role in segmenting the required regions very effectively. The individual case studies are summarized in the following sub-sections.

Case studies

Head CT

Case 1– Ocular globe

– Ocular globe

In the case of traumatic cataract (Kuhn, 2010; Segev et al., 1995), Terson Syndrome (Spraul and Grossniklaus, 1997), Vitreous Haemorrhage (Spraul and Grossniklaus, 1997; Goff et al., 2006), Orbital fractures and soft tissue injuries (Cellina et al., 2022), the lens regions of the globe have poor visibility in segmenting the inner regions from the raw CT image. CT scans remain pivotal in identifying injuries to ocular regions in head CT imaging (Chen et al., 2024a). Furthermore, Finite Element Analysis (FEM) serves as a valuable tool for evaluating damage inflicted upon the ocular globe (Ueno et al., 2024), complemented by the availability of segmentation models tailored specifically for the ocular globe region (Liu et al., 2022).

Effective segmentation technique only solves this problem to certain extent. Consequently, proper segmentation of ocular globe with density and attenuation characteristics will solve the above issues, and hence the CTD will serve as a suitable technique in extracting the diagnostic like orbital features and traumatic cataract cases very effectively. This is achieved by tuning the class values of the CTD as illustrated in Table 1.

Table 1 illustrates brain CT segmentation images of ocular globe tested in the proposed CTD and other algorithms. Iso-dense and hyper-attenuation are the unique features of ocular globe in CT scan. The original and denoised brain CT images have the dimension of 113×125. All the segmented images are resized to 117×128 dimension in order to view the images in a magnified form. From Tables 1 and it is evident from CTD segmented image that the shape functional plays a crucial role in identifying the edges and boundaries without much distortion, and hence the anatomical and physiological details are retained without noticeable errors. For this image, the parameter 1Ω i.e. 1Ω→{0} represents the ocular globe. In the segmentation of ocular globe ψ(ςε(xˆ)), Γb and E(dydx,ε) are all nicely witnessed. In the case of DTD derived, it is not at up to the level of CTD due to the poor class values.

In the K means clustering method, much distortion is noticed in the globe structure compared to CTD. The globe and lens are seen as a separate entity in K means clustering due to different depth factors, but there is a disadvantage that the features are not retained. The adaptive thresholding is good, but is not up to the mark. JI index for CTD derived image w. r.t to DTD, K Means clustering and adaptive threshold indicates 0%, 0.1% and 0% similarity. The same trends are observed in the case of J‾I‾, which indicates 100%, 98% and 100% of dis-similarity. The DI index for CTD derived image w. r.t to DTD, K Means clustering and adaptive threshold indicates 0%, 0.2% and 0% similarity. Similar trend is maintained in DI‾ that indicates 100%, 97% and 100% of dis-similarity. Thus, the CTD emerges as a right algorithm to identify trivial cases like ocular globe.

Brain CT - hematoma

Case 2– Epidural hematoma

– Epidural hematoma

Epidural hematoma occurs only at regions over the dura mater and is characteristic of blood draining out in to the dura matter space. Majority of the cases arise from tear in middle meningeal artery. Seizures, loss of brain function, coagulopathy, arteriovenous malformations (paralysis and numbness) and slurred speech are some of common signs of epidural hematoma. Bilateral epidural hematoma, characterized by the midline suture is a very rare and acute disease that can be diagnosed using CT scans (Görgülü et al., 2000). Acute epidural hematoma can also be monitored by changes in intracranial pressure in CT scans (Dolgun et al., 2011). Density and volume play a crucial role in epidural hematoma studies (Lobato et al., 1988; Koketsu et al., 2022). Segmentation challenges related to epidural hematoma (Chen et al., 2022a; Lin and Yuh, 2024) and the accompanying volume studies (Khademolhosseini et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2024) offer some resolution to these issues.

Presence of radio-density (65–95 HU) and volume measurements play critical role in epidural hematoma segmentation. This is made possible with CTD segmentation having optimized class values.

Table 2 illustrates brain CT image where segmentation in epidural hematoma case was attempted. Both the original image and denoised images are of size 255 × 166, whereas the focussed ROI images are resized to 253 × 161.

Here, blood or oozing of blood is the crucial part and the cerebral convexity nature forms the central part of segmentation. It is evident from CTD segmented image that the shape functional, edges and boundaries of the skull are noticed without much distortion and at the same time few features are also preserved. In this image, the parameter 1Ω i.e., 1Ω→{1} represents epidural nature of trauma representing hyper-density regions. The value of 1 associated with the characteristic function ηε representing ROI because it is a case of additional perturbation (by taking into account of the damages in the brain region). In the CTD segmentation of epidural hematoma, the parameters ψ(ςε(xˆ)), Γb and E(dydx,ε) are witnessed in the case of the CTD. But, the DTD derived image is not clear due to the missing of essential features in epidural region, since CTD has improved class values that decide the clarity of the image. In the K means clustering method, distortion is noticed in the epidural region compared to CTD. The adaptive thresholding is not good because the convexity nature is witnessed, but much distortion is noticed in other regions. The JI index for CTD derived image w. r.t to DTD, K Means clustering and adaptive threshold indicates 0%, 7% and 0% similarity. The DI index for CTD derived image w. r.t to DTD, K Means clustering and adaptive threshold indicates 0%, 1% and 0% similarity, which is further ascertained by the DI‾ index of 100%, 98% and 100% of dis-similarity scores of the three comparison methods. Consequently, the CTD algorithm may be chosen as an optimum method for segmenting the epidural hematoma cases.Case 3– Subdural hematoma

– Subdural hematoma

Unlike epidural hematoma, subdural hematoma occurs below the Dura mater, which shows syndromes like tear in veins/blood vessels in subdural hematoma. Persistence headache, vision changes (e.g., double vision), weakness pertaining to a single side, loss of memory, paralysis, difficulty in breathing and enlarged head are some of the symptoms of subdural hematoma. Even a thin subdural hematoma will be visible with proper setting over a regular CT scan. Acute subdural hematoma develops intra cranial pressure, which can be viewed on CT scans (Gennarelli and Thibault, 1982; Ernestus et al., 1997; Ducruet et al., 2012; Wilberger et al., 1991). Consequently, the measurement of hematoma volume, blood clot, midline shift and basal cistern studies played a crucial role in diagnosing the subdural hematoma (Bullock et al., 2006), which requires contrast enhanced CT (Yadav et al., 2016; McDonough et al., 2022). Existing Subdural hematoma segmentation (Chen et al., 2022a; Farzaneh et al., 2020; Vargas et al., 2024) and volume studies (Liao et al., 2024; Vargas et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024b), provide a moderate solution to these. But, any improvements in segmentation bring noticeable diagnostic value by way of identifying the symptoms accurately. Here, CTD performed better segmentation to improve the diagnostic efficiency of the CT images for typical subdural hematoma symptoms.

The original and denoised images are of size 325 × 130, and the focussed ROI images are resized to 314 × 133. Value of 1 is associated with the characteristic function ηε, representing ROI because it is a case of additional perturbation (by taking into account of the damage suffered in brain). This is evident from the isodense ROI crossing the suture line. – Intraparenchymal cerebral haemorrhage

Intraparenchymal cerebral haemorrhage (ICPH) is the primary cause for many of the stroke cases, in which blood drains into parenchymal regions of brain. Amyloid angiopathy, hypertension, aneurysm rupture, vasculitis, focal neurological deficits, syncope, vertigo and infections in blood are some of the symptoms of Intraparenchymal cerebral haemorrhage. Brain CT (without contrast) is the most preferred modality for diagnosing Intraparenchymal cerebral haemorrhage. Spot sign in CT attests to the presence of ICPH (Gross et al., 2019). CT scans for ICPH can be of use for the calculation of small volumes of haemorrhage (Lima et al., 2012). Hyperdense, hypodense and isodense are all other symptoms of ICPH during its different stages of growth over a time period (Anzalone et al., 2004). Pontine haemorrhage can also be detected using CT scans (Albin and Zafar, 2022). Segmentation techniques for intraparenchymal cerebral haemorrhage (Khademolhosseini et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2021a; Hoang et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024) prove invaluable for conducting comprehensive studies on ICPH.

Cerebellar decompression surgeries, craniectomy, surgical evacuation of blood and catheter-based procedures are some of treatment protocols in ICPH, which requires clear demarcation of the diseased areas. In the ICPH diagnosis, area, volume, density, expansion and shape functionals play a crucial role in ICPH segmentation. Presence of radiodensity (60->100 HU) attests the intensity of ICPH, i.e., the greater radiodensity (>100 HU) indicates higher concentration of haemoglobin protein, typical syndrome of ICPH. These symptoms are well-diagnosed using CTD segmentation, which has better class values compared to other segmentation methods.

In ICPH, blood leaked into the cranial spaces, which leads to blood collection inside the parenchyma region of brain and this forms the central part of segmentation. It is evident from CTD segmented image that the shape functional, edges and boundaries of blood accumulated regions besides skull are noticed without much distortion and at the same time most of the features are also preserved. In this image, the parameter 1Ω, i.e., 1Ω→{1} represents blood present inside the parenchyma region. The value 1 related to the characteristic function ηε represents the ROI because it is a case of additional perturbation (by taking into account of the damaged regions in brain), which is due to the hyperdense condition of the ROI. Consequently, the CTD segmented image resolved edges and boundaries of blood accumulated regions unambiguously with the clear vision of the skull and the finer details of the disease affected area by the shape functional. But, the DTD performed moderate enough in resolving the images, but not very precise like that of CTD in retaining the finer details of the image. Conversely, the K means clustering and adaptive thresholding images implies that there is a distortion in the Intraparenchymal Cerebral region with missing finer details. It is evident from the JI and J‾I‾ indices for CTD having similarity score of 8%, 1% and 0%, and dissimilarity score of 91%, 98% and 100%, respectively in comparison with the DTD, K Means clustering and adaptive threshold methods. Further, the DI index has 15%, 2% and 0% similarity and DI‾ has 84%, 97% and 100% dis-similarity scores with respect to the comparison methods. Hence, the CTD reveals as an optimum segmentation tool for identifying the haemorrhage regions in the brain.

Brain CT - cistern

Case 5– Superior Cistern

– Superior Cistern

Superior cistern/quadrigeminal cisterns (SC/QC) are the important cistern lying between midbrain and the third ventricle which is filled with cerebrospinal fluid. Pineal region meningioma is the characteristic of QC (Inoue et al., 2015). Radiological features of QC have a major role for early diagnosis of pineal parenchymal tumour/hemangioblastomas in CT/MR images (Xu et al., 2021b; Favero et al., 2021). Arachnoid cysts occurs in QC that can be overcome with the aid of burr hole, cyst shunting and cyst fenestration procedures (Ruge et al., 1996), which can be detected through better segmented CT images. Further, irregularities in the shape of QC in CT exhibit the presence of spontaneous cerebellar haemorrhage (Taneda et al., 1987) and the position of QC also aids in subarachnoid haemorrhage findings (WANG et al., 2022b). Segmentation of the quadrigeminal cistern (Muhammad et al., 2024) holds great importance in the examination of ventricle hematoma, aneurysm cases, malformations in regions such as the internal cerebral vein and vein of Galen (Ota, 2024), as well as in the investigation of cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease (Odano et al., 2024).

The cancer/cyst/lesions/haemorrhage over the QS region in CT can be identified precisely through better segmentation methods. Therefore, proper segmentation of QS can lead to accurate finding of the deformities in the QS and its vicinity through shape functionals and radiodensities. This is justified in CTD segmentation by reducing the number of iterations compared to DTD, even though the class values are close to each other.

Table 5 summarizes the segmentation results of the Superior Cistern. Here, the original image and denoised images are of the size 85 × 109 and the focussed ROI images are resized to 86 × 110. The reference point of the segmentation is the dilatation of the subarachnoid cistern filled with Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF). From Tables 5 and it is observed that the CTD segmented image clearly exhibited the edges, and boundaries of the skull with minimum distortion in the surrounding areas, due to the critical role of the shape functional. This is because of the hypodense nature of the ROI based on the 1Ω→{0} values of the CTD, which represents the CSF present inside the superior cistern region. Subsequently, the three class values of CTD viz., ψ(ςε(xˆ)), Γb and E(dydx,ε) are strategically tuned to achieve resolved segmented images. But, the quality of the DTD images are not matched with CTD images due to inability in tuning the correct class values. On the other hand, the K means clustering and adaptive thresholding methods results in acceptable cistern segmentation, but both the methods have distortion in the other regions of the Superior Cistern. This is clearly observed from the JI and J‾I‾ indices estimated for CTD with respect to the DTD, K Means clustering and adaptive threshold techniques. The similarity and dis-similarity scores are found to be 0%, 1% and 0%, and 100%, 98% and 100%, respectively. Further, the DI and the DI‾ scores are found to be 0%, 2% and 0% similarity, and 100%, 97% and 100% dis-similarity for the CTD with reference to the other three methods. Hence, the proposed CTD method is a better candidate for segmenting the cases like Superior Cistern.

Brain CT - tissue

Case 6– Grey and white matter

– Grey and white matter

White and grey matter is indicative of the colour, in which, Myelin is responsible for white matter and is surrounded by grey matter. In CT scan, the white matter appears as grey with less intensity and grey matter with more intensity. The former consists of nerve fibres and the latter is made up of nerve tissues. Low contrast CT is the best modality to differentiate the differences between white and grey matter. Radio density measurements for both white matter (25 HU) and grey matter (35 HU) serves as an indicator for the diagnosis of comatose or cardiac arrest (Choi et al., 2008). In most cases, HU of grey and white matter measured at basal ganglia and grey to white matter ratio (GWR) aids in foretelling the nature of outcome for comatose patients (Oh et al., 2019). For comatose/cardiac arrest patients, who are affected with Hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, hypothermia, hydrocephalus, cerebral edema, etc., the GWR is a potential indicator for taking a decision about the prognosis of the patients (Gentsch et al., 2015). Hemodynamic failure, cerebral ischemia, ventricular tachycardia, etc., are the few comatose cases, where grey white matter differentiation (GWR) from brain CT plays a crucial role in the prediction of the repercussion in the diseased patients (Adriaansens et al., 2022). Brain tissue segmentation is integral to studies on brain tumors (Singh, 2021). Furthermore, the segmentation of white matter hyperintensities, which serve as a hallmark of cerebral small vessel diseases, serves as a definitive indicator for neurovascular inflammation (Solé-Guardia et al., 2024). Additionally, volume studies on both white and grey matter aid in the diagnosis of metopic synostosis (Gaillard et al., 2024).

Targeted temperature management is one of the methods to overcome the above stated diseases caused by grey and white matter differentiation (GWD). Volume and radio densities of grey matter, white matter and GWD segmentation will play a pivotal role in many comatose cases. This is demonstrated in this case study using CTD segmentation.

The segmentation of skull, white and grey matter and their quality metrics are summarized in Table 6. The image size of the original and denoised images is 128 × 60, whereas resized ROI images have the size of 204 × 99. White matter, a fibrous region of Central Nerves System (CNS), and grey matter, a neuro cell region present in CNS and skull form the central part of segmentation. In this process, the white matter apart from grey matter is much differentiated based on the articulation between the cerebrum and cerebellum areas of brain, which is obvious from the CTD segmented image showing the shape, edges and boundaries of the white matter, grey matter and skull without distortion and having distinct features.

Hence, the CTD method is an optimum technique for the segmentation applications like white and grey matter.

Brain MRI - ventricles

MR Image Segmentation of Lateral Ventricles (LV) in Brain – Study of Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) Stroke cases with respect to Shape derivatives of LV.

The shape distortion observed in the lateral ventricle (LV) in brain MRI scans serves as a potential indicator for analyzing various types of strokes resulting from limited blood supply or leakage in the cerebral hemispheres from the middle cerebral artery (MCA). A unique approach involves deriving the shape of the LV while simultaneously highlighting the degree of damage in brain regions affected by MCA. The phenomenon of reversed end diastolic flow in the middle cerebral artery (MCA-REDF) is attributed to intense pressures in the intra-cerebral regions (Brownfoot et al., 2015), often associated with notable changes in the ventricular regions of the brain (Turňová et al., 2023), particularly witnessed in haemorrhage or stroke cases of grade II concentrated around the lateral ventricle regions (Brownfoot et al., 2015; Turňová et al., 2023). MCA-REDF is uniquely observed in parietal infarcts (Chen and Fang, 2019), with Motor-Evoked Potentials (MEP) aiding in prognosis studies of both MCA and parietal infarcts (Arac et al., 1994).

Compression of the ipsilateral lateral ventricle serves as a definitive indication of hyperacute MCA infarction in CT scans (Alshoabi et al., 2019), while acute hydrocephalus and lateral ventricle hematoma indicate MCA aneurysm (Nishimoto et al., 2005a). Centrum ovale infarcts represent a unique case affecting the cerebral cortex regions supplied by the MCA (Nishimoto et al., 2005b), with the measurement of anteroseptal shift aiding in the study of large MCA infarctions (Barber et al., 2003). These observations underscore the importance of LV in MCA infarction, where the anterior horn of the LV assists in ventricular drainage in burr hole surgeries for treating MCA aneurysms (Suzuki et al., 1984), and the temporal horn of the LV also plays a significant role in MCA pathologies (Fornari et al., 1981).

Arteriovenous malformations (AVM) affecting the LV can impact the middle cerebral regions of the brain (Stein, 1984), while the presence of multiple Deep Medullary Veins (DMV) in the vicinity of the LV provides a definitive indication of atherosclerotic ischemic strokes affecting the middle cerebral part of the brain, as observed in Susceptibility Weighted MR images (Han et al., 2016). The signal intensity of the LV is instrumental in detecting rare cases of MCA aneurysms in the M1 segment (Ahmad et al., 1992). Critical studies, such as measuring blood flow changes in the MCA, are assisted by intravenous injections into LV regions, providing insights into intra-cranial pressure (ICP) (Droste and Krauss, 1993). In CT perfusion, LV is selected as the region of interest (ROI) for feasibility studies regarding superficial temporal artery-middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) anastomosis procedures (Li et al., 2023).

MRI scans of the brain have revealed infarcts in the posterior horn of the LV and stenosis of the left MCA in the vicinity (Xie et al., 2023), while subcortical cerebral haemorrhage MRI scans have provided evidence of hypodense edema in the left LV and MCA occlusion in a patient suffering from bilateral Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss (SSNHL), a rare case in chronic cerebrovascular disease (Elmarawany, 2023). Rare lesions such as Colloid Cysts (CC) in the third ventricle are attested by ventricular drainage in the lateral ventricle and restricted diffusion in the midbrain (Dahal et al., 2023). Myxoma, a rare cardiac tumour, is diagnosed by the mass effect on the lateral ventricle and acute infarction in the MCA (Srinivas et al., 2023). Additionally, the presence of Rete mirabile of cerebral artery, a rare anomaly, is evidenced by saccular aneurysms in the lateral ventricle in CT scans (Nitta and Tsuji, 2023).

In cases of pure intraventricular haemorrhage, aneurysms were located in both the MCA and right anterior choroidal artery in the LV (Zhou et al., 2023), highlighting the potential of LV as a site for identifying different types of aneurysms in cerebral arteries, including the MCA (Hoz et al., 2023; Saito et al., 2023).

Changes in the shape of the LV serve as evidence of MCA-induced stroke conditions. The focus here is on MCA, its associated lesions, strokes, and other cerebral effects. Based on this perspective, LV segmentation can provide insights into abnormalities related to the MCA, thereby shedding light on disorders caused by MCA in the brain. This section underscores the significance of LV segmentation in understanding MCA-related conditions from a cerebral standpoint.

Motion artifacts assume high importance in pediatric imaging, where technologies like radial volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (radial VIBE), magnetization-prepared rapid-acquisition gradient-echo (MP-RAGE), and PROPELLER come in handy (Rasche, 2019). Apart from these sequences, acquisition techniques like single-shot echo-planar imaging (SS-EPI DWI), read-out segmented EPI (RESOLVE DWI), and 2-D turbo-gradient spin-echo DWI with non-cartesian BLADE trajectory (TGSE-BLADE DWI) are popular modes for correcting artifacts, particularly in cerebral regions where both LV ROI and MCA play significant roles in predicting disease outcomes (Park et al., 2018; Okuchi et al., 2022). LV segmentation (Crystal et al., 2024) can also indicate conditions such as hydrocephalus (Ziegelitz et al., 2024; Ragguett et al., 2024), depression (Murck et al., 2024), and Alzheimer's disease (Wittens et al., 2024).

The MR images taken from T2 PROP and T2 FLAIR modes aids in the studies about various kinds of strokes (Kalladka et al., 2016). FLAIR is the apt mode for the diagnosis of ischemic penumbra (Lee et al., 2022a) and FLAIR MCA can represent the cerebral hemodynamic status (Huang et al., 2019b). In the PROP mode, motion correction can be performed in MCA (Maclaren et al., 2007). Further, the PROP MCA can aid in hypoxia studies (Mikhail Kellawan et al., 2017), but PROP T2 is effective for identification of chronic ischemic lesions (André et al., 2022). Thus, both PROP T2 and FLAIR T2 are good in determining high intensity lesion (Higashino et al., 2022). Collateral circulation in acute ischemic stroke is best observed in PROP T2 and FLAIR T2 modes (Ahn et al., 2016). In the Targeted PROP T2 technique, inner volume imaging (IVI) of MCA provided fruitful results by overcoming field inhomogeneities, bulk motion, wrap-in and remnant motion artifacts. Concurrently, it provided higher spatial resolution with reduced acquisition time (Deng and Larson, 2009). This targeted PROP T2 will be of immense use for MCA volume studies. PROP T2 and FLAIR T2 serves as a good indicator for cerebral hyper perfusion syndrome (Wan et al., 2017). Moreover, PROP T2 provides a way for volume segmentation of glioma tumour based on hyper intensity in blood perfusion studies (Zhu et al., 2022b). Arterial transit artifact can be clearly noticed in FLAIR T2 images (Lee et al., 2022b; Zhang et al., 2022b) and FLAIR T2 MCA images can reveal early neurological disorders (Chen et al., 2022b). The treatment protocol of pneumothorax requires well-segmented EIT for the cases like atelectrauma and pulmonary perfusion (Cipulli et al., 2018; Rubin and Berra, 2022).

Conclusion

Imaging modalities like CT, HRCT, MRI and EIT continues to be the most demanding tools for diagnosing the problems related to head, brain, haemorrhage, cistern, etc. Any small improvements in the image registration process certainly improve the diagnostic efficiency in terms of precise recognition of abnormalities such as area/volume, density, contour, etc. Further, various types of noises hinder the visualization of minute details and hence proper segmentation is not possible without denoising. Hence, CTD denoised images were chosen for achieving the required segmentation output. Further, the segmentation of the denoised images was performed using CTD and compared its efficiency with other three traditional techniques. From this study, it was observed that the CTD not only proved to be effective in removing the noise, it also emerged as a systematic and well-structured tool for addressing different kinds of segmentation required for brain, tissue, head, artery and lung (breathing cycle) images. This is possible because of the inherent property of tuning the class values corresponding to the specific modality and anatomical position of interest, which is proved in this study by the information obtained for density, intensity, area, and the contour sector details for better visualization of abnormalities present over the ROI. The segmentation of denoised CT, MRI, and EIT images showcased the efficacy of the CTD method. This was evident through a comprehensive comparative analysis involving three distinct segmentation techniques, alongside visual scrutiny by radiologists and evaluation based on performance metrics. The findings underscored the systematic refinement of class values, crucial for precisely delineating Regions of Interest (ROIs) encompassing vital anatomical structures such as the brain, tissues, head, arteries, ventricles and lungs.

Moreover, the study yielded volumetric insights into cerebral blood flow regions and anomalies within the Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) territory, proving instrumental in gauging the severity of strokes in afflicted individuals. This underscores the potential of the novel mathematical approach in augmenting the diagnosis and comprehension of diverse brain-related disorders. Furthermore, the investigation introduced a novel application of the derivative technique in analyzing Electrical Impedance Tomography (EIT) images of the lungs. Leveraging this technique enabled researchers to accurately estimate the respiratory cycle.

Overall, this research underscores the promise of the continuum topological derivative as an effective tool for precise segmentation in medical imaging, particularly in CT and MRI scans of the head and brain. Its potential extensions into other realms of medical imaging suggest significant prospects in diagnosing and monitoring various brain and lung-related health conditions.

The outcomes of this research are highly motivating and offer significant insights into the challenges of implementing real-time imaging systems. Moving forward, it is essential to conduct field trials in hospital settings to evaluate the efficacy of the proposed algorithm in commercial hardware. This represents a promising avenue for future research, with a focus on testing the Continuum Topological Derivative (CTD) denoising and segmentation algorithm on platforms such as FPGA, Raspberry Pi, or Arduino boards. Validation through field trials will be crucial in assessing its practicality and reliability for real-world applications.
