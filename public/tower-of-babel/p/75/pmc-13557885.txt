Increasing incidence and improved survival of leptomeningeal metastasis in advanced lung cancer: a 10-year real-world cohort study
Authors: Jing Sun, Qian Zhang, Yehao Yang, Haicheng Wu, Wanchen Zhai, Yuwei Li, Linjing Zhou, Mengqi Bao, Yi Chen, Xiheng Yu, Yun Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn advanced lung cancer, leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) is a severe complication associated with an exceptionally poor prognosis. This study investigates the incidence, histopathological features, and genetic characteristics of LM, while also examining treatment outcomes and prognostic determinants in affected patients.MethodsFrom January 2014 through December 2023, 8,213 patients with advanced lung cancer were evaluated, among whom 234 developed LM. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS) in patients with advanced lung cancer complicated by LM.ResultsBy the data cutoff on December 28, 2023, the median follow-up was 39.5 (range: 0.2–45.0) months. The incidence of LM at initial presentation was 0.44%, with an overall incidence of 2.85%. A significant upward trend was observed, rising from 1.25% during 2014–2018 to 3.79% during 2019–2023 (P<0.05). Among patients with LM, adenocarcinoma comprised 96.6% of cases, and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations were predominant, detected in 82.5% of patients. The median OS (mOS) following LM diagnosis was 10.2 months [95% confidence interval (CI): 6.2–11.1]. Patients treated with targeted therapies achieved significantly longer survival than those receiving chemotherapy or best supportive care (11.4 vs. 7.9 vs. 3.3 months, P<0.001). High-dose tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy provided a superior benefit over standard dosing (12.6 vs. 10.8 months, P=0.001). Patients who received intrathecal chemotherapy (ITC) also showed a significantly longer OS than those who did not receive ITC (11.7 vs. 7.6 months, P=0.04). In multivariate analysis, lower Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) and synchronous LM were independent predictors of favorable prognosis (all P<0.05).ConclusionsOver the past decade, the incidence of LM in patients with advanced lung cancer has increased significantly. Targeted therapies (including dose-intensified TKIs) and ITC were strongly associated with meaningful survival improvements. While these active treatments offer substantial clinical benefits, their optimal outcomes are closely linked to favorable baseline patient characteristics, emphasizing the need for personalized multidisciplinary management.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this 10-year retrospective study of 8,213 advanced lung cancer patients, the incidence of leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) increased significantly over time.

• Targeted therapies [particularly high-dose tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)] and intrathecal chemotherapy (ITC) were strongly associated with prolonged overall survival, representing crucial components of multidisciplinary care for LM.

What is known and what is new?

• LM is a severe and often fatal complication of advanced lung cancer. Since the blood-brain barrier limits drug penetration, patients treated with standard chemotherapy have historically faced an exceptionally poor prognosis.

• This large real-world cohort demonstrates a nearly threefold increase in LM incidence over the past decade, temporally aligning with the prolonged survival conferred by next-generation targeted therapies. It provides robust real-world validation that active multimodal treatments—integrating dose-intensified TKIs and ITC—correlate with meaningful survival improvements in clinical practice, while highlighting the importance of favorable baseline performance status in maximizing these therapeutic benefits.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The growing burden of LM requires heightened clinical vigilance as systemic disease control improves.

• Proactive multidisciplinary management—integrating dose-intensified TKIs and ITC—provides substantial survival benefits. Maximizing these therapeutic outcomes relies on personalizing intensive treatments for well-selected patients with favorable baseline characteristics.

Introduction

Leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) is a serious and often fatal complication of advanced lung cancer. Since the blood-brain barrier (BBB) limits drug penetration, patients treated with standard chemotherapy have a very poor prognosis, with a median overall survival (OS) of 1–3 months. The advent of next-generation small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) with high BBB permeability has markedly improved survival in patients with LM with oncogenic driver mutations, particularly those with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations or anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) alterations. Multiple phase II clinical studies have reported intracranial objective response rates (ORRs) of 50–60% and median OS (mOS) ranging from 11 to 18.8 months in patients with non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and LM who received the third-generation EGFR inhibitor osimertinib (1-3). Comparable results have been achieved with other third-generation EGFR-TKIs (4,5). For ALK-positive LM, the available evidence comes mainly from subgroup analyses and case series, with reported intracranial ORRs of 50–60% and mOS ranging from 13 to 16 months (6-10).

Over the past decade, the incidence of LM in advanced NSCLC has steadily increased. It affects approximately 3–5% of all patients, with higher rates among those carrying EGFR mutations (9–16%) (11-13). Similarly, incidence in ALK-positive patients appears to be rising, with rates of 5–10% reported (14,15). This increase may be attributed to the prolonged survival conferred by successive generations of targeted therapies. In contrast, epidemiologic data on LM in small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) remain scarce. A prospective study of 458 patients with SCLC found LM in 2% of cases at baseline, with the cumulative incidence reaching 10% within 2 years (16). Overall, a comprehensive picture of LM incidence across the pathological and molecular subtypes of lung cancer is still incomplete, especially with regard to temporal patterns over the past decade.

Managing LM remains highly challenging due to its aggressive course and the historical lack of effective therapies. Advances in systemic agents and local treatment strategies have partially improved outcomes for some patients, yet new obstacles persist. These include LM in patients who progress after third-generation EGFR-TKIs and those with rare genetic alterations, for whom targeted treatment efficacy remains undefined. Such cases necessitate multidisciplinary management, with intrathecal chemotherapy (ITC) and intracranial radiotherapy serving as two important therapeutic options. ITC bypasses the BBB and enhances drug concentration in the central nervous system (CNS), making it a key treatment strategy for LM (17). A prospective phase II trial (N=132) showed that ITC achieved a mOS of 12 months and an ORR of 80.3% in TKI-resistant EGFR-mutant NSCLC based on Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) criteria (18). Whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT) is typically used in patients with extensive nodular or symptomatic LM; however, it may not provide survival benefits (19). In an analysis of 42 patients with NSCLC and LM, Li et al. reported that OS did not significantly differ between those who received WBRT and those who did not (12).

While LM is receiving growing clinical and research attention, and recent multicenter studies have described its evolution in NSCLC, the long-term dynamic incidence trends across the overall advanced lung cancer population still require real-world validation. Additionally, the real-world impact of different interventions, especially targeted therapies and ITC, on patient survival outcomes and independent prognostic factors needs further exploration. Therefore, we conducted a large-scale, 10-year single-center retrospective study to systematically track the dynamic incidence trends of LM in advanced lung cancer, analyze the clinical and genomic characteristics of this population, and comprehensively evaluate the clinical benefits and prognostic factors of various treatment strategies. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0531/rc).

Methods

Patient population

Between January 2014 and December 2023, we conducted a retrospective review of 8,213 hospitalized cases of advanced lung cancer at Zhejiang Cancer Hospital. Among them, 234 patients were diagnosed with LM. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (I) pathologically or cytologically confirmed lung cancer, (II) cytological diagnosis of LM in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), (III) receipt of at least two cycles of systemic anticancer treatment, and (IV) age ≥18 years. Patients with coexisting malignancies or missing follow-up data were not included in the final analysis. A detailed flowchart depicting the patient selection process is provided in Figure S1. Clinical data, including baseline demographics at the time of LM diagnosis, pathological characteristics, treatment regimens, and survival data, were extracted from the patient’s medical records at the hospital. Information regarding targeted therapies was extracted from electronic medical records. The designation of ‘high-dose’ versus ’standard-dose’ TKI therapy was retrospectively assigned based on the actual administered dose rather than a predefined clinical protocol. Specifically, high-dose regimens in our cohort were defined by the exact following daily dosages: gefitinib 500 mg/day, icotinib 450 mg/day, osimertinib 160 mg/day, aumolertinib 165 mg/day, or furmonertinib 120–240 mg/day. Conversely, standard-dose regimens were defined as the administration of these agents at their routinely approved standard dosages (e.g., osimertinib 80 mg/day). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (IRB-2023-520) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to characterize the baseline demographic profiles, clinical features, and treatment modalities of patients within the study cohort. Continuous data were summarized using medians and their respective ranges (minimum to maximum), whereas the categorical variables were reported as absolute frequencies and the corresponding percentages. To evaluate temporal trends, LM incidence was compared between the 2014–2018 and 2019–2023 periods using Pearson’s Chi-squared test. OS was estimated via the Kaplan-Meier method, with the median survival times reported along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). It is important to note that the 95% CIs for median survival times (e.g., 6.2–11.1 months around a mOS of 10.2 months) are inherently asymmetric. This is a standard characteristic of the Kaplan-Meier method reflecting the non-normal distribution of survival times and the presence of right-censored data. The differences in the survival distributions across groups were assessed by the log-rank test. The follow-up duration was estimated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier approach based on the OS data. To assess variability in the treatment outcomes, predefined subgroup analyses were performed, with a focus on the OS differences across treatment regimens for patients diagnosed with LM. All subgroup analyses were considered exploratory in nature, and no statistical adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Prognostic indicators of OS were investigated through Cox proportional hazards models. Both univariate and multivariate analyses were performed, yielding hazard ratios (HRs) and the corresponding 95% CIs. Variables displaying a univariate association with OS at a threshold of P<0.10 were selected for inclusion in multivariate modeling to control for the confounding effects and to identify the independent predictors. OS was calculated from the date of cytologically confirmed LM diagnosis to the date of death from any cause or the last follow-up. The median follow-up duration was also estimated from the time of LM diagnosis, calculated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and significance was set at P<0.05. Data analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

Patient characteristics and treatments

The baseline clinical characteristics and treatment patterns of patients with LM are presented in Table 1. The median age at enrollment was 57 (range: 29–75) years, with a slight female predominance (53.8%). Most patients were nonsmokers (72.2%). Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) was 0–2 in 207 patients (88.5%) and ≥3 in 27 patients (11.5%). Histologically, 230 patients (98.2%) had NSCLC, including 226 adenocarcinomas and 4 adenosquamous carcinomas; 2 patients (0.8%) had SCLC, and 2 (0.8%) were classified as carcinomas not otherwise specified. Among patients with identifiable molecular alterations, EGFR mutations were detected in 193 cases (82.5%), ALK rearrangements in 5 (2.1%), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) mutations in 4 (1.7%), and other rare mutations in 4 (1.7%). Additionally, 15 patients (6.4%) were identified as wild-type, while the mutation status for the remaining 13 patients (5.5%) was unknown. At LM onset, 221 patients (94.4%) presented with LM-related symptoms.

Based on the systemic first-line therapy received after LM diagnosis, patients were classified into three primary treatment groups. The majority (n=166, 70.9%) received TKIs. A smaller proportion (n=29, 12.4%) underwent chemotherapy-based regimens, which included chemotherapy alone or in combination with antiangiogenic agents or immune checkpoint inhibitors. The remaining patients (n=39, 16.7%) received best supportive care only. Additionally, 29 patients (12.4%) were treated with ITC, and 39 (16.7%) received WBRT.

Clinicopathologic profile

Figure 1 illustrates the clinicopathologic characteristics of patients diagnosed with LM. Regarding pathological subtypes (Figure 1A), 226 patients (96.6%) had adenocarcinoma, 4 (1.7%) had adenosquamous carcinoma, 2 (0.8%) had SCLC, and 2 (0.8%) were classified as not otherwise specified. In terms of the genetic profile (Figure 1B), 193 patients (82.5%) carried EGFR mutations, including 170 (72.6%) with common variants [93 with exon 21 L858R and 77 with exon 19 deletions] and 23 (9.8%) with uncommon variants [12 with exon 20 insertions, 8 with G719X mutations, and 3 with L861Q mutations]. Additional alterations included ALK rearrangements (n=5, 2.1%), HER2 mutations (n=4, 1.7%), mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET) alterations (n=3, 1.3%), and v-raf mouse sarcoma virus oncogene homolog B1 (BRAF) mutations (n=1, 0.4%); 15 patients (6.4%) were wild type. With respect to LM onset (Figure 1D), 36 patients (15.4%) were diagnosed with synchronous LM, defined as LM at the time of first advanced diagnosis or within one month thereafter, while 198 (84.6%) developed metachronous LM during disease progression. Furthermore, 121 patients (51.7%) presented with concomitant brain metastases (BM) (Figure 1C).

Analysis of LM incidence

The incidence of LM at the initial diagnosis of advanced lung cancer was 0.44% (36/8,213), while the overall incidence across the cohort was 2.85% (234/8,213). Longitudinal trends are illustrated in Figure 2A. Between 2014 and 2023, LM incidence demonstrated a significant upward trajectory (P<0.05), peaking at 5.18% in 2023. Comparative analysis showed that LM incidence rose from 1.25% (38/3,043) during 2014–2018 to 3.79% (196/5,170) during 2019–2023 (χ2=135.2, P<0.05; relative risk =3.03, 95% CI: 2.18–4.22; Figure 2B). Moreover, the median interval from advanced lung cancer diagnosis to LM onset lengthened across these periods, increasing from 14.6 (range: 0.2–51.8) months to 24.4 (range: 0.1–103.6) months.

Survival outcomes

As of the data cutoff date (December 28, 2023), patients with LM had a median follow-up duration of 39.5 (range: 0.2–45.0) months. The mOS after LM diagnosis was 10.2 months (95% CI: 6.2–11.1; Figure 3A). Notably, mOS improved from 7.4 months in 2014–2018 to 10.6 months in 2019–2023 (P=0.03; Figure 3B). Patients with ECOG PS scores of 0–2 demonstrated significantly longer survival than those with scores of ≥3 (10.9 vs. 2.5 months, P<0.001; Figure 4A). Significant OS differences were also observed across genetic subtypes: patients with EGFR mutations and ALK rearrangements experienced longer survival than those with HER2 mutations, other rare alterations (MET or BRAF mutations), or wild-type disease (10.6 vs. 14.0 vs. 3.0 vs. 5.5 vs. 3.4 months, P=0.03; Figure 4B). No survival difference was detected between patients with and without concurrent BM (10.6 vs. 7.2 months, P=0.93; Figure 4C). By contrast, patients with synchronous LM achieved markedly longer OS than those with metachronous LM (31.7 vs. 6.3 months, P<0.001; Figure 4D).

Moreover, our analysis showed that patients treated with TKIs had significantly longer OS than those who received chemotherapy-based regimens or best supportive care (11.4 vs. 7.9 vs. 3.3 months, P<0.001; Figure 5A). Patients who received ITC also achieved a superior mOS than those who did not (11.7 vs. 7.6 months, P=0.04; Figure 5B). Regarding intracranial local therapies, patients who underwent WBRT had longer OS than those who did not undergo WBRT (12.0 vs. 7.2 months, P=0.048; Figure 5C). Although this finding suggests a potential survival benefit from WBRT, its impact may have been confounded by the presence of concurrent BM. To account for this, we performed a subgroup analysis of patients with LM without BM (n=113). In this cohort, the mOS was 16.2 months for the 7 patients who received WBRT, compared to 6.8 months for those who did not. Although a numerical trend toward improved survival was observed, it did not reach statistical significance (HR =0.44, 95% CI: 0.18–1.08, P=0.07; Figure 5D).

We performed a subgroup analysis to assess the impact of different TKI treatment strategies on OS in patients with LM (Figure 6). The findings showed that patients receiving TKIs achieved superior OS than those who did not receive TKI therapy (Figure 6A). Among them, patients treated with high-dose TKI therapy exhibited significantly improved OS compared with those treated with standard-dose regimens (12.6 vs. 10.8 months, P=0.001) (Figure 6B). To further clarify whether the observed survival benefit was driven by dose intensification or the generation of the TKI, we evaluated the distribution of TKI types within these subgroups. Notably, both cohorts demonstrated a highly prevalent and balanced usage of third-generation TKIs, which were administered to 93.4% (57/61) of patients in the high-dose group and 97.1% (102/105) of patients in the standard-dose group. This balanced distribution indicates that the OS difference is primarily attributable to TKI dose intensification rather than the specific generation of the targeted agent used. By contrast, no meaningful difference in OS was observed between TKI combination therapy and monotherapy (10.6 vs. 11.8 months, P=0.90; Figure 6C). Moreover, the mOS was 12.2 months for patients treated with TKIs in combination with antiangiogenic therapy, compared to 10.8 months for those receiving TKIs alone, with no statistically significant difference observed (P=0.61; Figure 6D).

Univariate and multivariate analyses are presented in Figure S2. In the univariate survival analysis, several variables showed significant correlations with OS, including ECOG PS scores, genetic mutation subtypes, patterns of LM, first-line therapies administered after LM diagnosis, and ITC (all P<0.05). A subsequent multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was then applied to determine independent prognostic indicators. The results demonstrated that higher ECOG PS scores (HR =4.72, 95% CI: 2.15–10.36, P<0.001) and metachronous LM (HR =3.99, 95% CI: 2.08–7.68, P<0.001) were both independently and strongly linked to worse OS.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the largest and longest longitudinal real-world studies of LM in advanced lung cancer. Our analysis provides three principal contributions. First, we demonstrate a near tripling of LM incidence over ten years, temporally aligned with the introduction of more effective, CNS-penetrant targeted therapies that have extended patient survival. Second, we offer a granular, real-world comparison of targeted therapy strategies, showing that high-dose TKI regimens significantly outperform standard dosing. Third, we validate the survival benefit of ITC in a large, unselected cohort. Collectively, these findings directly inform the intensification and personalization of treatment for LM.

In this study, LM was identified in 0.44% of patients at the initial diagnosis of advanced lung cancer, with an overall incidence of 2.85% during 2014–2023. A statistically significant rise in incidence was observed, from 1.25% in 2014–2018 to 3.79% in 2019–2023 (P<0.05). Clinically, this increase is likely attributable to the improved survival of patients treated with next-generation targeted therapies. A clinical paradox has therefore emerged: although newer-generation TKIs, particularly third-generation EGFR inhibitors, offer enhanced CNS penetration and superior efficacy compared with first- and second-generation agents, the incidence of LM has continued to rise in recent years, most likely reflecting their broader clinical adoption. Multiple studies have confirmed that third-generation EGFR-TKIs confer substantial survival benefits compared with first-generation agents (20-22). Consequently, prolonged patient survival extends the time window during which tumor cells can colonize the CNS, a recognized “sanctuary site”, where they evade systemic drug activity, persist, and eventually manifest as LM (23). Supporting this hypothesis, our analysis revealed a significant prolongation of the median interval from the initial diagnosis of advanced lung cancer to LM onset (14.6 months in 2014–2018 vs. 24.4 months in 2019–2023). The prolonged latency period strongly suggests that improved systemic disease control is unmasking late-onset CNS progression rather than merely reflecting increased surveillance or detection bias. Additionally, advances in diagnostic modalities (notably high-resolution neuroimaging and CSF molecular profiling) and greater clinical vigilance among physicians and patients may have contributed to higher detection rates in recent years.

A notable observation in our cohort was the substantial survival advantage of patients with synchronous LM compared to those with metachronous LM (mOS 31.7 vs. 6.3 months). To adjust for early mortality and lead-time bias, landmark analyses across multiple time points (1–6 months; Figure S3) consistently confirmed this survival advantage. From a statistical perspective, this ’survival paradox’ is inherently influenced by lead-time bias, as synchronous LM is often identified during routine initial staging in largely asymptomatic patients. Beyond this statistical bias, however, we hypothesize that this disparity is fundamentally driven by tumor biology and treatment lines. Synchronous LM patients are typically treatment-naïve, harboring tumors highly sensitive to upfront targeted therapies. Conversely, metachronous LM usually represents a late-stage clonal evolution, emerging after the failure of multiple lines of systemic treatments. These patients face a highly refractory disease state with complex acquired resistance, exhausted therapeutic options, and compromised physical tolerance, culminating in an exceptionally poor prognosis.

We also systematically evaluated the pathological subtypes of advanced lung cancer associated with LM. Lung adenocarcinoma predominated overwhelmingly (96.6%). Our cohort included only two patients with SCLC and LM, both of whom demonstrated exceptionally long survival (14.5 and 24.4 months). Consistent with our findings, Higo et al. have also described extended survival (>12 months) in an immunotherapy-naive patient with extensive-stage SCLC and LM who was treated with WBRT and systemic chemotherapy (24). Due to the extreme prognostic heterogeneity of SCLC complicated by LM, larger scale studies are necessary to better characterize the clinical trajectory of this specific population. (25,26). Our genomic profiling further characterized the molecular features of LM. EGFR mutations were the most prevalent (82.5%), with classical alterations (exon 21 L858R, 39.7%; exon 19 deletions, 32.9%) being most common. Uncommon EGFR mutations were identified in 9.8% of cases, including exon 20 insertions (ex20ins) in 5.1% (12/234). Prior studies have shown that EGFR ex20ins mutations are intrinsically resistant to first- and second-generation TKIs, with ORRs of only 0–27% and median progression-free survival (PFS) of only 3 months (27,28). Although third-generation EGFR-TKIs demonstrate robust efficacy in classical EGFR mutations, their activity against EGFR ex20ins remains modest due to steric hindrance within the ATP-binding pocket (29,30). Patients harboring EGFR ex20ins mutations typically experience worse outcomes than those with classical mutations, with reported mOS ranging from 14 to 26 months (31-34). As suggested by previous literature, the relatively higher LM incidence observed in EGFR ex20ins carriers may be related to the inherently aggressive biology of these tumors (35,36). By contrast, the prevalence of ALK rearrangements in our LM cohort (2.1%) was substantially lower than that of EGFR mutations (82.5%). This discrepancy may reflect advances in next-generation ALK-TKIs incorporating macrocyclic designs that markedly enhance CNS penetration (37). Beyond EGFR and ALK, we identified smaller subsets of patients harboring other actionable alterations, including HER2 mutations (1.7%), MET alterations (1.3%), and BRAF mutations (0.4%). These findings underscore an unmet clinical need for the development of BBB-penetrant TKIs targeting these rarer driver mutations.

In our study, the mOS for the entire cohort of patients with advanced lung cancer and LM was 10.2 months. Notably, within the EGFR-mutated subgroup, the mOS was 10.6 months, which is consistent with the previously reported survival outcomes of 10–18 months in historical cases of EGFR-mutated NSCLC with LM (38,39). Further stratified analysis revealed a significant improvement in OS between 2014–2018 and 2019–2023 (7.4 vs. 10.6 months, P=0.03), likely reflecting recent advances in CNS-penetrant targeted therapies, particularly the clinical implementation of next-generation EGFR and ALK inhibitors. In line with these therapeutic developments, our data demonstrated that patients harboring EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements experienced substantially longer OS than those carrying HER2 mutations, other rare alterations, or wild-type tumors (10.6 vs. 14.0 vs. 3.0 vs. 5.5 vs. 3.4 months, P=0.03). Treatment with TKIs conferred a clear survival benefit, as patients receiving TKIs achieved significantly longer OS than those who did not (11.4 vs. 4.2 months, P<0.001). Subgroup analysis further indicated that high-dose TKI therapy was associated with improved OS compared with standard-dose treatment (12.6 vs. 10.8 months, P=0.001). This observation aligns with prior studies reporting enhanced antitumor efficacy and survival with high-dose EGFR-TKIs in EGFR-mutant NSCLC with LM (4,40,41). Notably, in a retrospective cohort of 10 patients with EGFR ex20ins-mutant LM, high-dose furmonertinib combined with ITC as salvage therapy achieved an intracranial ORR of 60%, with median intracranial PFS and OS of 7.3 and 8.8 months, respectively (42). Preclinical data have also suggested that combining antiangiogenic agents with EGFR inhibitors yields synergistic benefits (43). Supporting this hypothesis, a retrospective study of 27 patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC and LM demonstrated that adding bevacizumab to osimertinib significantly improved survival outcomes compared with osimertinib monotherapy (mOS: 18.0 vs. 13.7 months, P=0.046; median PFS: 10.6 vs. 5.5 months, P=0.04) (44). Furthermore, the addition of antiangiogenic therapy to TKIs did not yield a statistically significant survival benefit in our cohort (12.2 vs. 10.8 months, P=0.61). To date, most prospective investigations of this therapeutic strategy have focused on patients with BM, while evidence specific to LM remains largely retrospective. Importantly, a prospective phase II clinical trial (NCT04148898) is currently underway to evaluate the efficacy of this combination regimen in patients with LM. Collectively, these findings underscore the evolving role of next-generation TKIs, dose intensification strategies, and combinatorial regimens in optimizing outcomes for patients with LM. Further studies investigating the biology of LM, including mechanisms of CNS colonization and the role of the immune microenvironment, are warranted to refine therapeutic strategies and improve long-term survival.

Our study also highlights that patients receiving ITC had significantly longer mOS than those who did not (11.7 vs. 7.6 months, P=0.04). This result is supported by a prospective phase I/II trial of 30 patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC and LM who had progressed on prior TKI therapy, which reported a mOS of 9 months following treatment with intrathecal pemetrexed plus dexamethasone (45). These findings suggest that ITC moderately extends survival and alleviates CNS symptoms, although its optimal dosage and treatment cycles remain to be defined. The efficacy and safety of intrathecal therapies should therefore be confirmed in prospective, randomized clinical trials. Despite escalating doses of third-generation EGFR-TKIs, OS improvements remain limited for patients who progress on these agents (40), underscoring the need for alternative strategies. Intrathecal drug delivery warrants further investigation in this setting. For example, the phase I LMIS study reported encouraging efficacy of intrathecal sintilimab in NSCLC-LM patients previously treated with targeted therapy and intrathecal pemetrexed, achieving median PFS and OS of 3.5 and 11.5 months, respectively (46). In our cohort, patients who received WBRT demonstrated significantly longer OS than those who did not (12.0 vs. 7.2 months, P=0.048). However, this benefit is inconsistent with prior reports and may partly reflect the effect of WBRT on concomitant BM (12,47,48). In the subgroup of LM patients without concomitant brain metastases, WBRT demonstrated a numerical trend toward improved survival (16.2 vs. 6.8 months, P=0.07). While this observation must be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size, it suggests that WBRT may still offer localized palliative benefits and holds potential clinical relevance for this specific subpopulation (49). Collectively, these findings suggest that while WBRT provides rapid symptom relief, its universal impact on OS in unselected LM patients remains limited. Nonetheless, for patients with concomitant BM or nodular LM, intracranial radiotherapy (such as WBRT or stereotactic radiotherapy) remains a useful treatment option and should be considered in multidisciplinary discussions.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-institution retrospective analysis, our findings may reflect specific institutional treatment practices and are inherently subject to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Second, our strict reliance on positive CSF cytology for LM diagnosis, while ensuring high pathological specificity, likely excluded patients diagnosed solely via neuroimaging and typical clinical presentation. Given the imperfect clinical sensitivity of CSF cytology, the exclusion of 74 CSF-negative, clinically suspected patients likely underestimated the true LM incidence and may have biased our cohort toward patients with a higher CNS tumor burden. Nonetheless, cytological confirmation remains crucial, as neuroimaging alone may not always be sufficiently diagnostic (50). Third, significant heterogeneity in patient characteristics and nonrandomized treatment allocation introduced critical confounding factors. For instance, our finding regarding ITC must be interpreted with caution. Although patients receiving ITC (n=29) showed a prolonged mOS in the univariate analysis, our granular comparison (Table S1) revealed that this group was significantly younger and possessed markedly better baseline performance status (ECOG PS 0–2). Furthermore, the decision to administer ITC may have been heavily influenced by unmeasured favorable factors, such as lower extracranial disease burden, longer expected survival, or greater access to intensive multidisciplinary care (as evidenced by their disproportionate receipt of intensified concomitant treatments like high-dose TKIs). This substantial treatment imbalance perfectly explains why ITC did not emerge as an independent prognostic factor. Additionally, the subgroup analysis evaluating WBRT in LM patients without concomitant brain metastases is severely underpowered due to the small sample size (n=7). The observed numerical trend toward improved OS (16.2 vs. 6.8 months) must therefore be interpreted with strict caution as an exploratory signal rather than a definitive conclusion. Fourth, small sample sizes in certain molecular subgroups (e.g., n=5 for ALK-positive patients) reduce statistical power, rendering those specific results primarily hypothesis-generating. Fifth, the absence of CSF liquid biopsy testing [e.g., circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) profiling] limited our ability to evaluate CSF-specific genomic alterations and their influence on outcomes, which should be addressed in future biomarker-integrated studies. Finally, these limitations collectively highlight the essential need for future prospective, multicenter studies to validate and expand upon our findings.

Conclusions

Over the past decade, the incidence of LM in advanced lung cancer has steadily increased. Our results suggest that targeted therapies and ITC significantly improve OS in this population. However, large-scale, prospective, randomized trials are essential to confirm these findings and to optimize treatment strategies.
