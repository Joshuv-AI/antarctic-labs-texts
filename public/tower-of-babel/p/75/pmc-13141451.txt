High burden of Candida blankii (syn.Tardiomyces blankii) bloodstream infections among neonates in an intensive care unit in Bangladesh
Authors: Tanzir Ahmed Shuvo, Sayeeda Huq, Nusrat Jahan Shaly, Faiyaz Ahmed Khan, Sifat Binte Ibrahim, Md Shoieb Ul Haque, Fahmida Dil Farzana, Farhat Jaby, Md Kamrul Islam, Adiba Tasnim, Shams E Tabriz Bhuiyan, Zakir Hossain Habib, Nandita Banik, Sazzad Bin Shahid, Mahbuba Chowdhury, Tahsinul Amin, Jesmin Akter, Gazi Md Salahuddin Mamun, Sanzida Khan, Asifa Kumkum, Debashis Sen, Md Abdul Aleem, Md Mobarok Hossain, Tasnim Jabin, Mohammad Jubair, Ayesha Afrin, Fardousi Akter Sathi, Mustafizur Rahman, Dilruba Ahmed, Ismet Nigar, Fahmida Chowdhury

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvasive fungal infections (IFIs) remain underrecognized in low- and middle-income countries due to limited surveillance. This study aims to report the burden of Candida blankii infections detected through ongoing surveillance in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of a tertiary hospital in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Since August 2022, IFI surveillance in a NICU in Bangladesh enrolled neonates with sepsis who met predefined clinical criteria. Blood samples were collected and processed using the BACT/ALERT 3D system, and fungal isolates were identified with automated identification system. Antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) was performed on a subset of isolates using E-strip gradient diffusion. Whole-genome sequencing of nine representative C. blankii isolates, initially identified by VITEK MS, confirmed species identification by demonstrating 94% to 99% average nucleotide identity with reference sequences in the NCBI GenBank database. A total of 411 neonates were enrolled, with a mean age of 16 (± 7) days; 39% were male. More than half (225/411, 55%) had positive fungal blood cultures, among which C. blankii was the most common species (126/225, 31%). The isolates showed high MICs to fluconazole and voriconazole but lower MIC values against amphotericin B (AMP) and echinocandins. In-hospital mortality among C. blankii cases was significantly higher than in infections caused by other fungi. Findings indicate a substantial burden of C. blankii in the NICU and underscore the need for improved diagnostics, targeted treatment strategies, and strengthened infection control in resource-limited neonatal settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13118-8.

Introduction

Invasive fungal infections (IFIs) are escalating global health challenge, as emphasized by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. Recent evidence suggests that, every year around 6.5 million people suffer from IFIs worldwide. These infections are associated with 3.8 million deaths every year, 2.5 million of which are directly attributable to IFIs [2]. IFIs result from the defective immune mechanisms among individuals who are immunocompromised or undergo invasive procedures [3] etc. The pathogen penetrates through skin breaches, gastrointestinal tract (GIT) lesion, indwelling catheters and spreads through hematogenous pathway to different body parts causing blood stream infection/candidemia [4].

Candida Species, are a major group of opportunistic fungi and are significant contributors to IFIs that leads to blood stream infection (candidemia) with mortality rates ranging from 30% to over 50%, depending on the species involved [5], especially in hospital environments [6, 7].

Globally, candidemia is among the most frequently occurring infections in hospitalized individuals, specially affecting individuals of extreme ages [8]. Neonates, particularly those admitted in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) are highly immunocompromised, which enhances their susceptibility to all sorts of infection including candidemia [9]. Previous data have shown that the overall incidence of candidemia among NICU neonates is higher in low-middle income countries (LMICs) [10–13]. Studies have also suggested a mortality rate of up to 73% due to neonatal candidemia in NICU population who are preterm and low-birth-weight [14–17].

The rising incidence of non-albicans Candida (NAC) species presents a notable public health challenge, correlating with increased morbidity and mortality [18–21]. In recent years the trend has shifted with NAC being more prevalent in LMICs [11, 22]. While candidemia is a prominent problem in Bangladesh, very little is known about the actual situation caused by NAC [23–25]. Unlike other NAC species, an emerging candida species named Candida blankii (C. blankii) exhibits the rare ability to grow up to 42 °C, display slower growth in nutrient-poor media, and show a reduced susceptibility to azoles making treatment difficult [26]. Altogether, these features make C. blankii a particularly concerning pathogen, especially in vulnerable neonatal populations.

So far, very few studies have reported C. blankii since its first identification in 1968 in minks (dark-colored, semiaquatic, carnivorous mammals of the genera Neogale and Mustela and part of the family Mustelidae) [27].

Active surveillance against fungal infection is required given the rising burden of IFIs and antifungal resistance in low-middle income countries. icddr, b in collaboration with the Institute of Epidemiology Disease Control And Research (IEDCR) and US Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) is implementing a surveillance activity since 2022, to support collection as well as reporting of epidemiological and laboratory data on fungal infections burden from Bangladesh.

During the surveillance there was a surge in identification of this rarely reported Candida pathogen called C. blankii. This manuscript aims to report a high burden of C. blankii infection in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of one of the surveillance hospital (tertiary) in Dhaka, Bangladesh.

This rare species C. blankii has recently been proposed to be reclassified as Tardiomyces blankii due to phylogenomic analysis [26]. This taxonomic change reflects genetic relatedness to T. depauwii and T. digboiensis and does not indicate a change in the organism’s phenotype or clinical relevance. For consistency and clarity, we will continue to use the name C. blankii throughout this manuscript.

Methods

Study design, site and population

This study is a part of an ongoing hospital based IFI surveillance, aiming to identify the circulating fungal pathogens responsible for infections among patients admitted to different intensive care units (ICUs), including the general ICU, NICU, and Pediatric ICU (PICU) of two tertiary hospitals in Dhaka, Bangladesh since August 2022.

In this paper, we report findings from the NICU of one of the surveillance hospitals, where a high number of IFI was detected between August 2022 and August 2025.

Demographic and clinical data

Data were collected using a structured clinical case report form comprising of few demographic and clinical information including date of birth (DOB), mode of delivery, duration of hospital stay, clinical diagnosis, history of invasive procedure, underlying condition, documentation of drugs (antifungal and antimicrobial) administered etc.

Sample collection, transportation & processing

Approximately 2–2.5 ml of peripheral venous blood was collected aseptically from each enrolled neonate. The sampling needle was discarded and replaced by fresh sterile needle, after removing from venipuncture site and the collected (1 ml-2 ml) blood was introduced in to the automated Bact/Alert FA Plus blood culture bottle (Biomerieux, France), after disinfecting the top of the bottle with 70% ethyl alcohol swab. The inoculated bottle was then gently shaken a few times to mix the blood in the broth medium. Rest of the blood (minimum 1 ml) was introduced in an EDTA tube for microscopic evaluation under Gram stain. These procedures were performed at the bedside of patients. Collected blood samples were promptly sent to the Microbiology laboratory within 2–4 h for initial processing in order to ensure maximum viability of the fungal pathogen.

In cases with suspected respiratory infection or meningitis, additional specimens including endotracheal aspirates, sputum, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) were collected under sterile conditions into paul’s tracheal aspirate collector, sputum container, CSF collection kit respectively. In the present study, identification of C. blankii is reported exclusively from blood samples.

Laboratory investigation

Microbiological and automated identification

The samples were incubated in the BactAlert 3D System (Biomerieux, France). Samples giving positive signals were inoculated onto the following media and the plates were incubated at 25°-30 °C and 35°-37 °C for ten days to facilitate growth of molds and yeasts:

Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA), Bacton Dickinson, USA: supports the growth of many filamentous fungi and Candida species.

Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA), Bacton Dickinson, USA: supports the growth of many filamentous fungi and Candida species.

CHROMagar™ Candida Plus, CHROMagar, France: a Selective chromogenic culture medium for presumptive identification of Candida species.

CHROMagar™ Candida Plus, CHROMagar, France: a Selective chromogenic culture medium for presumptive identification of Candida species.

Blood Agar (BA), Bacton Dickinson, USA: supports the growth of many filamentous fungi.

Blood Agar (BA), Bacton Dickinson, USA: supports the growth of many filamentous fungi.

The inoculated plates were examined daily for fungal growth, and for colonies suspected for Candida species. The presumptive colonies were stained with Gram stain. Presumptive isolates were sub-cultured onto fresh media to ensure purity and species identification was performed using the VITEK 2 system (BioMérieux, France) with the YST identification card and Confirmation was carried out by Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Time of Flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry using the VITEK MS Prime (BioMérieux, France). Sample preparation included on MSDS Slide, extraction with formic acid (FA) followed by application of α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) matrix reagent. The positive isolates were preserved with Tryptone Soya Broth (TSB) with 15% Glycerol and stored at -80 °C for future analysis.

It should be noted that during the initial phase of surveillance, organism identification was performed using the VITEK 2 system. However, from April 2023 onward, all isolates were identified using the VITEK MS Prime system. Isolates identified prior to this transition were retrieved from storage, sub-cultured, and re-identified using the VITEK MS Prime platform. VITEK-2 has a limited database and can identify a restricted range of organisms compared to VITEK-MS Prime which contains a vast database, enabling it to identify at least 1316 organisms along with the ability to identify 15,000 distinct strains. In this study we particularly used Vitek MS 3.0 library which enabled the detection of C. blankii [28, 29].

Antifungal susceptibility testing

AFST for a subset of 28 randomly selected isolates were conducted using two gradient diffusion strip tests (E-strip, BioMérieux, France), with results interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [30].

Details of all culture media, chemical reagents, automated identification systems, and antifungal susceptibility testing platforms, including manufacturers and countries of origin, are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

DNA extraction and whole genome sequencing

To confirm species identification and enable in-depth genomic characterization, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on nine C. blankii isolates, initially identified by VITEK MS Prime, which were selected to be representative of the entire study period. Each isolate was retrieved from − 80 °C storage and cultured on CHROMagar™ Candida Plus and Sabouraud Dextrose Agar at 37 °C for 72 h, after which high-molecular-weight genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA purity was assessed via NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer and Qubit™ 4.0 Fluorometer. Sequencing libraries were prepared without fragmentation using the Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, SQK-NBD114.24) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the libraries were sequenced on a MinION MK1C device (ONT) for 72 h. Basecalling was performed in real-time using Guppy (v6.4.2) with the high-accuracy model, and the resulting raw reads were quality-filtered (Chopper v0.5.0, Q8, minimum length 100 bp). Sequencing depth and assembly statistics were assessed with NanoStat (v1.6.0) and SeqKit (v2.5.0), and high-quality reads were assembled de novo into draft genomes using Flye (v2.9.4). Assembled contigs were initially screened using the CZ ID platform, and definitive species confirmation was achieved through FastANI (v1.43) analysis, which demonstrated 94% to 99% average nucleotide identity with reference C. blankii sequences in the NCBI RefSeq database, providing robust, genome-wide validation of species identification.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as absolute numbers and relative percentages, while quantitative variables were presented as means with standard deviations or as medians with interquartile ranges, depending on the distribution. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square test. Student’s t-test was performed for normally distributed quantitative variables and median test was applied for skewed quantitative variables. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p-values were calculated to analyze the association between C. blankii infection and mortality, using bloodstream candidemia due to other fungal species as the reference group. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to estimate adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals by controlling for relevant risk factors. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis for the two groups (C. blankii infection vs. other IFIs) was used to generate survival curves and estimate median survival times. Geometric mean, 50th percentile and 90th percentile values were determined for minimum inhibitory concentration. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 15 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), and graphical visualizations were created using PyCharm Community Edition 2024.2 and Microsoft Excel version 2019.

Outcome

Half of the (63/126, 50%) C. blankii positive neonates died in the hospital, compared to 33% neonates with other IFIs. This difference in mortality among the two groups was statistically significant in a univariate analysis (P-value: 0·012) (Table 2). Multivariate regression model further supported these findings. As shown in Fig. 4 (a), neonates with C. blankii infection had nearly three times higher odds of in-hospital mortality compared to those with other IFIs (OR = 2.84; 95% CI: 1.38–5.86), after adjusting for relevant covariates. Among these, mechanical ventilation (OR = 6.60; 95% CI: 2.62–16.67), history of transfusion (OR = 5.12; 95% CI: 1.85–14.18), and non-invasive ventilation (OR = 3.02; 95% CI: 1.35–6.75) were independently associated with increased odds of mortality. Similarly, Fig. 4 (b) shows that neonates with C. blankii infection had higher odds of mortality compared to those without any IFI (OR = 1.29; 95% CI: 0.75–2.22), although this difference was not statistically significant. In this model, mechanical ventilation remained independently associated with increased mortality risk. Table 3 represents the outcomes in neonates with C. blankii infection, categorized by antifungal drug history. Out of 62 neonates who received fluconazole as the only antifungal agent, almost half (48.4%) died in the hospital compared to 27.3% of the 11 neonates who were treated with only voriconazole. Despite Fluconazole + AMP (Amphotericin B), Fluconazole + MIC (Micafungin), and Fluconazole + FCT (Flucytosine) showing 0% mortality each, these drugs were used in only one case each as subsequent therapy following failed fluconazole therapy.

Genomic analysis confirms C. blankii identification

De novo assembly of Oxford Nanopore long-read sequences yielded high-quality draft genomes with an average size of 13.69 Mb and a guanine-cytosine (G + C) content of 54.9%, features consistent with the genus Candida. The number of contigs per genome ranged from 6 to 17, indicating high-quality assemblies with good continuity, and annotation predicted approximately 4,390 protein-coding genes per isolate. Definitive species confirmation was achieved through FastANI (Fast Average Nucleotide Identity) analysis, which demonstrated 94% to 99% similarity between our isolates and reference C. blankii sequences available in the NCBI GenBank database. These ANI values substantially exceed the established species demarcation threshold, providing unequivocal, genome-wide validation that all nine isolates are indeed C. blankii. The generation of these high-quality draft genomes confirms our phenotypic and VITEK MS Prime identifications of our C. blankii isolates.

Microscopic and cultural characteristics of C. blankii

Microscopic examination (Fig. 7, left) using Gram stain revealed C. blankii cells as purple in colour, typical oval or elongated cells, measuring 2–6 micrometers in diameter with formation of pseudohyphae or true hyphae. The organism produces white to cream-colored colonies on CHROMAgar Candida media. Colonies are typically smooth, round and have a slight glossiness with a well-defined edge (Fig. 7, right). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents results from the first IFI surveillance in a NICU setting of Bangladesh. The surveillance identified a substantial number of laboratory-confirmed IFI cases over the study period, including an unexpectedly frequent detection of C. blankii. Globally, isolations of C. blankii from human samples have been rarely reported. Till date only a few numbers of studies have reported single case detection [31–34]. Thus, the detection of numerous isolates from a single NICU is alarming particularly given how rarely this organism is usually reported. Prior to this, the only reported outbreak occurred in an NICU in India, involving blood stream infections in nine neonates [29]. Retrospective surveillance studies in Norway and the United Kingdom also detected additional clinical isolates, although only in small numbers [35, 36]. Notably, a recent study from Bangladesh also identified C. blankii retrospectively in eight preserved blood samples collected between September and November 2021 from a tertiary academic hospital, suggesting a substantial burden of systemic C. blankii infection during that period [25]. These findings highlight the potential under-recognition of IFIs in neonatal settings in Bangladesh and underscore the importance of systematic microbiological testing and continued surveillance to better define the epidemiology and inform prevention and infection control strategies.

Another important finding from this study is the diagnostic limitations of automated identification systems such as VITEK 2, which initially misidentified these isolates as Candida ciferrii. Only upon reanalysis with VITEK MS Prime were these isolates identified with high confidence. Previous studies have stated similar situation where C. blankii was misidentified as Stephanoascus cifferi with high probability [31]. Chowdhary et al. also reported similar scenario where 5 out of the 9 C. blankii isolates were identified as C. cifferrii by the VITEK-2 system [29]. These findings suggest that this organism is present in healthcare settings, particularly in the NICU, but may remain underreported due to diagnostic limitations.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of nine isolates confirmed their species identification and yielded high-quality draft genomes (average size 13.69 Mb, 54.9% G + C content), providing a foundation for understanding the biology of this under-studied yeast. Furthermore, this genomic work allows us to contextualize C. blankii within its appropriate phylogenetic framework. Unlike common pathogens such as Candida albicans, C. tropicalis, and C. parapsilosis which belong to the CTG clade C. blankii is phylogenetically distant and instead resides within a distinct clade of the order Dipodascales, closely related to Candida digboiensis and the recently reclassified Tardiomyces depauwii [24]. This evolutionary divergence may have important clinical implications, potentially contributing to the unique phenotypic characteristics of C. blankii, including its thermotolerance, reduced azole susceptibility, and emergence as a neonatal pathogen [27, 30].

We compared C. blankii positivity across demographic and clinical exposures in the NICU. Overall, we found no evidence of differences between groups (C. blankii vs. other IFI cases) to establish any potential risk factor which is contributing to the high burden of C. blankii positivity in the NICU. In contrast, earlier studies have identified factors such as intubation, mechanical ventilation, central venous catheterization, and low birth weight as risk factors for candidemia caused by IFIs [20, 25, 37]. No study to date has specifically demonstrated an association between C. blankii infection and any particular clinical exposure or risk factor. The homogeneity of the study population and possible limitations in sample size may have reduced the power to detect subtle inter-group differences. These findings also indicate that C. blankii may not present clinically distinct symptoms compared to other Candida species in neonates, though further studies are needed to evaluate differences in pathogenicity, and long-term outcomes.

The monthly trend of C. blankii detection shows no specific pattern. However, despite decreased enrollment in 2024 due to challenges related to the unfavorable situation, the C. blankii positivity rate remained relatively consistent and did not show a substantial decline. The gradual decline in C. blankii cases observed in 2025 may be attributed to the implementation of infection prevention and control (IPC) measures introduced as part of the ongoing surveillance activities. In contrast, Kaur et al. reported a pattern of IFIs due to C. krusei peaking in September and October [20]. This highlights the need for further investigation, as to the best of our knowledge, no study to date has specifically examined the monthly distribution/seasonal variation of C. blankii infection. Identifying such seasonal patterns could provide valuable insights into environmental factors influencing transmission and also help anticipate high-risk periods, and guide infection, control and preventive strategies more effectively.

Our findings reveal a high geometric mean MIC value of Fluconazole (32 µg/ml) against C. blankii isolates. The mean MIC of voriconazole was also relatively higher (2·2 µg/ml) compared to that of Amphotericin B, Micafungin and Caspofungin. These values indicate that fluconazole and voriconazole requiring higher concentrations to be effective, while amphotericin-B and micafungin are more effective at lower concentrations, suggesting higher efficacy against the fungus. Although no approved susceptibility breakpoints (CLSI guideline) for C. blankii are available, our susceptibility results to antifungal agents are comparable with research papers where all isolates showed high MICs to fluconazole and voriconazole, while amphotericin B demonstrated potent antifungal activity [26, 38]. While Chowdhary et al. [29] reported a geometric mean MIC of 8 µg/ml for fluconazole against C. blankii, our study found a value four times higher (32 vs. 8 µg/ml). Similarly, the geometric mean MIC for voriconazole was eight times greater (2.2 vs. 0.25 µg/ml), indicating that substantially higher antifungal drug concentrations are needed to inhibit C. blankii. Although till date, C. blankii have been reported rarely, the elevated MIC values highlight the importance of precise species identification for appropriate clinical management.

Despite having high MIC for fluconazole and voriconazole, those who received only fluconazole or voriconazole exhibited lower mortality compared to those who could not receive any antifungal drug at all. The outcomes suggest that fluconazole and voriconazole may provide some therapeutic benefit compared to the absence of antifungal treatment. In contrast, Sathi et al. reported in a retrospective study that, two among nine C. blankii isolates having higher MICs to fluconazole and amphotericin B, caused fatal outcomes [25]. There is also a single report of a bloodstream infection in a preterm neonate who died despite treatment with amphotericin B and caspofungin [31]. These findings further indicate the presence of mutations in C. blankii that may cause reduced susceptibility to antifungals, similar to those identified in C. auris [39, 40]. Moreover, C. blankii can survive outside the host, allowing it to function as both a commensal organism and an environmental pathogen [33, 41, 42]. This dual capability enables organism’s prolonged survival in hospital environments. Such environmental persistence may further contribute to antifungal resistance through several adaptive mechanisms [39, 40].

On the contrary, the overall in hospital mortality rate among the C. blankii positive cases remained significantly higher than those neonates who were positive with other IFIs. We also found that C. blankii positive cases had almost three times higher odds of mortality as outcome compared to those infected with other IFIs after adjusting for potential covariates. There is lack of evidences linked to mortality in C. blankii infected cases. To date, only Chowdhary et al. have reported a case fatality rate, documenting 45% mortality among neonates with C. blankii infection [29]. However, that study did not provide comparative mortality data for neonates with other fungal infections or those without any fungal infection. The evidences likely suggest C. blankii candidemia carry a higher risk of mortality among vulnerable neonates. Neonates are highly immunocompromised, because their innate and adaptive immune systems are not fully developed which makes them more vulnerable to all sorts of infection including candidemia/IFIs, particularly those admitted in the NICU. These neonates, are at increased risk due to multiple predisposing factors such as, invasive medical procedures, cross-transmission, comorbidities, use of broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents etc. This highlights the importance of implementing infection prevention and control measures including regular monitoring system to established hygiene guidelines and proper training of staff handling this high-risk population. As part of the surveillance activities, we are conducting regular training sessions and routine monitoring of infection prevention and control (IPC) practices to help staff become familiar with the guidelines and consistently apply them in daily care. In addition, the possibility of co-infections/comorbid conditions may further complicate management and worsen prognosis. This persistent mortality despite treatment underscores the need for improved antifungal strategies and further investigation into optimal therapeutic approaches for this emerging pathogen. At present, there are no specific treatment guidelines for this genus, however, MIC data suggest that Amphotericin B and echinocandins (micafungin, caspofungin) perform better than other antifungals. A favorable outcome in a C. blankii positive case with echinocandin monotherapy has been documented in one case report [38]. In contrast, The Infectious Disease Society of America has strongly recommended Amphotericin B as the first line treatment of neonatal invasive fungal infection based on moderate quality evidence [43]. While step-down therapy with azoles may be considered based on AFST results, their effectiveness could be limited due to the consistently high MIC values [44]. Altogether, these features make C. blankii a particularly concerning pathogen and highlight its clinical and epidemiological significance as an emerging pathogen in intensive care settings.

Limitations

Our inclusion criteria are very specific and intended to include neonates who are possible cases of IFIs. This limits our ability to enroll in a broader neonatal patient population and creates missed opportunities to identify the true burden of the C. blankii infections as well as IFIs and the impact of IFIs. Moreover, due to resource constraints our surveillance is currently restricted to only two hospitals: one of which being a very recent addition and was not reported in this paper. This limits our ability to provide a comparative overview and an overall picture of the C. blankii burden in the country. Moreover, Moreover, the lack of rapid and cost-effective antifungal susceptibility testing facility and lack of reference guidelines or established standards for C. blankii further limited our ability to provide optimal guidance for better clinical management. Another limitation of the study is that we identified some [33] of the C. blankii isolates retrospectively from preserved isolates. Unfortunately, some of the isolates could not be revived to initiate the re-identification process.

Conclusion

This study presents the largest reported number of blood-stream infections caused by the emerging pathogen C. blankii. Our findings highlight the substantial burden of C. blankii infections in neonates, characterized by high mortality and higher potential of resistance to the accessible antifungal drugs leading to increased healthcare costs. While Echinocandins or Amphotericin B might be effective treatment options, their high cost in resource-limited settings like Bangladesh underscores the critical need for improved infection prevention and control strategies. Future larger, multicenter studies to better understand the epidemiology, clinical manifestations, and antifungal susceptibility patterns of C. blankii infections in neonates is required. This study also emphasizes the lack of readily available methods likely contributes to the underreporting of C. blankii infections. Existing reference laboratories may be utilized to implement hospital based fungal surveillance to identify the burden of such emerging pathogens at a broader level and plan appropriate mitigating measures to control their transmission. By addressing these critical areas, we can improve our understanding of C. blankii infections and develop effective strategies to prevent and manage this emerging threat to neonatal health.
