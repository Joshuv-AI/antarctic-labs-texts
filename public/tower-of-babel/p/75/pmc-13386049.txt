Impact of a rib fracture treatment protocol: a propensity score matching analysis
Authors: Thermutis Djanie, Ashley Pfaff, Claire Miller, Ashar Ata, Allan E Stolarski, Lauren Evans, Carl Rosati, Matthew Johnson, Ashley Bartscherer, Steven C. Stain, Marcel Tafen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRib fractures (RFx) increase morbidity, mortality, and resource utilization. This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a novel RFx treatment protocol (RFTP) on patient outcomes.Study designWe included 3679 RFx patients admitted to a single level 1 trauma center between January 2011 and October 2018. The RFTP, launched as a computer physician order entry on October 25, 2016, included an escalating multimodal pain management, a standardized nursing assessment with interventions, and an atelectasis and mucus clearance regimen. Patients’ characteristics and outcomes before and after the protocol were compared. Propensity score matching analysis was used to account for differences in baseline characteristics between groups. Intention-to-treat and protocol analyses were done. Outcome measures included mortality, length of stay (LOS), pulmonary complications, rapid response activations, and elevation in level of care.ResultsThere were 2393 and 1286 patients treated in the pre-protocol and post-protocol time periods, respectively. Adherence rate to the RFTP was 53.3%. Multivariate adjustment showed a significant reduction in hospital LOS by 1.4 days (95% CI 0.71 to 2.02), a significant decrease in the odds of requiring mechanical ventilation (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.00) and rapid responses (OR 0.55, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.72). Comparison of the matched samples also showed a significant decrease in LOS (14.7 days vs 11.2 days, p<0.001), rapid responses (17.7% vs 12.8%, p=0.010), and mortality (10.7% vs 6.5%) in those treated using the RFTP in the post-protocol period. These findings were mostly substantiated on per-protocol analyses.ConclusionThe RFx protocol was associated with significant improvements in the LOS and a reduction in rapid responses required. In a severely morbid matched sample, the RFTP was also associated with reduced mortality. Multidisciplinary protocolized care of patients with RFx is associated with better outcomes.Level of evidenceLevel III evidence (retrospective cohort study)

Introduction

Rib fractures (RFx) are present in 10% of patients with blunt chest trauma and account for 12% of patient mortality.1More than two-thirds of patients who die secondary to blunt chest trauma have RFx. RFx are a hallmark of severe injury: nearly all patients with RFx will have associated injuries, more than half will require an immediate operation or intensive care unit (ICU) admission, one-third will develop pulmonary complications, and one-third will require discharge to an extended care facility.1

Compared with blunt trauma, RFx lead to more ICU admission and longer hospital and ICU stays.2 Regarding long-term outcomes, RFx lead to chronic pain, dyspnea, poor quality of life, inability to return to work, and increased hospital costs.35 The risk of complications or death is particularly increased when other risk factors such as age and a high number of RFx are present6 .1 7 Further, identifying patients at risk of developing pulmonary complications from RFx is difficult, as clinical symptoms on presentation do not accurately predict outcomes.8 The significant chest wall pain associated with RFx leads to decreased walking and depth of respirations that, in turn, result in increased pulmonary morbidity.9 Despite their initial clinical appearance suggesting otherwise, patients often deteriorate during the following 24 hours to 72 hours as these complications manifest,8 10 particularly older patients.11 Patients with RFx that lack acutely life-threatening injuries are at risk of being overlooked in the short term and experiencing a delay in care until after they have developed complications.12 It is imperative for the managing team to be aware of this clinical course. The management of RFx has been focused on secondary prevention: minimizing morbidity and preventing complications. Multimodal analgesia, respiratory exercises, and respiratory support protocols effectively improve the outcomes of patients with RFx.13 14 Checklists and protocols reduce unnecessary use of resources and improve safety and patient outcomes.15 16

In our level 1 trauma center, a comprehensive multidisciplinary RFx treatment protocol (RFTP) was developed and integrated into the hospital’s Computer Physician Order Entry (CPOE) to standardize the care of patients with RFx. The RFTP was implemented in October 2016. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the RFTP. Toward this goal, we compared the patients’ outcomes before and after the implementation of the protocol.

Methods

Intervention

Our protocol was created by integrating the results of a literature search213 1720 and input from surgeons, anesthesiologists, respiratory therapists, radiologists, and nurses (figure 1). Patients were risk-stratified at admissionto analyze their general condition based on an internally developed score that incorporated fractures, age, pulmonary contusions, preexisting comorbidities, respiratory status, and physician judgment (figure 1).

Rib fracture treatment protocol algorithm. CPOE, Computer Physician Order Entry; H&P, history and physical; I.S, incentive spirometry; SICU, surgical intensive care unit.

RFTP consists of a multimodal pain management approach with a set of mandatory non-narcotic pain medications from four different drug classes ordered as a default unless otherwise contraindicated. Pain control was then supplemented with oral and intravenous narcotics as appropriate. If this approach was not adequate or at the discretion of the treatment team, and for certain categories (chronic opioid use), a pain service consultation was obtained. Options available to the pain service included epidural or paravertebral pain catheters, ketamine, intravenous lidocaine, and high-dose patient-controlled analgesia.

Additionally, the RFTP included an atelectasis and secretion clearance protocol with an associated respiratory therapy consultation for patient evaluation and subsequent application of lung expansion techniques and nebulizers if necessary. Lung expansion devices and techniques were chosen at the discretion of the respiratory therapist and included deep breathing and coughing techniques, positive airway pressure therapy, oscillating positive expiratory pressure, intrapulmonary percussive ventilation, vest therapy, cough assist therapy, hand-held percussor therapy, and manual chest physical therapy.

At the core of the RFTP was standardized nursing assessments with incentive spirometry (IS). Patients were instructed to use the device at least once every waking hour, each time attempting to complete 10 breaths. The incentive spirometer volume goal was set at admission at 15 mL/kg ≤1500 mL and was documented by the nurse every 8 hours. Pain was assessed and documented during activity, cough, or IS. Failure to meet the IS or pain goals prompted escalation of pain therapy and notification of the house officer. Early walking was mandatory unless specified otherwise. Lastly, the nurse documented cough as strong or weak every 8 hours.

Setting and population

The study was conducted at Albany Medical Center Hospital, a level 1 trauma center, between January 2011 and October 2018 after approval by the institutional review board (AMC IRB # 4282) with a waiver for the requirement to obtain informed consent owing to the study’s retrospective nature. We evaluated all admitted trauma patients aged >18 years with at least one RFx caused by blunt mechanisms. The patients were divided into two groups according to the time of admission before October 2016, prior to the implementation of the RFTP (baseline group), or after the implementation of the RFTP (RFTP group). To eliminate the effect of compliance, evaluate the real-world application, and reflect the pragmatic approach of our protocol, we adopted an intention-to-treat methodology where all patients with RFx after the implementation of RFTP were included, irrespective of whether the RFTP was ordered or not. This study was reported in accordance with the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence guidelines.21

Data collection

Data were collected from the Albany Medical Center trauma registry, the electronic health records system, billing and coding data, and the American Heart Association Get With The Guidelines Resuscitation registry data. Clinicodemographic data included age, sex, Injury Severity Score (ISS), emergency department Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, chest Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score, and number of fractured ribs. The number of RFx was abstracted via review of CT scans. The primary outcome measure was hospital length of stay (LOS). The secondary outcome measures were rapid response rates and escalation in level of care, in-hospital mortality, ICU admission, ICU LOS, development of pulmonary complications, need for mechanical ventilation, and duration of mechanical ventilation. Pulmonary complications were defined as a composite of pneumonia, empyema, and hemothorax not present at admission.

Statistical analysis

Linear and logistic regression was used to assess the association of RFTP on hospital LOS, ICU admission and LOS, mechanical ventilation requirements, and overall pulmonary complications. If associated at the 0.2 level on bivariate analyses, potential confounders were adjusted in the multivariable model and included for age, ISS, GCS, AIS chest, AIS head and neck, and number of ribs fractured.

We also conducted a comparison of the RFTP-compliant group from the post-protocol period to a matched baseline cohort using propensity score matching (PSM). We used nearest neighbor 1:1 matching, using 2×SD of propensity score as the caliper for matching. Covariates considered in the PSM were age, GCS score, ISS, head and neck AIS score, chest AIS score, and RFx count. We analyzed all primary and secondary outcome measures using the PSM. Finally, we performed a per-protocol analysis to further evaluate the efficacy of the protocol. Two analyses were conducted: the first compared the post-protocol compliant to post-protocol non-compliant patients. The second expanded on the analysis, incorporating all pre-protocol patients into the non-compliant group. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata V.15.0 statistical analysis software (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Of the 21 190 patients with adult trauma admitted during the study period, 3679 had one or more RFx. Among these, 2393 (65%) and 1286 (35%) belonged to the baseline and RFTP groups, respectively. The RFTP compliance rate was 53%, with 685 of the 1286 patients having the RFTP ordered at the time of admission (figure 2).

Total trauma admissions with rib fracture admissions during the study period (baseline group: admission before October 2016; RFTP group: admission after October 2016). In total, 53.3% of the patients in the RFTP group received the rib fracture treatment protocol (RFTP).

Patients in the baseline group were younger (55.4% vs 59.8%, p<0.001), more likely to be male (70.1% vs 66.5<0.009), and had a higher ISS (17.9 vs 15.3, p<0.001). The distribution of the number of RFx was not similar between the two groups: the baseline group had more patients with seven or more RFx compared with the RFTP group (24.7% vs 19.8%, p<0.001) (table 1).

Propensity score analysis resulted in the matching of 187 patients in the pre-protocol period to 187 protocol-compliant patients in the RFTP period. The matching resulted in the selection of a subset of patients representing more severe morbidity than that present in the groups they were drawn from. A comparison of the matched patients revealed that in the RFTP group, the hospital LOS decreased by 3.5 days (14.7±0.82 vs 11.2±0.57, p<0.001), and the rapid response rate was significantly lower (30% vs 13.9%, p<0.001). Interestingly, in this subset of patients, the in-hospital mortality also decreased significantly in the RFTP group (12.3% vs 4.8 p<0.001). However, there were no significant differences in ICU LOS (p=0.473) and composite pulmonary complications (p=0.195) between the propensity-matched groups (table 3).

Discussion

Implementation of a comprehensive multidisciplinary RFTP, including PSM, per-protocol, and expanded per-protocol analyses, at our level 1 trauma center was associated with a marked improvement in patient outcomes. We found a 25% reduction in hospital LOS and intubations, as well as >50% reduction in the number of rapid responses. Some studies reported improved outcomes after introducing RFx management protocols,12 14 22 with some centered around implementing thoracic epidural management.22 23 Todd et al demonstrated that the implementation of a clinical pathway focused on patients aged >45 years and the use of respiratory therapy, physical therapy, nutrition management, and pain management led to decreased mechanical ventilator-dependent days, LOS, infectious morbidity, and mortality.13

In this study, we could not analyze the exact mechanism or aspect of the RFTP responsible for its positive benefits. However, multiple factors are likely responsible for shorter LOS and decreased rate of rapid responses, including the culture change that accompanies the mere introduction of a protocol into any clinical setting24 . Services with large teams2527 will particularly benefit from the positive effect of standardization, including the elimination of individual biases, consistency, improved efficiency, and improved patient safety. Additionally, our protocol was a combination of practices shown to improve the outcomes of patients with RFx. Furthermore, mandatory order sets likely improved compliance and outcomes.

The regimented targeted assessment by nurses contributes to improved awareness and helps identify patients at risk for delayed decompensation. This may explain the sharp decline in rapid response rates without a corresponding change in elevation of care or mortality rates. Although not specifically evaluated in this study, it stands to reason that the combination of decreased LOS and reduced rapid response led to a reduction in resource utilization.

One of the intended goals of the RFTP was to raise awareness of the complications secondary to RFx within the medical center. Each element of the protocol has been demonstrated to improve outcomes of RFx in isolation. Improved analgesia,23 respiratory support mechanisms and protocols,13 early ambulation, IS,9 28 and surgical fixation may improve patient outcomes after RFx.3 29 30 Kourouche et al31 conducted a meta-analysis of studies reporting improved patient outcomes after blunt chest injury and concluded that the key components of a blunt chest injury care management are analgesia, respiratory support, preventive methods for pulmonary complications, and surgical fixation. Our current RFTP includes a comprehensive pain management protocol, pulmonary complication prevention protocol, and respiratory support evaluation and necessary interventions.31

Mortality rates and ventilator usage significantly reduced on per-protocol analysis, although this was not echoed in the intention-to-treat and PSM analyses. Notably, post-protocol compliance was associated with markedly lower rates of mechanical ventilation (8.3% vs 23.8%) and mortality (1.8% vs 6.0%); however, these differences are likely exaggerated by selection bias, as intubated and critically ill patients were not eligible for RFTP. Nonetheless, adjusted analyses still demonstrated significant reductions in hospital LOS (−1.4 days, p<0.001), rapid responses (OR 0.55, p<0.001), and mortality (OR 0.48, p=0.026), supporting a beneficial impact of protocol adherence.

There was also no significant difference in the rate of pulmonary complications between the two groups in this study. This may be due to the overall low rate of pulmonary complications in our patient population. Studies on the development of pulmonary complications after RFx suggest that an increased number of RFx and age are related to higher morbidity due to elevated rates of pulmonary complications. Bulger et al reported that in patients older than 65 years, the rates of mortality and pneumonia increased by 19% and 27%, respectively, for each additional RFx.14 This suggests that older patients may derive the greatest benefit from a RFx care protocol and should be specifically evaluated in future studies.

The limitations of this study include the retrospective analysis of two time periods from a single institution. Compliance with the RFTP among ordering providers was initially low after implementation and remained modest at 53.3%. Low and inconsistent RFTP compliance introduces selection bias, confounding by indication where the protocol was ordered bacause the patient was older, having more pain, more fractures, worse displacement, or at a perceived higher risk of pulmonary decline, limiting attribution of differences in mortality, LOS, and rapid‑response rates to the protocol itself. Additionally, long-term patient outcomes were not reported in this study, including out-of-hospital mortality, readmission rates, and post-discharge disposition. These are all important areas of interest in follow-up studies. Another limitation is that although we performed the PSM analysis to address potential biases introduced by the low compliance rate, the criteria used for PSM selection included confounders mostly associated with outcome and severity rather than enrollment into the protocol (age, number of RFx), which may have introduced a selection bias. Outcomes in PSM-matched patients were distinctively worse in the pre-protocol and post-protocol periods, suggesting the selection of sicker patients. Finally, there were statistical differences in the baseline characteristics of the two groups. Incomplete comorbidity data also prevented the inclusion of a comprehensive comorbidity index, which may affect interpretation. Although we attempted to control for this with PSM, there were variations between the two groups that may be related to the application of the protocol. This was an intention-to-treat analysis applied to a diverse patient population in a pragmatic manner. The impact of rib fracture repair was not assessed. This led to deviations from the protocol; nevertheless, we found a significant decrease in hospital LOS and frequency of rapid responses. Future studies should test RFTP in prospective, multicenter designs to reduce selection bias and include critically ill patients. Subsequent research is needed to refine eligibility, identify which parts of the protocol drive benefit, and assess outcomes beyond hospital stay, such as recovery, cost, and equity. Broader evaluation across diverse settings will help determine generalizability and sustainability.

Conclusion

The implementation of the RFTP was associated with significant reductions in hospital LOS, rapid responses, and mortality, with consistent findings across adjusted and per-protocol analyses. The apparent improvements in ventilator use and mortality, however, likely reflect selection bias given the likely de facto exclusion of intubated and critically ill patients from protocol eligibility. These results support the potential benefit of structured protocol adherence, while underscoring the need for prospective validation and refinement to ensure applicability across broader patient populations.
