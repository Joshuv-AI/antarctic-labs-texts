Estimating the Economically Justifiable Price of Limited-Duration Treatment with Donanemab for Early Symptomatic Alzheimer’s Disease in the United States
Authors: Malaz Boustani, Erin G Doty, Louis P Garrison Jr, Lee J Smolen, Timothy M Klein, Daniel R Murphy, Andrew W Spargo, Mark Belger, Joseph A Johnston
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe goal of this economic model is to estimate an economically justifiable price (EJP) for using donanemab for the treatment of early symptomatic Alzheimer’s disease (AD) in the United States based on clinical data from the phase 3 TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial (NCT04437511).MethodsWe adapted an AD Markov state-transition model developed by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review to estimate the EJP for donanemab at different willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds from the health care system perspective and the societal perspective as co-base cases.ResultsAssuming a WTP threshold of $150,000 per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) gained, the model estimates a 1-year (13-dose) EJP for donanemab of $80,538 from the health care system perspective and $91,126 from the societal perspective; at a WTP threshold of $100,000 per QALY gained, the model estimates a 1-year (13-dose) EJP for donanemab of $44,691 from the health care system perspective and $55,419 from the societal perspective. Mean total treatment costs per patient at the $150,000 per QALY gained EJP derived from the health care system perspective were estimated at $77,812 based on the average number of doses of donanemab patients received in the co-base case analysis. One-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA) indicated that treatment efficacy, disease severity at the time of treatment initiation, and duration of treatment effect were the main drivers of the potential EJP.ConclusionsResults from this modeling simulation informed by the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study support an EJP for limited-duration treatment with donanemab that exceeds per-dose list prices for currently available amyloid-targeting therapies, implying potentially lower lifetime costs and better value for money.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-024-00649-y.Keywords: Donanemab, Alzheimer’s disease, Cost-effectiveness, Economically justifiable price, Amyloid-targeting therapies

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Disease-modifying therapies for AD are becoming available, and an understanding of the costs of different treatments and approaches for treating the disease is vital to ensuring judicious allocation of scarce health care resources.What did the study ask?This study estimated the EJP of limited-duration treatment of AD with donanemab based on the clinical efficacy results observed in the phase 3 TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study and an existing economic model of the treatment of AD developed by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review.What were the study outcomes/conclusions?The model found that the EJP of donanemab for one year of treatment (13 doses) was $80,538 from a health care system perspective and $91,126 from a societal perspective at a WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained in the co-base cases.What has been learned from the study?Limited-duration treatment of AD that shows continued treatment benefit after completing therapy due to amyloid clearance has the potential to deliver strong value for money relative to continuous treatment until progression to later stages of disease.

Introduction

Dementia syndrome is characterized by progressive impairment in cognition and ability to function independently [1]. AD is the leading cause of dementia [1]. AD is characterized pathologically by a gradual accumulation of amyloid and tau proteins, leading to widespread neuronal loss [2]. The first clinically identifiable stage of the disease is mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD, where patients display cognitive impairment with limited functional disability [2]. As the disease progresses through mild, moderate, and severe dementia stages, patients experience worsening cognitive, functional, and psychological disability that leads eventually to death [1, 2].

In recent years, disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for patients with early symptomatic AD (MCI or mild dementia due to AD) to slow progression to later stages of disease [3, 4]. Two of these treatments, aducanumab and lecanemab, are indicated for continuous use, with appropriate use recommendations indicating that re-evaluation of continued treatment may be appropriate when patients progress to later stages of disease [3–6]. In contrast, results of the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial (NCT04437511) suggest that limited-duration treatment of 18 months or fewer with donanemab may be clinically appropriate for patients with early symptomatic AD: in the trial, patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD treated with donanemab, an amyloid plaque-specific monoclonal antibody, showed rapid clearance of accumulated amyloid (achieving amyloid levels < 24.1 Centiloids), with 76.4% of patients achieving clearance by 18 months (mean decrease in amyloid positron emission tomography [PET] of 87.0 Centiloids) [7]. Additionally, donanemab treatment resulted in a significant slowing of clinical disease progression in patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD compared to treatment with placebo across several measures, including the integrated Alzheimer’s Disease Rating Scale (iADRS), which is an integrated assessment of cognition and daily function measuring global disease severity (22.3% slowing of progression; p < 0.001), and the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale–Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB; 28.9% slowing; p < 0.01) [7].

A recent economic analysis of a hypothetical treatment for AD with a limited duration of treatment found that limited-duration therapies had the potential to deliver substantial value in the treatment of AD relative to best supportive care (BSC), with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of $157,288 per QALY gained (at an assumed price of $56,000 per year) as compared to $612,354 per QALY gained for a continuous treatment [8]. While illustrative of the potential value of limited-duration treatment, this prior model was based on a hypothetical treatment with an assumed treatment profile. The present work is an application of this model to determine the range of plausible EJPs for donanemab based on new phase 3 efficacy and safety data from the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study in patients with early symptomatic AD and limited-duration treatment [7].

Methods

TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 was conducted in accordance with consensus ethical principles, the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice Guideline, and applicable laws and regulations [7]. The protocol and its amendments were reviewed by an independent ethics committee/institutional review board at each site [7]. Written informed consent was obtained from participants and study partners [7]. An independent external data monitoring committee monitored the trial [7]. The present assessment is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. The parameters of the model, which specify the baseline population, disease progression and mortality, treatment effect, and downstream cost and utility consequences, were based on data from the published literature, including the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study.

Model Overview

We developed a semi-Markov state-transition model in Microsoft Excel to estimate the potential EJP of donanemab plus BSC versus BSC alone for the treatment of AD. Treatment was initiated in patients in the United States with the MCI or mild dementia stage of disease. BSC reflected management of AD before the advent of DMTs and included non-pharmacologic interventions and symptomatic medications. Disease progression was modeled as transitions between four living states (MCI due to AD [MCI], mild dementia due to AD [mild AD], moderate dementia due to AD [moderate AD], and severe dementia due to AD [severe AD]) and death (Fig. 1) [9]. The model also accounted for patients’ transitions from a community care to a long-term care (LTC) setting.

Model results represent a patient’s lifetime horizon. A model cycle length of 3 months was chosen to provide flexibility in modeling treatment monitoring and discontinuation. Costs and outcomes were discounted annually at 3% in accordance with recommended practice [10]. Co-base case results are presented for the US health care system and modified societal perspectives. The health care system perspective included treatment, administration, LTC, and patient direct medical costs and QALYs (Table 1). The modified societal perspective added direct medical costs for informal caregivers, productivity costs for patients and informal caregivers, and informal caregiver disutility. Health outcomes were calculated as life-years (LYs) and QALYs gained (Table 1).

The primary outcome of the model was the EJP (rather than a traditional ICER), defined as the maximum price at which donanemab would be cost-effective given a particular WTP threshold [20]. Compared to the ICER, the EJP is an intuitive way to interpret the annual estimated value brought by a novel health care intervention. Based on the value assessment framework used by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, the EJP was calculated at the $150,000 (base case) and $100,000 per QALY WTP thresholds [21]. As reviewed within the framework [21], these commonly cited thresholds are consistent with recent research, and the range allows for consideration of uncertainty and other factors. Notably, the recent health economics literature (e.g., [22]) suggests that this range may be conservative in the case of severe diseases such as AD. The EJPs were calculated and reported for a 1-year (13-dose) donanemab regimen as well as on a per-dose basis and an average per-patient basis.

Clinical Inputs

Model inputs are presented in Table 1. At baseline, the mean patient age was 73.0 years, and 57.4% of patients were female, as in the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study in which 860 patients were randomized to donanemab and 876 patients were randomized to placebo [7]. Patients were initially assigned as 55% MCI and 45% mild AD [23], with 92% of patients in the community care setting and 8% in the LTC setting [24]. Once a patient transitioned from the community to LTC, they remained there until death [12].

In the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study, patients were treated with donanemab every 4 weeks (Q4W) for up to 76 weeks (18 months) [7]. Efficacy was assessed using the iADRS [7]. Secondary measures included the CDR-SB, and a gated exploratory analysis evaluated risk of progression to the next clinical stage of disease using the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale–Global (CDR-G) [7]. The present model applied a 37% reduction in the risk of progression to later clinical stages of AD for patients treated with donanemab based on the CDR-G analysis in the combined population of individuals with low/medium tau load or high tau load assessed by flortaucipir PET scan in TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 [7]. This aligns with the modeling of efficacy in the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review model [11]. We used the efficacy estimate from the combined population rather than only the low/medium tau population because it represents a broader range of disease severity and because it was more conservative (37% reduction in risk of progression vs. 39% for the low/medium tau population) [7]. Although this population excludes those with no or very low tau pathology, safety and disease-relevant biomarker outcomes in these patients over 18 months with donanemab have been shown to be consistent with outcomes from TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 (e.g., similar safety profile, except for lower incidence of amyloid-related imaging abnormality of edema/effusions (ARIA-E) and ARIA-hemorrhage/hemosiderin deposition (ARIA-H); 89% of patients with no/very low tau achieving amyloid clearance < 24.1 CL within 1 year of treatment), supporting treatment regardless of the burden of tau pathology [25]. The model assumed no impact of donanemab on the small transition probabilities to states with less severe dementia due to AD under BSC (Table 2).

Donanemab was modeled as an infusion given Q4W (i.e., 13 times per year) [7] and administered for a maximum duration of 18 months or until patients stopped treatment due to an adverse event (AE), amyloid plaque clearance, or progression to severe AD. Maximum treatment duration was set at 18 months based on the available evidence from completed clinical trials, including the observed incidence of amyloid clearance below 24.1 Centiloids after 18 months (76.4% of patients in the combined population) [7].

Treatment-related amyloid-related imaging abnormality (ARIA) AEs are associated with amyloid-targeting therapies (ATTs) [26]. All cases of ARIA incurred costs, as described below, and symptomatic ARIA-E was assumed to impact the patient’s quality of life for 12 weeks [26], which aligns with clinical trial data for ATTs indicating that the typical ARIA resolution time ranges from 3 to 8 months [7, 27–30]. Symptomatic ARIA-H was not modeled because, in TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, the incidence of ARIA-H on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in the absence of ARIA-E was similar between treatment arms (12.7% in the donanemab group vs 12.4% in the placebo group). [7].

Based on TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, 13.1% of patients were modeled to discontinue donanemab due to AEs [7]. In TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, the most common reasons for discontinuation due to AEs were infusion-related reactions (3.6%) and ARIA-E (2.5%) [7]. Most ARIA-E events (58%) occurred after three or fewer doses of donanemab, and most infusion-related reactions occurred following infusions 2 through 5 [31]. Correspondingly, AE-related discontinuations were modeled to occur at 6 months (before the first scheduled PET scan to assess amyloid clearance)—a conservative assumption that likely overestimates the drug cost for these patients.

In TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, 29.7% and an additional 36.4% of patients in the combined population reached the predefined threshold for amyloid clearance on amyloid PET scans at 6 and 12 months, respectively [7, 32]. These patients were modeled as discontinuing donanemab at the time of the amyloid PET scan indicating amyloid plaque clearance, considering that this threshold is concordant with a negative amyloid PET scan on visual read, the current standard in clinical practice [33].

For patients who did not discontinue due to AEs, patients maintained the treatment benefit of slower clinical progression of AD with donanemab. This was based on the clinical trial protocols which treated patients for up to 18 months or until amyloid clearance was achieved, irrespective of clinical progression of disease, and data from clinical trials which demonstrate that donanemab modifies the course of AD, decelerating progression. For patients who discontinued due to AEs, treatment benefit was maintained for 18 months, as these patients were included in the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 reported efficacy outcomes [7]. After 18 months no treatment benefit was modeled for these patients.

Health Utility Inputs

Patient utility estimates stratified by AD severity and care setting were obtained from the published literature (Table 1) [12, 14]. A disutility of 0.14 was applied for 12 weeks for patients experiencing symptomatic ARIA-E, using headache (the most common symptom of ARIA-E) as a proxy [34]. The modified societal perspective additionally considered disutility experienced by informal caregivers (Table 1) which is one type of “family spillover” [12, 14, 15]. Unlike patient utility estimates, caregiver disutility remained constant when a patient transitioned from the community to the LTC setting.

Costs

All costs in the model were reported in 2023 US dollars (Table 1). Both the health care system perspective and the modified societal perspective included treatment administration costs, AE management costs, monitoring (amyloid PET scan and brain MRI) costs, patient AD state direct medical costs, and LTC costs. Resultant AE management, monitoring, and patient AD state direct medical costs are aggregated into the patient direct medical costs category. The modified societal perspective included patient and informal caregiver productivity costs and caregiver direct medical costs.

Donanemab is administered every 4 weeks (13 times annually) via intravenous infusion. In the model, each administration incurred a cost of $64.72 [19]. All patients on donanemab at 6 and 12 months incurred a $4664 diagnostic testing cost for an amyloid PET scan. Currently, an amyloid PET scan is the only biomarker test that can determine whether amyloid plaque is cleared [8].

For patients experiencing ARIA, regardless of the presence of symptoms, the model applied the cost of three brain MRIs (each costing $337.52): one every 4 weeks for 12 weeks [19]. In addition, all patients initiating donanemab incurred the cost of five brain MRIs during treatment (four in year 1, and one in year 2), as performed in the clinical trial.

As patients advanced across health states corresponding to later clinical stages of AD, direct medical costs increased. The direct medical cost multipliers for each health state were informed by the annual inpatient and outpatient direct medical costs stratified by AD severity [11, 16]. When patients transitioned to the LTC setting, they incurred LTC costs of $92,640 per year ($7720 per month) [17].

The analysis from the modified societal perspective considered the opportunity costs of reduced work productivity for employed patients and informal care provided by their loved ones, as well as the direct medical costs for informal caregivers, valued at the mean gross hourly wage for workers ($33.60) [15, 18, 35]. The percentage of patients with AD who report employment (MCI, 20.4%; mild AD, 11.1%) [18] and reduced work productivity were assumed to lose 20 hours of productive time per week. The time spent by loved ones providing informal care increased as patients advanced to later clinical stages of AD [18].

Scenario and Sensitivity Analyses

Along with the co-base cases, five additional health care system perspective scenarios were investigated. Three scenarios evaluated alternative treatment strategies using only one amyloid PET scan per patient to monitor for amyloid clearance while on treatment. This is consistent with a historical limit of one amyloid PET scan per lifetime set by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). While CMS removed this limit in a recent national coverage determination, it is unknown whether local Medicare Administrative Contractors will allow >1 amyloid PET scan per lifetime [36]. Two other scenarios evaluated patient populations beginning treatment in MCI due to AD stage (Scenario 4) and mild dementia due to AD (Scenario 5).

OWSAs were performed on a selected set of variables from the co-base case health care system perspective. OWSA variables included the reduction in risk of AD progression with donanemab; the initial population AD severity; the duration of donanemab efficacy; monthly LTC costs; and health state utility estimates. Base case values were modified by +10% and −10%.

Results

Results for the two co-base cases are shown in Table 3. From the health care system perspective, donanemab was estimated to result in an incremental gain of 0.683 total QALYs and 0.743 total LYs versus BSC. These outcomes were achieved at an incremental non-treatment cost of $25,740, which was attributable to increases in administration ($802), LTC ($9608), and patient direct medical costs ($15,330). These cost increases were due to additional health care costs associated with the modeled increase in life expectancy for patients receiving donanemab, along with donanemab-associated amyloid PET scan and brain MRI costs. The EJPs for donanemab at the $150,000 per QALY gained WTP threshold were estimated at $80,538 per year (13 doses), $6195 per dose, and $77,812 per treated patient. At the lower $100,000 WTP threshold, the donanemab 1-year (13-dose), per-dose, and per-treated-patient EJPs were $44,691, $3438, and $43,178, respectively.

From the modified societal perspective, clinical outcomes were identical to those estimated from the health care system perspective. Additional cost outcomes in this analysis included patient productivity costs ($300, due to productive hours lost), which were offset by reductions in the informal caregiver direct medical costs (−$374) and productivity costs (−$10,420 due to productive hours gained). As a result, incremental non-treatment costs were reduced to $15,247 relative to the health care system co-base case value of $25,740. The inclusion of informal caregiver QALYs in the modified societal perspective led to a slight decrease in incremental QALYs by 0.003, attributable to additional caregiver time associated with the modeled increase in life expectancy for patients receiving donanemab. The EJPs for donanemab at the $150,000 per QALY gained WTP threshold were estimated as $91,126 per year (13 doses), $7010 per dose, and $88,042 per treated patient. At the lower $100,000 WTP threshold, the donanemab 1-year (13-dose), per-dose, and per-treated-patient EJPs were $55,419, $4263, and $53,544, respectively.

Scenario Analyses from the Health Care System Perspective

Results of scenario analyses are listed in Table 4, with detailed outcomes presented in the supplement. Scenarios evaluating alternative treatment strategies with a single amyloid PET scan to monitor for amyloid clearance while on treatment suggest that direct medical costs saved through the use of a single PET scan to monitor for amyloid clearance are offset by an increase in donanemab treatment and administration costs. In these scenarios, slight reductions in the 1-year (13-dose) EJP for donanemab were observed relative to the co-base case analysis from the health care system perspective (Scenario 1, $75,400; Scenario 2, $77,450; Scenario 3, $69,145). Scenarios evaluating treatment initiation in the MCI versus mild AD stages suggest that earlier treatment initiation may yield greater improvement in incremental LYs (MCI, 0.846; mild AD, 0.616) and QALYs (MCI, 0.782; mild AD, 0.563) than BSC. Correspondingly, the value of donanemab was greater with treatment initiation in patients with MCI, as demonstrated by the greater 1-year (13-dose) EJP at the $150,000 WTP threshold (MCI, $93,038; mild AD, $64,792).

Discussion

Based on these results, treatment with donanemab Q4W is expected to deliver an additional 0.683 total QALYs and 0.743 total LYs relative to BSC. These benefits support a 1-year (13-dose) EJP for donanemab of $80,538 ($6195 per dose) at a WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY from the health care system perspective. From the modified societal perspective, the 1-year (13-dose) EJP for donanemab was $91,126 at the $150,000 per QALY WTP threshold. Because valuation for caregiver work productivity losses is not included in the model, only valuation of time spent providing informal care, the model underestimates the true societal impact of AD and therefore likely underestimates the EJP from the societal perspective. OWSAs from the health care system perspective showed that the EJP was most sensitive to the efficacy of donanemab in reducing risk of progression, the clinical stage of disease at treatment initiation, and the assumed duration of efficacy of donanemab.

In scenario analyses, the EJP for donanemab was found to increase when treatment was limited to patients with MCI due to AD compared to a mixed population consisting of patients with MCI due to AD and mild dementia due to AD and to decrease when treatment was limited to patients with mild dementia due to AD. Other scenario analyses evaluated the impact of alternative time points for monitoring amyloid levels in the brain and discontinuing treatment. Such a “test for treatment completion” strategy is a promising approach for reducing treatment costs and treatment burden on patients. For donanemab, this strategy is supported by clinical and biomarker responses observed in recent clinical trials that suggest that some ATTs produce disease-modifying benefits that are likely to persist beyond the period of treatment [37].

As noted above, how Medicare Advantage Contractors will administer benefits for PET scans now that CMS has removed the lifetime limit [36] makes it unclear what to expect from health care providers (HCPs) with respect to when amyloid PET might be used because the timing might depend on how many amyloid PET scans are ultimately covered. For example, if multiple amyloid PET scans are covered, HCPs may decide to use amyloid PET before treatment to establish the existence of amyloid plaques as well as during treatment to track amyloid clearance. If, however, only one amyloid PET scan is covered, HCPs may opt to only use amyloid PET scans before treatment or, if accurate assays of cerebrospinal fluid or plasma are available to be used to establish baseline amyloid pathology, HCPs may opt to only use amyloid PET scans to track amyloid clearance. It is also possible that HCPs may opt to follow an 18-month fixed treatment duration with no amyloid testing. While we did not model this scenario here, prior work by Boustani et al. [8] showed that such a strategy approached cost-effectiveness at a WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained for a hypothetical drug that reduced progression by 30% available at a 1-year price of $56,000 (ICER of $157,288 per QALY gained) [8]. Notably, the reduction in risk of progression for the hypothetical treatment in the prior model (30%) is less than what was observed for donanemab in the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial (37%) [7, 8]. Relatedly, tau burden is not a requirement for treatment with donanemab, an amyloid plaque-specific monoclonal antibody [38]. In TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, flortaucipir PET scan criteria were utilized to ensure treatment groups were well balanced and to enroll patients likely to have measurable clinical deterioration during the 18-month study period in which donanemab was assessed; the criteria were not intended to describe the potentially treatable patient population [25, 37]. As noted above, robust amyloid clearance results after treatment with donanemab have been shown across levels of tau burden, including in subjects with no or very low tau burden [25]. Further, the limited availability of flortaucipir imaging in clinical practice makes it an unfeasible patient selection criterion, as noted in the FDA Advisory Committee meeting regarding donanemab in June 2024 and consistent with the prescribing information for donanemab [38, 39].

This study expands on the work of Boustani et al. [8] by evaluating the potential cost-effectiveness of a limited-duration treatment using real clinical trial data. In the present model, the treatment effect is assumed to continue as patients progress to more advanced clinical stages of AD. This assumption is supported by results from the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study, in which patients continued treatment for up to 18 months or until amyloid clearance was achieved, irrespective of clinical progression of disease, and which demonstrated increasing differences between treatment groups on least-squares mean change from baseline in iADRS and CDR-SB over the 76-week trial duration, consistent with disease modification [7, 40]. Further, post hoc analyses showed that disease modification continued among patients who reached amyloid clearance and stopped donanemab at 24 or 52 weeks [37]. Based on data from the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ study (NCT03367403), amyloid is predicted to re-accumulate slowly. An estimated 4 years is needed for amyloid to re-accumulate from 11 CL to the threshold concordant with a positive amyloid PET scan (24.1 CL) [41] and 14 years to re-accumulate to pre-treatment levels [42].

Given that treatment duration with donanemab (limited-duration treatment of up to 18 months) may differ from treatment with aducanumab or lecanemab (treatment until progression to a certain clinical stage of AD), it is our view that the best way to compare costs for these treatments is by estimating total cost of care per patient over the treatment course. Currently, lecanemab is available at a cost of $26,500 per year in the United States, with an estimated total cost per patient for the drug alone, based on data from the phase 3 Clarity AD trial, of $95,400 over 3.6 years [43]. In contrast, our model results estimate a mean total per-patient treatment cost for the drug alone of $77,812 if the cost of donanemab was consistent with the 13-dose EJP of $80,538 at the WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained from the health care system perspective.

The results of the present analysis, the first to estimate the value of donanemab to the health care system and society using outcomes from the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial, indicate a greater value than previous evaluations by the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio [44] and Ross et al. [45]. In both of these prior analyses, the greatest driver of value for donanemab was the extent to which donanemab slowed disease progression, and the rate of slowing observed in TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 and applied here is greater than in both of these prior models. Further, in our model, dosing is stopped at 18 months if not stopped earlier for other reasons. In the Institute for Clinical Economic Review and Ross models, dosing continues indefinitely if patients do not reach amyloid clearance criteria, do not advance to severe disease, or do not discontinue due to AEs. Ross et al. note that the limited-duration nature of donanemab is a key driver of cost-effectiveness. Our model also differed from that of Ross et al. by using transition probabilities derived from an amyloid-positive cohort, which indicate faster progression of disease versus non-amyloid-positive cohorts, and by not including screening costs. In both of these respects, we followed the model by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review [11] and another model by the International PharmacoEconomic Collaboration on Alzheimer's Disease [46].

Notably, the recent introduction of the Generalized Risk-Adjusted Cost-effectiveness (GRACE) framework [22, 47] has challenged the application of WTP thresholds of $100,000 or $150,000 per QALY gained to determine cost-effectiveness for interventions regardless of the characteristics of the disease they treat. Unlike conventional cost-effectiveness analysis, the GRACE framework accounts for the idea that society should reward innovations that produce comparable health benefits for patients with severe diseases (like AD) over those for more mild diseases (like peptic ulcer disease) [22, 47]. Under this framework, interventions for severe conditions like AD could have an estimated WTP threshold of up to five times annual per capita non-health consumption (i.e., $250,000 to $400,000) [22, 48, 49]. At these higher WTP thresholds, the EJP of donanemab would be greater than reported in this analysis. GRACE is a formal and more rigorous implementation of two key elements (i.e., insurance value and the value of hope) of the broad concept of value embodied in the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research “value flower” [50, 51], which encourages the inclusion of these and other impacts that are underappreciated in conventional cost-effectiveness analysis (e.g., real option value, the value of knowing, and scientific spillovers).

Drug pricing should not only be based on cost-effectiveness, however, as aspects such as the unmet need, the population size and budget impact, and the need for health care system resources to be available to improve how AD patients are currently identified, diagnosed, and managed must also be considered. The Institute for Clinical and Economic Review noted similar considerations in its recent evaluation of lecanemab in the treatment of AD: the acuity of need for effective treatments due to the high burden of AD on patients, caregivers, and society, and the substantial impact on the patient after AD is manifest [11]. Factors like these are not readily adaptable to conventional cost-effectiveness analysis [11].

CMS has recently announced the Guiding and Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) payment model for dementia care, including dementia due to AD [52]. The overall goal of GUIDE is to improve quality of life for individuals with dementia and their caregivers and to keep individuals with dementia living at home and participating in their communities [52]. GUIDE seeks to achieve these goals through a focus on coordinated care across an interdisciplinary team for both the patient and the caregiver and by providing respite services for the caregiver [52]. DMTs such as donanemab may serve as a key element in achieving these goals: by decreasing the progression of AD, DMTs may help prolong the period during which people with AD can live at home and maintain their independence while doing so, reducing strain on caregivers. The GUIDE model is still being implemented and may be considered in future economic models of treatment for AD.

The results of this model must be interpreted with an understanding of several limitations. First, several aspects of the design and inputs to the model are based on assumptions, including timing of discontinuation due to AEs and follow-up care for ARIA. While the model included costs and disutilities for asymptomatic and symptomatic ARIA, it did not fully consider the additional costs, disutilities, and mortality associated with serious ARIA. While the impact of serious ARIA at the individual patient level is significant, its infrequent occurrence (1.6% of patients in TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2) is expected to have a relatively small impact on the EJP. This model also does not consider costs of screening patients for amyloid status at the start of therapy or long-term monitoring for amyloid re-accumulation. The decision problem for the model, consistent with the models by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review [11] and by the International PharmacoEconomic Collaboration on Alzheimer's Disease [46], was limited to comparisons versus BSC for treatment of AD, and discussion of the cost-effectiveness of various strategies for diagnosing patients with AD is beyond the scope of the paper. Additionally, modeling indicates slow re-accumulation of amyloid (approximately 4 years to reach 24.1 CL [41] and approximately 14 years to reach pre-treatment levels [42]), suggesting amyloid monitoring may be infrequent or unnecessary. Evidence from the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 long-term extension study and real-world observations will further inform our understanding of amyloid re-accumulation and the potential role for post-treatment amyloid monitoring.

Another limitation of the model is that it relies on clinical trial data rather than real-world evidence. Clinical trial participants are often healthier and more compliant with medication than individuals in real-world settings, so it is possible that the effectiveness of donanemab observed in real-world use will differ from what was observed in clinical trials, adversely affecting the EJP. The TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial population was predominantly white (> 90%; see detailed demographic information in the supplement) [7], and data from the Alzheimer’s Association indicates that individuals of Hispanic or non-Hispanic Black descent develop AD at a disproportionate rate, with these individuals 1.5 to 2 times as likely to develop AD as white individuals [1]. This leaves open the possibility that the results of the model will not generalize to the observed US AD population, though there was no significant difference in plaque reduction, other clinical outcomes, or the pharmacokinetics of donanemab based on race [25]. Finally, the analysis was limited to the US context. Future models should expand into other localities to assess the generalizability of these results.

The results and limitations of this model suggest several opportunities for future research. First, the model mirrors the approach taken by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review [11], however, future research could improve the robustness of the assumptions and analyses for ATTs in such models. Long-term efficacy and safety data, especially concerning assumptions around continued treatment benefit (for patients who stop treatment due to fixed duration, amyloid clearance, or AEs) and AD-related institutionalization and mortality, will eventually be available and will provide additional certainty about the cost-effectiveness of donanemab [7]. Additionally, the impact of ATTs on patients and caregivers is nuanced. On the one hand, by considering only one caregiver, the model may underestimate the value of donanemab for patients who have multiple caregivers. On the other hand, caregiver outcomes may differ according to the caregiver’s relationship to the patient and the patient’s care setting [53, 54], and additional caregiver impacts could be counterbalanced by the additional time burden to receive donanemab and associated monitoring, which was not considered. Addressing these evidence gaps can improve future health economic evaluation conducted from the societal perspective by incorporating these important impacts. Looking further into the future, advances in fluid diagnostics for monitoring treatment [13] could reduce treatment-related costs, further improving the cost-effectiveness of limited-duration treatments.

Conclusion

Donanemab has demonstrated a disease-modifying effect in early symptomatic AD in clinical trials, with a 37% reduction in risk of progression to later stages of disease [7]. The present modeling work indicates a potential EJP for donanemab under limited-duration dosing of $44,691 to $80,538 for 1 year (13 doses) at a WTP threshold of $100,000 to $150,000 per QALY gained from the health care system perspective and an EJP of $55,419 to $91,126 at a WTP threshold of $100,000 to $150,000 per QALY gained from the societal perspective. Treatment efficacy, disease severity at time of treatment initiation, and duration of treatment effect were the main drivers of the potential EJP. The results of this modeling study incorporating clinical data observed for donanemab in the treatment of early symptomatic AD in the phase 3 TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study indicate a potential EJP for limited-duration treatment with donanemab that exceeds per-dose list prices for currently available ATTs, implying potentially lower lifetime costs and better value for money.
