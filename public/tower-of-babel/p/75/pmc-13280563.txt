From Pharmacodynamic Biomarker to Evaluating Treatment Response: Biomarkers in Primary Mitochondrial Diseases
Authors: Sydney Stern, Karryn Crisamore, John Patton, Robert Schuck, Michael Pacanowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrimary mitochondrial diseases (PMDs) result from genetic variants in nuclear DNA and mitochondrial DNA which commonly lead to aberrant oxidative phosphorylation. The clinical complexity, often attributed to the underlying genetics, includes several distinct syndromes (e.g., Barth syndrome; Pearson syndrome; Mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis and stroke‐like episodes), some with overlapping symptoms. PMDs are highly heterogenous and affect multiple organs and tissues, prominently those with high energy demand such as muscle and neurologic tissues. Disease‐modifying therapies for PMDs approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration are few and disease‐specific, and treatment remains largely supportive in nature. The lack of robust biomarkers contributes to challenges associated with quantifying treatment responses in drug development. Recognizing this area of critical need, we sought to understand the landscape of molecular biomarkers that may inform treatment response, support clinical trials, and may be useful for regulatory decision‐making. In this review, we assess the extent of evidence and challenges for each biomarker. We propose considerations for future biomarker development to measure treatment response and facilitate early drug development in PMDs by guiding dose selection and trial enrichment.

Introduction

Primary mitochondrial diseases (PMDs) are genetic disorders characterized by defects in metabolic pathways contained in the mitochondrion, most commonly in oxidative phosphorylation, with downstream impairment of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production in muscle tissue [1]. Although rare individually, collectively PMDs are the most common inherited metabolic disorder and are a leading cause of inherited neurological disorders [2, 3]. PMDs may affect multiple organs, with or without skeletal muscle involvement; primary mitochondrial myopathies (PMMs) are the largest class of PMDs with myopathy as the defining characteristic.

The profound complexity of PMDs is often attributed to the underlying genetics. Variants in nuclear DNA (nDNA) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) genes that encode mitochondrial structural or functional proteins are the primary cause of PMDs. Among multiple classification systems, PMDs can be classified into groups according to genetics, including (1) mtDNA variants, (2) nDNA variants affecting the mitochondrial respiratory chain, (3) nDNA variants causing mtDNA deletions or depletion, leading to disruption in mtDNA homeostasis [4], and (4) nDNA variants impacting the mitochondrial membrane. Additional classification considers the multicopy nature of mtDNA and hetero‐ or homoplasmy, depending on the presence of variant and wild‐type mtDNA in the same cell. Deletions in mtDNA are more detectable in muscle and urine than other matrices, such as blood, oral swabs, and fibroblasts [1]. Disease manifestations occur when variant mtDNA exceeds a critical threshold impairing oxidative phosphorylation, and is most notable in muscle cells [5] (Figure 1).

PMDs are comprised of several syndromes with distinct and overlapping signs and symptoms including Barth syndrome, progressive external ophthalmoplegia (PEO), Kearns‐Sayre syndrome (KSS), Pearson syndrome (PS), Mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis and stroke‐like episodes (MELAS), Myoclonus epilepsy with ragged‐red fibers (MERRF), Leigh syndrome spectrum that includes nuclear and mitochondrial causes, Thymidine Kinase 2 deficiency (TK2d), and Neuropathy, ataxia, and retinitis pigmentosa (NARP) [4, 6, 7]. PMDs are highly heterogeneous and multi‐systemic, prominently affecting tissues with high energy demand such as muscle and neurologic tissues and frequently affecting multiple tissue types. The presentation of mitochondrial disease in individual patients is variable and depends on patient‐specific characteristics [8].

Specific or disease‐modifying treatment for certain PMDs have been approved in the United States. Elamipretide was granted accelerated approval for the treatment of Barth syndrome [9], and doxecitine and doxribtimine were granted traditional approval for the treatment of TK2d [10]. Apart from these recent approvals, the mainstay treatment for PMDs is largely focused on symptom management such as medications to control or prevent seizures, physical therapy to maintain muscle strength and function, nutritional management, and immunizations to prevent infections. Investigational agents are under development and most aim to improve the efficiency of ATP production. Disease‐modifying approaches (e.g., gene therapy) are also being explored. Several clinical trials have investigated the treatment of elamipretide in various PMMs (e.g., https://clinicaltrials.gov/ NCT02976038, NCT04689360, NCT5162768, NCT02805790, NCT03323749, NCT02367014, NCT02693119). Additional investigational agents are under development for the family of PMDs (https://clinicaltrials.gov/ NCT05650229 and NCT05972954).

Measuring treatment responses in clinical trials across various PMDs is challenging due to the heterogeneity of PMDs, the effort‐dependent nature of efficacy endpoints, and the subjectiveness of patient or clinician rating scales (e.g., 6‐min walk test, neurological assessment, and mitochondrial rating scales). Biomarker development to date has largely centered on diagnosis, with a limited focus on treatment response biomarkers. The discovery and characterization of biomarkers that quantify treatment response may accelerate drug development in these rare diseases. Biomarkers may be useful to overcome intrinsic challenges associated with clinical trial designs in small populations that are both genetically and clinically heterogenous and may facilitate early drug development by guiding dose selection and trial enrichment. For example, biomarkers can be used to define trial eligibility and enrich the study population, support subgroup analyses that inform which patients are most likely to respond to treatment, or evaluate exposure‐response relationships to optimize dose.

Molecular Biomarkers

Knowledge of a biomarker's relationship to disease mechanisms and clinical outcomes is pivotal in the interpretation of a treatment effect to support its use as a pharmacodynamic or surrogate endpoint biomarker in clinical trials. Due to the heterogeneous clinical presentation and lack of well‐characterized downstream pathophysiology (e.g., dysfunction of the Krebs cycle, folate cycle, lack of enzyme intermediates or accumulating toxic metabolites) [2], biomarkers under development may have an unclear relationship with or are distal from known disease mechanisms. Conventional biomarkers (e.g., plasma lactate, lateral ventricular lactate, lactate/pyruvate ratio) are measured as part of diagnostic workup, but their utility as treatment response biomarkers has not been fully elucidated. Thus, we sought to examine the landscape of molecular biomarkers for PMDs in published literature to assess their utility in informing clinical trial design and provide considerations for future biomarker development.

Fibroblast Growth Factor 21

Background and Cross‐Sectional Characterization

Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF‐21) regulates cell growth, differentiation, embryonic development, tissue repair, and angiogenesis [34]. FGF‐21 is distinct in its family of 23 fibroblast growth factors because its weak or absent heparin‐binding affinity allows it to escape local tissue retention and remain in systemic circulation [35, 36]. FGF‐21 signaling may modulate mitochondrial biogenesis and function in response to impaired oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial deprivation [5]. Mitochondrial oxidative capacity may be enhanced by FGF‐21 through promotion of glucose uptake, as occurs in skeletal muscles during exercise and in the setting of mitochondrial dysfunction [37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. While skeletal muscles are not the only source of FGF‐21, it is conceivable that the degree of relevance of this biomarker or magnitude of change expected may depend on skeletal muscle involvement.

Studies have demonstrated consistent baseline elevation in serum FGF‐21 concentrations in patients with PMDs with myopathy compared to patients with non‐mitochondrial neuromuscular disorders and healthy controls [14]. Findings were observed in both adults and pediatrics with age‐matched controls and were not confounded by age [11, 12, 13]. One study found serum FGF‐21 concentrations had a strong positive correlation with age, critical illness, and multisystem involvement, but no correlation with the type of PMM (e.g., MELAS and Leigh syndrome did not differ in magnitude of elevation), mitochondrial defect involved, pathogenic variants, or organ system involvement [12]. However, another study suggested differences in serum FGF‐21 concentrations based the type of respiratory chain deficiency [14]. Clinical and murine studies corroborated that structural respiratory chain subunits or assembly factor defects may be associated with lower or normal serum FGF‐21 concentrations compared to mitochondrial translation defects or mtDNA deletions [14]. These findings suggest FGF‐21 may be a more sensitive biomarker in patients with MELAS and KSS compared to Leigh syndrome or Leber hereditary optic atrophy, as the latter are associated with structural or assembly defects in the respiratory chain complexes. Differences in FGF‐21 concentrations based on patients' genetic features was supported in another study [42]. In addition, serum FGF‐21 concentrations in PMMs have been correlated with muscle disease severity and respiratory chain deficient muscle fibers [43, 44].

Treatment Response

In an open label proof‐of‐concept study evaluating the effects of bezafibrate in patients with mitochondrial myopathy caused by the MTTL1 m.3243A>G variant, FGF‐21 concentrations were used as an exploratory response biomarker. At baseline, serum FGF‐21 concentrations were elevated [24]. At 6‐ and 12‐weeks post‐treatment, all participants had increased serum FGF‐21 concentrations in a dose‐dependent manner, regardless of sex, body mass index, body surface area, and diabetes mellitus status [24]. The authors suggest that dose‐dependent increases in serum FGF‐21 concentrations may reflect exacerbation of the mitochondrial pathology [24]. Treatment with bezafibrate did not improve exercise testing parameters. In another open‐label study evaluating FGF‐21 concentration response after treatment with niacin, serum FGF‐21 concentrations did not change, though mitochondrial biogenesis increased and muscle strength improved in some muscle groups [31].

Summary and Future Considerations

Consistent baseline FGF‐21 elevations have been observed in PMDs. However, FGF‐21 elevations also occurred in other diseases and may be related to insulin resistance [13]. Data on FGF‐21 as a potential treatment response biomarker are limited and decreased concentrations accompanying clinical benefit have not been reported to date. Additional research is warranted to uncover whether serum FGF‐21 is a sensitive biomarker for patients with muscle manifestations (e.g., external ophthalmalgia), its utility in patients without muscle involvement, and with consideration to genetic populations studied (e.g., given patients with mitochondrial translation defects had elevated serum FGF‐21 concentrations).

Growth Differentiation Factor 15

Background and Cross‐Sectional Characterization

Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF‐15) is a member of the transforming growth factor beta family that is induced by the mitochondrial integrated stress response and secreted into systemic circulation [45, 46]. GDF‐15 and FGF‐21 have overlapping functionalities, particularly in energy metabolism and insulin sensitivity; FGF‐21 expression is also stimulated by key signaling pathways of the mitochondrial stress response. It is noteworthy that GDF‐15 elevation also occurs following exercise and may be considered non‐pathologic in this setting [47]. For example, plasma GDF‐15 concentrations increased by 34% with exercise and by 64% above resting value 120 min post‐workout in healthy males [48], though concentrations remained ~10‐fold lower than baseline values observed in patients with PMDs without exercise.

Studies have shown consistent elevation of serum GDF‐15 concentrations in patients with PMDs with and without muscle myopathy [43, 49, 50]. Baseline elevation in blood GDF‐15 concentrations in patients with PMDs compared to patients with non‐mitochondrial disorders and healthy controls has been observed in studies including adults and pediatrics [15, 16, 17]. A study in patients with PMMs found that plasma GDF‐15 concentrations were highest in patients with progressive myopathy phenotypes and lowest in patients with exercise intolerance [17]. Serum GDF‐15 concentrations have also distinguished patients with PMM from other myopathies or chronic fatigue syndromes, particularly those with respiratory chain dysfunction rather than general metabolic dysfunction or muscle defects [51, 52]. Serum GDF‐15 concentrations also have been positively correlated with serum FGF‐21 concentrations and disease severity [16].

Observational studies have attempted to link GDF‐15 concentrations and functional outcomes measures. Plasma GDF‐15 concentrations had a moderate negative correlation with motor performance as measured by the 6‐min walk test in patients with progressive myopathy [17]. Plasma GDF‐15 concentrations also had moderate positive correlations with lactate levels and mtDNA mutation load in muscles. No significant correlations between GDF‐15 concentrations and age of onset or duration of disease were found [17].

Treatment Response

Serum GDF‐15 concentrations reflected disease course and treatment response in a longitudinal study of patients with TK2d treated with deoxynucleosides [25]. In patients with TK2d, baseline serum GDF‐15 concentrations were associated with age, disease severity as measured by a 6‐min walk test, and body mass index. A significant reduction in serum GDF‐15 concentrations and a significant improvement in clinical endpoints were observed following treatment [25]. The decline in serum GDF‐15 concentrations was most pronounced in children with the greatest clinical benefit in the most severely affected patients [25].

In adults with PMDs, treatment with sodium pyruvate in a prospective exploratory study led to a significant reduction in serum GDF‐15 over the treatment period which was observed in conjunction with a reduction of other conventional biomarkers (e.g., plasma lactate, lateral ventricular lactate, lactate/pyruvate ratio) [26]. The reduction in biomarker concentrations trended with improvement in clinical endpoints such as Newcastle Mitochondrial Disease Adult Scale, a general clinical tool used to assess the severity and progression of PMDs in adults. However, the improvements in clinical rating scales did not reach statistical significance [26]. In other studies which evaluated bezafibrate, resveratrol, and niacin treatments in adults with PMDs, blood GDF‐15 concentrations either increased or had no change in response to treatment [24, 27, 31].

Summary and Future Considerations

GDF‐15 is a biomarker consistently altered in patients with PMDs, particularly those with progressive myopathy, severe disease manifestations, and in pediatric patients. In contrast to FGF‐21, GDF‐15 may be elevated regardless of muscle involvement. Preliminary assessment suggests that serum GDF‐15 concentrations may correlate with clinical endpoints such as motor performance or severity of mitochondrial disease. As such, data support GDF‐15 as a potential biomarker for mitochondrial dysfunction to monitor PMD progression. Since biomarkers are often paired with effort‐dependent clinical endpoints, consideration of the impact of preceding activities (e.g., exercise) on measurements and their interpretation is warranted. The use of GDF‐15 to distinguish patients with various diseases and in different potential contexts‐of‐use (e.g., disease severity monitoring, treatment response) underlines the importance of pre‐specified designs for appropriate therapeutic interventions and clinical trial designs. GDF‐15 requires additional optimization before applying in clinical trials as a response biomarker, including assessment of its stability over time, optimal (sub)population, sensitivity to change with a drug treatment, and magnitude of change that represents meaningful clinical benefit.

Mitochondrial Respiratory Chain Enzyme Activity and Acylcarnitines

Background and Cross‐Sectional Characterization

Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is orchestrated by five enzyme complexes, of which four complexes and two electron carriers form the mitochondrial respiratory chain [53]. Proteins for complexes involved in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation are encoded by mtDNA and nDNA [54]. Disease‐causing genetic variants can directly affect proteins involved in the mitochondrial respiratory chain and result in energy production deficiencies. Measurement of mitochondrial respiratory chain activities may therefore serve as a measure of the disease depending on the genetic variant present. Mitochondrial respiratory chain deficiency can impair other pathways like β‐oxidation [55]. Variants affecting other pathways also can have a secondary impact on oxidative phosphorylation and other cellular functions including impairment in mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation [53]. Acylcarnitines are critical components of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation and are generally considered biomarkers of β‐oxidation.

A study showed that most patients with mitochondrial myopathies caused by genetic variants in mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes had elevated plasma concentrations of hydroxylated long chain acylcarnitines compared to reference values [18]. A hydroxylated long‐chain acylcarnitine, 3‐hydroxytetradecanoyl carnitine (C14:0‐OH), had a moderate correlation with muscle mutation load in patients with mt‐tRNA variants and single large‐scale deletions of mtDNA. All six patients in the cohort with respiratory chain enzyme activity < 20% of normal mean activity had elevated concentrations of at least one hydroxylated long chain acylcarnitine species [18].

Treatment Response

One study found that the activity of the respiratory chain through complex II‐V was significantly reduced in lymphocytes of patients undergoing cofactor supplementation [32]. Several patients reported improvements in energy level and exercise tolerance while others reported no clinical worsening. In an open‐label proof‐of‐concept study evaluating bezafibrate treatment, respiratory chain enzyme (complexes I through V) and citrate synthase activities in skeletal muscle via needle biopsy were measured at baseline and 12 weeks after treatment [24]. No changes were observed with notable interpatient variability in all enzymes measured. Several acylcarnitines showed decreases with treatment which reflected increases in β‐oxidation. No improvements in clinical measures were observed except for improvement in parameters of cardiac function [24]. In a separate open‐label study of bezafibrate treatment, clinical improvement was observed but high variability in plasma acylcarnitine concentrations, potentially due to differences in nutritional status, precluded meaningful interpretation for the impact of treatment [28].

Mitochondrial respiratory chain activities were measured in a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study of resveratrol, which is proposed to promote mitochondrial function by increasing fatty acid oxidation, stimulating mitogenesis, and protecting against reactive oxygen species [27]. No significant differences between treatment and placebo were found in primary outcomes (i.e., change in heart rate during submaximal exercise and peak oxygen utilization during maximal exercise) or in respiratory chain activities measured in peripheral blood mononuclear cells [27]. As with bezafibrate treatment [24], the lack of observed changes in respiratory chain enzyme activities is consistent with the lack of improvement in clinical function, but it is not possible to distinguish findings as lack of biomarker utility versus ineffective treatment.

Summary and Future Considerations

Insufficient data exist to determine the utility of mitochondrial respiratory chain enzyme activities as a treatment response biomarker. Mitochondrial respiratory chain enzyme activities are an appealing biomarker given the proximity to disease etiology, while value as a potential treatment response biomarker would need to consider a drug's mechanism of action. Activities have been measured in skeletal muscle using a muscle biopsy, which is limited by its invasive nature and uncertainties about the biopsy's reflection of the overall tissue. Acylcarnitines have been investigated in the setting of baseline concentrations in patients with mitochondrial myopathies and in response to a treatment. Acylcarnitine concentrations can be influenced by different factors including nutritional status and sampling route, as urinary concentrations are more consistent and plasma concentrations do not accurately reflect acylcarnitine metabolism in tissues [18].

Lactate

Background and Cross‐Sectional Characterization

Lactate is commonly measured in blood or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to support the clinical diagnosis of PMDs, including PMMs [1, 56, 57]. Lactate is formed from pyruvate by lactate dehydrogenase. When oxidative phosphorylation is deficient, lactate and pyruvate accumulate [58, 59]; both may be measured and reported as a ratio [59]. Accumulation of lactate can lead to lactic acidosis, which is a hallmark of MELAS. Although recognized as a classic biomarker for PMD diagnosis, elevations in lactate are not specific as concentrations may be elevated in other conditions that affect glycolysis, lactate metabolism, or flux of pyruvate through the Krebs cycle (e.g., sepsis, cardiac failure, seizures, primary or secondary mitochondrial disorders) [19, 60].

To identify biomarkers for MELAS, metabolomic and proteomic analyses were performed with a focus on those which distinguish MELAS from controls and correlate with disease severity. Plasma lactate was significantly higher in patients with MELAS, had a positive correlation with brain lactate, and had a negative correlation with Karnofsky score [19]. However, correlation with disease severity was based on a single measurement (i.e., not longitudinal). In another study, serum lactate concentrations were elevated in about one‐third of patients with PMMs, but did not correlate with genotype, phenotype, or disease severity as measured by functional motor outcomes [17]. Serum lactate measurement timing in relation to motor outcomes was not specified.

Treatment Response

In a multicenter, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, dose‐ranging study, adult patients with PMMs were treated with placebo or omaveloxolone, which is proposed to act through activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2‐like 2 [29]. At Week 12, plasma lactate concentrations after submaximal exercise were significantly lower in patients treated with the highest dose of omaveloxolone compared to placebo and baseline. Trends of lower plasma lactate concentrations were also observed at an earlier timepoint. These findings showed consistency with a lower heart rate and were highlighted in the rationale to continue development of this drug despite no significant improvement in primary (i.e., change in peak workload during maximal exercise) and secondary (i.e., change in 6‐min walk test distance) outcomes at Week 12 [29].

In an open‐label study, adult patients with mitochondrial myopathies and healthy controls were treated with niacin, which is proposed to boost NAD+ [31]. Plasma lactate concentrations were measured at rest, during light and maximum exercise, and post‐exercise. Decreases in plasma lactate concentrations compared to baseline were observed at 4‐ and 10‐months post‐treatment and were coupled with improvements in some muscle strength and performance parameters [31]. In a prospective exploratory study in adults with PMDs, treatment with sodium pyruvate led to a significant reduction in plasma lactate over the treatment period, though improvements in clinical rating scales did not reach statistical significance [26].

In a double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, crossover study, patients with PMDs were treated with placebo or L‐carnitine, which is hypothesized to increase fatty acid influx and reduce lactate synthesis [30]. Plasma lactate concentrations were measured during the peak of an incremental cardiopulmonary exercise test and 5 min into the recovery period. No significant differences in lactate concentrations were observed and, similarly, no differences were found in metabolic, ventilator, or cardiovascular measurements [30]. In another randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled crossover study which evaluated resveratrol treatment, no changes in plasma lactate concentrations or treatment effect were observed [27].

Summary and Future Considerations

Plasma or CSF lactate is a biomarker used to inform PMD diagnosis and is mechanistically supported given deficiencies in oxidative phosphorylation. Clinical studies have evaluated plasma lactate concentrations before, during, and after exercise in pre‐ and post‐treatment settings for drugs with various proposed mechanisms of action in PMMs. Lactate concentrations generally trended with other measurements of exercise/muscle performance, but studies did not demonstrate improvements in clinical outcomes. It is worth noting that lactate has been measured during exercise, which introduces variability due to subjective effort‐dependent exertion. In general, the utility of lactate as a treatment response biomarker is hindered by its nonspecific nature. As with all biomarkers discussed, its utility may be greatest in a subset of PMDs. Given these limitations and in the context of a therapy's potential mechanism of action, consideration may be given to measuring lactate in combination with other biomarkers in response to treatment and to correlate with relevant clinical outcomes in future clinical studies.

Creatine and Creatinine

Background and Cross‐Sectional Characterization

Creatinine is a product of creatine degradation and is commonly measured in serum to characterize kidney function. The precursor compound creatine is stored in muscles and is converted by creatine kinase to serve as an energy source; this process is impacted by the balance of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and ATP demand [20]. Creatine is partially synthesized in the mitochondria and released into the plasma which may indicate mitochondrial dysfunction [61].

Plasma creatine concentrations were elevated in patients with respiratory chain deficiency with muscle involvement compared to healthy controls and excluding patients on a concomitant creatine supplement [20]. Metabolomic and proteomic analyses also showed increased plasma creatine concentrations compared to controls in discovery and validation cohorts [19]. Low serum creatinine concentrations were found in 76% of adults with PMMs compared to the normal reference range. Of these patients, creatine kinase activity was normal in 33% [17]. Serum creatinine concentrations were significantly correlated with measures of motor function (e.g., 100‐m run test) and forced vital capacity. The authors suggest restoration of serum creatinine concentrations has potential for monitoring treatment efficacy [17]. Timing of serum biomarker measurements in relation to functional assessments was not specified in this study and the longitudinal trajectory is unclear.

Treatment Response

In the randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study of resveratrol treatment, creatine and creatine kinase were measured as safety parameters and no changes were observed [27]. In the open‐label study of niacin, muscle creatine and creatinine concentrations increased to those of controls after 10 months of treatment [31]. Plasma creatine kinase was not significantly different from controls at baseline or after 4 months of treatment [31].

Summary and Future Considerations

Studies to date have largely focused on creatine and creatinine as biomarkers to distinguish patients with PMDs from controls and point to potential disease mechanisms to consider in biomarker development. Few studies examine these biomarkers in response to treatment. Kidney function and creatine intake (e.g., dietary, supplements) are important considerations for interpretation of creatinine and creatine concentrations as potential biomarkers for PMDs.

Neurofilament Light Chain

Background and Cross‐Sectional Characterization

Neurofilament light chain (NfL) is a subunit of neurofilaments with high expression in large caliber myelinated axons. In addition to its typical release in an age‐dependent manner, NfL concentrations rise from acute release into the CSF and plasma in response to neurological damage [62]. Plasma NfL concentrations have been evaluated as a biomarker across neurodegenerative diseases, including as a treatment response biomarker [63, 64, 65, 66].

Serum NfL concentrations were significantly higher for patients with PMDs and mtDNA sequence variants compared to mtDNA single deletions and for patients with versus without epilepsy [21]. Two patients with PMDs and multiple sampling timepoints had higher annual increases than the reported normal age‐related increases. Serum NfL concentrations increased as the degree of active neuronal damage increased according to clinical classifications by degree of neurologic involvement. The authors suggest that serum NfL concentrations may be a useful monitoring biomarker for patients with genetic variants which result in neurodegenerative disease (i.e., POLG, m.3243A>G variants) [21]. Another study found significantly lower serum NfL concentrations in patients with an mtDNA single deletion resulting in chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia compared to those with additional clinical features [22]. Overall, highest concentrations were observed in patients with the most severe neurological involvement. In patients with MELAS, serum NfL was not correlated with mutation load in blood or urine [22].

Serum NfL has been evaluated in patients with MELAS specifically [23]. Patients were characterized as being in the acute phase of a stroke‐like episode (occurring within 2 weeks) and remission (6 months post‐episode with no new lesions or symptoms). Cognitive performance was measured using the Mini‐Mental State Examination, which coincided with serum NfL measurements. Serum NfL concentrations were significantly higher in patients with MELAS compared to healthy controls and concentrations were higher in the acute versus remission phase. In the subset of patients able to complete the cognitive assessment, a significant negative correlation was found with lower scores (i.e., higher cognitive impairment) having higher serum NfL concentrations. Serum NfL concentrations were positively correlated with stroke‐like lesion volume in the brain [23].

Summary and Future Considerations

Multiple studies have shown consistent findings that serum NfL concentrations are increased in the setting of neurological involvement in PMDs and that concentrations trend with neurological severity. Studies were limited in longitudinal assessments. No studies incorporated measurement of serum NfL concentrations in relation to treatment response. As a potentially easily measured and accessible biomarker, this presents an opportunity in future studies. Evidence suggests incorporation of serum NfL measurements in studies evaluating potential treatment options for patients with PMDs who have neurological involvement, in addition to measures of cognitive performance, may be useful. Individual patient characteristics including age, body mass index, and estimated glomerular filtration rate may contribute to a significant proportion of NfL concentration variance [67]. It may be important to consider these factors in result interpretation in future studies.

Adenosine Triphosphate

Background and Cross‐Sectional Characterization

One of the downstream consequences of mitochondrial dysfunction and defective mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in brain tissue is insufficient ATP generation [68]. Insufficient ATP generation leads to neuronal death and toxic byproducts such as reactive oxygen species that are produced along the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Given its role in disease pathophysiology, ATP production may serve as a potential biomarker in PMDs.

Treatment Response

In an open‐label study evaluating cofactor treatment, patients with PMDs had a lower baseline ATP synthesis capacity in peripheral lymphocytes compared to controls. ATP synthesis capacity increased after treatment; however, functional outcomes were not systematically measured [32]. Findings from healthy control lymphocytes showed improvements in ATP synthesis were attributed specifically to coenzyme Q10, while other studies have found paradoxical results [69, 70, 71, 72, 73].

A case report including two patients with MELAS demonstrated that CSF ATP concentrations were negatively correlated with disease progression [33]. In the patient treated with edaravone, increased CSF ATP concentrations coincided with decreased severity in consciousness disturbances and stroke‐like episodes. In the untreated patient, reduction in CSF ATP concentrations corresponded with exacerbation of brain lesions [33].

Summary and Future Considerations

Two studies have investigated ATP as a biomarker following treatment in PMDs. Both studies included patients with MELAS, though the collective sample size was only three patients. Stroke‐like lesions can develop in regions of the brain, often the parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes, which are linked to ATP depletion in MELAS [74]. Thus, reduced ATP may be, in part, the pathogenic driver of disease, making it a proximal biomarker to the disease pathogenesis [75]. CSF ATP concentrations appear relevant for other PMDs, though findings are limited to a single study. Further research is warranted to understand CSF ATP concentrations and synthetic capacity as a biomarker in other PMDs.

Alanine

Background

Alanine is a non‐essential amino acid produced from pyruvate by transamination, ultimately contributing to gluconeogenesis and energy production. Alanine is an indicator of long‐standing pyruvate accumulation and subsequent oxidative phosphorylation diseases. Elevated plasma alanine concentrations have been reported in patients with PMDs [76]. Plasma amino acid profiles have been used to differentiate PMDs from other metabolic diseases (e.g., pyruvate dehydrogenase complex deficiency). Varying sensitivity was found based on the pattern of abnormal amino acid concentrations. For instance, amino acid analysis identified relative alanine elevation by comparison with lysine, which exists in a < 3:1 alanine: lysine ratio in healthy individuals, or as an absolute elevation of alanine above 450 mM to support PMD diagnosis [76, 77]. However, normal alanine concentrations do not exclude presentation or progression of PMDs. Lactate or pyruvate levels may be elevated within the CSF without parallel elevation in blood, especially in those with neurologic manifestations [78]. Alanine may be sensitive to changes in pathophysiology based on a treatment and is included in amino acid analyses. However, alanine concentrations are influenced by a variety of factors, such as fasting and ketogenic diets, which may complicate interpretation.

Treatment Response

Alanine, along with GDF‐15 and FGF‐21, was evaluated in an open‐label study of niacin, which is proposed to improve muscle strength and fatty liver disease by restoring NAD+ in patients with PMDs [31]. Niacin led to 8‐fold increases in NAD+, significant decreases in plasma alanine concentrations along with other amino acids at 4 and 10 months, and significant increases in muscle alanine concentrations at 10 months. Decreased plasma alanine concentrations corresponded with improved muscle strength as measured by 6‐min walk test, repetitive shoulder muscle lift, elbow flexion, knee extension, abdominal and back muscle strength compared to baseline [31].

Summary and Future Considerations

While premature, amino acid analyses demonstrate the common elevation of alanine in blood samples that may correlate with disease severity [20, 26, 79, 80, 81]. It is plausible that modulation of alanine may be a sensitive treatment response biomarker. However, the magnitude of change that would reasonably predict a clinical benefit is unknown and the contribution to disease manifestation or severity is unclear. More research is warranted to understand how patterns in alanine and other amino acids differ in each of the PMDs.

Discussion

The heterogeneity in genetic etiology and the fluctuating, multi‐systemic nature of disease manifestations in PMDs make patient population and endpoint selection challenging in trials. Use of molecular biomarkers could be a promising approach to assess treatment response and support drug development in PMDs by enriching trial enrollment and optimizing dose selection [82]. Trial enrollment based on biomarker‐specific eligibility criteria or clinical presentation, as opposed to genotype, potentially could be pursued since PMDs generally have poor genotype–phenotype correlation. In addition, enriching trial populations to reduce heterogeneity may improve the ability to target a PMD phenotype or symptom with a specific, targeted drug mechanism. Alternatively, molecular biomarkers potentially could support response assessments in early‐phase basket trials across multiple PMDs with shared molecular etiology where disparate disease presentations might preclude evaluation in a single trial (e.g., dose‐finding studies).

Ideally, a treatment response biomarker would be in the causal pathway, sensitive to drug exposure, change rapidly, have low intrasubject variability, and reflect a clinically meaningful change in the disease process. However, in PMDs, numerous patient and context‐specific factors such as tissue/organ involvement, age of onset, disease severity, and sampling conditions (e.g., pre‐ or post‐effort dependent endpoint) can influence biomarker measurements. The context‐dependent sensitivity (e.g., exercise, nutritional status) and high intra‐subject variability can make longitudinal changes difficult to interpret and necessitates large effect sizes for adequate assessment of treatment response. Natural history studies and longitudinal real‐world data can support the evaluation of these biomarkers across different stages of PMDs and elucidate biomarker utility in predicting disease progression.

Use of biomarkers as clinical trial endpoints also requires careful consideration of potential sources of variability. For example, muscle involvement, proximity of sampling timepoints to exercise, and patient age can lead to considerable variability for biomarkers such as FGF‐21, lactate, and NfL, respectively. However, biomarkers such as GDF‐15 and NfL generally have minimal intra‐subject variability and are independent of muscle involvement. To overcome inter‐subject variability of biomarkers in a clinical trial, a potential strategy may include biomarker‐specific eligibility criteria with patients serving as their own run‐in controls in a randomized, controlled design. This assumes that the biomarker(s) correlate with disease severity and are directly related to clinical course, and that the drug's mechanism modulates the disease process.

Several biomarkers discussed (e.g., NfL) are not specific to the pathogenesis of any one PMD but may reflect broader disease mechanisms (e.g., neuroaxonal damage) and thus have the potential to inform treatment response across multiple PMDs. Biomarkers also may be used in combination to characterize specific organ involvement (e.g., NfL vs. FGF‐21) and distinguish genetic backgrounds (e.g., single mtDNA deletions). It is unlikely a single unified biomarker will account for the extensive clinical heterogeneity of PMDs; thus, a composite panel of biomarkers may be appealing in both pediatric and adult patients to examine different stages and facets of the disease with and without treatment.

The evidence needed to support biomarker usage in drug development varies based on the context of its use. Biomarkers or a composite of biomarkers may support evidence for initial dose selection, dose optimization, pharmacodynamics, or treatment response. Given the current state of evidence, further biomarker characterization is needed to support their use as surrogate endpoints for clinical efficacy. Use of various biomarkers (e.g., as a composite) may enable interpretation of treatment effects on clinical outcomes if the biomarker is on the disease's causal pathway, is relevant to the drug's mechanism of action, and changes correlate with disease severity.

The clinical relevance of biomarker changes is critical in biomarker evaluation for treatment response, especially if proposed for use as a surrogate endpoint to support drug approval. Biomarkers reviewed showed baseline alterations and several correlated with clinical symptom severity. Anchoring these molecular biomarkers to clinical endpoints can help determine what degree of biomarker change is associated with clinical benefit. Further research to understand how these biomarkers change over time, with different disease severity, and in response to treatment as related to clinically validated and age‐appropriate clinical outcome measurements will further strengthen the support for their use in clinical trials.

Molecular biomarkers in blood and CSF offer a potential for advancement in drug development for patients with PMDs. Biomarkers could guide trial design, dose optimization, and evaluation of target engagement, though currently none have met the threshold to serve as validated surrogate endpoints. Their utility is challenged by the genetic and clinical heterogeneity of PMDs, including the fluctuating disease course and variability within and between individuals. Meaningful use of biomarkers requires understanding their role in the pathophysiology of PMDs, anchoring biomarker changes to validated and age‐appropriate clinical outcomes, and longitudinal data to define clinically relevant thresholds and interpret treatment effects with confidence.
