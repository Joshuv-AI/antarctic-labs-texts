Risk factors for severe acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months enrolled in the CMAM program at Al-Sabeen Hospital, Sana’a City, Yemen
Authors: Ghaida’a Ibrahim Ahmed Hafedh, Nabil Ahmed Ahmed Al-Rabeei, Aisha Faisal Ahmed Al-Wadhaf, Lina Saeed Saif Al-Sofiani, Elham Haider Yusef Al-Jadi, Wail Ahmed Naji, Abdulsalam Saif Qasim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere acute malnutrition (SAM) remains a major public health problem among children under five years of age in Yemen. Evidence on factors associated with SAM in Sana’a City, particularly among children enrolled in community-based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) program is limited. The aim of this study is to assess factors associated with SAM among children aged 6–59 months at Al-Sabeen Hospital, Sana’a City, Yemen.MethodsA facility-based frequency-matched case–control study was conducted among 194 children aged 6–59 months (65 cases and 129 controls). Cases were children diagnosed with SAM and enrolled in the CMAM program, while controls were non-malnourished children attending routine services at the same hospital. Data were collected using caregiver interviews and medical record review. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Crude odds ratios(CORs) and adjusted odds ratios(AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. P-value < 0.05 was significant.ResultsSeveral factors were independently associated with higher odds of SAM. Children living in rural areas (AOR = 4.62; 95%CI: 2.03–10.51), those with low birth weight (LBW) (AOR = 3.48; 95%CI:1.67–7.26). Recurrent diarrhoea (AOR = 3.11; 95%CI: 1.51–6.41), anorexia (AOR = 8.71; 95%CI: 3.11–24.42), and intractable vomiting (AOR = 4.92; 95%CI: 2.03–11.91) had higher odds of SAM. Non-exclusive breastfeeding (AOR = 2.47; 95%CI: 1.21–5.05), low dairy intake (AOR = 2.95; 95%CI: 1.44–6.07), inadequate vegetable consumption (AOR = 2.44; 95%CI: 1.12–5.31), lack of caregiver handwashing after handling child feces (AOR = 5.86; 95%CI: 1.41–24.39), and incomplete pentavalent vaccination (AOR = 3.21; 95%CI: 1.44–7.14) were also independently associated with SAM.ConclusionsSAM among children in this setting was independently associated with rural residence, LBW, suboptimal feeding practices, poor hygiene practices, incomplete vaccination, and selected clinical conditions identified at the time of assessment. These findings highlight the need for integrated nutrition, health, and preventive interventions in high-burden settings.Keywords: Severe acute malnutrition, Children, Case–control study, Community-based management of acute malnutrition, Yemen

Introduction

Malnutrition encompasses deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in energy and nutrient intake, including undernutrition, overweight, obesity, and diet-related noncommunicable diseases [1]. In children, undernutrition manifests as wasting, stunting, underweight, and micronutrient deficiencies, with wasting reflecting acute malnutrition [1]. SAM is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a weight-for-height z-score (WHZ) below − 3, a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) less than 115 mm, or the presence of bilateral pitting oedema [2]. As the most severe form of wasting, SAM remains a major threat to child survival and development.

Children with SAM are highly vulnerable to infections and have a markedly increased risk of mortality compared with well-nourished children [3]. SAM also increases the severity and case fatality of common childhood illnesses, including diarrhoeal diseases and pneumonia, and may result in long-term consequences such as impaired cognitive development, poor educational attainment, and reduced economic productivity in adulthood [4].

The development of SAM is multifactorial. According to the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) conceptual framework, undernutrition results from the interaction of immediate causes (inadequate dietary intake and disease), underlying causes (household food insecurity, inadequate care and feeding practices, unhealthy household environments, and inadequate health services), and broader socioeconomic and political determinants [5]. Understanding these interconnected factors is essential for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Despite global progress in reducing child undernutrition, SAM remains a significant public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [6]. Yemen continues to experience one of the highest burdens of acute malnutrition worldwide as a result of prolonged conflict, economic instability, widespread poverty, food insecurity, population displacement, and disruptions to health services [7–9]. Recent studies indicate that more than 2.3 million children under five years of age in Yemen are affected by acute malnutrition, including hundreds of thousands with SAM requiring urgent treatment [8–10]. The most affected areas included Abyan governorate (23%), Lahj governorate (21%) [11]. These conditions have created a complex humanitarian context in which children face multiple nutritional and health risks.

Studies conducted in Yemen have documented the high burden of child malnutrition and its adverse consequences. Al-Zangabila et al. reported substantial levels of stunting, wasting, and underweight among children under five years of age and highlighted the influence of socioeconomic and household factors on nutritional status [12]. Vieira et al. further emphasized the contribution of malnutrition to child morbidity and mortality in Yemen’s humanitarian setting [10]. However, these studies primarily focused on the prevalence and overall burden of malnutrition and did not comprehensively examine factors specifically associated with SAM. Moreover, available evidence is limited by differences in study design, geographical coverage, and populations studied, making it difficult to identify context-specific determinants relevant to different regions of the country.

Studies from other low-resource settings have identified a range of factors associated with SAM, including recurrent childhood infections, inadequate infant and young child feeding practices, poor dietary diversity, food insecurity, inadequate sanitation and hygiene, LBW, and limited access to healthcare services [13–18]. Nevertheless, the relative importance of these factors may vary according to local socioeconomic, environmental, and health system conditions. Therefore, evidence generated from other settings may not be fully applicable to Yemen, particularly in areas affected by conflict and prolonged humanitarian crises.

Although Yemen faces a substantial burden of acute malnutrition, few studies have comprehensively examined factors associated with SAM among children under five years of age in Sana’a. Context-specific evidence is needed to better understand the clinical, behavioral, environmental, and socioeconomic factors associated with SAM and to inform nutrition and child health interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to assess factors associated with severe acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months attending Al-Sabeen Hospital in Sana’a City, Yemen.

Methods

Study design and setting

A facility-based frequency-matched case–control study was conducted to identify factors associated with SAM among children aged 6–59 months attending Al-Sabeen Hospital in Sana’a City, Yemen. Sana’a City had an estimated population of approximately 3,000,000 in 2021. During 2024, a total of 2,180 children with SAM were enrolled in the CMAM program in the Al-Sabeen district. The study was conducted between 1 June 2024 and 1 January 2025 at Al-Sabeen Hospital, a major referral facility providing both inpatient and outpatient child health services. Cases and controls were recruited during the same study period and originated from the same hospital catchment area.

Study population and sampling

The study included 194 children aged 6–59 months (65 cases and 129 controls). A convenience sampling approach with consecutive recruitment was used because all eligible participants presenting during the study period were accessible at the study site and could be enrolled within available logistical and resource constraints. All eligible cases and controls presenting during the data collection period were consecutively approached because no sampling frame existed until the required sample size was achieved.

Selection of cases

Cases were children aged 6–59 months diagnosed with SAM according to WHO criteria and newly admitted to the CMAM program at Al-Sabeen Hospital. SAM was defined as MUAC < 115 mm, WHZ < − 3 standard deviations (SD), and/or the presence of bilateral pitting oedema [1, 2]. Eligible children were recruited at admission or on the next available data collection day if admission occurred outside data collection hours. Anthropometric and clinical information for cases was obtained from CMAM admission records.

Selection of controls

Controls were facility-based children aged 6–59 months attending the same hospital during the same period for routine child health services, including growth monitoring, immunization, minor skin, eye, and ear conditions. Controls were recruited from outpatient services serving the same catchment population as the CMAM program. All potential controls underwent anthropometric screening by trained data collectors. Children were eligible as controls if they had MUAC ≥ 125 mm, WHZ ≥ − 2 SD, no bilateral pitting oedema [1, 2], no evidence of acute malnutrition, and no documented history of SAM.

Both groups were drawn from comparable and explicitly defined source populations. Cases and controls were frequency matched by sex at a ratio of 1:2 to improve comparability while preserving analytical flexibility.

The exclusion criteria were the SAM children who had already been enrolled in the CMAM program and were receiving follow-up treatment before recruitment. Children with known chronic illnesses or congenital abnormalities affecting feeding or growth, those aged < 6 months or > 59 months, and families unwilling to participate were excluded from both groups.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using Epi Info version 7.2.6.0. The calculation was based on the association between incomplete immunization and SAM, based on previous literature, with 5.7% exposure among controls and 18.5% among cases [13]. Odds ratio was (3.755) automatically populates based on the value entered. Using a two-sided 95% confidence level, a power of 80%, and a control-to-case ratio approximately of 2:1, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 65 cases and 129 controls, for a total of 194 children aged 6–59 months.

Data collection

Data were collected through caregiver interviews, review of medical records, and direct anthropometric assessment using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. Data collection was conducted from 1 June 2024 to 1 January 2025. Supervisors reviewed completed questionnaires daily to ensure completeness and accuracy. For cases, information was collected at the time of CMAM admission. Anthropometric and clinical information was obtained from CMAM admission records. For controls, anthropometric measurements were performed by trained data collectors using standardized procedures. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a UNICEF electronic scale, and length/height was measured to the nearest millimeter using a UNICEF portable measuring board. Bilateral pitting oedema was assessed by applying thumb pressure for 3 s and observing for persistent indentation.

The questionnaire was developed based on previous literature and the UNICEF conceptual framework [5, 13–16], translated from English into Arabic, back-translated to ensure consistency, and pretested on 10% of the sample size before implementation.

Information collected included child characteristics (age, sex, residence, birth order, and birth weight), parental characteristics (maternal age at childbirth, education, occupation, parity, and household income). Comorbidities (selected childhood illnesses) included recurrent diarrhoea, intractable vomiting, gastroenteritis, ARI, anorexia, anemia. To minimize reverse-causality concerns, illness-related variables were assessed as conditions occurring before the diagnosis of SAM among cases and during the two weeks preceding interview among controls. Information was obtained from caregiver reports and verified using medical records when available.

Recurrent diarrhoea was defined as three or more loose or watery stools per day occurring repeatedly during the reference period. intractable vomiting was defined as caregiver-reported child vomits after every oral intake. Acute respiratory infection (ARI) was defined as caregiver-reported cough accompanied by difficult or rapid breathing. Gastroenteritis was defined as diarrhoea with or without vomiting diagnosed by a healthcare provider or reported by the caregiver. Anemia was defined as a documented clinical diagnosis in the medical record or prior diagnosis reported by the caregiver. Anorexia was defined as a marked reduction or loss of appetite reported by the caregiver.

Dietary factors were included exclusive breastfeeding, feeding type, breastfeeding duration, timing of complementary, age at weaning, meal frequency, or consumption of grains and animal-source foods. Household and environmental factors were included family history of malnutrition, family size, number of under-five children, food availability, source of drinking water, latrine availability, and hand hygiene practices. Household food availability was assessed based on caregiver reports regarding the consistent availability of sufficient food for household consumption during the month preceding the interview. Handwashing practices were assessed according to four critical times: before food preparation, before eating, before feeding a child, and after handling child feces. Vaccination status included BCG, polio, pentavalent, pneumococcal, rotavirus, measles vaccination, and vitamin A supplementation. Vaccination status was verified using vaccination cards whenever available; otherwise, caregiver report and physical evidence (e.g., BCG scar) were used.

Data analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Associations between potential explanatory variables and SAM were initially assessed using bivariate logistic regression. CORs with 95% CIs were calculated. Variables demonstrating a bivariate association with SAM at p < 0.05 were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. A total of 27 variables were initially included in the multivariable logistic regression model. A backward stepwise elimination procedure was applied in the multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors of the outcome. Variables were sequentially removed according to statistical significance based on the highest p-value (> 0.05), and contribution to model fit, with the model re-estimated after each step. To reduce the risk of model overfitting, the final model was restricted to a limited number of predictors consistent with recommended events-per-variable considerations. Model fitness was tested for goodness of fit using Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. AORs with 95% CIs were reported for variables retained in the final model. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. Data completeness was assessed before analysis. No missing data were identified for variables included in the final analysis, resulting in a complete-case analysis of all 194 participants and a response rate of 100%. Multicollinearity between independent variables was checked by using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF values > 4 indicating potential concern. Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no significant collinearity among included variables (all VIF values < 4).

Results

Children socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 194 children aged 6–59 months were included in the study, comprising 65 cases (children with SAM) and 129 controls (children without SAM). The mean age was 29 ± 18 months among cases and 32 ± 17 months among controls. Children aged 24–59 months constituted the largest proportion in both groups. Regarding sex distribution, females accounted for 53.8% of cases and 58.1% of controls. A higher proportion of cases resided in rural areas (40.0%) compared to controls (11.6%). LBW was more common among cases (44.6%) than controls (17.8%). Most children in both groups had a birth order of less than three (72.3%) among cases compared to controls (72.1%) (Table 1).

Socio-demographic characteristics of cases and controls

Comorbidities

Among children with SAM, recurrent diarrhoea was the most commonly reported comorbidity, affecting 28 (43.1%) cases, compared to 21 (16.3%) controls. Intractable vomiting was reported in 24 (36.9%) cases and 11 (8.5%) controls. Other conditions were also more prevalent among cases than controls, including gastroenteritis (26.2% vs. 9.3%) and ARI (23.1% vs. 6.2%). Anorexia was reported in 33.8% of cases, whereas only 4.9% of controls had anorexia. Anemia was observed in 18.5% of cases compared to 3.9% of controls.

Dietary practices and household environmental conditions

Compared with controls, children with SAM exhibited less favorable feeding and household environmental practices than controls. Exclusive breastfeeding after birth was less common among cases (49.2% vs. 69.8%), and fewer children with SAM were exclusively breastfed for at least six months (12.3% vs. 28.7%). Consumption of vegetables (66.2% vs. 85.3%), legumes (23.1% vs. 39.5%), and dairy products (50.8% vs. 76.7%) was also lower among cases. Inadequate household food availability was more frequent among cases (12.3% vs. 3.1%). Similarly, poor handwashing practices before food preparation (16.9% vs. 4.7%), before eating (13.8% vs. 3.1%), and after handling child feces (13.8% vs. 2.3%) were more common among cases than controls.

Parents socio-demographic characteristics

Socio-demographic characteristics were broadly similar between cases and controls. Most mothers and fathers in both groups had some level of education, and the majority of mothers were housewives (93.8% among cases vs. 92.2% among controls). The distribution of the number of deliveries was comparable between groups. However, paternal unemployment was more common among cases than controls (43.1% vs. 20.2%), while a lower proportion of mothers of cases had diploma-level education or higher (15.4% vs. 25.6%).

Independent factors associated with SAM (multivariable analysis)

The overall multivariable logistic regression model was statistically significant (Omnibus test: χ² = 68.346, p < 0.001). The model explained 41.2% of the variance in SAM (Nagelkerke R² = 0.412), and demonstrated adequate fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test: χ² = 7.764, p = 0.170). The model correctly classified 78.9% of participants.

After adjusting for potential confounders, several factors independently associated with SAM. Children residing in rural areas had significantly higher odds of SAM compared with those in urban areas (AOR = 4.62). LBW was independently associated with higher odds of SAM (AOR = 3.48). Anorexia (AOR = 8.71), intractable vomiting (AOR = 4.92) and recurrent diarrhoea (AOR = 3.11) were associated with higher odd of SAM. Non-exclusive breastfeeding was independently associated with SAM (AOR = 2.47). Low dietary diversity was independently associated, particularly low consumption of dairy products (AOR = 2.95) and vegetables (AOR = 2.44). Lack of caregiver handwashing after handling child feces, was also independently associated with SAM (AOR = 5.86). Incomplete pentavalent vaccination was also independently associated with SAM (AOR = 3.21) (Table 7).

Discussion

This study identified multiple factors independently associated with SAM among children in Sana’a, highlighting the multifactorial nature of malnutrition in Yemen. These findings are consistent with the UNICEF conceptual framework, which recognizes that undernutrition results from the interaction of immediate, underlying, and basic determinants, including inadequate dietary intake, disease, food insecurity, caregiving practices, and access to health services [5]. The findings are also particularly relevant in Yemen, where conflict, economic instability, and food insecurity continue to contribute substantially to the burden of childhood malnutrition [7–10].

Several infection-related conditions were independently associated with SAM. Recurrent diarrhoea may contribute to malnutrition through impaired nutrient absorption, increased nutrient losses, intestinal inflammation, and elevated metabolic demands [15, 16]. However, the relationship is likely bidirectional, as malnourished children have impaired immune function and are more susceptible to recurrent infections [15, 16, 18]. Similarly, anorexia and intractable vomiting may reduce dietary intake and worsen nutritional status; however, they may also represent clinical manifestations of SAM rather than preceding risk factors. Therefore, these associations should be interpreted cautiously, and causality cannot be inferred from the present study design. Similar associations between childhood illness and malnutrition have been reported in Ethiopia and Yemen, supporting the existence of a vicious cycle in which infection and malnutrition reinforce one another [13, 14, 18].

Infant and young child feeding practices were also important factors associated with SAM. Children who were not exclusively breastfed were more likely to experience SAM, consistent with evidence demonstrating the protective role of exclusive breastfeeding during the first six months of life [19]. Exclusive breastfeeding provides optimal nutrition, protects against infectious diseases, and reduces exposure to contaminated foods and water [19]. Furthermore, inadequate consumption of vegetables and dairy products was independently associated with SAM, suggesting that poor dietary diversity remains an important challenge. Insufficient dietary diversity may limit intake of essential nutrients required for growth and immune function and may reflect broader constraints related to food access and household resources. Similar findings have been reported in other low-resource settings [11, 14, 18, 20].

Household food insecurity was another important factor associated with SAM. Food insecurity can reduce both the quantity and quality of foods available to children and may compromise households’ ability to provide nutritionally adequate diets [20]. This finding is particularly relevant in Yemen, where prolonged conflict, economic decline, and disruptions to food systems have contributed to widespread food insecurity and high levels of acute malnutrition among children [8–10]. Previous studies in Yemen and other resource-constrained settings have similarly identified food insecurity as an important determinant of child malnutrition [12, 20, 21].

Environmental and hygiene-related factors also appear to play a significant role. Poor caregiver hand hygiene practices, particularly after handling child feces, was independently associated with SAM, supporting evidence that inadequate hygiene increases exposure to enteric pathogens and contributes to recurrent infections and environmental enteric dysfunction [22, 23]. These pathways may impair nutrient absorption and utilization, ultimately increasing the risk of undernutrition. The finding underscores the importance of integrating water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions within nutrition programs to address underlying determinants of malnutrition [22, 23].

Incomplete vaccination was independently associated with SAM. Although vaccination does not directly prevent malnutrition, it reduces the burden of vaccine-preventable diseases that can adversely affect nutritional status. In addition, vaccination status may serve as a proxy indicator of access to preventive healthcare services and caregiver engagement with child health programs. Similar associations have been reported in previous study examining determinants of acute malnutrition in low-resource settings [13].

Rural residence was also independently associated with SAM and may reflect broader inequities in access to essential services. Children living in rural areas often face barriers to healthcare, nutrition services, safe water, sanitation facilities, and diverse food markets. Geographic isolation, transportation challenges, poverty, and reduced availability of health infrastructure may further limit access to preventive and curative services [24–26]. Given the ongoing humanitarian challenges in Yemen, these disparities may be even more pronounced, contributing to elevated vulnerability among rural children [7–10]. Similar rural–urban associated with malnutrition have been documented in several low- and middle-income countries [24–26].

LBW was also independently associated with SAM. This finding is consistent with evidence suggesting that children born with LBW are more likely to experience growth faltering, infections, and adverse health outcomes during early childhood [24–26]. LBW may reflect fetal growth restriction, maternal undernutrition, inadequate antenatal care, or adverse maternal health conditions during pregnancy, all of which may increase susceptibility to malnutrition later in life. The observed association highlights the importance of interventions targeting maternal nutrition and antenatal care as part of comprehensive strategies to improve child nutritional outcomes.

The findings have important implications for public health policy and practice. Reducing the burden of SAM requires integrated interventions that address both immediate and underlying determinants of malnutrition. Strengthening CMAM services should be accompanied by efforts to promote exclusive breastfeeding, improve dietary diversity, support household food security, expand immunization coverage, and improve management of common childhood illnesses. Furthermore, integrating nutrition interventions with WASH programs may help reduce infection-related nutritional deterioration. Given the heightened vulnerability of children living in rural areas, expanding access to health and nutrition services in underserved communities should be a priority. Such multisectoral approaches are particularly important in Yemen, where humanitarian, economic, and health system challenges continue to threaten child nutrition and survival [7–10].

Strengths and limitations

First, this study provides context-specific evidence on factors associated with SAM among children aged 6–59 months in Sana’a City, Yemen, where recent data on determinants of malnutrition remain limited. Second, the use of WHO diagnostic criteria for identifying SAM, Third, the matched case–control design. Fourth, the application of multivariable analysis to account for potential confounding. Finally, the comprehensive assessment of clinical, behavioral, environmental, dietary and socioeconomic factors.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the facility-based recruitment strategy and convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as children attending healthcare facilities may differ from those in the general community. Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all children in Sana’a or other regions of Yemen. Second, information on several exposures relied on caregiver recall, creating the potential for recall and reporting bias. Third, the relatively modest sample size may have affected the precision of some estimates. Finally, the case–control design precludes establishing temporal relationships between exposures and outcomes. Some factors identified in this study, particularly ARI, anorexia, recurrent diarrhoea, anemia and intractable vomiting, may have been consequences or manifestations of SAM rather than preceding determinants, raising the possibility of reverse causation [16, 17].

Conclusion

This study identified several factors independently associated with SAM among children aged 6–59 months in Sana’a City, including infection-related conditions, suboptimal infant feeding practices, inadequate dietary diversity, poor caregiver hygiene practices, incomplete vaccination, household food insecurity, rural residence, and LBW. These findings highlight the multifactorial nature of SAM and suggest that prevention and control efforts should combine nutrition-specific and nutrition-sensitive interventions. Strengthening CMAM services, promoting optimal infant and young child feeding practices, improving immunization coverage, expanding WASH programs, enhancing food security, and increasing access to health and nutrition services in rural areas may contribute to reducing the burden of SAM.
