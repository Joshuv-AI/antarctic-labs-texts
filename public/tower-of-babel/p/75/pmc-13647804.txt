Cultural foundations of university student well-being: a grounded theory study of a multidimensional framework in Indonesia
Authors: M. Ahkam Alwi, Nurfitriany Fakhri, Nurfajriyanti Rasyid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Student well-being is increasingly recognized as a multidimensional educational concern, yet its meaning is often transferred across contexts without sufficient cultural clarification. In higher education, university student well-being should be distinguished from general psychological well-being and from school or campus climate because it concerns students’ capacity to function, belong, regulate themselves, and access resources within academic life. This study aimed to identify the culturally grounded dimensions of university student well-being among Indonesian students and to explain how these dimensions are connected within a substantive qualitative framework. A qualitative grounded theory design was used. Ninety Indonesian university students aged 18–21 years were selected through purposive maximum-variation sampling to capture diversity in cultural background, academic discipline, socioeconomic situation, and rural–urban experience. Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews and analyzed iteratively using open, axial, and selective coding, constant comparison, analytic memoing, and team-based coding consensus. The analysis generated four interrelated dimensions of university student well-being: academic well-being, social well-being, psychological well-being, and physical and environmental well-being. These dimensions were not independent categories. Selective coding identified cultural–contextual alignment as the core process explaining how students experience well-being when academic demands, relational support, psychological regulation, and resource conditions fit with local values, family expectations, peer norms, and future aspirations. The study contributes a culturally situated conceptual framework rather than a psychometrically evaluated instrument. It provides a foundation for future item generation, content validation, and cross-cultural measurement testing, while guiding universities to design well-being support that integrates learning conditions, belonging, self-regulation, and material access.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05175-0.

Introduction

Student well-being has become a central concern in educational research because students’ emotional, social, academic, and physical functioning are closely connected to engagement, achievement, persistence, and developmental outcomes [1–4]. However, the conceptual vocabulary used in the field remains uneven. Studies often refer to school well-being, student well-being, subjective well-being, eudaimonic well-being, psychological well-being, mental health, and campus climate in overlapping ways. Although these constructs are related, they are not equivalent. Subjective well-being usually emphasizes life satisfaction and affective balance; eudaimonic and psychological approaches emphasize meaning, autonomy, competence, relatedness, and personal growth; school or campus well-being refers to the educational setting and the institutional conditions that support or undermine students’ functioning [5–8]. This manuscript therefore clarifies the focal construct as university student well-being: a context-specific, multidimensional experience through which students perceive their capacity to learn, belong, regulate themselves, develop personally, and function within the physical and resource conditions of higher education.

The decision to focus on university student well-being is theoretically important because higher education is not merely a place where general well-being is expressed. It is a social, academic, and cultural ecology in which students negotiate learning expectations, peer relationships, identity development, family obligations, and future aspirations. The student well-being model identifies academic, social, emotional, and contextual domains as interdependent components of student life [9], and recent multidimensional models further demonstrate that educational well-being cannot be reduced to happiness or life satisfaction alone [7, 10]. At the same time, cross-cultural evidence indicates that the most important predictors of student well-being may vary across societies and educational systems [11]. These studies support a theoretical position in which university student well-being should be conceptualized as multidimensional, educationally situated, and culturally mediated.

Culture is central to this conceptual clarification. Students’ perceptions of what it means to be well are shaped by language, spirituality, family expectations, social obligations, peer norms, collective identity, and access to valued resources [12–20]. Research on Indigenous thriving and reciprocal research partnerships emphasizes that well-being frameworks should not simply import dominant Western assumptions but should attend to local strengths, relationships, and meanings [21]. Studies of Asian and immigrant students also show that cultural adaptation and cognitive mapping can shape the ways students interpret academic success, social belonging, and personal development [22, 23]. In Indonesia, where higher education includes students from diverse ethnic, linguistic, religious, socioeconomic, and rural–urban backgrounds, well-being may be interpreted through relational responsibility, family encouragement, religious or spiritual orientation, communal participation, and expectations of future contribution. These cultural meanings make it necessary to examine how students themselves organize the dimensions of well-being.

Existing models and instruments have made important contributions by identifying dimensions such as academic engagement, social support, psychological functioning, life satisfaction, and physical health [1, 5–10]. Nevertheless, the use of broad well-being theories without a clear conceptual boundary can make empirical work excessively diffuse. Literature on social media, parental involvement, rural–urban differences, cultural adaptation, and measurement invariance is relevant, but these topics should not be treated as separate and equivalent strands. In the present framework, they are understood as contextual influences that may shape how students experience academic agency, relational belonging, psychological regulation, and resource fit. For example, digital interaction may support belonging or intensify social comparison [24–27], parental expectations may motivate achievement or increase pressure [28–30], and rural–urban differences may influence the availability of educational and health-related resources [31–35]. These influences become theoretically meaningful only when integrated into a coherent model of university student well-being.

The main research problem addressed in this study is therefore not whether well-being is multidimensional, which is already widely accepted, but how the dimensions of university student well-being are organized and interpreted in a culturally diverse Indonesian higher-education context. A culturally grounded framework is needed because cross-cultural measurement can be compromised when constructs are translated without conceptual and contextual adaptation. Prior studies have shown that instruments developed in one context may not demonstrate equivalence in another, and that observed score differences may reflect cultural bias, translation problems, or divergent meanings rather than true differences in well-being [36–40]. Qualitative conceptual grounding is consequently important before item development, validation, or measurement invariance testing is attempted.

Grounded theory is appropriate for this purpose when it is used not merely to classify themes but to explain a social process. The present study therefore treats the dimensions identified in the data as components of a substantive theory rather than as a simple taxonomy. The proposed explanation is cultural–contextual alignment. This refers to the process through which students experience well-being when academic demands, social relationships, psychological self-regulation, and physical-environmental resources are perceived as sufficiently congruent with local cultural values, family expectations, peer norms, and future aspirations. When these elements are misaligned, students may experience academic stress, weak belonging, emotional strain, reduced motivation, or resource-related difficulty even when one dimension appears positive in isolation.

From this perspective, university student well-being is not a universal inventory of desirable traits but an educationally situated experience that emerges from the interaction between personal agency and cultural context. Western models often emphasize autonomy, self-realization, and individual satisfaction, while many Asian and collectivist contexts place stronger emphasis on relational harmony, responsibility to family, social support, and community recognition [22, 41–43]. Indonesian students may draw from both orientations. They may seek academic competence and individual growth while also interpreting success through family expectations, peer solidarity, spiritual values, and collective belonging. This intersection makes Indonesia a theoretically useful context for examining how multidimensional well-being is experienced in higher education.

Accordingly, this study aims to identify the culturally grounded dimensions and core process of university student well-being among Indonesian students. The study is guided by three explicitly formulated research questions. RQ1 asks: How do Indonesian university students describe the meaning of well-being in higher education? RQ2 asks: What dimensions and subdimensions constitute university student well-being in this cultural context? RQ3 asks: What core process explains the relationships among academic, social, psychological, and physical-environmental dimensions? The study contributes by clarifying university student well-being as the central construct, integrating previously broad literature into a focused conceptual framework, generating a substantive grounded theory of cultural–contextual alignment, and providing a qualitative foundation for future measurement development without claiming that a psychometric instrument has already been produced or validated.

Research method

Research design and methodological orientation

This study used a qualitative grounded theory design to generate a substantive explanation of university student well-being as experienced by Indonesian students. Grounded theory was selected because the study sought to move beyond applying a pre-existing Western-derived model and instead examine how students construct meanings of well-being in relation to academic life, cultural expectations, social relationships, and material conditions [44–46]. The design was used to develop a contextually grounded framework, not to conduct psychometric testing or to validate a measurement scale. The study was informed by a constructivist understanding of qualitative inquiry. Participants’ accounts were interpreted as situated meanings produced through interactions between personal experience, academic life, family expectations, peer relationships, cultural values, and institutional conditions. This orientation is consistent with the purpose of generating a substantive theory that explains a social process in a specific context rather than producing a universal model of well-being.

Participants and sampling strategy

The participants were 90 university students aged 18–21 years. They were recruited through purposive maximum-variation sampling to obtain information-rich accounts of university student well-being across different cultural, academic, socioeconomic, and rural–urban backgrounds. The sampling strategy was not intended to produce statistical representativeness. Instead, it was designed to broaden the range of experiences included in the analysis so that categories could be compared across students who differed in family background, community experience, academic discipline, and access to resources.

Participants were eligible if they were active university students, were within the target age range, and were willing to discuss their experiences of academic life, relationships, emotional regulation, health, resources, and cultural expectations. The available sociodemographic information is summarized in Table 1. Because the original qualitative dataset did not include a full numerical breakdown by every cultural group, curriculum pathway, year category, or specialization, the revised manuscript reports these characteristics as sampling dimensions rather than as quantitative subgroup distributions. This decision avoids unsupported numerical claims while responding to the need for a dedicated participant description.

Data collection and semi-structured interview procedure

Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews. The interview protocol was developed from the study purpose, the literature on multidimensional student well-being, and the need to explore culturally meaningful experiences rather than impose a predetermined instrument [1, 9, 10, 21]. The guiding questions invited participants to describe what well-being meant in university life, how academic demands affected their well-being, how peer and family relationships supported or challenged them, how they regulated emotions and maintained motivation, how physical health and resource access shaped daily functioning, and how cultural values influenced their interpretation of success and well-being.

The interviews were conducted in Indonesian by members of the research team who were familiar with qualitative interviewing. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw. Interviews were organized in a setting that protected privacy and enabled participants to speak openly. Probing questions were used to clarify meanings, obtain examples, and compare experiences across academic, social, psychological, and physical-environmental domains. Field notes and analytic memos were prepared after interviews to record emerging categories, contextual details, and questions for subsequent data collection.

Data collection and analysis occurred iteratively. Early interviews were coded to identify provisional categories, and later interviews were used to compare, refine, and test the boundaries of these categories. Theoretical saturation was considered achieved when additional accounts no longer generated new major dimensions, when the properties of each category were sufficiently specified, and when the core process of cultural–contextual alignment explained the relationships among categories. Saturation was assessed through repeated memo review and team discussion rather than through a numerical stopping rule.

Data analysis and coding procedure

Data analysis followed grounded theory procedures of open coding, axial coding, and selective coding [44–46]. During open coding, interview material was examined line by line or segment by segment to identify meaning units related to academic functioning, relationships, emotional regulation, health, resources, culture, and future orientation. Codes were kept close to participants’ meanings in the initial phase. During axial coding, related codes were grouped into categories and subcategories, and the research team examined the conditions, interactions, and consequences associated with each category. During selective coding, the categories were integrated around a core process that could explain how the dimensions were connected.

The coding process used constant comparison. Meaning units were compared within and across interviews, across students with different cultural or socioeconomic backgrounds, and across different domains of well-being. The research team discussed coding decisions to refine category labels, merge overlapping codes, and distinguish the central construct from broader psychological well-being or campus climate. Disagreements were resolved through consensus meetings and by returning to the coded material and memos. A statistical intercoder coefficient was not calculated because the study used a grounded theory consensus process; nevertheless, dependability was supported through team coding, memoing, an audit trail of category development, and explicit examples of coding decisions presented in the Results section.

Trustworthiness, reflexivity, and ethical considerations

Several strategies were used to strengthen qualitative rigor. Credibility was supported by purposive maximum-variation sampling, iterative questioning, constant comparison, and the use of analytic memos to examine emerging patterns. Dependability was strengthened through documentation of coding stages and team-based category refinement. Confirmability was supported by keeping the analysis tied to participants’ accounts and by distinguishing empirical findings from future implications for instrument development. Transferability was addressed by describing the Indonesian higher-education context and the available participant characteristics so that readers can judge relevance to other settings.

The researchers recognized that their academic and cultural positions could influence interpretation. Reflexive discussion was therefore used to question whether categories reflected participants’ meanings rather than the assumptions of existing Western models or the researchers’ expectations. The study received ethical approval from the Institute for Research and Community Service at Makassar State University under ethical approval number 3246/UN36.11/TU/2025. All participants were provided with information about the purpose, procedures, confidentiality, and voluntary nature of the study, and written informed consent was obtained before data collection.

Results

Analytic pathway and category development

The grounded theory analysis was organized around the three research questions. RQ1 asked how Indonesian university students described the meaning of well-being in higher education. Students described well-being not as a single emotional state but as the ability to study effectively, maintain supportive relationships, regulate pressure, sustain hope, and manage physical and material conditions. RQ2 asked which dimensions and subdimensions constituted university student well-being. Open and axial coding generated four dimensions: academic well-being, social well-being, psychological well-being, and physical and environmental well-being. RQ3 asked what core process explained the relationships among these dimensions. Selective coding identified cultural–contextual alignment as the central explanatory process.

Category development proceeded from meaning units to initial codes, from initial codes to axial categories, and from axial categories to integrated dimensions. Academic well-being

Academic well-being referred to students’ perceived capacity to participate effectively and meaningfully in learning. It included academic engagement, academic self-efficacy, learning satisfaction, and academic stress management. Students did not describe academic well-being only as high achievement or grade performance. Rather, they associated it with understanding academic expectations, receiving guidance, sustaining motivation, completing assignments, and interpreting academic challenges as manageable. In the coding process, accounts of clear lecturer expectations, meaningful learning tasks, peer discussion, and confidence in completing difficult work were grouped under academic well-being.

This result clarifies the role of culture in academic functioning. In the Indonesian higher-education context, academic well-being was not experienced only through individual autonomy or personal mastery. It was also shaped by family expectations, peer encouragement, respect for lecturers, and the perceived usefulness of education for future social contribution. This finding aligns with multidimensional approaches that position academic functioning as one component of broader student well-being rather than as a separate outcome [6, 9, 10]. It also supports the argument that educational well-being needs to be interpreted through culturally meaningful forms of agency rather than through a universal performance-centered model.

Social well-being

Social well-being referred to the quality of students’ relationships and their sense of belonging in the campus environment. It included social connectedness, social support, social competence, and participation in the campus community. Students connected well-being with having trusted peers, receiving practical and emotional help, being able to communicate and resolve conflict, and feeling accepted in academic and social groups. Accounts of peer assistance, family encouragement, and supportive campus relationships were coded as indicators of relational belonging.

This dimension was especially important because students often interpreted personal difficulties through relational meanings. Supportive relationships helped students manage academic pressure, whereas isolation, weak belonging, or unresolved conflict reduced their motivation and sense of security. The finding is consistent with cross-cultural evidence that belonging and support are among the strongest predictors of student well-being [11]. It also resonates with Asian and collectivist perspectives in which relational harmony, family expectations, and social responsibility may be central to the experience of well-being [30, 41–43].

Psychological well-being

Psychological well-being referred to students’ ability to regulate emotions, develop identity, pursue personal growth, maintain life satisfaction, and sustain optimism about the future. This dimension overlaps with eudaimonic and psychological well-being models, but in this study it was interpreted through the university context. Students’ psychological well-being was linked to academic pressure, self-understanding, social comparison, family expectations, and future career aspirations. Coding indicators included efforts to remain calm under academic pressure, to interpret problems as opportunities for growth, and to maintain hope despite uncertainty.

The findings suggest that psychological well-being functions as a bridge between internal regulation and contextual demands. It is not simply an individual trait or a general mental-health outcome. Rather, it is shaped by the fit between students’ internal coping capacities and the expectations, relationships, and resources surrounding them. This result supports the view that student well-being requires attention to both affective and functioning-based dimensions [5, 6, 8, 10]. It also indicates that culturally responsive research should examine how students define maturity, resilience, hope, and personal growth within their social and educational environments.

Physical and environmental well-being

Physical and environmental well-being referred to students’ bodily condition, life balance, access to resources, and capacity to function within material and institutional constraints. Students connected fatigue, physical health, financial pressure, transportation, learning facilities, and access to information with their ability to study, participate socially, and maintain psychological stability. These accounts were coded under physical health, life balance, optimism and hope for the future, and resource accessibility.

This dimension shows that university student well-being cannot be understood adequately if resource conditions are treated as external background variables only. In the data, resource access directly shaped academic engagement, social participation, emotional regulation, and future orientation. This finding is particularly relevant in contexts where socioeconomic and geographic differences influence access to educational support, health-related resources, and learning infrastructure [33, 34]. It also provides a bridge between individual-level and institutional-level understandings of student well-being.

Core process: cultural–contextual alignment

Selective coding generated cultural–contextual alignment as the core process linking the four dimensions. Cultural–contextual alignment means that students experienced well-being when academic activities, social relationships, psychological self-regulation, and physical-environmental conditions were perceived as fitting with local values, family expectations, peer norms, and future aspirations. Misalignment occurred when academic demands exceeded available support, when family or cultural expectations conflicted with students’ personal goals, when peer relationships failed to provide belonging, or when limited resources disrupted daily functioning.

This core process explains why the four dimensions should not be interpreted as independent domains. Academic stress could be reduced by peer support, family encouragement, self-regulation, and access to learning resources. Conversely, resource limitations could increase academic pressure, weaken social participation, and undermine optimism. The substantive theory therefore proposes that culturally grounded university student well-being emerges through dynamic alignment among academic agency, relational belonging, psychological regulation, and resource fit. Implications for future instrument development

The findings provide a conceptual foundation for future development of a university student well-being instrument, but they do not constitute instrument development or psychometric evaluation. The present study did not generate item pools, conduct expert validation, pilot test items, assess reliability, examine factor structure, or test measurement invariance. Therefore, conclusions about future measurement properties have been revised to avoid extending beyond the evidence generated by the qualitative design.

At the same time, the framework can guide later measurement work. Future studies may transform the subdimensions into candidate indicators, evaluate content validity with students and experts, conduct cognitive interviews, pilot test items, examine factor structure, estimate reliability, and test measurement invariance across cultural, regional, or institutional groups. The qualitative findings are useful for ensuring that future items reflect students’ locally meaningful experiences before quantitative validation is attempted [10, 40, 47].

Discussion

This study identified four interrelated dimensions of culturally grounded university student well-being among Indonesian students: academic well-being, social well-being, psychological well-being, and physical and environmental well-being. More importantly, the analysis generated cultural–contextual alignment as the core process connecting these dimensions. The findings therefore revise the manuscript from a broad review of well-being literature into a focused grounded theory explanation. The educational setting is treated as the context of the study, while university student well-being is defined as students’ perceived capacity to learn, belong, regulate themselves, grow, and function within culturally meaningful and materially supported academic life.

The academic dimension shows that students’ well-being is closely connected to engagement, self-efficacy, learning satisfaction, and the ability to manage academic stress. This finding is consistent with multidimensional student well-being models that include academic functioning as a core domain [9, 10]. However, the present study adds that academic well-being in Indonesia is interpreted through relational and cultural expectations. Students’ confidence and motivation are not only individual psychological resources; they are also shaped by lecturer guidance, peer discussion, family encouragement, and the perceived value of education for future contribution. This differs from models that privilege individual autonomy alone and suggests that academic agency in collectivist or relational contexts may be supported through social responsibility and connectedness.

The social dimension was central to the framework because students repeatedly connected well-being with belonging, trusted relationships, and practical support. This result aligns with cross-cultural evidence showing the importance of social support, belonging, and family-related resources for student well-being [11, 48]. In Western educational literature, belonging is often discussed as a predictor of engagement or retention. In many Asian contexts, including Indonesia, belonging may also carry moral and cultural meanings related to family obligation, peer solidarity, respect, and communal recognition. The present findings therefore suggest that social well-being should not be treated only as the presence of friends or social participation. It is a culturally embedded experience of being supported, accepted, and able to fulfill relational expectations without losing personal direction.

The psychological dimension connects the study to subjective, eudaimonic, and psychological well-being theories while also clarifying their boundaries. Students’ emotional regulation, identity development, life satisfaction, personal growth, and hope for the future are consistent with broader well-being literature [5, 6, 8]. Nevertheless, these psychological aspects were not experienced separately from academic and cultural life. Emotional regulation was linked to academic pressure, social comparison, family expectations, and future uncertainty. Personal growth was often understood in relation to becoming a responsible student, family member, and future professional. This supports the argument that psychological well-being should be interpreted within the specific life setting in which it is constructed rather than being assumed to have identical meanings across cultures.

The physical and environmental dimension is one of the important contributions of the study because it shows that material and institutional conditions are not merely background variables. Students’ physical health, fatigue, financial pressure, access to learning resources, transportation, and daily balance shaped their capacity to engage academically, maintain relationships, and remain optimistic. This finding is consistent with literature on rural–urban disparities, socioeconomic inequalities, and resource access in student well-being [33, 34]. It is also particularly relevant for Indonesian higher education, where students may differ substantially in family resources, regional origin, and access to academic facilities. Well-being support should therefore include not only counselling or psychological services but also attention to learning infrastructure, academic guidance, health promotion, and equitable access to resources.

The core process of cultural–contextual alignment integrates these four dimensions into a substantive grounded theory. The four dimensions themselves are not claimed to be entirely new; similar domains have appeared in existing models. The theoretical contribution lies in explaining how the dimensions interact in a culturally diverse Indonesian context. Alignment occurs when academic demands, relational support, psychological coping, and resources fit with students’ cultural values and future aspirations. Misalignment occurs when one or more of these conditions becomes incompatible, such as when family expectations exceed available resources, when peer belonging is weak, or when academic pressure outpaces emotional and institutional support. This explanatory process responds directly to the methodological requirement of grounded theory by moving beyond classification toward a substantive account of how student well-being is produced and disrupted.

The findings also clarify the role of literature on social media, parental involvement, rural–urban differences, and cultural adaptation. These topics are important, but they are not separate constructs competing with university student well-being. Rather, they are contextual influences that may affect one or more dimensions of the framework. Digital interaction may support belonging but may also intensify social comparison and emotional strain [6, 25, 26]. Parental involvement may provide motivation and security but may also create pressure when expectations and students’ resources are misaligned [28, 30, 47, 49, 50]. Cultural adaptation and migration-related experiences may alter students’ cognitive maps of achievement and belonging [23]. Interpreting these influences through cultural–contextual alignment creates a more coherent conceptual framework than presenting them as disconnected literatures.

Several practical implications follow from the findings. Universities should design well-being support as a multidimensional system rather than as a narrow mental-health intervention. Academic advising, peer support, culturally sensitive counselling, lecturer-student communication, resource accessibility, and family-aware support strategies may all contribute to student well-being. The framework also has implications for future measurement. Because this study did not develop or validate an instrument, it cannot support claims about reliability, factor structure, or measurement invariance. However, it can guide future item generation by identifying domains and culturally meaningful indicators that should be examined through content validation, cognitive interviewing, pilot testing, factor analysis, and invariance testing.

The study has limitations. The qualitative design supports conceptual development but does not permit causal inference, prevalence estimation, or psychometric claims. The available participant information supports a description of sampling diversity but not a detailed statistical comparison by cultural group, discipline, year of study, or specialization. The analysis is based on students’ accounts and should be extended through studies involving lecturers, families, and institutional stakeholders. Future research should test the transferability of the framework across Indonesian regions and higher-education institutions, compare it with Western and other Asian contexts, and conduct mixed-methods research to develop and validate a culturally responsive measurement instrument.

Conclusion

This qualitative grounded theory study identified four interrelated dimensions of culturally grounded university student well-being among Indonesian students: academic well-being, social well-being, psychological well-being, and physical and environmental well-being. The revised analysis clarifies that the study concerns university student well-being in a higher-education context, not general psychological well-being or school well-being as interchangeable constructs. The substantive theory generated from the analysis is cultural–contextual alignment, which explains that students experience well-being when academic demands, relational support, psychological regulation, and physical-environmental resources are perceived as congruent with local cultural meanings, family expectations, peer norms, and future aspirations. This framework contributes to the literature by integrating multidimensional student well-being with culturally responsive research and by demonstrating why qualitative conceptual grounding is necessary before instrument development and cross-cultural validation. The study does not claim to have developed or psychometrically evaluated a measurement instrument. Instead, it offers a conceptual foundation for future research that may generate items, test content validity, examine factor structure, evaluate reliability, and assess measurement invariance across student groups. Future studies should expand the sample across regions and institutions, include multiple informants, and combine qualitative and quantitative methods to refine and validate the proposed framework.
