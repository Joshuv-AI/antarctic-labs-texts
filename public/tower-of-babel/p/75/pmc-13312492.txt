Clinical Outcomes and Predictors of Adverse Events in Influenza Patients Managed in a Hospital-at-Home Program: A 10-Year Retrospective Study
Authors: Emmanuel Coloma-Bazán, Anna Aldea, Ainoa Ugarte, Celia Cardozo, Carles Lopera, Nicole García-Pouton, Verónica Rico, María del Mar Mosquera, Cristina Rodríguez, Genoveva Cuesta, María Ángeles Marcos, Irene Pereta, Eva Castells, Núria Seijas, Jordi Altés, David Nicolás
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-at-home (HaH) is increasingly used to manage acute conditions like influenza, but data on long-term outcomes remain limited.ObjectiveTo identify clinical predictors of 30- and 90-day adverse outcomes among influenza patients treated at HaH.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 270 adult patients with laboratory-confirmed influenza managed at HaH over 10 influenza seasons. Outcomes included emergency room (ER) visits, hospital readmissions, and all-cause mortality at 30 and 90 days.ResultsAt 30 days, 12.4% had ED visits, 5.1% were readmitted, and 3.4% died; at 90 days, these increased to 16.2%, 6.5%, and 6.8%, respectively. Multivariable analysis identified reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) at discharge and early ER visits as independent predictors of poor outcomes.ConclusionsRenal impairment and early postdischarge ER use are significant predictors of adverse outcomes in influenza patients managed through HaH. These findings help clinicians optimize risk stratification and follow-up strategies during seasonal influenza peaks.Keywords: Acute home care, Hospital-at-home, Influenza virus, Risk factors, Seasonal outbreaks

Introduction and Objectives

Influenza, an acute respiratory illness affecting all age groups, is linked to significant morbidity and mortality during pandemics, seasonal epidemics, and sporadic outbreaks. Approximately 10% of the global population contracts influenza annually, resulting in up to 500,000 deaths.1 The World Health Organization established the Global Influenza Surveillance Network in 1952 and renamed it the Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System in 2011. This network has been pivotal in tracking seasonal influenza strains and responding effectively to significant pandemics such as those in 1957, 1968, and 2009.2

Influenza viruses, belonging to the Orthomyxoviridae family, are categorized into types A, B, C, and D, with types A and B being the primary causes of human disease, leading to seasonal epidemics and occasional pandemics. The virus spreads through aerosols, droplets, and contact transmission.3, 4, 5 Clinical manifestations vary widely, from asymptomatic cases to severe illnesses involving complications such as pneumonia, myositis, and myocarditis.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Diagnosing influenza can be challenging due to symptom overlap with other respiratory pathogens like severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2), necessitating laboratory confirmation via reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (PCR).4,5

Vaccination remains the most effective prevention strategy, although its efficacy fluctuates based on the match between circulating strains and vaccine composition.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 The World Health Organization recommends vaccination for vulnerable groups, including pregnant women, young children, older people, individuals with chronic conditions, and healthcare workers.4,5 Antiviral therapy, particularly with neuraminidase inhibitors like oseltamivir, is critical when administered early in the disease course, especially for immunocompromised patients.4,5

Beyond in-hospital outcomes, influenza is increasingly recognized as a condition associated with substantial short-term postdischarge mortality. Large population-based studies have shown that nearly half of all deaths among patients hospitalized for influenza occur within the first 30 days after discharge, particularly among older adults and those with multiple comorbidities. Importantly, a significant proportion of these deaths are not directly attributed to influenza on death certificates, suggesting that the true mortality burden of influenza is often underestimated.9

In Spain, in-hospital case fatality rates for influenza range around 5%-6%, rising sharply in very old patients, although postdischarge outcomes are seldom evaluated. Together, these findings highlight the need to assess medium-term outcomes after influenza hospitalization and to identify patients at higher risk of adverse events beyond the acute phase.10

Despite these preventive and therapeutic measures, influenza burdens healthcare systems considerably, often leading to hospital overcrowding, especially during peak seasons. The hospital-at-home (HaH) model has emerged as a feasible alternative to traditional hospitalization, allowing patients to receive acute care at home while minimizing nosocomial transmission risks and alleviating hospital capacity strain.11,12 This model has also been identified as a core strategy for modern internal medicine in Europe, forming part of broader ambulatory care initiatives aimed at optimizing hospital resources and enhancing patient-centered care.13

This study aims to analyze the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of patients with confirmed influenza treated in a HaH unit and to identify risk factors associated with adverse outcomes, including readmissions, emergency room (ER) visits, and mortality at 30 and 90 days postdischarge.

Methods

Study Population

Participants included all patients diagnosed with Influenza A, B, or C who were admitted to the HaH unit at the Hospital Clinic of Barcelona from January 1, 2015, to March 31, 2024. The diagnosis was made through clinical, radiological, and laboratory criteria, with microbiological confirmation obtained for all cases in nasopharyngeal swabs.

Molecular Diagnostic Procedures

Two diagnostic circuits were used to confirm influenza virus infection:

Urgent Rapid Test: Utilized the Cobas Liat Roche analyzer system with Influenza A/B and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) reagents, controls, and calibrators.

Routine Diagnostic: From 2018 onward, the multiplex real-time PCR platform (FLOW System, Roche) replaced the previous nested multiplex PCR assay.14 This semi-automated system, consisting of a Primary Handling System, MagNA Pure 96 nucleic acid extraction system, PCR Setup System, and Light Cycler 480 II, detects pathogens including influenza A (AH1N1, AH3N2), B, C, RSV, enterovirus, rhinovirus, parainfluenza virus, adenovirus, human metapneumovirus, coronavirus, and human bocavirus. Manufacturer’s protocols were followed, and amplification curves were visually inspected to ensure accuracy. Only cases with molecular confirmation of influenza virus infection were included in the analysis.

Microbiological Sampling and Processing

Sputum samples were collected from patients who were able to expectorate spontaneously. The collection was performed under sterile conditions, with patients instructed to provide deep cough samples in sterile containers. Samples were processed within 2 hours of collection or stored at 4°C for a maximum of 24 hours before analysis. Microbiological cultures followed standard laboratory procedures to identify bacterial or fungal pathogens, ensuring adequate sample quality by evaluating polymorphonuclear leukocytes and epithelial cells under microscopy. Only samples meeting the quality criteria (>25 polymorphonuclear leukocytes and <10 epithelial cells per low-power field) were included in the analysis.

Data Collection

Data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records for 270 participants, including sociodemographic, clinical, microbiological, and laboratory findings at admission and outcomes data.

Setting and HaH Model

Hospital Clinic of Barcelona is a 750-bed public tertiary hospital in Barcelona, serving approximately 526,000 people. The HaH Unit, established in 1996, was expanded in 2006 to offer two modalities: admission avoidance and early discharge, accommodating up to 72 patients. Approximately 2300 patients are managed annually with a median HaH stay of 7.8 days.15,16 During the pandemic of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), the HaH model was adapted to include patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection.17

Patients were managed under the two standard HaH modalities mentioned: admission avoidance from the emergency room (ER) and early discharge following initial stabilization in a conventional hospital ward. The type of HaH admission was included as a covariate in multivariable analyses.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients were selected for HaH admission based on specific criteria to ensure suitability. Inclusion criteria required that patients meet the admission standards for influenza management outside a traditional inpatient setting. Exclusions included severe acute respiratory failure (PaO2/FiO2 < 100), impaired mental status (delirium or Glasgow Coma Scale <12), lack of caregiver support, and residence outside the HaH catchment area (Supplementary Table 1). Most admissions originated from the ER, though patients were also referred from various hospital wards following stabilization or primary care services.

In our setting, approximately 85% of patients who meet clinical eligibility criteria for HaH accept admission. However, for diagnosis than require isolation, such as influenza infection, patients living in nursing homes are generally excluded, as HaH requires a private home environment and adequate conditions to ensure safe acute care delivery.

HaH Services Provided

HaH patients received daily nurse visits, with physician assessments at admission, discharge, and as needed for complications. Diagnostic tests, including blood tests and electrocardiograms, were available at home, while patients requiring imaging were transferred to the hospital.

Outcome Measures

Following previous studies in traditional hospital settings, adverse events were defined as a composite endpoint to capture clinically relevant outcomes related to short-term mortality and healthcare utilization.18 The composite endpoint, which included all-cause mortality, ER visits, or hospital readmissions within 30 and 90 days postdischarge, was selected to reflect patient outcomes and care continuity.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as medians and IQRs. The chi-square test assessed differences in qualitative variables, with independent-sample t-tests applied to quantitative variables. Multivariate logistic regression identified risk factors for adverse outcomes, calculating odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs (CI). Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Analyses were conducted using SPSS for Mac, version 22.0.

Results

A total of 270 patients with confirmed influenza diagnoses were included in the study. Table 1 presents baseline characteristics, including demographics, season enrolling, underlying conditions, and comorbidities at admission. The cohort comprised 41.8% females, with a median age of 73 years (IQR 61-82), median Charlson Index was 3 (IQR 0-5), and a mean Barthel Index of 89.5 (SD 21.9). Among participants, 87.8% had at least one comorbidity (eg, hypertension, congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]), and 10% required ventilator support at admission (eg, noninvasive ventilation or home oxygen therapy). Immunosuppression, including HIV and immunosuppressive therapy, was present in 24% of the cohort. During the study period, 46% received seasonal influenza vaccination. The median time from symptom onset to admission was 3 days. Pneumonia was diagnosed in 20.7% of patients (Supplementary Table 2 and Table 2).

Patients were primarily admitted from the ER (75%), with additional admissions from other wards such Internal Medicine, Pneumology, Infectious Diseases, or Primary Care. A graphical representation of influenza activity from 2015 to 2024 (Supplementary Figure 1) illustrates the seasonal variation in admissions. During the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a marked decrease in influenza-related hospitalizations, with later seasons showing delayed influenza peaks as described in the literature.16,19

Laboratory and Microbiological Findings

Results of laboratory and microbiological tests are shown in Supplementary Table 2. Influenza A was identified in 85.9% of cases, with coinfections observed in 13%, including RSV and bacterial pathogens (eg, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa). The pneumococcus was the most frequently isolated in 6.3% of cases (Supplementary Table 2). At admission, the median C-reactive protein was 6.3 mg/dL, with lymphopenia present in 55.2% of patients. At discharge, C-reactive protein levels decreased to a median of 1.2 mg/dL, and lymphopenia was noted in 23%. These findings highlight the inflammatory profile at baseline and during recovery, potentially influencing outcomes in patients with severe presentations.

Treatment and Clinical Outcomes

Treatment protocols are detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Nearly all patients (98.1%) received oseltamivir within a median of 3 days from symptom onset. Seventy-nine percent received empirical antibiotics, with 43.7% on intravenous antibiotics (primarily ceftriaxone) and 61.1% on oral antibiotics (mainly levofloxacin and azithromycin). The median HaH stay was 7 days, with 4.1% requiring hospital transfer due to complications. These treatment protocols and patient responses provide insight into the typical HaH management pathway and associated outcomes.

Postdischarge Outcomes

Oxygen therapy at discharge was required by 16.7%, primarily among those ≥60 years with lower Barthel scores (median <70) and higher Charlson Indices (median >3). Postdischarge outcomes included a 16.6% hospital readmission rate, with 8.5% within 30 days and 8.1% within 90 days, mainly for exacerbations of chronic diseases (eg, COPD, heart failure) or respiratory infections. Mortality was low, with one patient death (0.4%) during HaH, six at 30 days (2.2%), and five (1.9%) at 90 days, all related to chronic illness.

Composite Endpoint and Predictive Factors

A composite endpoint of mortality, readmissions, and ER visits was observed in 41 patients at 30 days and 38 patients at 90 days. The multivariate analysis presented in Figure A highlights key risk factors associated with adverse events at 30 days, including length of stay (OR 1.07; 95% CI 1.01-1.13; P = .008), Charlson Index (OR 1.24; 95% CI 1.08-1.4; P = .002), and oxygen dependency at discharge (OR 3.51; 95% CI 1.6-7.7; P = .002). Conversely, variables such as immunosuppression and COPD did not reach statistical significance in this model. Figure B shows findings at 90 days, a low GFR at discharge (OR 0.971; 95% CI 0.95-0.98; P < .001), means that for every 1 mL/min decrease in GFR, the risk of adverse events increased by 3% and ER visits within 30 days (OR 4.43; 95% CI 1.69-11.61; P = .002) were significantly associated with poorer outcomes.

Multivariate Analysis

The ORs were obtained through multivariate logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, mode of HaH, and age ≥65 years to control for potential confounders. Age and sex were adjusted as they are established determinants of influenza severity, with older age and male sex frequently associated with higher complication rates. The specific adjustment for age ≥65 years was included, given that this population group is at heightened risk for severe influenza-related outcomes independent of other comorbidities. Additionally, the mode of HaH admission was adjusted to reflect differences in baseline disease stage and severity at the time of HaH initiation, optimizing the validity of our findings (see Supplementary Tables 4-7).

Discussion

This study presents the first 10-year cohort of patients with laboratory-confirmed influenza treated under a HaH model. The findings contribute to a growing body of evidence supporting HaH as a safe and effective alternative to inpatient care for selected patients with nonsevere influenza. This model may alleviate pressure on hospitals during peak influenza seasons, offering a viable strategy to manage high patient volumes while minimizing the risk of nosocomial transmission.

Across the cohort, outcomes were favorable despite the presence of multiple comorbidities, as reflected by the median Charlson Comorbidity Index. Only one patient died during HaH admission, and just 11 required hospital transfer, only four of which were directly attributed to influenza complications. These data reinforce the importance of selecting patients appropriately. The average Barthel Index of 89.5 indicates a moderate level of functional dependency, suggesting that HaH can safely accommodate patients with specific degrees of frailty, provided that close monitoring and clear escalation criteria are in place.

Most patients presented with mild to moderate respiratory failure, normal chest radiographs, and low inflammatory markers at baseline. This clinical profile aligns with the selection criteria used in HaH models, which exclude patients with a high risk of early deterioration. These results highlight the effectiveness of structured triage protocols in identifying candidates for home-based care while ensuring patient safety.

Epidemiological Insights

Most influenza cases involved Influenza A, which aligns with seasonal peaks and highlights the value of HaH in providing an alternative to in-hospital care, thus alleviating capacity pressures and reducing nosocomial transmission risks.16,19 Notably, the study reveals a sharp decline in influenza cases during the COVID-19 pandemic, which is attributed to measures like mask-wearing, social distancing, and increased hygiene.9 HaH models may further reduce influenza spread, supporting health system resilience and mitigating nosocomial transmission during concurrent seasonal and pandemic outbreaks.10

Treatment Practices and Considerations

Empirical antimicrobials were used in 78.5% of cases, yet fewer than 25% of patients had confirmed bacterial coinfections (Supplementary Table 3). This finding suggests an opportunity for clinicians to be more selective in antibiotic use, reserving them for cases where bacterial infection is confirmed by clinical, radiological, or laboratory findings to reduce the risk of antibiotic resistance.20

Impact of HaH on Healthcare Delivery during the Pandemic

HaH units gained prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic, proving effective for safely managing patients at home and reducing the strain on hospital resources.17,21,22 The pandemic experience reinforces the potential of HaH in infection control and cost reduction, suggesting that HaH may benefit influenza management and support broader strategies for controlling infectious diseases. Prior studies on HaH for influenza have reported favorable clinical and economic outcomes, but with limitations, including small sample sizes and short follow-up periods, making this study’s extended follow-up especially valuable.23,24

Comparison to Existing Literature

Despite the growing popularity of HaH, limited data exists on risk factors for adverse outcomes specific to influenza in this setting. Existing studies have identified risk factors in hospitalized cohorts; for instance, Lynfield et al25 noted that older age, symptom duration prior to admission, and immunosuppression were significant predictors of poor outcomes. Similarly, a German cohort study26 identified COPD, heart failure, chronic anemia, obesity, male sex, and older age as risk factors. Our findings align with this literature, adding that early ER visits and factors such as a higher Charlson Index, oxygen dependency, and prolonged stays are associated with elevated risk of complications, underscoring the need for tailored follow-up in HaH settings.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite the extended study period, the retrospective design and limited sample size are primary limitations. Given the criteria for admitting patients to HaH, selection bias is also possible. However, our 10-year data provides a strong foundation for future research, especially as influenza HaH models are widely adopted. Our experience may guide other healthcare providers in avoiding strain on the healthcare system during peak influenza seasons.

Another limitation of this study is the lack of systematic data on primary care visits during the 30- and 90-day follow-up period. While inclusion of these data could offer a more comprehensive view of healthcare utilization, we focused on ER visits, hospital readmissions, and mortality, as these outcomes are more consistently recorded and better reflect clinically significant deterioration.

Additionally, the absence of a direct comparison with patients with influenza managed exclusively in conventional inpatient wards represents an important limitation. Differences in patient selection, baseline severity, and data availability precluded a matched analysis. Future prospective or comparative observational studies are warranted to better define the relative effectiveness and safety of HaH compared with brick-and-mortar hospitalization. Finally, the coexistence of admission avoidance and early discharge pathways may have introduced baseline clinical heterogeneity, although this was addressed by adjustment in multivariable analyses.

These findings may help clinicians refine risk-stratification tools and postdischarge follow-up plans during influenza outbreaks. As health systems prepare for concurrent seasonal epidemics and emerging threats, HaH programs could play a strategic role in preserving inpatient capacity and maintaining continuity of care for vulnerable populations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that HaH is a viable and effective model for managing selected influenza patients outside traditional hospitals, reducing hospital overcrowding and nosocomial infection risks. Our findings emphasize the importance of targeted interventions for high-risk patients, particularly those with elevated Charlson Index scores, prolonged stays, oxygen requirements, impaired renal function, and early ER visits. Identifying and addressing these risk factors is crucial for optimizing short-term outcomes and minimizing readmissions. Clinicians should prioritize enhanced postdischarge monitoring for these patients to support recovery. Future research should focus on developing predictive tools to refine patient selection for HaH and exploring long-term strategies to improve outcomes.
