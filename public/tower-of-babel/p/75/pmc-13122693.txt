Difficulty diagnosing breast cancer recurrence in the context of a radio-frequency-identification device-containing breast implant – A case report
Authors: Nikki Burnett, Pakan Kleinig, Nicola R Dean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcerns around the risk of prosthetic post-mastectomy breast reconstruction obscuring breast cancer recurrence existed in the early days of breast reconstruction but were laid to rest with cohort studies. We present an unusual case where MRI images were limited in diagnosing breast cancer recurrence, partly due to an artefact produced by a radio-frequency-identification device (RFID) contained within the implant. Co-existing Tietze’s syndrome, an uncommon benign inflammatory condition effecting the chest wall, further confused the picture. This case highlights the diverse pathologies that can present as chest wall symptoms in breast reconstruction patients, and the diagnostic limitations of breast MRI with RFID-containing implants.Keywords: Breast neoplasms, Breast implants, Tietze’s syndrome, Radio frequency identification device

Case

A 63-year-old woman presented with aesthetic dissatisfaction with a right implant-based breast reconstruction that had been performed 9 months earlier at a different institution. She also complained of discomfort at the medial edge of the breast implant as a secondary concern. Her original surgeon had planned augmentation with autologous fat grafting, she sought a second opinion.

She had previously undergone contralateral mastectomy 18 years prior, with immediate pedicled TRAM flap reconstruction. This was later augmented with a silicone implant and balanced with right augmentation mastopexy. These procedures were complicated by implant rupture and silicone lymphadenopathy, with replacement of the implants being performed in the intervening years (Refer to Figure 1 graphical depiction of case).

Oncological and breast reconstruction history.

Timeline of diagnoses and procedures.

In 2021 she underwent right skin sparing mastectomy, sentinel lymph node biopsy and insertion of tissue expander. Nine months later the expander was exchanged to a RFID-containing implant (Motiva ERSF 375Q) with simultaneous nipple reconstruction. There were no documented complications.

Oncological information on her first (left) breast cancer was unavailable, she reported no adjuvant therapy. Her 2020 screen-detected right breast cancer was an Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC) Grade 3 with isolated tumor cells present in one node. She declined adjuvant treatment.

On examination at our clinic, breast volume symmetry appeared good with no sign of capsular contracture. On the right, there was an indrawn scar laterally and a less “natural” breast reconstruction than the contralateral side, but an otherwise unremarkable, implant-based reconstruction. The tentative operation plan to address her presenting complaint was fat grafting plus z-plasty, but due to concerns about her expectations a psychiatric consultation was sought, with deferral of surgical consent until a second appointment.

Worsening pain at the medial edge of her right breast implant led to an interim GP visit, followed by a breast ultrasound showing the possibility of implant rupture and prompting an MRI.

At the next plastic surgery appointment, the MRI images were reviewed. They showed a prominent blooming artefact from the RFID. Although there was thickening of the posterior capsule of the implant and an abnormal looking lymph node superior to the breast implant and a medial focus of enhancement, the external radiologist had found it difficult to be conclusive about the pathology or integrity of the implant due to this artefact (Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure 1). With these findings the plastic surgeon was concerned about the possibility of BI-ALCL and asked for the case to be reviewed by the Breast MDT.

The Radio Frequency Identification Device (RFID) causes interference.

MRI Breast demonstrates the blooming artefact due to RFID on T2 sequence (vertical white arrow).

After consulting with a specialist breast radiologist in the MDT, a CT scan and ultrasound were arranged. The CT scan helped to resolve the blooming artefact from RFID (Supplementary Figure 3) and when combined with the MRI, favored the inflammatory costo-chondritis known as Tietze’s syndrome rather than BIA-ALCL. The asymmetrical soft tissue thickening of the intercostal space and focal sclerosis of adjacent medial sternum, with sternal marrow oedema are radiological hallmarks of Tietze’s syndrome (Figure 3, Figure 4, Supplementary Figures 4–6). The ultrasound was to guide steroid injection for the Tietze's syndrome and to further evaluate the suspicious medial enhancement (Figure 4).

Tietze Syndrome and the utility of a CT.

Coronal images of post-contrast CT chest show subtle asymmetrical soft tissue thickening of the right intercostal space and the RFID in the implant (white arrow). There is focal sclerosis of the adjacent medial sternum (black arrow) corresponding to the bone marrow oedema on MRI.

Detection of the breast cancer.

A focal area of enhancement (white arrow) medially abuts the right implant's external capsule, with indeterminate kinetics.

The targeted breast ultrasound found two small irregular masses (Supplementary Figure 7), abutting the implant capsule. It also demonstrated silicone lymphadenopathy consistent with the patient’s history of implant rupture (Supplementary Figure 8). Concerns regarding the nature of the smaller mass, prompted the abandonment of the steroid injections and a fine needle aspiration. The results confirmed breast cancer recurrence.

The MDT concluded there were three separate diagnoses – Tietze’s syndrome, local recurrence of breast cancer within the chest wall and harmless silicone lymphadenopathy. The patient underwent removal of the reconstructive breast implant and partial excision of the posterior capsular mass. Histology showed a recurrent Intraductal Ductal Carcinoma with possible micropapillary component, grade 2. Further imaging demonstrated nonresectable metastatic sternal and spinal bony disease for which endocrine and radiotherapy were prescribed. Her Tietze’s syndrome improved with NSAIDs.

Discussion

This case highlights the complexities of diagnosis in a patient presenting with medial chest wall discomfort following post-mastectomy reconstruction with an implant. While the patient’s pain was due to Tietze’s syndrome, metastatic cancer was discovered on investigation.

Tietze’s syndrome is an uncommon benign inflammatory condition characterized as unilateral localized, painful mass, often at the second or third costal cartilages.1 The etiology of Tietze’s is unclear and diagnosis should be made after excluding other differentials.1 Ultrasound may demonstrate the soft tissue swelling, but MRI more clearly demonstrates soft tissue thickening and oedema with associated sternal bone marrow oedema, as was seen in this case.2 Treatment is typically NSAIDS plus ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injections into the affected intercostal space.2

Historical concerns about breast reconstruction obscuring cancer recurrence were laid to rest in the early 2000s3 but studies examining this question were performed at a time when CT scanning was the default modality, implants contained no metal foreign bodies, implant-related ALCL and SCC were unknown, and MRI was not widely used. Times have changed.

The implementation of RFID in human breast implants was triggered by a perceived need to enhance implant tracking, which has theoretical benefits.4 RFIDs in Motiva implants enable plastic surgeons to access information in the instance of implant recalls.5 While labeled “MRI safe”, RIFDs contain metal and as our case demonstrates, can affect the utility of MRI imaging. While “triple assessment” (clinical assessment, US or mammogram and FNA or core biopsy) is the standard for evaluating possible recurrence in reconstructed breasts, MRI is often used as a second line investigation, with a negative MRI typically more helpful than a positive one.6, 7, 8 However, in this case, the artifact from the RFID posed challenges in assessing recurrence, implant integrity, and took multi-modality imaging techniques to achieve a clearer picture.

In recent years plastic surgeons have become hyperaware of the risks of BIA-ALCL and more recently, BIA-SCC but may not be as conscious of other pathologies.9,10 This case represents the first report of a patient with both Tietze’s syndrome and breast cancer recurrence. BIA-ALCL or BIA-SCC were differential diagnoses and silicone lymphadenopathy was an incidental finding. It highlights the need for the development of a comprehensive assessment algorithm for investigation of symptoms following modern prosthetic post-mastectomy breast reconstruction.

Conclusion

The key lesson for plastic surgeons is to be aware of the limitations of MRI in the reconstructed breast with RFID implants, the need for multi-modality imaging in Motiva implants when recurrence is suspected, and to have an awareness of Tietze’s syndrome. The case highlights the need for a comprehensive algorithm for symptoms following modern prosthetic post-mastectomy breast reconstruction.
