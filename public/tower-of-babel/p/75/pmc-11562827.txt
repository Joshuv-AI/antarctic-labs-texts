Precision DNA methylation typing via hierarchical clustering of Nanopore current signals and attention-based neural network
Authors: Qi Dai, Hu Chen, Wen-Jing Yi, Jia-Ning Zhao, Wei Zhang, Ping-An He, Xiao-Qing Liu, Ying-Feng Zheng, Zhuo-Xing Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDecoding DNA methylation sites through nanopore sequencing has emerged as a cutting-edge technology in the field of DNA methylation research, as it enables direct sequencing of native DNA molecules without the need for prior enzymatic or chemical treatments. During nanopore sequencing, methylation modifications on DNA bases cause changes in electrical current intensity. Therefore, constructing deep neural network models to decode the electrical signals of nanopore sequencing has become a crucial step in methylation site identification. In this study, we utilized nanopore sequencing data containing diverse DNA methylation types and motif sequence diversity. We proposed a feature encoding method based on current signal clustering and leveraged the powerful attention mechanism in the Transformer framework to construct the PoreFormer model for identifying DNA methylation sites in nanopore sequencing. The model demonstrated excellent performance under conditions of multi-class methylation and motif sequence diversity, offering new insights into related research fields.

Introduction

DNA methylation is a crucial chemical modification process in biology [1, 2], involving the addition of methyl groups to bases of DNA molecules [3], such as 4-methylcytosine (4mC), 5-methylcytosine (5mC), and 6-methyladenine (6 mA) [4]. DNA methylation plays important biological roles in maintaining the precision of gene expression, cell specialization, genetic stability, and environmental adaptation [1]. Studying DNA methylation helps deepen our understanding of gene regulation, developmental processes, and disease mechanisms [5]. In recent years, single-molecule sequencing technologies such as Oxford Nanopore sequencing have emerged as frontier tools in DNA methylation research [6]. The uniqueness of this technology lies in its ability to directly sequence native DNA molecules without the need for any prior enzymatic or chemical treatment, while also detecting methylation modification signals on DNA bases. This provides us with an unprecedented perspective to delve into the biological phenomenon of DNA methylation.

During nanopore sequencing, DNA molecules generate continuous time-series electrical signals (also known as “squiggles [7]”), and training deep neural network models on these electrical signals is crucial for identifying DNA bases and methylation. Such data also constitute an important practical application scenario in deep learning research and applications. Currently, several DNA methylation identification models based on nanopore sequencing have been published. Among them, Nanopolish [8] and signalAlign [9] utilize hidden Markov models to identify DNA 5mC methylation, while mCaller [10] employs a neural network classifier to identify DNA 6 mA methylation. Additionally, DeepSignal [11] uses a convolutional neural network (CNN) model to identify DNA 5mC methylation, DeepSignal-plant [12] utilizes a bidirectional long short-term memory network model to identify plant DNA 5mC methylation, and DeepMod [13] employs a long short-term memory network (LSTM) model to identify DNA 5mC and 6 mA methylation. In addition to the publicly available software, Oxford Nanopore’s official basecalling software can also detect single-base methylation at the read level, but it still requires additional tools for genome-wide DNA methylation statistics. However, this software does not disclose algorithm details and lacks interfaces for customized retraining.

However, there is still room for improvement in the detection of DNA methylation sites using nanopore sequencing in several aspects, including: (i) Compared to identifying single-type methylation on fixed motifs ( e.g., 5mC at CpG, and 6 mA at GATC), it is necessary to be able to handle multiple types of methylation and different motif environments; (ii) Existing open-source research models typically rely on third-party current signal mapping tools (such as Tombo’s re-squiggle [14] and Nanopolish’s eventalign [8]), lacking the ability to directly read and process raw current signals; (iii) Most existing open-source research models usually only extract the mean value of the current signal for individual bases, failing to fully consider the diversity and hierarchical features of the signals; (iv) In recent years, neural network models based on attention mechanisms [15] have achieved significant success in various fields such as natural language processing [16]. In the field of biological sciences, such models have also been successfully applied to sequence processing and single-cell analysis (such as Geneformer [17], scBERT [18], scHybridBERT [19], scFormer [20], scGPT [21]), among other applications. However, the application of attention-based neural network models for DNA methylation recognition in nanopore sequencing is seldom reported.

Therefore, we have developed the PoreFormer framework, which integrates raw current signal input with neural networks based on attention mechanisms for DNA methylation site identification tasks. PoreFormer does not rely on third-party current signal mapping tools; its internal processing module can directly extract the raw current signals of sequencing reads. Subsequently, the framework employs clustering methods to extract richer hierarchical features from the current signal pileup and combines neural networks based on attention mechanisms to perform DNA methylation site classification. This study demonstrates that PoreFormer exhibits superior identification performance in diverse DNA methylation types (including 4mC, 5mC, 6 mA, etc.) and a variety of genome motifs (a total of 46) [22]. Furthermore, we also investigated the impact of factors such as genomic motif types, sequencing depth, current clustering number, and window size on the performance of PoreFormer.

Results

The summary of the PoreFormer algorithm

The proposed PoreFormer framework consists primarily of two main components: current signal processing (Fig. 1A) and neural network models (Fig. 1C). The current signals outputted by nanopore sequencing are stored in fast5 files on a per-read basis. The unprocessed raw current signals (squiggle) are sequential numerical vectors, requiring formatting for conversion into feature vectors that neural network models can process. To address this, we provide processing scripts within the PoreFormer framework to generate input feature vectors required by neural networks from the original fast5 files (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Illustration of the PoreFormer framework. (A) PoreFormer’s current signal extraction and processing include: (i) basecalling of sequencing data using guppy; (ii) extraction of current signals for each basecalled base, averaging them in units of every 5 signals; (iii) pileup stacking of current signals from multiple reads at the same genomic position, and (iv) feature extraction based on hierarchical clustering of the stacked current signals. (B) PoreFormer feature encoding involves averaging and standard deviation calculation of the current signal values within each category after hierarchical clustering. (C) PoreFormer model framework.

First, at the single-read level, we take reads as units from the fast5 files, where each read’s squiggle signal values are stored (in the Raw Group). After basecalling, the Guppy software provides a move table, which stores the specific positions of the current signal transitions from one base to the next during the basecalling process. With this information, we can segment the raw current signals corresponding to each base in every read (Fig. 1A-1). Because the speed at which each base passes through the nanopore is not uniform, there is significant fluctuation in the lengths of the current signal corresponding to each base. To obtain uniformly sampled current signals for all bases, we fold and average the current signal for each base based on the event length provided by the Guppy software (which is 5 in our version of Guppy 4.5.4, equivalent to dividing the current signal corresponding to each base into N/5 parts). Thus, we obtain five equally long current signal values for each base (Fig. 1A-2, Supplementary Fig. S1). This approach yields more information compared to Nanopolish eventalign and Tombo re-squiggle tools, which only provide the mean current signal value for each base.

Next, at the reference genome level, after aligning the sequencing reads to the reference genome, we collect all reads that can be aligned to fixed positions in the genome. Subsequently, we pileup the processed current signals based on events (Fig. 1A-3). Unlike other existing models that only take the mean and standard deviation of the piled-up current signals, to acquire multi-level current signal information, we perform hierarchical clustering on the piled-up current signals based on five positions of events, and then calculate the mean and standard deviation for each category of hierarchical clustering (Fig. 1A-4). Additionally, while existing models require input such as the mean and standard deviation of current signals, motif sequences, and quality value, the PoreFormer model only requires the features obtained from the current signals (Fig. 1B).

At the neural network model level, unlike other published software that typically employs CNN or LSTM neural networks, we adopted the attention mechanism of the popular Transformer deep learning framework (Fig. 1C). Specifically, we first process the input data using a learnable embedding layer. To retain the positional information of the input data, we utilize relative positional encoding in the embedding layer and add it to the final input data. We utilize multi-head attention layers (with a number of heads = 8) to process the data, generating corresponding query matrices, key matrices, and value matrices. These matrices are used to compute the required attention matrix based on the query matrix and key matrix. Subsequently, the processed data is obtained through the value matrix and attention matrix. Following a residual connection, the processed data is added to the original data and then normalized using a layer normalization layer to standardize processing, enhancing the training speed and convergence of the network while avoiding gradient vanishing and overfitting. We employ three fully connected layers to extract deeper features of the data. Each layer undergoes residual connections, and before obtaining the final model output, another layer normalization step is performed. Dropout operations with a dropout rate of 0.6 are applied at every layer of the network to randomly deactivate a certain proportion of neurons in the preceding layer, retaining their weights but no longer updating them in subsequent steps, further preventing overfitting and enhancing the model’s generalization capability. Finally, we use the Softmax activation function to generate probability scores for each category, selecting the category with the highest probability as the final prediction of the model.

Analyzing the raw current signal changes induced by DNA methylation bases

To investigate the variations in different types of DNA methylation across diverse sequence contexts, we utilized nanopore sequencing data from seven bacterial species (Supplementary Table S1) collected by Alan et al [22]. These seven species encompass a total of 46 unique methylation motifs, including 28 motifs for 6 mA, seven motifs for 4mC, and 11 motifs for 5mC, totaling 274 636 methylated genomic loci (Supplementary Table S2). Alan et al. conducted sequencing of native DNA and methylation-free DNA (whole-genome amplification, WGA) samples from these seven bacterial species using the R9.4 flow cell on the MinION platform, achieving an average coverage of 175 × .

Utilizing this dataset encompassing diverse methylation types and motif diversity, we examined the variations in electrical signals surrounding different DNA methylation types and motifs. Leveraging PoreFormer’s electrical signal extraction and processing module, we extracted the electrical feature values surrounding each methylated base (−7 bp, +6 bp) to illustrate the distributional changes of electrical feature signals around methylated bases. At different levels of electrical signal extraction: 1) employing PoreFormer’s electrical signal extraction and processing module to obtain the mean electrical signal for each base per read, similar to the electrical signal distribution demonstrated by Alan et al. [22] in their paper, we similarly observed significant variations in the mean electrical signal near methylated bases compared to distal bases (Fig. 2A); 2) utilizing PoreFormer’s unique event position-encoded electrical signal feature extraction, we also observed significant variations in the mean electrical signal near methylated bases compared to distal bases (Fig. 2B); 3) after hierarchical clustering of PoreFormer’s unique event position-encoded electrical signal features using k-means, we similarly observed significant variations in the mean electrical signal near methylated bases compared to distal bases (Fig. 2C-D, Supplementary Fig. S4). Additionally, we noted that the perturbation width and amplitude of the electrical features varied depending on the motif and methylation type (Fig. 2A-B, Supplementary Fig. S2-S3).

Next, to comprehensively illustrate the differences in electrical signals between different methylation types and methylation motifs, we conducted t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) analysis on the electrical feature values of 274 636 methylated sites spanning 14 base pairs before and after each methylation site (−7 bp, +6 bp) (Fig. 2E). We observed that methylated motifs belonging to the same methylation type typically clustered together. Furthermore, compared to features extracted by Nanopolish, features extracted by PoreFormer resulted in tighter sample clustering and clearer category boundaries (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. S5-S6). Based on these analyses, we infer that the electrical signal differences induced by methylation exhibit significant feature diversity in different sequence contexts. Therefore, classification models can be constructed to identify the types of genomic DNA methylation sites, referred to as methylation typing.

Performance of DNA methylation detection

Based on the feature extraction of the PoreFormer framework, we first compared the performance of the PoreFormer model with the LSTM and CNN deep learning frameworks commonly used in nanopore sequencing DNA methylation site identification models (Supplementary Fig. S7). For the 4mC and 5mC prediction models, we selected features extracted with six clusters and a window size of ±5 bp. For the 6 mA prediction model, we selected features extracted with seven clusters and a window size of ±6 bp. We used the complete hierarchical clustering features of the electrical signals (PoreFormer_full) in the PoreFormer, LSTM, and CNN frameworks, and also extracted the average electrical signal features for each genome base (PoreFormer_mean) for comparison with the PoreFormer framework. We constructed three-classification models for WGA, 4mC, and 5mC samples, and binary classification models for WGA and 6 mA. When comparing the results, we calculated the Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, Specificity, Sensitivity, and AUC metrics for WGA, 4mC, 5mC, and 6 mA as positive sample labels.

From Table 1, Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Table S4, and Fig. 3A, it can be seen that on the nanopore sequencing data of the seven bacteria, the PoreFormer_full model exhibits the highest Accuracy, F1-score, and AUC for WGA, 4mC, 5mC, and 6 mA. Compared to the other three models, the PoreFormer_full model shows significant advantages in Accuracy and F1-score values. Furthermore, we noted that the LSTM model exhibits higher prediction accuracy for 4mC and 5mC compared to the CNN model, whereas the CNN model demonstrates superior performance in predicting 6 mA accuracy compared to the LSTM model. However, the performance of the PoreFormer_mean model in accuracy is not ideal. In terms of Precision and Specificity, only the LSTM model slightly outperforms the PoreFormer_full model in 5mC (by 0.0065 and 0.0081, respectively) and 6 mA (by 0.0122 and 0.0162, respectively) predictions; while in Recall and Sensitivity, only LSTM performs slightly better than the PoreFormer_full model for WGA predictions (by 0.0021 and 0.0162, respectively). A comprehensive evaluation shows that the PoreFormer_full model performs more comprehensively across all performance metrics. This indicates that the attention-based PoreFormer model, combined with hierarchical clustering feature extraction of electrical signals, exhibits significant advantages in the detection of DNA methylation sites.

Exploring the impact of cluster number and window size on detection accuracy

As PoreFormer employs the k-means algorithm to cluster the squiggle signals of genomic position and subsequently extracts windows containing multiple positions upstream and downstream of methylated sites to achieve methylation detection, we investigated the impact of the number of clusters and the genome window size on PoreFormer’s performance. In our experiments, we varied the number of clusters from 2 to 8 and also varied the genome window size from 9 to 15 bp. According to the results in Fig. 4A, it can be observed that the prediction accuracy of the PoreFormer model gradually increases with an increase in the number of clusters and eventually stabilizes. For 4mC/5mC, with the window size remaining constant (±5 bp), the prediction accuracy is 0.92 when the number of clusters is 2. However, when the number of clusters is increased to 6, the prediction accuracy improves to 0.94, with a recall of 0.92. Similarly, for 6 mA, with the window size remaining constant (±6 bp), the prediction accuracy is 0.94 when the number of clusters is 2. However, when the number of clusters is increased to 7, the prediction accuracy significantly improves to 0.97, with a recall of 0.97.

If we keep the number of clusters constant, we can also observe that the performance of the prediction model continues to improve with an increase in the genome window size. For 4mC/5mC, when the number of clusters is 6, as the window gradually expands, the accuracy increases from 0.93 to 0.94. For 6 mA, when the number of clusters is 7, as the window size gradually increases, the accuracy improves from 0.96 to 0.97. Compared to the effect of the number of clusters, the impact of window size on prediction efficacy is relatively minor.

In conclusion, with the increasing number of clusters and window size, the predictive performance of the model will continue to improve. For 4mC/5mC, the model performs best when the number of clusters is 6 and the window size is ±5 bp. For 6 mA, the model performs exceptionally well when the number of clusters is 7 and the window size is ±6 bp.

Discrimination of multiple type DNA methylation among methylation motifs

To further explore the predictive performance of PoreFormer across different motifs, we systematically analyzed the predictive accuracy of each motif under different combinations of cluster numbers and genome window sizes, as illustrated by the cluster heatmap in Fig. 4B (see Supplementary Table S6 for specific data). All motifs can be categorized into three groups based on their predictive accuracy: high, moderate, and low. Except for BA_GGAT4mCC and CP_5mCCGG motifs, which exhibit relatively lower accuracy, the majority of motifs have accuracy scores above 0.8. Additionally, it can be observed from the horizontal axis that as the cluster numbers and genome window sizes increase, the data feature dimensions also increase, leading to an enhancement in accuracy.

To investigate the influence of different motif quantities on predictive accuracy, we further analyzed the relationship between the number of motif sites in the genome and their corresponding predictive accuracy, as shown in Fig. 4C (see Supplementary Table S6). The results indicate that in the dataset we utilized, the smallest motif quantity is for HP_GT6 mAC, with a total of 159 sites (PoreFormer predictive accuracy: 0.9874), while the largest motif quantity is for MH_G6 mATC, with a total of 40 841 sites (PoreFormer predictive accuracy: 0.9876). Furthermore, we computed the correlation between the quantity of all motifs and their predictive accuracy in PoreFormer, revealing a Pearson coefficient of only 0.177.

Evaluation of the data coverage effect on methylation detection

PoreFormer employs the k-means algorithm for clustering analysis of current signals, where the sequencing coverage of methylation sites influences the mean and standard deviation of signals in each cluster. We conducted a subinterval statistical analysis of predictive accuracy with a 25-step interval based on the sequencing coverage of each methylation site, according to the PoreFormer predictions for each bacterial strain (see Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table S6). The results indicate an overall increase in methylation predictive accuracy across the seven bacterial strains as the sequencing coverage of methylation sites increases. This suggests a significant positive correlation between the predictive performance of the PoreFormer model and the sequencing coverage of sites. We observed that, except for the Bacillus amyloliquefaciens bacterium, the Pearson correlation coefficients between the sequencing coverage depth of methylation sites and predictive accuracy exceed 0.7 for the other strains. For the B. amyloliquefaciens bacterium, we observed a decrease in overall predictive accuracy when the sequencing coverage exceeds 200-fold. This decrease is attributed to the presence of fewer than 10 motifs corresponding to sequencing coverage exceeding 200-fold, resulting in increased randomness in predictive outcomes due to the insufficient motif quantity.

Discussion

Seeking novel and comprehensive tools for nanopore sequencing-based DNA methylation site identification, this study utilized nanopore sequencing data encompassing various DNA methylation types and motif sequence diversities. By developing new methodologies for comprehensive processing of raw nanopore sequencing reads’ electrical current signals, and leveraging the powerful attention mechanism within the Transformer framework, we devised PoreFormer, a deep learning model equipped with multi-head attention mechanisms, aimed at learning patterns of DNA methylation from the electrical signals of large-scale, well-annotated multi-strain nanopore DNA sequencing data. Through systematic analyses of PoreFormer regarding sequencing depth at methylation sites, number of electrical signal clusters, genome window size, and strain differences, we offer insights into the application of Transformers in nanopore DNA methylation data analysis.

We comprehensively evaluated the performance of PoreFormer across multiple benchmarks. Specifically: 1) Evaluation was conducted using nanopore DNA sequencing data encompassing genomes of seven bacterial strains, featuring three types of DNA methylation (5mC, 4mC, 6 mA), and covering diversity in 46 motif sequences; 2) PoreFormer was contrasted against LSTM and CNN architectures widely employed in nanopore sequencing DNA methylation identification; 3) Additionally, comparison was made between PoreFormer and the existing nanopore sequencing DNA methylation identification model, DeepSignal2. Notably, we utilized multiple metrics including accuracy, macro F1 score, and confusion matrix to assess the classification performance of DNA methylation identification methods, ensuring fair comparison among different approaches. The comprehensive evaluation from various perspectives indicates that PoreFormer exhibits strong generalization performance and robustness.

While our proposed PoreFormer model, based on the Transformer architecture, exhibits the advantages mentioned above, it also has certain limitations. Firstly, although PoreFormer can extract the electrical signal features of each base from sequencing reads, it still employs a folding-style statistical approach to the original electrical signal, which may result in information loss. Future research should aim to design more rational methods for encoding electrical signals. Secondly, due to limitations in data availability, PoreFormer has only been trained on 46 methylation motifs representing 7 bacterial strains. Future studies have the opportunity to expand the scope of DNA methylation recognition at both the single-molecule and genome-wide levels by synthesizing random-mer motifs artificially. Thirdly, With rapid advancements in Oxford Nanopore technology, including the transition from the R9 to the more accurate R10 chip, and the shift in data formats from fast5 to pod5, future versions of PoreFormer will need to be updated to accommodate new sequencing chemistries and file formats. Additionally, in terms of the model framework, the network structure currently employed by PoreFormer is relatively simple, and it may be beneficial to consider incorporating the latest Transformer architectures to further enhance performance. In the future, after addressing the aforementioned limitations, we plan to further improve PoreFormer to accurately identify methylation at the single-molecule level in random-mer mode, including both DNA and RNA.

Methods

Datasets

The collection of bacterial samples and nanopore library preparation and sequencing were described by Alan et al. [22] The dataset we utilized includes seven bacterial strains (B. amyloliquefaciens, Bacillus fusiformis, C. perfringens, E. coli, H. pylori, M. hu

ngatei, N. gonorrhoeae), all of which were sequenced for both native and WGA samples. Sequencing was performed using R9.4 and R9.4.1 MinION flow cells, with sequencing runs conducted on MinKNOW software (versions 1.5.12 and 1.10.11). The specific sequencing details refer to the paper by Alan et al. [22] More information of these bacterial strains can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Sequencing reads and current signal processing

The nanopore sequencing reads were base-called using ONT Guppy Software (v.4.5.4), with the ‘—fast5_out’ parameter enabled to save the move table. The move table captures the starting and ending positions of the current signal timing samples for each base-called base within the reads. Leveraging the move table data, we devised a script within the PoreFormer framework to gather and arrange the current signal samples for each base pair, aggregating and averaging them based on a 5-length window.

Base called reads were mapped to corresponding references using minimap2 [25] (v2.15-r905 with ‘--secondary=no -ax map-ont’ option). Reads were separated by strand according to the initial alignment, and both groups were processed as forward strand reads by mapping reverse strand reads on the reverse complement of the reference genome using minimap2. Supplementary and reverse strand alignments were then filtered out with samtools (v.1.3, flags 2048 and 16). For each mapped reads, we filtered out the insertion and deletion parts of the bases, and only retained the matched bases (including match and mismatch).

For every position in the genome, we first pileup the mapped reads. Subsequently, utilizing the read mappings and the aggregated current signals of the base pairs, we compute the average value (5-dimensional) across the signal positions. Moreover, the five current positions of different events were clustered using the k-means algorithm from the Python package, we compute the mean value and standard deviation for each cluster category across the five current signal positions (m × 2 × 5 dimensions, where m represents the predetermined number of clustering algorithm categories). This procedure enables the convenient integration and concatenation of current signal features to generate the final model input with the dimension size ‘N × 2 × m × 5’, N represents the window size, m represents the number of clusters for the five current positions across different events, 2 indicates the mean and standard deviation of the clustered data, and 5 corresponds to the five current positions from the different events.

DNA methylation motif detection

Bacterial epigenomes are highly motif-driven, with over 95% of occurrences of a methylation sequence motif being methylated [26, 27, 28]. In line with the methodology outlined in the study by Alan et al. [22], we utilized the results of motif identification for 4mC, 5mC, and 6 mA methylation across seven bacterial strains’ genome sequences (Supplementary Table S2). In brief, initially employed MEME [29] software for preliminary motif identification, followed by a refinement process utilizing differential electrical signal information obtained from nanopore sequencing of native and WGA samples to further enhance the initially identified motifs by MEME.

PoreFormer model framework

The Transformer architecture, based on multi-head attention, is adept at capturing long-term dependencies. With Oxford Nanopore DNA methylation current signal, which comprises time series sequences, the Transformer architecture can be applied directly, eliminating the need for manual feature engineering or preprocessing. This enables the model to grasp the relationships between signals more effectively, thereby enhancing its performance in the task. The multi-head attention mechanism enables the model to attend to various parts of the input sequence simultaneously, thereby enhancing its ability to comprehend both local and global features. In the context of DNA methylation tasks, this facilitates a deeper understanding of correlations and patterns across different genomic regions. Thus, we have devised a transformer framework based on a multi-layer, multi-head attention mechanism, termed PoreFormer (Fig. 1).

Model training and testing

In order to effectively evaluate the efficiency of the models, this study employed ten-fold cross-validation method during the construction of PoreFormer, LSTM, and CNN models. The basic approach involved randomly shuffling the data and then dividing it into ten equal-sized subsets. Nine of these subsets were selected as training sets, while the remaining one subset served as the testing set. Through ten iterations, predictions were made for all samples, enabling comprehensive evaluation of the entire dataset. This method helps mitigate errors that may arise during sample partitioning.

Running DeepSignal2

We utilized DeepSignal2, a deep learning-based method for methylation detection (https://github.com/PengNi/deepsignal2), to conduct a comparative analysis of its detection performance on 5mC with PoreFormer. Initially, the raw sequences underwent re-squiggle processing, aligning the raw signal to the reference sequence using Tombo (v1.5) with parameters ‘corrected-group’ set to ‘RawGenomeCorrected_000’ and ‘basecall group’ set to ‘Basecall_1D_000’. Subsequently, we employed the pre-trained model (https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1F8tImt4aMC4HPznhjczUdMJ64mre8dG9) of DeepSignal2 to detect 5mC methylation and calculate methylation frequency.

Analysis of bacterial evolutionary relationship

The 16S rDNA sequences of B. amyloliquefaciens, B. fusiformis, C. perfringens, E. coli, H. pylori, M. hungatei, and N. gonorrhoeae were aligned using ClustalW [30] in BioEdit software. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using Molecular & Evolution Genetic Analysis software version 7 (MEGA7 [31]) through Maximum-Likelihood test. The reliability of the tree was assessed using Bootstrap values with 1000 replications. Subsequently, the strain names and chromosome lengths, gene distributions, methylation site distributions, and site coverages were mapped using the RCircos [32] package. Given that some bacteria lack 4mC or 5mC modifications, this study solely focuses on predicting 6 mA modifications across species. Evolutionary relationships are combined, and datasets are constructed for training purposes.
