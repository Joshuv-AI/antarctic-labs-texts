scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq reveal the characteristics of macrophage copper metabolism and establish a risk signature in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Xiling Liu, Zixuan Huang, Huijiao Wang, Jialu Wang, Haiyan Kang, Jianhua Lu, Huimin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a prevalent malignancy with an urgent need for improved prognostic stratification and treatment-response prediction. This study aimed to explore a macrophage copper metabolism-associated prognostic model and to investigate the relationship between this risk model and the tumor immune microenvironment.MethodsThe FindClusters function was used to analyze cell clusters, and CellChat and CellPhoneDB/LIANA were employed for cell-cell communication analysis. Copper metabolism-related genes were sourced from the MSigDB database. A prognostic risk model was established using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis and multivariate Cox regression analysis, and a nomogram was constructed by integrating the prognostic model with clinicopathological factors. Additional analyses were performed to map the seven model genes in single-cell data, assess model uncertainty and robustness, evaluate macrophage/copper/cuproptosis-related transcriptional programs, and examine the correlations between risk score, immune infiltration and predicted drug sensitivity.ResultsUsing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, we identified four macrophage subpopulations. Macrophages with high SPP1 expression showed close interaction with T cell populations and were associated with copper ion metabolism. By incorporating 141 copper metabolism-related genes and using The Cancer Genome Atlas Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) cohort, we constructed a seven-gene risk prediction model. Additional single-cell mapping showed that the model genes were detectable in the HCC single-cell dataset and showed a macrophage-associated expression pattern. The model showed moderate prognostic discrimination in TCGA-LIHC, whereas its external performance was heterogeneous and remained evaluable across external cohorts, with performance varying among datasets. Immune and mechanism-related analyses suggested that the risk signature was associated with macrophage-related infiltration, copper metabolism and cuproptosis-related transcriptional programs. Drug sensitivity analysis nominated Daporinad as a computationally predicted candidate compound, supporting Daporinad as a pharmacogenomic candidate for follow-up investigation.ConclusionsBy integrating scRNA-seq and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, we constructed a macrophage copper metabolism-associated prognostic signature for HCC. The risk score was associated with survival, immune microenvironment features and predicted drug response, providing a transcriptomic framework for risk stratification and therapeutic hypothesis generation.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq), copper metabolism, prognostic signature

Highlight box

Key findings

• Using single-cell sequencing data, we identified four macrophage subpopulations. Macrophages with high SPP1 expression showed close interaction with T cell populations and were associated with copper ion metabolism. A seven-gene macrophage copper metabolism-associated risk signature was constructed and further evaluated using single-cell gene mapping, model-robustness assessment, immune-infiltration correlation analysis and drug-sensitivity exploration.

What is known and what is new?

• Abnormal copper metabolism can trigger the development and progression of many tumors, yet the role of macrophage-specific copper metabolism genes in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) prognosis remains poorly understood.

• In this study, we first analyzed macrophage subpopulations associated with copper metabolism using single-cell RNA sequencing data, and established a prognostic model based on seven macrophage copper metabolism-related genes, and explored the connections between this signature and the tumor immune microenvironment, chemotherapy sensitivity, and molecular docking.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Characterization based on macrophage copper metabolism-associated genes may provide a useful approach for prognostic stratification and hypothesis generation in HCC. Follow-up studies should further validate its predictive capability in large-scale prospective cohorts and experimentally explore the molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets of the identified genes.

Introduction

Liver cancer ranks as the sixth most common cancer globally and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths, characterized by insidious onset and rapid progression. Primary liver cancer comprises a heterogeneous group of malignant tumors, of which hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most prevalent type (1). HCC has high incidence and mortality rates due to its high heterogeneity and complex molecular mechanisms. Although there are several treatments for HCC, such as surgery, transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE), targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint therapy, only a few patients can benefit from them due to drug resistance and chemotherapy toxicity (2,3). Therefore, it is necessary to reveal the underlying mechanism and establish an efficient risk model with sensitive predictive capabilities for monitoring and intervening during the disease course for HCC patients.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in tumor initiation, maintenance, progression and anti-tumor treatment response. Tumor cells regulate the key components of TME, such as endothelial cells, stromal cells, immune cells, and related cytokines, to establish a conducive TME for tumor initiation and development (4-7). Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) comprise the largest proportion of immune cells in the TME and modulate cancer development by secreting various cytokines and influencing other immune cells (8-10). Notably, extensive research indicates that metabolic processes play a vital role in regulating macrophage polarization, and the metabolic phenotype of macrophages undergoes reprogramming, significantly affecting their biological behavior in HCC (11,12). TAMs have been found to enhance tumor glycolysis and progression by upregulating circMRCKα in HCC cells (13). A recent study also suggests that TMEM147 can activate M2 macrophage polarization by regulating cholesterol homeostasis and inhibiting iron dysregulation, promoting invasion and migration of HCC cells (14). However, previous studies on macrophage metabolism in HCC have predominantly focused on glucose metabolism, redox metabolism, lipid metabolism, lactate metabolism, and iron metabolism (15-18), while relatively few studies addressing copper metabolism in macrophages within HCC.

In fact, disruption of copper homeostasis in cancer has been well-documented (19-21). Studies have shown that elevated serum copper levels are associated with poorer prognosis in HCC (22,23). Additionally, numerous basic studies and early clinical trials have found that copper chelators can reduce copper concentration in tumor cells and possess anti-tumor properties (17,19,24). Although copper metabolism has been extensively studied as a prognostic biomarker for HCC (25,26), existing studies approach this from a macroscopic perspective and lack the specificity to identify specific cell populations. Excitingly, a recent notable study has discovered that macrophage activation leads to an increase in Cu (II) ions, which is driven by the upregulation of CD44. These Cu (II) ions can catalyze NAD(H) redox cycling, promoting metabolic changes and subsequent epigenetic alterations that result in an inflammatory state (24). The results of that study provide a novel therapeutic strategy targeting copper metabolism in macrophages to treat diseases associated with macrophage activation.

In this study, we identified a subset of macrophages closely related to copper metabolism by analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from HCC patients. A prognostic risk model was developed based on macrophage copper metabolism-related genes. Furthermore, we analyzed the association between the risk model and the tumor immune environment. These findings will provide new potential strategies for more targeted treatments and improved outcomes in HCC patients. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0604/rc).

Methods

Data collection and processing

scRNA-seq dataset GSE156625 was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/), which includes one healthy liver tissue sample, fourteen HCC tissue samples and adjacent non-tumor tissues. Bulk sequencing data and associated clinical information were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), encompassing 371 HCC samples and 50 adjacent non-tumor tissue samples. Samples with incomplete survival and clinical information were excluded, 363 HCC patients were obtained as a training set. Additionally, GSE14520 and GSE116174 datasets were retrieved from the GEO database for external model validation. After removing duplicate and clinically incomplete samples, 241 and 62 samples were included from these datasets, respectively. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Integration and analysis of single-cell data

We integrated scRNA-seq data using the Seurat package (version 4). Single cells were filtered by requiring at least three cells per gene and a minimum of 300 genes per cell. Mitochondrial content was calculated using the PercentageFeatureSet function. Filtering parameters were set to include cells with mitochondrial content less than 15%, and more than 1,000 UMIs per cell, resulting in 54,485 cells for further analysis. The FindVariableFeatures function identified the top 2,000 highly variable genes (Figure S1A). Gene scaling and PCA dimensionality reduction were performed using the ScaleData function, the harmony algorithm was used to remove batch effects, and set dim =20, resolution =0.5. We selected marker genes using the FindAllMarkers function and the COSG R package. Cell types were annotated using marker genes from the CellMarker database (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/) and previous studies (27-33). Cell-cell communication was analyzed with the CellChat R package, retaining receptor-ligand pairs with high interaction scores. Additionally, CellPhoneDB analysis was performed using the LIANA package to further validate and complement the interaction profiles identified by CellChat. Cluster Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was visualized with the ClusterGVis R package. In addition, gene set scoring of T cell populations was performed using the AddModuleScore function. Furthermore, the same function was applied to score copper metabolism-related genes in macrophage subsets. Based on COSG results, we scored single-cell subpopulation gene sets in The Cancer Genome Atlas-Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) patient expression matrix using gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and conducted survival analysis by grouping samples according to the median GSVA score.

Identification of copper metabolism-related macrophage genes

A total of 141 copper metabolism-related genes were obtained from the MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) (Table 1). Expression data for copper metabolism genes and macrophage subtype marker genes were extracted from TCGA-LIHC. Pearson correlation analysis was performed, and genes with P value <0.05 and |r|>0.5 were defined as candidate copper metabolism-related macrophage genes.

Construction and evaluation of prognostic risk model

To identify genes associated with prognosis, 363 samples from the TCGA-LIHC dataset were utilized as the training set. We employed the glmnet R package for LASSO-Cox regression to eliminate collinear genes. Genes selected from least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression were included in multivariate Cox regression analysis, and the risk model was constructed using the following formula: risk score = gene exp1 × β1 + gene exp2 × β2 + ... + gene expression n × βn. Patients were stratified into high and low-risk groups based on a threshold (median), and Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to plot survival curves. The log-rank test was used to assess significant differences. Visualization of survival differences and states for each patient was performed using the “survminer” and “ggrisk” packages. Additionally, the “timeROC” package in R software was used to plot receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the performance of the risk score in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) of HCC patients. Furthermore, we randomly sampled 50% of patients from the TCGA-LIHC cohort as the internal validation set, and the GSE14520 and GSE116174 datasets served as independent external validation sets to validate the utility of the risk model.

To further characterize the cellular distribution of genes retained in the prognostic risk model, the expression patterns of the selected genes were examined across major cell types and macrophage subsets in the HCC scRNA-seq dataset. For each cell type or macrophage subset, average expression levels and detection rates were summarized. To reduce pseudo-replication arising from treating individual cells as independent observations, comparisons among macrophage subsets were additionally performed using sample-level aggregated expression profiles. Furthermore, a coefficient-weighted signature score derived from the bulk RNA-seq prognostic model was calculated to evaluate transcriptional concordance among macrophage subsets rather than to infer prognosis at the single-cell level.

To further address model reliability, Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) were calculated in TCGA-LIHC and external validation cohorts. Bootstrap resampling was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Leave-one-gene-out sensitivity analysis was performed by recalculating the risk score after removing each signature gene in turn, thereby evaluating the contribution of individual genes to model discrimination.

Subtype clinical feature analysis

To validate the stability of the model, patients were grouped according to age (age ≤65, >65 years), gender (female, male), histological grade (G1–G2, G3–G4), and stage (stage I–II, stage III–IV). Kaplan-Meier survival curves for high- and low-risk groups in each subtype were plotted using the “survminer” package. A significance level of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Construction of nomogram

In order to further optimize the predictive performance of the risk score model, we conducted multivariable Cox regression analysis integrating clinical and pathological features of patients (including age, gender, race, histological grade, and stage) with risk features, and variables with P<0.05 were incorporated into a nomogram using the “cph” function. Additionally, calibration curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year periods were generated using the “rms” R package to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the nomogram. Subsequently, decision curve analysis (DCA) curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year outcomes were plotted using the “ggDCA” package to evaluate the clinical utility of the nomogram.

Immunological microenvironment analysis

The R software Estimation of Stromal and Immune cells in Malignant Tumors using Expression data (ESTIMATE) arithmetic was utilized to compute overall stroma level (StromalScore), immunocyte infiltration (ImmuneScore), and combination (ESTIMATEScore) of samples in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. Additionally, the Cell-Type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm (http://cibersort.stanford.edu/) was employed to assess the immune cell infiltration status of patients, and the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) was used to evaluate differences in the immune escape capability of tumor cells. Furthermore, we compared the expression levels of 34 common immune checkpoint genes. Differences between high and low-risk groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test, with statistical significance set at P<0.05.

For immune-infiltration analyses, the continuous risk score was matched with CIBERSORT-derived immune-cell fractions in TCGA-LIHC. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between the risk score and 22 immune-cell populations. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple testing. A sensitivity analysis was additionally performed in the subset of samples with CIBERSORT P<0.05.

Drug prediction analysis and molecular docking

Using the “oncoPredict” R package, we predicted the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 198 drugs for HCC patients based on GDSC (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). We then compared the differences in drug sensitivity between high and low-risk group patients using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To assess the binding affinity and interaction patterns between candidate drugs and targets, protein structures of feature genes were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (http://www.rcsb.org/), and the chemical structures of drug active ingredients were downloaded from the PubChem database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pccompound/). Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutodockVina 1.2.2, and the resulting complexes were visualized using PyMol software.

To complement patient-level drug-response prediction, we further examined public cell-line pharmacogenomic data. For GDSC2, cell-line expression data were used to calculate a standardized weighted score based on the genes retained in the model, and correlation analysis was performed between this score and Daporinad response across matched cell lines. Liver-/biliary-related cell lines were analyzed separately when available. CellMiner/NCI60 data were also explored as an additional pan-cancer pharmacogenomic resource. These analyses provided pharmacogenomic support for Daporinad as a prioritized candidate, while experimental assays will be needed for therapeutic confirmation in HCC models.

Enrichment analysis

To evaluate potential biological functional changes between high and low-risk groups, we downloaded Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) gene sets from the MSigDB. The GSEA software (version 3.0) was utilized to evaluate the pathways and molecular mechanisms associated with high and low-risk groups. Furthermore, for the copper metabolism gene set related to feature genes, enrichment analysis was performed using the H gene set from the Molecular Signatures Database. Enrichment analysis parameters were set with a minimum gene set of 5, maximum gene set of 5,000, and 1,000 permutations. Results with P<0.05 and FDR <0.25 were considered statistically significant.

To strengthen the biological interpretation of the risk model, macrophage- and copper-related transcriptional program scores were calculated in TCGA-LIHC. The Macro_C3_SPP1 signature score was derived from marker genes of the SPP1+ macrophage subset. M1 and M2 macrophage scores, a copper metabolism score and a cuproptosis score were calculated from curated gene sets. Spearman correlation analysis was then performed to evaluate the relationships between the risk score derived from genes retained in the prognostic risk model, individual model-retained genes, and these mechanism-related scores.

Validation of protein expression levels of feature genes using the human proteome atlas

To further validate the protein expression levels of feature genes in liver cancer and normal tissues, we obtained immunohistochemistry (IHC) data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA; http://www.proteinatlas.org).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software. Between-group differences were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses were used to evaluate associations between variables. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to evaluate prognostic factors. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve values. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Construction and validation of macrophage subtype-copper metabolism risk score

Given the close association between macrophage subtypes and copper metabolism, we aimed to integrate these factors to construct a risk model. Initially, correlation analysis between 161 marker genes of the Macro_C3_SPP1 macrophages subtype and 141 copper metabolism genes revealed 104 genes with a significant correlation (P value <0.05 and |r|>0.5). Subsequently, these genes were subjected to Lasso-Cox regression analysis to reduce the gene count, resulting in 22 genes (Figure 5A,5B). Finally, after conducting multivariable Cox regression analysis, FTH1, ANXA5, HMOX1, POLR2L, COX6B1, CALM1, and SMS were included in the risk model (Figure 5C). The final risk model equation is as follows: riskscore = −0.25395195 * FTH1 + 0.17660104 * ANXA5 + 0.06942555 * HMOX1 + 0.65606921 * POLR2L − 0.53934226 * COX6B1 + 0.11810478 * CALM1 + 0.58173089 * SMS. Consistent with our sequencing data, HPA results showed that FTH1 and COX6B1 are highly expressed in normal tissues, whereas ANXA5, HMOX1, CALM1, and SMS exhibit elevated expression in liver cancer tissues (Figure 5D). POLR2L was not detected in clinical samples. Interestingly, these seven key genes were found to be closely associated with 25 copper metabolism-related genes (Figure 5E). To further explore the potential biological mechanisms underlying this association, we performed enrichment analysis on these copper metabolism-related genes. The results revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, and protein secretion (Figure 5F), suggesting that dysregulation of copper metabolism may partially mediate the functional roles of these key genes in HCC progression.

To directly connect the single-cell macrophage findings with the seven-gene bulk RNA-seq model, we further mapped FTH1, ANXA5, HMOX1, POLR2L, COX6B1, CALM1, and SMS in the HCC scRNA-seq dataset. All seven genes were detectable in the single-cell dataset. FTH1, HMOX1, and SMS showed prominent expression in the myeloid compartment, and the seven genes were generally detectable across macrophage subsets, supporting a macrophage-associated transcriptional context (Figure S4A,S4B). After aggregation by sample of origin, POLR2L expression was significantly higher in Macro_C3_SPP1 than in Macro_C2_EGR1 (median expression, 1.63 vs. 1.05; FDR =0.022). In contrast, the coefficient-weighted seven-gene score suggested that the full signature reflected a broader macrophage-associated transcriptional state rather than exclusive specificity to the Macro_C3_SPP1 subset (Figure S4C,S4D).

We further examined whether the risk score reflected macrophage- and copper-related transcriptional programs in TCGA-LIHC. The risk score was strongly correlated with the Macro_C3_SPP1 signature score (Spearman’s ρ =0.624, FDR =9.30×10−40), and was also positively correlated with copper metabolism (ρ =0.498, FDR =6.93×10−24) and cuproptosis scores (ρ =0.413, FDR =2.46×10−16) (Figure S5A). Correlation analysis of individual signature genes showed concordant associations with macrophage-, copper metabolism-, and cuproptosis-related scores (Figure S5B). Consistently, the risk score was correlated with several copper-regulatory genes, further supporting a copper-related transcriptional context for the model (Figure S5C). Together, these results indicate that the seven-gene signature is linked to macrophage activation, copper metabolism, and cuproptosis-related transcriptional programs.

To evaluate the prognostic performance of our risk model, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated significantly lower OS in the high-risk group compared with the low-risk group in TCGA-LIHC (P<0.001) (Figure 6A,6B), with AUC values of 0.769, 0.734 and 0.791 for 1, 3 and 5 years, respectively (Figure 6C). Consistent results were observed in subgroup analyses and external validation cohorts (Figures 6D,7A-7C and Figure S6A,S6B). Bootstrap-based analyses further showed moderate discrimination in TCGA-LIHC, with a C-index of 0.707 (95% CI: 0.659–0.755) and 1-, 3- and 5-year time-dependent AUCs of 0.771, 0.734 and 0.791, respectively (Figure S7A,S7B). The external-cohort performance was heterogeneous and overall modest, with C-indices of 0.587 in GSE116174, 0.576 in GSE14520 and 0.517 in ICGC (Figure S7A,S7B). Leave-one-gene-out analysis showed that removing SMS and POLR2L resulted in the largest decreases in C-index (Delta C-index =−0.087 and −0.069, respectively), suggesting that these genes contributed most strongly to model discrimination in TCGA-LIHC (Figure S7C). Together, these analyses strengthen the evidence that the seven-gene model captures reproducible prognostic information, while also defining the datasets in which its performance is strongest. GSEA analysis showed that the high-risk group was enriched for cell cycle and Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis pathways, whereas the low-risk group was enriched for retinol metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, drug metabolism cytochrome P450 and linoleic acid metabolism pathways (Figure S8).

Identification of independent risk factors and nomogram development

To optimize the predictive performance of risk model, we integrated clinical and pathological characteristics with risk score through multivariable Cox regression analysis. The results revealed that the model was an independent prognostic factor for HCC [hazard ratio (HR) =2.51; 95% CI: 1.90–3.30; P<0.001], followed by stage (Figure 8A). Consequently, we constructed a nomogram integrating stage and risk score (Figure 8B). Calibration plots demonstrated close agreement between actual survival time and the predicted values of the nomogram at 1, 3, and 5 years (Figure 8C). Furthermore, DCA indicated higher net benefit of the nomogram than either risk score or stage alone in the evaluated TCGA cohort (Figure 8D). These findings suggest that the nomogram may improve risk stratification in this dataset, supporting its value for future prospective evaluation.

Tumor immune microenvironment analysis

The tumor immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in tumor initiation and progression. Therefore, we further investigated the relationship between risk score and immune microenvironment. The results showed that TIDE scores were significantly higher in high-risk group than in low-risk group (P<0.001) (Figure 9A). Additionally, ESTIMATE algorithm results indicated that the stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score were all higher in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (P<0.001) (Figure 9B). CIBERSORT analysis revealed that activated CD4 memory T cells and NK cells were higher in the low-risk group (P<0.05), while neutrophils and M0 macrophages were significantly elevated in the high-risk group (P<0.05) (Figure 9C). Finally, differential expression analysis of immune checkpoint genes showed significant differences in expression levels between the two groups (P<0.05), including key immune therapy targets such as PDCD1 (PD-1), CD274 (PD-L1), and CTLA4, with all checkpoint expressions higher in the high-risk group (Figure 9D).

We further performed continuous risk score-based correlation analysis with CIBERSORT immune-cell fractions. In all matched TCGA-LIHC samples (n=363), the risk score was positively correlated with Macrophages_M0 (ρ =0.221, FDR =0.000458), activated memory CD4 T cells (ρ =0.207, FDR =0.000808) and neutrophils (ρ =0.149, FDR =0.0247), and negatively correlated with resting mast cells (ρ =−0.161, FDR =0.0152) and resting NK cells (ρ =−0.139, FDR =0.0352) (Figure S9A,S9B). These results were broadly consistent with the high-/low-risk group comparison and support an association between the risk score and macrophage-related immune remodeling. A sensitivity analysis restricted to samples with CIBERSORT P<0.05 showed broadly similar correlation trends, although the reduced sample size limited statistical significance after FDR correction (Figure S9C).

Drug sensitivity assessment and molecular docking

We estimated the IC50 values of 198 chemotherapy drugs or inhibitors to assess the therapeutic implications of the risk model. Predicted IC50 values differed between high- and low-risk groups for multiple compounds, and Daporinad_1248 was among the candidates with lower predicted IC50 values in the high-risk group (Figure 10A). In Daporinad-focused analyses, the high-risk group showed lower predicted Daporinad IC50 than the low-risk group (median IC50, 0.010 vs. 0.014; P=0.001) (Figure S10A). Molecular docking suggested potential binding affinity between Daporinad_1248 and five available signature-gene protein structures, including FTH1, ANXA5, HMOX1, CALM1, and SMS (Figure 10B). To further explore this candidate, we examined public GDSC2 and CellMiner/NCI60 pharmacogenomic resources. Across all matched GDSC2 cell lines with Daporinad response data, the seven-gene weighted score was associated with Daporinad response (Spearman’s ρ =0.263, P<0.001) (Figure S10B). The liver-/biliary-related GDSC2 subset provided an HCC-relevant analysis, although only nine evaluable cell lines were available and the association did not reach statistical significance (Figure S10C). CellMiner/NCI60 analysis was used as an additional pan-cancer pharmacogenomic screen (Figure S10D). Together, the patient-level prediction, docking results, and public cell-line pharmacogenomic data support Daporinad as a prioritized candidate for subsequent HCC-focused experimental testing.

Discussion

HCC, as a highly immunogenic malignancy, is characterized by extensive immune cell infiltration in the TME. Macrophages are a critical component of this microenvironment and have garnered significant attention due to their ability to directly or indirectly regulate key features of malignancies (35,36). Consequently, targeting macrophages has emerged as a viable strategy for cancer immunotherapy (37-39). In recent years, metabolic reprogramming of macrophages has provided new avenues for cancer immunotherapy. As previously mentioned, Solier et al. (24) successfully identified a signaling pathway involving copper metabolism that leads to the expression of pro-inflammatory genes in macrophages. Some studies have also found that elevated copper levels are associated with more aggressive tumor characteristics and poorer prognosis in cancer patients (40,41). These findings all indicate that copper metabolism genes in macrophages play a crucial role in modulating the immune landscape of diseases.

In this study, we obtained eight types of cells including myeloid cells, T/NK cells, B cells, plasma cells, etc. Given the close interaction between the myeloid and T/NK cell populations, we performed a detailed subpopulation analysis of myeloid cells. Notably, we found that a subset of macrophages with high SPP1 expression was strongly associated with copper metabolism processes such as copper detoxification and copper stress response, and subsequent survival analysis showed that this subset was associated with poor prognosis in patients in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that dysregulated copper homeostasis is closely related to the progression of HCC (42,43). Clearly, our findings are in line with these studies. Subsequent cell-cell communication analysis revealed that the SPP1-CD44 axis was the dominant signaling axis between this macrophage subpopulation and the T/NK cell population, with Treg and exhausted T cells being the main signal receivers. In fact, previous research by He et al. also demonstrated that in AFP-positive HCC, SPP1+ macrophages primarily interact with exhausted T cells through the SPP1-CD44 axis, and targeting this axis can impede tumor immune evasion (44). In conclusion, targeting copper metabolism to reprogram macrophages and disrupt the SPP1-CD44 signaling axis may represent a viable strategy to prevent tumor cell escape.

Recent single-cell and spatial studies further support the biological relevance of SPP1+ macrophages in HCC. Fan et al. reported co-localization of cancer stem cells and SPP1+ macrophages in hypoxic regions associated with poor prognosis, while Wang et al. showed that SPP1+ macrophages can promote liver cancer stemness through vitronectin- and CCL15-related signaling crosstalk (45,46). These findings are consistent with our observation that Macro_C3_SPP1 macrophages interact with T/NK-cell subsets through SPP1-related ligand-receptor pairs. Nevertheless, our additional single-cell mapping showed that the seven model genes are macrophage-associated rather than uniformly specific to SPP1+ macrophages.

To better characterize the relationship between this subpopulation and copper metabolism, we constructed a risk prediction model. Eventually, a risk signature of macrophage copper metabolism-related genes consisting of seven genes was established. It comprises two protective genes (FTH1 and COX6B1) and five risk genes (ANXA5, HMOX1, POLR2L, CALM1, and SMS). These genes have been implicated in various biological processes: FTH1 and COX6B1 play critical roles in oxidative stress protection, respectively, while HMOX1 and ANXA5 are associated with inflammatory responses and cellular stress (47,48). It is noteworthy that, although there is currently no direct evidence linking FTH1 with copper metabolism, both FTH1 and HMOX1 are closely related to ferroptosis (49,50). Previous studies have shown that the balance between copper and iron metabolism is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis, and disruption of this balance may lead to the progression of HCC, and they found that low expression of the copper metabolism-related protein family member COMMD10 activates the HIF1α/CP loop, which in turn inhibits ferroptosis, thereby reducing the radiosensitivity of HCC (43). As a result, many current studies focus on the synergistic activation of ferroptosis and cuproptosis to enhance antitumor efficacy (51). Our findings further emphasize that maintaining copper-iron balance could provide a new therapeutic window for cancer treatment. As for COX6B1, it is an important subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV, playing a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial function and cellular energy metabolism, and studies have shown that mutations in COX6B1 lead to mitochondrial dysfunction (52). In particular, mitochondria are a major site for copper ion storage, this suggests that COX6B1 may indirectly influence copper homeostasis in tumors, thereby affecting tumor progression. In conclusion, although the precise mechanisms by which FTH1 and COX6B1 affect copper metabolism and influence HCC progression remain to be elucidated, it is undeniable that our study provides new insights into this field.

The additional mechanism-score analysis provides further transcriptomic support for this interpretation. The risk score was strongly associated with the Macro_C3_SPP1 signature and was positively correlated with copper metabolism and cuproptosis scores. This is compatible with recent reports that copper-related and cuproptosis-related signatures can characterize HCC prognosis and immune microenvironment features, including macrophage polarization-related programs (53,54). Together with the single-cell findings, these data support the interpretation that the seven-gene signature captures macrophage/copper-related transcriptional states in HCC.

Considering the impact of the TME on tumor prognosis, we also investigated the differences in immune cell infiltration between high-risk and low-risk HCC patients. We observed higher levels of activated CD4+ memory T cells and NK cells in the low-risk group, while neutrophils and M0 macrophages predominated in the high-risk group, consistent with current evidence (55-60). Surprisingly, significant differential expression of most checkpoint genes was observed between high and low-risk groups. All immune checkpoints were highly expressed in the high-risk group, including currently approved targets for HCC, such as PDCD1 (PD-1), CD274 (PD-L1), and CTLA4 (61). However, given the suboptimal response rates of existing immune checkpoint inhibitors in most cancer patients, other immune checkpoints may hold promising potential. Of course, there are studies currently underway investigating the role of immune checkpoints such as CD47 and LAG3 in HCC (62,63). Therefore, further research is necessary to explore the viability of inhibitors targeting these checkpoints as prospective HCC antitumor agents.

The immune-infiltration analysis further linked the risk score to the TME. The positive association between risk score and Macrophages_M0, together with the high-risk enrichment of neutrophils and immune-checkpoint genes, suggests that the high-risk group may represent a more inflamed but potentially immunosuppressive microenvironment. Recent liver cancer studies also emphasize that macrophage reprogramming can reshape the immune contexture and influence responses to immune checkpoint blockade (64). Because CIBERSORT is an in silico deconvolution method and the quality-filtered subset was small, spatial or flow-cytometry-based studies will be useful to further validate these immune associations.

Additionally, our drug-sensitivity results provide a pharmacogenomic extension of the risk model. Patient-level oncoPredict analysis suggested that high-risk tumors may have lower predicted IC50 values for several compounds, including Daporinad_1248. Daporinad is a NAMPT inhibitor that can perturb NAD+ metabolism and has shown antitumor activity in experimental models (65-67). Molecular docking further provided structural plausibility, and pan-cancer GDSC2 cell-line analysis showed an association between the seven-gene weighted score and Daporinad response. Although the available liver-/biliary-related cell-line subset was small, these convergent computational and pharmacogenomic observations nominate Daporinad for future testing in risk-score-high HCC cells or macrophage-rich co-culture systems.

As an important aspect of copper metabolism, cuproptosis has recently attracted attention because it links copper accumulation to mitochondrial metabolism and cell death. Copper-dependent toxicity is thought to be particularly relevant in cells relying on oxidative phosphorylation, and copper-rich tumor regions have been associated with metabolic rewiring, extracellular matrix remodeling and immune suppression (68,69). In our analysis, copper metabolism-related genes associated with the seven signature genes were enriched in oxidative phosphorylation and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, and the risk score was positively correlated with a cuproptosis score. These findings suggest that the model may capture a copper-associated metabolic state in HCC. Dissecting the relative contribution of malignant-cell cuproptosis, macrophage metabolic remodeling and broader TME-level copper handling will be an important direction for future mechanistic work.

This study has several limitations. First, although the signature was evaluated in multiple retrospective public datasets, prospective cohorts will be needed to further define its clinical utility. Second, the added single-cell mapping, mechanism-score analysis, immune-deconvolution analysis and public pharmacogenomic exploration strengthen the biological interpretation of the model, but mechanistic experiments are still needed to test direct regulatory relationships. Third, the liver-/biliary-related cell-line subset with available Daporinad response data was small. Future studies should combine spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunostaining, macrophage-HCC co-culture systems and prospective clinical validation to extend these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, we identified a macrophage subpopulation associated with copper metabolism using HCC scRNA-seq data and constructed a seven-gene macrophage copper metabolism-associated prognostic signature using bulk RNA-seq cohorts. The integrated validation analyses support the association of this signature with macrophage-related transcriptional programs, copper metabolism, cuproptosis-related features, immune infiltration and predicted Daporinad sensitivity. These findings strengthen the biological and translational rationale of the signature and provide a basis for prospective clinical evaluation and experimental studies of macrophage copper metabolism in HCC.
