The relationship between anxiety, self-compassion, and well-being in pregnant women
Authors: Abdurrahim Uyanik, Gülten Koç, Fatma Uslu Şahan, Çiğdem Yücel Özçirpan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy involves physiological, psychological, and social changes. Pregnancy-related anxiety can negatively affect maternal mental health, whereas self-compassion and well-being serve as protective factors. This study examined the relationships between pregnancy-related anxiety, self-compassion, and well-being in pregnant women.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 220 pregnant women attending a gynecology outpatient clinic in northern Türkiye. Data were collected using the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale (PrAS), Well-Being in Pregnancy Scale (WIP), and Self-Compassion Scale-Short Form (SCS-SF). Pearson correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were performed.ResultsPregnancy-related anxiety was negatively associated with well-being (r = -.232, p < .01) and self-compassion (r = -.605, p < .01). Regression analyses showed that subdimensions of anxiety and well-being significantly predicted self-compassion, explaining 55.2% of the variance (Adjusted R² = 0.552, p < .001).ConclusionHigher pregnancy-related anxiety is linked to lower self-compassion and well-being, whereas positive psychological resources may buffer these effects. Interventions targeting anxiety reduction and enhancement of self-compassion and well-being are recommended to support maternal mental health.Keywords: Anxiety, Pregnancy, Self-compassion, Well-being

Background

Pregnancy is a period in a woman’s life that involves profound changes not only physiologically, but also emotionally and socially. During this process, women face challenges such as increased responsibilities, physical changes, hormonal fluctuations, and adapting to the role of motherhood. Uncertainty, feelings of loss of control, and concerns about childbirth are among the common psychological consequences of pregnancy-related anxiety [1, 2]. Worldwide, approximately 15–23% of pregnant women experience pregnancy-related anxiety [3, 4]. It has been reported that anxiety levels increase, particularly in the third trimester, and that this is due to factors such as approaching delivery and concerns about fetal health [4]. Understanding these psychological difficulties experienced during pregnancy requires assessing not only anxiety but also the woman’s overall well-being.

Well-being is a multidimensional concept that encompasses not only physical health but also an individual’s psychological and social functioning. It is defined by the World Health Organization as “a state of well-being in which an individual realizes his or her own potential, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively, and is able to make a contribution to his or her community.” [5]. High levels of well-being are associated with better mental balance, strong interpersonal relationships, a positive disposition, and healthier cognitive patterns [6]. In contrast, emotional regulation deficits, anxiety, and prenatal depression have been shown to be associated with maternal and neonatal complications such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and postpartum depression [7]. Therefore, supporting psychological well-being during pregnancy is critically important for both maternal and infant health [8, 9]. At the same time, the mother’s compassionate approach towards herself is also an important factor that supports psychological well-being.

Self-compassion is a learnable emotion regulation strategy that enables individuals to develop a gentle, understanding, and non-judgmental attitude toward themselves in the face of stressful life events [10]. Self-acceptance is known to be associated with many positive psychological variables such as life satisfaction, happiness, and psychological resilience [11]. During pregnancy, high levels of self-compassion have been shown to ease the transition to motherhood and reduce depressive symptoms, anxiety, and sleep problems [12, 13]. In this context, self-compassion is considered a potential resource for maintaining mental health during pregnancy.

In conclusion, this study aims to examine the relationship between anxiety experienced during pregnancy and psychological well-being and self-compassion. Given the negative impacts of frequently observed anxiety levels on well-being during pregnancy, understanding the role of protective psychological resources such as self-compassion is crucial for both mental health services and perinatal care practices. In this context, the findings are expected to contribute to the literature by providing unique insights into monitoring anxiety and self-compassion levels during pregnancy, supporting well-being, and guiding interventions in these areas.

Method

Aim

This study aims to examine the relationship between anxiety experienced during pregnancy and psychological well-being and self-compassion.

Study design

This descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted between October 15, 2024, and January 31, 2025, at the gynecology and obstetrics outpatient clinic of a district state hospital in northern Türkiye. The STROBE checklist was used in the reporting of the study.

Study sample

The study population consisted of pregnant women who visited the gynecology and obstetrics outpatient clinic. A total of 220 participants were selected using simple random sampling. The minimum required sample size was calculated based on a similar study by Yüksel and colleagues, and it was determined that 180 participants would be sufficient [14]. The sample size was increased by 20% to account for potential data loss due to dropouts or incomplete responses [15]. Pregnant women aged 18–35, in their 20th to 37th weeks of pregnancy, who volunteered to participate and had no clinically diagnosed maternal or fetal complications such as diabetes, preeclampsia, or fetal anomalies were included in the study. All eligible pregnant women were informed about the purpose, procedures, and confidentiality of the study, and their written informed consent was obtained before participation.

Data sources and instruments

Research data were collected using the Sociodemographic Characteristics Form, the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale, the Well-Being in Pregnancy Scale, and the Self-Compassion Scale Short Form developed by the researchers.

Socio-demographic characteristics form

Developed by the researchers with expert input, this form included nine items assessing age, educational status, employment status, perceived economic status, gestational age, number of living children, desired pregnancy status, planned pregnancy status, and prenatal education status.

Pregnancy-related anxiety scale

The Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale was developed by Brunton and colleagues to assess specific anxieties experienced during pregnancy. Its adaptation into Turkish and validity-reliability study were conducted by Solt Kırca and Kanza Gül [16, 17]. The scale is a 4-point Likert-type scale consisting of 33 items and 9 subscales. Eleven items on the scale are reverse-coded. Scores on the scale range from 33 to 132, with higher scores indicating higher levels of pregnancy-related anxiety. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale in the original study was 0.85, while in this study it was found to be 0.89.

Well-being in pregnancy scale

The Well-Being in Pregnancy Scale was developed by Alderdice and colleagues to assess psychological well-being during pregnancy. Its adaptation into Turkish and validity-reliability study were conducted by Sunay and colleagues [9, 18]. The 6-item Likert-type scale consists of a total of 12 items and has two subscales: “Positive Impact and Satisfaction” (7 items) and “Concerns” (5 items). High scores on the scale are associated with a higher level of well-being. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was calculated as 0.73 in the original study and 0.71 in this study.

Self-compassion scale short form

The scale developed by Neff to assess self-compassion levels was shortened by Raes, Pommier, Neff, and Van Gucht [19, 20]. The validity and reliability study of the scale in Turkish was conducted by Yıldırım and Sarı [21]. The scale consists of a total of 11 items and is unidimensional, featuring a 5-point Likert-type response format. Items 1, 4, 8, 9, 10, and 11 are reverse-coded. High scores on the scale indicate that the participant has a high level of self-compassion. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.89. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was determined to be 0.86.

Data collection

The data collection process was conducted using face-to-face interviews with pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria for the study. Pregnant women were selected from among those attending the gynecology outpatient clinic for follow-up, based on eligibility criteria. Participants were informed verbally and in writing about the purpose and scope of the study, that it was based on voluntary participation, that data would be kept confidential, and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. Written informed consent was obtained from participants who agreed to volunteer. All data collection procedures were conducted in individual interview settings, with participant privacy safeguarded. The scales were explained in a way that participants could understand so they could answer them themselves; the researcher provided guidance when necessary. Completing the forms took approximately 20 min. The collected data was used solely for research purposes and was not linked to any personally identifiable information. The data was collected through face-to-face interviews.

Data analysis

The data obtained within the scope of the research were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were used to describe the sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics of the participants. The reverse-coded items were systematically checked before data analysis, the accuracy of the coding was verified, and the analyses were conducted accordingly. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and all variables were approximately normally distributed.

Pearson correlation analysis was applied to examine the relationships between pregnancy-related anxiety, self-compassion, and well-being during pregnancy. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the predictive effects of pregnancy-related anxiety subdimensions and well-being subscales on self-compassion. A significance level of p < .05 was accepted in all statistical tests. All analyses were interpreted in terms of associations, rather than causal relationships, due to the cross-sectional design of the study.

Strong positive correlations were found between the Pregnancy-related Anxiety Scale (PrAS) and Birth anxiety (r = .651, p < .01) and Body image concerns (r = .742, p < .01). This indicates that pregnancy-related anxiety increases in parallel with birth anxiety and body image concerns. The PrAS also shows significant positive correlations with Attitude toward childbirth (r = .577, p < .01), Concerns about motherhood (r = .645, p < .01), Signs of anxiety (r = .717, p < .01), and Attitudes toward medical staff (r = .566, p < .01). In contrast, significant negative correlations were found between PrAS and the Well-being Scale in Pregnancy (r = -.232, p < .01) and the Self-Compassion Scale Short Form (r = -.605, p < .01). In other words, as pregnancy-related anxiety increases, both general well-being during pregnancy and self-compassion scores decrease. Positive impact and satisfaction (PIS) showed a strong positive correlation with WIP (r = .803, p < .01) and a negative correlation with PrAS (r = − .492, p < .01), supporting the notion that high psychological well-being is inversely related to anxiety. A weak positive relationship was observed between the Concerns scale and PrAS (r = .178, p < .01), while a strong positive relationship was found with WIP (r = .735, p < .01).

A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the effects of pregnancy-related anxiety subdimensions and well-being subscales on self-compassion among pregnant women. The overall model was significant, F(11, 208) = 25.534, p < .001, and explained 55.2% of the variance in self-compassion (Adjusted R² = 0.552). Among the predictors, Birth Anxiety (β = 0.153, p = .004) and Positive Impact & Satisfaction (β = 0.269, p < .001) were positively associated with self-compassion, indicating that higher scores in these domains corresponded to higher self-compassion. In contrast, Body Image Concerns (β = -0.239, p < .001), Attitude Toward Childbirth (β = -0.132, p = .015), Concerns About Motherhood (β = -0.176, p = .003), Signs of Anxiety (β = -0.147, p = .012), and WIP Concerns subscale (β = -0.107, p = .032) were negatively associated with self-compassion. These results suggest that specific aspects of pregnancy-related anxiety and well-being significantly influence self-compassion during pregnancy. Other subdimensions did not significantly predict self-compassion (p > .05) (Table 4).

Discussion

The findings indicate that pregnancy-related anxiety and its subdimensions exhibit significant associations with both well-being and self-compassion. It was observed that as levels of pregnancy-related anxiety increased, levels of well-being and self-compassion decreased; conversely, when self-compassion and well-being were high, pregnancy-related anxiety was relatively lower.

The total pregnancy-related anxiety score was found to be an average of 67.10 ± 14.42. This value is higher than the average of 63.03 ± 16.45 reported by Brunton et al. [22]. In another study, the total PrAS score was found to be 64.40 ± 14.17 [23]. This suggests that the sample in our study experienced higher levels of pregnancy-specific anxiety. It is thought that the reason for the high scores may be due to cultural characteristics, country conditions (e.g. economic difficulties, access to health services), and demographic characteristics of the sample.

In our study, the total Feeling Well During Pregnancy (WIP) score was found to be 49.23 ± 8.74; the Positive Impact and Satisfaction subscale score was 32.38 ± 6.03 and the Concerns subscale score was 16.84 ± 5.30. These values are higher than those found in similar studies in the literature. For example, in the study by Çerçer and Ayar, the total WIP score was reported as 30.01 ± 8.27, Positive Impact and Satisfaction as 14.50 ± 4.69 and Concerns as 15.51 ± 6.01 [24]. Sabancı Baransel and Uçar reported the Wip total score as 29.87 ± 7.73, the Positive Impact and Satisfaction sub-dimension as 16.26 ± 5.67, and the Concerns sub-dimension as 13.61 ± 4.63 [25]. Similarly, in another study, the total Wip score was determined to be 33.18 ± 7.34, the Positive Impact and Satisfaction score was 15.12 ± 6.27, and the Concerns score was 18.06 ± 6.59 [26]. Based on these comparisons, the high total score obtained in our study, and particularly the positive impact and satisfaction level, suggest that pregnant women perceive their pregnancy experiences as generally more positive. These differences may stem from factors such as participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, levels of social support, whether the pregnancy was planned, cultural structure, and access to healthcare services.

Our study findings indicate that the level of self-compassion among pregnant women was 41,30 ± 7,89. This value is above the mean score of 37.14 ± 6.70 reported in the study by Akdoğan and Kaya and is similar to the value of 40.56 ± 10.40 reported by Fernandes et al. In another study, it was found to be 39.34 ± 8.11 [27]. In the study by Hajihatamloo et al. ,the self-compassion level of those attending pregnancy classes was found to be high at 48.8 ± 5.35, while this value was reported as 34.74 ± 6.08 for those who did not attend. These findings indicate that self-compassion levels can be increased through social support and informative education [28–30].

In their study, Mahrer et al. found a moderate positive correlation (r = .60, p< .001) between pregnancy-specific anxiety scores in the second and third trimesters and demonstrated that anxiety measured in both periods was significantly associated with PRAS [31]. Furthermore, the fact that pre-pregnancy general anxiety levels are not related to trimester-specific pregnancy anxiety supports the notion that pregnancy anxiety is a distinct psychological construct. Similarly, in a study conducted by Obrochta et al. , multiple psychological problems (depression, stress, and/or anxiety) were observed simultaneously in 41.0% of women experiencing prenatal psychological distres [32]. These findings indicate that anxiety during pregnancy can often be seen alongside other psychological problems and must be addressed with a holistic approach. The high anxiety levels observed in our study once again highlight the necessity of psychosocial support interventions for pregnant women. High levels of pregnancy-related anxiety lead to a decrease in well-being among pregnant women [33]. Similarly, our study also found that well-being during pregnancy decreased as anxiety increased. Another study reported a stronger negative relationship between anxiety and psychological well-being in pregnant women with low maternal-fetal bonding levels, meaning that well-being decreased more as stress increased. In contrast, it has been observed that stress has a weaker negative effect on well-being during pregnancy in women with high attachment levels [34]. This situation suggests that a strong maternal-fetal bond may play a role in reducing the negative impact of pregnancy anxiety on well-being.

The study found a positive and significant relationship between self-compassion and well-being during pregnancy; that is, well-being increases as the level of self-compassion increases. Although studies on well-being and self-compassion during pregnancy are limited, some studies have obtained similar results to our findings. Karayel reported that as the level of self-compassion increases in pregnant women, their well-being during pregnancy also increases, while Kaya and Akdoğan similarly reported a positive relationship between these two variables [27, 28]. Given the protective role of self-compassion, higher levels are expected to be associated with better well-being.

This study demonstrates that pregnancy-related anxiety and its subdimensions play a decisive role in psychological well-being and self-compassion during pregnancy. The findings reveal that as pregnancy-related anxiety increases, levels of well-being and self-compassion decrease; conversely, high levels of self-compassion and well-being may mitigate the negative effects of pregnancy-related anxiety. The protective effects of self-compassion and well-being can be considered as potential intervention targets for supporting psychological adjustment during pregnancy. Consistent with the existing literature, these results emphasize the importance of anxiety management and enhancing mental well-being during pregnancy for both maternal and infant health and may serve as a foundation for future intervention studies.

Conclusion

Pregnancy-related anxiety and its subdimensions were negatively associated with both well-being and self-compassion. Regression analyses indicated that specific dimensions of anxiety, including birth anxiety, body image concerns, attitudes toward childbirth, and signs of anxiety, as well as subdimensions of well-being such as positive impact and satisfaction, significantly predicted self-compassion levels. Higher levels of anxiety were associated with lower self-compassion, while greater well-being contributed positively to self-compassion. These findings underscore the importance of psychosocial interventions targeting both the reduction of pregnancy-related anxiety and the enhancement of well-being to support maternal mental health during pregnancy.

Limitations

This study was conducted in a single center and employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the generalizability of the findings and precludes causal interpretations. Although regression analyses were conducted to examine predictive relationships between pregnancy-related anxiety, well-being, and self-compassion, the cross-sectional nature of the data prevents establishing causal effects.

The study did not adjust for socioeconomic confounders. This omission suggests that the observed relationships may be influenced by these factors, thereby limiting their generalizability.
