Awareness and management of sepsis among emergency medicine practitioners in China: a national cross-sectional study
Authors: Yidan Hu, Yun Qu, Qijia Liu, Quan Li, Hai Kang, Chuanzhu Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the efforts of the Surviving Sepsis Campaign over two decades, sepsis remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with 19–48.9 million cases and 11 million deaths annually. At present, research on sepsis is predominantly focused within the intensive care unit domain; however, the initial diagnosis of sepsis predominantly occurs in the emergency department. Thus, a national cross-sectional study is needed to assess emergency department healthcare workers’ awareness and treatment of sepsis in China and to evaluate the geographic distributions of sepsis prevalence, in-hospital mortality, and economic burden.MethodsWe conducted a national cross-sectional study to evaluate the cognitive and therapeutic landscape of sepsis among emergency department healthcare workers in China. The study used purposeful sampling in tertiary hospitals across different regions of mainland China. A questionnaire was developed via the Questionnaire Star platform and administered electronically via WeChat and email to physicians across multiple Chinese medical institutions between September 27 and October 31, 2024; multicenter data on sepsis diagnosis patterns, therapeutic interventions, mortality outcomes, and associated economic burdens were collected.ResultsA total of 163 hospital datasets were collected, and after exclusions, 113 eligible hospital datasets were included in the analysis. Regarding the adoption of sepsis diagnostic criteria in emergency departments, the results indicate that northern regions have a significantly greater adoption of Sepsis-3.0 than southern regions do (P = 0.048). There is significant regional variation in the prevalence and in-hospital mortality rates of sepsis across mainland China, with the eastern coastal regions generally having lower sepsis in-hospital mortality rates than the western regions do. The number of patients in the emergency intensive care unit with expenses exceeding 80,000 RMB is nearly double that of the entire hospital. The expenditure range of 21,000–40,000 RMBs has emerged as the predominant cost bracket for hospitalized patients and has been consistently observed across both the emergency intensive care unit and hospital-wide settings.ConclusionIn mainland China, several emergency departments have adopted internationally recognized diagnostic criteria (Sepsis-3.0) for sepsis. However, there is still significant variation in the diagnosis and treatment outcomes of sepsis across different regions, highlighting the urgent need for standardized and homogeneous sepsis management practices.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01534-7.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome characterized by dysregulated host inflammatory responses to infection, leading to multiple organ dysfunction [1]. According to current epidemiological studies, there are 19 to 48.9 million cases of sepsis annually worldwide, with approximately 11 million related deaths, making sepsis a major cause of mortality worldwide [2, 3]. On the basis of these data, the World Health Organization has declared sepsis a global health priority [3]. In recent years, numerous high-quality epidemiological studies on sepsis in China have been published [4, 5]. These studies have provided valuable insights into the prevalence, mortality, risk factors, and outcomes associated with sepsis in the Chinese population [6, 7]. They have also highlighted the need for improved diagnostic criteria, treatment protocols, and public health strategies to address the significant burden of sepsis in China [8].

A study conducted by Fiest et al. in 2022 demonstrated that healthcare professionals’ awareness and knowledge of sepsis are important for timely diagnosis and effective treatment [9]. However, relatively few studies have focused on the awareness and diagnosis and treatment of sepsis among emergency department healthcare workers in China. China is the most populous developing country in the world, and its emergency medicine has undergone rapid development over the past 40 years [10, 11]. Therefore, studying the epidemiological patterns of sepsis in China is highly important for assessing the global prevalence of sepsis. Most patients with sepsis are first encountered by emergency department healthcare workers, hence the critical role of the emergency department in the recognition and treatment of sepsis [12, 13]. Enhancing the awareness and diagnostic and treatment capabilities of emergency department personnel regarding sepsis is highly important [14, 15].

Therefore, we conducted a national cross-sectional study on sepsis among emergency department healthcare workers in China, and assessed the geographical distribution of sepsis prevalence and in-hospital mortality rates, as well as the economic burden in China. The use of questionnaire-based surveys offers several advantages, including economic efficiency, low cost, standardized comparisons, and broad coverage. This study underscores the pivotal role of emergency departments in the recognition and management of sepsis, highlighting the critical need to increase the knowledge and diagnostic and treatment capabilities of emergency healthcare professionals regarding sepsis.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a national cross-sectional study to evaluate the cognitive and therapeutic landscape of sepsis among emergency department healthcare workers in China. In study preparation and administration, we followed the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guideline, which has been detailed elsewhere [16].

Data collection

A questionnaire was designed via the Questionnaire Star platform and distributed through WeChat and email from September 27, 2024, to October 31, 2024, to physicians at multiple medical institutions in China. The questionnaire titled “Current Status of Sepsis in Chinese Emergency Departments”. The English version of the questionnaire is provided as a supplementary file (see Supplement Materials). The respondents who received the questionnaire push notifications were able to complete the survey via mobile phones or personal computer (PC). The questionnaire consisted of 24 items: (1) Basic information about the respondent’s hospital (full name of the hospital, contact person, contact phone number), the hospital’s level, and the hospital’s management of sepsis (including whether they admit sepsis patients, whether they routinely screen for sepsis in patients with infection or suspected infection, which criteria they currently use for diagnosing sepsis, and whether they routinely manage sepsis patients with the Hour-1 Bundle), to assess the admission and treatment of sepsis patients across different hospitals; (2) the total number of cases, number of sepsis cases, and number of sepsis-related death cases in the respondent’s hospital for the past year (from September 1, 2023, to August 31, 2024) in the whole hospital, intensive care unit (ICU), and EICU, respectively, to compare the burden of sepsis in the whole hospital, ICU, and EICU; (3) the scoring system used by the respondent’s hospital for sepsis screening to evaluate the application of different sepsis screening methods in various hospitals; and (4) the average total hospitalization costs for sepsis patients and the average hospitalization costs for sepsis patients in the EICU in the respondent’s hospital for the past year (from September 1, 2023). The cost data in this study were derived from aggregated total hospitalization costs for sepsis episodes, as recorded in institutional electronic medical records. These costs encompass the sum of expenses related to inpatient diagnostic procedures, medication expenses, and nursing care costs. The questionnaire included multiple-choice questions and fill-in-the-blank questions, allowing respondents to enter other answers if they felt that none of the options were suitable. Each respondent was allowed to complete the survey only once.

Study sample

This study employed a purposive sampling approach to recruit tertiary hospitals with a complete emergency department structure from different regions of mainland China. (1) Hospitals should have a dedicated emergency department. (2) Individuals who are directly involved in the care of sepsis patients or have the ability to report on their hospital’s sepsis management practices. (3) Respondents must have had experience in the diagnosis and treatment of sepsis patients within the past year (from September 1, 2023, to August 31, 2024). (4) Respondents should have access to data regarding the total number of hospital admissions, sepsis cases, and sepsis-related mortality within their hospital for the specified time frame (from September 1, 2023, to August 31, 2024). (5) Respondents should be able to provide information on the hospitalization costs for sepsis patients, including those in the emergency intensive care unit (EICU) [17]. The costing data in this study were derived from aggregated total hospitalization costs for sepsis episodes, as recorded in institutional electronic medical records. These costs encompass the sum of expenses related to inpatient diagnostic procedures, medication expenses, and nursing care costs. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) intra-response logical inconsistencies across questionnaire items, (2) critical variable incompleteness (missingness > 20% in Sepsis-3.0 adoption indicators), (3) biomedically implausible numerical values beyond predefined clinical ranges, and (4) noninformative null responses with > 80% data field vacancies.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for this cross-sectional study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital (Approval No.2024 − 0606). As the research utilized exclusively anonymous, aggregated data on hospital-level practices and outcomes, the requirement for written informed consent was formally waived by the ethics committee. No personal identifiable information of patients or healthcare staff was collected or analyzed.

Data analysis

The experimental data were analyzed via descriptive statistics and chi-square tests, with all the statistical procedures conducted via IBM SPSS Statistics 29 software.

Results

Diagnosis and treatment of sepsis in emergency departments in China

From the initial purposive sample of 200 contacted hospitals, 163 participated (response rate: 81.5%). After excluding contradictory data, obviously unrealistic data, and invalid data, 113 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis. Among the data from 113 hospitals, 111 hospitals (98.23%) treated sepsis patients in the emergency department, and 2 hospitals (1.77%) did not treat sepsis patients. A total of 106 hospitals (93.69%) routinely screened for sepsis in patients with infection or suspected infection. Currently, 104 hospitals (91.89%) use the Sepsis 3.0 criteria for diagnosing sepsis, 5 hospitals (4.50%) use the Sepsis 2.0 criteria, and 4 hospitals (3.60%) rely on doctors’ clinical experience. A total of 97 hospitals (85.59%) routinely managed sepsis patients with the Hour-1 Bundle.

The sepsis screening scoring systems used in emergency departments across mainland China were as follows

52 (50.00%) hospitals utilized the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scoring system, 42 (40.38%) employed the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) system, 7 (6.73%) relied on physician clinical experience, 2 (1.92%) used the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), and 1 (0.96%) hospital applied the modified early warning score (MEWS) as the sepsis screening scoring system (Fig. 1). The use of the SOFA and qSOFA scoring systems accounted for the vast majority (90.38%), yet a small fraction of hospitals (9.62%) have not adopted the most recent scoring systems (Fig. 1).

The adoption of diagnostic criteria and sepsis screening scoring systems for sepsis in emergency departments across mainland China was as follows

A total of 44 hospitals in the northern region used Sepsis-3.0 for the diagnosis of sepsis, with only 1 hospital employing diagnostic criteria other than Sepsis-3.0; in the southern region, 58 hospitals used Sepsis-3.0 for the diagnosis of sepsis, and 10 hospitals did not use Sepsis-3.0 diagnostic criteria. There was a difference in the adoption of Sepsis-3.0 as a diagnostic criterion between the northern and southern regions (P = 0.048), which suggests that a greater number of hospitals in the northern region than in the southern region use the Sepsis-3.0 diagnostic criterion (Table 1). In the northern region, the SOFA scoring system was adopted by 26 hospitals, and the qSOFA scoring system was adopted by 13 hospitals; in the southern region, the SOFA scoring system was adopted by 26 hospitals, and the qSOFA scoring system was adopted by 29 hospitals. The chi-square test (X²) was used to assess whether there was a significant difference in the use of sepsis screening scoring systems between the northern and southern regions: the chi-square value was 3.472, with a P value of 0.062, indicating that there was no significant difference in the use of sepsis screening scoring systems between the northern and southern regions of China at the 0.05 level of significance (Table 2).

In-hospital mortality rates of sepsis patients in hospitals, ICUs, and EICUs across 28 provinces in China

Hospital-wide, there were 93,573 sepsis cases, with 12,925 deaths, resulting in an in-hospital mortality rate of 13.81%. In the ICU, there were 25,230 sepsis cases, with 4,789 deaths, yielding a higher in-hospital mortality rate of 18.98%. In the EICU, there were 12,861 sepsis cases, with 2,577 deaths, resulting in the highest in-hospital mortality rate of 20.04%. Compared with the hospital-wide range, the in-hospital mortality rates among sepsis patients in both the ICU and EICU were significantly higher (Table 3).

Map of sepsis prevalence and in-hospital mortality rates in emergency departments in China

The highest prevalence rate of sepsis was reported in Beijing, with a rate of 6.00%, whereas the lowest was in Hebei Province, at 0.035%. In addition to Beijing and Hebei, other provinces also present varying degrees of sepsis prevalence. Provinces such as Xinjiang and Qinghai reported relatively higher rates, whereas coastal provinces in the east, such as Jiangsu, Fujian, and Zhejiang, presented comparatively lower rates. Among all the regions surveyed, the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region had the highest sepsis in-hospital mortality rate, at 34.69%. In contrast, Jiangxi Province had the lowest in-hospital mortality rate of sepsis, at 3.44%. The study revealed a wide range of sepsis in-hospital mortality rates across different regions, from as low as 3.44% to as high as 34.69%. This significant disparity underscores the heterogeneity in sepsis outcomes and the potential influence of regional factors on patient survival (Fig. 2).

Distribution of average hospitalization costs for sepsis patients in the whole hospital and EICU

The proportion of sepsis patients in the whole hospital with hospitalization costs ranging from 21,000 to 40,000 Renminbi (RMB) is 28.13%, which is the highest among all cost ranges, indicating that the hospitalization expenses of a significant portion of sepsis patients are controlled at a relatively low level. The proportion of patients with costs between 61,000 and 80,000 RMB is 20.83%, ranking second, suggesting that nearly one-fifth of the patients fall within this cost range. The proportion of patients with costs between 41,000 and 60,000 RMB and between 11,000 and 20,000 RMB is 19.79%, similar to the proportion for the 61,000 to 80,000 RMB range. Patients with costs exceeding 80,000 RMB account for 11.46%, which is relatively low but still indicates that more than one-tenth of the patients reach this higher level of hospitalization expenses (Fig. 3). Distribution of average hospitalization costs for sepsis patients in the EICU: The proportion of patients with costs between 21,000 and 40,000 RMB is 36.14%, which is the highest among all cost ranges in the EICU, followed by patients with costs exceeding 80,000 RMB at 21.69%, a proportion significantly higher than the whole hospital’s proportion. Together, these costs account for more than half of the EICU’s cost proportion (57.83%) and have increased compared with the whole hospital’s proportions, whereas the other cost ranges have all decreased compared with the whole hospital’s proportions (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the awareness and management to sepsis among emergency medicine practitioners in China, offering valuable insights into the state of sepsis management within a rapidly evolving healthcare system. Our findings confirm significant gaps in both knowledge and practice, which have important implications for the quality of sepsis care in emergency departments across the country. The findings of our study revealed that the mortality rate of sepsis in the EICU was 20.04%, which was higher than the mortality rate of 18.98% reported in the ICU. Compared with the previously reported ICU mortality rate of 19.30% within the country [18], the mortality rate in our EICU study remains elevated. Currently, there is a paucity of data regarding sepsis in Chinese EICUs, and this gap in knowledge necessitates greater attention and research focus. These findings underscore the need for further investigations into sepsis management within the EICU context. Future studies should prioritize the collection and analysis of data specific to EICU sepsis patients to better understand the factors contributing to these mortality rates and to develop more effective interventions.

Our study demonstrated that while a majority of emergency medicine practitioners possess a fundamental awareness of sepsis, substantial gaps remain in advanced sepsis management practices, particularly in the timely administration of antibiotics and fluid resuscitation. Notably, 91.89% of the participants were familiar with the early recognition criteria for sepsis, aligning with international guidelines set forth by the Surviving Sepsis Campaign (2021) [19]. However, only 85.59% of respondents adhered to practices in fluid resuscitation within the first hour, an important window identified in several studies for improving patient outcomes [20, 21]. This is concerning, as timely intervention has been shown to reduce in-hospital mortality rates by up to 40% [22]. Our findings align with those of prior studies, including those of Zhao et al. [23], which identified comparable disparities in sepsis management among Chinese healthcare providers. However, our investigation, which draws from a nationwide perspective without strictly stratifying hospitals by the economic status of their regions, reveals the intrinsic inequalities in sepsis management across healthcare infrastructures of varying calibres. A salient point is the marked contrast in sepsis identification and management proficiency between emergency departments in economically advanced regions, which are more adept, and those in regions of moderate or lower economic status, which may lack the necessary essential medical equipment, laboratory support, and specialized training, potentially leading to delays in critical interventions.

Our study revealed profound geographical disparities in both sepsis prevalence and in-hospital mortality across Chinese provinces. Given our rigorous quality control measures, it is unlikely that data quality alone can explain differences. Instead, these findings most likely reflect genuine differences in clinical practices and patient population among the hospitals. We infer that these disparities may be attributed to several factors. These include: (1) the “siphon effect” in patient referral patterns, where major tertiary centers (e.g., Beijing) concentrate more severe and complex cases, potentially inflating both prevalence and mortality rates; (2) regional differences in case-mix influenced by demographics, comorbidities, and socioeconomic factors; and (3) variations in diagnostic and practices across hospitals, affecting the sensitivity of case identification and reported incidence. In summary, the importance of our findings is that they reveal a significant gap and direct future research efforts. Future studies should aim to determine the causes of these pronounced variations, which is essential for creating targeted strategies to improve sepsis care uniformly across China.

These findings highlight the pressing need for targeted educational initiatives to improve sepsis recognition and treatment among emergency medicine practitioners, particularly in less developed regions. Global guidelines, such as those proposed by the Surviving Sepsis Campaign, have proven effective in improving sepsis outcomes when properly implemented [24, 25]. However, our study highlights that mere guideline availability does not necessarily translate into effective clinical practice, especially when healthcare systems are stretched or lack adequate training infrastructure [26]. This aligns with research from Alhazzani et al. [24], who reported that despite the availability of sepsis guidelines, the implementation of sepsis bundles remains inconsistent, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Moreover, this study highlights the importance of not only improving cognitive knowledge but also ensuring that clinical settings are equipped to manage sepsis effectively. According to studies by Seymour et al. [27] and Angus et al. [28], sepsis-related mortality can be substantially reduced with timely identification, early antibiotic therapy, and appropriate fluid resuscitation. The findings from our study indicate that there are notable differences in management practices. These discrepancies suggest that systemic barriers, which can range from insufficient training to logistical hurdles, may prevent optimal outcomes. Our study is consistent with findings from other national surveys that have assessed sepsis knowledge and management across various healthcare professionals. For example, a study by Wang et al. [29] reported similar levels of knowledge deficiencies among Chinese emergency medicine practitioners, particularly in the administration of sepsis protocols. However, our study extends these findings by emphasizing the regional disparities within China, which have been less explored in prior work.

Research conducted in other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) has highlighted similar trends. In a study conducted by Hussein et al. [30]. In Egypt, knowledge gaps in sepsis management are attributed to the lack of continuous professional development and inadequate diagnostic tools in emergency departments. This parallels the challenges identified in our study, where respondents in LMICs were less likely to follow established sepsis management protocols, primarily owing to resource limitations and a lack of ongoing training. Our findings align with those of Souza et al. [31], who identified significant discrepancies in sepsis knowledge and management among healthcare workers in LMICs. This research offers a unique contribution by focusing on China, a country that has experienced rapid urbanization and expansion of its healthcare system in recent decades, potentially leading to varied levels of sepsis awareness and care practices across regions. Prior studies have often overlooked these regional differences, concentrating instead on urban settings or single-center studies.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to establish causal relationships between knowledge gaps and clinical outcomes. Additionally, our study focused exclusively on emergency medicine practitioners, which may not fully capture the multidisciplinary nature of sepsis care, which involves intensivists, nurses, and laboratory staff. Moreover, this study is not a census and did not fully document Chinese emergency department healthcare providers’ awareness of and ability to manage sepsis. Thus, its representativeness is limited. Future studies should consider longitudinal designs to assess the impact of sepsis education and the implementation of sepsis bundles on clinical outcomes. Additionally, research exploring the effectiveness of telemedicine or decision support systems in improving sepsis care in underserved areas would be valuable [32]. This could be particularly relevant in the context of China, where technological advances such as telehealth are increasingly being integrated into healthcare delivery [33–36], but their impact on sepsis management remains poorly understood [37, 38]. Moreover, future studies could investigate the role of hospital-level factors, such as EICU bed availability, diagnostic capabilities, and access to advanced medical technologies, in shaping sepsis management practices. As highlighted by Rhodes et al. [39], understanding the interaction between clinical guidelines and the availability of resources is critical in optimizing sepsis outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this national cross-sectional study provides a thorough assessment of the cognitive landscape and therapeutic practices surrounding sepsis management among emergency medicine practitioners in China. Our findings underscore significant knowledge gaps and highlight important regional disparities in sepsis care. This study contributes to the literature and advancements by providing data on the knowledge and management of sepsis among emergency department personnel nationwide, providing important implications for policy, training, and healthcare resource allocation. Addressing these gaps through targeted education, resource improvement, and ongoing monitoring is crucial for enhancing sepsis management, ultimately improving patient survival rates and reducing the burden of sepsis.
