Real-time cognitive performance metrics derived from a digital therapeutic for inattention predict ADHD-related clinical outcomes: Replication across three independent trials of AKL-T01
Authors: Caitlin A. Stamatis, Andrew C. Heusser, Tony J. Simon, Titiimaea Ala’ilima, Scott H. Kollins
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AKL-T01 is a digital therapeutic (DTx) that targets attention by generating conflict at dynamically updated difficulty levels during a multitasking game. Clinical trials support AKL-T01’s efficacy in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), but there is a need to understand how in-game data can be used to monitor patient changes in cognition. We aimed to derive a real-time measure of attention from AKL-T01 gameplay data and validate it against clinical outcomes. Trials of AKL-T01 included: STARS-ADHD-Adult (NCT05183919), a 6-week trial in adults 18 and older (n = 221; M age = 39.9; 70% female); STARS-ADHD-Adolescent (NCT04897074), a 4-week trial in adolescents ages 13–17 (n = 162; M age = 14.4; 41% female); and STARS-ADHD (NCT02674633), a 4-week trial in children ages 8–12 (n = 180; M age = 9.7; 31% female). A cognitive metric was derived from targeting response speed, targeting sensitivity (d-prime), and navigation skill level. Using multiple linear regression models, we analyzed relationships between cognitive metric change and changes in the Test of Variables of Attention (TOVA)-Attention Comparison Score (ACS), controlling for TOVA-ACS baseline, cognitive metric baseline, age, and sex. We explored associations with ADHD symptoms and quality of life. Increases in the cognitive metric significantly predicted increases in TOVA-ACS in the adult (β = 0.16, p < 0.001), adolescent (β = .09, p = 0.007), and pediatric (β = 0.06, p = 0.014) trials. Cognitive metric changes additionally related to self-reported quality of life in adults and clinician-rated ADHD symptoms in adolescents. Findings support the clinical validity of a real-time measure of attention derived from AKL-T01 patient-device interactions.

Introduction

Digital therapeutics (DTx) are evidence-based clinical technologies targeting specific medical conditions [1]. A significant advantage of DTx is the ability to conduct real-time assessment via the collection of high-dimensional data from patients as they engage with treatment [2]. Data collected from DTx patient-device interactions can provide clinicians with objective measures of treatment adherence, engagement [3, 4], and symptom change [5], reducing the need for retrospective, subjective symptom reporting and enhancing the efficiency of office visits. Such data can also streamline treatment decisions; for example, if a patient fails to show clinically meaningful improvement in the outcome monitored using DTx data, a provider (or the patient themself) can explore alternative treatment options [6].

DTx for mental health show promise in scaling access to evidence-based treatments; however, relative to other therapeutic areas such as diabetes in which DTx leverage objective, real-time monitoring of biological processes via patient interactions with devices [7, 8], mental health DTx still rely broadly on self-report to measure change. This is understandable given the complexity of extracting meaningful information about mental processes from patient-device interactions, which requires both a theoretical mapping of data from these interactions onto the therapeutic properties of the DTx, as well as validation with clinical outcomes. There is a particular need to establish the validity of measures derived from DTx targeting cognition [9], where there has been a proliferation of games that purport to enhance cognitive functioning via similarity to laboratory-based cognitive tasks with gamified elements to promote engagement, with limited evidence of ecological validity [10]. Understanding the degree to which patient interactions with DTx correlate with validated measures of cognition is essential, given documented evidence that changing even minor elements of a cognitive task may interfere with its validity as a scientific tool [11–13].

One such cognitive intervention, AKL-T01, is a DTx for attention delivered as a videogame. AKL-T01 is FDA-authorized for children ages 8–12 with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and attentional impairment. AKL-T01 has shown efficacy across multiple pediatric clinical trials [14, 15], with more recent trials in adolescents and adults extending evidence of efficacy over the lifespan [16]. Across trials, AKL-T01 was linked with improvements on the Test of Variables of Attention (TOVA) [17], a computerized measure of attentional functioning and primary trial endpoint, as well as other measures of ADHD symptoms and impairment. AKL-T01 is theorized to improve attention and cognitive control by generating cognitive conflict at dynamically adapted personalized levels of difficulty during multitasking (navigating a ship while tapping in response to target creatures and ignoring distractors). These tasks are thought to enhance the sustained efficiency of frontoparietal networks through improved attentional control that persists beyond the training itself and can be measured both behaviorally and with electrophysiological signals [18], such as the medial frontal theta pattern [18–21]. AKL-T01 produces a flow of high-resolution individualized performance data on attentional control many times per second. However, no study to date has directly linked patient performance on AKL-T01 treatment game tasks to changes in objective attentional functioning, a key step in validating the potential to use this DTx for monitoring cognitive changes over time.

In order to derive a measure of attentional performance from AKL-T01 use, elements of gameplay must be mapped onto theoretically-grounded, constituent attentional processes. Neural models suggest that attention consists of three interacting networks [22, 23], including the alerting network (sustained vigilance), the orienting network (prioritization of sensory inputs), and the executive network (conflict monitoring). AKL-T01 is thought to activate these attentional networks in different ways: during the simultaneous execution of the navigation and targeting tasks (i.e., multitasking condition), patients are required to leverage the alerting network to sustain attention on steering and selecting correct targets, and the executive network to flexibly allocate resources under competing and evolving task demands (e.g., inhibiting non-target responses while navigating the ship). Research supports deficits in the alerting and executive networks as particularly relevant for characterizing ADHD [24, 25]. Consequently, a measure of attention derived from AKL-T01 should involve assays of sustained attention and stimulus discrimination under multitasking conditions, wherein cognitive processes required to simultaneously complete the navigation and targeting tasks compete for the same cognitive capacity as patients detect and resolve conflict in a goal-oriented manner. Decades of research support reaction time and d-prime from continuous performance tasks (CPTs) as measures of sustained and selective attention [26, 27], which—combined with a patient’s ability to continuously sustain attention to perform the navigation task—would theoretically provide an attentional metric capturing aspects of vigilance and executive control that are central to ADHD [24, 25].

In the present study, we aimed to (1) derive a cognitive metric from AKL-T01 patient-device interactions that is grounded in attentional theory [22, 23, 28], can be measured in real-time, and follows a predictable temporal pattern across subjects, and (2) validate this metric by evaluating the its associations to attention and clinical symptoms across three independent clinical trials. We hypothesized that changes in the cognitive metric would be associated with changes in an objective, validated neuropsychological measure of attentional functioning (TOVA) across pediatric, adolescent, and adult samples. From an exploratory perspective, we also examined how changes in the cognitive metric related to changes in ADHD symptoms and—in adults—quality of life.

Methods

Participants and procedures

This study involved secondary analysis of three clinical trials of AKL-T01. The first trial, STARS-ADHD (NCT02674633), was a 4-week trial in children ages 8–12; since this was a two-arm randomized controlled trial of AKL-T01 versus an active digital control, only those assigned to the AKL-T01 condition were included in this analysis (n = 180 enrolled) [14]. The other two were single-arm trials of AKL-T01: STARS-ADHD-Adolescent (NCT04897074) was a 4-week trial in adolescents ages 13–17 (n = 162 enrolled), and STARS-ADHD-Adult (NCT05183919) was a 6-week trial in adults 18 and older (n = 221 enrolled). All participants had a diagnosis of ADHD and impairment in attentional functioning on the TOVA-Attention Comparison Score (TOVA-ACS) [17]. For additional information on inclusionary and exclusionary criteria, see the individual study protocols (Supplementary Materials).

Measures

The primary outcome was the TOVA-ACS, an objective, computerized measure of attentional functioning [17]. TOVA-ACS is computed based on: average response time to correct trials (half 1), d-prime (half 2), and variability of response times to correct trials [17]. TOVA is not a diagnostic measure but has high sensitivity in distinguishing ADHD from non-ADHD groups [29]. Exploratory outcomes included ADHD symptoms on the ADHD-Rating Scale-5 (ADHD-RS-5) for pediatric and adolescent samples [30], and on the ADHD-RS-IV for adults [31]. In the adult study, we also measured ADHD-related quality of life on the Adult ADHD Quality of Life (AAQoL) rating scale [32].

Analytic methods: cognitive metric derivation

The data processing pipeline to derive the attentional metric from multitasking game missions is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1 and described in detail below.

Calculating submetrics

Three primary submetrics (navigation 80% threshold, response speed to correct trials in the targeting task, and targeting task d-prime) were derived from the multitasking phases of game data. Note that while single-tasking phases are leveraged as a comparison against multitasking in a small subset of missions— which are used to help calibrate subsequent training task levels in the treatment, as part of the adaptive personalized algorithm—single-tasking phases were filtered out in the calculation of this cognitive metric. Navigation 80% threshold represents an estimate of the navigation task level at which a player can achieve 80% accuracy. Response speed to correct trials in the targeting task represents the average speed at which a player can correctly identify targets. While other cognitive and motor abilities not measured here are also required to perform well on navigation tasks, we selected these two submetrics as assays because they measure aspects of cognition most relevant to the core processes targeted by AKL-T01 (sustained attention and distraction-resistance). Prior to averaging, response times are transformed into game-level (i.e., task difficulty) space, which corresponds more directly to the mechanics of the difficulty adjustment algorithm, producing a more normal distribution. Selectiveness in the targeting task (d-prime) represents the player’s ability to tap on target trials while not tapping on non-target trials. We selected this submetric to assay the cognitive processes required to maintain a rule in memory, perceive and analyze an object, find a match or mismatch to the rule, and then initiate or inhibit a tap response. To ensure d-prime can be computed for missions with low trial numbers, we include a half-trial correction when calculating hit and false alarm rates [33].

Adjusting for differences in task difficulty between worlds and mission types

The game-based treatment has four different “worlds” that increase in difficulty, and the targeting and navigation task complexity differs by game world due to the unique world-specific rulesets. For example, the targeting task in the first world includes 3 uniquely colored stimuli (1 target and 2 distractors), whereas the second world includes 6 identically colored stimuli (1 target, 5 distractors). Further, within each world, there are several mission types. Baseline missions measure players’ ability level and produce values that initialize the adaptive algorithm. Training missions deliver the titrated treatment in a continuously adaptive manner personalized to the user; there are a minimum of 5 training missions between a baseline mission and challenge mission. Challenge missions evaluate progress and determine if the user will move onto the next level of difficulty. These differences in rulesets and mission types cause predictable differences in difficulty (and thus submetrics derived from task performance). In order to create a metric that measures improvement in cognitive performance over time irrespective of the task difficulty/complexity, we modeled these differences and removed the effects. Specifically, we fit a linear model that included factors for world, mission type, their interaction, and a term (log(mission_order)) to capture the fact that the cognitive metric tends to improve more quickly at the beginning of treatment and more gradually later in the treatment. Rescaling submetrics

We then transformed the metrics to be on the same scale using a robust scaling approach, which normalizes using the median and interquartile range (IQR) and is less sensitive to outliers. Rescaling parameters were calculated separately for each of the submetrics and derived from the distribution of adjusted submetric values across all missions played separately for each clinical study. Extreme values (1.5x IQR lower than the 1st quartile or higher than the 3rd quartile) were winsorized prior to rescaling to maximize the spread of the data between 0 and 1.

Combining submetrics into a single cognitive metric

In order not to overweight the targeting task, we combined the two targeting submetrics into one by computing the vector norm. There is a well documented tradeoff between speed and accuracy/selectivity in decision making tasks [34]. The vector norm of the intermediate targeting variable has the following properties: greatest when both speed and accuracy improve; high when either speed or accuracy improves, but the other stays constant; either lower or higher if the two metrics trade off; lowest if both metrics get worse. We averaged the intermediate targeting variable with the navigation submetric and multiplied by 100 to produce a final score between 0 and 100. For each dataset, the resulting metric was normally distributed, centered at ~50 with few extreme values (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Calculating change in the cognitive metric over the course of a treatment

At the group-level, the cognitive metric increased smoothly and logarithmically over missions played, suggesting rapid improvement initially followed by gradual improvement (Fig. 2; top panel). To capture this at the individual patient level, we fit a Bayesian hierarchical linear-log model to each individual’s gameplay data across the 4-6 week treatment, separately for each study. We extracted group-level and individual subject-level posterior distributions for the slope and intercept terms. To calculate the cognitive metric baseline and change from baseline, we computed the median of the posterior distribution for the slope and intercept for each subject. We then applied the resulting slope/intercept parameters to the linear-log function and recorded the model values for the first and last missions played. Analytic methods: clinical validation of cognitive metric

We tested multiple linear regression models (one per clinical trial), regressing the change in TOVA-ACS (baseline to study endpoint) on the change in the cognitive metric (initial measurement to final score), controlling for TOVA-ACS baseline, cognitive metric baseline, age, and sex. A post-hoc analysis was conducted to evaluate how the individual submetrics comprising the cognitive metric (i.e., navigation 80% threshold, response speed to correct trials in the targeting task, and targeting task selectiveness or d-prime) independently relate to TOVA-ACS. To evaluate secondary outcomes, we regressed the change in ADHD-RS on the change in the cognitive metric, again controlling for ADHD-RS baseline, cognitive metric baseline, age, and sex. In the adult data only, we evaluated a similar model with AAQoL change as the outcome.

Results

Cognitive metric derivation

The cognitive metric was derived for all game missions (STARS-ADHD-Adult n = 25,907; STARS-ADHD-Adolescent n = 15,983; STARS-ADHD n = 15,258). On average for each clinical trial, the cognitive metric increased rapidly followed by more gradual improvement (Fig. 2; top panel).

The Bayesian hierarchical linear-log models converged properly and fit well to individual subject data (see representative example in Fig. 3), and most subjects showed a consistent logarithmic trend in the cognitive metric over AKL-T01 treatment (Supplementary Figures S3-S5). Primary endpoint: TOVA-ACS

Increases in the cognitive metric significantly predicted increases in TOVA-ACS in the adult (B = 0.16, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001), adolescent (B = 0.09, SE = 0.03, p = 0.007), and pediatric (B = 0.06, SE = 0.02, p = 0.014) trials. The magnitude of the effect was largest in adults (Cohen’s F2 = 0.18, medium effect size), followed by adolescents (Cohen’s F2 = 0.08, small effect size), and smallest in the pediatric trial (Cohen’s F2 = 0.05, small effect size).

Relationships of the cognitive metric to TOVA submetrics are included in Supplementary Tables S2 (adult trial), S3 (adolescent trial), and S4 (pediatric trial). Generally (and with some exceptions across samples), the associations of the cognitive metric with the TOVA-ACS appeared to be driven by significant relationships with TOVA response time, response time variability, d-prime, and ex-Gaussian tau, and not omission or commission errors. Results from the post-hoc analysis evaluating associations between the individual cognitive metric submetrics (navigation 80% threshold; targeting response speed; targeting d’) and TOVA-ACS indicated a consistently significant relationship between navigation task improvement and TOVA-ACS improvement across samples. In the adult and adolescent samples, targeting response speed was correlated with TOVA-ACS, but targeting d’ was not; the inverse was true for the pediatric sample (see Supplementary Table S5).

Secondary endpoint: ADHD symptoms

In the adolescent data, increases in the cognitive metric were significantly related to ADHD-RS improvement (B = -0.14, SE = 0.07, p = 0.042), with a small effect size (F2 = 0.06). There was no association between the cognitive metric and ADHD-RS change in the adult (B = -0.07, SE = 0.07, p = 0.277) or pediatric (B = -0.03, SE = 0.06, p = 0.535) trials.

Secondary endpoint: quality of life

In the adult data, increases in the cognitive metric were also related to statistically significant increases in AAQoL, a self-reported measure of quality of life (B = 0.25, SE = 0.11, p = 0.020), with a small effect size (Cohen’s F2 = 0.04). Quality of life was not measured in the pediatric or adolescent trials.

Discussion

Results from the present study support the clinical validity of a real-time measure of attention derived from AKL-T01 patient-device interactions, with replication across the lifespan providing evidence for the developmental stability of the effects. Mapping elements of the treatment game onto component processes of attention that recruit the alerting and executive networks [22, 23]—including navigation 80% threshold, response speed to correct targeting trials, and targeting task selectiveness—allowed us to create a metric that can be used to understand how a patient’s attentional functioning changes with AKL-T01 treatment.

The pattern of associations between the cognitive metric derived in this study and TOVA-ACS supports the validity of this metric in capturing attentional functioning. TOVA-ACS is an FDA-cleared, objective measure of ADHD-relevant cognitive processes such as attention and inhibitory control [35], with high sensitivity in distinguishing ADHD from non-ADHD groups [29]. Cognitive data from TOVA relate to clinically-relevant outcomes such as academic and social functioning [36–38]. The correlation between the AKL-T01 metric and TOVA-ACS change aligns with the theoretical cognitive components required to successfully perform each task in the AKL-T01 game, including sustained attention to identify target stimuli and inhibitory control to ignore non-target distractors [17]. By training these common attentional processes in the context of multitasking—which creates challenges to cognitive control thought to result in neuroplasticity [18, 21]—AKL-T01 promotes targeted change in attentional functioning. Notably, the strength of effects between the cognitive metric and TOVA-ACS mirrored the magnitude of TOVA-ACS change reported in each of the clinical trials, which was highest in adults, followed by adolescents, then children [14, 16].

In exploratory analyses, we did not observe a relationship between changes in the cognitive metric and changes in ADHD-RS, with the exception of a small effect in the adolescent data. These results suggest that the cognitive metric derived from AKL-T01 gameplay captures improvements in attentional functioning that may be more difficult to detect using broader symptom rating scales. In a sense, this is expected: Unlike other treatments designed to address a broad range of ADHD symptoms, AKL-T01 is a bottom-up treatment specifically developed to target attentional circuitry [14, 15, 18], whereas symptom measures such as the ADHD-RS are several steps removed from core processes (i.e., they cover broader areas such as organization and planning). Further, symptom rating scales such as the ADHD-RS often demonstrate weak correlations with functional outcomes [38], and both pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments show differential impacts to symptoms versus impairment [39]—in one trial of a stimulant, for example, over 40% of pediatric patients categorized as responders based on the ADHD-RS failed to show improvements in functional impairment [40]. Given the myriad factors that can account for variance in patient symptoms and functioning, it is promising that we observed even small effects of a metric derived from gameplay on patient-reported quality of life; however, with the small effect size, it will be important for future studies to attempt to replicate these findings.

The ability to conduct high-dimensional, longitudinal assessment of attention in ADHD has implications for clinical practice, including the ability to notice fine-grained changes to cognitive function that may not yet be noticeable in everyday behavior, promote earlier intervention, and change treatment course in the case of non-response. Traditionally, evaluating ADHD symptom progress in a clinical setting requires client self-report, which is subject to retrospective reporting biases [41, 42]. Neuropsychological measures provide objective information on attentional functioning, but they are often costly and time-intensive, rendering them impractical for symptom monitoring. A promise of DTx has been the ability to conduct ecologically-valid, granular assessment over time using patient interactions with the device or treatment itself [2, 41]. While this promise has been realized in fields where clear-cut biological assays are available for diagnostic purposes (e.g., diabetes management), our study represents the first DTx for cognition to provide a means of assessing and tracking objective cognitive changes in real-time.

The current study has limitations. For one, the cognitive metric derived here is one of many that could be derived from AKL-T01 gameplay data. While these data were selected specifically to capture aspects of attentional functioning highly relevant to ADHD [24, 25], it will be important for future studies to explore additional metrics to characterize the range of cognitive processes AKL-T01 recruits (e.g., the other cognitive and motor abilities required to perform well on navigation tasks). For another, surface-level similarities of AKL-T01 and TOVA may raise concerns about “training to the test”; however, whereas TOVA is a traditional CPT, AKL-T01 is distinct in having a second task to generate interference, spatial and selective attention components, multiple dimension target rules, and personalized adaptive difficulty levels. Further refuting the likelihood of training to the test, post-hoc analyses of individual submetric relationships with TOVA-ACS (Supplementary Table S5) indicated that navigation threshold improvement correlates with TOVA-ACS across study samples, despite being the submetric with the least overlap with TOVA-ACS tasks. As another consideration, participants were recruited with high levels of attentional impairment on TOVA, raising questions of how the cognitive metric generalizes to more heterogeneous samples. An early look at our adult commercial data suggests that the cognitive metric performs similarly in real-world populations as in the clinical trial (Fig. 2 bottom panel; WCG IRB #: 1353416), though it will be important for future studies to continue to evaluate generalizability with real-world evidence.

The study also has several strengths, which—along with the aforementioned limitations—suggest directions for future research. The cognitive metric was developed a priori and agnostic of clinical data (i.e., based on theoretical models of attention) [22, 23], then validated against multiple outcomes in three independent samples. In evaluating its validity, we considered both near transfer with the TOVA and far transfer with measures of symptoms and functional impairment, the latter of which suggested evidence of an effect, albeit small in magnitude, on real-world functioning. Future studies could consider how the cognitive metric relates to more intermediate outcomes, perhaps using ecological momentary assessment or other data captured in real time, in patients with ADHD and other populations for whom AKL-T01 has demonstrated efficacy (e.g., depression) [43, 44].

Overall, our findings provide an example of how DTx can use information from patient-device interactions to objectively measure change during mental health treatment, without requiring additional input from the patient beyond engaging with the treatment itself. We illustrate the process of deriving such a metric from AKL-T01, including the theoretical mapping of treatment game properties onto cognitive tasks and validation against a neuropsychological measure of attention and subjective patient outcomes. An early look at the metric’s performance in real-world commercial data further supports its validity. By documenting this derivation and validation process, we hope to empower researchers of other DTx in the mental health space to develop similar tools for in-vivo cognitive monitoring, an important step in advancing precision treatment.
