Exploring the effects of visible atopic eczema on willingness for direct and indirect contact
Authors: Karen Lander, Claire Harris, Azeez Al-Attar, C Elise Kleyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVisible dermatological conditions are associated with stigma and social avoidance. Atopic eczema is a common, noncontagious skin disease that may affect visible areas such as the face and hands. Despite misconceptions regarding contagiousness, no previous research has systematically examined whether visible atopic eczema influences willingness for interpersonal contact.ObjectivesTo investigate whether the presence and severity of visible atopic eczema reduce willingness for direct and indirect contact, and to examine whether health anxiety, disgust propensity/sensitivity, COVID-19-related social withdrawal and perceived attractiveness predict willingness for contact.MethodsTwo experimental studies were conducted. In study 1 (n = 150), participants viewed images of a face or hand manipulated to display no, mild or severe atopic eczema. In study 2 (n = 168), a fully between-participants design was employed. Participants rated their willingness for direct contact and indirect contact, as well as perceived attractiveness. Measures of health anxiety, disgust propensity/sensitivity and the social impact of COVID-19 were also completed. Mixed and between-­participants Anovas examined the effects of atopic eczema severity and stimulus type, and regression analyses assessed predictors of overall willingness for contact.ResultsAcross both studies, willingness for direct and indirect contact decreased significantly as atopic eczema severity increased (all P < 0.01). Participants were more willing to engage with individuals depicted with no eczema compared with mild eczema, and with mild compared with severe eczema. These effects were observed for both faces and hands and replicated across experimental designs. Perceived attractiveness was the strongest positive predictor of willingness for contact in both studies. Disgust propensity/sensitivity was a significant negative predictor in multivariate analyses, whereas health anxiety and COVID-19-related social withdrawal were not reliable predictors.ConclusionsVisible atopic eczema significantly reduces willingness for both direct and indirect interpersonal contact, even at mild levels of severity. These findings are consistent with behavioural immune system accounts of disease-avoidance responses and suggest that stigma towards atopic eczema may be driven by perceptual and affective mechanisms. The results highlight the psychosocial burden associated with visible skin conditions and have implications for public education, stigma reduction and dermatological practice.

Atopic eczema, more commonly known as eczema, is a chronic, inflammatory, pruritic and relapsing skin disorder.1 It is one of the most prevalent skin diseases, with its onset and occurrence being highest in childhood, affecting up to one in five children2 and persisting into adulthood in less than 5% of cases.3 The distribution and appearance of the rash is influenced by factors including the person’s age and ethnicity.4 Atopic eczema may clinically manifest as macular erythema, papules or papulovesicles, eczematous patches with crusting, lichenfication, excoriation and skin dryness.5 Although any part of the body may be affected, common areas include the face, hands, and elbow and knee flexures.6 Most cases of atopic eczema are mild, with less than 10% of patients displaying severe skin lesions.7 While the physical symptoms of atopic eczema, including pruritus, are challenging for patients, psychological difficulties are often associated with the presence of visible skin disease (e.g. see Fink-Lamotte et al.8 regarding shame and self-disgust).

Indeed, there is a large psychosocial burden associated with visible skin conditions as superficial anomalies in the affected person’s appearance can lead to discrimination and stigmatization.9 This often results in feelings of embarrassment and decreased confidence among affected individuals who may also face social rejection from their peers and experience social withdrawal (e.g. Yew et al.10). Evidence suggests that individuals with a dermatological condition are likely to anticipate social rejection and negative evaluation from others due to their appearance, impairing their social life11 and leading them to pre-emptively avoid social situations.12,13 These social impacts, in addition to the physical implications, are responsible for the well-established association between dermatological conditions and decreased quality of life.13,14 Furthermore, they are also associated with an increased prevalence of mental illness, notably depression and anxiety.15–17

Schaller and Park (2011)18 suggested that we are adapted to have a ‘behavioural immune system’, which protects us from the potential threat of infectious diseases. Exposure to cues that are perceived to be indicative of disease illicit a psychological response and the experience of aversive emotions, such as disgust. This response motivates avoidance behaviours, aiming to reduce pathogen transmission. However, the effectiveness of this relies on one’s ability to accurately identify stimuli that are associated with disease. As perceptual cues are not always perfectly correlated with an actual risk of infection, false positives may occur. Several common visible skin conditions, including psoriasis, acne and atopic eczema, are noncontagious and so pose no real risk of transmission to others. Despite this, there is a common misconception that they are contagious diseases. For example, Letulé et al. (2014)19 investigated the perceptions of hand eczema in individuals without eczema compared with those with a history of the condition and found that 37.6% of participants without eczema suspected the disease to be infectious. In addition, Misery et al. (2007)20 reported that approximately 15% of couples involved in the study had misunderstandings and limited knowledge of the contagiousness of the disease. Finally, Roosta et al. (2010)11 also found that 23.6% of participants with atopic eczema stated that others believed their skin condition was contagious, compared with only 10.1% of those with acne. Along with stigmatization, such ‘contagion’ misconceptions may trigger the behavioural immune system and an aversive response in others (see Kong et al.21).

Few studies have investigated whether the presence of a dermatological condition influences willingness for social contact by others. Timms (2013)22 found that participants considered an individual with the appearance of acne as having lower friendship potential and rated them as less attractive than the same individual with the appearance of clear skin. Halioua et al. (2016)23 investigated perceptions of psoriasis among individuals without the condition and found that half had some level of reluctance for physical or social contact with people with psoriasis. A further study has investigated interpersonal aversion towards individuals with acne and psoriasis.24 Participants were more willing to engage in indirect contact with individuals with no visible dermatological condition compared with those with acne or psoriasis. To our knowledge there are no existing studies that have investigated the effect of visible atopic eczema on willingness for direct and indirect contact with a viewed individual.

Accordingly, in study 1, we explore the role of visible atopic eczema on willingness for contact. Specifically, we show participants pictures of faces or hands with no, mild or severe eczema and ask them to report their willingness to engage in direct contact (e.g. social interaction or relationship-based contact with the person) and indirect contact (e.g. contact with objects used by the person) with the pictured individual, to complete health anxiety and disgust propensity questionnaires, rate the impact of COVID-19 on their social withdrawal and rate how attractive they found the person viewed.

Health anxiety is an excessive and persistent fear of perceptual cues that imply a threat towards one’s physical wellbeing.25 Individuals with greater health anxiety are more likely to carry out avoidance behaviour in response to illness cues. Furthermore, disgust is an adaptive emotional response that encourages the avoidance of disease-causing pathogens.26 Some individuals may be more susceptible and sensitive to the experience of disgust than others (disgust propensity27). In this study, following the COVID-19 epidemic, social withdrawal due to COVID-19 was also investigated, as the pandemic may have a legacy, negatively affecting willingness for interpersonal contact. Participants were also asked to rate the attractiveness of the pictured person in each condition. It is hypothesized that participants will be significantly more willing to engage in both direct contact and indirect contact with a person depicted with no atopic eczema compared with the same person depicted with mild and severe atopic eczema. There may be an impact of the severity of atopic eczema, and the effects may be found with faces and hands, or be linked specifically to faces. Effects may also differ for direct and indirect measures and be associated with health anxiety, disgust propensity and social withdrawal following the COVID-19 pandemic, and the rated attractiveness of the person viewed.

Materials and methods

Study 1

An opportunity sample of 150 participants from the University of Manchester [mean (SD) age 20.4 (8.3) years, 37 men], all with normal or corrected-to-normal vision was used. We used the Gorilla Experiment Builder (www.gorilla.sc) to create and host our experiment.28 To detect a medium effect size (0.25) with 80% power, G*Power 3.129 suggested that a minimum sample size of 86 was needed (alpha = 0.05).

Materials and design

A mixed-participants design was employed; each participant was presented with three versions of the same face or hand, depicted with different levels of severity of atopic eczema: no atopic eczema, mild atopic eczema and severe atopic eczema (atopic eczema; within factor). Participants viewed faces or hands (stimuli type; between factor). The dependent variables were willingness for (i) direct contact and (ii) indirect contact, measured using a questionnaire. Direct contact refers to willingness to engage in direct interpersonal interaction and social closeness with the individual. Indirect contact refers to willingness to engage with objects or environments previously used by the individual, reflecting indirect or mediated forms of contact. Other factors that may influence willingness for contact were also measured, and these were health anxiety, disgust propensity and sensitivity, social withdrawal due to concerns about COVID-19 and person attractiveness.

Participants were shown a digital colour image depicting a 30-year-old woman of a White British background with and without visible facial atopic eczema taken from the Sun newspaper (portrait; sized 7.62 cm × 12.70 cm). The same photograph was used for each condition, but the appearance of the skin was altered. The original photograph was edited (using Adobe Photoshop) so that the same face image had no visible skin condition, mild atopic eczema or severe atopic eczema (face edited in each condition). A similar process was followed for the hand image, with a hand image with visible atopic eczema downloaded online and edited using Adobe Photoshop. All images were edited by the same experimenter and were reviewed by a consultant dermatologist (C.E.K.), who confirmed clinically appropriate skin appearances for the different atopic eczema severities.

Dependent variables

Five items drawn from Breheny (2007)30 were used to assess willingness for direct contact. While keeping the pictured person in mind, participants were asked to rate their willingness to meet, move next door to, work with, become friends with and move in with them. Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = ‘definitely unwilling’, 5 = ‘definitely willing’). The responses to each item were summed to obtain a score (range 5–25). A higher score suggested a greater willingness to engage in direct social contact with the person depicted.

Willingness for indirect contact was assessed using seven items adapted from Rozin et al.’s (1994)31 work on ‘interpersonal aversion’. Again, while keeping the depicted person in mind, participants were asked to rate their willingness to eat food served and prepared by them, use a pen and cup used by them, wear a sweater worn by them, use a rental car they have used and to sleep in a hotel bed they have previously slept in. Each of the seven items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = ‘definitely unwilling’, 5 = ‘definitely willing’). The responses to each statement were summed to obtain a score (range 7–35). A higher score indicated greater willingness to engage in indirect contact with the person depicted. Participant scores for willingness for direct contact and indirect contact were also summed to obtain an overall willingness for contact score for each condition (range: 12–60). A higher score suggested greater willingness for contact with the pictured individual.

Health anxiety was measured using the adapted version of the Whiteley Index: the Whiteley-732 [adapted by Fink et al. (1999)33 from 14 items to 7 items]. Participants answered seven statements about their health (e.g. ‘Do you worry a lot about your health?’) with a binary yes or no. The scale has one factor that assesses general ‘health anxiety’ (item 1) and two more specific subscales of ‘Illness Conviction’ (items 2, 4 and 6) and ‘Illness Worrying’ (items 3, 5 and 7). We calculated an overall health anxiety score by summing the responses to each item (no = 1, yes = 2; range: 7–14). A higher score indicates that the participant experiences greater health anxiety.

Participants completed the Disgust Propensity (i.e. the extent to which disgust is experienced in any particular situation) and Sensitivity (i.e. how intense the disgust experience is) Scale-Revised (DPSS-R27). Participants rated 16 statements (e.g. ‘I avoid disgusting things’) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = ‘never’, 5 = ‘always’). The responses from each item (both propensity and sensitivity items) were summed, to obtain a disgust score (range 16–80). A higher score indicates that the participant has greater disgust propensity and sensitivity.

The social impact of the COVID-19 pandemic was measured using a 12-item questionnaire. While this study was conducted after the COVID-19 lockdown in the UK, we included this measure as willingness for social contact score may be still affected by this factor. The scale was devised by modifying and adding newly created items to the Social Withdrawal Scale.34 Three different dimensions of withdrawal were measured: withdrawal from the community and wider world (items 1–4); withdrawal from friends and family (items 5–8); and emotional withdrawal (items 9–12). Participants rated each statement (e.g. ‘I feel less confident amongst other people than before’) on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = ‘agree’, 6 = ‘disagree’). Items 1, 4, 5, 8, 9 and 12 were reversely scored and ratings for each item were summed in order to get an overall score for each participant (range 12–72). A higher score indicated a greater negative social impact of COVID-19.

Participants were asked to rate the attractiveness of the depicted person using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = ‘very unattractive’, 7 = ‘very attractive’)

Procedure

Participants were presented with the study information, followed by a consent form. Once consent was obtained by selecting a tick-box, each participant completed the questionnaires (health anxiety, disgust propensity and sensitivity, social impact of COVID-19) in a random order. Participants were then presented with each of the three atopic eczema conditions consecutively, with the order of conditions counterbalanced across participants. After viewing each photograph, participants completed measures of their willingness to engage in direct and indirect contact with the pictured individual, and they rated the attractiveness of the pictured individual.

Study 2

This study replicated study 1, except that a completely between-participants design was used. In this experiment, participants only viewed a single face or hand, and they were randomly allocated to atopic eczema condition (no atopic eczema, mild atopic eczema and severe atopic eczema). This resulted in six experimental groups (3 × 2 between design). To detect a medium effect size (0.25) with 80% power, G*Power 3.129 suggested that a minimum sample size of 158 was needed (alpha = 0.05). In study 2, an opportunity sample of 168 participants [mean (SD) age 21.5 (9.2) years, 49 men], all with normal or corrected-to-normal vision was used (28 per group).

We used 3 × 2 mixed Anovas to investigate the main effect of atopic eczema (no, mild and severe; within), the main effect of stimuli type (faces, hands; between) and the two-way interaction between atopic eczema and stimuli type on direct contact, indirect contact, overall willingness for contact (direct and indirect scores summed) and attractiveness scores. They revealed a significant main effect of atopic eczema on direct contact [F(2, 296) = 30.29, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.17]; indirect contact [F(2, 296) = 83.53, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.36]; overall willingness for contact [F(2, 296) = 75.85, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.31]; and attractiveness [F(2, 296) = 127.55, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.46]. In all cases, post hoc pairwise comparisons showed that the comparisons were significant (all P-values <0.01). There was higher willingness for contact (and higher attractiveness ratings) towards people with no atopic eczema compared with mild atopic eczema, and mild atopic eczema compared with severe atopic eczema.

There was no significant main effect of stimuli type on direct contact [F(1, 148) = 0.94, P > 0.05, partial η2 = 0.006]; indirect contact [F(1, 148) = 0.54, P > 0.05, partial η2 = 0.004]; or overall willingness for contact [F(1, 148) = 0.74, P > 0.05, partial η2 = 0.005]. However, there was a significant main effect of stimuli type on attractiveness, with faces rated as more attractive than hands [F(1, 148) = 15.28, P < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.094].

There was no significant interaction between atopic eczema and stimuli type on direct contact [F(2, 296) = 1.79, P > 0.05, ­partial η2 = 0.012] but this interaction was significant for indirect contact [F(2, 296) = 6.92, P < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.045] and overall willingness for contact [F(2, 296) = 5.69, P < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.037]. For no atopic eczema, indirect contact and overall willingness for contact was significantly lower for faces than hands (P < 0.05). With mild and severe atopic eczema, there was no significant difference in indirect contact or willingness for contact between faces or hands. There was also a significant interaction between atopic eczema and stimuli type on attractiveness [F(2, 296) = 5.37, P < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.035]. With no atopic eczema, there was no significant difference in attractiveness for faces and hands (P > 0.05). However, with mild and severe atopic eczema, faces were rated as significantly more attractive than hands (P < 0.05).

Correlations and multiple regression analyses were conducted to investigate the relationship between overall willingness for contact and health anxiety, disgust propensity/sensitivity, the social impact of COVID-19 and the perceived attractiveness of the viewed person. Descriptive statistics for these variables are presented in Table 2. Preliminary analyses confirmed that assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity and multicollinearity were not violated, with variance inflation factor values <1.20 for all predictors. However, inspection of distributions indicated that most variables deviated from normality, as assessed by Shapiro–Wilk tests (P < 0.05), with the exception of the social impact of COVID-19 scores (P = 0.22). Consequently, Spearman’s rank-order correlations were used to examine bivariate associations between variables (see Table 3). At the bivariate level, perceived attractiveness was strongly and positively associated with overall willingness for contact (r = 0.540, P < 0.01). In contrast, health anxiety (r = 0.018), disgust propensity/sensitivity (r = −0.069) and the social impact of COVID-19 (r = 0.032) were not significantly correlated with overall willingness for contact. In addition, disgust propensity/sensitivity showed a moderate negative association with health anxiety (r = −0.379, P < 0.01) and a positive association with the social impact of COVID-19 (r = 0.265, P < 0.05), indicating shared variance between these constructs. No other correlations were observed (all P-values >0.05).

A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to assess whether health anxiety, disgust propensity/sensitivity, the social impact of COVID-19 and perceived attractiveness predicted overall willingness for contact when considered simultaneously. The regression model was statistically significant [F(4, 145) = 17.40, P < 0.001], accounting for 31% of the variance in overall willingness for contact (adjusted R2 = 0.31). Examination of the regression coefficients revealed a divergence between bivariate and multivariate effects. Perceived attractiveness emerged as a significant predictor of willingness for contact [β = 0.57, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.43–0.71, t = 8.25, P < 0.001]. In addition, ­disgust propensity/sensitivity was a significant negative predictor (β = −0.18, 95% CI −0.33, −0.03, t = −2.37, P = 0.02), despite not showing a significant bivariate correlation with willingness for contact. Health anxiety and the social impact of COVID-19 were not significant predictors when controlling for the other variables. This pattern suggests a suppression effect,35 whereby variance in disgust propensity/sensitivity shared with health anxiety and COVID-19-related social withdrawal was controlled in the multivariate model, allowing the unique negative association between disgust and willingness for contact to emerge. This relationship remained when the no atopic eczema condition was removed from the analysis, with the overall regression model significant [F(4, 145) = 18.7, P < 0.001]; a positive association between attractiveness and overall willingness for contact scores (standardized coefficient β = 0.68, 95% CI 0.45–0.72, t = 8.50, P < 0.001); and disgust scores negatively associated with overall willingness for contact scores (standardized coefficient β = −0.23, 95% CI −0.34, −0.04, t = −2.50, P = 0.01).

Study 2

Descriptive statistics for study 2 are presented in Table 1. As in study 1, participants showed generally high levels of willingness for both direct and indirect contact with the depicted individual.

A series of 3 × 2 between-participants Anovas examined the effects of atopic eczema severity (no, mild, severe) and stimulus type (face, hands) on direct contact, indirect contact, overall willingness for contact and attractiveness. Replicating study 1, there was a significant main effect of atopic eczema severity across all outcome measures: direct contact [F(2, 162) = 5.47, P < 0 .01, partial η2 = 0.06]; indirect contact [F(2, 162) = 6.71, P < 0 .01, partial η2 = 0.08]; overall willingness for contact [F(2, 162) = 8.63, P < 0 .001, partial η2 = 0.10]; and attractiveness [F(2, 162) = 13.05, P < 0 .001, partial η2 = 0.14]. In each case, willingness for contact and attractiveness ratings decreased with increasing atopic eczema severity. Stimulus type did not significantly affect direct contact, indirect contact or overall willingness for contact (all P-values >0.05), although faces were rated as more attractive than hands [F(1, 162) = 4.02, P < 0.05, partial η2 = 0.02]. No significant interactions between atopic eczema severity and stimulus type were observed for any dependent variable (all P-values >0.05).

Correlational analyses showed that perceived attractiveness was positively associated with overall willingness for contact (r = 0.404, P < 0.01), whereas health anxiety, disgust propensity/sensitivity and the social impact of the COVID-19 pandemic were not significantly related to willingness for contact. Disgust ­propensity/sensitivity was negatively associated with health anxiety and positively associated with the social impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, indicating shared variance between these ­constructs (see Table 3). A multiple regression analysis confirmed that the overall model was significant [F(4, 163) = 10.82, P < 0.001], explaining 21% of the variance in willingness for contact. Perceived attractiveness was a significant positive predictor (β = 0.44, P < 0.001), while disgust propensity/sensitivity was a significant negative predictor (β = −0.21, P = 0.005). Health anxiety and the social impact of the COVID-19 pandemic were not significant predictors.

Overall, study 2 replicated the key findings of study 1 using a fully between-participants design, confirming that reduced willingness for contact in response to visible atopic eczema is robust across experimental designs.

Discussion

In study 1, participants expressed significantly lower willingness for both direct and indirect contact with individuals depicted with visible atopic eczema compared with those shown with no skin condition, even when atopic eczema severity was mild. Increasing severity further reduced willingness for contact. These findings support the hypothesis that visible atopic eczema negatively relates to interpersonal engagement and extends previous work demonstrating social avoidance in response to other dermatological conditions such as acne and psoriasis.22,24 Reductions in willingness were observed for direct forms of social interaction (e.g. friendship and coworking) and indirect contact involving shared objects, suggesting that visible atopic eczema broadly affects perceptions of interpersonal risk rather than being limited to intimate or in-person contexts. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence that visible skin conditions influence social distance and aversion across multiple domains of interaction.23

A limitation of study 1 is the within-participants factor of atopic eczema, in which participants viewed multiple versions of the same face or hand differing only in atopic eczema severity. Although this design allowed for sensitive detection of differences across conditions, it may have encouraged relative comparisons between stimuli. To address this limitation and test the robustness of the observed effects, study 2 employed a fully between-­participants design in which each participant viewed only a single stimulus.

Study 2 replicated the principal findings of study 1, showing that visible atopic eczema reduced willingness for contact and that willingness generally decreased as eczema severity increased. Together, these results extend previous work on acne and psoriasis22–24 by showing that atopic eczema, despite being noncontagious, elicits comparable patterns of social avoidance. Importantly, these effects were robust across within- and between-participants designs, suggesting that reduced willingness for contact reflects a genuine response to visible dermatological cues rather than a methodological artefact.

The findings are consistent with the behavioural immune system account,18 which proposes that humans are predisposed to avoid perceptual cues that signal potential disease risk. Visible atopic eczema may act as such a cue, eliciting disgust and contamination concerns that motivate avoidance behaviour. This is particularly evident for indirect contact, where participants expressed reluctance to interact with objects previously used by the affected individual. Such responses align with the concept of sympathetic magic,36 whereby objects are believed to retain contaminating properties through contact, even in the absence of realistic infection risk. The fact that reduced willingness for contact was observed even for mild atopic eczema highlights the low perceptual threshold at which avoidance responses may be triggered and helps explain why individuals with atopic eczema frequently report stigma, anticipated rejection and social withdrawal.11,13

Across both studies, perceived attractiveness was the strongest predictor of willingness for contact, even when controlling for disgust propensity, health anxiety and COVID-19-related social withdrawal. This aligns with evidence that attractiveness shapes social judgements, including perceptions of health, trustworthiness and approachability.37,38 Attractiveness did not merely covary with atopic eczema severity, but independently predicted willingness for contact. Furthermore, higher disgust propensity was also associated with reduced willingness for contact, highlighting that appearance-based cues and affective sensitivity influence avoidance of people with visible skin conditions. In contrast, health anxiety and the social impact of COVID-19 did not reliably predict willingness for contact, suggesting avoidance is driven more by immediate perceptual and emotional responses than by broader health concerns. Indeed, the impact of COVID-19 decreased from study 1 to study 2, most probably reflecting the increased time from the pandemic when the studies were conducted. Our findings underscore the largely unconscious nature of stigma towards visible skin conditions, where negative social responses are common and difficult to shift through rational information alone. This is evident in research reporting elevated stigmatization in patients with visible skin diseases (e.g. psoriasis, acne and atopic dermatitis) across multiple social contexts39 and conceptual models of stigma in dermatology that highlight external and self-stigmatization processes.40 Future research should investigate how these perceptual and affective mechanisms interact with contextual, cultural and individual factors to shape social responses to visible dermatological conditions.

The lack of consistent interactions between atopic eczema severity and stimulus type indicates that the effects of visible atopic eczema are not face-specific, but extend to other visible body areas, such as the hands. However, only facial and hand presentations were examined here. Future research should test whether similar effects are observed when atopic eczema is shown in other clinically relevant formats, such as more localized skin patches or on less socially focal body areas, including the trunk. This would help determine whether reduced willingness for contact is driven primarily by lesion visibility per se, by the body site affected or by the social salience of the affected area. While faces were generally rated as more attractive than hands, atopic eczema-related reductions in willingness for contact were evident across both stimulus types. This suggests that visible dermatological cues exert a broad influence on social behaviour, with implications for everyday interactions in workplaces, shared environments and public spaces.

Several overall limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the participants were predominantly young, female and university-­educated, and the single stimuli depicted a young female face of a White British background. This limits the generalizability of the findings across genders, ages, cultures and ethnicities. In particular, atopic eczema visibility may be perceived differently across skin tones,41 and future research should explicitly examine atopic eczema presentation across different ethnicities and influence on social judgements. Furthermore, these findings may not be specific to atopic eczema, but instead may reflect ‘visible skin disease’ in a more general sense.

Secondly, although digitally edited images allowed for tight experimental control, they may lack ecological validity. In addition, the stimuli represented a relatively restricted range of eczema presentations and severity cues. Future research could incorporate a broader range of identities, dynamic stimuli or live social interactions. Advances in artificial intelligence-generated imagery may offer promising opportunities42 to create highly realistic, parametrically controlled variations in atopic eczema severity [e.g. redness (in more lightly pigmented skin) vs. scaling], although care must be taken to avoid ‘uncanny-valley’ effects43 that could independently influence social responses.

Thirdly, participants were not provided with a diagnostic label or symptom-related information about the depicted individual and were therefore left to interpret the condition from visual cues alone. As a result, responses may have been influenced not only by visible atopic eczema itself, but also by participants’ assumptions about symptoms such as itch or about possible contagious conditions. This may be particularly relevant for some of the ­indirect-contact items involving shared clothing or bedding, which could plausibly evoke concerns about transmissible conditions such as scabies. Future research could address this by manipulating diagnostic or symptom information directly, or by assessing participants’ beliefs about itch, contagion and contamination alongside willingness for contact.

Fourthly, the present research focused on observers’ perceptions and did not ask participants about their experiences of atopic eczema or skin disease. Individuals with lived experience of dermatological conditions may show different responses (see Van Beugen et al.39), potentially including reduced disgust sensitivity or greater awareness of noncontagiousness. Including matched participants with and without atopic eczema44 would allow future studies to examine how personal experience moderates social judgements and avoidance behaviour.

Our findings have important implications for understanding the psychosocial burden of atopic eczema. We support the view that reduced willingness for contact, particularly when driven by unconscious contamination fears, may contribute to the social withdrawal, reduced quality of life, and increased anxiety and depression reported by individuals with atopic eczema.45 Given that these responses are rooted in misperceptions of contagion, public education and awareness initiatives may play a critical role in reducing stigma (see Gori et al.46). In parallel, psychological interventions targeting internalized stigma and anticipated rejection may help individuals with atopic eczema better manage the social consequences of their condition.47 These findings are also relevant to cosmetic and aesthetic research, as subtle variations in visible skin appearance can have meaningful social consequences, highlighting the potential value of interdisciplinary approaches that integrate dermatology, cosmetic formulation and psychosocial research.48
