High-resolution distribution map of apple orchards in China based on multisource data
Authors: Wenqiang Xie, Yunkun Song, Xuefeng Cui, Xiaodong Yan, Shuaifeng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChina is the world’s leading apple producer and vital to global fruit supply and food security. Apple production depends on yield and planting area, both strongly influenced by climate. Regional climate variations cause differing cultivation suitability, exacerbating production disparities. Although climate models can project impacts on apples, the lack of high-resolution distribution data limits reliable large-scale assessment. Mapping apple orchards is more challenging than field crops due to complex growing conditions. To tackle this challenge, we developed the first 30-m national-scale apple orchard distribution map of China (AOMC) for 2019–2021, covering 96% of China’s total apple orchard area. This dataset is generated based on an apple-phenology-driven algorithm framework, which integrates apple phenology and multi-source geographical information with the time-weighted dynamic time warping algorithm. Validations revealed that the dataset accurately represented orchard locations (Overall accuracy: 87%) and correlated strongly with municipal-level statistics (R²: 0.892–0.925). The AOMC supports the monitoring of spatiotemporal changes in China’s apple production patterns, serves as critical input for crop models, and advances the sustainable development and climate resilience of apple industry.

Background & Summary

Apple (Malus pumila) is a deciduous fruit crop widely distributed in temperate regions, and holds significant agricultural value as a globally prominent fruit species. China is the leading apple producer globally, with a total apple orchard area of 1.9425 million hectares and a production volume of 44.066 million tons, contributing significantly to the global fruit supply and food security. Apple growth and development are highly sensitive to climatic conditions, and production and quality are significantly affected by climate change. High-resolution spatial distribution data of fruit crops in China serve as the foundation for projecting fruit yield and quality, while also assessing food security1–3. Alongside substantial achievements in the distribution mapping of staple crops4, the mapping of economic crops is now becoming a new research focus5. The existing CROPGRIDS dataset6 provides global apple harvested area data at a 10 km spatial resolution. Derived from interpolated statistical data, it is thus considered more appropriate for analyzing regional differences at broader scales7 rather than interpreting individual grid cells. Due to the complex and varied topography and climate in China, systematic identification of fruit cultivation areas at the national scale is constrained, with existing research largely limited to classification and identification within small regional areas. Yan et al. applied multi-temporal Sentinel-2 imagery and Random Forest classification to map apple orchards at the county level in Yantai, Shandong. Chen et al. developed an Orchard Mapping Index (OMI) based on the phenology of fruit trees and utilized Sentinel-2 imagery to map the distribution of various orchards across the Loess Plateau. However, the absence of high-resolution apple orchard spatial distributions limits the accuracy of large-scale yield and quality projections8, thereby impeding quantitative research on climate change’s impact on apple production9. Therefore, accurate and timely identification of apple orchard locations and areas is crucial for predicting apple yield and apple quality.

The integration of remote sensing interpretation with field survey samples has become an effective method for mapping crop cultivation distributions4. Compared with field surveys, remote sensing technologies offer more comprehensive data to monitor land use10. An increasing amount of satellite images has been extensively utilized in crop classification, with advancements in high-resolution imagery providing essential datasets to support identifying both crop and orchard lands11. Numerous studies have identified planting maps for major food crops (wheat, rice, and maize) at the county, regional levels12 and provincial scales13. These high-resolution crop thematic maps serve as the solid foundation for large-scale projections of crop yield and quality. Compared with research on food crops, research on orchard identification via remote sensing methods has been relatively limited, with most studies focused primarily on smaller regions, such as municipalities or provinces14. National-scale high-resolution distribution maps of apple orchards in China are unavailable, which hinders progress in related food security research9.

For crop and orchard mapping, both supervised and unsupervised classification methods combined with high-resolution remote sensing imagery have been extensively applied15. Machine learning dominates current classification methodologies16, which include decision trees17, random forest algorithm14, support vector machine algorithm18, and neural network classification methods19. However, these methods require great numbers of training samples at the state and national scales, and classification precision predominantly hinges on both the adequacy and reliability of field-collected reference data, which limits their performance at provincial and national levels20.

Phenology-based classification algorithms are extensively utilized in crop identification21, functioning under the premise that individual crop species demonstrate distinct seasonal phenological patterns applicable to spatiotemporal classification22. Compared with machine learning methods, phenology-based classification methods require fewer training samples at the national scale, making them more suitable for large-scale mapping of crop and orchard distributions23. Traditional phenology-based classification methods combine machine learning methods with phenological features from single or multiple time phases for crop classification15. Dynamic time warping (DTW) serves as a crop-cycle-driven vegetation mapping algorithm extensively employed that identifies crops by comparing the seasonal variations in vegetation indices among target crops and other classes24, but accuracy may be affected by image quality and environmental factors shifting apple orchard phenology across regions, leading to misclassifications24. The time-weighted dynamic time warping algorithm (TWDTW) offers an effective method for time series analysis25. This algorithm incorporates time weights to address seasonal variations in crops, ensuring a balance between shape matching and phenological changes. It has shown excellent performance in identifying various crops and forests while requiring only a small amount of training data23. Therefore, the TWDTW algorithm was employed in this study to ensure the reliability and national-scale consistency of the generated apple orchard dataset. Furthermore, the spatial distribution of apple orchards is also constrained by the regulation of policies and traditional agricultural practices, which makes the mapping process more complex. Integrating remote sensing, phenology-based methods, natural environmental factors, and socioeconomic conditions can increase the identification results accuracy.

The lack of high-resolution spatial distribution map for apple orchards limits the accuracy of large-scale yield and quality projections, hindering quantitative research on climate variability’s impact on apple production and quality at the national scale. Therefore, the objective of this study is to automatically identify apple orchards spatial distribution in China covering 2019–2021 with 30 m spatial resolution. The development of the dataset involved the following steps: (1) Screening potential areas of apple orchard spatial distribution by integrating topographic, climatic, and existing planting patterns; (2) Fusing multi-source satellite imagery to generate gap-free vegetation index composites; (3) Collecting representative samples across provinces within the potential areas identified in Step 1 to define standard phenological curves; (4) implementing the TWDTW algorithm to map orchards by quantifying the similarity between standard phenological curves and pixel-level trajectories, with classification thresholds calibrated using province-level statistical yearbook data; and (5) validating the results through field-level assessment and statistical comparison. This provides basic data for monitoring the spatial distribution and area of apple orchards, further supporting refined simulations of apple yield and quality at the national scale and accurate division of apple production regions.

Methods

Study area

Apple orchards are widely distributed throughout China. This study identified areas of apple orchards harvested from 2019 to 2021 in 13 provinces in China covering an area of 1.975 × 106 ha, including Shaanxi (SAX, 31.11%), Gansu (GS, 12.47%), Shandong (SD, 12.37%), Shanxi (SX, 7.24%), Liaoning (LN, 6.99%), Hebei (HB, 6.77%), Henan (HN, 5.90%), Xinjiang (XJ, 3.48%), Yunnan (YN, 2.74%), Sichuan (SC, 2.29%), Inner Mongolia (IM, 2.08%), Ningxia (NX, 1.43%), and Jiangsu (JS, 1.43%), which are the main apple producing provinces of China (Fig. 1) (https://data.stats.gov.cn/). These selected provinces constitute more than 96% of the total harvested areas of apple orchards. Agricultural production within the study region exhibits diversity. Staple crops are dominated by wheat and maize, while rice cultivation is primarily distributed in the southwestern areas. Among orchard types, apple orchards predominate. Peach, pear, and grape orchards are also common deciduous fruit tree types. Additionally, cultivation of evergreen fruit trees, such as citrus, occurs in the southwestern provinces.

Data collection

Statistical data

Apple orchard area data at provincial and municipal levels for 2019 to 2021 were obtained from the National Bureau of Statistics of China (NBS, https://data.stats.gov.cn/index.htm, last access: 26 July 2025). It provides information on the harvested area and production for 13 major apple cultivation provinces, which were used to identify apple orchards and evaluate the accuracy.

Satellite data

Surface reflectance from Landsat-8 Operational Land Imager (OLI)26 and Sentinel-2 Multispectral Instrument (MSI) (2018–2022)27 was processed to generate 16-day 30 m soil-adjusted vegetation index (SAVI) composites using the Google Earth Engine (GEE). After atmospheric correction and cloud removal, cloud-free data from both satellites were combined. Maximum value composites were generated for each 16 days, followed by histogram matching to fuse Landsat-8 and Sentinel-2 SAVI at 30 m resolution. To reconstruct high-fidelity time series, we implemented a two-step pre-processing chain: (1) Linear interpolation was applied to fill data gaps not exceeding 32 days. (2) A subsequent dynamic-threshold filter28 was then applied point-wise to detect and replace outliers caused by residual atmospheric noise. This filter flags a pixel as an anomaly if its SAVI value changes by more than 0.4 (|ΔSAVI| > 0.4) relative to adjacent valid observations and replaces it via interpolation, thereby preserving the natural phenological signal. The 16 d composited SAVI cloud-free frequencies from 2019 to 2021 for each pixel are shown in Fig. 2.

Good observation frequencies for the 16-day composited SAVI images in 2020 (a). Figures (b–d) illustrate the observation frequencies by province from 2019 to 2021.

The Sentinel-1 synthetic aperture radar (SAR) products29 were acquired through GEE. This product was processed to acquire the backscatter coefficient for each image and convert it into decibels (dB). The refined-Lee filter, as outlined by Abramov, et al.30, was applied for speckle noise correction. In this study, the vertical transmit and horizontal receive (VH) band was composited into 16-day maximum images (2018–2022) on GEE.

Climate data

To determine the suitable climatic conditions for apple cultivation, this study applied the CN05.1 dataset published by the Laboratory for Climate Studies of the China Meteorological Administration as observational data31. This dataset is generated by ground-based observational data from meteorological stations and essentially covers the entire land area of China (excluding statistical data for Taiwan Province).

Generation of standard SAVI curves

To derive the standard phenological curves for apple orchards, we collected survey samples through visual interpretation of very high-resolution Google Earth imagery from 2020. This process integrated known field locations and ancillary information. Information on locally representative, stable, and mature commercial orchards was gathered through multiple sources, including literature, agricultural maps, local government websites, and social media platforms. Within each province, samples were collected according to the spatial pattern of the main production areas, covering different ecological planting zones. Cities with large apple orchard area in the statistical yearbook were prioritized for sampling. To comprehensively reflect the province’s cultivation conditions, a suitable number of samples were also selected from other prefecture-level cities where apple cultivation is present. Reference samples (forest land, cropland and other orchards) were selected nearby to maintain consistent image conditions. Apple orchard samples were selected to represent key agronomic and topographic variations, while samples from other land cover types included other orchards, staple crops, forests, and built-up areas. To ensure representativeness, sample points were randomly and uniformly distributed within each delineated patch. Furthermore, all sample points were validated using vegetation index time series to confirm their phenological consistency. This study collected 9142 samples, comprising 6162 samples for apple orchards and 2980 samples for other land use types (Table 3 and Fig. 1). All samples were randomly divided into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets. Training samples were used for feature extraction and analysis, while validation samples were used to evaluate classification accuracy.

In this study, L is 0.5, which is recommended in its original paper and has been widely adopted in crop identifications33.

The SAVI time series was derived from 16-day composited images of Landsat 8 and Sentinel-2 to represent the seasonal variation in apple orchards. Owing to variations in climate and topographical conditions, apple orchards in each province exhibit unique phenological characteristics. For each province, apple orchard training samples were used to establish representative SAVI curves. Key phenological stages for each pixel were identified based on accumulated temperature34. Each SAVI curve was temporally adjusted to align all provincial training samples to a consistent phenological timeline. Given the stability of apple phenology34, we applied the 2020 standard SAVI time series to identify the spatial distribution of apple orchards for 2019–2021, thereby further examining the scalability of the method35.

Adjusted SAVI curves were averaged to generate standard seasonal curves for each province. Apple orchards across all 13 provinces showed similar seasonal patterns (Fig. 5). Apple trees enter the early growth stage before Day of Year (DOY) 81 with low SAVI. Throughout the flowering stage (DOY 81–129), SAVI increases rapidly as the apple trees bloom and grow. From DOY 129 to DOY 257, during the fruit set stage, the trees reach peak growth and then gradually stabilize, with the SAVI following a similar pattern. The fruit expansion and colouring stage is characterized by stable SAVI values. After DOY 257, ripening and harvest occur, followed by leaf fall and dormancy, leading to a gradual decline and eventual stabilization of SAVI.

Standard curves of SAVI for apple orchards in 13 provinces of the study area.

To differentiate apple orchards from other land cover types, we conducted a detailed comparative analysis of standard SAVI curves (Fig. 6). Apple orchards showed distinctive traits in Google Earth imagery, displaying rougher canopies than crops, smaller canopies than pear trees, and more regular spatial patterns than both pear orchards and forests. Significant phenological differences were evident between apple and other fruit trees (Fig. 6). The flowering period was optimal for identification: pear orchards flowered slightly earlier but had shorter phenological cycles, and also exhibited earlier autumn leaf drop, leading to faster SAVI decline. Notable differences were also found between apple and peach orchards. Apple orchards typically flower from March to May, with SAVI stabilizing by June, whereas peach orchard SAVI continues rising to peak in August or September. Throughout this period, apple SAVI values remained higher than those of peach. These distinct flowering phenology patterns allow reliable differentiation of apple orchards from other fruit crops.

Given the minimal variation in SAVI curves for apple orchards and forestlands, distinguishing between these land cover types using SAVI alone is inherently challenging. To overcome this limitation, Sentinel-1 SAR products were utilized for improved differentiation. According to the samples, the VH backscatter value in April, corresponding to the apple flowering period, was identified as a reliable discriminant. Specifically, the VH backscatter value for apple orchards in April is lower than −15 dB, whereas it is higher for forestlands (Fig. 6(b)). This discrepancy indicates that pixels with a VH value exceeding −15 dB are unlikely to correspond to apple orchards. By assigning greater dissimilarity to such pixels, the study effectively distinguished apple orchards from forestlands.

TWDTW algorithm

To map apple orchard spatial distributions in China, this study employed the TWDTW algorithm, an enhanced version of the DTW algorithm, widely recognized for its outstanding performance in crop classification and mapping applications36,37. The TWDTW algorithm was applied to measure the similarity between the standard SAVI time-series curves of apple orchards and the SAVI time-series curves of unclassified pixels within potential areas for apple orchards.

The principle of the TWDTW algorithm operates as follows:

First, we assume there exists a reference curve U of length n and an unclassified curve V of length m. An n × m matrix ψ is constructed from U and V, where each element ψi,j represents the absolute difference between Ui ∈ U (i = 1, …, n) and Vj ∈ V (j = 1, …, m), augmented with a Logistic time cost ωi,j acting as a penalty term.

Where gti,tj represents the temporal interval (in days) between two corresponding dates. The midpoint β and spread α were determined based on Maus et al.’s25 methodology and the phenological stage durations of apple trees, with values set to α = 0.1 and β = 50 days.

Using matrix ψ, we constructed a minimum-cost cumulative matrix D through recursive summation. The elements di,j of this matrix are subject to the following boundary conditions:

For all other elements di,j, starting from the top-left corner of matrix D as the origin, the computation progresses recursively by accumulating the minimum ψi,j value from three adjacent cells: left (di,j−1), upward (di−1,j), and diagonal upper-left (di−1,j−1). This recursive accumulation continues until reaching the bottom-right element dn,m of matrix D.

The TWDTW algorithm begins at the top-left cell d1,1 and fills the entire matrix D as described above, resulting in a total accumulated cost at dn,m, which is the TWDTW distance for the current alignment. Then, we shift the starting position of the unclassified curve V by one element and restart the computation (i.e., rebuild the matrix D for the new segment of V aligned with the entire reference curve U). Since the algorithm allows variable curve lengths, multiple segments of V are matched against U, producing multiple warping paths and thus multiple TWDTW distances. The minimal TWDTW distance among all segments is selected as the ‘optimal warping path’ and is defined as the ‘TWDTW difference’ between the curves U and V. A smaller TWDTW difference indicates higher similarity between V and U, implying a higher probability that the pixel belongs to the target feature.

In this study, we computed the TWDTW distance between the standard apple orchard SAVI curve U and each unclassified SAVI curve V within the potential areas. In order to capture the apple phenology more flexibly, we move the standard curve of each province forward and backward (the max time window less than 5-time intervals), calculate the distance between each pixel and each translated curve, and select the smallest distance as the final matching result of the pixel35. Pixels were ranked by ascending TWDTW distance, and cumulative areas were calculated. Provincial thresholds were determined using statistical orchard area data: the TWDTW distance of the terminal pixel where the cumulative area matched the statistical value became the threshold. Pixels with TWDTW distance smaller than or equal to the provincial threshold were mapped as apple orchards.

Validation methods

The identification accuracy for apple orchards was evaluated in two aspects: (1) validation at the field level via a validation set of survey samples and (2) comparison with the apple orchard area statistics from the statistical yearbooks. The confusion matrix is a common method for evaluating classification accuracy, typically presented in an n-by-n format38. It provides key accuracy metrics, including overall accuracy (OA), producer’s accuracy (PA), and user’s accuracy (UA), each reflecting different aspects of classification performance. In image accuracy assessment, the confusion matrix is used to compare identification results with ground truth data, visualizing the accuracy within the matrix39. To further evaluate the effectiveness of the identification process, the coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean absolute error (RMAE) were applied.

Data Records

The 30 m distribution maps of apple orchards in China (AOMC) from 2019–2021 are available online at figshare40. The dataset is provided in Geotiff (*.tif) format. Data named “AOMC_30_<province>_<year>.tif” means apple orchard map of China for 2019–2021. The dataset is provided in the “Asia_North_Albers_Equal_Area_Conic” spatial reference system, and the spatial extent of the dataset ranges from 18°26′ N to 50°01′ N and 53°22′ E to 128°17′ E. The pixel values in the dataset are: (1: apple orchard; 0: non-apple orchard). These data can be opened and applied in geographical software and other statistical applications.

Technical Validation

To validate the reliability, the apple orchard spatial distribution was first identified at the provincial scale using the previously discussed methods and then validated with survey samples and statistical data. The identification accuracy for apple orchards was evaluated using two methods: (1) field-level validation with a validation set of survey samples and (2) comparison with apple orchard area data from yearbooks.

Statistical comparison

Compared with agricultural statistics from various provinces from 2019–2021, the method provided consistent apple orchard area estimates for China. The estimated apple orchard area for 2020 demonstrated strong agreement with the agricultural statistical data obtained at the municipal level from statistical yearbooks (Fig. 7), with an R² of 0.912 (N = 179), a corresponding MAE of 3.399 Kha, and an RMAE of 31.7%. Similarly, the identified apple orchard areas for 2019 (R² = 0.925) and 2021 (R² = 0.892) also strongly agreed with the municipal-level agricultural statistics, respectively.

Uncertainties and prospects

While our method can well identify apple harvest areas in China, some uncertainties still exist.

First, due to the differences in sensor parameters and observation limitations, the quality of remote sensing images is uneven. Although the Sentinel-2 data of the same period is selected for image fusion and filling, there still exist some potential errors. In addition, the SAVI dataset has a time interval of 16 days, which may introduce bias when accurately capturing the phenological characteristics of apple orchards.

Second, to differentiate apple orchards from other orchards and forested areas, this study analysed standardized SAVI curves across vegetation types (Fig. 4). Phenological characteristics enabled discrimination among crops; however, class separability remains significantly compromised by spectral resemblance between apple and pear orchards during critical phenological phases. Notably, this similarity persists even though pears demonstrate an abbreviated phenological cycle with earlier phenophase onset compared to apples. This finding aligns with Chen, et al.41 empirical research in Linyi, Shandong, which documented spectral confusion between these phenologically analogous temperate pome crops in medium-resolution imagery. Future enhancements could integrate higher spatiotemporal resolution data with: (1) multidimensional spectral-temporal metrics, (2) red-edge band analysis14, and (3) spatial texture features42. The development of AI-driven spectral-phenological-topographic decoupling models shows particular promise for improving inter-crop discrimination43,44.

Third, the identification accuracy for Xinjiang was lower than that for other provinces. Apple cultivation in Xinjiang is mainly concentrated plantings in oasis cores (Aksu45, Xinjiang) alongside scattered, small-scale orchards in other regions. This disparity results in a provincial phenological curve that is less effective in characterizing marginal orchards, especially those in counties contributing under 0.5% to the provincial apple area. This can introduce local confusion between small orchards and other sparse vegetation in similar settings. Since Xinjiang contributes only about 3.48% to the national apple harvested area (Fig. 1) and the method performs reliably in its core production regions such as Aksu (Fig. 10(c1)), the impact of this provincial uncertainty on the national-scale dataset can be regarded as limited.

Future studies could incorporate object-based recognition approaches alongside satellite imagery with enhanced spatial and temporal resolutions to potentially achieve more precise mapping outcomes. Additionally, classification accuracy may be influenced by the reliability of meteorological datasets, agricultural statistics, and land-use products. Therefore, as high-precision basic farmland datasets and updated land-use inventories become increasingly accessible, they would likely provide substantial support for improving orchard identification accuracy. Usage Notes

China is the leading apple producer globally, accounting for about 47% of global apple production. Using available Landsat-8 and Sentinel datasets, along with multisource data, including geographic information, remote sensing products, and meteorological data, we integrated various phenological conditions of apples in China to generate the first 30 m national-scale apple orchard distribution map in China (AOMC). This study mapped the distribution of apple orchards across 13 provinces in China (2019–2021), covering 96% of the nation’s apple cultivation area. The method effectively identifies apple orchard distribution areas with minimal training sample data. Validation results based on 2,980 samples obtained from Google Earth indicated that the UA, PA, and OA in 2020 across the 13 major apple-producing provinces in China were 84.79%, 95.47%, and 87.00%, respectively. The distribution map represents the locations of apple orchards within these 13 provinces. These maps help monitor the distribution and extent of apple orchards, providing essential data for national-scale, refined simulations of apple production.
