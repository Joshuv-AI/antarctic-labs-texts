Neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy versus neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in patients with borderline resectable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Jun-Peng Lin, Feng-Nian Zhuang, Wei-Jie Chen, Zhou Lin, Yu-Jie Chen, Xiao-Feng Chen, Hui Lin, Hao He, Peiyuan Wang, Hang Zhou, Wen-Wei Wei, Peng-Qiang Gao, Shuo-Yan Liu, Feng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal treatment strategy for patients with borderline resectable (cT3br) esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains unclear. Thus, the aim of this study was to compare the perioperative outcomes and prognoses between neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy (NACI) and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT) in cT3br ESCC patients.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, patients with cT3br ESCC who received either NACI or NCRT followed by surgery between January 2016 and December 2022 were identified. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to minimize the influence of confounding factors. Survival curves were constructed and analyzed using Kaplan–Meier log-rank survival analysis.Results97 patients with cT3br ESCC received NACI and 111 received NCRT. After PSM, the R0 resection rate (85.7% vs. 90.5%, P = 0.341) and pCR rate (19.0% vs. 26.2%, P = 0.269) were comparable between two groups, while the MPR rate in the NACI group was significantly lower than in the NCRT group (32.1% vs. 56.0%, P = 0.002). However, NCRT was associated with a greater risk of severe postoperative complications (21.4% vs. 6.0%, P = 0.004). The 3-year overall survival (69.4% vs. 55.7%, P = 0.242), disease-free survival (55.7% vs. 49.7%, P = 0.242), overall recurrence (32.1% vs. 29.8%, P = 0.739), and recurrence patterns were comparable between the NACI and NCRT groups.ConclusionNACI followed by surgery appears to be a safe and effective treatment strategy for patients with borderline resectable ESCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06672-3.Keywords: Borderline resectable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, Neoadjuvant immunotherapy, Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, Perioperative outcomes, Survival

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) ranks sixth overall as one of the leading causes of cancer death worldwide, and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) accounts for more than 80% of all cases [1, 2]. Owing to the lack of serosa in the esophagus, EC is prone to invade adjacent organs and structures, including the trachea, aorta, and pulmonary vein. Thus, most ESCC cases are usually locally advanced at diagnosis. Since current preoperative examination methods cannot accurately determine whether the tumor can be completely removed, some patients with suspected organ involvement cannot be definitively diagnosed with stage T4b disease, which is considered borderline resectable ESCC. In 2022, the 12th edition of the Japanese Classification of Esophageal Cancer defined this type of EC as a T3br tumor [3].

According to guidelines, neoadjuvant therapy, especially neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT) followed by surgery, is the standard treatment for locally advanced resectable EC. For ESCC patients with adjacent organ involvement that cannot be resected, definitive chemoradiotherapy (DCRT) without planned esophagectomy is considered the main treatment method [4]. However, there is currently no unified consensus on the optimal treatment strategy for borderline resectable (T3br) ESCC. The CROSS and NEOCRTEC5010 trials have shown that NCRT plus surgery can improve the prognosis of patients with locally advanced EC compared with surgery alone. However, 49% and 35% of patients still experience postoperative recurrence and metastasis, respectively [5, 6]. Moreover, Tang et al. reported that NCRT did not significantly improve the long-term survival of patients with locally advanced ESCC [7]. In recent years, the emergence of immunotherapy has significantly changed the treatment strategy for ESCC. Multiple prospective clinical trials have shown that neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy (NACI) is safe and feasible for patients with locally advanced ESCC, with pathological complete response (pCR) and major pathological response (MPR) rates ranging from 20 to 50% and 45.2% to 72.0%, respectively [8–11]. Moreover, the latest phase III randomized controlled clinical trial revealed that for patients with resectable locally advanced ESCC, NACI has a better pCR rate and tolerable safety than does neoadjuvant chemotherapy [12]. However, there is still controversy over whether NACI is better than NCRT [13–16], especially for T3br ESCC, as there are no relevant studies on this specific group. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare NACI with NCRT followed by surgery in terms of perioperative outcomes and prognosis in T3br ESCC patients, with the goal of identifying the optimal treatment strategy.

Methods

Patients

Between January 2016 and December 2022, our study retrospectively identified patients with borderline resectable locally advanced ESCC (cT3br) who received either NACI or NCRT, followed by esophagectomy at Fujian Cancer Hospital. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) histologically and clinically proven cT3br ESCC; (2) 18–80 years old; and (3) underwent neoadjuvant therapy and surgery. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) the presence of other malignancies; (2) no surgery; and (3) missing clinical or follow-up data. Based on the above, 208 patients were included in the study (Fig. 1); 97 patients received NACI followed by surgery (NACI group), and 111 patients received NCRT followed by surgery (NCRT group). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fujian Cancer Hospital (K2024-042–01), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. This retrospective study has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria (Supplemental Digital Content 1) [17].

Definition of borderline resectable tumor (cT3br)

T3 tumors were classified into borderline resectable tumors (cT3br) and resectable tumors (cT3r) according to the 12th edition of the Japanese Classification of Esophageal Cancer [3]. Additionally, borderline resection tumors are defined as having no T4 findings on imaging studies. When there was partial loss of the fat plane between the aorta and esophagus with nearly 90° contact, or partial loss of the fat plane with findings of significant protrusion into the pulmonary vein or deformation of the trachea or bronchus due to high compression of the tumor without extensive airway lumen irregularities suggesting evident invasion, the primary tumor was evaluated as cT3br. The representative cases of cT3br ESCC are shown in Supplemental Fig. 1.

Treatment protocols

In this study, patients in the NACI group received 1–4 cycles of a single immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) plus concurrent chemotherapy. The immune checkpoint inhibitors used were pembrolizumab (200 mg), camrelizumab (200 mg), tislelizumab (200 mg), sintilimab (200 mg), nivolumab (200 mg), and toripalimab (200 mg), which were intravenously administered once every 3 weeks. NCRT group patients received neoadjuvant radiotherapy followed by concurrent chemotherapy, with a total radiotherapy dose of 36–40 Gy in 20 fractions administered 5 days per week. All patients in both groups received at least 2 cycles of platinum-based two-drug combination chemotherapy, such as platinum (cisplatin or carboplatin) combined with Taxol (paclitaxel or albumin-bound paclitaxel), with an interval of 21 days for each cycle. The dosage and administration of chemotherapeutic agents and ICIs were determined by the physician in charge, based on the patient’s condition and body surface area. The method of neoadjuvant therapy was mainly based on the patient’s personal physical condition, tumor staging, and the opinions of multidisciplinary discussions, combined with the patient's personal opinions.

The operation was performed 4–8 weeks after the completion of the neoadjuvant therapy. All patients underwent open or minimally invasive esophagectomy, including McKeown, Ivor-Lewis, and Sweet esophagectomy. Completion of a two-field lymphadenectomy was standard, and a three-field lymphadenectomy was performed in some patients with suspected metastatic lymph nodes in the cervical area.

Postoperative pathology and complication evaluation

Pathological specimens from each patient, were evaluated by two experienced pathologists. Tumor staging was performed based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM Staging Manual, 8th Edition [18]. R0 resection was defined as the absence of viable tumor cells within 1 mm of the resection margin (distal, proximal, or circumferential) [19]. Pathologic complete response (pCR) was defined as the absence of tumor cells in the entire sample, including the lymph nodes (ypT0N0M0), while major pathologic response (MPR) was defined as < 10% viable residual tumor cells in the sample. The Mandard tumor regression grade (TRG) scoring system was used to evaluate tumor response as follows: TRG1, absence of vital residual tumor (pCR); TRG2, vital residual tumor < 10% of the original tumor area; TRG3, 10–50%; TRG4, > 50%; and TRG5, absence of regressive changes [15, 20]. The definition and classification of postoperative complications were based on the Esophageal Complications Consensus Group Complications and the Clavien–Dindo classification [21, 22].

Follow-up and survival

Postoperative follow-up evaluations were performed every 3 months for 2 years and then every 6 months thereafter. Most patients routinely received physical examinations, upper gastrointestinal X-rays, and enhanced computed tomography (CT) scans. Positron emission tomography (PET)/CT and endoscopic examination were employed if necessary. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of last contact or death. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time interval from the date of the operation to the date of tumor recurrence, death, or the last follow-up. Recurrence was diagnosed via radiologic findings or biopsies of suspicious lesions detected during surveillance after surgery. In this study, recurrences were categorized based on the site involved as locoregional, distant, or multiple. Locoregional recurrence was defined as intraesophageal, anastomotic, or regional lymph node recurrence, whereas distant recurrence was defined as metastasis to distant organs, nonregional lymph nodes (such as supraclavicular or paraaortic lymph nodes), or the peritoneum [23]. The presence of recurrent disease at two or more sites was categorized as multiple recurrences.

Statistical analysis

One-to-one propensity score matching (PSM) was used to adjust the imbalances in potential confounding factors between the NACI and NCRT groups via the matching package in R version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing) [24]. The propensity score for an individual was calculated according to the covariates of age, sex, BMI, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score, history of smoking, history of alcohol consumption, tumor location, comorbidities, cN stage, and adjuvant therapy. A matching approach was then conducted using nearest neighbor matching with replacement, and the caliper was set at 0.5.

The baseline characteristics are summarized as the mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as the frequency and proportion for categorical variables. Continuous variables were analyzed via Student’s t test or the Wilcoxon test. Categorical variables were compared between the two groups by the χ [2] test or Fisher's exact test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify potential prognostic factors for the MPR. The significant factors noted in the univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. OS and DFS curves were plotted using the Kaplan‒Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis was utilized for identifying independent risk factors for prognosis. Two-sided P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS® version 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA).

Results

Study population

A total of 208 patents were screened in our study, of whom 97 patients in the NACI group and 111 patients in the NCRT group met our inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the two groups are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the patients in the two groups was comparable (NACI: 61.0; NCRT: 59.2), with males being the majority (NACI: 77.3%; NCRT: 83.8%) and tumors located mainly in the middle thoracic segment (NACI: 60.8%; NCRT: 58.6%). Notably, 56 of 97 (57.7%) patients in the NACI group and 52 of 111 (46.8%) patients in the NCRT group received adjuvant therapy, with no significant difference (P = 0.117). The two groups differed significantly only in the clinical N stage (P = 0.043). However, there were no statistically significant differences in the other clinical characteristics between the two groups (all P > 0.05). After 1:1 matching by propensity score, 84 patients in the NACI group and 84 patients in the NCRT group were included in the subsequent analyses. After PSM, the differences in baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups (all P > 0.05, Table 1).

Operation and pathology

Before PSM, the operation time of the NACI group was significantly greater than that in the NCRT group (299.5 vs. 270.6, P = 0.004), but there was no statistically significant difference in intraoperative bleeding between the two groups (185.1 vs. 209.0 ml, P = 0.102; Table 2). In terms of postoperative pathology, the NACI group had a higher TRG score (TRG 2: 14.4% vs. 27.0%, TRG 4–5: 45.4% vs. 18.9%, P = 0.001; Fig. 2A) and a lower MPR rate (36.1% vs. 56.8%, P = 0.003; Table 2 and Fig. 2B). However, the R0 resection rate (85.6% vs. 91.0%, P = 0.222) and pCR rate (20.6% vs. 27.0%, P = 0.281) were similar between the two groups. The other pathological variables were not significantly different between the two groups (all P > 0.05).

Complications

The postoperative morbidity and mortality characteristics of the two groups are shown in Table 4. Before PSM, the overall incidence of complications in the NACI group was significantly lower than in the NCRT group (24.7% vs. 38.7%, P = 0.031). Compared to those in the NCRT group, fewer patients in the NACI group experienced pulmonary infections (17.5% vs. 30.6%, P = 0.028), anastomotic leakage (2.1% vs. 13.5%, P = 0.004), and respiratory failure (1.0% vs. 7.2%, P = 0.039). Additionally, according to the Clavien‒Dindo classification of complications, more patients in the NCRT group experienced higher-grade complications than did those in the NACI group (P = 0.016). There was no significant difference in the rates of other complications between the two groups (both P > 0.05). Consistent results were observed for complications with a Clavien‒Dindo classifications grade > 2. Notably, 3 patients (3.1%) in the NACI group and 8 patients (7.2%) in the NCRT group died within 90 days, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups. The mean length of hospital stay in the NACI group was significantly shorter than in the NCRT group (10.6 vs. 15.1 days, P < 0.001). After PSM, the rates of complications (grade > 2) (6.0% vs. 21.4%, P = 0.004), pulmonary infection (grade > 2) (4.8% vs. 19.0%, P = 0.007), anastomotic leakage (grade > 2) (1.2% vs. 9.5%, P = 0.034), and respiratory failure (grade > 2) (0.0% vs. 7.1%, P = 0.028) in the NACI group were still significantly lower than those in the NCRT group.

Survival and recurrence

After the median [IQR] follow-up time of 25 (14–40) months, patients who received NACI had a better 3-year OS rate than did those who received NCRT (71.8% vs. 52.0%, P = 0.039; Fig. 3A) before PSM. However, there were no significant differences in 3-year DFS between the two groups (58.8% vs. 47.0%, P = 0.236; Fig. 3B). After PSM, the 3-year OS (69.4% vs. 55.7%, P = 0.242; Fig. 3C) and DFS (55.7% vs. 49.7%, P = 0.242; Fig. 3D) rates of the NACI group were comparable to those in the NCRT group. And multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis revealed that R1 resection and MPR were independent prognostic factors for OS (HR (95%CI): 2.379 (1.248–4.535), P = 0.008; HR (95%CI): 0.451 (0.249–0.814), P = 0.008; Supplemental Table 1) and DFS (HR (95%CI): 2.039 (1.109–3.749), P = 0.022; HR (95%CI): 0.515 (0.311–3.749), P = 0.010; Supplemental Table 2).

After PSM, 27 patients (32.1%) in the NACI group and 25 patients (29.8%) in the NCRT group experienced recurrence, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.739; Table 5). There was no statistically significant difference between the NACI and NCRT groups in terms of locoregional recurrence (9.5% vs. 7.1%, P = 0.577), distant recurrence (20.2% vs. 17.9%, P = 0.694), or multiple recurrences (2.4% vs. 4.8%, P = 0.682). Additionally, there was no significant difference in any type of locoregional or distant recurrence (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

At present, there is no unified consensus on the optimal neoadjuvant therapy strategy for T3br ESCC. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the perioperative outcomes and survival of NACI and NCRT followed by surgery in patients with T3br ESCC. Our study revealed that the R0 resection rate and pCR rate of the NACI group were comparable to those of the NCRT group. Although the NCRT group had lower TRG scores and higher MPR rates than did the NACI group, there was no significant difference in survival or recurrence patterns between the two groups. Moreover, regarding postoperative complications, the NACI group had a lower incidence of severe complications (grade > 2) and a shorter hospital stay than did the NCRT group. Thus, NACI followed by surgery is a safe and effective treatment option for T3br ESCC patients compared to NCRT.

In recent years, multiple clinical trials have confirmed the role of neoadjuvant therapy in locally advanced esophageal cancer. According to the results of the CROSS and NEOCRTEC5010 studies, NCRT followed by surgery has become the standard treatment for resectable locally advanced ESCC [5, 6]. However, immunotherapy, as an important component of cancer treatment, has also received increasing attention. The immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) mainly used in this study were PD-1 inhibitors, including pembrolizumab, which could activate the human immune system by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway, helping to recognize and attack tumor cells [25, 26]. In recent years, multiple studies had confirmed that PD-1 inhibitors had shown good clinical efficacy in the treatment of various tumors [27–29]. And several studies have confirmed that NACI is safe and effective for locally advanced ESCC [8, 9, 12]. A meta-analysis involving 21 phase II trials revealed that the pCR rate and MPR rate of locally advanced ESCC patients receiving NACI were 32.4% and 49.4%, respectively [30]. Multiple previous studies have shown that NACI and NCRT achieve similar pCR rates in the treatment of locally advanced ESCC [14, 15, 31]. Nevertheless, Xiao et al. reported that NCRT could achieve a better tumor therapeutic response than NACI [13]. Yang et al. reported that the MPR rate in the NCRT group was significantly better than that in the NACI group [14]. Currently, there are no relevant studies comparing the therapeutic efficacy of NACI and NCRT for T3br ESCC. Our study first confirmed that the pCR rates in the NACI group and the NCRT group were similar, at 19.0% and 26.2%, respectively. Moreover, there was no significant difference in R0 resection rates between the two groups, which were 85.7% and 90.5%, respectively. However, the MPR rate of the NCRT group (56.0%) was significantly better than that in the NACI group (32.1%), and multivariate analysis confirmed this finding. Furthermore, the TRG score in the NACI group was significantly lower than that in the NCRT group, which may be related to the local direct antitumor effect of radiotherapy on the primary tumor.

In addition to treatment response, postoperative complications are also important factors affecting the prognosis and quality of life for EC patients [32, 33]. Previous studies have shown that neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy may increase the severity and mortality rate of postoperative complications in patients with EC [34, 35]. In this study, we found that the overall incidence of complications in the NACI group after PSM was 23.8%, which was comparable to that in the NCRT group (34.5%). However, according to the Clavien–Dindo classification, the severity of complications in the NCRT group was significantly greater than that in the NACI group. Thus, we further analyzed the incidence of severe complications (> grade 2) and reported that the incidence of severe complications (> 2 grade) in the NCRT group (21.4%) was significantly lower than that in the NACI group (6.0%). Furthermore, the incidence of severe pneumonia, anastomotic leakage, and respiratory failure was significantly greater in the NCRT group compared to that in the NACI group. This also resulted in a significantly longer average hospitalization time in the NCRT group than in the NACI group. This phenomenon may be due to local tissue fibrosis caused by radiotherapy, which increases the difficulty of tissue dissociation and leads to a higher incidence of postoperative complications [36]. Moreover, NCRT could also affect the cardiopulmonary function of patients with EC [37, 38].

In terms of prognosis, some studies have shown that locally advanced ESCC patients who receive NACI have comparable OS to those who receive NCRT [13, 14]. However, Yu et al. reported that the OS in the NACI group was significantly better than that in the NCRT group [15]. Our study revealed that for T3br ESCC patients, the 3-year OS (69.4% vs. 55.7%) and 3-year DFS (55.7% vs. 45.7%) rates in the NACI group were comparable to those in the NCRT group. After a median follow-up of 25 months, 27 patients (32.1%) in the NACI group and 25 patients (29.8%) in the NCRT group experienced recurrence. In terms of the recurrence pattern, both groups were primarily affected by distant metastasis, followed by local recurrence and multiple metastases, which is consistent with the findings of a previous study [23]. Moreover, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of specific recurrence sites after surgery. Therefore, although the treatment response of NACI patients was inferior to that of NCRT patients, there was no significant difference in long-term survival or recurrence patterns between the two groups. The reason for this phenomenon may be that the local antitumor effect of NACI is not as strong as that of NCRT. However, NACI could improve the long-term prognosis of T3br ESCC patients by regulating human immune function and enhancing systemic antitumor effects [39].

This study has several limitations. First, patients with T3br ESCC were included from only a single center, which may have introduced selection bias. Additionally, personal factors such as economic status may introduce bias in the selection of neoadjuvant treatment regimens. To mitigate this, propensity score matching was used to reduce potential interference. However, there are still some unmeasured confounding factors, such as the patient's personal economic status and the state of the tumor immune microenvironment. Second, multiple ICIs were used in the NACI group, which may have affected the results. Thirdly, due to the small sample size of this study, it may limit the statistical power and lead to type II error. Given these limitations, the generalizability of the results may be limited. Thus, future multicenter prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm the findings of this study. Third, owing to the short follow-up period, we analyzed only the 3-year OS and DFS rates. We will continue to follow these patients and analyze longer-term outcomes.

Conclusion

This study revealed that although NACI had a lower MPR rate and higher TRG scores in the treatment of T3br ESCC than did NCRT, the survival and recurrence patterns of the two groups were comparable. Moreover, patients who receive NACI had a lower incidence of severe postoperative complications. Therefore, NACI following surgery is a safe and effective treatment option for T3br ESCC.
