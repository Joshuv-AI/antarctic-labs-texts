Predicting Interval Pulmonary Metastasis in Extremity Soft Tissue Sarcoma Treated with Preoperative Radiotherapy
Authors: Sadegh Saberi, Alireza Talebi, Hamed Naghizadeh, Mohaddeseh Bahmani, Hadi Jahanbin, Seyyed Saeed Khabiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Preoperative radiotherapy (RT) is widely used in high-risk extremity soft tissue sarcoma (STS) because of its benefits in local control and functional preservation. However, the interval between diagnosis and definitive surgery raises concern regarding the potential development of interval distant metastases, particularly in aggressive tumors. Data defining the incidence and predictors of such progression remain limited.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with nonmetastatic extremity STS treated with standardized preoperative RT followed by limb-sparing surgery between 2018 and 2024. All patients underwent baseline staging and mandatory preoperative chest computed tomography. The primary end point was new distant metastasis detected before surgery. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors, and a clinical risk score was developed.Results:Among 378 patients, interval distant metastases occurred in 29 (7.8%), almost exclusively involving the lungs. Tumor size greater than 12 cm, synovial sarcoma histology, FNCLCC grade 3, and age 50 years or younger were independently associated with interval metastatic progression. A 4-factor risk score stratified patients into low, intermediate, and high-risk groups, with metastasis rates of 2.4%, 8.4%, and 25.6%, respectively.Discussion:Interval metastasis during preoperative RT is rare and linked to unfavorable baseline tumor biology. This supports the treatment's feasibility for most patients and highlights the need for routine preoperative chest restaging. Interval metastasis in a standardized preoperative RT protocol for extremity STS occurs mainly in patients with adverse tumor characteristics. Routine preoperative chest CT restaging is necessary, and a simple 4-factor risk score may identify patients needing enhanced surveillance and personalized counseling.Level of Evidence:Prognostic Level IV.

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) are a heterogeneous group of malignant mesenchymal tumors, accounting for about 1% of adult cancers, with a known propensity for pulmonary metastasis and local recurrence1,2. For limb and limb-girdle lesions, limb-sparing surgery with wide oncologic margins is crucial for curative-intent treatment; however, adjuvant therapies are vital to optimize local control and long-term outcomes3,4. Radiotherapy (RT) is key in managing high-risk extremity STS. Combined with wide excision, RT reduces local recurrence versus surgery alone. Both preoperative and postoperative RT are endorsed by major oncology guidelines, with practice patterns varying across institutions and continents5-7. Preoperative RT offers advantages such as smaller radiation targets, better dosimetry, lower long-term fibrosis and lymphedema rates, and improved resectability in selected cases8,9. Conversely, it is linked to higher acute wound complications and introduces a waiting period between diagnosis and surgery10. Standard protocols include pathological confirmation, baseline staging, RT planning, a 5-week treatment, and a post-RT recovery of 6 to 8 weeks, resulting in about 3 to 4 months before surgery11,12.

A critical oncological concern is whether this interval allows subclinical systemic disease to progress to detectable metastases, particularly in large, high-grade tumors13. However, literature on this question is sparse. No study has quantified the incidence of interval metastasis in an initially M0 extremity STS population or identified patients at greatest risk14. Clarifying the incidence and predictors of interval metastatic progression is important because it informs the oncologic safety of preoperative RT, may identify patients needing more intensive surveillance or altered treatment sequencing, and provides a framework for individualized patient counseling14,15. Therefore, we conducted a single-center cohort study of consecutive patients with extremity STS treated with preoperative RT and standardized restaging. Our aims were to (1) quantify the incidence and pattern of new metastatic disease between baseline staging and presurgical imaging, (2) identify independent clinical and pathological predictors of interval metastasis, and (3) develop a simple risk stratification score for routine clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at a high-volume tertiary referral sarcoma center between January 2018 and December 2024. All consecutive patients treated with preoperative external-beam RT followed by limb-sparing surgery were screened for inclusion. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1403.506) and adhered to the STROBE reporting guidelines for observational cohort studies16.

Patient Selection

Patients aged ≥18 years with extremity STS were eligible. Superficial lesions (<5 cm) had excisional biopsy, others had imaging-guided core needle biopsy. Preoperative RT is standard for FNCLCC grade 2 to 3 sarcomas. Low-grade tumors considered for RT after tumor board approval if they showed high-risk imaging features like signal heterogeneity on T2-weighted/STIR sequences, irregular enhancement on T1 postcontrast, infiltrative margins, or proximity to major neurovascular structures17,18. Tumor grade was assigned on diagnostic biopsy, but multidisciplinary team (MDT) treatment decisions also incorporated radiologic and clinical features when biopsy grade appeared discordant with tumor behavior. In our cohort, 8 low-grade tumors received RT; final pathology revealed grade 2 areas in 3 cases (37.5%), supporting magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–based risk stratification for histologic heterogeneity. Patients with atypical lipomatous tumors, baseline distant metastases, prior unplanned resection, recurrent disease, fungating lesions, or extraskeletal Ewing sarcoma/osteosarcoma were excluded.

Imaging and Staging Protocol

All patients had baseline staging with contrast-enhanced MRI (1.5T) for local tumor assessment and chest CT for systemic evaluation. Chest CT scans were reviewed under a standardized protocol by the sarcoma MDT, requiring consensus radiologic classification for event definition. Presurgical restaging, including MRI and chest CT, occurred within a week before surgery, with comparison of baseline and presurgical scans by a thoracic radiologist. The primary end point was new distant metastases during the preoperative interval, defined as lesions absent on baseline but present on presurgical imaging. Pulmonary nodules <3 mm were not metastatic unless they showed progression; nodules ≥5 mm in a compatible context were considered metastatic. Indeterminate lesions required MDT adjudication before classification as events19 (Fig. 1).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Interval metastasis incidence was calculated with 95% exact binomial confidence intervals (CIs). Univariable associations were assessed using χ2, Fisher’s exact tests, and Mann-Whitney U test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis determined the optimal tumor size threshold for predicting interval metastasis, using Youden’s index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1). ROC identified 11.5 cm as the Youden-optimal cutoff (J = 0.604; area under the curve = 0.855, 95% CI 0.795-0.915). A 12 cm threshold was chosen for clinical use due to its proximity to the optimum (Δ = 0.5 cm), high specificity (87.1%), and interpretability as a whole-centimeter threshold (sensitivity 65.5%, positive predictive value 29.7%, negative predictive value 96.8%) (Figure S1). Variables selected a priori (tumor size ≥12 cm, depth, FNCLCC grade, histologic subtype, age) and those with a univariable p < 0.10 were included in a multivariable logistic regression model with backward stepwise elimination (retention threshold p < 0.05). Model discrimination was assessed using the C-statistic and calibration with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. A point-based risk score stratified patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. Analyses used SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.0; p < 0.05 was statistically significant. Additional methodological details are in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

During the study, 378 patients met eligibility criteria. The median age was 53.2 years (SD 15.7; range 18-82), with 52.4% male. The lower extremity was involved in 78.8% (n = 298), mainly the thigh (53.2%) and leg (25.7%). The mean maximum tumor diameter was 9.2 ± 4.1 cm. Frequent histologic subtypes were undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS; 25.9%), synovial sarcoma (20.1%), myxoid liposarcoma (14.3%), and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST) (11.1%). FNCLCC grade was 3 in 57.4%, 2 in 33.6%, and 1 in 9.0%. The median RT-to-surgery interval was 55 days (range, 42-78) (Table I).

Incidence and Pattern of Interval Metastasis

New distant metastases were identified on presurgical chest CT in 29 of 378 patients, corresponding to an interval metastasis incidence of 7.8% (95% CI 5.3%-10.9%). Among the affected patients, the mean number of metastatic nodules was 3.2 (range, 1-12), with a mean largest lesion diameter of 8.4 mm (range, 3-18 mm). The 3-mm measurement occurred within a multinodule metastatic pattern and was not used as an isolated criterion for metastatic classification. Pulmonary disease was unilateral in 18 patients (62.1%) and bilateral in 11 (37.9%), with a predominance of lower lobe nodules (Table II).

Predictors of Interval Metastasis

Tumor size >12 cm was the strongest univariable predictor, with a metastasis rate of 16.9% (15/89) vs. 4.8% (14/289) for tumors ≤12 cm (odds ratio [OR] 3.52, 95% CI 1.78-6.94; p < 0.001). Histologic subtype significantly influenced the risk: synovial sarcoma and MPNST had interval metastasis rates of 14.5% and 14.3%, respectively, compared with 4.1% for UPS and 3.7% for myxoid liposarcoma (p = 0.008). FNCLCC grade 3 conferred approximately a 3-fold higher risk versus grade 1 to 2 (9.6% vs. 4.9%; OR 3.76, 95% CI 1.65-8.56; p = 0.001). No interval metastases occurred among patients classified as FNCLCC grade 1 on diagnostic biopsy. Younger age (≤50 years) was associated with an increased risk (11.0% vs. 5.1%; OR 2.34, 95% CI 1.12-4.89; p = 0.024) (Table III).

Multivariable Model and Risk Score

Four independent predictors were identified: tumor size >12 cm (adjusted OR 3.45, 95% CI 1.62-7.35; p = 0.001), synovial sarcoma histology (adjusted OR 3.12, 95% CI 1.38-7.05; p = 0.006), FNCLCC grade 3 (adjusted OR 2.89, 95% CI 1.31-6.38; p = 0.009), and age ≤50 years (adjusted OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.10-4.72; p = 0.027). MPNST lacked independent significance due to subgroup underpowering (n = 42, 6 events; events per variable [EPV] = 6.0) and collinearity with FNCLCC grade 3 (OR = 6.41, p < 0.0001), with 88% being grade 320. MPNST's metastatic risk is largely histologic grade-mediated but remains clinically significant, requiring intensified surveillance21. The final model showed robust discrimination (C-statistic 0.79, 95% CI 0.72-0.86) and acceptable calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow: χ2 = 8.43, p = 0.392), with an EPV ratio of 7.25. A point-based risk score was derived: tumor size >12 cm and synovial sarcoma histology were each 2 points; FNCLCC grade 3 and age ≤50 years were 1 point each (total range 0-6). Risk strata: low-risk (0-1 points; n = 168): 2.4% (4/168); intermediate-risk (2-3 points; n = 167): 8.4% (14/167); and high-risk (4-6 points; n = 43): 25.6% (11/43). The trend was significant (p < 0.001) (Table IV).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that in a contemporary preoperative RT program for extremity STS, clinically overt interval metastasis is uncommon but oncologically significant, affecting 7.8% of initially M0 patients over a median prediagnosis-to-surgery interval of 110 days. A 4-factor score stratified patients into groups with interval metastasis rates of approximately 2%, 8%, and 26%, a difference large enough to meaningfully influence clinical decision-making.

The 7.8% absolute incidence aligns with the limited literature. In the cohort of Acem et al., routine chest restaging after neoadjuvant RT altered management in 19% of patients and cancelled planned resection in 18% of patients14. Our lower rate reflects our stricter event definition (new distant lesions only) and confirms that preoperative RT does not precipitate early metastatic failure in appropriately selected patients.

Evidence argues against the idea that the preoperative interval or RT induces metastasis. First, interval metastases concentrated in patients with adverse baseline tumor biology22,23. Second, large series have not shown worse distant control or survival with preoperative versus postoperative RT24-26. Third, interval duration within the standard 6- to 8-week window did not correlate with metastasis risk, implicating tumor biology rather than treatment timing14,15. Interval metastases likely represent radiologic “unmasking” of micrometastatic disease present at diagnosis. The 12-cm tumor size threshold aligns with prognostic thresholds in STS literature. Coindre et al. identified tumor size >10 cm as a predictor of metastasis27, Liu et al. confirmed this in a multicenter study (OR 2.54; p < 0.01)28, and Callegaro et al.’s nomogram includes tumor size as a predictor of metastasis-free survival29. The prognostic significance of synovial sarcoma, driven by the SS18-SSX fusion oncogene, is consistent with series reporting 40% to 50% metastatic rates in localized disease30,31. UPS and myxoid liposarcoma showed low short-term interval metastasis rates (3%-4%), consistent with their indolent early systemic behavior.

The risk score has direct clinical value, but it should be interpreted as a tool for stratifying surveillance intensity rather than omitting restaging. Even patients in the lowest-risk stratum had a 2.4% interval metastasis rate (4/168), which remains clinically relevant given the consequences of proceeding to major limb-salvage surgery with undetected pulmonary disease. Therefore, baseline chest CT followed by mandatory presurgical chest CT should remain the minimum staging approach for all patients treated with preoperative RT. In selected very high-risk patients, particularly those with scores ≥4, an additional chest CT after completion of RT may be considered to detect rapidly progressive metastatic disease earlier, although whether earlier detection improves patient-centered outcomes remains unproven. These findings support continued use of preoperative RT for most high-risk extremity STS patients, given its advantages in R0 resection rates and long-term functional outcomes32.

The imaging strategy during the preoperative interval has practical implications. We believe baseline chest CT at diagnosis followed by a single presurgical chest CT suffices as the minimum staging approach in patients treated with preoperative RT, as this protocol identified new metastatic disease in 7.8% of the overall cohort and about 1-quarter of patients in the highest-risk stratum. In selected very high-risk patients, like those with large, grade 3 synovial sarcoma or MPNST, an additional chest CT at RT completion may be considered to detect rapidly progressive metastatic disease earlier, though whether this improves patient-centered outcomes, rather than just advancing diagnosis time, remains unproven33,34.

These findings may refine discussions on systemic treatment. In this cohort, neoadjuvant chemotherapy wasn't routinely given to nonmetastatic patients; however, the risk model identified a small subgroup with significant short-term metastatic risk justifying intensified treatment strategies. For such patients, especially younger ones with large, high-grade synovial sarcoma or MPNST, the score may prioritize multidisciplinary discussion, closer surveillance, and referral for clinical trials evaluating intensified systemic or combined-modality approaches, rather than supporting routine treatment escalation for all localized disease patients35-38.

In a standardized preoperative RT pathway, interval metastasis was rare and linked to adverse tumor biology, supporting its oncologic feasibility for most patients. Emerging biomarkers such as ctDNA could refine interval risk assessment; initial data show high concordance between ctDNA positivity and radiologic recurrence in high-risk STS cohorts39,40. However, ctDNA remains investigational and should be part of prospective studies, not routine practice41.

This study had several limitations. As a single-center retrospective cohort study, although protocols were homogeneous, practice patterns and case mix may differ elsewhere. As a tertiary referral sarcoma center, our patient population may not fully represent the broader population with extremity STS. Our cohort had higher synovial sarcoma (20.1%) and MPNST (11.1%) rates than most Western registries, where leiomyosarcoma and liposarcoma predominate42-44. This partly reflects regional epidemiological differences and selective referral of these subtypes to specialized centers for molecular diagnostics and complex surgery45,46. Our findings should be interpreted in the context of tertiary centers managing similar high-risk populations. The risk model requires external validation before being considered definitive. Our RT-to-surgery interval (median 55 days) was longer than at some high-volume centers (approximately 21-35 days)11,47, possibly limiting the generalizability of our findings. The modest number of events (n = 29) constrained modeling complexity, and MPNST was underpowered due to strong collinearity with FNCLCC grade 3. Downstream survival outcomes were not analyzed; thus, the impact of earlier metastasis detection on overall survival, quality of life, and healthcare costs remains undetermined.

Another limitation is the possibility of occult baseline pulmonary metastases below the reliable detection threshold of routine chest CT. If present, such lesions may have been classified as interval metastases when detected on presurgical imaging. This would likely overestimate, rather than underestimate, the true incidence of new metastatic events during the preoperative pathway and further supports our interpretation that early metastatic detection reflects aggressive tumor biology rather than the RT-to-surgery interval itself.

Conclusion

In a modern preoperative RT program for extremity STS, interval metastasis is uncommon, affecting about 1 in 12 patients overall and 1 in 4 in a high-risk subgroup, reflecting aggressive disease rather than harm from RT or treatment timing. Systematic restaging chest CT before surgery is justified as the minimum standard of care. A 4-factor risk score (tumor size > 12 cm, synovial sarcoma histology, FNCLCC grade 3, and age ≤50 years) helps clinicians identify high-risk patients, adjust surveillance, and provide personalized counseling while maintaining the benefits of preoperative RT in local control, margin status, and functional outcomes.
