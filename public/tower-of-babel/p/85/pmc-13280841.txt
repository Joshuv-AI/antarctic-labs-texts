Stimuli-Responsive Polypyrrole Hydrogel Integrated into a Mg Battery for Controlled Drug Release
Authors: Yueming Mu, Junze Fan, Yan Zhou, Qin Liang, Yaping Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although stimulus-responsive drug delivery systems allow for precise drug release, electro-assisted delivery methods are seldom incorporated into transdermal platforms, highlighting a major research gap. In this work, we developed a self-powered, controllable drug delivery platform using a conductive gelatin methacryloyl-polyacrylamide-polypyrrole hydrogel (GPP) as the cathode in a magnesium battery. The GPP hydrogel was synthesized via UV cross-linking and in situ polymerization, exhibiting a compact porous structure, enhanced mechanical strength (fracture strain: 234%), stable electrochemical performance, and excellent cytocompatibility. It also functioned as a sensitive strain sensor (gauge factor: 1.14). Drug release was precisely tuned by electrical stimulation, increasing from 0.2 mg/g at 0 V to nearly 1.6 mg/g at −0.8 V within 180 min. The integrated Mg-GPP battery powered drug delivery effectively, achieving a 5-fold enhancement over passive diffusion. This system provides a promising strategy for advanced personalized transdermal therapy with combined electrically controlled release and motion sensing capability.

Introduction

Stimulus-responsive drug delivery systems exhibit considerable potential in precision medicine and personalized therapy. By integrating advanced materials science and biotechnology, these systems can enhance the accuracy and efficacy of drug treatment. − Among them, electro-assisted targeted drug delivery systems facilitate precise modulation of drug release profiles, including pulsed, dose-adjustable, and time-controlled administration, by utilizing redox reactions within electroactive carrier materials. − However, research integrating electrically controlled drug release technology into transdermal delivery systems (TDDS) remains relatively limited.

TDDS have emerged as a key alternative to traditional oral and injectable routes due to their ability to bypass hepatic first-pass metabolism, reduce systemic side effects, and enable sustained, patient-controlled administration. − By utilizing low-intensity electric currents to facilitate the transport of drug ions or charged particles, it not only enhances drug penetration efficiency but also reduces the side effects associated with conventional drug delivery methods. Studies indicate that iontophoresis can effectively enhance drug penetration into the skin, particularly in the management of skin disorders such as psoriasis. However, most existing TDDS employ rigid electrode materials with poor biocompatibility. − They often rely on external bulky power sources, resulting in low device integration and poor wearability. Furthermore, these electrode materials typically lack real-time monitoring capabilities for physiological or mechanical conditions at the delivery site, limiting their advancement toward intelligent, closed-loop control systems.

In recent years, electronic materials possessing flexibility, stretchability, and sensing capabilities have attracted considerable attention. − Among them, conductive hydrogels have emerged as a promising class of electroactive drug-reservoir materials. They exhibit excellent biocompatibility and mechanical flexibility, allowing conformal contact with skin and adaptation to dynamic deformation, thereby improving wearing comfort. − Their three-dimensional porous networks facilitate efficient loading of drug molecules, − and their intrinsic redox properties enable precise drug release control via electrochemical reactions. − In addition, researchers have initiated investigations into the integration of polypyrrole (PPy) with hydrogels, capitalizing on its attributes, including high conductivity, extensibility, self-healing capacity, adhesiveness, and sensing capabilities, to augment its potential applications in drug delivery systems. Utilizing electrochemical polymerization, PPy is integrated with polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) to develop an electro-responsive drug delivery system. This system facilitates the controlled release of pharmaceuticals upon stimulation while preserving the activity of the drugs. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that hydrogels with excellent conductivity and mechanical properties can be prepared by conjugating PPy with poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), which exhibit outstanding performance in human motion sensors. Although research on PPy for transdermal drug delivery remains limited, its integration with hydrogels demonstrates significant potential.

In this study, a flexible composite hydrogel with excellent compatibility and mechanical adaptability was fabricated using gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), polyacrylamide (PAM), and PPy. The physicochemical and electrochemical properties of the GelMA-PAM-PPy (GPP) hydrogel were systematically investigated. Uniformly dispersed PPy particles established effective conductive pathways within the elastic network, promoting electron transport and providing mechanical reinforcement to the hybrid system. Preliminary investigations into drug release kinetics under different battery operating parameters revealed that the applied electric field significantly weakened the electrostatic interaction between the drug molecule dexamethasone sodium phosphate (DEX) and the PPy matrix, thereby triggering controllable drug release. An innovative aspect of this work lies in the dual use of PPy as both the cathode active material for a Mg-based battery and the drug reservoir, integrating energy storage, controlled drug release, and signal-sensing capabilities within a single platform. Mg was selected as the anode material for its high theoretical energy density, which provides a strong driving force for therapy, and for the inherent biocompatibility of its degradation byproducts. The incorporation of an integrated Mg battery with iontophoresis obviates the necessity for external power sources, thereby enhancing patient compliance in epidermal disease treatments. This approach provides an important material foundation for developing flexible, integrated, and intelligent transdermal drug delivery systems.

Results

Morphological and Chemical Characterization

The hydrogel matrix was synthesized by forming a semi-interpenetrating network via UV irradiation of oligomer solution. This network then served as a template for the in situ polymerization of PPy into long chains. The resulting cationic PPy facilitated the efficient loading of the anionic drug DEX through strong electrostatic interactions (Figure a).

Synthesis and physicochemical properties hydrogels. (a) Schematic illustration of the synthesis, and molecular structures of GPP hydrogel. (b) SEM micrographs. (c) FTIR spectra. (d) Swelling ratio. (e) Water retention capacity. (All data for GM, GP, and GPP hydrogels).

To evaluate the impact of each component, the fundamental characteristics of hydrogels with varying compositions (GM, GP, and GPP) were systematically compared. SEM imaging revealed distinct microstructural differences among the hydrogel groups (Figure b). The polymerization of pyrrole within a preformed GelMA-PAM dual network resulted in a significant reduction in observed pore size, indicating that PPy chains effectively occupied interstitial spaces. The pure GM displayed a loose network with large pore diameters. In contrast, the GP exhibited a denser network structure following the introduction of the polyacrylamide network. To directly colocated distribution of phosphorus (P, from DEX) with carbon (C) and oxygen (O, hydrogel matrix) across the 2 mm thickness confirms homogeneous drug dispersion within the 3D network (Supporting Information Figure S1).

FTIR spectra confirmed the chemical composition of the hydrogels (Figure c). The GM spectrum showed a broad absorption band centered at 3200–3400 cm–1, corresponding to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations. This band persisted in the GP hydrogel. In the GPP hydrogel, additional peaks appeared in the fingerprint region, specifically corresponding to CC/CN vibrations derived from the pyrrole ring.

Dynamic swelling tests in PBS indicated that all hydrogels reached equilibrium within approximately 24 h. The equilibrium swelling ratios were recorded as 15.2 g g–1 for GM, 9.8 g g–1 for GP, and 7.8 g g–1 for GPP (Figure d). The gradual decrease in swelling rate directly reflects the progressive increase in polymer network cross-linking density. The low swelling rate is fundamental to achieving sustained-release and stimulus-responsive drug delivery. Regarding water retention, the GPP hydrogel maintained a retention rate of 87%, compared to 76% for GM and 83% for GP (Figure e). The superior water-retaining capacity of GPP hydrogels prevents drug precipitation or loss due to moisture evaporation during storage or initial application, thereby enhancing the stability and efficacy of drug delivery.

Mechanical Properties and Sensing Performance of Hydrogels

The hydrogel matrix can be embedded with PPy to improve electrical performance while maintaining favorable mechanical properties.

Tensile testing provided stress–strain profiles for each group (Figure a). The GM hydrogel fractured at a stress of approximately 9 kPa with a strain of 130%. The GP hydrogel exhibited a fracture stress of 26.1 kPa and an elastic modulus of 14.8 kPa, with toughness calculated at 22.8 kJ m–3. The GPP hydrogel recorded a fracture stress of 18 kPa, an elastic modulus of 10.4 kPa, and a toughness of 21.2 kJ m–3. Notably, the fracture strain for GPP extended to 234%, exceeding the 170% strain observed for both GM and GP. The high fracture strain indicates that GPP can absorb a significant amount of energy before breaking. This property ensures that it does not fail easily when subjected to unexpected impacts or sudden pulls, thereby maintaining structural integrity.

Cyclic loading–unloading tests on GPP hydrogels (50% to 150% strain) showed hysteresis loops that increased in area with higher strain levels (Figure b). In fatigue resistance testing (10 cycles at 110% strain), a stress softening was observed in the first cycle, while subsequent cycles (second through 10th) displayed overlapping stress–strain curves (Figure c). Under repeated stretching, the conductive polymer network (GPP) does not break easily, ensuring the stability and durability of both discharge and electrical stimulation functions.

The GPP hydrogel sensor demonstrated a linear relative resistance change (ΔR/R 0) versus strain (R 2 > 0.99) (Figure d). The gauge factor (GF) was calculated as 1.14 for the 0–10% strain range and 1.03 for the 10–100% strain range (Figure e,f). Operational stability within the network may rearrange under high strain, thereby slowing the increase in resistance per unit strain. , This dynamic reorganization enhances its stability under cyclic loading conditions. , Step-like resistance changes were recorded at strains of 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10%, as well as at larger deformations up to 100%. Response and recovery times were measured at 853 and 987 ms, respectively (Figure g). Cyclic testing at 30% strain showed stable resistance waveforms over repeated cycles (Figure h). Its exceptional sensing capabilities enable it to function like a second skin, accurately capturing both faint physiological signals and vigorous physical movements while simultaneously monitoring the skin’s physical state and the patient’s itching behavior in real time.

Lap-shear tests on porcine skin demonstrated robust adhesion (2.66 kPa) for the GPP hydrogel, exceeding that of GP counterpart, confirms its suitability for reliable wearable applications (Supporting Information Figure S2).

Electrochemical Characterization

The integration of the conductive PPy polymer was critical. It transformed the hydrogel into an electroactive platform (GPP) with highly efficient charge transport, substantially lower resistance, and remarkable charge injection performance.

Within the GPP composite system, PPy interdigits within the three-dimensional GP hydrogel network, forming a continuous conductive network that permeates the entire matrix. In contrast, GP hydrogels without PPy rely solely on the migration of ions within the hydrogel matrix (e.g., ions in PBS buffer) to achieve limited ionic conductivity (8.83 mS cm–1), which is significantly less efficient than the electronic conductivity provided by PPy (16.34 mS cm–1) (Figure a). Nyquist Plot shows that the diameter of the semicircular arc in the high-frequency range for GPP (42 Ω) is smaller than that for GP (46.6 Ω) (Figure b). CV of the GPP hydrogel showed quasi-rectangular shapes without distinct redox peaks across scan rates from 10 to 100 mV s–1 (Figure c). Charge injection curves result under biphasic voltage pulses showed that the GPP hydrogel produced higher current response amplitudes than the GP control (Figure d). Therefore, the formation of the PPy conductive network significantly enhances the material’s overall charge transport capability, enabling a magnitude-level improvement in the conductivity of GPP compared to GP.

Cytotoxicity and Biocompatibility

GPP materials exhibit excellent biocompatibility, which is a prerequisite for the long-term use of hydrogels in skin-electronic interfaces. Fluorescence microscopy indicated that cells in the treated group maintained a spindle-like morphology and formed a confluent monolayer by day 3 (Figure a). OD analysis at 490 nm showed increasing metabolic activity over 3 days for both control and extract-treated groups (Figure b). NIH-3T3 fibroblast viability remained above 90% across all GPP extract concentrations (up to 0.10 g mL–1) (Figure c).

Coculture with the operational Mg-GPP battery system (and its potential byproducts) did not compromise cell viability or morphology compared to controls (Supporting Information Figure S3). The culture medium exhibited only a minimal pH shift, which had no significant impact on the cells, collectively confirming the absence of detectable cytotoxicity (Supporting Information Figure S4).

In Vitro Drug Electro-Responsive Release

We designed a self-powered ion electroosmotic patch driven by a Mg battery, the GPP hydrogel serves as both an integrated cathode and drug reservoir within this system. When the Mg anode undergoes oxidation, the polypyrrole (PPy+) within the GPP hydrogel gains electrons and is reduced to neutral PPy0. This process triggers the release of encapsulated DEX–. The overall reaction is ultimately represented as follows:

At anode Mg+2OH−→Mg(OH)2+2e−

At cathode (PPy+)(DEX−)+e−→PPy0+DEX−

Overall reaction Mg+2PPy+(DEX−)+2H2O→Mg(OH)2+2PPy0+2DEX−+2H+

The mechanism is likely attributed to the electrostatic repulsion that occurs when the hydrogel network and the entrapped drug molecules become negatively charged under the applied potential, which weakens their interaction and facilitates the diffusion of GPP out of the hydrogel (Figure a).

The Mg-GPP primary battery system demonstrates stable galvanostatic discharge performance, it exhibited discharge voltage plateaus between 0.5 and 0.8 V at current densities of 5, 10, 20, and 50 μA cm–2 (Figure b). This battery exhibits a relatively stable discharge plateau across current densities ranging from 5 to 50 μA/cm2 (Figure c). Discharge curves of GPP at different load resistances (10 Ω to 104 Ω) showed that discharge duration ranged from approximately 8 h (10 Ω) to over 100 h (104 Ω) (Figure d). When the battery is loaded with a 103 Ω resistor, its output voltage is 0.5 V and can sustain operation for 28 h. Operational stability was confirmed over 7 days of intermittent discharge, with the system maintaining a consistent working voltage to support repeatable drug release (Supporting Information Figure S5). It achieves energy autonomy and long-term operation without worrying about charging issues, while also eliminating the risk of electric leakage or short circuits that may occur with external power sources. This significantly improves patient compliance and quality of life.

We measured the drug concentration of DEX released from GPP under different voltages or resistances, revealing that the release rate and cumulative amount can be precisely controlled by adjusting the resistance. The released DEX was quantified by UV–vis spectrophotometry at 242 nm using a standard curve (2.5–50 μg mL–1) that showed excellent linearity (y = 0.0265x + 0.0664, R 2 = 0.9991). A standard curve for DEX was established by HPLC, yielding the linear regression equation y = 3 × 10–5 x + 0.0564 with a correlation coefficient (R 2) of 0.9998. The drug loading capacity was determined to be 2.21 ± 0.11 mg g–1 using solvent extraction.

The electro-responsive release behavior of DEX from the hydrogel was investigated under different electrical potentials (−0.8 V, −0.4 V, and 0 V vs Ag/AgCl). The cumulative release profiles are presented in Figure e. At 0 V (representing passive diffusion without electrical stimulation), the release of DEX was minimal, reaching only about 0.2 mg g–1 after 180 min. This indicates that DEX is stably retained within the hydrogel matrix in the absence of an external trigger. When a negative potential was applied, a significant increase in the release rate and cumulative amount was observed. Under a −0.4 V potential, the cumulative release reached approximately 1.0 mg g–1 in 180 min, and release efficiency reaches 45%. When the potential drops to −0.8 V, drug release is significantly enhanced, with cumulative release reaching nearly 1.6 mg/g within 180 min and a release efficiency as high as 70%, release by 6.94-fold over passive diffusion (Supporting Information Figure S6a). This voltage-dependent release phenomenon suggests that the application of a negative potential effectively triggers the discharge of GPP from the hydrogel network. XPS analysis provides direct evidence that the DEX is electrostatically incorporated as a dopant anion within the PPy matrix, confirming the proposed redox-active drug-loading mechanism (Supporting Information Figure S7a,b). Cyclic voltammetry confirmed drug release is directly coupled to the redox-mediated dedoping of PPy upon its electrochemical reduction (Supporting Information Figure S7c).

Coupling with the Mg anode increased release in a resistance-dependent manner. The system with a 102 Ω load exhibited the fastest drug release rate (65%) (Figure f), achieving a cumulative release of 1.43 mg g–1 over 180 min, and generated a 4.83-fold enhancement (Supporting Information Figure S6b). The cumulative drug release reached 0.99 mg g–1 (release efficiency 45%) and 0.56 mg g–1 (release efficiency 25%) for systems with circuit resistances of 103 and 104 Ω, respectively after 180 min. Redox-mediated doping/dedoping of the conductive polymer enables drug release superior to passive diffusion alone.

In the assessment of cutaneous drug deposition, the application of iontophoresis utilizing a 102 Ω resistor markedly augmented drug delivery into deeper tissue strata, yielding a 3-fold increase in DEX deposition relative to controls utilizing Mg-free hydrogel. The cumulative release from the 102 Ω hydrogel attained 0.13 mg g–1 over a duration of 180 min (Figure g). The notable enhancement in the drug release profile, coupled with the augmented volume of skin deposition, collectively suggests that DEX released from GPP is capable of achieving rapid and efficient drug release when subjected to an electric field.

Discussion

Traditional conductive fillers often exhibit inadequate dispersion within hydrogels, leading to the formation of aggregates that compromise the material’s mechanical properties. Additionally, rigid fillers can impede polymer chain mobility, thereby reducing the material’s overall flexibility. In contrast, the incorporation of PPy into the interwoven network formed by gel-cross-linked GelMA and PAM has demonstrated superior mechanical properties. This integration facilitates continuous and stable signal acquisition during bodily movements, mitigating the discomfort and signal distortion typically associated with traditional rigid electrodes. Although initial cyclic stretching induces some irreversible structural rearrangements in the GPP network, it rapidly attains a highly stable and recoverable equilibrium state, exhibiting remarkable resilience and durability. This characteristic ensures reliable long-term monitoring with minimal baseline drift.

Transitioning from passive hydrogel scaffolds to structures incorporating active electronic interfaces, GPP hydrogel possess distinct electrochemical properties that ensure safe and efficient signal transmission. The porous architecture of GPP hydrogels facilitates rapid ion transport and minimizes charge transfer resistance, while their remarkable charge injection capability is efficiently converted into quantifiable electrical signals. When utilized as a cathode in conjunction with a Mg anode, this system generates sufficient voltage to enable rapid, self-powered modulation of drug release.

Most existing active systems depend on large external power sources, which makes them unsuitable for long-term, portable applications. By integrating the power source, specifically the Mg anode, directly into the therapeutic circuit, we establish a self-powered drug delivery platform that offers a self-sustaining solution. During operation of the Mg-GPP battery, the Mg anode undergoes surface passivation (Mg­(OH)2), which is a common problem faced by aqueous Mg-based batteries (Supporting Information Figure S8). Current research on biodegradable batteries frequently identifies a significant challenge: the rapid passivation of Mg anodes, which typically results in a sharp voltage decline shortly after activation. Nevertheless, the Mg-GPP system overcomes this common limitation. Both galvanostatic and load-resistance discharge curves show stable voltage plateaus. This stability, attributed to reduced ohmic losses from the conductive polypyrrole network in the GPP anode, is further corroborated by its rate performance tests. Although the GPP cathode demonstrates commendable performance, future optimization efforts might necessitate concentrating on the modification of the Mg anode surface to enhance the extraction of useable energy from the same volume of material, particularly for long-term applications.

Through electrochemical polymerization, PPy can effectively bind to drug molecules and facilitate their release upon electrical stimulation. , The application of an electric field significantly weakens the electrostatic interactions between DEX and PPy, resulting in the detachment of DEX from the PPy. Analysis of the drug release profiles reveals that the cumulative release amount and rate are influenced not only by the applied potential but also by external resistance. This suggests that current density is the predominant factor influencing release kinetics, thereby providing a more precise control variable (resistance) for potential clinical applications. These findings not only lay the groundwork for the application of PPy in drug delivery systems but also expand its potential utility in other biomedical fields.

The drug-loaded conductive hydrogels developed in this study were evaluated against previously reported PPY containing conductive hydrogels utilized in drug delivery systems (Table ). Compared to other PPy-containing conductive hydrogels, the GPP hydrogel developed in this work demonstrates superior integrated performance, including excellent electrochemical and mechanical properties, as well as a high drug release capacity of 69.95%. Differing from conventional electrically responsive drug delivery systems, our integrated platform combines self-powering, real-time motion sensing, on-demand drug release, and demonstrates good biocompatibility within a single, wearable patch. Compared to other self-powered systems, the Mg-GPP battery demonstrates a higher power density (Supporting Information Table S1). To enhance drug delivery efficacy, future research should not only focus on optimizing release kinetics but also on systematically assessing the long-term biosafety of the materials and their degradation products. Additionally, it is crucial to maintain the driving voltage within a safe threshold, with particular emphasis on transdermal delivery efficiency, penetration depth, and the distribution and metabolism of the drug within skin tissues under an electric field.

Conclusions

In summary, this study introduces a platform utilizing PPy conductive hydrogels that integrates the cathode of a Mg battery with an electroactive drug reservoir, facilitating self-powered and programmable transdermal drug delivery. The hydrogel demonstrates superior biocompatibility, mechanical flexibility, and stable electrochemical performance, thereby enabling both efficient energy storage and precise electro-controlled release of DEX. Its intrinsic piezoresistive properties allow for real-time mechanical sensing, which supports responsive operation within dynamic physiological environments. By amalgamating energy storage, controlled drug release, and sensing functionalities into a single flexible system, this research offers a promising approach for the development of next-generation self-powered drug delivery systems, specifically designed for personalized long-term medical management.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Gelatin (derived from porcine skin), methacrylic anhydride (MA), acrylamide (AM), pyrrole (Py), N, N′-methylenebisacrylamide (BIS), dexamethasone sodium phosphate (DEX), lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) and ammonium persulfate (APS) were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China) and were not purified. Fetal bovine serum, Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), 1% penicillin streptomycin solution, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay, calcein, and propidium iodide were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. All other chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification. Deionized (DI) water was used throughout the study.

Synthesis of GelMA

GelMA was synthesized according to a modified protocol based on previous literature. Briefly, 10 g of gelatin was dissolved in 100 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 50 °C under continuous magnetic stirring. To introduce methacryloyl groups, 10 mL of MA was added dropwise to the gelatin solution, maintaining a 1:10 (v/w) ratio. The reaction proceeded under vigorous stirring at 50 °C for 3 h and was subsequently terminated by adding 500 mL of warm PBS. To remove unreacted MA and salts, the mixture was dialyzed against distilled water at 40 °C for 7 days using dialysis tubing (MWCO: 12–14 kDa), with water changes performed twice daily. The purified solution was lyophilized for 1 week to obtain a white porous foam, which was stored in the dark at −20 °C until further use.

Fabrication of DEX-Loaded GPP Hydrogels

The preparation of GPP drug-loaded hydrogels involves three main steps: UV light cross-linking of a mixed solution of GelMA, AM, Py, and BIS to form a semi-interpenetrating network; drug loading by immersion in an ethanol solution of DEX (100 μg mL–1). Finally, polymerizing the gel in an APS solution at 4 °C for 24 h, followed by purification through rinsing with deionized water. The drug loading capacity was quantified using a solvent extraction method. Measurements were performed in triplicate on parallel-prepared hydrogels. The specific formulas are shown in Table .

Preparation of Mg Battery

A sandwich-structured Mg battery was constructed using a polished Mg foil (2 mm thickness) as the anode and the GPP hydrogel as the cathode. A PVA-based solid electrolyte (10 wt % PVA in PBS) was interposed between the electrodes to facilitate ion transport.

Characterization of Hydrogel

The physicochemical properties and mechanical performance of this hydrogel have been comprehensively characterized. Its morphology was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Nova Nano SEM 450), and its chemical structure was analyzed with Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10) in the 4000–500 cm–1 range. Mechanical integrity was evaluated on dumbbell-shaped samples (40 mm × 10 mm × 2 mm) using a universal testing machine (Huabo Technology Industry Co., Ltd.).

The electromechanical performance of hydrogel as a strain sensor was also assessed. The gauge factor (GF) was calculated by recording the relative resistance change (ΔR/R 0) under increasing tensile strain. Signal fidelity was tested under both small (2%–10%) and large (20%–100%) cyclic strains. The response and recovery times were defined as the time to reach 90% of the signal change during a single loading–unloading cycle. Finally, durability was confirmed by monitoring the signal’s stability over 500 consecutive cycles at 30% strain.

Electrochemical Properties of Hydrogels and Mg Batteries

The hydrogel’s electrochemical properties were analyzed at room temperature in PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) using a three-electrode system (CHI 660E, CH Instruments). The system comprised the hydrogel as the working electrode, a Pt wire counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed from −0.8 V to +0.8 V at scan rates of 10, 20, 30, 50, and 100 mV/s to determine the charge injection capacity (CIC). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz at a 10 mV amplitude to analyze impedance characteristics via Nyquist plots. The ionic conductivity of the hydrogel was measured using AC impedance spectroscopy (Gamry EIS 3000 system) over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 1 Hz.

To evaluate the hydrogel as a cathode for a self-powered system, its performance was tested using a Neware workstation. Galvanostatic discharge profiles were recorded at current densities from 5 to 50 μA cm–2 to determine capacity. To simulate practical use, discharge tests were also run under constant loads of 10, 100, 1k, and 10k Ω. Finally, rate capability was assessed by cycling through various discharge currents to confirm its suitability for iontophoresis.

Biocompatibility Assessment

The cytocompatibility of the GPP extract was assessed utilizing NIH-3T3 fibroblasts obtained from Cell Bioscience Company. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with hydrogel extracts (0.1 g mL–1) for 1, 2, and 3 days. Cell viability was quantitatively determined by an MTT assay measuring optical density (OD) at 490 nm, and qualitatively confirmed through Live/Dead staining.

To directly evaluate the safety of the fully integrated and operational system, a coculture test was performed. The battery was operated in a simulated therapeutic regimen, discharging for 1 h per day. Cell viability and morphology were assessed using MTT assay and Live/Dead staining after 1, 2, and 3 days of this intermittent operation.

Constant potentials of 0 V (passive diffusion), −0.4 V, and −0.8 V were applied to determine the effect of electrical stimulation on drug elution. To validate the battery concept, release was triggered by the integrated Mg-GPP system connected to varying external resistors (102, 103, 104 Ω).

To quantify the skin permeation of DEX, the iontophoretic patch was assembled on fresh porcine skin (25 × 25 × 5 mm) and operated continuously for 3 h. The skin was then minced, immersed in fresh PBS, and incubated for 24 h to extract DEX from the epidermal/dermal layers. DEX content was analyzed using an HPLC system (Shimadzu UFLC 20A) equipped with a C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 5 mM ammonium acetate and acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) delivered at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The injection volume was 30 μL, and detection was performed at 242 nm. Under these conditions, the retention time of DEX was approximately 2.4 min.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Each experiment was performed with a minimum of three independent replicates (n ≥ 3).
