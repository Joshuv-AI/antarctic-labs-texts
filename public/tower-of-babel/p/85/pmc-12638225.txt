Antibiotic Persister Cells in Acinetobacter baumannii : Overview of Molecular Mechanisms and Removal Strategies
Authors: Delphine Vergoz, Emmanuelle Dé, Corinne Loutelier‐Bourhis, Stéphane Alexandre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Acinetobacter baumannii is a bacterium classified as ‘Critical’ by the World Health Organization due to the rapid development of antibiotic resistance leading to many therapeutic failures. However, the existence of persister bacterial subpopulations may also partly explain these therapeutic failures. Persister cells are a bacterial subpopulation that may survive to higher concentrations of antibiotics compared to isogenic individuals. This temporary and reversible phenotype may eventually lead to the development of new antibiotic resistance or to the recurrence of infection making it important to study. The mechanisms involved in the selection of persister cells are multiple and complex involving toxin/antitoxin systems, second messengers, the SOS response, the phenyl acetic catabolic pathway or membranes modifications. Some of these mechanisms, like toxin/antitoxin systems presenting a great diversity, appear to be species‐ or strain‐specific. This review focuses on current advances in knowledge concerning the molecular mechanisms involved in the physiology of persister cells in A. baumannii , and the eradication strategies developed to combat this dormant subpopulation.Keywords: antibiotics, lipids, phage, phenylacetic acid catabolic pathway, second‐messengers, SOS‐response, toxin/antitoxin

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii is a pathogen responsible for infections that are extremely problematic in hospitals. It has no typical virulence factors such as toxins to explain its pathogenicity (Lucidi et al. 2024). Therefore, this pathogen is referred to as having a ‘persist and resist’ virulence strategy (Harding et al. 2018), meaning that it is capable of surviving in very hostile environments (Zeidler and Müller 2019). It is known in particular for its resistance to dessiccation, its ability to form a biofilm to protect itself from external stresses, and its capacity to easily develop antibiotic resistance, notably to the carbapenem class (Cain and Hamidian 2023; Farrow et al. 2018; Gedefie Id et al. 2023). The difficulty of eradicating this bacterium makes it particularly dangerous when found in a hospital environment (Fahy et al. 2023). Consequently, it has been classified in the ‘Priority 1’ pathogen group by the World a Health Organization in 2017—a status which was later confirmed in 2024—to prioritise the development of new antibacterial agents [W1].

Beside the existence of resistant bacteria, the persister phenomenon may also be responsible for the difficulty of eradicating this bacterium and the recurrence of infection. Persister cells in bacteria were first described by Hobby in 1942 and Joseph Bigger in 1944 (Hobby et al. 1942; Bigger 1944). Hobby observed that after treatment with penicillin, 99% of the hemolytic streptococcus culture had been eradicated leaving 1% of the bacteria unkilled. In 1944, Bigger called such small number of surviving bacteria ‘persisters’. Currently, persister cells are defined as a bacterial subpopulation able to survive higher concentrations of antibiotics compared to isogenic individuals (Balaban et al. 2019; Lewis 2010). Recently, it was shown that phages can also induce a subpopulation of persister cells, similar to those selected by antibiotics (Fernández‐García et al. 2024). The formation of persister occurs without genomic modification.

This state can be compared to ‘dormancy’ due to the downregulation of genes involved in growth, cell division, and metabolism, while stress‐related genes are upregulated. However, dormancy corresponds to the arrest of growth in a viable cell under favourable growth conditions (Amato et al. 2013). Dormancy appears to be directly linked to a decrease of multiplication and metabolism, whereas persistence appears to be a more complex phenomenon, as persister cells can be metabolically active (Orman and Brynildsen 2013).

This recalcitrant phenotype is reversible and thus temporary since persister cells are able to regrow after the removal of selection pressure, and therefore to re‐establish a population similar to the original, that is, susceptible to antibiotics (Lewis 2010). While persister cells are generally characterised by a growth arrest, it seems that this feature is neither necessary nor sufficient to enter into persistence (Orman and Brynildsen 2013). Two types of persister cells have been described (Figure 1) (Balaban et al. 2019). One category, which is very poorly/rare represented in a population, comprises of spontaneously occurring cells which, without responding to any particular stress, can enter into a very slow multiplication phase even under favourable conditions (Figure 1A) (Balaban et al. 2004). The other type, the triggered persister, formerly called type I (Balaban et al. 2004), is generated by an environmental stressor like a late stationary phase starvation (Gutierrez et al. 2017), a high cell density (Vega et al. 2012), or an antibiotic exposure (Fernández‐García et al. 2024) (Figure 1B). It should be noted that the viable but non‐culturable cells (VBNC) could sometimes be considered as persister exhibiting a longer latency (Bamford et al. 2017). The VBNC remain intact and metabolically active after exposure to an antibiotic, but unlike persister cells, they cannot resume growth after antibiotic removal (Dong et al. 2020). However, it has been shown that under certain conditions, some VBNC could regrow with a transcriptomic profile very similar to that of a persister (Bamford et al. 2017). Two main methodologies are available for studying persister cells: the first is the study at individual cell level using microfluidics, growth reporters and ScanLag as examples, while the second is the study of the subpopulation level via genetic screens, evolution of whole genome sequencing, −omics techniques or mathematical modelling, etc. (Hansen and Weinberger 2019; Arbel‐Goren et al. 2013; Mcadams and Arkin 1997; Swain 2004). These have been used to highlight some mechanisms involved in the formation, selection or physiology of persister cells, like the toxin‐antitoxin systems, some second messengers activating specific transcription factors, the activation of the SOS response, the oxidative stress response and the membrane modifications. It is still difficult to determine whether these mechanisms are directly or indirectly involved, whether they are a cause or a consequence of the cell's persister nature, but they play a role in the physiology of persister cells.

Persister cells selection by antibiotics. Spontaneous persister cells occur naturally, though rarely, even without the existence of external stressors (A). Triggered persister cells, on the other hand, are induced by an environmental change such as an antibiotic shock (B). (Adapted from (Fernández‐García et al. 2024; Grant and Hung 2013)).

It is generally agreed upon that persister cells can represent up to 10% of the cells living in biofilm mode and < 1% of the cells in planktonic growth mode (Yan and Bassler 2019). As they might be partly responsible for the recurrence of chronic infections (Cabral et al. 2018; Fisher et al. 2017; Gomez and McKinney 2004) and could also promote resistance mechanisms development (Levin‐Reisman et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2020), the ‘persister’ field of research is increasingly being explored. In this review, we synthesise recent findings about the molecular mechanisms involved in the physiology, and potentially the appearance of A. baumannii persister cells. We also discuss which molecules may be used to prevent their appearance or to destroy them.

Factors Influencing the Level of Persister Cells

Several studies have investigated the formation of persister cells by A. baumannii , starting with the study by Barth et al. (2013). Thirty‐seven clinical strains from the hospital of Porto Alegre (Brazil), isolated from January to September 2012, were tested for persister cell formation with two antibiotics, polymyxin B and tobramycin (Barth et al. 2013). This study showed the proportion of persister cells strongly depended on the strain and varied between 0.0007% to 10.1% and 0.0003% to 11.84% of the total population, for polymyxin B and tobramycin respectively (Barth et al. 2013). However, for the majority of isolates, the most likely proportion was about 1.2% for polymyxin B and 0.5% for tobramycin.

In another study about the same hospital (Gallo, Donamore, et al. 2017), 20 other clinical isolates were classified according to their ability to form biofilms. As expected, the proportion of persister cells in biofilm mode was much higher than in planktonic mode but surprisingly, strong biofilm producers had some of the lowest persister levels. Persister levels show a higher heterogeneity (0.2%–7.2%) for isolates producing low biofilm compared with those producing more biofilm. Among the isolates, 5 were tested in planktonic mode at different concentrations (15–200 times the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of meropenem). The proportion of persister did not vary as a function of antibiotic concentration. The authors suggested that this persister level heterogeneity could have four distinct and non‐exclusive origins: the individual ability to produce persistence stochastically, specific regulatory mechanisms, the metabolic structure of each isolate and the influence of other previous stresses.

Persister cells were also observed after ciprofloxacin treatment of non‐multidrug resistance (MDR) strong biofilm former clinical isolates (Shenkutie et al. 2020). This treatment also increased the level of ciprofloxacin resistance in regrown planktonic cells, suggesting a link between biofilm, persistence and resistance.

Treatments by aminoglycosides, in particular tobramycin, were also shown to promote persister formation (Donamore et al. 2018), the prevalence of which was modulated by oxygenation level and composition of the culture medium (Donamore et al. 2018; Nicol et al. 2019). This modulation via nutrient availability was also observed on persister levels obtained after ciprofloxacin treatment (Nicol et al. 2019). The growth phase is another factor that was shown to strongly influence the proportion of persister cells (Wood et al. 2013), but this has not yet been demonstrated for A. baumannii . Thus, while the amount of newly‐formed persister depends weakly on the culture parameters or on the antibiotic used, it was suggested to be essentially related to the strain in A. baumannii . Indeed, the diversity of the A. baumannii genomes reflects a high degree of diversity in the strains themselves (Mangas et al. 2019). In fact, out of the 2500 A. baumannii genomes, only 2221 genes are shared among all strains (core genome), out of the 19,272 that correspond to the pangenome. This genome diversity could be linked to the strain's ability to form persister cells. Indeed, the studies of different isolates suggests that the proportion of persister formation is strain‐dependent, implying that the genetic factor is involved (Barth et al. 2013).

The presence of subpopulations is also due to phenotypic heterogeneity. This phenomenon can explain the individual differences observed when genetic and environmental differences between cells are minimised (Elowitz et al. 2002; Ozbudak et al. 2002). This phenomenon can constitute a strategy to maximise the chance of survival in case of a drastic environmental change (Ackermann 2015). Several factors can influence the difference between individuals in the same environment: growth rate, division time, age and history of the cell, asymmetric division, gene expression or unequal distribution (Dhar et al. 2016). Gene expression is the most important factor influencing cell differentiation.

Stochastic genome expression occurs when the level of expression of a gene fluctuates significantly between genetically identical individuals in a population (Elowitz et al. 2002). Noise is therefore a genome‐wide phenomenon that results from the stochastic nature of the biochemical reactions that take place during gene expression and the relatively low abundance of the molecules involved (Raser and O'Shea 2005). This notion was introduced by Elowitz et al. who distinguished two different cell noises population (Elowitz et al. 2002). The intrinsic noise leads to differential expression of the genome from one cell to another and it is due on the one hand to the probability that two actors in a reaction meet, (e.g., the RNA polymerase and the gene promoter). On the other hand, the difference in abundance between certain compounds that influence gene expression, the extrinsic noise population (Elowitz et al. 2002; Swain et al. 2011; Eldar and Elowitz 2010). Other parameters can influence gene expression by influencing intrinsic noise, for example, DNA topology, promoter architecture, bursty gene expression, random partitioning of key players during cell division (Engl 2018).

Indeed, epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation, alter gene expression without modifying genomic sequences (Casadesús and Low 2013). This phenomenon is increasingly considered to be related to the persister state of cells due to its reversible nature (Riber and Hestbjerg Hansen 2021; Lin et al. 2025). A recent study showed that, when tracking individual cells, the last cells to stop growing are the first to regrow in response to nutrients. These results indicate the presence of a long‐term ‘memory effect’ of the previous cellular state (Jõers and Tenson 2016). In A. baumannii , overexpression of a methyltransferase has been observed in strains resistant to various antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, imipenem, cefotaxime, and rifampicin) (Kim et al. 2022).

Toxin/Antitoxin Systems Related to the Formation of Persister Cells

Toxin/antitoxin (TA) systems are composed of a stable toxin and an unstable antitoxin, which neutralises the activity of the toxin (Schuster and Bertram 2013). Initially presented as a plasmid maintenance system (Hayes 2003), new advances have shown that these systems also act on the physiology of the bacteria by slowing bacterial growth down in response to a stress factor, thus promoting an entry into bacterial persister state (Jurėnas et al. 2022).

The TA systems are currently only partially described in A. baumannii and present a significant variability depending on the strain (Jurenaite et al. 2013; Al Marjania et al. 2020; Shin et al. 2020). The study of 476 genomes of A. baumannii isolates predicted 15 different TA systems, detected either on the strain genome or on their plasmids for instance (ACICU, AYE, p3ABAYE…) (Jurenaite et al. 2013). The authors subcloned the toxins and expressed them in E. coli under an inducible promoter, before investigating the functionality and toxicity of the protein. In this group, five TA systems appear to be functional, all type II. Three of these TA are orthologs of the bacterial and archaeal systems RelB/RelE, HicA/HicB and HigB/HigA and two are unique TA modules, AbkA/AbkB (also called SplT/SplA) and CheT/CheA (Jurenaite et al. 2013; Al Marjania et al. 2020; Shin et al. 2020).

AbkA/AbkB: Among these five potential TA systems, the AbkA/AbkB system was the only one studied for its involvement in persister formation in A. baumannii . It is mainly carried by plasmid but does not appear to be directly involved in plasmid maintenance (Lean and Yeo 2017; Mosqueda et al. 2014; Armalytė et al. 2018). The toxin AbkB is an endoribonuclease that inhibits translation, and AbkA is its cognate antitoxin (Armalytė et al. 2018). A study with a clinical strain tolerant to chlorhexidine and carbapenem resistant showed that persister formed when cultured with chlorhexidine and imipenem 10 × MIC. In this case the abkB gene was overexpressed (2.77‐fold change) compared to a similar carbapenem‐resistant strain that was not tolerant to chlorhexidine (Fernández‐García et al. 2018). In this study, when comparing two reference strains containing or not containing the ABkA/B system, persister were only formed with the strain containing the AbkA/AbkB system (Fernández‐García et al. 2018). Overexpression of the AbkB gene was also observed with colistin treatment (10 × MIC) in Iranian clinical isolates (Latifi et al. 2022). It is also interesting to note that a significant correlation was observed between the presence of abkA and blaOXA‐24 genes in carbapenem‐resistant isolates obtained from patients in Iran (Japoni‐Nejad et al. 2021). In this case, it was suggested that these systems play a potential role as plasmid stabilisation systems, contributing to the evolution of antibiotic resistance in A. baumannii isolates from Lithuanian and Malaysian hospital (Jurenaite et al. 2013; Lean et al. 2016). In addition, plasmid stabilisation by AbkAB appears to be conserved in the rep 3 family plasmid. Rep 3 corresponds to 478 of the 813 plasmids studied in this study, and this family is present in several distinct STs (Lam and Hamidian 2024).

Concerning the other TA systems, their function was hypothesized regarding their identity with already described TA systems in different bacteria.

HigA/B: The involvement of HigB/HigA in the formation of A. baumannii persister has yet to be studied. However, in Mycobacterium tuberculosis it is involved in stress response (Sharma et al. 2021). In Pseudomonas aeruginosa , the toxin HigB regulates virulence factor production (e.g., pyocyanin production, pyochelin, swarming mobility) and promote biofilm formation where a high proportion of cells are known to persist (Wood and Wood 2016; Song et al. 2021). HigA antitoxin represses the expression of a key transcriptional regulator of the virulence systems by directly binding to their promoter regions, and it controls pyocyanin synthesis, type III secretion system (T3SS), and type VI secretion system (T6SS) expression (Song et al. 2021). In A. baumannii , the HigB2/HigA2 system was found on the 11 kb plasmid pAB120 also carrying genes for resistance to carbapenems (Armalytė et al. 2018). The hetero‐oligomer has a feedback control on its expression. It was also modulated by environmental stresses and was shown to be overexpressed in stationary phase or under iron deficiency (Armalytė et al. 2018). Both HigB/HigA and AbkA/AbkB were shown to be the most abundant systems, and had currently been shown to be carried by plasmids. They were predominantly present in isolates from the international clones one and two (IC‐I and IC‐II) (Hayes 2003; Japoni‐Nejad et al. 2021; Armalytė et al. 2018; Povilonis et al. 2013; Shahbazi et al. 2023).

CheT/CheA: Regarding A. baumannii CheT/CheA system, CheA presented 25% identical with the E. coli DinJ antitoxin, and has 37% identity with an N‐acetyltransferase superfamily (Japoni‐Nejad et al. 2021). The predicted structure of the protein seems to show that CheA possesses helix‐turn‐helix (HTH) domains characteristic of DinJ. In E. coli , DinJ/YafQ system is involved in the formation of persister cells (Keren et al. 2004). Kill‐rescue assays on A. baumannii isolates showed that CheT/CheA served as a TA module: the HTH domain of the protein CheT acted as a toxin and the GNAT domain of CheA protein neutralised the HTH effect acting thus as an antitoxin (Japoni‐Nejad et al. 2021).

RelB/RelE: The RelB/RelE system is detected at genomic level in some A. baumannii clinical strains (AC1633, ACICU, MDR‐ZJ06, TCDC‐AB0715, TYTH1 or AB 5075) (Mangas et al. 2019; Jurenaite et al. 2013; Al Marjania et al. 2020; Ghazi Alattraqchi et al. 2021). In E. coli , the target of the RelB toxin is the A ribosomal site. The blockage of this site promotes the cleavage of the messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) during translation (Fernández‐García et al. 2018), halting it and thus putting a stop to protein production (Pedersen et al. 2003).

HicA/B: The last TA system hicA/hicB was shown to be overexpressed in A. baumannii ATCC 17978 upon ciprofloxacin treatment (50 × MIC), suggesting this system was involved in the response of bacteria to antibiotic shocks (Kashyap, Sharma, and Capalash 2021). In E. coli K12, the toxin HicA cleaves mRNA and tRNA in a ribosome‐independent manner and HicA is reported to be inactivated by HicB (Jørgensen et al. 2009). In Burkholderia pseudomallei , this system was suggested to play a role in the formation of persister cells (Butt et al. 2014).

Finally, a Zeta/Epsilon system has been predicted and detected in the plasmids p2ABTCDC, pAbSK‐OXA‐82, pABTJ1 and pACICU2 (Jurenaite et al. 2013). Zeta toxins are notably larger (360 amino acids) than their currently known functional orthologs (270 amino acids). The Zeta toxin in A. baumannii could block cell wall biosynthesis by phosphorylation of the peptidoglycan precursor uridine diphosphate‐N‐acetylglucosamine (UNAG) (Karthika et al. 2021). Indeed, Zeta would form an ATP‐UNAG‐Zeta complex. This complex will block the action of MurA, responsible for peptidoglycan biosynthesis (Sonkar et al. 2017), which is the initial step in membrane biosynthesis (Jørgensen et al. 2009). Zeta then appears to have a strong implication in cell multiplication and would be an interesting target, as it prevents bacteria from growing rapidly.

Although not an antitoxin‐toxin system, PPK kinase is also involved in protein degradation, particularly in antitoxin degradation. A study in A. baumannii investigated the effect of polyP via a mutant of PPK (Karthika et al. 2021). In this study, ATCC 17978 strains deficient in PPK1 (Dppk1::Apr) and supplemented with PPK1 (Dppk1::Apr/PJL02‐ppk1) were studied. As expected, the authors showed that PPK1 is essential for polyP formation in vivo. Consequently, the PPK1 mutant had reduced motility due to destabilisation of the pili structure, reduced biofilm formation and reduced persistence under antibiotics (40 × MIC of Ampicillin) but also under other stresses such as H2O2, heat shock and starvation stress. These phenotypes were restored by complementation. Interestingly, metabolomics analysis revealed that PPK1 was associated with glycerophospholipid metabolism and fatty acid biosynthesis. The authors therefore suggested that alteration of glycerophospholipid metabolism could alter the membrane charge, resulting in a resistance of bacteria to the tested stresses (Lv et al. 2022).

Second Messengers

The second messengers, (p)ppGpp and cyclic dimeric diguanosine monophosphate (c‐di‐GMP), are main signal molecules, the level of which determines the entry in persister state (Sonkar et al. 2017; D'argenio and Miller 2004; Jenal and Malone 2006).

(p)ppGpp: Indeed, in E. coli , (p)ppGpp is clearly a major player in induction of persister state (Pacios et al. 2020). Increased levels of (p)ppGpp induce transcription of RpoS and RpoE (Hirsch and Elliott 2002). ppGpp inhibits deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) primase (Maciag et al. 2009), and ribosomal ribonucleic acid (rRNA) transcription (Zuo et al. 2013), deregulates an early step in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) formation (Brückner et al. 2023), and binds to ribonucleic acid (RNA) polymerase (Chatterji et al. 1998). ppGpp could directly inhibit the negative DNA supercoiling (Kraemer et al. 2019), and it could indirectly induce transcription of ribosomal modulation factor (Rmf) and hibernation promotion factor (Hpf), which plays a role in ribosome dimerization (90S and 100S) (Maki and Yoshida 2022). In A. baumannii , it was shown that the formation of (p)ppGpp is mediated by RelA (ABUW_3302) (Table 1) (Pérez‐Varela et al. 2020), a monofunctional protein that only contributes to pppGpp synthesis. Moreover, the reduction of pppGpp into ppGpp is mediated by SpoT, a monofunctional (p)ppGpp hydrolase (Tamman et al. 2022). The relA deletion resulted in the formation of 4 times less persister cells than in WT AB5075 strain under 50 × MIC colistin or 80 × MIC rifampin treatments. The mutant relA also underwent variations of its motility, morphology or colony morphology (Pérez‐Varela et al. 2020). In other strains like ATCC 17978, it was shown that a mutant deficient in (p)ppGpp production (ΔA1S_0579) is 2 to 4 times more susceptible to antibiotics, that is, MICs are reduced by a factor of 1–4 compared to wide type (WT) or complemented strain (Jung et al. 2020). The authors suggest this susceptibility could be due to the under‐expression of efflux transporters (AdeI, AdeJ and AdeK) and, less obviously, to the over‐expression of certain proteins belonging to the transporter systems, such as AetA, AdeB and AdeM (Jung et al. 2020; Kim et al. 2021).

c‐di‐GMP: It has been shown that this second messenger molecule increases the proportion of persister cells in E. coli (Liao et al. 2024). It also influences virulence factors in many bacteria (Antelmann et al. 2021; Hall and Lee 2018; Valentini and Filloux 2019). In P. aeruginosa , HigB decreases the intracellular level of c‐di‐GMP, which is responsible for the increased expression of the T3SS genes and the repression of biofilm formation (Zhang et al. 2018). It is worth noting that the diguanylate cyclase enzymes involved in c‐di‐GMP synthesis contain a catalytic site with a GGDEF/EAL motif (Dahlstrom and O'toole 2017). In A. baumannii ATCC 17978 strain, several diguanylate cyclases were identified and characterised, but their involvement in persister cells has never been studied (Ahmad et al. 2020).

SOS Response in Persister Cells

The SOS pathway is an inducible DNA damage repair system that is pivotal for bacterial adaptation, pathogenesis and diversification of population. The SOS response activates systems to carry out adaptation processes to face hostile or even toxic environments. These systems can contribute to growth arrest, DNA repair, repair of replication errors or mobilisation of mobile genetic elements (transposons and insertion sequences) (Maslowska et al. 2019; Fornelos et al. 2016; Lima‐Noronha et al. 2022). Activation of the SOS response in persister cells could explain the appearance of adaptative mutations, especially in the presence of antibiotics inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production leading therefore to DNA damages (Jara et al. 2015). Persister could thus be a key stage in the course of genetic resistance development (Norton et al. 2013). The SOS response seems to be a key player in persister physiology, for both maintaining DNA integrity and remodelling it (Tomas et al. 2020). However, DNA damage response is a multifactorial response that appears to be species‐specific and may have a major influence on the development of new capabilities of the species (Hare et al. 2012).

The DNA damages induced by ROS can be produced by antibacterial molecules and leads to an SOS response (Tiwari et al. 2018). This response is mediated by RecA, which binds to single‐stranded DNA and protects it from degradation. In many bacteria, LexA is the transcriptional repressor of RecA and of all SOS response genes. It is cleaved by RecA if the latter is bound to a single strand of DNA activating the transcription of SOS genes. In Acinetobacter, the mechanism seems different, as the genome lacks the lexA gene (Cook et al. 2022; Candra et al. 2024). Indeed, the error‐prone reserve polymerase UmuD (polymerase V) is recruited during SOS response. UmuD is repressed by UmuDAb, itself repressed by DdrR. DdrR overexpression represses the DNA repair genes (Pavlin et al. 2022).

It was shown that, after 1 h tobramycin treatment at 10 × MIC, umuD and ddrR of ATCC 17978 strain were over‐expressed (Kashyap et al. 2022). The transcriptomics of ATCC 17978 persister selected by treatment of 50 × MIC of ciprofloxacin showed overexpression of different systems, like the DNA repair system, the phenylacetic acid degradation pathway, the leucine catabolism, the HicAB toxin‐antitoxin system and the ROS response (Table 1) (Kashyap, Sharma, and Capalash 2021). Several genes displayed significant changes like pare (A1S_3359); recA (A1S_1962); umuC (A1S_2008); umuD (A1S_1174, A1S_1389); ddrR (A1S_1388); ruvB (A1S_2588); recB (A1S_0356) and repA (A1S_0663). The construction of deletion mutants of recA (A1S_1962) and umuD homologue (A1S_1389) was performed. The umuD mutant formed fewer persister cells than the WT strain, while the recA mutant did not form any persister cells after 3 h of antibiotic treatment (Kashyap, Sharma, and Capalash 2021). These results suggest that these proteins (umuD and recA) are involved in persister formation. It should be also noticed that RecA is involved in resistance to some β‐lactam antibiotics and contributes to survival after ultraviolet (UV) treatment, and to virulence in macrophages in vitro and in mice in vivo (Aranda et al. 2011; Lin et al. 2019).

Other transcriptomic analyses on ATCC 19606 and two clinical strains treated by 10 mg/L of ciprofloxacin and 10 mg/L of imipenem for 6 h were also performed (Hayes 2003). They similarly showed that the three strains overexpressed dam, recC, umuD, phoU and glpD. The methyltransferase Dam, when subcloned under the control of an inducible promoter, positively regulated recC, umuD, phoU and glpD genes. Consequently, the percentage of persister cells was more abundant in the Dam‐overexpressing mutant. The RecC overexpressing‐mutant also presented an increased rate of persister cell formation (Table 1) (Hayes 2003).

Antibiotic shock treatment frequently leads to the generation of ROS (Van Acker and Coenye 2017; Herb et al. 2021; Masadeh et al. 2016; Yeo et al. 2024). As seen previously, ROS may damage DNA leading to the SOS response (Tomas et al. 2020). However, the bacteria may also activate some other mechanisms to face the ROS stress and maintain the cell integrity. These mechanisms are essential for the rapid adaptation or survival of the bacteria. This response is indirectly involved in the state of persister cells (Kouhsari et al. 2023). As an example, pyocyanin generates ROS in A. baumannii . It was shown that ROS production leads to catalase and Sod increased expression in order to eliminate ROS (Bhargava et al. 2014). Clearance of A. baumannii polymyxin B persister by Rifampicin was shown to be related to a significant down regulation of sodB and therefore to an enhanced generation of ROS (Table 1) (Dubey et al. 2021). Additionally, it was shown that a SodB A. baumannii mutant was more sensitive to antibiotics and generated less persister cells (Dubey et al. 2021).

The Phenyl Acetic Catabolic Pathway

In ATCC 19606 strain under a ceftazidime (50 × MIC) antibiotic shock, the aromatic compound degradation (PAA) genes were overexpressed in persister cells (Table 1) (Alkasir et al. 2018). The authors suggest that the degradation of the aromatic ring of antibiotics was used by A. baumannii under conditions of nutrient limitation. In ATCC 17978, under antibiotic subinhibitory concentrations, the PAA operon was shown to be overexpressed while Csu pilus expression and biofilm formation were repressed (Hooppaw et al. 2022). This suggests that this pathway may be a part of the regulatory network used in response to antibiotic treatment.

Changes at Membrane Level in Persister Cells

The next section of this review, which deals with therapeutics, will show that the membrane is main target for anti‐persister cell agent development (Schrank et al. 2021). However, few studies have investigated the lipidome of persister cells (Table 1). In M. tuberculosis dormant cells, an accumulation of triacylglycerol (TG) linked to the membrane or as inclusion bodies (Bansal‐Mutalik and Nikaido 2014; Christensen et al. 1999) was highlighted (Canaan et al. 2019). TG could be involved in energy storage used during hibernation (Daniel et al. 2004; Low et al. 2009) and used to reduce the toxic burden of free fatty acids causing a reductive stress (Weir et al. 1972). It could also contribute to antibiotic tolerance by redirecting cellular carbon fluxes away from the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Baek et al. 2011). In addition, genes encoding TG biosynthetic enzymes were found predominantly in virulent M. tuberculosis strains, suggesting a potential role of these genes in the infection (Canaan et al. 2019). In A. baumannii , the lipidome of persister cells under ciprofloxacin treatment (100 × MIC) was analysed using LC–MS (Table 1) (Vergoz et al. 2024). It was demonstrated that membranes of persister cells displayed specific lipids like lyso‐1‐phosphatidyl‐2‐acyl‐glycerol‐3‐phosphoethanolamines (LPAGPE) and 1‐phosphatidyl‐2‐acyl‐glycerol‐3‐phosphoethanolamines (PAGPE). These specific lipids may compensate for a repulsive effect of the phosphate negative charges due to an increase in cardiolipin abundance. Moreover, the acyl chains of their lipids were modified by hydroxylation or di‐unsaturations. The presence of di‐unsaturation and hydroxylation could contribute to a change in membrane fluidity. In persister cells, a rigidification of the membrane could allow bacteria to modify the permeability of their membranes for survival, and may accompany the bacterial growth arrest. Finally, the persister cells were shown to accumulate wax esters (WE) composed of two fatty acids and a fatty diol. These uncommon WE could be a specific metabolic key for A. baumannii to store energy during growth arrest and used for rapid regrowth (Vergoz et al. 2024).

Concerning membrane proteins, genes coding for OmpA and OmpW were shown overexpressed by a factor of 5.5 and 10.5 respectively, in 15 × MIC meropenem persister of the Acb‐1 strain (Table 1) (Schmitt et al. 2023). In agreement with this, virulence assays on Galleria mellonella indicated that these persister were more virulent than control cells (Schmitt et al. 2023). On the other hand, ompW was under‐expressed in tobramycin persister. (Kashyap, Sharma, and Capalash 2021) Its expression in persister may thus be drug‐dependent.

Finally, it was already shown that A. baumannii persister cells underwent morphological changes. Indeed, a study on persister generated by 100 × MIC meropenem showed that cells had a spherical shape with a reduced permeability (Table 1) (Bhando et al. 2020). These morphological changes were also observed for the ATCC 17978 strain under a 6 mg/L meropenem treatment (Zou et al. 2020). Furthermore, images of persister cells under tobramycin (10 mg/L) or meropenem (15 mg/L) treatment by transmission and scanning electron microscopies showed the presence of a division septa at the cell midpoint (Donamore et al. 2018). The presence of this septum was intriguing. It was suggested that the persister cells could still be in a multiplying state to maintain a balance between multiplication and cell death, as also suggested in Mycobacterium smegmatis (Wakamoto et al. 1979).

Strategies Against A. baumannii Persister Cells

Two main approaches can be used to fight persister cells: (i) the combined therapeutic approach, which uses antibacterial compounds to eradicate/eliminate as mains cells as possible so that no persister cells remain or form. (prevention concept: do not form). (ii) the development of direct killing agents that are able to kill persister cells specifically (elimination concept: kill persister cells). In this approach, the most effective strategy for eliminating persister cells is to take growth arrest into account and find a target not linked to multiplication activity (Defraine et al. 2018). Effective anti‐persister agents often take the bacterial membrane as a primary target. In A. baumannii , a combination of treatment strategies and direct killing has been explored and will be presented in this section (Table 2) (Khan et al. 2020; Gallo, Alexandre, et al. 2017).

Preventing Approach: Combination of Treatments

The combination of treatments to prevent (do not form) the formation of A. baumannii persister cells is a strategy that generally uses antibiotics with radically different targets, the membrane being among the preferred ones. Thus, most of the studies combine the use of the polymyxin B or colistin with β‐lactams.

Recently, it has been shown that meropenem or carbenicillin treatments resulted in the appearance of a particular cell phenotype: round, metabolically active cells that have lost their cell wall (Zou et al. 2020). A modification of their envelope leads to a susceptibility to treatment with β‐lactam (ampicillin and carbenicillin) with a 100–1000‐fold increase in killing power (Zou et al. 2020). These results suggest that antibiotic treatment can cause a modification of the envelope, making the bacteria more sensitive to another antibiotic targeting the membrane. Another study focused on three A. baumannii clinical strains (Acb‐1, Acb‐8 and Acb‐2) and determined the level of persister cells generated by polymyxin B and meropenem, tested at different concentrations alone or in combination (Gallo, Alexandre, et al. 2017). Mono‐treatment with these antibiotics at 5, 10 or 15 mg/L, induced between 0.0031% and 0.9743% of persister depending on the choice of antibiotics. However, these antibiotics in combination did not produce persister cells. This study highlighted and confirmed the useful effects of combined therapy.

Synergistic effects were also observed with other antibiotic combinations: 30 mg/L carbenicillin with 2 mg/L colistin or 200 mg/L ampicillin with 2 mg/L colistin. They increased the killing efficiency on two A. baumannii clinical isolates (Zou et al. 2020). These combinations provide a two‐log increase in bacterial eradication.

Colistin has also been tested in combination with other antibiotics, for example, colistin plus tobramycin, rifampicin or colistin (Kashyap, Kaur, et al. 2021). These authors show that these combinations eradicate all cells. Sequential antibiotic combinations were also examined (Xie et al. 2022). It was demonstrated that treatment by colistin followed by amikacin eradicated the entire population, when the reverse sequence did not. Even though no high antibiotic concentration was used to select A. baumannii persister, this study clearly shows that sensitization by a membranolytic agent is highly effective. Colistin in combination with the econazole, an imidazole derivative, was also tested (Xie et al. 2022). This combination showed a synergic effect with a 128‐fold reduction of MIC colistin with 10 mg/L econazole.

The combination of an antimicrobial agent with a phage cocktail to control or limit biofilm formation has also been investigated (Rastegar et al. 2024). Combinations of four temperate bacteriophages (SA1, Eve, Ftm and Gln) and different antibiotics (ampicillin/sulbactam, meropenem and colistin) enhanced biofilm inhibition and degradation on 30 extensively drug‐resistant (XDR) strains of A. baumannii . Moreover, it has been shown that a combination of Paride phage and meropenem can sterilise deep‐dormant cultures in vitro and greatly reduce a resilient bacterial infection of a tissue cage implant in mice (Maffei et al. 2024). In this context, it would be interesting to test these phage/antibiotic combinations on persister cells.

Persister Eradication Approach

In A. baumannii , the first bactericidal agent used to eradicate persister cells was Art‐175 from the Artilysine family (Defraine et al. 2016). Art‐175 is a phagic endolysin which degrades peptidoglycan, fused with specific peptides that destabilise the outer membrane (Defraine et al. 2016). The specificity between peptidoglycan and endolysin results from the long and intense coevolution between phages and bacteria. Art‐175 (60 mg/L) was highly effective at 1 h and eradicated all strains after 24 h of treatment. Persister selected with tobramycin and treated with 30 × MIC Art‐157 on the RUH134 strain led to a drastic population reduction in just 20 min. It took only 120 min to reach the limit of bacteria detection.

The effect of molecules with an aromatic core (1‐BC or 2‐NC), an L‐lysine moiety (K), and a variable lipophilic chain (8, 10, 12 carbon chain) were also tested on E. coli , Klebsiella pneumoniae , P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii persister cells (Ghosh et al. 2015). NCK‐10 and BCK‐12 activity on A. baumannii clinical isolates (MTCC 1425, R676 and R674) showed that lytic activity between 1.5 and 9 μM. NCK‐10 (8 × MIC) did not induce resistance even after 20 cycles in E. coli . NCK‐10 appears to depolarize and permeabilize the membranes of E. coli persister cells (induced by 300 mg/L of ampicillin for 3 h). NCK‐10 was burn‐tested on female Balb/c mice infected with A. baumannii at concentrations of 17.5 mg/kg and did not induce any toxic effects. Moreover, this compound significantly reduced the bacterial load after a 7‐day application.

Squalamine, a polycationic aminosterol isolated from the shark Squalus acanthias with antibacterial properties, was tested on A. baumannii persister cells generated by ciprofloxacin (Nicol et al. 2019). This molecule contains a polyamine tail, which is known to be able to quickly induce membrane depolarization and disruption (Blanchet et al. 2018). In this study, unlike those presented above, the methodology is slightly different. The authors generated persister cells using an antibiotic (ciprofloxacin 1000 × MIC). They first characterised the selected population to determine the proportion of persister cells and VNBCs. Then the authors tested squalamine, the molecule of interest, with colistin as the control for cell lysis. Squalamine and colistin were able to eradicate persister cells at 100 mg/L (50 × MIC) and 500 mg/L (100 × MIC) within 9 and 12 h respectively. Colistin concentrations (100 × MIC) were higher than those of squalamine (50 × MIC), yet required more time to eradicate the persistent population, which underlines the efficacy of squalamine. The latter was used at concentrations below the minimum hemolytic concentration (IC50 > 66 mg/L (Vergoz et al. 2023)) demonstrating a low toxicity.

Carvacrol, Eugenol, and Thymol are compounds approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), having a GRAS (generally recognised as safe) status (Bhando et al. 2020). They can be used as food additives. These compounds were tested on persister cells selected with meropenem (100 × MIC). Thymol used alone at its MIC concentration led to a complete eradication of meropenem induced persister. In combination with meropenem, it completely eradicated persister at lower concentration (0.5 × MIC). It also fully eradicated a 48 h mature biofilm at concentration 1 × MIC. Meanwhile, eugenol and carvacrol eradicated biofilm persister at much higher concentrations than thymol. Thymol treatment significantly inhibited meropenem persister of MDR A. baumannii isolates, both as monotherapy and co‐therapy, and was also effective on rifampicin, tigecycline and polymyxin B persister at concentrations equal to or less than 1 × MIC. Thymol was demonstrated to induce membrane depolarization and permeability, as well as an increased ROS production.

Recently, it has been shown that phages can have an effect on dormant cells (Sanchez‐Torres et al. 2024). The action of the phage Paride on dormant cells of P. aeruginosa has been studied. This study showed that this phage has an active lytic cycle on P. aeruginosa cells in deep dormancy (Maffei et al. 2024). To our knowledge, phages with an effective lytic cycle on persistent A. baumannii cells have not yet been studied.

Conclusion

As shown by this review, current approaches to eradicating persister focus on different strategies: the use of molecules that inhibit persister formation, such as naturally‐occurring compounds like Art‐175, phages and squalamine, or the combination of antibiotics, one of which acts mechanistically on the bacterial membrane. However, persister formation in A. baumannii is highly dependent on bacterial strain and culture conditions. This physiological complexity potentially involves various molecular determinants, still currently under investigation. Among them, toxin‐antitoxin systems could modulate the formation of these persister cells (in particular AbkA/AbkB). Other systems involved in persister cell formation for other bacteria, like second messengers, the SOS response are still poorly explored in A. baumannii . Interestingly, membrane modifications appear to be an important factor in the physiology of A. baumannii persister cells that could be taken into consideration to develop new anti‐persister molecules. Nevertheless, further investigations regarding the physiology of this particular bacterial population are needed to identify specific biomarkers and expandour therapeutic arsenal to combat this particular bacterial population.
