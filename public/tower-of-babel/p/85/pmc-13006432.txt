Suboptimal therapeutic responses associated with semaglutide in type 2 diabetes and obesity: A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Donghyun Ko, Do Han Kim, Faith Xin Ning Tan, Glenn Jun Kit Ho, Rubhan Chandra, Zhang Zi Xuan, Ethan Tham Kai Jun, Nicole Shu Ying Tang, Hee Seung Yang, Jeffery Wei Heng Koh, Pojsakorn Danpanichkul, Mayank Dalakoti, Nicholas L. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySemaglutide has emerged as a leading therapy for metabolic disorders, with robust evidence supporting its role in treating obesity and type 2 diabetes while reducing cardiovascular and renal risks. However, limited data exist on the proportion of patients who have suboptimal therapeutic responses or discontinue treatment due to adverse events. We present an analysis including 33 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comprising 21,023 participants. Across included trials of patients with and without type 2 diabetes, 33.4% and 65.1% of participants did not achieve ≥5% or ≥10% total body weight reduction, respectively. Stratified analyses demonstrated higher rates of suboptimal outcomes among individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with up to 79.8% not achieving ≥10% weight loss, compared to 30.1% among individuals without T2DM. While semaglutide is effective in many patients, a substantial subset of patients has suboptimal weight or glycemic improvements or discontinues therapy. This study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251086132).

Introduction

The global prevalence of obesity has more than tripled, currently affecting 880 million adults.1,2 By 2050, up to 60% of adults over the age of 25 are projected to have obesity, with a corresponding rise in the burden of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).3 Glucagon-like-peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have played a key role in revolutionizing weight management and its associated metabolic comorbidities in the past decade. Among the many GLP-1 RAs introduced, semaglutide produced by Novo Nordisk was the major game changer to the metabolic disease industry. Initially approved for the management of T2DM, semaglutide was later approved for chronic weight management in patients with obesity and obesity-related complications, with or without T2DM.

Semaglutide has gained increasing popularity in recent decades for its efficacy in weight loss, glycemic control, and improvements in cardiovascular and hepatic parameters.4,5,6,7 Recent evidence shows that the number of prescriptions filled for semaglutide has increased substantially, with over 2.6 million prescriptions filled by the end of 2023, and this is expected to grow as rising studies have proven its efficacy in other chronic conditions.8 Principally, semaglutide works by activating glucagon-like-peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) throughout the different brain regions and specific nuclei essential for the physiological control of food intake, leading to weight reduction.9 Subsequently, the effects of semaglutide have also been shown to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events10 and have recently been shown to improve liver fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).7 Although numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses have underscored the significant clinical benefits of semaglutide, there remains a lack of pooled analysis assessing the variability in treatment response, particularly among patients who do not achieve key clinical outcomes. The rationale for using semaglutide is that it was the first highly effective GLP-1 RA approved and therefore has far more published data than the newer agents, such as tirzepatide. Thus, this study aims to evaluate the rate of suboptimal responses to achieve therapeutic endpoints to denote the importance of individualized therapy in terms of semaglutide and the incidence of discontinuation of participants during clinical trials with semaglutide across a broad patient population.

Results

Search results

An initial search from Medline and Embase and an independent search yielded 3,264 studies. After removing duplicates, 2,597 articles were included in the screening process. After the removal of 2,142 studies (the studies were not RCTs or not related to the outcomes required for the analysis), 455 full-text studies were assessed for eligibility. In total, 33 articles were included in the meta-analysis after 422 studies were further excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria after detailed scrutiny (Figure 1). The outcomes of 21,023 patients using semaglutide were extracted from these studies, and the mean time from study enrollment to last follow-up was 59 weeks. Overall, the included studies focused on the efficacy of semaglutide in total body weight reduction and HbA1c reduction in patients with obesity and T2DM, respectively. The summarized characteristics of each study can be found in Table S2. The complete risk of bias in the included trials was low across all studies (Tables S3 and S6). Lastly, among the patients with T2DM, 46.1% (95% CI: 42.9–49.3; I2: 76.4%) did not achieve ≥5% weight reduction (Table 1). MASH-related outcomes

Two studies have analyzed the efficacy of semaglutide in patients with MASH, fibrosis stage F2–F3. Binary outcomes were unable to be pooled as each study had a different dosing schedule (daily vs. weekly). Among the patients with stage F2 or F3 fibrosis treated with 0.4 mg daily, the proportion of those who did not achieve MASH resolution with no worsening of fibrosis was 41.0% at 72 weeks.40 Among those treated with 2.4 mg weekly, 37.0% of patients with F2 or F3 fibrosis did not achieve MASH resolution with no worsening of fibrosis at 240 weeks.7 One study of semaglutide with 2.4 mg weekly in MASH-related cirrhosis showed no significant difference in the proportion of patients with an improvement in liver fibrosis of one stage or more without worsening of MASH (11.0%) compared to placebo (29.0%) at 48 weeks.41

Renal-related outcomes

One study analyzed the efficacy of semaglutide specifically in patients with chronic kidney disease and T2DM. The primary outcome was major kidney disease events, a composite of the onset of kidney failure, a sustained 50% or greater reduction in estimated glomular filtration rate (eGFR) from baseline, or death from kidney-related or cardiovascular causes. Overall, 18.7% of patients deteriorated to meet the primary outcome. 9.3%, 5.2%, and 4.9% met the criteria of a persistent ≥50% reduction from baseline in eGFR, a persistent eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2, and initiation of kidney replacement therapy, respectively.39

Adverse reactions

Discontinuation rates of semaglutide due to overall adverse events (AEs) and gastrointestinal-related AEs were 7.4% (95% CI 6.0–9.2; I2: 93.6%) and 4.7% (95% CI: 3.7–5.9; I2: 89.6%), respectively. Most common AEs observed throughout the trials were nausea (21.8% [95% CI: 16.9–27.7; I2: 97.6%]), diarrhea (13.5% [95% CI: 10.5–17.3; I2: 96.6%]), constipation (9.8% [95% CI: 6.5–14.5; I2: 97.0%]), and vomiting (9.4% [95% CI: 7.0–12.4; I2: 96.2%]). Such AEs were more noticeable in patients without T2DM, nausea (32.0% [95% CI: 20.9–45.6; I2: 98.9%]), diarrhea (19.9% [95% CI: 13.5–28.4; I2: 98.5%]), constipation (19.6% [95% CI: 13.1–28.3; I2: 98.5%]), and vomiting (14.1% [95% CI: 9.1–21.2; I2: 98.5%]). Gallbladder-related AEs and hepatic AEs ranged between 1% and 2% in general and subgroups. Discussion

This meta-analysis provides a different perspective for evaluating the magnitude of suboptimal responses to semaglutide in patients with obesity and with and without type 2 diabetes. While it builds upon previously published RCTs, its key distinction lies in the analytical focus, specifically the systematic evaluation of the limitations in achieving core therapeutic endpoints directly associated with the metabolic actions of semaglutide. Importantly, this study does not seek to detract from the substantial body of evidence supporting semaglutide’s clinical utility, which remains one of the major revolutions in metabolic care of the 21st century, but rather to draw attention to the unmet need that still exists, while also alerting clinicians to an important subgroup of patients who may have a suboptimal response and thus require additional care without stigmatizing these patients. Emerging evidence has also suggested that weight loss therapeutics may be modulated by genetic variations.42 Furthermore, some alleles, such as Gly168Ser and Thr370Met, have shown novel insight guiding future research studies into establishing possible management of patients with suboptimal response.43 However, the lack of weight loss remains significantly different between patients with and without T2DM.

While the Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity (STEP) 1 and STEP 2 trials showed that individuals with T2DM treated with semaglutide 2.4 mg appear to have reduced efficacy in weight reduction, the dosing in most of the T2DM studies is significantly lower at 1 mg weekly. However, there are also possible considerations that T2DM may have reduced sensitivity to semaglutide, given the evidence of GLP-1R desensitization in long-standing diabetes.44 Additionally, chronic hyperglycemia can lead to downregulation of GLP-1R expression, which weakens the protective effects of GLP-1 RAs.45 In non-cirrhosis MASH trials, the effectiveness of semaglutide in the resolution of steatohepatitis without worsening of fibrosis was observed to vary between 59% and 63%; furthermore, reduction in liver fibrosis without worsening of steatohepatitis was evident between 37% and 43% of the patients.7,40 Importantly, a large proportion of patients have a combined metabolic milieu and cannot be examined in isolation.46 In a recent meta-analysis of MASH prevalence, over 28.8% had a BMI >27 kg/m2 and were afflicted by T2DM, while 45.7% had obesity with concomitant T2DM.47 Importantly, semaglutide, similar to most chronic medication, does not work when it is discontinued, as shown in the post hoc analysis of the STEP 1 trial, with two-thirds of the initial weight reduction regained after 1 year of discontinuation, with most cardiometabolic improvements reverting toward baseline.48 Thus, semaglutide cannot cure the disease of obesity but rather can maintain control of the disease as shown in STEP 5.15 AEs resulting in the 7.4% of patients who discontinued trial medication were predominantly gastrointestinal. Although the specific breakdown of which gastrointestinal events were responsible for discontinuation is not reported, the most common overall AEs were reported as nausea (21.8%), diarrhea (13.5%), constipation (9.8%), and vomiting (9.4%). These events were more frequent in people without diabetes, where nausea alone affected up to 32% of patients but resulted in discontinuation in only 5% of patients. Other AEs, including gallbladder and hepatic events, occurred in 1%–2% of cases, while acute pancreatitis was rare (<1%). Beyond those reported in RCTs, semaglutide has also been shown to reduce muscle mass, with approximately 20%–40% of total weight loss attributable to muscle loss, albeit patients reported improvements in functionality, and in the STEP-Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF) studies, patients also showed an increase in their 6-min walk test.49,50,51 Additionally, emerging real-world data have identified a potential link between semaglutide and non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION), which was not previously reported in RCTs, most likely because NAION is extremely rare; even pooling all the RCTs together, the signal did not emerge.52

Despite these caveats, semaglutide has demonstrated substantial and clinically meaningful benefits across a broad spectrum of metabolic and systemic outcomes. Longitudinal pooled analyses indicate a significant reduction in major adverse cardiac events by approximately 20%, highlighting its value not only as a weight-loss agent but also as a cardiometabolic risk reducer. Latest studies have even proved the benefit of semaglutide in reducing the risk of first-time Alzheimer’s disease and craving in alcohol use disorder.53,54 Collectively, these developments illustrate the evolving role of semaglutide and its analogs in addressing complex, overlapping domains of metabolic, cardiovascular, neurocognitive, and addictive disorders, signaling a paradigm shift in how we approach chronic disease management. Together, this makes it more important to understand why some patients do not respond and how this can be addressed.

Limitations of the study

The current study presents the most recent updates on semaglutide in patients with different underlying conditions. However, there are several limitations. Inherent heterogeneity was also present due to the presence of different antidiabetic medications, baseline BMI, and other comorbidities. Although the present study reports substantial heterogeneity, I2, a surrogate measure of absolute heterogeneity, has been shown to be artificially inflated in studies of larger sample sizes.55 A large sample from the studies originated from North America, for which our results should be interpreted with caution. As this is a metadata approach, subgroup analysis required beyond the information presented in the included trials is limited. Furthermore, the present study was unable to account for other factors that might mitigate or aggravate the effect of semaglutide. For instance, the different doses of semaglutide in the treatment of T2DM or obesity might have further mitigated its effect. Additionally, the discontinuation data were self-reported, predisposing our analysis to potential reporting bias. Nonetheless, the granularity of our result can exemplify the real-life circumstance in which patients with obesity or T2DM have suboptimal responses to treatment with semaglutide due to external reasons.

Conclusion

Semaglutide represents a paradigm-shifting advancement in medicine with significant implications on the management of metabolic diseases. The efficacy in inducing sustained weight loss, enhancing glycemic control, and improving a spectrum of cardiometabolic risk factors is well established and continues to reshape clinical practice. However, while the therapeutic benefits are substantial, it is equally important to recognize that a significant proportion of patients may experience variable clinical responses or discontinue treatment due to AEs, even within the context of well-controlled RCTs. As such, a nuanced and comprehensive understanding of both the transformative potential and the clinical limitation of semaglutide is essential for guiding future research, optimizing patient selection, and maximizing long-term outcomes in the field of metabolic disease. Treating patients with suboptimal responses needs to be done with empathy while we wait for a better understanding of the potential biology resulting in suboptimal responses and the next generation of obesity medications.

Experimental model and study participant details

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We included randomized, double-blind or single-blind, parallel-group clinical trials that evaluated the efficacy and safety of semaglutide compared with placebo or standard of care in adult participants. Eligible trials employed a randomized allocation design with at least 1 treatment arm and reported prespecified clinical outcomes of interest.

In RCTs with T2DM, participants were enrolled if the following: Adults (≥18 years) with type 2 diabetes for at least180 days before screening, inadequately controlled (HbA1c 8·0–10 · 0%; 64–86 mmol/mol) on a stable dose of metformin therapy (≥1500 mg or maximum tolerated or effective dose) alone or in combination with a sulfonylurea (half or more of maximum approved dose according to local label or maximum tolerated or effective dose) for at least 90 days before screening were enrolled. In RCTs with overweight or obesity, participants were enrolled if the following: Male or female, age ≥18 years at the time of signing informed consent, body mass index (BMI) ≥30.0 kg/m2 or ≥27.0 kg/m2 with the presence of at least one of the following weight-related comorbidities (treated or untreated): hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, or cardiovascular disease, and history of at least one self-reported unsuccessful dietary effort to lose body weight. Informed consents were obtained before any trial-related activities.

The screening process identified 33 eligible studies, comprising a total of 21,023 participants who received the intervention. The average trial duration was 59 weeks. Given the single-arm design of the studies included, no comparator group was required. The mean participant age across studies was 55 years, with a balanced sex distribution (48% male and 52% female). All studies reported racial and ethnic composition; pooled estimates indicated that participants were predominantly White (74%), followed by African American (7%) and Asian(15%). Given the high proportion of Whites of the studies included, the results reported in our study might not be representative of all races or ethnicities.

Method details

Search strategy

A comprehensive query was conducted through Medline (Ovid) and Embase databases for articles related to RCTs of semaglutide without any time restriction. Briefly, the search strategy included keywords such as “randomized clinical/control trial”, “Semaglutide”, “Ozempic”, and “Wegovy”. The full search strategy is described in Table S1. A manual search on previous meta-analyses was also conducted to ensure the comprehensiveness of the data. All references were imported to EndNote 20 for duplicate removal, followed by manual screening for the selection of articles.

Study selection and extraction

Eligibility for inclusion was determined by eight authors (D.H.K., D.K., F.T., G.H., R.C., Z.X., E.J., N.T.) who screened articles independently from the initial sieve, with a third independent author involved in the resolution of disputes (C.N.). Inter-rater reliability was good (κ = 0.73). RCTs with double-blinded, placebo-controlled, or active-controlled, parallel-group design and studies evaluating adult patients (aged 18 and above) with metabolic diseases were included in our study. Observational studies, post-hoc studies not reporting outcomes of interest, case-control studies, reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, commentaries, and non-English language articles were excluded. As our study focuses primarily on the suboptimal effectiveness of semaglutide in metabolic diseases for which semaglutide is indicated, those who did not achieve the outcome of interest were extracted from the RCTs. Metabolic diseases were defined as the presence of overweight, obesity, T2DM, or MASH. We excluded studies that solely focus on medical conditions beyond those stated above, including those pertaining specifically focusing on heart failure, polyvascular disease, and peripheral artery disease, to maintain an analysis specific to the metabolic population directly akin to the mode of action of semaglutide. Duplicated studies that obtained results from the same databases were removed, whereas the latest or the most comprehensive article was retained. When multiple studies used the same sample, the data were extracted from the primary study.

Outcomes of interest and data extraction

The major binary outcomes of interest in this study were ≥5%, ≥10%, ≥15%, and ≥20% on total body weight (TBW) reduction, and ≥6.5% and ≥7% on hemoglobin A1c reduction. The latest Food and Drug Administration (FDA) draft guidance considers a drug effective for weight reduction if, after 1 year of treatment at the maintenance dosage, the difference in mean percentage weight reduction is at least 5%.56 Supporting the prior, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) suggests that responder definitions should include patients with at least 5% and 10% weight reduction.57 Therefore, a total body weight (TBW) reduction of less than 10% was defined as a suboptimal response for the 2.4 mg weekly dose of semaglutide, whereas a reduction of less than 5% was considered suboptimal for the 1.0 mg dose. For both dosages, a TBW reduction of 10% or more was regarded as an optimal response. Other relevant events include improvement in liver fibrosis in patients with biopsy-defined MASH and improvement in major kidney disease events in patients with T2DM and CKD. Other outcomes were related to the proportion of adverse events (AEs) and discontinuation rates. Supplementary outcome data were correspondingly extracted from ClinicalTrials.gov for the respective RCTs only when absent in the primary articles. Data were extracted by (D.H.K., D.K., F.T., G.H., R.C., Z.X., E.J., N.T.) in an independent and blinded manner. Data extraction included, but was not limited to, study characteristics (author, year, study design, and duration), patient characteristics (sample size, the underlying condition for which semaglutide was administered, and the different injectable semaglutide doses), and outcome measured at trial completion. In the control arm of the RCTs involving patients with T2DM, participants received varied regimens of antidiabetic medications and/or basal insulin and were usually balanced with randomization. Data on drug-related side effects in the included trials were primarily collected via self-reporting by participants and on discontinuation of treatment due to severe side effects reported by the examiners in the trials. All of these data were primarily extracted from the published version of each study and its respective supplementary material, and secondarily from ClinicalTrials.gov.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (R version 4.0.3) and STATA (version 17.0). The meta-analysis was conducted primarily using a single-arm analysis of proportions with a generalized linear mixed model with Clopper-Pearson intervals58,59 to estimate pooled proportions of suboptimal response among semaglutide-treated participants. The single-arm random-effects model provides descriptive estimates of the absolute frequency of suboptimal response, reflecting real-world variability across heterogeneous trial populations without implying comparative efficacy.60 To reduce heterogeneity, a sensitivity analysis was conducted based on the population (T2DM vs. non-T2DM), trial duration (≤52 weeks vs. > 52 weeks), and sample sizes. T2DM trials were standardized as a dose of 1 mg while non-T2DM trials were standardized as a dose of 2.4 mg. RCTs specific to the study of MASH, CKD, or obesity with T2DM population were excluded from the pooling, given the differences in dosing regimens and studied population and instead were systematically reported. Longitudinal analysis, where pooling of results was deemed unsuitable, was additionally systematically reported. A random-effects model was used for all analyses regardless of heterogeneity measures, as evidence has shown more robust effect estimates with random-effects models compared to fixed-effects models.61,62 Statistical heterogeneity was assessed via I2 and Cochran’s Q test values, where an I2 value of 25%, 50%, and 75% equated to small, moderate, and large amounts of heterogeneity, respectively,63 and a Cochran’s Q test with a p-value ≤0.10 was taken as statistically significant.64,65 Subsequently, with the intent of exploring heterogeneity, random effects meta-regression analysis was conducted to assess the effects of study duration, mean age, male/female distribution, baseline BMI, and baseline HbA1c on the primary outcomes. Measures for heterogeneity are limited in single-arm meta-analyses, as these depend heavily on sample size and event rate; however, these were calculated and included for completeness.63,64 Publication bias was assessed in T2DM trials (Semaglutide 1 mg) using funnel plots of study size against logit-transformed prevalence.66 Non-T2DM trials were not assessed, as this subgroup included fewer than 10 studies, consistent with Cochrane and Hunter et al.66,67

Risk of bias assessment

The quality and reliability of the included articles were graded using the Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias for randomized trials (ROB 2).68 The quality assessment was based on the following aspects: (1) risk of bias arising from the randomization process, (2) risk of bias due to deviations from the intended intervention, (3) missing outcome data, (4) risk of bias in the measurement of the outcome, (5) risk of bias in the selection of the reported result, and (6) overall risk of bias.
