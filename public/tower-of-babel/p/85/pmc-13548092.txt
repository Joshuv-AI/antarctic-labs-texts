Persistent expression of recipient-type ABH antigen following ABO-incompatible stem cell transplantation despite complete donor-type chimerism
Authors: Najla Haneefa Basheela, Aniruddha Hazra, Durba Biswas, Debapriya Basu, Suvro Sankha Datta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients undergoing ABO-incompatible hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (ABOi-HSCT) demonstrate a gradual transition from their original blood group to that of the donor in the post-transplantation period. However, ABH antigens present on non-hematopoietic tissues continue to express the recipient's antigens for life. These soluble ABH antigens may adsorb from the plasma onto the donor-derived red blood cells. This phenomenon not only complicates the interpretation of the recipient's blood typing and transfusion decision after transplantation but also impacts the assessment of the patient's clinical conditions, which includes raising concerns about transplant outcomes and the potential for relapse. Herein, we report two cases of persistent weak A antigen expression in recipients following minor ABOi-HSCT, despite achieving complete donor-type chimerism. In Case 1, a 35-year-old male, AB, RhD-positive, with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), was transplanted with an HLA-matched sibling carrying the B, RhD-positive blood group. In Case 2, a 4-year-old boy, A, RhD-positive, with B-cell ALL, was transplanted with an HLA-matched related group O, RhD-positive donor. Both patients exhibited persistent antigen A in forward typing more than 18 months post-HSCT, without any sequelae. These two cases underscore the necessity of performing detailed serological investigations to accurately determine blood type following ABOi-HSCT.

Introduction

Since its beginning, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) has brought intriguing challenges to the field of transfusion medicine. This process has generated immense interest in immunohematology, in addition to managing transfusion needs for patients undergoing HSCT, particularly in understanding the immune responses and compatibility issues that arise during and after ABO-incompatible (ABOi) transplantation1. ABO is the major human blood group system and involves three carbohydrate antigens (ABH). Individuals with type A, B, or AB express glycosyltransferase activity, converting the H antigen into A or B antigens, whereas group O individuals lack such activity. The ABH antigens are widely expressed in various body fluids and tissues but are absent on the hematopoietic progenitor cells. The solubility of ABH antigens is regulated by the secretor gene (FUT2) and the Lewis gene (FUT3), which are both located on chromosome 19p13.32. However, several alleles demonstrate enzymatic, ethnic, and geographic variations. In Japan and other Asian populations, there is a high prevalence of weak FUT2 and FUT3 alleles. These individuals have diminished or absent soluble ABH antigens in the plasma, although ABH expression is retained on tissues. Such variations might also contribute to the reported increase in severe hepatic and gastrointestinal graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) among Japanese ABOi- HSCT recipients3. Furthermore, the immunohematological challenges are greater in ABOi-HSCT, which could impact engraftment, survival, and other post-transplantation outcomes4. However, transplant-related research often overlooks the role of blood group genetics, leading to limited focus on blood group switching after transplantation5. Determining the blood group in the post-transplant period requires a standardized approach. Complete conversion of the recipient's blood type typically occurs within 90 to 110 days after ABOi-HSCT6. Following an ABOi-HSCT, the patient's blood group changes to that of the donor; however, the body fluids and tissues continue to express the recipient's ABH antigens for life7. These soluble ABH antigens present in the recipient's plasma can adsorb onto circulating blood cells. As a result, there may be persistent weak expression of recipient-type ABH antigens on red blood cells (RBCs), despite complete white cell donor chimerism8. This phenomenon not only complicates the interpretation of the recipient's blood typing and transfusion decision after transplantation but also impacts the assessment of the patient's clinical conditions, which includes raising concerns about transplant outcomes and the potential for relapse. Herein, we report two cases of persistent weak A antigen expression in recipients following minor ABOi-HSCT, despite achieving complete donor-type chimerism.

Case Presentation

Case 1: A 35-year-old Indian male diagnosed with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) was taken up for allo-HSCT due to the disease progression. He received unmanipulated peripheral blood stem cells (3.6 x 106 CD34+ cells/kg) from an HLA-matched sibling donor in April 2023. Conditioning for this HSCT was performed using fludarabine and total body irradiation. The patient's original blood group was AB, RhD positive, and the donor's blood group was B, RhD positive. Considering a case of minor ABO-mismatched HSCT, anti-A titration was performed on the donor sample using the tube method (anti-A=32)9, along with red cell phenotyping for minor blood group antigens as a part of pre-transplant investigations. This included testing for extended Rh antigens CcEe, Kell, Kidd, Duffy, and the MNS blood groups for both the donor and recipient, following the institutional protocol10. The blood grouping was performed using the column agglutination technique. Donor chimerism was confirmed by sex chromosome fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and found to be 100% on day+28 of transplantation. The patient received a total of five group B red cells and five group AB single donor platelets until day+70 of transplant. All blood products were leukoreduced and irradiated. According to the institutional policy, blood group typing was performed on day+90, which showed a weak reaction with monoclonal anti-A and strong agglutination with monoclonal anti-B (Figure 1A[a]). The patient did not show any signs of hemolysis, and his hemoglobin was normal. The direct antiglobulin test was negative, and further blood group testing indicated a complete conversion to the donor's minor blood group antigens. Results of blood group typing, which was monitored 6 months after HSCT, showed a persistent A-antigen, despite a 100% donor-type white cell chimerism. Even after 18 months of HSCT, the patient's forward typing showed the same reactions. Results of the patient's blood typing along with chimerism studies conducted at various time intervals post-transplantation are shown in Table 1. Unsurprisingly, no agglutination was observed in reverse typing, which is consistent with the recommendation for an ABO-mismatched transplantation6.

Case 2: A 4-year-old boy underwent allo-HSCT from an HLA-matched related donor in October 2023 for treatment-resistant B-cell precursor ALL. He received unmanipulated peripheral blood stem cells (2.3 x 106 CD34+ cells/kg) after myeloablative conditioning. The patient's original blood group was A, RhD positive; the donor's blood group was O, RhD positive. Donor chimerism was found 100% on day+28 of transplantation. The same testing protocol was followed as previously discussed. The patient did not require transfusion after day+50. However, blood group typing on day+90 showed a weak reaction of A antigens (Figure 1B[a]), although the minor antigens had fully converted to the donor type. From then on, the results of blood group typing showed a persistent presence of A-antigen (Table 1), despite a 100% donor-type chimerism.

For both cases, we suspected that the persistent weak expression of the recipient's type A antigen on red cells might be due to soluble blood group substances secreted by the patient's non-hematopoietic cells. Therefore, we conducted a series of immunohematological investigations following the methods outlined by Misaki Y et al.8. A healthy donor's O, RhD-negative RBCs were mixed with the patient's plasma and stored overnight at 4°C. This mixture was washed with saline the next day and divided into two test tubes. Monoclonal anti-A was added to one test tube, and monoclonal anti-B was added to another and stored overnight at 4°C. The next day, unadsorbed antibodies were removed by washing with saline. A heat elution was performed at 56°C for 10 minutes according to the AABB Technical Manual11. We tested eluates with A1 and B RBCs at both room temperature and 37°C. Results showed agglutinations with A1 red cells, while the reaction with B cells was negative (Figure 1A[b] and Figure 1B[b]), confirming the presence of soluble A substance in the recipient plasma.

Discussion

Both cases underscore the importance of correct serological interpretation of blood typing results after ABO-mismatched HSCT, which is an integral part of monitoring transplantation outcomes. Post-HSCT mixed-field ABO typing could be an early indicator of disease relapse in ABO-incompatible transplantations, where closer monitoring for disease recurrence may be warranted12. Traditionally, white blood cell chimerism is assessed using FISH or short tandem repeat (STR) analysis as a marker for donor engraftment following HSCT. However, transplant physicians often overlook blood group switching, another potential marker for a successful transplant in cases of ABOi-HSCT. The presence of a persistent blood group antigen is not detrimental; however, the impact for the patient may be substantial, including the fear that the transplantation was not successful, concern of relapse, and other anxieties that influence the quality of life13. Although this concern can be better addressed by performing flow cytometry analysis of red blood cells8, measuring A and B glycosyltransferase activity, or ABO genotyping14, these facilities are often unavailable in routine transfusion laboratories. Therefore, an adsorption-elution study remains an effective way for determining the weak expression of recipient-type ABH antigens on red cells after ABOi- HSCT15.

This case report has some limitations. First, the recipient's secretor status was not evaluated through saliva secretor studies, as conducting such studies is not part of our institutional protocol. Second, STR analysis, which is more sensitive than FISH and capable of detecting micro-chimerism after stem cell transplantation, was not performed for white cell chimerism in the recipient.

Both of our patients showed no signs of engraftment failure or relapse of the disease, with a follow-up time of more than 18 months after transplantation. The persistent weak expression of recipient-type A antigens, as described in this report, is not harmful. Although a persistent patient ABH antigen, despite complete donor-type white cell chimerism, has no impact on transplant outcomes, the psychological implications, such as the patient's anxiety, may be profound13. However, it is sometimes difficult to interpret the ABO blood typing results after ABOi-HSCT, especially when an ambiguous or weak reaction is observed in routine tests. Since the final report is important for clinical decision-making, transfusion support, and patient well-being, a detailed serological investigation should be performed before determining the precise blood type in the post-transplantation period. We also suggest keeping these patients on follow-up and assessing whether the persistence of donor-type ABH antigens is harmless or impacts some delayed event. Finally, it is crucial to counsel the recipient about the benign nature of this phenomenon, and transfusion laboratories should have a defined testing protocol in place for patients undergoing ABOi-HSCT.
