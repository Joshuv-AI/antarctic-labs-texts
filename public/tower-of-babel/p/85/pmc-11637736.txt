When is the use of intravenous immunoglobulin appropriate in immune thrombocytopenia?
Authors: Sydney Dubois, Richard Layese, Nicolas Limal, Laetitia Languille, Willy Kini‐Matondo, Matthieu Mahevas, Marc Michel, Etienne Audureau, Bertrand Godeau
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryIntravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) is the gold standard treatment for severe cases of immune thrombocytopenia (ITP). However, its cost, limited duration of efficacy and market supply tension have led French guidelines to reserve IVIg for ITP patients with formal contra‐indications to corticosteroids, with French bleeding score (‘Khellaf score’) > 8, and corticosteroid‐resistant patients either with Khellaf score ≤ 8 or in preparation for an invasive procedure or during pregnancy. We studied the prescribing practices of IVIg for ITP in real‐life conditions and assessed their compliance with French guidelines. A monocentric retrospective study was conducted between 2016 and 2020 among 114 patients hospitalized in our unit, for a total of 208 IVIg treatments. In 37% of cases, the Khellaf score was >8, validating IVIg prescription according to French guidelines. In the remaining cases, reasons noted for use of IVIg included corticosteroid resistance (33.7%), preparation for an invasive procedure (8.5%), context of pregnancy (6.6%) and contra‐indication to corticosteroids (3.3%). After analysis, IVIg prescription was considered valid according to current French guidelines in 84.4% of cases. Non‐compliant IVIg prescription was more frequent in younger patients (p = 0.027). Concomitant anti‐coagulation was also noted as an argument for IVIg prescription outside of the current French guidelines.Keywords: immune thrombocytopenia, intravenous immunoglobulin

INTRODUCTION

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an auto‐immune disorder characterized by an isolated thrombocytopenia (platelets <100 × 109/L) in the absence of other aetiologies. 1 The main issue at the heart of ITP patient management is an increased bleeding risk, which guides treatment indications. Corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) are the cornerstone of first‐line treatment in ITP. There is a consensus in international guidelines that IVIg should be reserved for the most severe forms and in preparation for surgery in patients resistant to corticosteroids. 2 , 3 It has been shown that in emergency situations with a platelet count <20 × 109/L, response to IVIg is faster, more important and more frequent than that obtained with corticosteroids. 4 However, even if all guidelines clearly place IVIg as the reference treatment in severe ITP, there are no clear recommendations as to its indications and the assessment of bleeding severity is most often left to the discretion of the physician, with no consensual bleeding score. 2 , 3 A widely accepted and validated bleeding measurement tool specific to adult ITP is lacking, with the literature identifying at least 12 different definitions of ‘severe bleeding’. 5 , 6 Hence, the classification of bleeding severity in patients with ITP remains unstandardized and is a challenge.

In 2005, we developed the Khellaf bleeding score to quantify haemorrhage in adults with ITP. Bleeding severity was graded from 0 to 19 based on an evaluation of the different bleeds (mucosal, cutaneous and organ) and integrating the patient's age to guide the indication of IVIg according to the severity of the score. 7 A cutoff was decided at 8, with patients with a Khellaf score ≤ 8 being treated with steroids alone and patients with a Khellaf score > 8 being treated with IVIg in combination with steroids. This strategy was prospectively applied for the management of 60 consecutive adult patients with ITP and a platelet count ≤20 × 109/L. 7 Based on this strategy, IVIg was required in only 50% of patients and no life‐threatening bleeding occurred in patients treated with steroids alone, showing the relevance of the Khellaf score and its capacity to spare IVIg use. This score is now widely used in France and has been adopted by other countries. 8 , 9 However, some authors have questioned its validity, 2 arguing that its bleeding assessment remains subjective, and a more elaborate standardized international score has been developed (‘SMOG score’) 10 with the aim of obtaining a more objective bleeding assessment. Unfortunately, the SMOG score is quite complex and not suitable for use in real‐life daily practice and, to our knowledge, has not been included in a clinical study or any of the recently published national or international guidelines.

In France, since 2009, ITP treatment is based on national guidelines, updated in 2017. 11 French guidelines indicate that IVIg should be reserved for patients with a Khellaf score > 8. 7 Outside of this scenario and in accordance with international consensus, IVIg is recommended only for ITP patients with a formal contra‐indication to corticosteroids, and corticosteroid‐resistant patients in preparation for either an invasive procedure or delivery in the case of pregnant women. 11 French health authorities have recently reiterated the need to comply with French guidelines, given the supply difficulties encountered with IVIg. 12

Despite these strict guidelines, IVIg was administered in approximately half of patients hospitalized for ITP, 13 suggesting that some IVIg uses were not compliant with national guidelines. Of note, in France, IVIg prescription is only restricted to hospital use, not to certain medical specialities.

Our objectives were to describe the prescribing practices of IVIg in ITP, to assess their compliance with national guidelines and to identify the potential factors associated with prescriptions outside recommendations. To do so, we performed a real‐life, retrospective monocentric pilot study of patients hospitalized for ITP who had received IVIg in this indication between 2016 and 2020 in the internal medicine unit of the Henri Mondor Hospital in Créteil, France.

METHODS

Patient selection

We retrospectively included patients older than 18 years of age, hospitalized between 2016 and 2020 for ITP in the Internal Medicine Department of the Henri Mondor Hospital in Créteil, France. Data were gathered from the Health Data Warehouse of the Assistance Publique–Hôpitaux de Paris (AP‐HP), which collects data from inpatients and outpatients of all AP‐HP hospitals (including Henri Mondor hospital) on socio‐demographics, clinical summaries, biological data, medication prescriptions and medical charts with diagnoses and interventions related to the hospital stays, and vital status at hospital discharge.

In this monocentric study, patients hospitalized for ITP were identified by the presence of a D693 ICD‐10 code (ITP) as a principal or significantly related diagnosis. Text contents of medical reports and structured medical prescriptions were then analysed in order to identify IVIg prescriptions among these patients. The search terms ‘IVIg’, ‘immunoglobulin’ and brand names of IVIg were flagged by text search in hospitalization reports. IVIg brands and corresponding international non‐proprietary name were further detected within structured medical prescriptions. Information from AP‐HP data warehouse was finally cross‐referenced with data from the hospital pharmacy, allowing identification of patients who actually were prescribed IVIg for ITP over the period studied.

To be included in this study, IVIg prescription had to be in regard to an acute event: maintenance IVIg infusions for multirefractory ITP patients were excluded.

Collected data

Hospital reports were manually checked to confirm IVIg prescription during the study period. Khellaf bleeding score noted in the report or the clinical elements necessary for its calculation were collected.

IVIg indications were collected and IVIG treatments were classified by indication:

In the case of Khellaf bleeding score > 8, IVIg use was considered compliant with current French guidelines.

In the case of Khellaf bleeding score > 8, IVIg use was considered compliant with current French guidelines.

In the case of Khellaf bleeding score ≤ 8, IVIg use was considered in accordance with French guidelines for patients with a formal contra‐indication to corticosteroids and for corticosteroid‐resistant patients in preparation for an invasive procedure or delivery in corticosteroid‐resistant pregnant women. The absence of a clearly explicit reason to use IVIg was also noted and in this case, IVIg use was considered as ‘non‐compliant’ with the guidelines.

In the case of Khellaf bleeding score ≤ 8, IVIg use was considered in accordance with French guidelines for patients with a formal contra‐indication to corticosteroids and for corticosteroid‐resistant patients in preparation for an invasive procedure or delivery in corticosteroid‐resistant pregnant women. The absence of a clearly explicit reason to use IVIg was also noted and in this case, IVIg use was considered as ‘non‐compliant’ with the guidelines.

Statistical analyses

Variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range] for continuous variables and number of patients (percentage) for categorical variables. Univariate analysis to assess associations between patients' characteristics and compliance with national guidelines was performed using χ 2 or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, and Student t‐tests or Mann–Whitney tests for continuous variables, as appropriate, estimating crude odds ratios (OR) along with their 95% confidence intervals by unadjusted logistic regression. Variables associated at the p < 0.2 level in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate logistic regression analysis. Analyses were performed at the two‐tailed p < 0.05 level, using R v4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Prism software (GraphPad Software, version 5.00.288).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Pharmacy data identified 135 patients having effectively received IVIg for ITP between 2016 and 2020 in our department. Our multicriteria automated search successfully identified 118 (87%) patients, with no false positives. Among them, one patient was removed as he had received IVIg for severe combined immunodeficiency although he was also known to have ITP. Three additional patients were removed as they had received IVIg as maintenance treatment for multirefractory ITP, without an acute event leading to IVIg prescription. A total of 114 patients, accounting for 208 IVIg courses, were therefore included. Flowchart of patient selection is shown in Figure 1. Main patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. Briefly, a slight majority (54%) of patients were female, with a median age of 55 years old. An anti‐platelet or anti‐coagulant therapy was noted in 32.5% of patients. The cohort was fairly evenly split among newly diagnosed ITP (less than 3 months of duration) and chronic ITP (more than 1 year of duration), with a minority of persistent ITP. The cohort included heavily pretreated patients, with a median number of two ITP therapies prior to the IVIg prescription studied (min 0 − max 17). At time of IVIg prescription, 47% of patients had an ongoing ITP treatment. Median platelet levels at admission, prior to IVIg prescription, were 6 × 109/L (min 1 × 109/L − max 70 × 109/L), and 105 of 114 patients had platelet levels <20 × 109/L.

Indications of IVIg

Figure 3 details IVIg indications. As noted above, Khellaf bleeding score was >8 in 37.5% of cases, validating IVIg prescription according to current French guidelines. When Khellaf score was ≤8, known steroid resistance was the reason for IVIg use for 33.7% of these cases. Preparation for an invasive procedure, preparation for childbirth and a contra‐indication to corticosteroids were noted as the reasons for IVIg use in, respectively, 8.5%, 6.6% and 3.3% of these cases. We were not able to identify a clearly stated reason for IVIg use in 6.6% of cases and IVIg use was then considered as non‐compliant. Of note, in 2.4% of these cases, the presence of concomitant anti‐coagulant therapy was explicitly highlighted as the reason for IVIg use, which was still considered non‐compliant. Finally, IVIg was prescribed as a therapeutic test in 1.4% of cases (non‐compliant).

Compliance with national guidelines

French guidelines allow IVIg prescription if preparing for an invasive procedure or childbirth but only in corticosteroid‐resistant cases. Among the 32 patients in whom IVIg was infused for these reasons, 8 patients were not known for steroid resistance, leading us to flag these IVIg treatments as non‐compliant (Figure 4). Likewise, among the seven patients in whom IVIg was infused due to a contra‐indication to corticosteroids, three were not deemed to present formal contra‐indications (Figure 4): two presented with SARS‐Cov‐2 infections at the beginning of the pandemic and one required a lymph node biopsy for suspicion of lymphoma. As such, these IVIg prescriptions were also flagged as non‐compliant.

Adding to these cases those where no motive was noted, as well as those prescribed due to concomitant anti‐coagulant therapy or as a therapeutic test, we flagged a total of 33 IVIg prescriptions (15.9%) in our study as non‐compliant with current national guidelines.

In several patients under the age of 65, whose age is not taken into account in the calculation of the Khellaf score, but with a score close to 8, physicians reported using IVIg because they were concerned about the risk of a serious bleeding event, suggesting that young age could be a factor associated with guideline non‐compliance. We tested this hypothesis by performing mixed‐effects logistic regression modelling, which showed a trend towards younger age in patients for whom IVIg prescription was non‐compliant (Table 2). Considering patients older than 65, 27 IVIg treatments in our study were validated in this subgroup based on a Khellaf score > 8. When the Khellaf score was recalculated without taking age into consideration for these patients, in 15 cases (55.5%), the score dropped to ≤8, highlighting the influence of age in the score.

DISCUSSION

In our study, IVIg prescription was compliant with national guidelines in 84% of cases. This result shows good compliance with national guidelines, demonstrating that an IVIg prescription based on a bleeding score can be widely followed by clinicians. There remains room for improvement notably for patients in preparation for invasive procedures or during pregnancy, where we recommend testing corticosteroid sensitivity if not known before prescribing IVIg, if Khellaf score is ≤8. In order to reduce unnecessary IVIg prescription in these cases, we recommend that all patients with a new ITP diagnosis be tested for corticosteroid sensitivity, so that this information can be clearly noted in their medical file. An ITP patient card with mention of corticosteroid sensitivity or resistance could be distributed and this would warrant further efforts in partnership with patient outreach programmes. Of note, the prescribing IVIg recommendations studied in this paper apply exclusively to France; therefore, the conclusions cannot be readily extrapolated to other countries.

Our study was not designed to examine the details of IVIg use, including dose, scheduling and adaptation of dose according to chronic conditions. French guidelines clearly indicate the following IVIg prescription dosages: 1 g/kg/day on day 1, and renewed on day 3 if bleeding symptoms persist or platelet levels remain low. A renewal at day 2 is only indicated in case of immediate life‐threatening bleeding. Dosage must be adapted to 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days with daily surveillance of renal function in case of potential poor tolerance, such as in case of heart or renal failure, mainly in elderly patients. We were not able to verify concomitant corticosteroid prescription either in our study. Therefore, a future study to gather these data and verify complete compliance is warranted.

Despite good adherence to the French guidelines highlighted by this study, there remains room for progress. Regarding the Khellaf bleeding score, one of its main limitations is the arbitrary threshold of 8 for IVIg prescription. While the threshold was indeed selected arbitrarily in the original article, the score and its threshold were validated prospectively and enabled IVIg prescription reduction, while maintaining overall prognosis. 7

The other limit of the Khellaf bleeding score is the weight granted to age, with 2 points for patients older than 65 and 5 points for patients older than 75. As such, younger patients must present with more severe bleeding symptoms in order to obtain a Khellaf bleeding score > 8 and receive IVIg. Our study showed physicians' reticence in certain cases to deny younger patients IVIg treatment when bleeding symptoms were significant but not enough for a Khellaf score > 8, leading to a trend towards younger age among patients for whom IVIg indication was non‐compliant with guidelines. Furthermore, more than half of patients older than 65 for whom IVIg treatment was initially motivated by a Khellaf score > 8 in our study ended up with a Khellaf score ≤ 8 when age was removed from the equation, suggesting that the current Khellaf score might lead to overexposure to IVIg in the elderly population.

The choice to include age in the Khellaf bleeding score was initially based on studies having identified age as a risk factor for severe bleeding in ITP. 14 Indeed, Cortelazzo et al. reported an OR of 2.8 for severe bleeding in patients between 40 and 60 years old versus 28.9 in patients over 60 years old. Other studies tended to support this observation, such as a study performed by Guthrie et al., which reported a 30% incidence of severe bleeding among their cohort of elderly patients, significantly higher than in the general ITP population. 15 However, these studies are largely retrospective, based on small cohorts, and most importantly do not take into account other potentially confounding factors in this elderly population, such as concomitant medication inducing an increased bleeding risk. Our French cooperative research group (CERECAI) studied bleeding episodes among 302 ITP patients according to multiple factors including platelet levels <20 G/L, age ≥ 80 years, male gender, Charlson comorbidity index ≥ 1, high blood pressure, secondary ITP and medication by non‐steroid anti‐inflammatory drugs, anti‐platelet drugs, anti‐coagulant or serotonin reuptake inhibitors. 16 Within this cohort, age ≥ 80 years was indeed linked to increased severe bleeding risk in univariate analysis; however, multivariate analysis showed that only platelet levels <20 G/L and anti‐coagulant drugs were statistically significantly linked to increased severe bleeding risk. In our study, the presence of concomitant anti‐coagulant therapy was indeed explicitly mentioned in several cases as the reason for IVIg prescription despite a Khellaf score ≤ 8, and might also have influenced physicians' decision in other non‐compliant prescriptions, without being explicitly mentioned in the hospital report. In view of the results of the CERECAI study, which showed that anti‐coagulant use is the factor most strongly associated with the occurrence of severe bleeding in ITP, this factor should probably be taken into account when deciding to use IVIg and should probably be included in a modified Khellaf score. In contrast, the weight attributed to age should probably be less important, and only very elderly patients at greater risk of bleeding should receive more IVIg.

Given the results of this pilot study, we propose a modified Khellaf score, which would maintain the same point system for intensity of the different bleeds but would keep 2 points only for patients over 75 years of age, and would add a category for concomitant anti‐coagulant treatment (0 points if ‘no’, 5 points if ‘yes’). The threshold for IVIg treatment would remain at 8. Of course, to be of value, this modified Khellaf score would have to be tested prospectively against the current Khellaf score in order to confirm whether it would reduce IVIg use without reducing overall prognosis.

Other bleeding scores do already exist for adult ITP, such as the ITP Bleeding Scale 17 or the ITP‐BAT (Bleeding Assessment Tool). 10 However, they do not take concomitant anti‐coagulant treatment into account either and most importantly, no score other than the Khellaf bleeding score has prospectively established a threshold for IVIg prescription. 7 There is an urgent need for more in‐depth work on the development and validation of bleeding scores in ITP, which can be used worldwide, and should facilitate the application of international guidelines on the use of IVIg in ITP.

The limitation of our study lies in its retrospective nature, which does not allow us to make a detailed assessment of the reasons that led the prescriber not to follow the French recommendations on the use of IVIg, other than what is clearly written within the hospitalization report. Furthermore, the fact that the study was carried out in the French reference centre, which initially published the Khellaf score and which published the French guidelines for the use of IVIg in ITP, represents a selection bias that could potentially overestimate adherence to guidelines and we have consequently planned a further study to analyse whether these recommendations are correctly followed in less expert groups in this field. The third limitation of our study is the Khellaf score itself, which consists of items that were arbitrarily defined. This study as well as others conducted by our group suggest that the score probably needs to be modified, and, in particular, that anti‐coagulants should be included in the score and that the weight given to age should, on the contrary, be reduced. Ideally, this score should be defined internationally and validated prospectively. Finally, our main objective in this study was to analyse the overprescription of IVIg. Our data therefore do not allow us to determine the percentage of patients with a Khellaf score > 8 who did not receive IVIg but this question seems less crucial to us.

In conclusion, our study shows excellent overall compliance of IVIg use in ITP with French guidelines, with further studies to be performed to extend the study to non‐expert centres. Nevertheless, we highlighted room for improvement, with a probable need to include concomitant anti‐coagulant treatment in IVIg prescription algorithms and, on the other hand, greatly reduce the weight attributed to age in this indication. The conclusions from this retrospective study warrant further, prospective, studies in order to best adjust the Khellaf bleeding score to treat ITP patients efficiently, while preserving precious IVIg products.
