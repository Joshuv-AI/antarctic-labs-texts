National analysis of the incidence and prognostic importance of nodal metastases in atypical lung carcinoid tumors
Authors: Adam B. Sauer, Ntemena Kapula, Lillian Tsai, Douglas Z. Liou, Irmina A. Elliott, Mark F. Berry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the relationship of atypical lung carcinoid (AC) tumor size, nodal involvement, and survival.MethodsPatients recorded in the National Cancer Database who underwent lobectomy or sublobar resection for lung AC between 2006 and 2022 were included. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to identify independent factors associated with lymph node involvement. Survival was assessed using the Cox proportional hazard model and Kaplan-Meier method.ResultsA total of 1927 patients met the study’s inclusion criteria. The incidence of overall node positivity was 33.4%. The incidence of positive pathologic nodes in patients who were clinically considered node-negative was 25.2%. Lymph node involvement was more likely with increasing tumor size, ranging from a rate of 19.1% in the subset of patients with subcentimeter-sized tumors to 45.9% with tumors larger than 7 cm. Increasing tumor size was associated with a higher risk of nodal involvement in a multivariable analysis. Positive nodes were associated with significantly worse survival in both Kaplan-Meier analysis (5-year survival, 71.7% vs 88.5%; P < .001) and multivariable analysis (hazard ratio, 1.77; 95% confidence interval, 1.41-2.23; P < .001).ConclusionsNodal involvement is a critical factor associated with survival for AC tumors of the lung and occurs at a high rate, even when tumors are very small. Lymph node evaluation at the time of AC resection should be considered regardless of tumor size to most accurately stage patients.

Central Message

Roughly 1 in 3 atypical carcinoid patients have nodal involvement, including approximately 1 in 5 with subcentimeter tumors. Regardless of tumor size, careful pathologic node evaluation is recommended for accurate staging.

Perspective

Surgeons should always consider pathologic lymph node evaluation when resecting atypical lung carcinoids to optimally stage patients, as lymph node metastases are common even in small or cN0 tumors.

Lung neuroendocrine tumors arising from the epithelium of the bronchopulmonary tree account for 20% of all lung cancers.1 They are separated into 4 groups by their histologic characteristics: typical carcinoid (TC), atypical carcinoid (AC), large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, and small cell lung carcinoma. The current standard of care for ACs, which are intermediate-grade neuroendocrine tumors with significantly worse 5-year survival compared to TCs,2,3 is surgical resection with lymph node evaluation.4 While studies of non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) have shown that less extensive surgery in the forms of sublobar resection and lymph node sampling rather than dissection does not compromise survival for smaller tumors,5,6 it remains unclear whether a similar logic can be applied to ACs, for which large cohort studies are somewhat limited. The present study was undertaken to quantify the relationship between AC tumor size, the incidence of nodal involvement, and survival.

Methods

The National Cancer Database (NCDB) is a collaboration between the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society that collects data from more than 1500 facilities in North America on approximately 70% of newly diagnosed cancers in the United States and contains more than 30 million records. This study used only deidentified data from the NCDB and thus was considered exempt by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board. Patient consent was not required.

Patient selection and cohort derivation are summarized in Figure E1. Patients included in the study were those who underwent lobectomy or sublobar resection for AC tumors between 2006 and 2022. Patients without a pathologic node examination were excluded, to reduce the chance of including patients with false-negative nodal involvement. Patients undergoing pneumonectomy also were excluded, given the inherently higher risk of morbidity and mortality. Also excluded were patients without documentation of exact tumor size or tumor stage, patients with prior malignancy, patients with distant metastases on clinical staging, patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiation, and patients without adequate survival data.

Baseline demographic, clinical, and tumor-specific characteristics were compared between node-negative and node-positive patients using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. The Fisher exact test was used for categorical variables with <5 outcomes. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to assess the following variables for independent association of lymph node involvement: age, sex, race, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), income level, education level, and tumor size (the primary variable of interest). Variables included in the model were selected a priori based on previous studies showing that these characteristics can influence not only treatment overall but also specifically the extent of surgery performed for cancer. Patients who had missing data for any of these variables (n = 318; 16.5%) were excluded from this regression analysis, with missing data attributable predominately to income and education level.

Overall survival was compared over time between negative and positive node status using the Kaplan-Meier method. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was used to identify variables associated with survival. Variables assessed included age, sex, node status, CCI, tumor size, margin status, extent of surgical resection, and adjuvant radiation or chemotherapy. These variables were selected a priori based on previous studies that established relationships between these characteristics and survival. Patients who had missing data for any of these variables (n = 6; 0.3%) were excluded from this Cox analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors for all covariates included in the logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models, with values <5 considered indicative of acceptable collinearity. A sensitivity analysis was performed excluding patients with positive margins and those who received adjuvant therapy, to assess potential confounding by these factors. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed including only patients with >3 or >6 lymph nodes examined.

Analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A 2-sided P value <.05 was considered significant. The continuous variables of age and number of lymph nodes examined are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR), while tumor size and hospital length of stay are reported as mean with standard deviation.

The majority of patients underwent lobectomy (84%), and the median tumor size was 2.1 cm. Six hundred forty-three patients (33.4%) had positive lymph nodes. The median number of lymph nodes examined per patient in the entire cohort was 9, and more nodes were examined in node-positive patients compared to node-negative patients (median, 11 [IQR, 7-17] vs 8 [IQR, 4-13]; P < .001). The incidence of positive nodes increased with the number of nodes examined, ranging from 16% in patients with 1 to 3 nodes examined to 46% in those with >15 nodes examined (P < .001) (Table 1).

Lymph node positivity increased with tumor size, although the relationship was not strictly monotonic (Table 1). Even subcentimeter AC tumors were associated with a node positivity rate of 19.1%, with the largest tumors having a node positivity rate of 45.9%. Increasing tumor size was independently associated with nodal involvement (Table 2). Other factors independently associated with worse survival were age (HR, 1.56 per decade; 95% CI, 1.43-1.71; P < .001) and CCI (CCI 1: HR, 1.32, P = .017; CCI 2+: HR, 1.73, P < .001). We also evaluated whether the relationship between positive lymph node status and survival differed by tumor size categories; no statistically significant interactions were observed, and inclusion of the interaction terms did not meaningfully alter the HRs for the main effects. Discussion

This study using a large nationwide cancer database quantifies the relationship between AC tumor size, lymph node status, and survival. Increasing tumor size was associated with a higher risk of nodal involvement. Nodal involvement was associated with worse 5-year survival for patients, while size alone was not independently associated with survival. Notably, the rate of node positivity in the smallest subset of AC tumors was high, at 19.1%. The rates of nodal upstaging also were high, as 25.2% of patients labeled node-negative on clinical evaluation were node-positive on pathologic evaluation, and 52.0% of cN1 patients were pN2 following surgery.

Our findings build on a previously published systematic review identifying lymph node status and histologic subtype as the dominant prognostic factors in pulmonary carcinoid tumors and advocating for a staging system distinct from that used for NSCLC.7 This study also builds on a previous Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database8 study, as well as other NCDB studies that reported rates of lymph node metastases for AC tumors9,10 similar to the overall 33.4% node positivity observed in this study. In this context, our study contributes the largest cohort to date focused specifically on AC tumors. It provides more detailed data than previously available on the impact of tumor size on lymph node involvement and documents that lymph node involvement has a dominant impact over tumor size on prognosis for AC tumors, which potentially could support consideration of modifying the staging system used for these tumors.

These findings suggest a pathologic lymph node evaluation at the time of AC resection will most accurately establish stage to provide patients with accurate information on their prognosis. Although the study dataset cannot be used to formally define what that pathologic nodal evaluation optimally would entail, one reasonable approach certainly could be to remove all nodes in any stations with any suspicious findings based on size or hypermetabolic activity on preoperative imaging, as well as to remove level 11 nodes at the bronchial takeoff for the lobe for which the tumor is located and to sample 3 mediastinal lymph node stations overall. Our results also support maintenance of current guidelines regarding AC tumor management, even for small tumors, in terms of surgical resection and lymph node evaluation. This stands in contrast to more recent evidence regarding other types of NSCLC histology, for which both less extensive parenchymal resection and less extensive lymph node evaluation have been shown to not compromise outcomes.5,6,11, 12, 13, 14, 15

As the ability to perform thorough lymph node evaluation can be limited in nonanatomic resections, our study supports continued consideration of anatomic resection with thorough lymph node evaluation for ACs, given the likely presence of lymph node metastases in even the smallest subset of tumors and the previously reported high frequency of nodal upstaging when comparing clinical to pathologic nodal status.9,14 Indeed, previous work from this group demonstrated the inferior ability of positron emission tomography (PET) imaging to identify positive nodes in carcinoid tumors compared to NSCLC.16 Our results are in concordance with these data, with overall upstaging rates approaching 30%, further emphasizing the critical importance of thorough nodal evaluation of these tumors to most accurately stage patients.

Given the substantial potential for positive lymph nodes and upstaging in ACs, distinguishing between TCs and ACs can be critically important when surgeons are considering the extent of resection and lymph node evaluation. This distinction is not always easily obtained prior to resection, however. Guidelines from the World Health Organization, the North American Neuroendocrine Tumor Society, the European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society, and the Commonwealth Neuroendocrine Tumor Research Collaboration emphasize that TC and AC tumors cannot be reliably distinguished on preoperative specimens.4,17,18 A recent study by Moonen and colleagues19 showed that >50% of patients had a discordance between their preoperative diagnosis and final pathology. Similarly, intraoperative analysis of frozen sections has been shown to have a poor ability to correctly diagnose ACs and TCs.20 This uncertainty may influence operative decision making in patients without a clearly defined histology. Consideration of the overall clinical presentation and the potential for atypical histology may support a more thorough lymph node evaluation at the time of resection when preoperative biopsy does not strongly support TC, to reduce the possibility of performing an inadequate nodal assessment if final pathology shows atypical histology.

It should be noted that several modalities have shown a promising ability to differentiate between carcinoid types and predict prognosis. Dual-tracer PET/computed tomography (CT) combining 68Ga-DOTA peptides and 18F-FDG has shown excellent discriminatory ability between TCs and ACs.21 In recent years, several groups have reported a significantly higher SUVmax ratio of 68Ga-DOTA peptide to 18F-FDG in TCs compared to ACs.22,23 The Ki-67 immunohistochemical proliferation index has been explored as a potential way to differentiate tumors, and several studies have demonstrated only modest diagnostic accuracy for Ki-67, which remains inferior to traditional histopathologic criteria, such as mitotic count and necrosis.24 Both the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and international guidelines state that while it may be helpful in distinguishing small cell lung cancer from carcinoid tumors, Ki-67 cannot reliably distinguish between ACs and TCs.18,25 Therefore, surgeons should always consider the possibility of ultimately finding atypical histology when planning resection of a tumor shown or suspected to be a carcinoid tumor.

The results of our current study are also important to consider given the emerging use of endobronchial techniques, such as parenchymal-sparing treatment of pulmonary carcinoid tumors.26 Currently, these therapies are considered only in the setting of unacceptable surgical risk owing to medical comorbidities or for palliation of patients with distant metastatic disease.18 For AC tumors in particular, endobronchial treatment probably should be avoided, given the high rate of lymph node positivity and associated poorer survival. Surgical resection and lymph node evaluation remain the mainstays of treatment for patients with resectable disease and acceptable bronchopulmonary status.27,28

This study is subject to multiple limitations. Given its retrospective nature, there is potential for selection bias and confounding. Surgeons may be making decisions based on factors not controlled for in the study, which could result in a more limited or extensive resection based on tumor location, imaging findings, age, or intraoperative circumstances. The extent and quality of lymph node evaluation also are not fully captured in the NCDB, although we excluded patients without available pathologic node data, to ensure that only patients with some lymph node evaluation were considered. Variability in nodal assessment across patients suggests that some nodal metastases may have gone undetected and some patients were understaged in cases where surgeons performed limited sampling rather than a thorough dissection. Consequently, our observed rates of nodal positivity may underestimate the true burden of disease, which perhaps further highlights the potential importance of adequate lymph node evaluation. An additional limitation is that methods of clinically staging nodes changed over the course of the study, with patients in the earlier years of the study likely evaluated with CT scans, conventional PET scans, and possibly nodal sampling via mediastinoscopy. The use of DOTA-TATE PET scans, approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2016, as well as the likely expanded use of endobronchial ultrasound to evaluate and sample hilar and mediastinal nodes, could have led to more accurate nodal staging in the later years of the study.

Despite these potential limitations, our study demonstrates the importance of thorough nodal evaluation for AC given the high rate of lymph node positivity and the negative impact on survival. Strengths of our study include the NCDB capturing around 70% of newly diagnosed cases in the United States and the large sample size, representing the largest study of AC patients reported to date, to our knowledge. Our cohort is specific to AC tumors rather than considering carcinoid tumors in general, which may allow more detailed understanding of the importance of histology on management. Clinicians can use the data provided in this study to help guide AC patient care with easily accessible and realistic estimates of lymph node involvement. Our data also include critical nodal upstaging information specific to the size of AC tumors, which may provide more clinically relevant data to guide surgical decision making. Although the data do not explicitly direct the extent of lymph node resection that should be performed, our study does indicate that surgeons should carefully plan lymph node evaluation when performing AC resection.

Conclusions

This large NCDB study of patients with AC tumors demonstrates that nodal positivity increases with tumor size and occurs in 33.4% of patients overall, including in 25.2% of those with clinical stage N0. Node positivity was independently associated with worse overall survival, while tumor size alone was not independently associated with survival. These findings underscore that performing pathologic nodal evaluation when resecting AC tumors, regardless of tumor size, likely will allow for optimal staging.
