The ChinaSTAD Chinese Registry in Patients with Moderate-To-Severe Atopic Dermatitis Not Controlled by Topical Therapy: Baseline Characteristics, Disease Burden and Treatment
Authors: Ping Liu, Bingjiang Lin, Yanna Shen, Yonghong Lu, Jingyi Li, Dong Lv, Xu Yao, Xiaoyong Man, Hong Fang, Guan Jiang, Nan Yu, Dongmei Liu, Siyan Zhan, Jianzhong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn China, real-world evidence on the burden and management of moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD) is needed. The ChinaSTAD study aimed to characterise the treatment and disease and health economic burden of patients with moderate-to-severe AD uncontrolled on topical therapy in China.MethodsThe ChinaSTAD national prospective registry study enrolled individuals aged ≥ 12 years with AD, with a SCORing Atopic Dermatitis (SCORAD) score of ≥ 25, at participating hospitals. At baseline (enrolment visit), clinical status (SCORAD, Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure [POEM], Peak Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale [PP-NRS], Atopic Dermatitis Control Tool [ADCT]), health-related quality of life (Dermatology Life Quality Index [DLQI] and Children’s DLQI [CDLQI]), AD treatment, and healthcare resource use in the previous year, were evaluated. Costs were estimated from the patient’s perspective. Baseline visit data are reported here for the subgroup of patients who had not received prior systemic AD therapy continuously up to the point of study enrolment. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation unless stated otherwise.ResultsOf 2962 patients enrolled at data cut-off (31 October 2022), 2550 were included in this analysis (aged 40.2 ± 21.0 years). The baseline SCORAD was 54.4 ± 15.7; 85.6% of patients had uncontrolled AD (ADCT score ≥ 7), and DLQI and CDLQI scores were 10.9 ± 7.1 and 9.9 ± 6.3, respectively. At enrolment, patients were being treated with antihistamines (62.1% of patients), topical corticosteroids (61.3%) and topical moisturising emollients (31.3%). In the year prior, 37.5% of patients had one to three outpatient visits (cost per visit 662.3 ± 1563.4 renminbi (RMB) and 12.9% had one to three hospital admissions (cost per hospitalisation 822.4 ± 3149.2 RMB).ConclusionsPatients in China aged ≥ 12 years with moderate-to-severe AD uncontrolled on topical therapy experience considerable disease burden.Clinicaltrial.gov identifierNCT05023668.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-025-01540-w.Keywords: Dermatitis, atopic; Disease burden; Quality of life; Real-world; Registries; Treatment

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?The prevalence of atopic dermatitis is increasing in China, but real-world data on disease burden, treatments, healthcare resource use and costs are lacking.The ChinaSTAD study aimed to characterise the treatment and disease and health economic burden of moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis in adolescent and adult patients in China.What was learned from this study?Baseline ChinaSTAD registry results showed substantial disease burden in patients with uncontrolled moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis, despite treatment with topical therapy.These initial results for real-world treatment practices in the management of atopic dermatitis in China suggest that there are still unmet needs to be addressed.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic type 2 inflammatory systemic disease predominantly affecting the skin and is associated with allergic comorbidities, such as allergic asthma, food allergies and allergic rhinoconjunctivitis [1, 2]. In China, the estimated prevalence of AD was 35.6 million individuals in 2019, representing an increase in prevalence of 26% since 1990 [3].

AD has a profoundly negative effect on a patient’s quality of life (QoL) [1, 4, 5], attributed to the chronic and relapsing nature of the signs and symptoms of the disease (eczema, severe pruritus) and co-existing type 2 inflammatory diseases (e.g. asthma, allergic rhinitis) [1]. Sleep deficits and itching are particularly burdensome in both children and adults [4, 5], with adults reporting that AD limits their lifestyle, activities and social interactions because of their appearance [5]. As such, AD represents a significant health and economic burden to society. This burden is much less well-studied in Asian than in Western countries [6], and pharmacoeconomic studies of AD in China are lacking.

Numerous real-world surveys have characterised patient demographics, clinical characteristics and treatment of AD in China [7–9]. However, the effectiveness of treatments used in routine clinical practice in China, as well as of patient education/self-help (e.g. avoiding trigger factors, applying topical treatments) and the health economic burden of AD, are still poorly understood, especially among patients with moderate-to-severe AD.

The China Atopic Dermatitis Registry Study (ChinaSTAD) was conducted to collect data prospectively for at least 1 year from 10,000 patients with moderate-to-severe AD uncontrolled on topical therapy. Its main aims were to characterise the disease burden and real-world treatment of these patients, and to evaluate the associated health economic burden of AD. Patients in whom disease control was not achieved using topical therapy were of particular interest since they represent a significant proportion of patients with moderate-to-severe AD. This article reports the baseline patient characteristics, disease burden, treatments, healthcare resource use (HRU) and costs for the subpopulation of patients from ChinaSTAD who had not received systemic AD therapy continuously up to the date of study entry.

Methods

Study Design

This was a prospective observational registry study in China (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05023668). Overall, 80 study sites, all hospitals, were planned to be included. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking University People’s Hospital (approval no. 2021PHB076-001; 7 April 2021) and the local ethics committees at other sites (Supplementary Table S1). Patients were enrolled at the hospital outpatient clinic into this study by the participating physician (i.e. principal study investigator) at each site.

The study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles that have their origins in the Declaration of Helsinki and that are consistent with International Conference on Harmonisation/Good Clinical Practice, applicable regulatory requirements and the sponsor’s bioethics and human biospecimen policies. All patients or their legal guardian provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Inclusion Criteria

Individuals aged ≥ 12 years who met either William’s diagnostic criteria [10] or Zhang’s diagnostic criteria [7] for AD were included if they had moderate-to-severe AD (defined as a SCORing Atopic Dermatitis [SCORAD] Index score of ≥ 25) that was inadequately controlled by topical therapy.

Study Assessments and Data Collection

The first study visit (baseline) was the enrolment visit. Patients were followed for ≥ 12 months, with study visits at 1, 4, 8 and 12 months. Only data from the baseline visit are reported in this publication, where patient demographics, disease diagnosis, personal and family disease history, AD treatments and disease-related health economic burden data were collected. Results from laboratory tests (haematology, blood biochemistry, and total serum immunoglobulin E [IgE] and allergen-specific IgE levels) were recorded if they happened to be conducted at a study visit (tests were not mandated).

Disease severity assessments performed at each visit were: objective clinical disease severity, using the SCORAD index [11]; disease symptoms, using the Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM) tool [12, 13]; pruritus severity, using the Peak Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (PP-NRS) [14]; and long-term disease control, using the Atopic Dermatitis Control Tool (ADCT©) [15, 16], where disease control was defined as an ADCT score of < 7. Health-related QoL (HRQoL) was assessed using the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) [17] questionnaire for adolescents aged > 16 years and adults, and the Children’s DLQI (CDLQI) for children aged 12–16 years [18]. Further details on these tools are available in the online Supplementary Methods. Chinese-language versions of each assessment/scoring tool were used.

Treatments used at the time of the patients’ baseline visit, and at subsequent visits, were recorded; if treatments were switched, reason(s) were recorded.

Adverse events (AEs) and adverse reactions were recorded at each visit during follow-up. Adverse reactions were defined as AEs with a possible, probable or definite relationship to an AD treatment, as determined by investigators.

Health-economic data recorded at baseline and each visit (including numbers of inpatient admissions and outpatient visits, and number of days absent from school or work) were obtained via patient recall and from patient medical records. Baseline health economic data were for the year prior to registry enrolment. Costs included direct medical and non-medical costs (e.g. hospitalisation/inpatient costs, treatment costs, clinic visit costs). Costs were reported by patients. All data were entered into electronic case report forms (eCRFs) in a pseudonymised fashion.

Endpoints and Analysis Sets

The primary efficacy variables were the SCORAD, POEM, PP-NRS, ADCT and DLQI/CDLQI scores. The full analysis set (FAS) was defined as all patients for whom all of the following effectiveness variables were recorded at baseline: SCORAD, POEM, PP-NRS, ADCT and DLQI/CDLQI scores. The current analysis of baseline data applied an exclusion criterion as follows: any patients who had received prior systemic therapy continuously up to the time of study enrolment (i.e. the baseline visit) were excluded. The following systemic therapies were assessed for continuous use prior to ChinaSTAD study entry: systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine), biologics (dupilumab, omalizumab) and Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi; tofacitinib, baricitinib, abrocitinib and upadacitinib, although JAKi drug names were not specifically required to be recorded on the eCRF). Therefore, the data reported in this article are from a subgroup analysis of ChinaSTAD.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used, expressing continuous variables as means, medians, standard deviations (SDs), quartile range (first quartile [Q1] and third quartile [Q3]) and minimum and maximum, and categorical variables as number and percentage. All analyses were conducted using SAS® version 9.4 or later software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients

The first patient was enrolled on 21 April 2021. At this interim analysis data cut-off (31 October 2022), 2999 patients had been screened at 69 sites (Supplementary Table S1). In total, 2962 patients met study inclusion criteria and were enrolled, and 2927 patients were included in the FAS, of whom 2550 were included in the subgroup and analysed herein (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Patients had a mean ± SD age of 40.2 ± 21.0 years, and 56.8% were male (Table 1). Most patients (89.5%) lived in urban areas and 48.0% had tertiary-level education (college, university and above).

Baseline Disease Severity, Symptoms and Control

The mean ± SD SCORAD total score was 54.4 ± 15.7 (Table 3). The median (Q1, Q3) score was 53.3 (42.5, 64.3). Disease severity as assessed by patients was moderate: the mean ± SD POEM total score was 16.0 ± 7.1, and the median (Q1, Q3) score was 16.0 (11.0, 22.0). The mean ± SD PP-NRS total score was 6.8 ± 2.4. The majority of patients (85.6%) did not have controlled disease (ADCT score ≥ 7).

Baseline HRQoL

The mean ± SD DLQI total score for individuals aged > 16 years (n = 2284) was 11.0 ± 7.1 (Table 3), being within the range of total scores (11–20), indicating ‘a very large effect’ of AD on HRQoL [19]. The CDLQI mean ± SD total score was 10.0 ± 6.4 for adolescents aged 12–16 years (n = 266), which was within the score range (7–12) considered to indicate a ‘moderate effect’ of AD on HRQoL [20].

Treatment for AD

The most commonly used treatments at the time of enrolment were topical corticosteroids (TCS; 61.3% of patients), topical moisturising emollients (31.3%), topical calcineurin inhibitors (TCIs; 15.1%) and antihistamines (62.1%; Table 4).

Baseline Health Economic Burden

In the year prior to study enrolment, the majority of patients (87.2%) had no AD-related hospital admissions, whereas 12.3% had been admitted one to three times (Supplementary Table S4). Outpatient visits were common, with 37.5% having one to three visits in the previous year. The mean ± SD duration of work/school absence was 2.3 ± 12.3 days.

The mean ± SD cost per hospitalisation was 822.4 ± 3149.2 renminbi (RMB), and per outpatient visit it was 662.3 ± 1563.4 RMB (Table 5). For patients who incurred hospital costs, a greater proportion incurred per-hospitalisation costs of < 5000 RMB than of 5001–10,000 RMB (7.5% versus 3.6%). The majority of patients (75.5%) with outpatient visit costs had costs per visit in the lowest cost category (1–500 RMB). A similar proportion of patients reported spending 1–500 RMB on AD-related skin care products/medical beauty products (‘other AD-related costs’) in the year prior to enrolment as those with no spending (44.3% versus 46.4%). Only 9.1% of patients spent > 1000 RMB in the previous year on ‘other AD-related costs’.

Discussion

This interim analysis presenting real-world baseline ChinaSTAD registry data from adolescent and adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD captured the clinical status of patients, their treatment and burden of disease in terms of HRQoL at the time of their enrolment, as well as HRU during the year prior to enrolment. Most patients had a history of allergic disease, and around one-half had a family history of allergic disease. Patients had symptomatic moderate-to-severe AD, which was uncontrolled in the majority of patients (84%), despite receiving a wide array of topical anti-inflammatory AD treatments. The effect of AD on the HRQoL of patients was moderate in adolescents (aged 12–16 years) but severe in adolescents aged > 16 years and adults. There was also notable HRU, especially outpatient clinic visits.

In our study, there was a greater proportion of male than female participants, consistent with a number of prior real-world studies of AD in China and the Asia-Pacific region [7–9, 21, 22]. In contrast, a large cross-sectional population-based survey by Mao and colleagues reported a higher proportion of female than male individuals with AD (60.4% versus 39.6%), and in this study, female sex was identified as a risk factor for AD [8]. In a review of studies from countries around the world, it was found that globally, female individuals are more likely to have AD than male individuals [23]. The majority of participants in our study lived in an urban environment; again, this has been identified as a risk factor for AD in China [8].

Unlike previous real-world studies in China [7–9], this study focussed specifically on patients with moderate-to-severe AD [24]. Patient-assessed measures of symptom severity – the POEM tool and the PP-NRS – indicated moderate bordering on severe symptom severity, with a mean total POEM score of 16.0 (moderate = 8–16 [13]) and mean PP-NRS score of 6.8 (moderate = 4–6 [25]).

According to DLQI/CDLQI scores at baseline, AD had a very large effect on HRQoL in adults and older adolescents, albeit at the lower end of the range of scores and closer to scores indicating a moderate effect (mean score of 11.0 versus 11–20). In adolescents aged 12–16 years, the effect on HRQoL was moderate. A large cross-sectional study of patients with AD of all ages in China (56.6% adults; 33.3% adolescents aged 12 to < 18 years) by Zhao and colleagues similarly reported at least a moderate effect of AD on patient HRQoL: patients with moderate AD (n = 8337; 49.5% of patients in the study) had a mean DLQI score of 7, and patients with severe AD (n = 2487; 14.8%) had a mean DLQI score of 11 [21]. That AD had a moderate-to-large impact on patient QoL was a relatively unsurprising result given that patients in our study had moderate-to-severe disease, and the majority had uncontrolled disease. Despite patients receiving treatment with a variety of topical anti-inflammatory AD therapies, the impact of AD on their HRQoL had not diminished. This is notable because one of the goals of AD treatment is to improve HRQoL by relieving symptoms such that patients can lead a relatively normal life [2], and that active management with the specific aim of improving HRQoL is recommended for patients with a DLQI total score > 10 [19].

The most frequently used AD treatments at baseline were antihistamines, TCS, TCIs and topical moisturising emollients. A total of 62.1% of patients were treated with antihistamines at baseline, which seems to be in line with Chinese clinical practice recommendations to prescribe oral antihistamines as adjuvant therapy to relieve itch, particularly in patients with co-existing allergic conditions [2], since 63.4% of patients had a history of allergic disease. These results are also consistent with Chinese dermatologists’ perceptions that antihistamines are widely used for AD in China [26]. In contrast, lower proportions of patients used antihistamines in both the Zhao et al. study (~31% of patients with moderate or severe AD; n = 10,405 [21]), and the Mao et al. study (36%), although in the latter study, fewer patients had co-existing allergic conditions (~26%) and only ~27% had moderate or severe AD [8]. Topical emollients are an essential component to the management of AD in China [2]; however, only 31.3% of patients reported using topical moisturising emollients at baseline. This seems to be in agreement with a low rate of use of emollients in the Zhao et al. study (~20% of patients) [21], and prior observations by dermatologists that there is inadequate use of emollients among patients with AD in China [26].

Use of traditional Chinese medicine in our study (19.5%) was consistent with Mao and colleagues reporting that 18.7% of patients in their study visited traditional Chinese medicine doctors [8]. Although Zhao and colleagues reported a lower rate of traditional Chinese medicine use among patients with moderate or severe AD in their study (~19%) [21], their study was limited to recording use of Tripterygium glycosides and glycyrrhizin, whereas our study collected data on all Chinese medicines used. While the Chinese clinical practice guidelines make no specific recommendations regarding the use of traditional Chinese medicine [2], it is acknowledged that there is wide use of traditional Chinese medicine for patients with AD [26].

TCS/TCI treatment is recommended for patients with moderate AD in China [2]. A relatively high use of TCSs (64.9%) was reported in the study by Mao et al., which was similar to our study (61.3%), although TCI use was less frequent in their study than ours (4.5% versus 15.1%) [8]. While rates of TCS and TCI use in the Zhao et al. study (~34% and ~6%, respectively [21]) were lower than in our study, systemic corticosteroid use was broadly similar (~5% versus 5.7% in our study). However, systemic corticosteroid use was lower in the Mao et al. study than in our study (2.2%). These variations between studies can be explained, at least in part, by the differences between study populations, as all patients had moderate-to-severe AD in our study compared with 26.7% of the Mao et al. study population [8]. In China, AD treatment guidelines do not recommend routine long-term use of systemic corticosteroids; their use should be reserved for short-term treatment of acute disease flares [2]. Although we did not assess clinician adherence to treatment guidelines in this study, this may explain the low rate of use of systemic corticosteroids we observed, an observation made also by Zhao and colleagues in their recent study [21]. A lack of experience in the use of systemic immunosuppressants among dermatologists in primary hospitals in China [26] may also account, at least in part, for our findings.

Targeted therapies are available for the treatment of AD [1, 2]. One of these, dupilumab, is a fully human monoclonal antibody directed against the α-subunit of the interleukin (IL)-4 receptors, thus blocking IL-4 and IL-13 signalling. Our results suggest that after dupilumab’s approval in China in 2020 [27], clinicians started prescribing dupilumab for patients with moderate-to-severe disease uncontrolled on topical therapies, since 22.7% of patients in our study appear to have been started on dupilumab at the baseline visit (as patients on systemic treatment continuously prior to study entry were excluded from our analysis).

We observed a considerable disease burden in terms of HRU: 12.3% of patients had one to three hospital admissions in the prior year, and more than one-third of patients (37.5%) made outpatient clinic visits at the same frequency. Our results indirectly support those from the Taiwanese study, where patients with moderate or severe AD eligible for targeted therapy (but not treated with targeted therapy) had much higher AD-related HRU over a 1-year period than those not eligible for targeted therapy [28].

A registry study conducted in routine clinical practice by its nature has some limitations. There was no control population. Data were obtained at each study site by participating investigators, and as such, there may have been errors or omissions in data collection. There was also potential for selection bias when applying the inclusion criteria. Some data collection relied on patient recall, leading to the possibility of recall bias. Although there were 69 study sites across 25 provinces of China, there may have been under-representation of remaining provinces, municipalities and autonomous regions. Nevertheless, ChinaSTAD is one of the largest registry studies of AD in China, and is providing generalisable evidence of real-world treatment of AD, including long-term effectiveness, safety and HRU in China.

Conclusions

In this preliminary analysis of the ChinaSTAD registry study, moderate-to-severe AD not controlled on topical treatment was associated with a substantial disease burden in adolescent and adult patients in China. A 1-year follow-up of patients has been completed, and data analyses are underway. The full analysis from this study will be reported at a future date and will provide further evidence on the impact of treatments on disease control and HRQoL outcomes, as well as HRU and costs, related to AD treatment.
