Protocol for formulation and evaluation of phytosterol-based lipid nanoparticles as cholesterol alternatives for mRNA delivery
Authors: Jaewook Lee, Jae Hoon Kim, Seung-Yeul Lee, Jun Hyeong Hong, Tae Jeong Oh, Sungwhan An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a protocol for formulating and evaluating lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) that incorporate the plant-derived sterols ginsenoside Rg2 and protopanaxadiol (PPD) as substitutes for cholesterol. The protocol describes mRNA preparation, manual mRNA-LNP encapsulation, and physicochemical characterization. We further detail in vitro and in vivo procedures to evaluate mRNA delivery efficiency. This protocol provides a robust and cost-effective platform for efficient mRNA delivery.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Park et al.1Subject areas: Model Organisms, Biotechnology and bioengineering, Chemistry, Material sciences

Before you begin

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are the leading delivery system for nucleic acids, typically composed of ionizable lipids, phospholipids, cholesterol, and PEG-lipids.2,3,4 Cholesterol plays a structural role in stabilizing the lipid bilayer but presents several drawbacks: it is prone to oxidation, produces toxic metabolites, and its animal origin raises safety and ethical concerns for pharmaceutical use.5,6 To address these limitations, this study explores the replacement of cholesterol in LNPs with the plant-derived sterols ginsenoside Rg2 and protopanaxadiol (PPD).1,7 The objective is to evaluate whether phytosterol-based LNPs can maintain stability and efficient mRNA delivery in vitro and in vivo, thereby expanding options for safe and sustainable nucleic acid delivery.

Innovation

This protocol introduces an innovative strategy for formulating lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) using plant-derived sterols—ginsenoside Rg2 and protopanaxadiol (PPD)—as sustainable and animal-free substitutes for cholesterol in mRNA delivery systems. Unlike conventional cholesterol-based LNPs, these phytosterol-based formulations show enhanced biocompatibility, oxidation resistance, and ethical advantages while maintaining high delivery efficiency.

The protocol provides a manual and reproducible method for LNP synthesis without requiring microfluidic instruments, making it broadly accessible to research laboratories. It suggests key experimental stages, including in vitro transcription (IVT) of mRNA, encapsulation, biophysical characterization, and functional validation in both in vitro and in vivo systems.

By demonstrating that phytosterols can stably substitute cholesterol while supporting efficient mRNA expression, this protocol introduces a cost-effective, eco-friendly, and adaptable platform for developing next-generation nucleic acid therapeutics and vaccine delivery systems.

Timing: 2 days

This protocol uses EGFP mRNA and luciferase (Luc) mRNA as representative examples.

5.Preparation of the PCR product for in vitro transcription (IVT).a.Construct the PCR template for IVT via PCR amplification (Table 1).Note: While various methods for mRNA production exist, this protocol specifically employs IVT method for synthesizing mRNA.b.Preparation of a 1.5% agarose gel.i.Weigh out 3 g of agarose powder.ii.Dissolve the agarose in 200 mL of 0.5× TBE buffer and heat in a microwave until fully melted.iii.Allow the solution to cool slightly, then add EcoDye at a ratio of 1 μL per 20 mL of agarose solution and mix gently.iv.Pour the solution into a gel tray and allow it to solidify to obtain a 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel.c.Electrophorese the amplified PCR products on a 1.5 % agarose gel at 150 V for 15 min.d.After confirming the band size of PCR products, purify the PCR products for IVT template using the QIAquick® Gel Extraction Kit.e.Measure the purified PCR products concentration using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and assess its quality by gel electrophoresis (Figure 1).

Expected outcomes

This protocol outlines the formulation and evaluation of LNPs that incorporates plant-derived compounds—specifically, ginsenoside Rg2 and PPD—as cholesterol substitutes in mRNA delivery systems. Additionally, we present an efficient manual preparation method that enables successful mRNA delivery and expression without the need for costly microfluidic devices.

The anticipated outcome of this protocol is the successful encapsulation of EGFP or Luc mRNA into Rg2-LNP and PPD-LNP formulations, followed by effective delivery and expression both in vitro and in vivo.

After mRNA synthesis and purification, mRNA concentration and integrity should be confirmed via agarose gel electrophoresis. Expected band sizes are approximately 2,032 bases for EGFP and 1,119 bases for Luc (Figure 2).

The size and uniformity of LNPs should be evaluated by DLS, aiming for an average particle size of 80∼120 nm and a PDI less than 0.3 (Figure 4; Table 3). TEM, following negative staining, can be employed to visualize the spherical morphology of the mRNA-loaded LNPs (Figure 5).

Encapsulation efficiency can be assessed using agarose gel analysis and Ribogreen assays, with successful encapsulation indicated by a reduction of free mRNA bands compared to the naked mRNA controls (Figures 6 and 7).

Upon transfection of A549 or HeLa cells with EGFP mRNA-loaded Rg2-LNP or PPD-LNP, EGFP expression should be observable via fluorescence microscopy within 24 hrs (Figures 8 and 12). Flow cytometry should demonstrate dose-dependent increases in both mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and the percentage of EGFP-positive cells (Figure 9).

In vivo, intramuscular injection of Luc mRNA-encapsulated LNPs into BALB/c nude mice should result in detectable bioluminescence within 5∼30 minutes of D-luciferin administration, as captured by IVIS imaging (Figure 11).

Limitations

Despite the well-established manual method for the preparation of mRNA-LNP complexes, several limitations exist. These include challenges in achieving precise size uniformity and optimal mRNA encapsulation per LNP. To address these limitations, future studies will incorporate microfluidic devices to enhance the development of mRNA-based therapeutics.

While the use of plant-derived cholesterol alternatives, such as Rg2 and PPD, has demonstrated effective mRNA encapsulation and delivery, their performance may vary depending on the target cell type and mRNA construct. Therefore, optimization of lipid ratios may be required for different cell types. Additionally, surface modification of LNPs with peptides, antibodies, or small molecules will be necessary for targeted delivery to specific tissues, organs, or tumors.

Furthermore, immunogenicity of plant-derived LNPs were not fully evaluated in this protocol and will require further investigation.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The size and morphology of the LNP are unstable (related to Step 1 and 2).

Potential solution

Excessive sonication (longer than 1 min or at high power) may disrupt mRNA–LNP complexes and reduce transfection efficiency.

Ensure that LNP formulations are stored at 4 °C prior to use.

Problem 2

Low EGFP expression in in vitro expression test (related to Step 8).

Potential solution

Change components ratio for LNP formulation (Figure 12).

Ensure that the mRNA is stored at – 80 °C or lower to maintain stability. Prior to use, assess its integrity by verifying that no degradation has occurred.

Confirm the size of the transcribed mRNA using agarose gel electrophoresis to ensure it aligns with the expected length. The gel should show a single, distinct band without smearing or additional non-specific bands, indicating high purity and integrity.

Problem 3

Reduced cell viability post-LNP treatment (related to Step 9).

Potential solution

Dilute the LNP formulation further prior to application to the cells to reduce cytotoxicity.

Adjust the concentration of PPD or Rg2 in the LNP formulation as needed. After each modification, confirm that the mRNA remains efficiently expressed in the target cells.

Optimize transfection or treatment conditions by adjusting the seeding density of cells in the 24-well plate.
