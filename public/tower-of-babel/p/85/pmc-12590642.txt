From home garden to market: ethnobotanical insights from Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, Baise City, Guangxi, China
Authors: Yuefeng Zhang, Bin Huang, Wei Shen, Lingling Lv, Piyaporn Saensouk, Thawatphong Boonma, Tammanoon Jitpromma, Surapon Saensouk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBaise City in Guangxi, China, is a culturally and ecologically diverse region where traditional plant knowledge persists despite ongoing urbanization. Traditional markets in such areas play a crucial role in the transmission and preservation of ethnobotanical knowledge. However, no prior ethnobotanical studies have focused on the Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, making this the first systematic documentation of plant diversity and use at this important site.MethodsThis study surveyed plant species sold at the Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market. A total of 78 species from 38 families were documented through market observations and vendor interviews. Quantitative ethnobotanical indices, including species use value (SUV), family use value (FUV), and relative frequency of citation (RFC), were applied to assess plant importance.ResultsA total of 78 species belonging to 38 plant families were recorded during the ethnobotanical survey. The market featured a wide range of edible, medicinal, packaging, and spice plants. Cucurbitaceae, Apiaceae, and Solanaceae were the most represented families. Availability of plant materials closely followed natural phenological cycles supported by staggered planting and imports. Key species included Oryza sativa L. (staple food), Cucurbita maxima Duchesne (versatile vegetable), and Allium sativum L. (aromatic spice). Medicinal plants were fewer in the market, reflecting its primary focus on food crops, while traditional plant-based packaging highlights the unique preparation of certain foods, as well as ecological awareness and sustainable practices.ConclusionThis study provides the first ethnobotanical account of the Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, contributing new insights into plant diversity, traditional knowledge, and cultural practices in Southwestern China. The market functions as a critical node for conserving plant biodiversity, supporting food and livelihood security, and maintaining ethnobotanical knowledge in an urbanizing region. Further research should explore seasonal dynamics, ethnographic perspectives, and phytochemical properties to better understand the food–medicine continuum in this context.

Background

Baise City, located in the western part of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, is a culturally and ecologically diverse area [1]. Over the past few decades, the city has undergone continuous development, with new infrastructure and growing integration into the national economy [2]. However, amid this process of urbanization, many residents—especially those from nearby rural communities and ethnic minority groups—continue to live according to traditional ways of life [3]. Local plant knowledge remains an important part of daily living, serving purposes in food, health-care, and cultural practices [4]. This coexistence of urban development and traditional plant use provides a valuable context for ethnobotanical study.

Recent ethnobotanical studies in Guangxi and other regions of China have emphasized the significance of traditional markets as spaces for the exchange and transmission of plant knowledge. For example, Long et al. [5] surveyed herbal tea species traded across multiple markets in Guangxi, revealing a high diversity of culturally important plants. Similarly, Lu et al. [6] and Hu et al. [7] documented the persistence of traditional plant use among ethnic groups in Guangxi, highlighting how market trade contributes to the circulation of both wild and cultivated species. Broader studies across China also describe regional networks of medicinal and edible plant trade that sustain local livelihoods and ecological knowledge [8, 9]. Despite these advances, there remains a lack of localized studies focusing on individual urban markets that reflect rural–urban interaction and cultural continuity.

Local markets often serve as important community spaces where traditional knowledge is practiced and conveyed [10]. Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, located in the heart of Baise City, is a vibrant site characterized by its rich diversity of local plants. Many of the species sold here are not found in commercial agriculture or mainstream supermarkets; instead, they come from household gardens, nearby forests, or semi-wild environments. The vendors—comprising both men and women from surrounding villages and urban neighborhoods—bring these plants to market not only as a source of income, but also as a way of sustaining and sharing local ecological knowledge [11, 12].

What makes Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market especially valuable for ethnobotanical study is the continuity of traditional plant use. Despite the city’s development, plants such as Oryza sativa L., Pyrus pyrifolia (Burm.f.) Nakai, and Toona sinensis (A.Juss.) M.Roem., remain commonly available [13]. Some are cultivated in home gardens using inherited techniques passed down through generations, while others are foraged from the wild [14]. The variety of species and their uses reflect an active and adaptive local knowledge system that remains closely tied to cultural identity and the surrounding environment [15].

This study aims to document and analyze the plant species found in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, particularly those that originate from home gardens or wild collection. Through interviews with vendors, participant observation, and plant specimen collection, we examine how traditional plant knowledge is preserved, practiced, and adapted in everyday life [16]. Special attention is given to the experiences and insights of the vendors themselves, who serve as important sources of local ethnobotanical information. The study also considers the broader socioeconomic context of plant trade in the market.

While much ethnobotanical research has focused on rural or remote areas, mid-sized cities like Baise—where urbanization is underway yet traditional practices remain robust—also warrant attention. This study contributes to the field of urban ethnobotany by highlighting how local markets function as sites of both biodiversity conservation and cultural continuity. Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market is not only a place of commerce but also a space of memory, identity, and ecological connection.

Documenting and interpreting the uses and meanings of plants in this market have both academic and practical significance. It serves as a record of local knowledge during the time of social and environmental transition and offers insights into sustainable resource use, cultural heritage preservation, and resilient livelihoods [17]. Above all, it sheds light on how people in Baise continue to maintain a deep relationship with nature—bringing plants from gardens and forests into the heart of a changing city.

To address these aims, this study seeks to document the traditional knowledge and ethnobotanical uses of plants sold in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market. Specifically, the objectives are to (1) identify the plant species available; (2) record the parts of plants used and their preparation or utilization methods; (3) classify the plants according to their functional uses (e.g., food, medicine, cultural practices); and (4) analyze their relative cultural importance using standard ethnobotanical indices such as Species Use Value (SUV) and Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC).

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted at Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, located in Baise City, in the western part of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, Southern China, spanning approximate coordinates 23° 53′ 31.5″ N, 106° 36′ 5.2″ E (Fig. 1). The region is characterized by a subtropical monsoon climate, with distinct wet and dry seasons, an average annual temperature of 17.5–23.5 °C, and annual precipitation ranging from 1200 to 1600 mm [18]. Baise City has a population of approximately 472,000 inhabitants, predominantly composed of Zhuang ethnic groups, alongside Han, Yao, and other minority communities [19]. The main languages spoken include Zhuang and Mandarin Chinese, and the predominant religions are local folk beliefs and Buddhism [20]. The multi-ethnic composition and rich cultural traditions make Baise an important area for studying ethnobotanical knowledge systems. The combination of urbanization, traditional home gardening, and access to nearby forests allows for a dynamic interaction between ecological knowledge and market activity.

Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market functions as a central hub for the exchange of locally grown vegetables, wild edible plants, and other plant-based products. It attracts vendors and buyers from both rural and urban areas, reflecting the flow of plant species from home gardens, nearby forests, and semi-wild areas into local commerce. The surrounding communities engage in small-scale agriculture, home gardening, and wild plant collection, maintaining extensive traditional ecological knowledge. This interplay between traditional practices and market demand provides insights into the cultural, ecological, and economic roles of local plant species in everyday life.

The study area focuses on the Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market and its surrounding communities, providing a representative site to document and analyze the local ethnobotanical knowledge in an urban–rural interface (Fig. 1).

Ethnobotanical data collection

Data for this study were collected over a 12-month period, from May 2024 to April 2025, to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the diversity and cultural significance of local vegetable species in the Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, Baise City, Guangxi, China. A mixed-method approach was employed to capture both qualitative and quantitative dimensions of ethnobotanical knowledge. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with a total of 30 participants, including 15 men and 15 women, aged between 20 and 65 years. These participants were drawn from market vendors, farmers, and community members, including both buyers and sellers. Prior to the interviews, the study’s purpose was clearly communicated, and prior informed consent was obtained in accordance with the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE) Code of Ethics [21] and the principles of the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing [22]. Participants were fully informed of their rights, including voluntary participation and the option to withdraw at any stage. The interviews focused on capturing traditional knowledge, such as local plant names, utilized plant parts, preparation techniques, and their respective uses. Complementary botanical surveys were undertaken to systematically identify and record the vegetable taxa available in the market. Species identification was verified in collaboration with local botanists and taxonomic experts to ensure scientific accuracy.

Taxonomic identification

Plant species were identified through comparison with original descriptions and authoritative references, primarily the Flora of China and Plants of the World Online (POWO) [23]. Relevant literature was also reviewed in major scientific databases (Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar) to ensure taxonomic accuracy and reflect current classifications. High-resolution digital images and morphological data from the Kew Herbarium and Kew Science online resources were consulted to support visual verification. Specimens were identified by the Chinese co-author of this study, who has expertise in the regional flora. Voucher specimens are deposited at the Agriculture and Food Engineering College, Baise University, Baise City, Guangxi, China, where a registered herbarium is planned to be established in the near future.

Phenology study

This research explores the phenological characteristics of local vegetable species available at Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, with an emphasis on their flowering and fruiting periods. To ensure consistency in temporal analysis, months were coded numerically from 1 (January) to 12 (December). Insight into these reproductive stages is vital for assessing both the ecological adaptability and economic value of the species within the regional context [24].

Data analysis

The species use value (SUV) is employed in this study to quantify the ethnobotanical significance of each plant species recorded at Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market. This index, adapted from the approach developed by Hoffman and Gallaher [25], provides a standardized measure of how important a species is within the local knowledge system. UV is a quantitative index that measures the relative importance of a plant species by combining the number of distinct uses and the number of informants who mention those uses. It is determined by dividing the total number of use citations for that species (across all use categories, treated with equal weight) by the number of times that informant mentioned the species. By averaging the individual use values across all participants, SUV provides an objective, comparative index of each species’ local relevance—highlighting which plants are most widely known, used, or valued within the marketplace context of Baise City.

The family use value (FUV) was used in this study to assess the relative ethnobotanical importance of plant families represented at Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market. This index provides a standardized measure of the overall cultural significance of each family based on the use values of its constituent species. FUV is a quantitative index that measures the cultural and practical importance of plant families within a community. By summing the UVs of all species in a family and dividing by the total number of species, FUV highlights which families are most significant for cultural uses such as medicine, food, or fuel. This metric allows for comparison across families, helping researchers identify those that contribute substantially to local ethnobotanical knowledge and daily use in the marketplace context.

The plant part value (PPV) was calculated to assess the relative importance of various plant parts utilized by vendors and informants at Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market. This metric identifies which parts—such as leaves, roots, stems, fruits, bark, and flowers—are most frequently cited in local ethnobotanical practices, particularly for medicinal use. Each citation corresponds to a single mention of a plant part being used, as reported by an informant. Expressing the data as a percentage allows for comparison of use frequency across different plant parts, providing insight into their cultural relevance and functional role in the local knowledge system. PPV is a quantitative index that indicates the relative importance and usage frequency of specific plant parts within a community. It is derived by dividing the total number of use reports for a particular plant part (e.g., leaf, root, bark) by the total number of use reports for all parts of all species. The plant part with the highest PPV is considered the most culturally significant and widely used among local respondents.

The relative frequency of citation (RFC) was calculated to determine the local importance of each plant species based on how frequently it was mentioned by informants. This index reflects the extent of familiarity and perceived value of a species within the community, independent of the number of uses. RFC values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating species that are more commonly cited and therefore more widely recognized or used within the study area. RFC is a straightforward and widely applied index that highlights culturally significant species. A high RFC suggests that a plant is well known in the community and frequently used, often reflecting its availability, perceived effectiveness, and low risk of side effects. This index complements other ethnobotanical measures by focusing on citation frequency rather than diversity or specificity of use.

This seasonal rhythm not only mirrors plant biology but also influences market availability, harvest practices, and dietary patterns in local communities, where seasonal produce plays a significant role in traditional food systems.

Species use value (SUV) of plants in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market

Local vegetables were assessed for their species use value (SUV), reflecting the relative importance of each species based on frequency of mention and reported uses among local people (Table 1). The calculated SUV values ranged from 0.267 to 1.000, indicating a gradient of cultural and utilitarian significance among the documented plants.

The highest SUV (1.000) was assigned to Oryza sativa, underscoring its foundational role as a staple crop in the local diet. This was followed closely by Cucurbita maxima and Houttuynia cordata, each with an SUV of 0.900, highlighting their importance not only as food sources but also for their multifunctional uses in traditional dishes and medicinal applications.

Other species with high SUV values included Zea mays and Capsicum annuum (both SUV 0.867), and Prunus persica (SUV 0.833), reflecting the integration of cereals, spices, and fruits into daily consumption. Several culinary and spice plants, such as Zingiber officinale (SUV 0.800), Allium sativum (SUV 0.767), and Solanum americanum (SUV 0.767), also showed high SUV scores, indicating their consistent use across multiple contexts including flavoring, health maintenance, and treatment of ailments.

Moderately valued species (SUV 0.500–0.700) included both fruit crops such as Vitis vinifera (SUV 0.567), Mangifera indica (SUV 0.533), and Ziziphus jujuba (SUV 0.500), as well as leafy vegetables or herbs such as Coriandrum sativum (SUV 0.567), Centella asiatica (SUV 0.500), and Melissa officinalis (SUV 0.500). These plants reflect strong culinary relevance with selective medicinal or symbolic functions.

Lower SUV values (≤ 0.433) were observed in less frequently used or context-specific plants such as Arachis hypogaea (SUV 0.467), Spinacia oleracea (SUV 0.367), and Artemisia argyi (0.300). These species may be regionally or seasonally relevant or primarily used by specific demographic groups.

Plants in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market used as beverages

The Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market in Baise City presents a notable diversity of plants used in both traditional and contemporary beverages. Plant species utilized for beverage in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market

Among the plants used for beverage preparation in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, fruit is the overwhelmingly dominant plant part utilized, accounting for 84.62% of the species. This highlights a strong local preference for fruit-based drinks, such as fresh juices, smoothies, and fermented fruit beverages. In contrast, leaves and tubers were each used in only 7.69% of the species, typically incorporated into vegetable-based drinks for their flavor or nutritional value. The predominance of fruit reflects both its appealing taste and its central role in local refreshment and dietary habits.

Plants in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market used as food

The edible plant diversity at Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market spans a wide range of species and reflects notable variation in the parts of plants used for food. Leaves and shoots together follow at 21.43%, demonstrating their widespread culinary use in local dishes. Tubers represent 10.00% of the species, underscoring their role as a significant staple. Less frequently used are bulbs and rhizomes, each comprising 1.43% of the species. This diversity in plant part utilization highlights the complexity and adaptability of local food traditions, where nearly every edible component of a plant is integrated into daily cuisine.

Plants in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market used as medicine

The medicinal flora represented at Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market comprises three species distributed across three distinct families: Plantaginaceae, Rutaceae, and Verbenaceae (Table 1). These species underscore the market’s critical role in sustaining traditional herbal medicine practices within the local community. Among them, Plantago major and Verbena officinalis are utilized in their entirety, typically harvested as whole plants for preparation via infusion or decoction. Conversely, Bergera tetramera is principally employed for its leaves and shoots, reflecting prevalent ethnobotanical conventions favoring the use of aerial plant parts for therapeutic applications.

Plants in Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market used as packaging

In addition to edible and medicinal plants, the Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market also features species traditionally utilized for natural food packaging, reflecting sustainable and culturally embedded practices. Both species are primarily valued for their large, durable leaves, which are used to wrap or contain various food items, particularly prepared dishes and snacks. The use of these plant leaves as biodegradable packaging materials illustrates the integration of ecological knowledge with local market practices, reducing reliance on plastic and promoting environmentally friendly alternatives.

Discussion

Regional variations in traditional knowledge of edible plants

This study systematically documents 78 cultivated edible plant species in the Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market, illustrating the intricate integration of diet, medicine, and cultural practices among local communities. Compared with ethnobotanical studies in India [28, 29], several families—including Fabaceae, Cucurbitaceae, and Zingiberaceae—show widespread use across subtropical Asia, indicating shared human-plant interactions shaped by climate, nutrition, and health priorities. However, our study reports novel local uses for species such as Selenicereus undatus (dragon fruit) and Houttuynia cordata, highlighting region-specific cultural adaptations.

Unlike Indian markets, which prioritize leafy vegetables and tubers for both nutrition and ritual purposes, Baise markets show a greater emphasis on fruits and functional beverages. This regional variation reflects local dietary preferences, microclimatic conditions, and long-established culinary traditions, underlining the close relationship between ethnobotanical knowledge and ecological specificity [30]. The diversity in plant part usage—leaves for vegetables, fruits for beverages, roots, and rhizomes for medicinal purposes—demonstrates a selective adaptation to resource availability and functional properties, consistent with global ethnobotanical patterns [31, 32].

Integration of culinary and medicinal functions

Local practices embody the principle of “food as medicine,” with 64% of documented species serving dual roles. Plants such as Zingiber officinale and Allium spp. are simultaneously used in daily cooking and as therapeutic remedies for digestive or immune-related disorders. The frequent use of detoxifying herbs, including Taraxacum officinale and Plantago major, aligns with prevalent health issues in the subtropical environment, such as gastrointestinal infections [33]. Compared with synthetic drugs, these plants provide complex bioactive compounds—flavonoids, polysaccharides, and essential oils—that are gradually released and support long-term preventive healthcare [34]. These multifunctional uses underscore their role in nutritional security and culturally relevant healthcare.

Cross-cultural perspectives and regional comparisons

When placed in a cross-cultural context, the Baise edible plant system shows both convergence and divergence with Indian and South-east Asian markets. Shared strategies include the prioritization of Fabaceae and Cucurbitaceae species, the use of fruits and roots for nutrition and therapy, and the focus on bioactive compounds for preventive health [35]. Divergences are notable: Baise communities integrate fruit-based beverages and multifunctional leafy vegetables into daily diets, while Indian markets emphasize leafy vegetables and tubers in ritual and medicinal contexts [36]. Similarly, Indonesia and Lao markets share overlapping species but differ in culinary applications and symbolic significance. These patterns illustrate how ethnobotanical knowledge is finely tuned to microhabitat, cultural preference, and local health priorities, reflecting a dynamic interplay of ecological and social factors [37, 38].

Implications for environmental sustainability and public health

The ethnobotanical practices observed have direct implications for food security, public health, and environmental sustainability. The use of large leaves for packaging (e.g., Indocalamus tessellatus) reduces reliance on plastic, demonstrating an eco-consciousnes [39]. Seasonal availability of key species underscores vulnerability to climate variability, which could compromise nutrient intake and medicinal plant access [40]. The multifunctional use of cultivated plants supports preventive healthcare, addressing widespread issues such as digestive disorders, infections, and micronutrient deficiencies [41]. These findings suggest that safeguarding traditional plant knowledge is essential for both ecological resilience and community health.

Sustainability and emerging challenges

Cultivation and commercialization of high-value species, such as Selenicereus undatus and Hippophae rhamnoides (in other subtropical regions), offer economic opportunities but also pose risks to genetic diversity and ecological stability [42, 43]. Traditional harvesting practices—collecting only ripe fruits or preserving roots—reflect a sophisticated ecological awareness; yet, intensifying market pressures and monoculture practices may compromise adaptive traits [44]. Climate-induced phenological shifts further threaten resource availability, highlighting the need for community-based conservation strategies integrated with sustainable agricultural practices [45].

Limitations and future directions

While this study provides a comprehensive overview of edible plant use in the Baise market, it is limited to selected villages and market sites. Broader regional surveys are necessary to capture intra-community variation and cross-border knowledge transmission, particularly in neighboring Lao and Vietnamese communities. Future research should prioritize phytochemical and pharmacological validation of high-use species, longitudinal monitoring of seasonal availability, and strategies to engage younger generations in knowledge preservation. Integrating traditional knowledge into conservation and public health planning will enhance both ecological resilience and community well-being.

In summary, the cultivated edible plants of Baise represent a rich biocultural heritage, integrating nutrition, medicine, and cultural identity. Comparative analysis underscores both universal ethnobotanical strategies and region-specific adaptations, emphasizing the critical role of traditional knowledge in shaping sustainable diets, preventive healthcare, and ecological stewardship. Protecting this heritage is imperative not only for cultural preservation but also for addressing future environmental and public health challenges in subtropical Asia.

Conclusions

The Jīn Sān Jiǎo Agricultural Market in Baise City serves as a vital hub for conserving plant diversity and transmitting traditional knowledge, where 78 edible, medicinal, and culturally significant species from 38 families are actively maintained through local food systems. Patterns of species diversity, phenology, and market availability illustrate adaptive cultivation and harvesting strategies that sustain year-round supply, while indices such as SUV, FUV, and RFC highlight the prominence of staple and multifunctional species in daily life. Beyond food, the presence of medicinal, packaging, and spice plants underscores the integration of ecological awareness and cultural practices in local markets. These findings demonstrate that traditional marketplaces are not only centers of commerce but also repositories of agroecological resilience, food-medicine continuum, and intangible cultural heritage. Future research should apply longitudinal, ethnographic, and comparative approaches to capture temporal changes, deepen understanding of knowledge transmission, and link ethnobotanical practices with nutritional and public health perspectives.
