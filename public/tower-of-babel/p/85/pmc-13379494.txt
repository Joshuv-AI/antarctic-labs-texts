Identification of divergent organ-specific gene and protein expression signatures for mitochondrial function, inflammatory response, and proteostasis in the liver and brain in the rotenone-induced rat model of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Tuba Oz, Juan Fraile-Ramos, Radosław Kujawski, Olga Wojciechowska, Marta Karaźniewicz-Łada, Przemysław Łukasz Mikołajczak, Lydia Giménez-Llort, Małgorzata Kujawska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence suggests that peripheral organs, particularly the liver, may influence brain homeostasis and neurodegenerative diseases. This study investigates the differential expression of Parkinson’s disease (PD)-related, oxidative stress, and inflammatory genes in the liver and brain of six-week-old male albino Wistar rats (250–300 g) subchronically exposed to rotenone (ROT, 1.3 mg/kg/day, 35 days, b.w.), a pesticide commonly used to model PD. Relative expression levels were measured using quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) and western blot. Genes involved in mitophagy (Parkin (PARK2), p = 0.0039), oxidative stress response (Parkinson’s disease protein (DJ-1), p = 0.0209), lysosomal function (Low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1 (LRP1), p = 0.0418; ATPase cation transporting 13a2 (ATP13a2), p = 0.0308), and inflammation (Tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), p = 0.0171) were found upregulated in the brain of ROT-induced rats as compared to control rats, and were also significantly higher than in the liver (p < 0.05). In contrast, significantly higher phosphatase and tensin homolog-induced kinase 1 (PINK1) expression was found in the liver as compared to the brain (p = 0.0198). Notably, these inter-organ differences and transcriptional shifts were absent in the controls. Moreover, the liver exhibited distinct molecular responses, including significant downregulation of ATP13a2 and SNCA (Encoding alpha-synuclein) and overexpression of NFe2-like basic leucine zipper transcription factor 2 (NFe2l2), compared to control rats (p < 0.05). Nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), and interferon gamma (IFN-γ) showed no significant changes in either tissue (p > 0.05). These findings demonstrate that distinct molecular alterations in the liver and brain following ROT treatment, including differences in the regulation of genes associated with mitophagy, oxidative stress, proteostasis, and inflammation. Our findings demonstrate tissue-specific molecular associations in the liver and brain within the ROT-induced PD model, providing new insights into the pathophysiology of neurodegeneration and identifying potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for future studies.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11033-026-12195-x.Keywords: Alpha-synuclein, Neurodegeneration, Parkinson's disease, Cerebral cortex, Liver, Gene expression

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, affecting approximately 2% of the population over 60 years of age and up to 3% of those over 85 years [1]. The neuropathological hallmark of PD is the progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, accompanied by the accumulation of intraneuronal inclusions known as Lewy bodies. These aggregates are primarily composed of alpha-synuclein (α-Syn), a presynaptic protein critically involved in synaptic vesicle trafficking and closely related to dopamine metabolism, including synthesis, transport, and storage [2].

Despite extensive research, the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms driving neurodegenerative disorders like PD remain only partially understood. Growing evidence suggests that metabolic risk factors contribute meaningfully to the initiation and progression of these diseases, pointing to new opportunities for prevention and treatment [3]. The liver, as the central metabolic organ of the human body, plays a pivotal role in systemic metabolic homeostasis. Alterations in hepatic metabolism may influence neuronal function in the brain through the release of specific metabolites, inflammatory mediators, or signaling molecules, thus linking to the occurrence and development of PD [4]. Recent studies have shown multiple molecular mechanisms underlying the hepatic involvement in neurodegeneration. Sperringer et al. (2017) have demonstrated that hepatic dysfunction impacts neurotransmitter synthesis and neuronal function, leading to neurotoxicity and cognitive decline [5]. Liver fibrosis was associated with cognitive decline in PD [6]. Beyond hepatic metabolism, the gut–liver–brain axis represents another key interface, whereby dysbiosis-induced microbial metabolites can trigger liver dysfunction, alter immune responses, and contribute to α-Syn pathology in the brain [7, 8]. Animal studies involving the transplantation of gut microbiota from patients with PD into mice have demonstrated elevated α-Syn expression in recipient animals, accompanied by enhanced neuroinflammatory responses and the development of motor deficits [9]. The recent identification of α-Syn aggregates in the liver, as reported by Reyes et al. further supports the hypothesis that peripheral organs may actively participate in PD progression [10].

As depicted in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway, several PD-related genes are involved in key pathological processes such as mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and impaired proteostasis (Fig. 1, Supplemental Data: Table S1). These include SNCA (encoding α-Syn), PARK2 (Parkin), DJ-1 (Parkinson’s disease protein 7), PINK1 (PTEN-induced kinase 1), Atp13a2/PARK9 (ATPase cation transporting 13a2), and LRP1 (low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor-related protein 1). In addition, genes regulating oxidative stress—such as NFe2-like basic leucine zipper transcription factor 2 (NFe2l2, encoding Nrf2)—and inflammatory response, including nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and interferon gamma (IFN-γ) have been implicated in the molecular cascade leading to neuronal degeneration [11–13]. Although the involvement of multi-organ systems in PD pathogenesis has been increasingly recognized, including multi-organ axes beyond the brain [4], comparative experimental data on PD-related gene expression in the liver and brain within a single model remain limited. In our previous study [14], we induced a PD-like phenotype in rats by prolonged low-dose ROT exposure, and then we assessed the influence of the treatment on antioxidant, inflammatory, and apoptotic markers, as well as α-Syn levels in the midbrain. The results showed bodyweight (b.w.) loss in this animal model along with behavioral deficits observed as 40% greater postural instability as compared to controls, loss of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH+) positive neurons of the substantia nigra, increased apoptosis, and decreased antioxidant defense in the midbrain [14]. In the present work, we aimed to assess whether the subchronic systemic exposure to ROT induces distinct, tissue-specific molecular responses in the liver and brain, with particular attention to PD-related oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways, in the context of the frontal lobe dysfunction described in PD [15–17]. While the substantia nigra and striatum are the most prominently affected regions in PD, cortical involvement has been increasingly recognized as a feature of both clinical PD and toxin-based models. PD also involves cortical dysfunction linked to non-motor and cognitive features, which we have previously reported [18]. Rotenone (ROT), as a systemic mitochondrial complex I inhibitor, induces widespread oxidative damage that is not confined to nigrostriatal circuits [19, 20]. The cerebral cortex is particularly relevant for studying early and diffuse molecular changes, including oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory signaling, that may precede or accompany nigrostriatal degeneration [15, 17, 21].Consistent with this rationale, ROT treatment significantly decreased dopamine (DA) and 3,4-Dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC) levels in the frontal cortex (Supplementary Data, Figure S1), indicating a dopaminergic deficit in the investigated region. Therefore, given the research findings, we examined the expression of genes involved in PD-related pathways, oxidative stress, and inflammation—SNCA, PARK2, DJ-1, ATP13a2, PINK1, LRP1, NFe2l2, NF-κB, TNF-α, and IFN-γ—in both the brain and liver.

Materials and methods

Animals

The animal experiment was performed on six-week-old male albino Wistar rats (250–300 g), bred at the Department of Toxicology, Poznan University of Medical Sciences, Poland. Animals were housed four per cage (polycarbonate, wide × long × high cm, Tecniplast, Italy) with wood shaving bedding under controlled conditions: 12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 40–54% humidity, and controlled circulation of air. Rats had ad libitum access to water and food (ISO 22000-certified chow, Labofeed H) [14, 18].

ROT-induced model experimental design

To induce PD in rats, ROT (Sigma-Aldrich, Poznań, Poland) was injected subcutaneously once daily for 35 days in a dose of 1.3 mg/kg body weight, and did not exceed 1 mL per animal during the whole experiment. ROT was dissolved in helianthin oleum raffinatum (FAGRON a.s., Olomouc, Czech Republic), as a vehicle, as previously described [18]. The ROT solution was prepared fresh immediately prior to each administration. The injection volume was calculated individually based on each animal’s body weight to deliver the intended mg/kg dose. The doses and exposure of ROT used in the present study were established based on our results from preliminary studies [14, 18]. Control animals were treated with the vehicle. 24 h after the last injection, rats were euthanized via intraperitoneal ketamine/xylazine (100 U/7.5 mg/kg b.w.), followed by cardiac perfusion with isotonic saline. Samples were collected, snap-frozen in dry ice, and stored at − 80 °C. The study complied with Polish law (Dz. U. 05.33.289), EU Directive 2010/63/EU, and was approved by the Local Ethics Committee in Poznań (14/2018,27 Apr 2018).

Sample collection

The samples were removed from the − 80 °C freezer and cut into smaller pieces using a razor blade while keeping them cold on dry ice. 100 mg of frontal cortex and the right hepatic lobe tissue were homogenized in 5 mL 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C in a homogenizer at 600 rpm for 5 min.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was isolated from homogenized tissues using Tri-RNA Extraction Reagent (GenoPlast Biochemicals, Poland), following the manufacturer’s instructions and a modified Chomczynski and Sacchi protocol [23]. Subsequently, total RNA concentration and purity were evaluated through Nanodrop (ND-1000 spectrophotometer, USA). When the ratio of absorbance at 260 nm to 280 nm was greater than 1.9 and less than 2.1, the extracted RNA had good purity. 500 ng/µl RNA was used to synthesize cDNA using the iScript Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, USA) with Oligo (dT) and random primers.

Two-step quantitative real-time PCR

A relative, two-step quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) method was applied for the evaluation of mRNA level changes for genes of interest, such as SNCA, DJ-1, ATP13a2, PARK2, LRP1, PINK1, NFe2l2, NF-κB, IFN-γ, and TNF-α. For that purpose, a set of reference genes (PBGD, encoding porphobilinogen deaminase; ACTB, encoding β-actin; and GAPDH, encoding glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) was evaluated as a potential reference gene to select the quantitatively most stable one. Primer sequences for all analyzed genes were designed using free online Primer3 v. 4.0 software (https://primer3.ut.ee/). The sequence of primers is shown in Supplemental Data, Table S2. The qPCR process was implemented by using a 5x SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Biorad, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reaction mixture compositions were as follows: 3.2 µl RNA-free water, 0.8 µl mix of starter forward and reverse (final concentration 0.8 µM of both), 5 µl SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix, 1 µl cDNA. The thermal cycling conditions were as in Supplemental Data, Table S3. The PCR reactions were conducted using a Light Cycler 480 qPCR thermocycler (Roche Applied Sciences, Germany). The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the relative expression of targeted genes. Values were expressed as fold change. All experiments were repeated at least twice.

Western blotting

Tissues were homogenized and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of total protein were separated on 8–12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Following transfer, membranes were blocked in an appropriate blocking buffer and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibodies. The following primary antibodies were used: Anti-α-Syn (1:1000; ab212184, Abcam), Anti-TNF-α (1:1000; ab1793, Abcam), Anti-DJ-1 (1:2000; ab76008, Abcam),Anti-ATP13a2 (1:1000; ab303528, Abcam), Anti-PARK2 (1:1000; ab77924, Abcam), Anti-PINK1 (1:1000; ab324681, Abcam), Anti-NFe2l2 (1:1000; ab313825, Abcam), Anti-LRP1 (1:50000; ab92544, Abcam). After incubation with the primary antibodies, membranes were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using the enhanced Invitrogen iBright Imaging System, and signal intensities were quantified using iBright analysis software. All experiments were repeated at least twice.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). All data were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) (n = 5) and analyzed by Unpaired Student t-test and two-way ANOVA with uncorrected Fisher's Least Significant Difference (LSD) test. p < 0.05 was regarded as a statistically significant difference. The reference genes were assessed using the free online RefFinder software (https://www.ciidirsinaloa.com.mx/RefFinder-master/).

Results

Selection of reference genes

To assess the stability of the selected reference genes, data were analyzed using online mathematical algorithm: RefFinder tool [24]. The most stable gene (PBDG) was selected for normalization using the mean of SD of their expression values. Cycle quantification (Cq) values from triplicate qPCR runs were used for the analysis (Supplemental Data, Figure S2).

Alterations in Parkinson’s disease-related gene expression in the liver and brain of rotenone-treated rats

In the brains of ROT-induced rats, expression levels of DJ-1 (Fig. 2A, p < 0.05), ATP13a2 (Fig. 2C, p < 0.05), PARK2 (Figure 2E, 2F, p < 0.01), SNCA (Figure 2J, p < 0.05) and LRP1 (Fig. 2K and L, p < 0.05) were significantly higher than those in the brains of control rats. The expression levels of DJ-1 (Fig. 2A and B, p < 0.05), ATP13a2 (Figure 2C, p < 0.01), SNCA (Figure 2I, 2J, p < 0.05), and PARK2 (Fig. 2E and F, p < 0.01) were significantly higher in the brains of ROT-induced rats than in the liver of ROT-induced rats. In contrast, PINK1 expression in the brains of ROT-induced rats was significantly decreased than in the liver of ROT-induced rats (Fig. 2G and H, p < 0.05). In the liver, ATP13a2 and SNCA expression levels were significantly reduced in ROT-induced rats (Fig. 2C, p < 0.05; Fig. 2I, p < 0.01). RT-qPCR did not detect increased SNCA expression (Fig. 2I, p > 0.05), whereas western blotting revealed elevated SNCA protein level in ROT-induced rat brains (Fig. 2J, p < 0.01). This result suggested that SCNA expression is primarily regulated at the translational or post-translational level rather than at transcription.

Discussion

ROT, a lipophilic inhibitor of mitochondrial complex I, impairs electron transport and promotes ROS generation. Due to its ability to cross the blood–brain barrier, chronic low-dose ROT exposure induces selective nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurodegeneration, leading to PD-like neuropathological and motor deficits through sustained mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [19]. In the present study, we investigated tissue-specific molecular responses to subchronic ROT exposure in the brain and liver by assessing genes associated with mitophagy (PARK2, DJ-1, PINK1) [25], lysosomal trafficking (ATP13a2) [26], endocytosis (LRP1) [27], and neuroinflammation (TNF-α) [25]. This study provides novel evidence for the involvement of the differential tissue-specific responses in PD pathogenesis, as demonstrated by organ-specific transcriptional responses to subchronic ROT exposure. By assessing a panel of genes implicated in mitophagy (PARK2, DJ-1, PINK1) [25], lysosomal trafficking (ATP13a2) [26], endocytosis (LRP1) [27], and neuroinflammation (TNF-α) [25]. Our findings demonstrate that ROT-induced molecular alterations are not restricted to the central nervous system (CNS), but are also detectable in the liver, highlighting distinct organ-specific responses.

Interestingly, while ATP13a2 was upregulated in the brain, its expression was significantly downregulated in the liver, suggesting distinct, organ-specific molecular responses to ROT administration. Given the role of ATP13a2 in lysosomal function and α-Syn degradation [26], its cerebral overexpression further reflects a compensatory activation of lysosomal pathways in response to α-Syn overload and oxidative stress, which we previously reported in ROT-induced rats [14]. ATP13a2 is a lysosomal P5-type ATPase located in neuronal ER, endosomal, and lysosomal membranes [28], and its dysfunction leads to impaired polyamine transport, lysosomal accumulation, and increased cell death. Silencing of ATP13a2 has been shown to induce lysosomal dysfunction and accumulation/oxidation of α-Syn [29], whereas its overexpression promotes α-Syn clearance via exosome-mediated release [30]. These findings strongly support our interpretation that increased ATP13a2 expression in the brain reflects an adaptive, neuroprotective response aimed at maintaining proteostasis under stress conditions. However, contrasting data exist regarding ATP13a2 regulation in PD models. While our results are consistent with previous in vitro and in vivo data showing that ATP13a2 activity protects against ROT-induced mitochondrial toxicity via polyamine-mediated antioxidant mechanisms [31]. Wan et al. reported downregulation of ATP13a2 in the midbrain of 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced PD mice [32], suggesting model-specific regulation or time-dependent expression dynamics.

In contrast, the significant downregulation of ATP13a2 observed in the liver may indicate compromised lysosomal capacity or regulatory suppression of lysosomal activity, possibly due to sustained metabolic stress or different ROS handling dynamics in hepatic cells. Concurrently, SNCA expression was significantly decreased in the liver of ROT-induced rats, while no significant changes were observed in the brain. This liver-specific downregulation may represent an adaptive response to impaired lysosomal degradation capacity. Given the established role of ATP13a2 in promoting α-Syn turnover [28, 33], the reduced hepatic expression of SNCA may reflect a protective feedback mechanism that downregulates α-Syn synthesis under conditions of dysfunctional proteostasis. This finding underscores the intrinsic sensitivity of the liver to proteostatic imbalance and highlights its potential role in peripheral regulation of α-Syn levels. Moreover, it supports the emerging concept that peripheral organs, including the liver, may actively coordinate with central mechanisms to modulate α-Syn homeostasis according to their degradation capacity [34].

Although α-Syn pathology has predominantly been studied in the CNS, accumulating evidence indicates that α-Syn also accumulates in peripheral tissues, particularly the liver [34]. Hepatic α-Syn deposits have been observed in both experimental models of PD and in patient liver biopsies [10], supporting the notion that liver involvement may constitute an integral component of systemic synucleinopathy. Mechanistically, α-Syn oligomers are internalized by hepatocytes via Connexin 32-dependent pathways—originally characterized in neurons and oligodendrocytes [35] and implicated in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, where elevated hepatic α-Syn expression correlates with hepatocellular degeneration [36]. Notably, a recent study identified an inverse correlation between hepatic fibrosis and cerebral α-Syn burden, suggesting a dynamic interplay between peripheral organ pathology and central synucleinopathy [37]. Altogether, these findings suggest that the liver may actively participate in regulating α-Syn homeostasis and that hepatic proteostatic adaptations—such as SNCA downregulation—could reflect systemic compensatory responses to neurodegenerative stressors.

The increased expression of PARK2 and DJ-1, core regulators of mitophagy and oxidative stress responses [38], likely reflects a shared endogenous attempt in the brain to counteract mitochondrial dysfunction induced by ROT [39]. Similar interpretations have been proposed in the literature using ROT-based models of PD. In such models, increased PARK2 expression was viewed as a compensatory mechanism aimed at enhancing mitophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria, while DJ-1 played a pivotal role in protecting cells from oxidative stress and regulating mitochondrial integrity [40, 41]. Furthermore, silencing of DJ-1 has been shown to exacerbate α-Syn accumulation and aggregation both in vitro and in vivo, while DJ-1 overexpression effectively reduced α-Syn burden [38, 42]. In line with this, overexpression of DJ-1 provided significant neuroprotection against ROT-induced neurodegeneration in a rat model of PD [43]. These findings support the notion that DJ-1 upregulation observed in our model reflects a neuroprotective, adaptive response aimed at limiting oxidative damage and maintaining proteostatic balance under mitochondrial stress.

Equally significant is the increased brain expression of LRP1 in ROT-induced rats, which parallels elevated α-Syn accumulation, demonstrated previously [14]. LRP1 is known to mediate α-Syn uptake and intercellular spread, implicating it in the propagation of synuclein pathology [11]. In postmortem PD brains, LRP1 is upregulated in the substantia nigra, suggesting involvement in disease pathogenesis [44]. Additionally, LRP1 participates in debris clearance [45], which may reflect a protective response to ROT-induced neurodegeneration. Notably, elevated serum levels of soluble LRP1 have been observed in advanced PD patients and correlated positively with disease severity, pointing to a dynamic, stage-dependent regulation of LRP1 between the brain and periphery [46]. In our study, the increase in LRP1 expression following ROT exposure likely reflects a cellular response to ongoing proteotoxic stress, with cells attempting to enhance the clearance of accumulated α-Syn and cellular debris. However, because LRP1 can also mediate α-Syn uptake and intercellular transfer, its upregulation may paradoxically facilitate the spread of pathology. Notably, α-Syn mRNA levels remained unchanged while protein levels increased, suggesting that its accumulation is driven primarily by impaired degradation or enhanced aggregation rather than increased gene expression. Thus, we interpret LRP1 elevation as a marker of ongoing pathological stress with both potential protective and detrimental (facilitated uptake/spreading) consequences.

Interestingly, despite the prominent upregulation of DJ-1 in the brain—where it likely serves as a redox-sensitive mediator of mitochondrial and antioxidant defense—no such induction was observed in the liver. In contrast, the liver displayed a significant upregulation of NFe2l2 (Nrf2), a master transcriptional regulator of the antioxidant response element pathway, which coordinates the expression of multiple cytoprotective and detoxifying genes. While DJ-1 is known to activate Nrf2 signaling by stabilizing it under oxidative stress [47], its relatively low expression in the liver of ROT-induced rats suggests that hepatic Nrf2 activation may proceed via DJ-1-independent mechanisms [48]. Taken together, the elevated expression of NFe2l2 in the liver, coupled with its unchanged expression in the brain, further supports active hepatic attempts to counter oxidative injury through enhanced expression of cytoprotective genes.

Despite the well-established role of NF-κB in mediating oxidative and inflammatory responses to neurotoxins, we observed no significant changes in NF-κB levels in either the brain or liver following ROT exposure. This result contrasts with previous reports demonstrating ROT-induced NF-κB activation, particularly in the brain, where its upregulation was linked to dopaminergic neurodegeneration and increased pro-inflammatory cytokine expression [13, 49]. Similarly, Jiang et al. reported significant increases in both NF-κB and TNF-α transcripts in the rat liver following ROT exposure [50]. Notably, in our study, we did confirm a significant upregulation of TNF-α in the brain, consistent with prior work demonstrating that ROT elicited a robust pro-inflammatory response in the CNS [13, 49]. This selective activation pattern suggests that ROT-induced neuroinflammation is primarily CNS-targeted, whereas the liver may respond differently or in a temporally distinct manner. The absence of hepatic NF-κB activation, despite oxidative challenge, may reflect an early-stage prioritization of antioxidant defenses (via Nrf2 activation) over pro-inflammatory signaling, or potentially a higher activation threshold for inflammatory transcriptional programs in peripheral tissues. This divergence may reflect differences in temporal activation thresholds, cell-type specificity, or stimulus-dependent regulation of inflammatory signaling. This selective responsiveness is in line with previous reports indicating stimulus-dependent activation of the NF-κB pathway [51, 52].

Of particular significance is the differential regulation of PINK1, which expression in the brain was significantly lower than in the liver in ROT-induced rats. In control animals, PINK1 expression was comparable between the two organs. This is noteworthy given that PINK1 is a key upstream regulator of mitophagy, and its downregulation has been associated with impaired mitochondrial quality control in previous PD models [53]. This tissue-specific divergence is notable given PINK1’s role in sensing mitochondrial damage and recruiting Parkin to initiate selective autophagic degradation of dysfunctional mitochondria [54, 55]. Under physiological conditions, PINK1 is constitutively degraded following mitochondrial import and proteolytic processing. In damaged mitochondria, however, it accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane, acting as a molecular signal for Parkin activation [54]. In our study, the significantly reduced PINK1 expression in the brain of ROT-exposed animals may reflect impaired initiation of the mitophagy cascade, limiting the capacity for clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria. This is particularly relevant in the context of repeated ROT exposure, which has been shown to impair mitophagy and contribute to dopaminergic neuron degeneration [20]. These findings align with previous reports indicating that disruption of the PINK1/Parkin pathway plays a central role in the pathogenesis of PD [56, 57]. In contrast, PINK1 expression in the liver was not significantly altered by ROT exposure, yet remained significantly higher than in the brain of the same animals. This organ-specific pattern may indicate differential sensitivity to mitochondrial insults or a preserved capacity for mitophagy-related signaling in the liver. These findings highlight tissue-specific regulation of mitochondrial homeostasis genes and support the hypothesis that the brain and liver respond to systemic toxic insults via distinct molecular mechanisms. The relatively elevated hepatic PINK1 expression may represent a compensatory response aimed at maintaining mitochondrial function and preserving peripheral cellular integrity—potentially modulating systemic metabolic or inflammatory signals that, in turn, influence central neurodegeneration.

Conclusion

This study presents relevant and novel contributions to the understanding of neurodegeneration in PD by highlighting distinct molecular responses in the brain and liver following ROT exposure. Our findings demonstrate that these organs respond differently to the same toxic insult, with evidence suggesting organ-specific impairment of mitophagy in the brain, while mitophagy-related signaling appears to be relatively preserved or more effectively regulated in the liver. In addition, the upregulation of NFe2l2 in the liver compared with control rats supports a stronger activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms in peripheral tissues. Furthermore, although increased TNF-α expression was observed in the brain compared with the liver of ROT-induced rats, no activation of NF-κB was detected in either organ, indicating distinct inflammatory profiles between central and peripheral tissues. We also observed reduced SNCA expression in the liver alongside increased α-Syn protein levels in the brain, suggesting that peripheral tissues may differentially regulate α-Syn homeostasis during ROT-induced pathology. Importantly, these findings reflect tissue-specific supporting the concept that PD involves systemic alterations extending beyond the central nervous system, with peripheral organs such as the liver exhibiting distinct molecular adaptations.

Limitation

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the liver and brain were analyzed independently; our data do not address inter-organ communication or causal relationships between peripheral and central molecular changes. In addition, the study primarily relied on gene and protein expression analyses, which may not directly correspond to alterations in cellular function, metabolic activity, or signaling processes. Functional and mechanistic studies will be necessary to determine the physiological relevance of the observed molecular changes. Furthermore, analyses were limited to selected tissues and did not address broader regional or cell-type–specific responses that may contribute to ROT-induced pathology. Taken together, our findings should be considered descriptive and exploratory, providing insight into distinct tissue-specific molecular adaptations in the liver and brain during ROT- induced PD-like pathology.
