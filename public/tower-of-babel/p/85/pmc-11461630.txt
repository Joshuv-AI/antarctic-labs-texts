The relationship between radiomorphometric indices and fractal dimension analysis: a cone-beam computed tomography study
Authors: Efie Mariyam Nursari, Bramma Kiswanjaya, Aloysius Putut Wijanarko, Menik Priaminiarti, Hanna H. Bachtiar-Iskandar, Akihiro Yoshihara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this study is to evaluate the relationship between radiomorphometric indices and fractal dimension (FD) analysis in jawbone radiographic density using Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT). The study included 87 CBCT datasets from dental patients aged 50–79. Four radiomorphometric indices (Computed Tomography Cortical Index (CTCI), Computed Tomography Mental Index (CTMI), Computed Tomography Index-Superior (CTI-S), and Computed Tomography Index-Inferior (CTI-I)) along with age, gender, remaining teeth, and mandibular bone height were evaluated. FD was assessed in trabecular bone near the mental foramen (ROI 1) and on the mandibular cortical bone (ROI 2). Relationships between indices and variables were analyzed using Pearson chi-square, Fisher’s exact, one-way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, and Spearman’s rho tests. ANOVA showed significant differences in mandibular bone height (p = .004) and FD at ROI 2 (p = .000) across three mandibular cortex types (C1, C2, C3). Spearman’s rho indicated significant correlations between FD at ROI 2 and CTMI, CTI-S, CTI-I (p = .000), and between mandibular bone height and CTMI (p = .010). The correlations of FD at ROI 2 ranged from strongest to weakest with CTCI, CTMI, CTI-S, and CTI-I. High-risk osteoporosis radiomorphometric indices are associated with FD analysis of cortical bone.

Introduction

Studies have documented osteoporosis-related oral radiographic examinations on two-dimensional radiographs over the past 40 years. These studies focused on panoramic radiographic examinations, and the results showed evidence of a decrease in bone density and morphological changes in the jaws of osteoporosis patients1−3. Although the use of CBCT in dentistry has increased, its use in assessing bone quality requires further improvement4,5. Using CBCT for assessing bone density in the mandible for osteoporosis diagnosis is still an area of ongoing research and debate. While it shows promise, further studies are needed to validate its effectiveness and establish standardized protocols for its use in clinical practice. Additionally, concerns regarding radiation dose and cost may limit its widespread adoption for osteoporosis screening compared to panoramic radiography.

The first study to adopt CBCT to assess bone mineral density (BMD) was published by Koh6. The indices used were modified based on measurements from panoramic radiographs based on the studies of Klemetti1 and Ledgerton2 to assess mandibular cortical bone morphology and CBCT-derived volume. Koh compared these indices with dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA), the gold standard for BMD examination, and found that the radiomorphometric indices could be used to assess women with osteoporosis6.

Fractal dimension (FD) analysis has been used to assess trabecular bone structure and the biomechanical quality of various bones. Previous studies have stated that research on radiomorphometric indices and dimension fractals using CBCT still needs to be completed7. FD analysis research using CBCT has produced heterogeneous results4,8. Studies have also used different FD assessment methods and investigated different regions of interest (ROIs) in terms of shape, size, and location9−11. Furthermore, the limitations of previous studies are due to differences in the radiomorphometric index method and their relatively small sample sizes6,9,12−15. Previous studies have recommended a minimum sample size of 25 samples for each group and a slice thickness of 25 mm, as this allows for the inclusion of more image slices and closely resembles a two-dimensional panoramic radiograph4,16.

Since no studies have analyzed FDs in mandibular cortical bone using the CBCT modality17, research is needed to evaluate the relationship between radiomorphometric examination and FD analysis. The study was designed to evaluate the hypothesis that FD analysis of ROIs on cortical bone would exhibit a statistically significant correlation with radiomorphometric indices. Thus, this study objective is to evaluate the relationship between radiomorphometric indices and FD analysis in the radiographic density of the jawbone related to BMD using CBCT. We also evaluate the risk factors that might affect the radiographic density of the jawbone, including age, gender, number of remaining teeth, and mandibular bone height.

Materials and methods

Ethical issues

The Dentistry Research Ethics Commission’s research protocol was accepted by the Faculty of Dentistry, Universitas Indonesia (protocol No. 050690722), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant provided informed consent to the use of their data. The human ethics and consent to participate declaration was achieved through a clear written consent, which informed patients about the procedure and the use of radiographs for scientific purposes. This consent was obtained to ensure that patients were fully aware of their participation in the study.

Data selection

A total of 91 digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) CBCT file datasets were obtained from two hospitals: 52 samples from the Dental Hospital Faculty of Dentistry at the University of Indonesia and 39 from Pondok Indah Hospital in Jakarta, Indonesia. The decision to include CBCT images from two different centers was made to meet the required sample size, as the number of eligible samples from a single center was insufficient. Both centers use the same CBCT device—the CS New 9,300 3D Digital Imaging System from Carestream Dental—ensuring consistency in image acquisition and quality across both locations. Demographic data related to gender and age were recorded according to the inclusion criteria, which were male and female patients aged 50–79 years. The CBCT dataset volume was then evaluated according to the quality criteria and field of view (FOV). The exclusion criteria included CBCT images with extensive jaw lesions that might impact interpretation, jaw fractures, or bone-affecting diseases present at the time of the patient’s admission. Additionally, images with high artifact levels due to dental materials in adjacent teeth were excluded, as artifacts can significantly affect the accuracy of the research results. Cases with severe alveolar ridge resorption, where accurately identifying the mental foramen is challenging, were also excluded to ensure the accuracy of the radiomorphometric index evaluation. Four CBCT datasets were excluded because the ROI was truncated, so a total of 87 CBCT datasets were included in the study.

Acquisition of radiographic images

All DICOM CBCT file datasets were obtained from CBCT with the CS New 9,300 3D Digital Imaging System from Carestream Dental, which has voxel sizes of 90–500 μm. The FOV selected according to the inclusion criteria was FOV 17 × 13 and 10 × 10. Reconstruction images and axial, coronal, and sagittal sections from CBCT using a laptop with CS Imaging Patient Browser 7.0.23 and CS 3D Imaging v3.8.7 software from Carestream Health Inc. These two software components are integrated as part of the same system provided by Carestream Health Inc. The Patient Browser, which is the initial window displayed upon launching CS Imaging Version 7 in stand-alone mode, allows access to all patient card functions. Subsequently, the CS 3D Imaging software is employed for viewing and analyzing the 3D images. The specifications of the laptop used for viewing and analyzing the CBCT images were as follows: HP Pavilion Gaming Laptop 15-ec1xxx, 64-bit, equipped with an AMD Ryzen 5 4600 H @ 3.00 GHz processor (1 processor, 6 cores, 12 threads) and 16.00 GB DDR4 SDRAM. The software integration ensures a seamless workflow for managing patient data and conducting image analysis. Utilizing this unified software system enabled us to maintain consistency in both patient data management and imaging analysis throughout the study.

Radiomorphometric examination

The radiomorphometric indices used were adapted from Koh’s studies10 consisting of Computed Tomography Cortical Index (CTCI), Computed Tomography Mental Index (CTMI), Computed Tomography Index-Superior (CTI-S), and Computed Tomography Index-Inferior (CTI-I). The CTCI is an index that assesses the morphology of the mandibular inferior cortex and is classified using the Klemetti classification1. This index was evaluated using panoramic reconstruction CBCT, which was assessed visually from the bilateral mental foramen to the molar region. If there is a difference in classification between the right and left sides, then the side with the more severe change is used for classification. To obtain a panoramic reconstruction view of the CBCT, a panoramic curved slicing curve was drawn in the axial plane with a minimum slice thickness of 300 μm. The mental foramen was identified in the most clearly visible slice and the widest mesiodistal distance, and then a line was drawn in the middle of the mandible. A slice thickness of ± 30 mm was chosen for panoramic CBCT reconstruction. CTCI, as shown in Fig. 1, was assessed subjectively using the following scoring criteria: C1 (cortical endosteal margins were even and sharp on both sides of the mandible), C2 (endosteal margins had a semilunar defect (resorptive cavity) with cortical residue one to three layers thick on either or both sides of the mandible, and C3 (endosteal margins consisting of the thick residual cortex and porous).

The CTMI is the cortical width of the inferior mandible in the area of the mental foramen and is measured in the coronal plane by measuring the width of the mandibular cortex on a line perpendicular to the tangent line of the inferior border of the mandible in the region of the mental foramen. Measurements were taken on both sides of the mandible, and the average was assessed (Fig. 2A).

The Computed Tomography Index-Superior (CTI-S) represents the ratio of the width of the inferior cortex to the distance from the mental foramen’s upper margin to the mandible’s lower border. The CTI was measured in the coronal plane. “S” is the distance from the superior margin of the mental foramen to the inferior border of the mandible, and “W” is the width of the inferior cortex of the mandible such that CTI-S = W/S (Fig. 2B).

The Computed Tomography Index-Inferior (CTI-I) represents the ratio of the width of the inferior cortex to the distance from the mental foramen’s lower margin to the mandible’s lower border. The CTI was measured in the coronal plane. “I” is the distance from the inferior margin to the inferior border of the mandible, and “W” is the width of the inferior cortex of the mandible such that CTI-I = W/I (Fig. 2C).

Mandibular bone height and number of remaining teeth

The panoramic CBCT reconstruction obtained to assess the CTCI was then used to measure mandibular bone height. The vertical line measured from the alveolar crest through the center of the mental foramen is perpendicular to the lower border of the mandibular inferior cortex (Fig. 3). Measurements were taken on both sides of the mental foramen, and then the average was assessed. The number of remaining teeth analyzed, including the third molars, was counted via panoramic CBCT reconstruction, excluding the remaining roots and teeth indicated for extraction.

Fractal dimension analysis

FD was assessed from the same coronal section used to measure the CTMI, CTI-S, and CTI-I and consists of two ROIs (Fig. 4): ROI 1 (a 3 × 3 mm square shape in the trabecular bone region between the mental foramen and the upper border of the mandibular cortical bone) and ROI 2 (a 3 × 3 mm square shape at the inferior border of the mandibular cortical bone). Each image was saved as a TIFF file, and the FD analysis was performed using ImageJ version 1.53t, which is public domain and was downloaded at http://imagej.nih.gov/ij. All stages of the FD analysis were carried out using the box-counting method described by White and Rudolph19 in the order presented in Fig. 5.

Statistical analysis

A reliability test was conducted on 30 randomly selected research samples two weeks after the first calculation. The intra- and interobserver reliability assessments were performed by two dentomaxillofacial radiologists. The first observer, who is the first author, has 3 years of experience in dentomaxillofacial radiology, while the second observer, a co-author, has 16 years of experience in the field. Both radiologists have extensive expertise in interpreting cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and conducting radiomorphometric measurements. Intra- and interobserver tests on the CTCI data were carried out using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, and for FD analysis at ROI 2 (cortical) using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

The sample was divided into three age groups: Group I (aged 50–59 years), Group II (aged 60–69 years), and Group III (aged 70–79 years). These were in turn divided into four groups based on their number of remaining teeth: Group I (25–32 teeth), Group II (17–24 teeth), Group III (9–16 teeth), and Group IV (1–8 teeth). Samples were also divided according to CTCI criteria based on three mandibular cortical types: C1, C2, and C3. The gender distribution was measured using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To determine the gender distribution of the mandibular bone height, CTMI, CTI-S, and CTI-I variables, as well as the FD value on ROI 1 and ROI 2, an independent t-test or Mann–Whitney test was carried out.

Statistical analysis was then performed to assess the relationship between radiomorphometric indices and all age, gender, and number of remaining teeth using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test was used for mandibular bone height, as well as FD ROI 1 and ROI 2. Finally, Spearman’s rho statistical analysis was performed to assess the correlation between FD analysis and age, gender, number of remaining teeth, and radiomorphometric indices. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 22 for Windows (IBM Corporation).

Results

The findings of the intra- and interobserver tests on the CTCI data using Cohen’s Kappa were 0.845 (almost perfect agreement) and 0.740 (substantial agreement), respectively. For the FD analysis tests using ICC, they were 0.934 and 0.861, respectively. In the FD analysis, both observers were found to be in almost perfect agreement. The measurements were determined to be consistent and were applied equally.

The gender distribution of the subjects is shown in Table 1. The total sample (87 patients) consisted of 55 men and 32 women, with the largest age group being 50–59 years (25 men, 22 women). Means and standard deviations in men and women were 60.80 ± 6.93 years old and 58.41 ± 6.69 years old, respectively. Mandibular bone height was significantly higher in men than in women (p = .003, Mann–Whitney test). Meanwhile, the radiomorphometric indices and FD values for both ROIs showed no statistically significant differences between men and women.

Discussion

This study explored the relationship between radiomorphometric indices from panoramic CBCT reconstruction and FD values in trabecular and cortical bone ROIs using CBCT. Results revealed a significant correlation between the four radiomorphometric indices (CTCI, CTMI, CTI-S, and CTI-I) and cortical bone FD values, particularly in patients classified with a CTCI of C3, indicating porous cortical bone. While FD values in trabecular bone showed no statistical significance, research on using FD for osteoporosis detection via CBCT remains scarce, primarily focusing on trabecular bone and yielding heterogeneous results. Our study is the first to utilize CBCT to evaluate radiomorphometric indices and FD analysis in trabecular and cortical ROIs. Additionally, our findings contrast with previous research by Sindeaux et al., who observed lower cortical bone FD values in osteoporosis patients20. This difference may be due to the image processing method not including the inversion stage in Sindeaux et al.’s study, which resulted in a significant measurement difference. In our study, inversion was applied after binarization to enhance the accuracy of the FD measurement. The inversion step is critical because it allows for a clearer representation of the trabecular bone structure, which can otherwise be obscured by image noise or inconsistencies in pixel intensity. By inverting the image, we focus on the structural complexity of the bone, highlighting the porous areas, which are essential for assessing the fractal dimension. This method has been widely adopted in studies that evaluate bone microarchitecture because it ensures a more consistent identification of trabecular patterns, which is crucial for accurate FD analysis6,21. Moreover, inversion has been shown to improve the contrast between the bone and surrounding tissues, allowing for more precise delineation of the regions of interest (ROIs).Given that FD reflects the complexity and heterogeneity of the bone structure, a method that enhances contrast and structural clarity, like inversion, is essential for reliable measurements. FD analysis, reflecting structural complexity, indicates that increased FD values correlate with more porous cortical bone, signifying higher complexity in the image6,21,22.

This study found significant correlations between mandibular bone height and the CTCI and CTMI radiomorphometric indices, as well as between the number of remaining teeth and the CTMI and CTI-S indices. Previous research has consistently shown a link between osteoporosis and tooth loss23−25, with osteoporotic women being three times more likely to experience tooth loss compared to their non-osteoporotic counterparts in the same age range23,25. Despite age and gender being recognized as risk factors for osteoporosis, this study did not identify significant relationships between these factors and the radiomorphometric indices or FD values in both regions of interest (ROIs). Challenges included an unequal distribution of male and female subjects, with males outnumbering females. However, efforts were made to mitigate this issue by including more than 25 samples from each gender group, as recommended by previous research4, and ensuring a minimum sample size of 87. Additionally, the lack of available clinical data, such as osteoporosis history, weight, height, smoking habits, and lifestyle factors, which are known risk factors for osteoporosis, may have influenced the results. Further research with a more balanced distribution of patients across age and gender groups is warranted to better understand the relationship between osteoporosis risk factors and radiomorphometric indices and fractal dimensions.

This study also evaluated age, gender, tooth loss, and mandibular bone height and their relationship with FD value on trabecular and cortical ROI through CBCT examination. The results of the correlation test show that there was no significant relationship between these variables. This study used two different FOVs to measure FD in the study sample—17 × 13 and 10 × 10—which included the maxilla and mandible according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. FD analysis via radiograph has been used to evaluate trabecular bone structure and the biomechanical properties of various bones22. Altered bone microarchitecture due to osteoporosis is seen in decreased thickness and number of trabeculae and increased spacing between trabeculae22,26,27. Measurement of bone microstructure via CBCT is affected by the size of the FOV in terms of image resolution and sharpness, and it is advisable to use a smaller FOV to measure thin bone structures22,28,29. A three-dimensional image’s smallest identifiable box-shaped part is referred to as a “voxel.” A larger FOV typically results in decreased resolution because it contains larger voxels for a fixed number of pixels30. For the majority of bone characteristics, voxel size had a significant impact on image quality. This is consistent with the trabecular bone losing details due to a “merging” of adjacent trabeculae caused by decreased spatial resolution at larger voxel sizes [27]. This challenge requires further research to measure FD analysis through CBCT modality by using a smaller FOV to obtain image detail and sharpness in analyzing FD and using a uniform FOV in all study samples to avoid bias.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, it relied solely on retrospective data from dental radiology records, aimed to explore the correlation between fractal dimension (FD) analysis and osteoporosis radiomorphometric indices. Secondly, we did not directly measure the clinical status or bone mineral density (BMD) using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) for osteoporosis assessment. FD analysis was used to investigate fractal regions in cortical and trabecular bone, assessing correlations with radiomorphometric indices. While significant relationships were found between FD analysis on trabecular bone and osteoporosis radiomorphometric indices, their diagnostic value for low BMD remained limited. Thus, the study did not focus on assessing FD analysis for diagnostic accuracy, highlighting the need for further research incorporating patient clinical status and DXA values.

In conclusion, our study revealed a statistically significant correlation between the cortical bone FD values and the four radiomorphometric indices evaluated (CTCI, CTMI, CTI-S, and CTI-I). The null hypothesis, which stated that there is no difference in correlation between cortical bone FD values and the four radiomorphometric indices (CTCI, CTMI, CTI-S, CTI-I), was rejected. The findings showed a significant correlation between FD values of cortical bone ROIs and these four radiomorphometric indices, but not with ROIs on mandibular trabecular bone. In this study, the high risk of osteoporosis radiomorphometric indices is associated with the fractal dimension (FD) analysis specifically conducted on cortical bone.
