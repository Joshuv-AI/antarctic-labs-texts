Evaluating diagnostic performance of urinary biomarkers in the surveillance of non-muscle invasive bladder carcinoma: A systematic review
Authors: Reena Prhiya Ramasamy, Chu Ann Chai, Angelo Cormio, Retnagowri Rajandram, Daniele Castellani, Wei Shen Tan, Vineet Gauhar, See Tong Pang, Paramananthan Mariappan, Jeremy Yuen Chun Teoh, Teng Aik Ong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeUrinary biomarkers have been proposed to play an integral role in detecting recurrent non-muscle invasive bladder carcinoma (NMIBC). This review delineates the diagnostic accuracy of such biomarkers and management strategies in the event of discordance between biomarker findings and flexible cystoscopy, the current gold standard for NMIBC surveillance.Materials and MethodsIn accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement, literature searches were conducted across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and CENTRAL (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials), focusing on studies comparing urinary biomarkers to cystoscopy. The inclusion criteria were based on the PICOS (Patient Intervention Comparison Outcome Study type) model. The quality of included studies was assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool.ResultsA total of 23 studies were included, encompassing 21 types of urinary biomarkers. The analysis revealed significant variability in diagnostic performance. Sensitivity ranged from 14.8% to 94.3%, specificity from 33.3% to 99.2%, positive predictive value from 9.9% to 100%, and negative predictive value from 35.3% to 98.8%. Heterogeneity was observed across studies, indicating variability in different biomarker performance. Genetic biomarkers demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy compared to protein-based markers. However, their clinical utility needs further validation.ConclusionsUrinary biomarkers could complement, rather than replace cystoscopy, in the surveillance of NMIBC, potentially reducing the frequency of invasive procedures. In cases of discordance, an algorithm focusing on shared decision-making and tailored surveillance strategies can be undertaken. Large-scale multicentric studies are needed to confirm their efficacy and establish standardized clinical protocols.Keywords: Biomarkers, Cystoscopy, Non-muscle invasive bladder cancer, Systematic review, Urine

INTRODUCTION

Bladder cancer is among the ten most common malignancies globally, with non-muscle invasive bladder carcinoma (NMIBC) making up 70%–75% of all cases [1,2]. Despite advances in diagnostics, therapeutics, adjuvant treatment as well as surveillance techniques, the high risk of recurrence (up to 50%) and progression (up to 20%) in NMIBC remain a significant challenge to all healthcare systems, necessitating long-term surveillance cystoscopy, at the very least [3,4].

Urinary biomarkers are putative non-invasive alternatives for NMIBC surveillance. These biomarkers, including DNA, RNA, and protein-based assays, show promise in improving the detection of recurrence, progression and monitoring of bladder cancer [5]. However, the benchmarks that must be achieved for these non-invasive modalities to replace cystoscopy are comparable levels of diagnostic accuracy [6]. The perceived discordance between the accuracies of urinary biomarkers and cystoscopy would inevitably raise questions about their clinical utility and reliability [7,8]. Furthermore, the rising healthcare cost and impact on quality of life (QoL) with surveillance in an aging population necessitates reasonable clarity around the clinical utility of urinary biomarkers in this context.

Therefore, to evaluate the diagnostic performance of urinary biomarkers and establish the extent of discordance between urinary biomarkers and cystoscopy in the setting of NMIBC surveillance, we conducted a systematic review of recent studies on this topic. Additionally, we looked into their management options in the event of discordance, for urinary biomarkers to enhance NMIBC surveillance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Aim of the review and literature search

We conducted a systematic review to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy between urinary biomarkers and cystoscopy in the surveillance of NMIBC. The primary objective was to determine whether the diagnostic accuracy varied among urinary biomarkers and cystoscopy. The secondary objective was to review the approach on discordance and its management options in such cases. We defined discordance as: (a) Cystoscopy positive – referring to visible evidence of bladder cancer, such as papillary tumors, flat lesions suspicious for carcinoma in situ (CIS) with concomitant negative biomarkers. (b) A positive urinary biomarker and a concomitant negative cystoscopy.

This systematic review adhered to the 2020 PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) framework.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted on September 17, 2024, using PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and CENTRAL (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials). We employed MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms and keywords such as (“bladder cancer” OR “urothelial carcinoma” OR “bladder neoplasm” OR “bladder tumor”) AND (“urinary biomarkers” OR “urine biomarkers” OR “urinary markers”) AND (“cystoscopy”) without any date restriction. The search was limited to English-language articles, and animal and pediatric studies were excluded. Additional articles were identified through reference lists of the included studies.

2. Selection criteria

The PICOS (Patient Intervention Comparison Outcome Study type) model was used to frame and answer clinical questions. P – adult patients with patients with NMIBC on surveillance; I – any urinary biomarker; C – cystoscopy surveillance; O – discordance between urinary biomarker and cystoscopy.

3. Study screening and selection

Two independent investigators screened all retrieved records through Covidence Systematic Review Management (Veritas Health Innovation). Discrepancies were solved by a third investigator. Studies were accepted based on PICOS eligibility criteria. Reviews, case reports, meeting abstracts, editorials, and letters to the editor were excluded. The full text of the screened articles was selected if they were relevant to the purpose of this review. The quality assessment of the included studies was performed using the QUADAS-2 [9].

4. Statistical analysis

All selected studies were reviewed. Relevant data was then extracted and tabulated as per Table 1. The accuracy and performance of tests and cystoscopy were described with: (1) sensitivity, (2) specificity, (3) positive predictive value (PPV), and (4) negative predictive value (NPV).

The remaining 367 papers were screened against title and abstract and 331 papers were further excluded because they were not suitable for the present review. The remaining 36 full-text papers were screened for pertinence and 13 papers were excluded. Finally, 23 papers were accepted and included. Eight studies showed a low overall risk of bias, eleven studies had some concern regarding overall risk of bias and the remaining four had a high overall risk of bias. The most common reason for the risks of bias were the flow and timing of index test administration, followed by selection bias.

RESULTS

Literature review yielded 23 publications relevant to the subject under study, which included 21 types of urinary biomarkers. They were then classified in accordance with their type; namely exfoliated cells (similar in principle to urinary cytology), protein, mRNA and DNA [7]. All studies were prospective in nature, and they were all compared to the gold standard method of surveillance, i.e., cystoscopy.

Table 1 outlines the various types of urinary biomarkers and their respective mechanisms of action in the detection of recurrent bladder cancer. Tables 2 and 3 provide a detailed summary of various studies evaluating the diagnostic performance of the different urinary biomarkers used in bladder cancer detection. The biomarkers are categorized based on the type of test—exfoliated cells, protein, mRNA, and DNA—and their diagnostic metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV.

The studies highlight a wide range of sensitivity and specificity values across different biomarkers. For instance, CellDetect shows a sensitivity of 66.7%, while the ADXBLADDER test exhibits a sensitivity range from 35.4% to 73.5% and specificities from 33.3% to 76.0%. The UBC test results also vary significantly, with sensitivity values between 14.8% to 83.9% and specificities between 79.4% to 97.5%. This variation indicates the differing effectiveness of each biomarker in detecting bladder cancer. The data also reveal variable accuracy with these biomarkers. For example, the Bladder EpiCheck test shows high sensitivity in some studies, such as 94.3% in Pierconti et al. [22], but lower sensitivity, like 62.3%, in Trenti et al. [24]. On the other hand, the Uromonitor test by Ramos et al. [28] demonstrates both high sensitivity (87.2%) and specificity (99.2%).

DISCUSSION

Urinary biomarker utilization has the potential to enhance diagnosis, surveillance, and risk stratification for NMIBC. Numerous molecular marker tests have been developed to improve accuracy, with some receiving FDA approval [8,33]. However, despite these advancements, no urinary biomarker has been incorporated into clinical follow-up and surveillance algorithms recommended by guidelines [33,34,35,36].

Biomarkers are categorized into cellular, protein, and genomic markers. Genomic markers consist of mRNA and DNA markers. They offer reliability, ease of use, and objectivity [37,38]. They perform significantly better in bladder cancer due to their ability to detect multiple loci accurately and simultaneously, compared to the lower-throughput protein-based biomarkers. This is important because biomarkers interrogating multiple loci have been shown to outperform single target biomarkers [39]. However, aberrant DNA methylation is not exclusive to malignant tissues and cannot distinguish between cancerous and pre- or non-neoplastic cells [37]. Nevertheless, genomic biomarkers have the advantage of occurring consistently in the same DNA locations and their chemical stability makes them easier to detect than gene mutations. Protein-based and cell-based biomarkers are more susceptible to being influenced by benign conditions such as infections, inflammation, and bladder treatments [22].

Currently, surveillance for NMIBC comprises cystoscopy, without or with urinary cytology (for high and very high risk NMIBC) and periodic upper tract imaging [34,35]. Nevertheless, it is important to appreciate that cystoscopy and enhanced cystoscopy invasive, can adversely affect the QoL and is operator dependent with a sensitivity of approximately 98% [40]. Voided urine cytology, on the other hand, requires trained cytopathologists and is subject to interobserver variability interpretation as per the Paris System [41].

Researchers developing urinary biomarkers aim for high sensitivity and high NPV. This profile is particularly important in follow-up scenarios, as the goal of these tests is ultimately to reduce the number of cystoscopies during the surveillance of urothelial cancer by alternating procedures if not eliminating them. A positive urine test would prompt a cystoscopy (urine-first strategy), thus minimizing the frequency of invasive procedures while ensuring that necessary examinations are not overlooked [42]. Particularly in patients who were previously tested and were biomarker negative. These patients would be ideal candidates to utilize this surveillance strategy. On the other hand, those with previous biomarker positivity may not be suitable for this approach. As cystoscopy is not 100% sensitive and operator dependent, the expectation is that urinary biomarkers would minimise missed significant recurrences by augmenting cystoscopy. This however works best if the biomarker has a high specificity to minimize the risk of false positives.

This approach could enhance cost-effectiveness, while maintaining the rigour of surveillance [42,43]. The cost of running a urinary biomarker test is variable but is reported to be at least 5 to 10 times cheaper than performing a cystoscope, after consideration of additional logistical, operating theatre and staffing arrangements [11].

Our systematic review collectively demonstrated that several urinary biomarkers, including Uromonitor, Bladder EpiCheck, and Xpert Bladder Cancer Monitor show high sensitivity and specificity for detecting bladder cancer recurrence. These biomarkers offer reliable, non-invasive alternatives to standard cystoscopy, providing significant benefits in terms of patient comfort and compliance [10]. Their robust performance across multiple studies highlights their potential for routine clinical use in monitoring NMIBC [21,23,44,45].

However, our review also highlighted the heterogeneity of these urinary biomarkers, with considerable variation in their diagnostic performance. Biomarkers such as CellDetect and ADXBLADDER have demonstrated moderate sensitivity and specificity, while others like UBC Rapid and NMP22 show varied results across different studies [12,26].

Our review showed that low detection rates are found particularly for early-stage and low-grade tumours, accounting for a significant proportion of recurrences [10,11,12,17,23]. This could be due to the difficulty in obtaining sufficiently large quantities of high-quality RNA, DNA, exfoliated cells or protein from voided urine. Some have also reported that this may be due to small amount of tumour present at initial diagnosis [13,28]. In order to overcome this, some studies retested the samples with double filtration while others repeated the test with samples taken during TURBT (trans-urethral resection of bladder tumor), which yielded a positive result, supporting the hypotheses mentioned above [16,20,28].

Furthermore, the method of microbial DNA extraction is a critical determinant of test performance. Protocols utilizing water dilution have demonstrated superior efficacy compared to standard and chelation-assisted methods. This improvement is attributed to the incorporation of ultra-pure distilled water, which increases the overall sample volume and enhances DNA recovery, thereby optimizing the efficiency of DNA extraction. Given the limitations, several studies have adopted a composite approach [30]. Combining multiple urinary biomarkers can significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy, as demonstrated by studies like the European FP7 UROMOL project [46]. Multi-marker panels, such as those combining FGFR3, TERT, and OTX1, leverage the strengths of individual biomarkers to provide a more comprehensive assessment of bladder cancer recurrence [24,29,32]. In addition, novel statistical approaches using machine learning may aid in maximizing both sensitivity and specificity of multi-loci biomarkers although prospective validation is required [47]. This approach improves overall diagnostic reliability and can lead to more precise and effective surveillance strategies [31].

Another limitation of urinary biomarkers are equivocal results, despite appropriate collection and utilization of standardised test kits. In these instances, it remains vital to rely on the gold standard investigation. This discordance has been explained by anticipatory positivity [15]. It is accepted in the literature that anticipatory detection would include recurrences that occur within the next 18 months after a positive biomarker test [14,35,48]. Consequently, Trenti et al. [25] recommended to conduct more frequent surveillance using urinary biomarkers before the next cystoscopy, possibly as early as eight weeks, to ensure early detection and timely intervention. This highlights the role of urinary biomarkers as effective adjuncts in the detection of recurrence rather than completely replacing cystoscopy.

Finally, after reviewing the reported limitations and suggested mitigations, a systematic approach may be utilised for addressing discordance, as outlined in Supplementary Fig. 2. All patients who are diagnosed with bladder carcinoma are to be risk stratified according to the EAU NMIBC Risk Calculator [49]. As our review suggests low detection rates amongst urinary biomarkers particularly for earlystage and low-grade tumours, we do not propose its utilisation among those stratified as low risk NMIBC. This approach, even though not based on strong available evidence, outlines the least possible risk in a given cancer type and at the same time emphasizes the importance of careful patient counselling and shared decision making. For example, ultrasound of the kidney ureter and bladder (KUB) may be used in investigating low grade cancers first as the presence hydronephrosis may raise the concern of possible upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC). However, in high-risk group, if there was the presence of a high grade UTUC driving a positive biomarker, an upfront contrasted computed tomography urography may be the preferred investigation.

This review has several limitations. Firstly, the inclusion of some studies with high risk of bias. Secondly, we were not able to perform a cost-effectiveness analysis which should balance between the cost of the biomarker and the number of follow-up cystoscopies. Finally, we have currently no strong data to show significant superiority of one biomarker over another.

CONCLUSIONS

Urinary biomarkers offer promising diagnostic value in current clinical practice, potentially enhancing bladder cancer surveillance and decision-making protocols. However, their reliability requires further validation through largescale studies. In the event of discordance between surveillance cystoscopy and urinary biomarkers, a structured approach may assist in guiding the shared decision making with patients, emphasizing the integration of urinary biomarkers into current practices to improve patients’ outcomes.
