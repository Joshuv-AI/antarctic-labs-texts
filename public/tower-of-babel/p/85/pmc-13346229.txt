Bioinformatics screening identifies USP9X as a pathogenic gene underlying thyroid and breast cancer comorbidity
Authors: Siping Xiong, Yimin Guo, Miao Zhang, Shijun Sun, Yanghui Wei, Chao Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe co-occurrence of thyroid cancer (TC) and breast cancer (BC) has attracted much attention in recent years. The aim of the study was to explore the potential molecular mechanisms in the comorbidity of TC and BC. We identified the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for TC and BC using the TCGA database and screened the common driver genes for the comorbidity of TC and BC. Immunohistochemical staining was performed to verify the expression of the common gene (USP9X) in double primary cancers (co-occurrence of TC and BC). Migration, invasion and colony formation assays were used to evaluate the role of USP9X in TC and BC cells in vitro. In the present study, we confirmed 8 driver genes shared by TC and BC through bioinformatics analysis. In samples with co-occurring TC and BC, USP9X immunoreactivity was detected in 100% (14/14) TC and 100% (14/14) BC samples. Interfering with USP9X expression could inhibit the colony formation, migration and invasion abilities of TC and BC cells. Protein-protein interaction network analysis revealed USP9X/MCL-1 and USP9X/NUAK1 might be molecular pathways involved in the comorbidity of TC and BC. Our findings suggest that USP9X overexpression is a molecular event involved in the comorbidity of TC and BC and it could be a promising therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10735-026-10882-4.Keywords: USP9X, Comorbidity, Thyroid cancer, Breast cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC), the most common malignancy among women worldwide, accounted for 11.6% of newly diagnosed cancer cases in 2022 (Bray et al. 2024). The incidence of thyroid cancer (TC) is also on the rise, currently ranking as one of the most common cancers affecting females (Siegel et al. 2025). With BC and TC being two malignancies with high incidence among women, the co-occurrence of these cancers in the same patient has attracted significant attention in recent years. Tanaka et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of 2786 patients who were newly diagnosed with BC, revealing that this population had a significantly elevated risk of developing TC (Tanaka et al. 2001). Piek et al. also reported that both the standardized incidence ratios of TC after BC diagnosis (1.86) and of BC after TC diagnosis (1.46) were higher than the predicted rates in the general population (Piek et al. 2022). The risk of BC in TC survivors increased from 21% to 89%, while that of TC in BC survivors increased from 31% to 73% (An et al. 2015).

While TC and BC evidently differ in pathogenesis and clinical symptoms, growing evidence indicates that they share common features and molecular mechanisms (Halada et al. 2022; Tan et al. 2023; Joseph et al. 2015; Tran et al. 2020; Nielsen et al. 2016; Jung et al. 2017). The positive rate of hormone and HER2 receptors was enhanced in patients with coexisting TC and BC, suggesting hormonal crosstalk between TC and BC (Halada et al. 2022; Baumgarten et al. 2024). The comorbidity of TC and BC may be ascribed to multiple factors. Common risk factors include sex, radiation exposure history (Bhatti et al. 2010; Tran et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2022), genetic susceptibility (Lucotte et al. 2023; Lu et al. 2022), obesity, and metabolic disorders (Brown 2021; Cleary and Grossmann 2009; Pappa et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2012). The interplay and overlap of these factors create environmental conditions that are conducive to the occurrence of both types of cancer. Cartilage oligomeric matrix protein could promote tumorigenesis and progression in patients with TC and BC through estrogen (ER) signaling and epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways (Fu et al. 2023). However, we need a more detailed exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying the comorbidity of TC and BC and provide molecular targets for clinical treatment.

In this study, we performed a systematic bioinformatic analysis integrating transcriptomic data from both TC and BC to identify common hub genes and shared signaling pathways. USP9X, a well-characterized deubiquitinase, plays essential regulatory roles in multiple fundamental cellular biological processes, including cell proliferation, migration and apoptosis (Chen et al. 2019). KRAS-driven cancer development critically depends on the deubiquitination of KRAS protein mediated by USP9X/NDRG3 (Koo et al. 2025). USP9X promotes the occurrence and development of BC through regulating the cell cycle (Song et al. 2023). Moreover, USP9X interacts with and stabilizes MTH1 to promote the proliferation, survival, migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells (Xu et al. 2024). We further focused on investigating the effects of USP9X on TC and BC cells, thereby revealing potential mechanisms underlying their comorbidity.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue samples

Fourteen cases of concurrent TC and BC were obtained from the Department of Pathology, the Eighth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University and Zhongshan People’s Hospital between 2018 and 2025. The average age of female patients was 53 years (range: 35 to 70y). All enrolled patients in this study had double primary cancers of TC and BC. Among these patients, 3 patients with BC received targeted therapy and 9 received endocrine therapy. Additionally, 10 patients with TC were administered levothyroxine. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University.

Cell culture and transfection with small interfering RNA (siRNA)

We purchased the human TC cell line B-CPAP and BC cell line MCF-7 from the China Center for Type Collection (CCTCC) (Wuhan, China). Cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Procell, Wuhan, China) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sera Gold, America). Homo sapiens USP9X siRNA was obtained from Guangzhou RiboBio Biological Technology (Guangzhou, China). B-CPAP and MCF-7 cells were seeded at 2 × 105 cells/well in six-well plates. We transfected B-CPAP and MCF-7 cells with USP9X siRNA (sense strand, 5′-GGGUUAUUCCC GCACUG AATT-3′; antisense, 5′-UUCAGUGCGGGAAUAACCCTT-3′) or Negative control siRNA (sense strand, 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′; antisense, 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′) with Lipofectamine 3000 at a final concentration of 100 nM and incubated the cells at room temperature for 15 min. Add 200µL of the mixed solution to each well. After 6 h, add normal culture medium and incubate for 24 h at 37℃ with 5% CO2.

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and functional enrichment analysis (KEGG and GO) in TC and BC

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, version GDC-42.0 https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) database was used to download the mRNA expression data files of TC and BC. A total of 1207 BC samples with mRNA expression profiling data were collected, including 1094 BC samples and 113 normal breast tissue samples (Table S1). We also collected the mRNA expression profile data of 564 TC samples, which included 505 TC samples and 59 normal thyroid tissue samples (Table S2).

The DEGs in the TC (Table S3) and BC (Table S4) datasets were analyzed using the DESeq2 software. Following the analysis of DEGs, genes with log2|FoldChange| > 1 and an adjusted P < 0.05 after correction were exclusively selected as the target DEGs.

Using the ClusterProfiler package, we carried out Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) signaling pathway enrichment analysis on the DEGs. The GO analysis encompassed biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). The enrichment analysis provided details about enriched pathways, gene proportions, background proportions, and P-values. A threshold of P < 0.05 was used to select relevant data for visual analysis.

Analysis of significant copy number variation (CNV) regions, hotspot mutated genes and common driver genes in TC and BC

Copy number–related data were downloaded from the TCGA database. To identify regions with significant CNVs and the genes contained in these regions, the CNV fragments were statistically analyzed using GISTIC software (with a confidence level of > 0.9).

MutSigCV software was used to identify hotspot mutated genes, including significantly mutated genes (SMGs) in TC and BC samples. The screening criteria were P < 0.05 and a proportion of mutated samples > 3%. Genes with a significantly higher mutation frequency than the background mutation rate were identified as driver genes for TC and BC. Using the method outlined above, the driver genes for TC and BC were identified, and driver genes that were found in both cancer types were considered common driver genes for TC and BC.

Expression and prognostic analysis of USP9X and RPL22 in TC and BC

TC (56 controls and 491 TC samples; Table S5) and BC (99 controls and 1072 BC samples; Table S5) expression data (detected using Illumina HiSeq 2000 RNA sequencing) and corresponding clinical data were downloaded from the GDC database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). In TC and BC samples, based on survival duration, patients were divided into two groups: a high-expression group, where expression levels were higher than the median value, and a low-expression group, where expression levels were lower than the median value according to the median expression levels of USP9X and RPL22 in TC and BC. Prognosis was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier curve in the survival package version 2.41-1 of R4.5.0 language (http://bioconductor.org/packages/survivalr/).

Analysis of methylation and mutations of USP9X and RPL22 and their association with various immune cells in TC and BC

Using the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/), we studied the correlations between methylation levels and expression levels of USP9X and RPL22 in TC and BC, as well as their mutation status. TIMER (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) was used to identify correlations between USP9X and RPL22 and various immune cells in TC and BC.

GO and KEGG analysis of USP9X in TC and BC

In TC and BC samples, based on the expression levels of USP9X, significant GO functions and KEGG signaling pathways were identified using the clusterProfiler (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler. Ht ml) in R4.3.1. The selection criterion was to set a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 as the screening threshold.

Immunohistochemistry determined the expressions of USP9X and RPL22 in double primary cancers (co-occurrence of TC and BC)

Paraffin samples of double primary cancers (co-occurrence of TC and BC) were collected from the Department of Pathology of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and Zhongshan People’s Hospital. The slides were incubated overnight with anti-USP9 × (1:100, 55054-1-AP, Proteintech, Chicago, USA)and RPL22༈1:100, AWA63085, Abiowell, Changsha, China༉at 4 ℃. These slides were subsequently incubated with a second antibody (MaximBio Company, Fuzhou, China) to monitor labeling by using diaminobenzaldehyde (Maxim-Bio Company, Fuzhou, China). Finally, hematoxylin was used to stain the slides. We scored the expression levels of USP9X and RPL22 based on the staining intensity (0, no staining; 1, weak staining; 2, moderate staining; 3, strong staining) and percentage (0, none stained; 1,<10% stained; 2, 10–50% stained; 3, > 50% stained; 4, > 75% stained) of TC and BC cells. If the product of multiplying staining intensity by the percentage of positively.

stained cancer cells was ≥ 2, it was regarded as positive (+).

Western blot

The total proteins of B-CPAP and MCF-7 cells were extracted with RIPA lysate and split on ice. BCA protein determination kit (Nanjing Nuoweizan Biotechnology Co.,

Ltd.) was used for quantitative analysis. Twenty micrograms of total protein was boiled for 5 min before being loaded onto 10% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were incubated with primary antibody, including anti-USP9 × (1:1000 dilution, M02594-1, Boster, Wuhan, China), anti-GAPDH (1:1000 dilution) at 37 °C overnight. Next, membranes were incubated with the secondary antibody (anti-Rabbit IgG-HRP, 1:50000, Boster, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) under a chemiluminescence imaging system (SH-523, Shenhua Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). Gray value quantification of bands was performed using Image-Pro Plus software.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeated with 0.3% TritonX-100, and blocked with 1% BSA. Then cells were incubated with rabbit anti-USP9 × (1:200) at 4°C overnight. Subsequently, cells were incubated with the secondary antibodies (FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG dilution, 1:100) in the dark for 1 h. At last, nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and images captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Nikon Fi3, Tokyo, Japan). The magnification of the images was 400 times. The filter combination was CY3 and DAPI. The exposure time of the CY3 channel is 55ms, and that of the DAPI channel is 4ms. The images were analyzed using the Imagepro-Plus software.

Colony formation assay

After adjusted the concentrations of B-CPAP and MCF-7 cells to appropriate densities, we inoculated 200 cells for each culture dish at 37 °C and change the culture medium every 4 days. Two weeks later, the cells were stained with trypan blue and the number of colonies was counted by light microscopy. Each clone is defined as a visible colony consisting of ≥ 50 cells, and this criterion is verified under a microscope (40×). During the counting process, the ImageJ software was used to assist in the identification. The experiment was repeated three times.

Transwell cell migration and invasion assay

B-CPAP and MCF-7 cells were suspended in serum‑free RPMI‑1640 medium at a density of 3 × 105 cells/ml. The cells were serum-starved for 6 h before the experiments. A total of 800 µl of medium containing 10% FBS was added to 24-well plates, and transwell chambers were placed. After 1 h, 200 µl of cell suspension was added from each group to the upper chamber of the transwell. The cells were then cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, cells on the surface of the upper chamber were swabbed and those under the surface of the lower chamber were stained with crystal violet. Finally, the stained cells were observed and photographed using a light microscope (magnification, ×200; OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan). The invasion assay was conducted in the same way as the migration assay, except that 100 µl of Matrigel (BD Biosciences) was added to the transwell upper chamber of each group and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The migration and invasion assays were performed in triplicate independently.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 17.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). We performed experiments in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± SEM. Data from two groups were analyzed by unpaired t tests; and, if more than two groups, by oneway ANOVA. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Identification of differentially expressed genes and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses in TC and BC

In the dataset obtained from the TCGA database, a total of 3215 DEGs were identified in TC. Of these, 1872 DEGs were upregulated and 1343 were downregulated (Fig. 1A; Table S3). The online tool DAVID was used to perform GO analysis and KEGG signaling pathway analysis of the screened DEGs in TC. With respect to BP in TC, DEGs were significantly enriched in the DNA metabolic process, cellular response to decreased oxygen levels, regulation of fatty acid β-oxidation, cellular response to oxygen levels, and response to gamma radiation (Fig. 1B). The top five most significantly enriched CC terms of DEGs in TC included the nuclear periphery, integral component of mitochondrial outer membrane, intrinsic component of the mitochondrial outer membrane, respiratory chain complex, and dendrites (Fig. 1B). In TC, the MF showed a significant enrichment of DEGs associated with protein tyrosine kinase binding, phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphatase activity, phosphatidylinositol monophosphate phosphatase activity, DNA-binding transcription factor inhibitor activity, and oxidoreduction-driven active transmembrane transporter activity (Fig. 1B). Moreover, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs in TC were particularly enriched in the neurotrophin signaling pathway, insulin signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, Sphingolipid signaling pathway, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, and cellular senescence pathways (Fig. 1C). A gene-KEGG pathway interaction network was constructed based on differentially expressed genes and corresponding enriched KEGG terms in TCGA-THCA cohort (Fig. 1D). Green nodes represent DEGs and orange nodes represent KEGG pathways, node size is positively correlated with degree value which reflects connection quantity. Central hub genes including BRAF, HRAS, NRAS, AKT1, PTEN and TP53 clustered tightly with core pathways. The reactive oxygen species related chemical carcinogenesis pathway and hepatocellular carcinoma common oncogenic pathway were highly connected with multiple hub genes, indicating oxidative stress damage and hepatocellular carcinoma signaling cascades jointly promote TC progression.

In total, 5147 DEGs were identified in BC, with 3076 upregulated and 2071 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 2A; Table S4). DEGs in BC were primarily involved in BP, including the positive regulation of macromolecule biosynthetic, macromolecule metabolic, RNA metabolic, cellular biosynthetic, and biosynthetic processes (Fig. 2B). CC analysis demonstrated that most DEGs in BC were involved in the composition of transcription regulator complexes, transcription repressor complexes, nucleoplasm, ribonucleoprotein complexes, and nuclear lumen (Fig. 2B). The MF was primarily concentrated in DNA-binding transcription factor binding, RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription factor binding, transcription factor binding, nucleic acid binding, and ubiquitin protein ligase binding (Fig. 2B). Additionally, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that DEGs in BC were highly enriched in the thyroid hormone signaling pathway, platinum drug resistance, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, FoxO signaling pathway, sphingolipid signaling pathway, ErbB signaling pathway, and cellular senescence pathways (Fig. 2C). We constructed a gene-KEGG pathway interaction network based on differentially expressed genes and KEGG enrichment annotations in the TCGA-BRCA cohort (Fig. 2D). Green nodes denote DEGs and orange nodes represent KEGG pathways, with node size positively correlated with node degree value. PIK3CA, AKT1, PIK3R1, TP53, KRAS and CDKN1A were identified as central hub genes with high connectivity, and the pan-cancer pathway acted as the core enriched pathway in promoting BC progression.

Screening of shared driver genes underlying TC and BC comorbidity

We identified 293 candidate driver genes in BC and 91 driver genes in TC. Among these, eight driver genes were shared by TC and BC: AKT1, MEN1, PSG8, PTEN, RPL22, TMSB15A, TP53, and USP9X (Fig. 3J). PTEN and TP53 represent canonical tumor suppressor genes that have been extensively characterized in numerous previous investigations. AKT1 functions as a central effector within the PI3K-AKT signaling cascade, whose biological roles have also been thoroughly documented by abundant prior studies. MEN1 is well-established as the causative gene underlying multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1. PSG8 belongs to the pregnancy-specific glycoprotein family, with its primary biological function implicated in placental immune modulation. TMSB15A encodes an actin-regulatory protein predominantly responsible for facilitating migration and invasion of neoplastic cells. Given the above background, USP9X and RPL22 were selected as the molecular targets for subsequent mechanistic research in the present study.

Expression patterns of USP9X and RPL22 and their prognostic value in TC and BC

The Wilcoxon test was used to compare the differential expression of USP9X and RPL22 between the tumor and control groups (healthy control tissues) in TC and BC samples. Compared with the healthy control tissues, the tumor group exhibited a significant decrease in USP9X and RPL22 expression in both TC and BC samples (Fig. 4A, B, E, F). Kaplan–Meier curves were used to evaluate correlations between the expression levels of USP9X and RPL22, and the prognosis of patients with TC and BC. Our results demonstrated that samples with low USP9X expression had better prognosis in both TC and BC (Fig. 4C, G). Conversely, samples with high RPL22 expression had a better prognosis both in TC and in BC (Fig. 4D, H).

Methylation status and mutation profiles of USP9X and RPL22 in TC and BC

Using the cBioPortal database, we analyzed the correlation between the methylation and expression levels of USP9X and RPL22 in TC and BC. No correlation was observed between USP9X expression and methylation levels in both TC and BC (Fig. S1A, E). As shown in Figure S1B, RPL22 expression was significantly negatively correlated with methylation levels (P = 7.94 × 10− 20) in BC. No correlation was observed between RPL22 expression and methylation levels in TC (Fig. S1F). As illustrated in Figures S1C-D and G-H, the UALCAN online tool enabled us to obtain results comparable to those obtained from the CBioPortal database.

We also analyzed the mutation status of USP9X and RPL22 in TC and BC. As shown in Fig. S2A, the genetic alteration of USP9X in BC was 3%, including amplification, mutation, and deep deletion. The genetic alteration of USP9X in TC was 1.2%, including amplification and mutation (Fig. S2C). The genetic alteration of RPL22 in BC was 1.2%, including amplification, mutation, and deep deletion (Fig. S2B), while the genetic alteration of RPL22 in TC was 0.2% and included only mutation (Fig. S2D).

Immune cell infiltration analysis

TIMER was used to study the correlations between USP9X and RPL22 expression in TC and BC with various immune cells. USP9X expression was found to be significantly correlated with infiltration of B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells in BC (Fig. 5A) and TC (Fig. 5C). RPL22 expression was significantly correlated with infiltration of B cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, and dendritic cells in BC (Fig. 5B) and correlated with infiltration of B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells in TC (Fig. 5D).

Given the important role of the tumor immune microenvironment (TME) in the initiation and progression of BC, we also analyzed USP9X expression within the TISCH database. Analysis of GO and KEGG signaling pathways based on the expression levels of USP9X and RPL22 in TC and BC

Based on the expression levels of USP9X and RPL22 in TC and BC samples, we screened out the significantly related GO functions and KEGG signaling pathways of USP9X and RPL22. GO analysis indicated that USP9X was associated with humoral immune response and antimicrobial humoral response in both TC and in BC (Fig. S3A, C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that USP9X was associated with arachidonic acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions, and cytoskeletal processes in muscle cells in TC and BC (Fig. S3B, D).

GO analysis revealed that RPL22 was associated with the adaptive immune response, positive regulation of lymphocyte-mediated immunity, cytoplasmic translation, and ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis in TC and BC (Fig. S3E, G). KEGG pathway analysis showed that RPL22 was associated with cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions in both TC and BC (Fig. S3F, H).

In addition, high RPL22 expression was detected in both TC (Fig. 6B) and BC tissues (Fig. 6E), as well as in the corresponding adjacent normal tissues.

Effects of USP9X on colony formation, invasion, and migration abilities of TC and BC cells

To assess the influence of USP9X on malignant biological behaviors of TC and BC cells, TC cell line B-CPAP and BC cell line MCF-7 were transfected with USP9X-targeting siRNAs. Among the constructed siRNA candidates, USP9X-siRNA − 3 possessed the highest silencing efficiency against USP9X (Fig. S4). Following transfection with USP9X-siRNA, USP9X expression was downregulated in both B-CPAP (Fig. 8A) and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 8B). Immunofluorescence assays further confirmed that, after interference with USP9X-siRNA in B-CPAP (Fig. 8E) and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 8F), the expression level of USP9X was decreased. Compared with cells transfected with control siRNA, interference with USP9X expression by siRNA inhibited migration in both B-CPAP (Fig. 8C) and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 8D). Similarly, downregulation of USP9X expression markedly inhibited the invasion-promoting effects in B-CPAP (Fig. 8G) and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 8H). Furthermore, interference with USP9X expression attenuated colony formation in B-CPAP (Fig. 8I) and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 8J).

Enrichment analysis of USP9X-related partners

Using the STRING database, we identified 20 experimentally validated proteins that interact with USP9X (Fig. 9A). GO analysis showed that USP9X was associated with lys48-specific deubiquitinase activity and heteromeric SMAD protein complexes (Fig. 9B, C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that USP9X was associated with ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis and the Hippo signaling pathway (Fig. 9D). Moreover, using the GEPIA2 platform, we identified the top 100 USP9X-correlated genes in TC and BC from the TCGA database. We performed an enrichment analysis using Metascape, and the results revealed associations with “processing of capped intron-containing pre-mRNA,” “snRNP assembly,” and “intracellular protein transport” in TC, as well as “protein modification by small protein conjugation or removal,” “DNA-templated transcription,” and “chromatin remodeling” in BC (Fig. 9E, F). To further explore the interactions between USP9X and the correlated genes, we conducted protein interaction analysis using the GeneMANIA platform (Fig. 9G) and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database. The results of the HPA database analysis suggested that USP9X may interact with MCL1 and NUAK1 (Fig. 9H, J).

Discussion

In recent years, the comorbidity of TC and BC in women has attracted considerable clinical attention. Physiologically, the thyroid and breast are regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, which provides the structural basis for their molecular association. Both the thyroid and mammary glands are hormone-sensitive organs, and their function and structure are regulated by a variety of hormones, especially estrogen (ER) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). While ER promotes the proliferation of TC cells by activating the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways (Liu et al. 2021), thyroid hormones can indirectly regulate the ER signaling pathway by activating the MAPK/ERK pathway in BC cells through integrin αvβ3 (Voutsadakis 2022; Yang et al. 2025). Recently, Li et al. (2021) reported that hub genes involved in the co-occurrence of the TC and BC are associated with autophagy and ferroptosis, including ATG14. As a key autophagy regulator, ATG14 can promote tumor cell proliferation and drug resistance (Li et al. 2021). In the present study, we found EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, the FoxO signaling pathway, and the Sphingolipid signaling pathway were involved in the development of TC and BC. Notably, DEGs in BC were enriched in the thyroid hormone signaling pathway, strongly suggesting potential correlations between TC and BC.

In the present study, we identified eight driver genes that coexisted in both TC and BC. We selected USP9X and RPL22 for further investigation. Immunohistochemical analysis indicated that the positive expression rate of USP9X was higher than that of RPL22 in cases of dual primary cancers of TC and BC. As a result, USP9X was used to further explore its role in the comorbidity of TC and BC. USP9X belongs to a member of a family of deubiquitinating enzymes that regulate various cellular functions by deubiquitinating and stabilizing their substrates (Toloczko et al. 2017). In this study, we also found USP9X expression was significantly correlated with infiltration of B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells in BC and TC. Moreover, upregulation of USP9X was confirmed in particular cell types, including neutrophils and fibroblasts in stroma of BC, suggesting the potential roles of USP9X in modulating the function of these immune cells.

Bioinformatic analysis showed that USP9X expression was significantly reduced in TC and BC compared with the control group. In contrast, our study revealed that the positive rate of USP9X in dual primary cancers of TC and BC was 100%. Furthermore, USP9X expression was significantly upregulated compared with the adjacent normal tissues. This finding suggests that the expression pattern of USP9X in double primary cancers differs from that in TC or BC. USP9X overexpression is a molecular event involved in comorbidity of TC and BC.

Interfering with USP9X expression may inhibit the colony formation, migration, and invasion capabilities of TC and BC cells, signifying that USP9X has a significant role in promoting the malignant biological behaviors of TC and BC cells. To understand the underlying mechanisms by which USP9X is involved in the co-occurrence of TC and BC, we screened for significantly related GO functions and KEGG signaling pathways based on USP9X expression levels. GO analysis results revealed humoral immune response might be involved in the comorbidity of TC and BC mediated by USP9X. Aneed (Aneed et al. 2025) further reported BC with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis patients exhibited significantly elevated TSH, CD33, Anti-TPO, and Anti-Tg levels, along with reduced levels of T3 and T4. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that arachidonic acid metabolism is also implicated in the comorbidity of TC and BC mediated by USP9X. By promoting immune suppression, tumor growth, and metastasis, the cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2)/prostaglandin E2 pathway plays a pivotal role in the progression of BC (Zheng et al. 2025). COX-2 is known to be a key enzyme in the arachidonic acid metabolic pathway. COX-2 is upregulated in TC, but not in benign thyroid nodules, and that COX-2 promotes TC progression (Specht et al. 2002). However, the specific mechanisms by which USP9X contributes to the comorbidity of TC and BC through humoral immune response and the arachidonic acid metabolism pathway warrant further investigation.

The protein-protein interaction networks of USP9X were meticulously analyzed using the STRING database and the GeneMANIA platform, facilitating a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms that underlie TC and BC comorbidity mediated by USP9X. Our study revealed that USP9X/MCL-1 and USP9X/NUAK1 might be the molecular pathways involved in TC and BC comorbidity.

The present study has several limitations. Clinical specimens of co-occurring TC and BC are relatively limited. Additional experiments are required to obtain more specimens of dual primary cancers to ascertain the role of USP9X in TC and BC comorbidity. Moreover, the functions of USP9X in vivo need to be validated through animal experiments and clinical trials.

In conclusion, our study showed that TC and BC comorbidity involves the overexpression of USP9X. These findings enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the comorbidity of TC and BC, suggesting that USP9X may serve as a promising therapeutic target.
