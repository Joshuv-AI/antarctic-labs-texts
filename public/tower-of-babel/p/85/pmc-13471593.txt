Biomechanical evaluation of a novel cervical interfacet self-locking cage versus ACDF: a goat cadaveric study combined with human CT-based finite element analysis
Authors: Junwei Zhang, Xiaolei Sheng, Kexin Chen, Zhi Xu, Haitao Lu, Shuo Feng, Maji Sun, Feng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a well-established treatment for cervical degenerative disease (CDD). Nevertheless, its anterior approach carries inherent risks to critical neck structures, prompting the exploration of posterior fusion alternatives for selected patients. Traditional anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) for cervical degenerative disease involves navigating complex anterior neck anatomy with potential risks to critical neurovascular structures, motivating the development of safer posterior fusion alternatives.MethodsTwenty-four fresh C5-C6 segments from adult goats were randomly assigned to the intact, ACDF, and CILC groups (n = 8 per group). After confirming the anatomical similarity between goat and human cervical spines via three-dimensional reconstruction, corresponding surgical procedures were performed at the C5-C6 level. Range of motions (ROMs) in flexion-extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation were measured under a pure moment of 1.5 N·m using a material testing machine. A finite element model of C3-C7 was developed based on computed tomography data from healthy adults. ACDF and CILC procedures were simulated, with a 1 N·m moment and 73.6 N pre-load applied to analyse the stress distribution in adjacent segment discs, facet cartilage, and implants.ResultsAnatomical measurements confirmed a high similarity between goat and human C5-C6 segments (P > 0.05), validating its use as an experimental model. In biomechanical tests, both CILC and ACDF significantly reduced segmental motion (P < 0.001). Compared with ACDF, CILC demonstrated greater ROM in flexion (3.31° vs. 1.52°, P < 0.05), but superior stability in extension (1.86° vs. 3.86°) and axial rotation (left: 5.89° vs. 8.19°; right: 6.11° vs. 8.89°) (P < 0.05). Finite element analysis confirmed model reliability, demonstrating close alignment between predicted and reported ROM values. CILC generated lower or comparable stress in C3/4 and C4/5 discs compared with ACDF, but higher stress at C5/6. Moreover, CILC resulted in higher facet joint contact forces and implant stress than ACDF, particularly during flexion and extension.ConclusionAlthough CILC demonstrates biomechanical performance comparable to ACDF with specific advantages in controlling extension and axial rotation without significant advantages, its posterior minimally invasive approach effectively avoids neurovascular risks associated with anterior surgery. The device provides a safer alternative for cervical fusion, particularly in cases with complex anterior anatomy or those requiring revision surgery. However, the observed higher facet and implant stresses underscore the need for careful patient selection, particularly regarding pre-existing facet joint condition and bone quality.Keywords: Cervical degenerative disease, Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, Cervical interfacet self-locking cage (CILC), Biomechanics, Adjacent segment disease

Introduction

Cervical degenerative disease (CDD), commonly occurring after spinal surgery, involves disc degeneration, facet joint deterioration, and ligament changes, leading to neck pain, stiffness, and nerve root or spinal cord compression symptoms [1]. Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), considered the gold standard for CDD treatment, directly decompresses neural structures, restores disc height, and promotes bone fusion [2]. However, post-ACDF adjacent segment disease (ASD) remains a major complication that affects long-term outcomes, with incidences reaching 25% and approximately 11% in patients requiring revision surgery [3, 4].

Traditional ACDF achieves segmental fixation using interbody fusion cages and anterior plate systems. Although this approach provides good immediate stability and fusion rates, the altered biomechanical properties cause stress redistribution in fusion site–adjacent segments, accelerating degeneration at these levels [5]. Reportedly, anterior plate fixation systems significantly increase motion loads at adjacent segments while restricting movement at the fused level, with particularly pronounced compensatory increases in flexion-extension motion [6]. Furthermore, anterior revision surgery faces challenges, including complex anatomy and increased surgical risks, especially for upper cervical spine (C2-4) procedures, where mandibular obstruction and vertebral artery variations significantly increase technical difficulty [7]. These limitations highlight the clinical need for alternative fusion strategies, particularly for specific patient subgroups where the anterior approach is suboptimal.

In recent years, cervical interfacet distraction fusion techniques have emerged as alternative treatments for post-ACDF ASD. These techniques involve implanting distraction devices between facet joints to expand foraminal and spinal canal volumes while promoting facet joint fusion. Compared with traditional posterior surgery, interfacet distraction fusion yields minimal trauma, reduced blood loss, and lower complication rates, making it suitable for treating post-ACDF upper cervical ASD [8, 9]. However, existing interfacet fixation systems typically use multicomponent designs that require the combined use of transarticular facet screws and interfacet spacers to achieve effective fixation, increasing surgical complexity and screw insertion risks [10].

To address these technical limitations, we developed a novel cervical interfacet self-locking cage (CILC) with an integrated design that combines distraction, fixation, and fusion functions (Patent No. LU501275, Luxembourg, 2022). The CILC aims to offer a streamlined posterior fusion option. This study systematically evaluated the biomechanical differences between CILC and traditional ACDF fixation systems through in vitro biomechanical testing and finite element analysis, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation and technical support for clinical application. Specifically, we sought to clarify its biomechanical profile and identify potential clinical scenarios where its unique posterior approach might be advantageous.

Materials and methods

The study protocol adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (XYFY2024-KL585-01).

CILC design and development

CILC consists of three integrated components: a titanium alloy spacer, a screw guide plate, and transarticular screws (Fig. 1A-C).

Design of the cervical interfacet self-locking cage (CILC) device. Front view (A), lateral view (B), and oblique view (C) of the CILC device. Physical prototype (D) and individual components (E) of the manufactured CILC system

The spacer and guide plate were manufactured as a single unit to ensure structural integrity and optimal load distribution. The guide plate incorporated precisely angled drill holes designed according to the Klekamp method for safe transarticular screw insertion, with entry points positioned to minimise risk to the vertebral arteries and nerve roots. The spacer features a cylindrical design with serrated superior and inferior surfaces to enhance initial stability and osseointegration. The central hollow portion of the spacer and the space around its serrated surfaces are intended to accommodate bone graft material following decortication of the facet joint articular surfaces to prepare the fusion bed.

The device, manufactured from a medical-grade titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4 V) using precision machining techniques, was fabricated at multiple sizes (heights: 2–4 mm, diameters: 8–12 mm) to accommodate anatomical variations. CILC allows for controlled facet joint distraction, indirect neural decompression, and immediate biomechanical stability, while facilitating subsequent bony fusion through integrated screw fixation (Fig. 1D-E).

Biomechanical testing

Specimen preparation

Twenty-four fresh cervical C5-6 segment specimens were obtained from adult goats (body weight: 40 ± 5 kg) acquired from a local commercial slaughterhouse. The animals were slaughtered by licensed slaughterhouse personnel following standard commercial food processing procedures, which typically involve stunning (electrical or mechanical) followed by exsanguination in accordance with local animal welfare regulations for food animals. As all specimens were obtained post-mortem from animals slaughtered solely for food purposes completely unrelated to this study, no experimental procedures were performed on live animals. Therefore, anesthesia, euthanasia, or ethical approval for animal experimentation were not applicable to this study. Bone mineral density (BMD) was measured in all goat specimens using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) prior to testing. The mean BMD of included specimens was 0.93 ± 0.11 g/cm², consistent with the range reported for non-osteoporotic middle-aged human cervical vertebrae [11]. Specimens with T-score < − 2.5 (osteoporosis) were excluded to ensure bone quality comparable to the target clinical population (non-osteoporotic adults). This screening step was implemented to minimise the confounding effect of bone quality on biomechanical outcomes and to enhance the translational validity of the goat model. Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs, along with CT, were performed to exclude specimens with tumours, fractures, or deformities. All specimens were sealed in double-layer plastic bags and stored at -20 °C until further use. Before experiments, specimens were thawed at room temperature for 8 h, followed by careful removal of the surrounding muscle tissue while preserving the ligaments and joint capsules to maintain structural integrity. Anatomical measurements

To identify the cervical segment most similar to the human cervical anatomy for subsequent biomechanical testing, three-dimensional reconstruction was performed using computed tomography (CT) images of 20 goat and 20 human cervical spines with MIMICS software version 21.0. Four key parameters of facet joints from C2 to C7 segments were measured: anterior-posterior diameter (APD), left-right diameter (LRD), joint surface tilt angle (JTA), and facet joint interspace height (FIH).

Experimental groups

Specimens were randomly divided into three groups (n = 8 per group).

Intact group: No intervention, preserving all anatomical structures.

Surgical procedures

The ACDF group underwent anterior approach exposure of C5-6 vertebral bodies, discectomy, endplate cartilage removal, and appropriately sized cage implantation, followed by four-screw anterior titanium plate fixation of the vertebral bodies. The CILC group underwent posterior approach exposure of the C5-6 laminae and bilateral facet joints, articular cartilage removal, appropriately sized CILC device implantation into facet joints, and transarticular screw fixation.

Radiographic evaluation

All specimens underwent postoperative anteroposterior and lateral radiographic examinations to confirm correct implant positioning and assess the integrity of fixation constructs. Representative radiographic images of both fixation techniques are shown in Fig. 2.

Biomechanical testing protocol

All specimens were assessed using a dynamic-static dual-axis universal material testing machine (Orthopedic Research Institute, Soochow University) equipped with Bluehill 2.0 and WaveMatrix software. Under a pure moment of 1.5 N·m, the range of motion (ROM) values of cervical specimens were measured in flexion, extension, lateral bending (left and right), and axial rotation (left and right). Each motion direction was tested three times, with the average value used for further analyses. Throughout the procedure, specimens were continuously moistened with physiological saline to prevent dehydration. Finite element analysis

Model construction

Herein, we constructed a C3-C7 cervical spine model (Fig. 4A). Geometric data were derived from high-resolution CT images (slice thickness, 0.625 mm) of a 36-year-old healthy adult male volunteer without spinal pathology (height, 171 cm; weight, 70 kg). Imaging data were imported into MIMICS software (version 21.0) to generate bone tissue masks and create C3-C7 cervical spine STL triangular mesh models. Subsequently, the model was imported into Geomagic Wrap 2017 software for optimisation. Next, the SolidWorks 2016 software was used to supplement and design structures that were unclear on CT scans, based on anatomical structures such as endplates, nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, facet joint cartilage, and major ligament structures (anterior longitudinal, posterior longitudinal, and interspinous ligaments, as well as capsular ligament and ligamentum flavum).

Surgical model simulation

Two surgical models were established at the C4-C5 segment (Fig. 4B, C). This level was chosen for the FE analysis as it represents a common clinical site for symptomatic cervical degenerative disc disease, allowing for the evaluation of adjacent segment biomechanics in a prevalent human context. The preceding cadaveric tests at C5-6 in goats validated the fundamental stability and feasibility of the CILC technique.

CILC Model: Bilateral CILC implantation was simulated with transarticular screw fixation performed according to the Klekamp technique.

Both implants were modelled based on the actual dimensions and material properties of the devices (CILC: Ti-6Al-4 V alloy; ACDF cage: polyetheretherketone material; plate: titanium alloy).

After geometric cleaning of the models, appropriate elements were selected for mesh generation. Loading and boundary conditions

Figure 5 shows the motion patterns and loading boundary conditions of the C3-C7 cervical spine finite element model. In all models, the inferior endplate of the C7 vertebral body was completely constrained with six degrees of freedom, while a reference point was established on the superior endplate of the C3 vertebral body for loading. By applying a 1 N·m moment at this reference point, six physiological motion states were simulated: flexion, extension, left/right lateral bending, and left/right axial rotation. Additionally, a preload of 73.6 N was applied to reflect the combined effects of the head weight and cervical muscle forces [16]. Contact between facet joint cartilages was modelled using surface-to-surface contact with a friction coefficient of 0.01 [17]. The interface between the implant system and surrounding bone structures utilised “tie” constraints to simulate stable bonding and complete biological integration following postoperative bony fusion [18].

Orthographic views of the C3-C7 cervical finite element model illustrating applied moments and loads in different directions

Model validation

Model accuracy was validated by comparing the ROMs measured from each segment under each motion mode in the model with experimental data from previous studies [13, 19, 20]. It is acknowledged that FE models and cadaveric experiments provide complementary insights based on different assumptions and are not directly comparable.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance was used for multiple group comparisons, with pairwise comparisons conducted using the least significant difference test. For statistically significant results, effect sizes were calculated: Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons and η² for analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed data are presented as medians (minimum, maximum) and analysed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Finite element model validation

The C3-C7 finite element model was validated against previously reported experimental data to ensure biomechanical accuracy. Under physiological loading conditions of 1.0 N·m moment and 73.6 N follower load, the predicted ROMs demonstrated strong agreement with reported data (Fig. 6). Regarding flexion, the model predicted ROMs of 6.0°, 7.0°, 7.0°, and 5.0° at the C3/4, C4/5, C5/6, and C6/7 segments, respectively, which closely matched the experimental findings of Panjabi et al. (2001) and Wang et al. (2023).The extension ROMs showed a similar consistency at 5.5°, 6.0°, 5.5°, and 4.5° across respective segments. The lateral bending and axial rotation predictions fell within ranges reported in multiple validation studies, confirming model reliability for subsequent biomechanical analyses of the CILC and ACDF constructs.

Stress distribution and numerical analysis of intervertebral discs

von Mises stress distributions in adjacent intervertebral discs at C3/4, C4/5, and C5/6 are presented in Fig. 7A-C. Across all evaluated segments, intact models demonstrated relatively uniform blue stress distributions, indicating physiological stress levels. Both ACDF and CILC models exhibited elevated stress patterns compared with the intact condition, with ACDF consistently showing more pronounced green-yellow stress concentrations, particularly during extension and rotational movements; CILC demonstrated intermediate stress elevations, with more moderate green zones distributed across all segments.

Stress nephograms and quantitative comparison of peak von Mises stress for (A) C3/4 intervertebral disc, (B) C4/5 intervertebral disc, and (C) C5/6 intervertebral disc under six loading directions (left bending, right bending, extension, flexion, left rotation, right rotation)

The quantitative analysis revealed distinct stress distribution patterns across the evaluated segments. At C3/4, CILC demonstrated lower mean von Mises stress values than ACDF under most loading conditions. Specifically, CILC showed stress values of 0.55 MPa versus 0.57 MPa for ACDF in flexion, and 0.54 MPa versus 0.73 MPa in extension, representing notable stress reductions. Regarding lateral bending and rotation, CILC values (0.55–0.63 MPa) remained consistently lower than ACDF values (0.59–0.69 MPa). At the C4/5 level, CILC showed stress values closely approximating the intact condition across all motion directions, ranging from 0.47 to 0.65 MPa compared with intact values of 0.45–0.65 MPa. Conversely, at C5/6, CILC exhibited higher stress concentrations than the intact and ACDF models. CILC demonstrated elevated values, particularly in extension (0.78 MPa) and flexion (0.68 MPa) compared with ACDF (0.48 MPa and 0.58 MPa, respectively).

von Mises stress distributions and contact force analysis of facet joint cartilages at C3/4, C4/5, and C5/6 are presented in Fig. 8A-C. The intact model demonstrated a uniform blue stress distribution, whereas both surgical models exhibited extensive green-yellow high-stress regions. CILC exhibited a more pronounced stress elevation, particularly at C3/4, with extensive green-yellow zones, and at C5/6, with prominent yellow-red regions, compared with moderate green distributions for ACDF.

Stress distribution and contact force analysis of facet joints under different loading conditions. Von Mises stress nephograms and quantitative contact force comparison for (A) C3-4 facet joints, (B) C4-5 facet joints, and (C) C5-6 facet joints across intact, ACDF, and CILC models

Quantitative analyses confirmed these visual patterns. At C3/4, CILC demonstrated higher contact forces than ACDF: 62.2 N versus 53.9 N in left bending, and 118.9 N versus 116.3 N in right rotation. At C4/5, ACDF showed reduced contact forces (16.2–33.9 N) compared with the intact model (34.5–84.0 N). At C5/6, CILC exhibited markedly elevated forces, particularly in extension (111.9 N versus 57.4 N), demonstrating significant load redistribution to adjacent segments inherent to posterior fusion approaches.

Stress distribution and numerical analysis of implants and vertebrae

von Mises stress distributions in the implant systems and vertebral bodies are presented in Fig. 9A-B. CILC exhibited higher stress concentrations during left/right bending and extension movements, showing pronounced yellow-red high-stress zones with mean stress values exceeding 48 MPa; the ACDF plate system demonstrated more moderate green-blue stress distributions with lower peak stress levels under equivalent conditions. Both systems showed similar stress distribution patterns during left and right rotation and flexion movements.

Von Mises stress distribution in implants and vertebral bodies under different loading conditions. Comparative stress analysis showing (A) implant stress distribution and average surface stress values for ACDF plate system/cage and CILC device, and (B) vertebral body stress patterns at implant-bone contact interfaces for C4 and C5 vertebrae under six loading directions (left bending, right bending, extension, flexion, left rotation, right rotation)

Quantitative analysis revealed significant differences between the two systems. Under bending loads, ACDF exhibited mean stress values of 15–26 MPa, whereas those of CILC reached 58–67 MPa. During extension and flexion, CILC achieved a maximum mean stress value of approximately 58 MPa, whereas the ACDF stress levels remained below 50 MPa. Regarding rotational movements, the ACDF mean stress values were consistently 1.21–2.95 times higher than those of CILC.

Vertebral body stress analysis at the implant-bone interface demonstrated distinct stress distribution patterns between the two constructs. ACDF showed stress concentrations primarily in the anterior vertebral regions corresponding to the plate-screw insertion sites, with stress values ranging from to 15–43 MPa. CILC transmitted stress more extensively to the posterior structures through the facet joint interfaces, with stress concentrations of 43–64 MPa observed in the transarticular screw contact regions.

Discussion

This study used biomechanical testing combined with finite element analysis to systematically evaluate the biomechanical characteristics of a novel CILC system compared with traditional ACDF. Herein, CILC demonstrated a biomechanical performance comparable to that of ACDF; however, the two differed in load transmission mechanisms and stress distribution patterns. Specifically, CILC showed a greater ROM in flexion, while performing less well in extension and axial rotation. Finite element analysis further confirmed that the CILC achieved a more favourable adjacent segment stress distribution through its unique posterior fixation mechanism, providing a theoretical basis for its clinical application. These findings help delineate the biomechanical profile of CILC and inform its potential clinical niche.

Biomechanical testing revealed significant differences between CILC and ACDF in different motion directions. CILC allowed a greater ROM in flexion, potentially related to its posterior fixation mechanism, preserving partial flexibility of the anterior column. CILC demonstrated a significantly smaller ROM in extension and axial rotations, indicating distinct advantages in controlling these critical motion directions. This finding has important clinical significance, as axial rotational instability is a major pathogenic factor in CDD and one of the primary mechanisms leading to neurological symptom deterioration [21, 22]. Through bilateral interfacet fixation, CILC can effectively limit intersegmental rotational motion, providing potential advantages for treating CDDs with rotational instability.

Finite element analysis clarified the mechanisms by which the two fixation methods affected adjacent segments. At the C3/4 and C4/5 levels, CILC showed lower or comparable intervertebral disc stress than ACDF. At the C3/4 level, CILC showed stress values of 0.55 MPa vs. 0.57 MPa for ACDF in flexion, and 0.54 MPa vs. 0.73 MPa in extension. At the C4/5 level, CILC stress values (0.47–0.65 MPa) approached those of the intact condition (0.45–0.65 MPa). This favourable IVD stress distribution matched the corresponding ROM changes in these directions in the biomechanical testing, indicating that CILC’s posterior approach achieved a more uniform load distribution and partially relieved the stress concentration characteristic of rigid anterior fixation. From a long-term prognostic perspective, this favourable stress distribution pattern may reduce the risk of adjacent segment disc degeneration, which has important implications for preventing ASD. In a meta-analysis by Burch et al. [23], stress concentration was an important factor in ASD development, which is consistent with our findings. However, it is noteworthy that at the C5/6 level (fused segment), CILC showed higher stress concentration than ACDF, particularly in extension (0.78 MPa vs. 0.48 MPa) and flexion (0.68 MPa vs. 0.58 MPa). This result suggests that, while CILC may have a protective effect on the upper adjacent segment (C3/4), it may increase the load on the lower adjacent segment (C5/6), reflecting the complex stress redistribution mechanisms inherent to posterior fusion techniques [24].

A notable feature of CILC is the alteration of the facet joint loading patterns. In finite element analysis, CILC demonstrated a higher facet joint stress than ACDF in most motion states. At the C3/4 level, CILC showed contact forces of 62.2 N vs. 53.9 N for ACDF in left bending, and 118.9 N vs. 116.3 N in right rotation. At the C5/6 level, this difference was more pronounced, particularly in the extension, where the CILC contact forces reached 111.9 N versus only 57.4 N of ACDF. This phenomenon corresponded to the reduced rotational ROM observed in biomechanical testing, reflecting the biomechanical trade-offs of direct joint fixation. From a pathophysiological perspective, this “stress transfer” rather than “stress shielding” suggests that while CILC may reduce the risk of disc-related adjacent segment disease, it may increase the risk of facet joint arthritis and related complications. Related studies have confirmed that increased facet joint stress may accelerate articular surface degeneration [25–27]. Therefore, when selecting patients, the degree of facet joint degeneration and bone quality should be carefully considered. For patients with significant facet degeneration or severe osteoporosis, CILC should be applied cautiously.

Stress distribution analysis revealed different characteristics between the two techniques regarding long-term stability. CILC showed a higher stress concentration in the left/right bending and extension movements, demonstrating distinct yellow-red high-stress regions with mean stress values exceeding 48 MPa, whereas ACDF demonstrated a more moderate green-blue stress distribution with lower peak stress levels. Conversely, ACDF showed obvious stress concentrations at the cage-endplate interface and screw-plate connection sites, suggesting potential risks of endplate subsidence or internal fixation failure. Furthermore, the two techniques differed significantly in vertebral body stress distribution: ACDF concentrated stress mainly in the anterior and middle regions of the vertebral bodies (15–43 MPa), whereas CILC transferred stress posteriorly to the pedicle and facet joint regions (43–64 MPa), reflecting the distinct load transmission characteristics of the anterior and posterior approaches and highlighting different potential failure modes. In fatigue testing studies by Wu et al. [28] and Martinez-Mondragon et al. [29], the uniformity of the implant stress distribution directly affected long-term stability.

Clinical implications and considerations

Although CILC shows no significant advantages over ACDF regarding pure biomechanical performance, its minimally invasive posterior approach provides distinct clinical benefits. Traditional ACDF requires navigation through the complex anatomical layers of the anterior neck, including the carotid sheath, oesophagus, thyroid, and recurrent laryngeal nerves. Studies report an average postoperative dysphagia rate of 19.4% and voice hoarseness rates of 0.3%-27% [30, 31]. Mandibular obstruction and vertebral artery variations significantly increase surgical difficulty, particularly for upper cervical surgery [32]. For revision surgery, patients with short thick necks or obesity, or those with thyroid enlargement or anterior neck scarring, the anterior approach often poses significant technical challenges and anatomical limitations [33–36]. Consequently, the CILC’s posterior approach presents a valuable alternative for cervical fusion in specific clinical scenarios, such as patients with challenging anterior anatomy, previous anterior surgery, upper cervical pathology, or multilevel disease. It is important to note that the indirect decompression achieved through facet distraction may not be sufficient for cases with severe foraminal stenosis or large osteophytes, where direct decompression remains necessary.

Another advantage of CILC is the complete intervertebral disc preservation at the surgical segment. Herein, the C4/5 disc was completely preserved with CILC, whereas it required complete removal with ACDF. This tissue preservation has multiple benefits: the preserved disc maintains biomechanical functions, such as load distribution and shock absorption; theoretically avoids complications from disc removal, such as disc space height loss and endplate injury; reduces the number of foreign implants; and lowers implant-related complication risks.

The disc preservation strategy has clinical value regarding patients’ psychological acceptance. Many patients instinctively resist removal of their normal or partially normal anatomical structures, fearing that disc removal will cause irreversible functional loss or accelerate adjacent segment degeneration. By preserving the disc structure, CILC largely relieves the patients’ psychological burden and improves their acceptance and cooperation with the surgical plan. Additionally, disc preservation maintains the structural foundation for potential future applications of emerging technologies such as disc regeneration and cell therapy [37, 38].

Study limitations and future perspectives

This study has certain limitations. First, in vitro biomechanical experiments cannot fully simulate the complex in vivo physiological environment, including dynamic factors such as muscle activity and soft tissue healing processes. Second, although animal models have anatomical similarities, interspecies differences exist, particularly regarding bone density and ligament characteristics, and overall spinal alignment (quadrupedal vs. bipedal). The lack of formal equivalence testing for anatomical similarity is also noted. Third, the biomechanical testing and finite element analysis were performed at different spinal segments (goat C5-6 vs. human C4-5), each selected for its specific rationale (maximizing morphological similarity for model validation vs. analyzing a clinically prevalent level). While this design provides complementary information, it precludes a direct, segment-to-segment comparison of all quantitative results. The finite element analysis provided detailed stress distribution information; however, its results were based on specific material property assumptions and boundary condition settings, and the actual clinical conditions may vary. Additionally, this study lacks long-term follow-up data and did not assess long-term efficacy and complication rates of CILC. The higher stresses observed on facet joints and the CILC implant itself require validation in clinical studies to understand their implications for fusion success, implant longevity, and patient-reported outcomes. Future prospective clinical trials are warranted to establish individualised finite element models, perform fatigue and long-term stability assessments, and explore the combined applications of CILC and other therapies to comprehensively optimise clinical treatment strategies.

Conclusion

CILC demonstrates biomechanical performance comparable to traditional ACDF but exhibits better performance in axial rotational stability and certain advantages in adjacent segment stress distribution through its unique load transmission mechanism. Although CILC shows no significant advantages in terms of pure biomechanical indicators, its posterior minimally invasive approach, safety, broader indication range, and disc preservation strategy provide a valuable alternative treatment option for patients with CDDs. Specifically, it may serve as a suitable alternative for select patients, such as those requiring revision surgery or presenting with complex anterior anatomy, provided that facet joint health and bone quality are adequate. Future clinical studies are needed to confirm its efficacy and safety.
