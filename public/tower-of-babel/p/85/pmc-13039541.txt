Evolution of mesoscale force chains at the structural steel-coal rock interface and macro-scale mechanical response characteristics
Authors: Hongyan Chen, Peng Tao, Jindong Liu, Hongyue Chen, Ying Ma, Fuqiang Gao, Dingtang Zhang, Yuhang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo elucidate the influence mechanism of coal powder moisture content on the evolution of mesoscopic force chains at the steel-coal rock interface under compression and the mechanical response of the structural steel surface, First, the surface morphology of structural steel and coal rock was reconstructed based on three-dimensional fractal theory. Simultaneously, integrating particle adhesion and fragmentation theories, a three-body contact model involving structural steel, coal powder, and coal rock was established. Subsequently, finite element-discrete element coupling (FEM-DEM) was employed for three-body contact simulation analysis. This approach investigated the evolution characteristics of the force chain network within the coal powder layer at the interface and the dynamic mechanical response properties at the interface.Finally, contact mechanical property tests were conducted to determine the contact mechanical parameters of the three-body system. Results indicate that under different moisture content conditions, external loads are first transmitted to the structural steel surface through the loading plane, then transferred to the coal-rock surface via mesoscopic force chains within the coal powder layer.The number of force chains in the coal powder bed exhibits a trend of increasing initially and then stabilizing over time. The length of force chains shows a trend of increasing initially, decreasing subsequently, and ultimately stabilizing over time. The moisture content (W) increased from 2% to 12%. The number of force chain particles in the coal powder layer exhibits an exponential decay relationship with the number of force chains, with the longest force chain comprising 11 particles. The evolution pattern of the mesoscale force chain network at the compressed structural steel-coal rock interface transitions from global force transmission dominated by long chains at low moisture content to local cooperative force transmission via short chain networks at high moisture content. The force chain structure’s capacity to transmit external loads gradually increases over time. At W = 2%, the structural steel surface exhibits the highest peak stress, which decreases rapidly over time. At W = 6%, the structural steel surface shows the lowest peak stress. The maximum deviation between simulation and experimental results for surface stress on the structural steel is 10.7%. The FEM-DEM coupled model for three-body contact between structural steel, coal power, and coal rock is effective and demonstrates high accuracy.Keywords: Hydraulic support, Tunnel floor, Three-body contact, Micro-mechanical properties, Force chain networkSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Smooth relocation of hydraulic supports is the core guarantee for safe and efficient coordinated advancement in coal mines’ fully mechanized mining faces. However, in actual scenarios, the relocation process is influenced by the micro-contact mechanical properties at the interface between the metal base and the coal-rock floor, making it difficult to ensure smooth relocation. Among these factors, the coal dust layer at the structural steel-coal-rock interface under different moisture conditions is a key determinant of the micro-contact mechanical response at the interface.

The presence of coal dust layers with varying moisture content at the contact interface transforms the ideal two-body contact between the hydraulic support base and the roadway floor into a three-body contact system involving the base, coal dust layer, and floor. More fundamentally, this constitutes a three-body contact system comprising structural steel, coal dust, and coal-rock. The concept of three-body contact was introduced by Godet in 1984, with research focusing on analyzing force distribution between contacting bodies, deformation at the contact interface, and wear issues under varying conditions1,2. In recent years, driven by advancements in experimental and testing technologies, experts worldwide have conducted in-depth investigations into the microscopic contact mechanics of three-body systems, yielding numerous significant research outcomes.Liu et al.3 investigated the failure characteristics and load-bearing mechanisms of anchored fractured rock masses. Using a proprietary three-dimensional physical simulation testing system, they conducted comparative load tests on anchored specimens with varying degrees of fracturing and reinforced them with prestressed bolts.Zhang et al.4 using a comprehensive research approach that included theoretical analysis, laboratory experiments, and numerical simulations, conducted anchorage quality tests on a self-developed bottom-hole single-wing inverted wedge-shaped expansion device.Zhu et al.5 used the Random Finite Element Method (RFEM) to study the reliability of two-layer slopes without drainage.

Brink et al.6 discovered that third-body abrasive particles generated by friction contact aggregate to form a nanoscale particle surface layer that interacts morphologically with the surface of the primary object. Payrebrune et al.7 investigated interactions among particle shape, roughness, and turntable speed parameters. Yan et al.8 developed a tribological model for third-body particles constrained on rough surfaces, revealing mechanisms of particle-induced friction instability. Bian et al.9 studied alloy metal micro-motion experiments under high temperature and pressure, finding that the oxide type in the third-body layer significantly influences the coefficient of friction. Lian et al.10 developed a metal-straw powder-metal tribological contact model, concluding that straw powder reduces contact area and friction coefficient at the bonding interface. Horng, J. et al.11 investigated the influence of wear particles, environmental particles, and nanoadditives on tribological contact characteristics under varying particle concentrations, sizes, surface roughnesses, and contact modes.

Li et al.12 developed a three-dimensional sphere-plane simulation model incorporating third-body particle contact interactions to investigate the influence of third-body particles on fretting wear under partial slip conditions. With advances in simulation technology, the behavioral characteristics of third-body particles can now be analyzed from multiple scale perspectives to describe and predict their impact on macroscopic mechanical behavior13,14.

Among particulate materials as third bodies, the common microstructure consists of force chains formed by particles that bear and transmit primary loads. These force chains interweave to form a network that permeates the particulate material system, bearing the majority of external loads and playing a decisive role in the macroscopic mechanical properties of the particulate system15. Extensive research has been conducted on force chains in three-body contacts. Guo et al.16 demonstrated the maximum length of force chains, revealed the existence of a critical length, and identified influencing factors. Xinwei Yang et al.17,18 established a three-body contact model incorporating the interface of moist coal powder, elucidating the evolution of force chains within moist coal powder layers under gap filling and overlay effects. They further revealed the load-bearing characteristics of three-body contact interfaces. Cao et al.19 discovered at the microscopic level that ultrasonic vibration thickens force chains within granular systems, enhancing force transmission efficiency. Chen Q et al.20 developed a topological data analysis model for force chain networks, analyzed their topological features, and proposed an early warning method for particle blockage in mining engineering.

Ge Kang et al.21 further revealed through numerical manifold methods that force chain breakage preferentially occurs at contact points of coarse particles, forming secondary networks via geometric reorganization. Dai et al.22 analyzed force chain networks during gyratory compaction of SMA-13 and OGFC-13 asphalt mixtures by integrating contact information with complex network theory. You et al.23 employed the discrete element method to investigate the spatial distribution of particle weight and force chain network evolution in flat-bottomed silos, accounting for particle size, silo diameter, and powder type.

In summary, current research on three-body contact has advanced from macroscopic mechanical analysis to the exploration of microscopic mechanisms, encompassing a broad spectrum of topics: from single-particle wear behavior to complex granular media, from static contact to dynamic evolution, and from experimental observation to multiscale simulation. The introduction of force chain theory and the application of multiscale numerical simulation provide new perspectives and tools for gaining a deeper understanding of interfacial friction and wear, load transfer, stability, and failure mechanisms.

However, existing research, such as that in Reference24 on the contact mechanical characteristics between the base of coal mine hydraulic supports and the working face floor, primarily focuses on the macroscopic direct contact relationship between the support base and the working face floor, concentrating mainly on the direct contact coupling between them. Research remains lacking on the microscopic mechanisms and multiscale coupling effects involving third-body coal dust at the contact interface between the hydraulic support base (metal) and the working face floor (coal-rock), as well as the force chain network formed by this third-body coal dust. Therefore, investigating the microscopic contact mechanics of the three-body system (hydraulic support base-coal dust-bedrock) involving coal dust at the contact interface with varying moisture content characteristics is crucial. This research aims to reveal the influence patterns of coal dust with different moisture states on the microscopic contact mechanics at the support base-bedrock interface, holding significant practical implications for enhancing support stability and safety during support relocation.

Given the medium characteristics of the coal powder layer in a three-body system, this paper employs a coupled finite element method (FEM) and discrete element method (DEM) approach. Based on particle adhesion and fragmentation theory, a three-body contact model incorporating metal-coal powder-coal rock is constructed to characterize the discrete properties of coal powder particles and the micro-continuum mechanical response of the metal substrate surface. A comprehensive analysis is conducted on the force chain evolution patterns within the coal powder particle system at the interface and the stress response at the coal powder layer interface. Finally, experimental validation confirms the accuracy and reliability of the three-body contact model and the credibility of the simulation results.

Structural steel-coal powder-coal rock three-body model

The finite element domain for the three-body contact model was modeled with structural steel, the loading plane, and the coal-rock body as elements of 1.0 mm edge length. The coal-rock body was defined as rigid, while the structural steel and loading plane were set as flexible bodies. The discrete element region represents the third-body coal powder layer at the interface, with a thickness of 0.7 mm and a porosity of 0.5. Elastic adhesive contact behavior is described using the Hertz-Mindlin with JKR Version 2 (JKR V2) adhesion theory28. The coal rock is fixed, while the side walls surrounding the interface coal powder layer are assigned periodic boundaries. Through proportional calculation of the loading area, the normal load Pw to be applied to the loading plane is 0.02 MPa.

Due to adhesion on the surfaces of wet coal particles containing a certain amount of moisture, the commonly used Hertz-Mindlin contact model—which accounts only for elastic deformation and ignores interparticle bonding—fails to adequately simulate the mechanical behavior of wet coal particles in relation to coal mining and transportation equipment29.

The Hertz-Mindlin with JKR Version 2 (JKR V2) adhesion theory is a classical model for describing the behavior of elastic adhesive contacts, and is particularly suitable for van der Waals-dominated systems involving micrometer-scale particles (< 100 μm)28. This model is based on Hertz contact theory, incorporating a surface energy term to characterize short-range adhesion effects, resulting in a nonlinear force-contact overlap relationship. In the initial stage (A→B), the normal force is zero; upon reaching physical contact (point B), the normal force rapidly increases to − 8/9Fnc (point C), where Fnc is the critical adhesion force. During the loading phase (C→D), the normal force hardens as the contact depth increases. During the unloading phase (D→C→E), the stored elastic potential energy is converted into kinetic energy, leading to reverse motion. Complete separation requires the application of additional energy (red region); contact rupture occurs at the tensile strength threshold (point E), and final detachment occurs at point F, as shown in Fig. 3.

Evolutionary model of the coal powder layer force chain

Particle chaining requires satisfying three conditions: the force contact criterion, the force chain angle criterion, and the length criterion36. In this study, particles with contact force Fp > Fav and particle size larger than the average particle size were defined as the effective detection range for particle chaining. Based on the aforementioned chaining conditions, a 3D force chain identification program was developed to extract all information regarding local contacts within the particle system (including ID number, spatial coordinates, contact direction, and other parameters) from the discrete element software.

The dynamic evolution of the force chain structure within the coal powder bed under different moisture content states (W), as obtained from simulation analysis, is illustrated in Fig. 8.

Dynamic evolution of coal particle force chain network under different moisture content.

As shown in Fig. 8, the binding effect provided by the lack of moisture between coal powder particles at W = 2% results in a loose, discontinuous, and low-strength force chain structure. The force chain network is highly susceptible to fracture and reorganization, making it difficult for stable force transfer paths to form between the particles. At W = 6% and W = 12%, liquid bridges formed by moisture between coal powder particles generate capillary and viscous forces. These enhance interparticle cohesion, promoting the formation of a more complete, coherent, and robust force chain network. Furthermore, W = 6% coal powder effectively transmits and distributes external loads, maintaining the structural stability of the particle system. Under varying moisture contents, external loads are initially transmitted through the loading plane to the structural steel surface, then conveyed to the coal rock surface via mesoscopic force chains within the coal powder layer. However, particle fragmentation during load transfer impacts both the structure and quantity of force chains. The formation of dense contact force chain networks between particles results in an interlaced force chain configuration, aligning with the peak distribution on the coal rock surface.

Discussion

This study addresses the macroscopic mechanical response of structural steel surfaces in a three-body contact system involving structural steel, coal powder, and coal rock with varying moisture contents. Employing a coupled finite element method (FEM) and discrete element method (DEM) based on a three-body contact model, comprehensive analysis was conducted through simulation and experimental measurement. This analysis examined the evolution of force chains within the particle system and the stress response of the structural steel surface under the influence of the coal powder layer.

Regarding the evolution patterns of force chains, the force chain structure of W = 2% coal powder particles is loose, discontinuous, and low in strength. The force chain network is highly prone to breaking and reorganize, making it difficult to form stable force transmission pathways between particles. The primary reason is the lack of cohesive action provided by moisture. In contrast, at W = 6% and W = 12%, liquid bridges formed by moisture between coal powder particles generate capillary and viscous forces. These forces significantly enhance cohesion between particles, promoting the formation of a more complete, coherent, and robust force chain network.

Among these, W = 6% coal powder effectively transmits and distributes external loads, maintaining the structural stability of the particle system. This is primarily because agglomeration within the moist coal powder layer reduces the number of force chains, while capillary-driven liquid bridge adhesion prolongs the time required for force chain reconstruction. The force chain characteristics of the coal powder layer evolve with moisture content, transitioning from the “few and long” pattern (long chains dominate global force transmission) in low-moisture coal to the “many and short” pattern (short chain networks enable localized cooperative force transmission) in high-moisture coal. Consequently, a gradual increase in coal moisture within a narrow range significantly enhances the system’s energy dissipation capacity and impact resistance stability. Furthermore, this process is closely linked to particle fragmentation. As force chains continuously reconfigure with particle motion, changes in the size and shape of fragmented particles redistribute contact forces, forming new force chain networks.

Regarding stress distribution on structural steel surfaces, the primary reason for the gradual reduction in stress at the contact interface under normal pressure is that coal powder undergoes elastic deformation, converting external impact energy into elastic strain energy between particles. This pressure buffering is achieved through an energy storage-release-dissipation mechanism. Under different moisture contents, energy dissipation during coal powder particle compression exhibits either a single peak characteristic or a double-peak characteristic. The first dissipation phase is primarily driven by particle fragmentation, generating fine particles and significantly enhancing the system’s compressibility. This phase exhibits concentrated and intense dissipation. Subsequently, a stable transition period occurs, characterized by stress redistribution and particle pre-adjustment. The second dissipation phase is dominated by particle rearrangement. Benefiting from moisture lubrication and fine particle filling, the particle network efficiently reorganizes. Energy dissipation becomes steady and uniform, with optimized energy transfer pathways, ultimately achieving higher overall energy dissipation efficiency.

When W = 2%, the structural steel contact surface experiences the highest peak stress, which declines rapidly over time. This is primarily due to the high friction coefficient of dry coal powder, which provides strong initial load-bearing capacity but poor flowability, resulting in significant pressure concentration. Over time, the lack of lubrication causes localized collapse or pressure redistribution among coal powder particles, leading to a rapid decrease in pressure at the structural steel contact interface. At W = 12%, excessive moisture may partially slurry the coal powder, though its structural strength remains partially intact. Under compression, localized dense hardened areas form, temporarily increasing the hydraulic support base pressure. At W = 6%, the structural steel contact surface exhibits the lowest peak pressure primarily because moisture optimizes stress transfer between particles. Moisture lubricates particle contacts, reduces friction coefficients, and improves flowability. This results in more uniform pressure distribution and delays the rate of peak pressure decline under loading.

During the experimental measurement process, variations in coal powder layer thickness were observed between the peak and valley regions of the coal-rock interface. This variation delayed the time response of stress state changes within the region. However, the overall trend of stress state changes remained consistent across the monitored area. The normal stress at the three-body contact point exhibited a pattern of initial increase followed by decrease, ultimately stabilizing. Comparing simulation and experimental results, the simulated value was 253,105 Pa while the experimental value was 283,432 Pa, revealing a maximum stress discrepancy of 10.7%. Analysis of potential causes for simulation-experiment discrepancies includes: (1) Errors in surface roughness data extraction during coal-rock morphology modeling within specific regions, leading to final stress condition inaccuracies. (2) Variations in coal powder layer thickness within the coal-rock peak region causing final stress condition errors. (3) The simulation process assumed spherical coal particles, which slightly deviated from their actual shape, resulting in errors in the final stress distribution.

Conclusion

This paper addresses the evolution of mesoscopic force chains within the coal powder layer at the structural steel-coal powder-coal rock tripartite contact interface, as well as the mechanical response of the structural steel surface, under varying coal powder moisture content. A three-body contact FEM-DEM bidirectional coupling model was established using a finite element (FEM) and discrete element (DEM) coupling method. Comprehensive analyses were conducted on the force chain evolution patterns within the particle system and the stress response of the structural steel contact surface under the influence of the coal powder layer. Finally, experimental measurements were performed to determine the stress state of the structural steel contact surface within the monitored region at different moisture contents of the coal powder. The following conclusions were drawn from the research:

Under different moisture content conditions, external loads are first transmitted to the surface of the structural steel via the loading plane, and then transferred to the surface of the coal-rock through the mesoscale force chain of the coal powder layer; the force chain’s capacity to transmit external loads gradually increases over time.The number of force chains in the coal powder bed initially increases over time and then levels off, while the length of the force chains initially increases, then decreases, and eventually stabilizes.The mesoscale force chain network at the steel-coal-rock interface in compressed structures evolves from a “few but long” pattern in low-moisture coal powder (where long chains dominate global force transmission) to a “many but short” pattern in high-moisture coal powder (where short-chain networks facilitate localized cooperative force transmission).

The stress trend within the monitored area of the structural steel contact surface generally aligns with simulation results. The normal stress at the three-body contact point exhibits an initial increase followed by a decrease, ultimately stabilizing. When W = 2%, the peak stress on the structural steel surface is highest and decreases rapidly over time. At W = 6%, the peak stress on the structural steel surface was the lowest. The maximum deviation between simulation and experimental results for the stress on the structural steel surface corresponding to the coal powder layer at different moisture contents was 10.7%. The FEM-DEM coupled model for the structural steel-coal powder-coal rock three-body contact demonstrated effectiveness and high accuracy.
