Microsurgical resection of a giant thoracolumbosacral Schwannoma in an adolescent with intraoperative neuromonitoring and anatomical laminoplasty reconstruction
Authors: Wenhua Zhang, Yaxiong Li, Honglei Liu, Jianfeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal schwannomas represent benign neoplasms arising from spinal nerve root sheaths, predominantly occurring in the cauda equina region. By reviewing this case and literature, our aim was to highlight the diagnostic and therapeutic considerations in managing such rare and challenging cases in the pediatric population. We present an exceptional pediatric case of a giant thoracolumbosacral schwannoma with multi-root involvement, neuroimaging revealed a 36-cm intradural mass extending from T10 to the coccyx, representing the longest documented spinal schwannoma to date. Taking into account the patient’s previous cervical spinal canal meningioma surgical history, the coexistence of a schwannoma added complexity to the preoperative diagnosis. Under intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM), multi-level laminectomy with anatomical reconstruction and tumor resection from T10 to S4 was performed. The pathological result confirmed it to be a schwannoma. The coexistence of two types tumors within the spinal canal is rare, in addition to MRI scans, pathological diagnosis is gold standard at this time, and surgery is still the preferred option.Keywords: Schwannoma, Spinal canal, Adolescent, Surgery, Meningioma

Background

Spinal schwannomas, benign nerve sheath tumors predominantly arising in the cauda equina region [1], typically demonstrate indolent growth patterns. These lesions often attain substantial dimensions while preserving neurological function, attributed to slow tumor progression, root mobility, and expansive intradural space [2]. However, giant multisegmental variants involving multiple sacral roots remain exceptionally rare. We present an adolescent case of a 36 cm thoracolumbosacral schwannoma (T10-sacrococcygeal) with detailed analysis of its clinicoradiological features and microsurgical management. To our knowledge, this represents the longest contiguous spinal schwannoma documented in medical literature [2–5]. The tumor exhibited extensive craniocaudal extension occupying the complete spinal canal diameter across ten vertebral segments [2]. Our technique emphasizes selective microsurgical resection with intraoperative neuromonitoring to maximize tumor excision while preserving neural integrity, combined with laminoplasty reconstruction to maintain spinal stability [6].

Case presentation

A 16-year-old female presented with a 4-year history of chronic dysuria and 1-year progression of right thigh pain (VAS 7), significantly impairing supine positioning. Conservative management including pharmacotherapy and physical rehabilitation proved ineffective. MRI evaluation of the thoracolumbar and sacrococcygeal regions revealed an extensive intradural mass spanning 36 cm from the inferior T10 level to the coccyx.

As demonstrated in Fig. 1, the lesion exhibited homogeneous contrast enhancement with hypointense T1-weighted and hyperintense T2-weighted signal characteristics. The tumor caused marked expansion of the dural sac and substantial osseous erosion of adjacent vertebral bodies, particularly in the sacrococcygeal region. Quite interestingly, the patient underwent a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination due to weakness in both lower limbs in 2016, a spinal meningioma at the level of cervical vertebra 7(C7) to thoracic vertebra 1(T1) was detected, and a posterior approach meningioma resection was performed. This time, no tumor recurrence was found in the MRI examination. These radiological features supported a preliminary diagnosis of giant spinal meningioma? The diagnosis was worthy of our consideration.

The patient was admitted to the Neurosurgery Department at First Hospital of Hebei Medical University in March 2022. Initial examination revealed independent ambulation with ataxic gait, T10-coccygeal dermatomal hypoesthesia, and absent patellar/ankle deep tendon reflexes.

Notably, the patient’s maternal lineage demonstrated a cancer predisposition with both her mother and grandmother having succumbed to unspecified malignancies. No neoplastic history was reported in the paternal family.

Preoperative evaluation demonstrated predominant distal weakness in the right lower extremity without neurofibromatosis stigmata. The patient underwent prone positioning for posterior midline exposure from T10 to coccyx. Multilevel laminectomy (T10-S2) revealed a tense, thinned dura mater. Upon dural opening, an elongated tan-white translucent neoplasm with prominent vascularity and engorged surface vasculature was observed occupying the subdural extramedullary space from thoracolumbar to sacral regions. The lesion exhibited moderate parenchymal consistency with brittle texture, demonstrating minimal adhesion to adjacent neural structures in the thoracolumbar region where spinal cord and nerve root compression was evident. Microscopic dissection facilitated careful separation of tumor-arachnoid interfaces, preserving neural elements through intermittent intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM). Notably, significant electromyographic responses (quadriceps and tibialis anterior) necessitated periodic procedural interruption to reassess neural-tumor relationships, ensuring minimal iatrogenic injury. Sacral exploration revealed extensive tumor infiltration through disrupted dura, with osseous destruction of sacral canal posterior walls and multidirectional extension through neural foramina. Following tumor resection, watertight dural closure was achieved primarily using a running 4 − 0 Prolene suture. Given the extensive dural resection required in the sacral region due to tumor infiltration and bony erosion, an autologous fascial graft (harvested from the paraspinal region) was incorporated using a combination of sutures and fibrin glue to ensure a robust closure. Histopathological analysis of frozen sections confirmed schwannoma diagnosis. The lesion’s growth pattern suggested sacral nerve origin with craniocaudal extension into thoracic levels. Combining the tumor texture during the operation, the attachment characteristics, and the results of the intraoperative frozen pathological examination, we resolved our doubts. However, this is a rare occurrence where two tumors of different origins coexist within the spinal canal.

Following complete thoracolumbar tumor resection, meticulous reconstruction involved anatomical lamina repositioning with instrumentation to maintain spinal stability. Postoperative imaging confirmed appropriate hardware placement and adequate canal decompression (Fig. 2A-B). The subarachnoid space demonstrated restored patency after total excision, with IONM parameters returning to baseline post-resection (Fig. 3) [6].

(A) The lamina was reduced and fixed in situ with connect plates and screws. (B). Postoperative CT image

Motor evoked potentials (MEP) were used to monitor intraoperative nerve traction and injury using needle electrodes fixed to the quadriceps, tibialis anterior, bunions, and anal sphincters bilaterally; both hands were used as controls. Intraoperative stimulation of the nerves resulted in electromyographic activity, with large amplitudes in the quadriceps and tibialis anterior bilaterally, which returned to baseline levels after cessation of stimulation

Histopathological examination revealed characteristic schwannoma features without malignant transformation, confirmed by intraoperative frozen section analysis. Final immunohistochemical profiling demonstrated S-100 (+), SOX-10 (+), Vimentin (+), EMA (-), PR (-), and low proliferative activity (Ki-67 + 3%).

The patient achieved an uncomplicated postoperative recovery. Early rehabilitation protocols, including lower limb functional exercises and electrotherapy, commenced on postoperative day 3. Transition to ambulation with a walking aid and rigid brace occurred within 10 days, with continued brace use for one month postoperatively. Neuropathic pain remained well-controlled throughout recovery. At six-month follow-up, significant improvement in dysuria, lower limb hypoesthesia, and motor function was documented, with near-complete resolution of neurological discomfort. Serial telephone surveillance tracked clinical progress, revealing restored bowel/bladder function and sustained pain reduction correlating with improved sleep quality. To mitigate potential lumbar kyphosis secondary to paraspinal muscle weakness, a rigid lumbar orthosis was maintained during mobilization. One-year evaluation demonstrated independent ambulation without assistive devices and resolution of urological symptoms. Postoperative MRI confirmed complete tumor resection with spinal cord decompression (Fig. 4A-B). Surveillance thoracolumbar-sacral MRI at 6–12 months was recommended for recurrence monitoring, though socioeconomic constraints prevented the patient from attending scheduled imaging follow-up despite repeated outreach. Reoperation remains indicated for any future symptomatic recurrence. A timeline of the disease progression and treatment is demonstrated in Fig. 5.

Discussion

Schwannomas are benign nerve sheath tumors arising from Schwann cell proliferation, most commonly affecting cranial and peripheral nerves. Accounting for 25% of adult intradural spinal tumors, these lesions demonstrate equal gender distribution with typical diagnosis in the fourth to fifth decades of life [6]. While Wager et al. [1] reported schwannomas constitute 49.3% of spinal neoplasms in their series, the presented case represents an exceptional pediatric occurrence. The insidious growth pattern of spinal schwannomas frequently leads to delayed diagnosis (mean 24.6 months [1]), with this case demonstrating an extraordinary 48-month diagnostic interval. To our knowledge, this 36 cm T10-coccygeal lesion represents the longest reported spinal schwannoma, exceeding previous documentation of T11-S2 involvement [7]. Based on the growth characteristics of schwannomas and the patient’s medical history in this case, we inferred that the patient had two different-origin tumors in the spinal canal simultaneously, namely meningioma and schwannoma (Two years after the surgery, the patient developed new symptoms, while the cervical spinal meningioma did not show recurrence. Therefore, it can be reasonably inferred that during the surgery for the cervical intraspinal tumor, due to the slow growth characteristic of the schwannoma, a huge tumor in the lumbosacral region had already existed.) There are no results of MRI examinations of the lumbar and sacral regions in the other hospital medical records. Although the data is not perfect, it still reflects the scarcity of medical records. This case reminded us that if there was weakness in the lower limbs, we should strive to conduct comprehensive imaging examinations of the entire spinal canal to avoid missed diagnoses.

MRI remains the key inspection method for preoperative evaluation [8–16], though differentiation from neurofibromas, hemangioblastomas, and ependymomas can prove challenging [3]. Key distinguishing features include:⑴Absence of neurofibromatosis stigmata (café-au-lait spots, acoustic neuromas);⑵Characteristic enhancement patterns: schwannomas demonstrate homogeneous enhancement vs. myxopapillary ependymomas’ non-enhancing hypointense components;⑶Typical T2 hyperintensity shared by both entities. While MRI optimally demonstrates soft tissue relationships and foraminal extension (“dumbbell” morphology), CT better characterizes osseous erosion [9]. Notably, our case exhibited atypical sacral nerve origin without classical dumbbell configuration, evidenced by bidirectional sacral canal expansion (Fig. 1E-G). Meningioma can show similar signals on T1/T2 scans, with uniform enhancement. The “dural tail sign” can be observed, and the broad base adheres to the meninges.

While the patient’s history of two distinct spinal tumors (meningioma and schwannoma) and maternal family cancer history raise the possibility of an underlying syndrome such as Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) or Schwannomatosis, several factors mitigate against this diagnosis. The patient exhibited no clinical stigmata of NF1 (e.g., café-au-lait spots, Lisch nodules, neurofibromas) or NF2 (e.g., vestibular schwannomas, cataracts, skin plaques) on thorough physical examination. The schwannoma exhibited classic histology without features suggestive of schwannomatosis-specific mutations (though comprehensive genetic testing was not performed due to resource constraints). The prior cervical meningioma was a solitary lesion with no recurrence, and the current schwannoma was a single, albeit giant, lesion. While the family history warrants mention, the lack of classic syndromic features or multifocal schwannomas makes a definitive diagnosis of NF2 or schwannomatosis less likely in this adolescent presentation. Nevertheless, long-term monitoring for new lesions is prudent.

For this case, in addition to using magnetic resonance imaging for a clear diagnosis, when dealing with patients who may have two types tumors simultaneously in the spinal canal, the preoperative diagnosis must be extremely cautious. Differential diagnosis is very important, but ultimately, we still rely on pathological diagnosis to make a definite conclusion. Pathological diagnosis remains the gold standard for a clear diagnosis.

No matter what kind of huge tumor is in the spinal canal, complete microsurgical resection remains the therapeutic cornerstone [2]. However, the tumor’s extensive sacral nerve adherence necessitated balanced surgical strategy: ⑴Radical resection of sacral canal components; ⑵Partial resection of nerve-entwined portions; ⑶Intentional residual tumor preservation to maintain neurological function. This approach reflects current consensus advocating selective resection when radical excision risks neurological deficits [11], particularly given conflicting reports regarding adjuvant radiotherapy’s efficacy [14].

Giant schwannomas pose unique stabilization challenges [2, 12]. Our technique incorporated: ⑴Laminectomy with in-situ lamina repositioning; ⑵Paraspinal muscle preservation; ⑶Dural sac protection through anatomical reconstruction. This non-fusion strategy aims to minimize postoperative kyphosis (reported in 15–30% of laminectomy cases [17]) while preventing epidural scarring and preserving spinal mobility.

Preoperative neurological status remains the strongest outcome predictor [10]. Our patient’s preserved ambulatory capacity despite prolonged symptomatology suggested favorable recovery potential.

The present case has some limitations. Firstly, although rare about coexistence of a schwannoma and a meningioma within the spinal canal, schwannoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of spinal canal preoperatively. Secondly, it is just a single hospital center study and the retrospective nature of the study causes information bias. Thirdly, the follow-up period is too short to monitor the long-term therapeutic effect of such a large tumor after surgery. Also, the tumor genetic testing for special cases of adolescent (with a special family history) has not been effectively carried out, which may result in the omission of some important information.

Conclusion

Surgery is the preferred option for the treatment of intraspinal large schwannoma, and MRI is the test of choice for the diagnosis of intraspinal schwannoma. It is extremely rare for two types of different origins tumors can occur simultaneously within the spinal canal. It also posed a significant challenge for our preoperative diagnosis. Pathological diagnosis is particularly important at this time. Resection of intradural huge tumors and preservation of spinal anatomy and function are all very important. For the resection of large intraspinal schwannoma that grows longitudinally in the spinal canal and affects multiple segments, we need the assistance of IONM to maximally resect the tumor under the microscope to minimize damaging the nerve roots. We would like to highlight the diagnostic and therapeutic considerations in managing such rare and challenging cases in the pediatric population.
