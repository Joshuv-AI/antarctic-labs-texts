Protocol for detecting causal variants by co-localizing GWAS and QTL studies using colocRedRibbon
Authors: Theodora Papadopoulou, Aristeidis Sionakidis, Anthony Piron, Miriam Cnop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for using colocRedRibbon to link disease-associated variants to gene expression variation by co-localizing genome-wide association study (GWAS) SNPs with cis-expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs). We describe steps for shortlisting GWAS and eQTL variants, computing co-localization statistics, and assessing posterior probabilities. Co-localized variants provide insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. Beyond GWAS-eQTL integration, the protocol can be applied to co-localization analyses across different QTL types and distinct GWAS datasets.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Piron et al.1Subject areas: Bioinformatics, Health Sciences, Genomics, High Throughput Screening

Before you begin

colocRedRibbon is an R package designed to identify causal genetic variants by integrating two association studies. It typically performs co-localization analyses between two datasets: GWAS and QTL summary statistics. This protocol illustrates colocRedRibbon application on diabetes GWAS and eQTL data from human pancreatic islets. Of note, colocRedRibbon can be applied to a broad range of datasets such as GWAS and diverse types of QTLs, including protein QTL (pQTLs), methylation QTLs (meQTLs), as well as other GWAS datasets. The algorithm is described in the associated Cell Genomics manuscript.1 Detailed R vignette documentation is available in the R package and replicated on its GitHub page. The R package also provides reference documentation for each function and method accessible through R standard documentation system. The method follows a two-step approach: first, variants are split according to their direction of effect on gene expression; next, variants are ranked by their P-values in both datasets, and RedRibbon rank-rank hypergeometric overlap2 is used to shortlist variants that overlap between the two association studies. Finally, posterior probabilities are computed to pinpoint shared causal variants, among shortlisted variants.

Common abbreviations: GWAS, genome wide association study; eQTL, expression quantitative trait locus; SNP. single nucleotide polymorphism.

Innovation

colocRedRibbon employs a novel prefiltering approach based on the RedRibbon hypergeometric overlap method,2 to shortlist variants before applying existing co-localization tools. This prefiltering step isolates common signals present in both association studies, enabling the discovery of previously undetectable co-localizations. The algorithm disentangles the confounding effect of multiple GWAS signals, a frequent obstacle that obscures co-localization, by keeping only the ones matching QTL signals. colocRedRibbon also allows to independently analyze the two directions of effect for the QTL signals. This allows the identification of gene regions associated with risk variants for which deviation in either direction (from the regulatory equilibrium) is associated with increased trait risk. colocRedRibbon nearly doubles the number of detected co-localizations compared to previous methods, providing a robust framework for uncovering the complex interplay between genetic variation and disease risk.

Expected outcomes

colocRedRibbon employs novel pre-filtering steps and a variant shortlisting method based on the direction of effect on gene expression before applying co-localization, in order to identify a significantly larger set of co-localizations. The algorithm produces a statistical summary list including (1) the PP of at least one common causal variant between the two association studies, (2) the PP of the identified lead variant being present in both datasets, and (3) the SNPs composing the 99% credible set, along with their names.

For each direction of effect in GWAS, when applicable, a co-localization plot can be generated to visually assess overlap and shared causality. The resulting multi-panel plot summarizes variant overlap, credible set composition, and lead variant co-localization, shedding light on the complex dynamics of genetic regulation.

Limitations

colocRedRibbon is a powerful method for integrating GWAS with QTL data allowing to identify causal variants, while its applicability can be expanded to a wider range of data types. Due to the stochastic nature of the evolutionary model that colocRedRibbon employs, the list of overlapping variants between two datasets can slightly vary across runs. To ensure reproducibility, perform multiple independent iterations of the overlap step, prior to co-localization.

Potential solution

Problem 3

“The two datasets I want to co-localize are aligned to different genome assemblies (e.g., GRCh37/hg19 and GRCh38/hg38). How can I properly harmonize or merge these datasets before co-localization analysis?”. Related to Setup environment Step 4c.

Potential solution

Use variant identifiers (e.g., rsID) to merge the variants across datasets, since they remain stable across genome assemblies. It is important, though, to verify that the reference and alternate alleles are not reversed between the two genome assemblies. This is a common obstacle, occurring due to genome updates. If the reference and risk allele are opposite, swap them according to the newest genome assembly to ensure consistency in the direction of effect.

If this is what you expected, this is not a problem.

Potential solution

If varbeta is not provided, you can either extract it from the summary statistics (if available) or compute it using the code below, which implements the formulas described in the corresponding manuscript.6

{R}

> computeBeta <- function(zscore, maf, n) {

varbeta <- 1 / (2 ∗ maf ∗ (1 - maf) ∗ (n + zscore ∗ zscore))

beta <- zscore / sqrt(varbeta)

return(list(beta = beta, varbeta = varbeta))

}

temp <- as.data.table(

404)

})))
