Postnatal Changes in Fetal Hemoglobin and Tissue Oxygenation in Preterm and Term Neonates: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Ena Suppan, Thomas Suppan, Bernhard Schwaberger, Christina H. Wolfsberger, Nariae Baik-Schneditz, Daniel Pfurtscheller, Christoph Schlatzer, Melina Winkler, Gerhard Pichler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPhysiological postnatal changes in fetal hemoglobin fraction (FHbF) may affect neonatal tissue oxygenation through shifts in the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve. We examined cross-sectional associations between FHbF and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)-derived tissue oxygenation, and longitudinal associations between within-infant changes in FHbF and cerebral tissue oxygenation (cStO2) and oxygen extraction (cFTOE) during the first 3 postnatal weeks in neonates without red blood cell transfusions.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 25 preterm (median [IQR] gestational age 33.6 [1.6] weeks) and 25 term (39.9 [1.4] weeks) neonates in a tertiary-center neonatal unit. Measurements were performed at T1 (days 2–3), T2 (days 5–10), and T3 (days 12–18). cStO2 was assessed by time-resolved NIRS, peripheral tissue oxygenation by spatially resolved NIRS and peripheral fractional oxygen extraction by venous occlusion. cFTOE was calculated. Total hemoglobin (Hb) and FHbF were measured by co-oximetry. Cross-sectional associations were assessed using Spearman’s rho correlations. Longitudinal trajectories and gestational age-adjusted within-infant change-on-change associations were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models.ResultsNo significant cross-sectional correlations were identified between FHbF and cerebral or peripheral NIRS-derived variables at any time point. Over time, Hb and cStO2 declined, cFTOE increased, while peripheral oxygenation and extraction remained stable. Within-infant Hb changes were not associated with concurrent cerebral NIRS changes. In contrast, FHbF change was associated with change in cStO2 (T2–T1: β per 1% FHbF, −0.19; 95% CI −0.33 to −0.05) and cFTOE (β 0.22; 95% CI 0.06–0.38).ConclusionWithin-infant FHbF decline was associated with increased cStO2 and decreased cFTOE, independent of total Hb.

Introduction

Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) is crucial for fetal oxygen delivery and undergoes marked maturation-dependent changes in its fractional content (FHbF) due to the hemoglobin switch and synthesis of adult hemoglobin (HbA). Owing to its higher oxygen affinity compared to HbA, HbF is optimally suited for oxygen transport in the relatively hypoxic intrauterine environment [1, 2]. The gradual switch from HbF to HbA begins around the 30th week of gestation and progresses at an individual rate for each neonate, independent of the birth event [3, 4]. This transition shifts the oxygen dissociation curve (ODC), thereby modulating tissue oxygen availability [5, 6].

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) enables noninvasive monitoring of regional tissue oxygenation in various body regions [7]. This technique has been widely applied for monitoring cerebral and peripheral oxygenation in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). Different NIRS devices employ distinct measurement principles to derive regional tissue oxygenation, either by detecting relative changes in oxy- and deoxyhemoglobin concentrations using spatially resolved spectroscopy or by quantifying their absolute concentrations with time-resolved NIRS technology [8].

Because regional tissue oxygenation reflects a dynamic balance between oxygen delivery, consumption, and perfusion, several studies have focused on physiological variables influencing NIRS-derived tissue oxygenation [9, 10]. Given shifts in the ODC, changes in FHbF may alter arterial-venous oxygen gradients and also influence NIRS-derived parameters. Although oxygenation changes during the immediate fetal-to-neonatal transition have been explored extensively [11, 12], longitudinal data beyond this period remain limited, particularly regarding the relationship between cerebral and peripheral oxygenation and the postnatal changes in FHbF [13].

This study investigated associations between HbF parameters and NIRS-derived tissue oxygenation and oxygen extraction at three predefined time points during the first 3 postnatal weeks. We further assessed longitudinal cerebral and peripheral NIRS changes and whether within-infant FHbF changes were associated with distinct oxygenation and extraction patterns in preterm and term neonates.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective observational pilot study was conducted at a single tertiary NICU between April 2020 and March 2024. The study protocol is provided in online supplementary 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553520).

Participants

Preterm and term neonates admitted to the NICU were eligible. Exclusion criteria were known congenital malformations and a family history of hemoglobinopathies. Because of sensor-number and sensor-size limitations, infants with birth weight below 1,500 g were not approached. None received a red blood cell (RBC) transfusion during the study period. Neonates born before 37 completed weeks of gestation were classified as preterm, and those born at or after 37 weeks as term. Given the pilot design, a target sample size of 25 neonates per group was prespecified. Antenatal, perinatal, and postnatal clinical data were recorded, including gestational age (GA), birth characteristics, respiratory support and oxygen therapy, primary diagnoses, and medications.

HbF Analysis and Physiological Monitoring

NIRS measurements were synchronized with routine clinically indicated capillary blood sampling and started immediately afterward. FHbF and total hemoglobin concentration (Hb) were measured using the ABL800 Flex co-oximeter (Radiometer, Denmark). Total fetal hemoglobin concentration (HbFc) was calculated as HbFc (g/dL) = FHbF (%)/100 × Hb (g/dL).

Pulsatile arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2) and heart rate were continuously monitored using the IntelliVue MP70 monitor (Philips, Netherlands). SpO2 and NIRS signals were sampled at 1 Hz. Recordings with at least 60 min (≥3,600 data points) of artifact-free data were included and averaged for analysis.

NIRS Measurements and Time Points

Cerebral and peripheral NIRS measurements were recorded simultaneously during resting conditions for up to 2 h at 3 time points: T1, 24–80 h (days 1–3); T2: 120–240 h (days 5–10); T3: 288–432 h (days 12–18) after birth. cStO2 was measured using a t-NIRS device (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) with the sensor applied on the left forehead. cFTOE was calculated as: cFTOE (%) = (SpO2 − cStO2)/SpO2 × 100 [14].

Peripheral tissue oxygenation index (pTOI) was measured using a spatially resolved NIRS device (NIRO-200NX, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) with the sensor placed on the right forearm. At each time point, peripheral NIRS monitoring was combined with three venous occlusions (VO) according to a standardized protocol and prespecified quality criteria to derive peripheral fractional oxygen extraction (pFOE) [15–17]. Mean values were calculated from the 3 VOs. All monitoring signals were recorded and stored in alpha-trace (Medical Systems, Austria) for subsequent analysis.

Device Selection

The original protocol-specified t-NIRS for both cerebral and peripheral measurements, including VO. After quality control identified excessive light scattering during peripheral t-NIRS VO measurements, NIRO-200NX was adopted for subsequent peripheral measurements. Only NIRO-200NX-derived pTOI and VO data were analyzed, resulting in higher missing data rates for pTOI and pFOE.

Protocol-Specified Primary and Secondary Analyses

The original study protocol (online suppl. 1) specified the primary analysis as the assessment of correlations between FHbF and HbFc and NIRS-derived tissue oxygenation parameters at T1, T2 and T3. Protocol-defined secondary objective was to assess cohort-based and within-infant longitudinal changes in tissue oxygenation depending on FHbF.

Statistical Analysis

Primary Outcome

Continuous variables were analyzed at the 3 time points. Descriptive statistics were based on all available observations and are reported as mean (SD) or median [IQR], as appropriate. Distributional assumptions were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients assessed associations between primary outcome parameters at each time point.

Secondary Outcomes

Within-infant associations between changes in hemoglobin-related parameters (FHbF and Hb) and concurrent changes in oxygenation and oxygen extraction were assessed using change-on-change analyses. This approach captures individual trajectories rather than cohort-level systematic mean changes. To distinguish changes in Hb from changes in the relative contribution of HbF, FHbF and Hb were modeled as separate predictors. Because the objective was to quantify within-infant longitudinal changes, analyses were not stratified by GA, instead GA was included as a continuous covariate in all models.

Changes between time points (ΔT2–T1) were calculated for predictors (ΔFHbF and ΔHb) and outcomes (ΔSpO2, ΔcStO2, and ΔcFTOE). Linear regression models were fitted with ΔFHbF, ΔHb, and GA as predictors. Regression coefficients are reported with 95% CIs and Wald t test p values. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied within each outcome.

Cohort-level longitudinal changes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with a subject-specific random intercept and time as a categorical fixed effect. Prespecified pairwise contrasts were T2 vs. T1, T3 vs. T2, and T3 vs. T1. Effect estimates and 95% CIs were derived using Wald tests, with q < 0.05 (FDR adjusted p) considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB R2024b and Python version 3.13.

Missing Data

Missing data were handled by multiple imputation when suitable for reliable imputation (<30% missing observations per parameter and time point). Estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rule. For peripheral NIRS measures and later contrasts (T3–T2 and T3–T1), missingness exceeded this threshold and was considered too high for valid imputation. Therefore, only the T2–T1 contrast for cerebral NIRS measures was included in change-on-change analyses.

Results

Study Population

Twenty-five moderate-to-late preterm (median GA [IQR], 33.6 [1.6] weeks) and 25 term neonates (median GA [IQR], 39.9 [1.4] weeks) were included. The cohort consisted mainly of clinically stable neonates without cardiovascular support and with low incidence of respiratory support. Participant inclusion is shown in Figure 1 and baseline characteristics in Table 1. Besides GA, significant between-group differences were observed in birth weight and umbilical artery pH, but not in subsequent respiratory support or supplemental oxygen use.

Within-Infant Longitudinal Changes

Results of the T2–T1 change-on-change analyses are shown in Table 3 and Figure 2a, b. A larger decline in FHbF was associated with a greater increase in cStO2 (β = −0.19; 95% CI −0.33, −0.06; q = 0.018) and a greater decrease in cFTOE (β = 0.22; 95% CI 0.08, 0.37; q = 0.018). No significant associations were observed between changes in FHbF and SpO2, or between changes in Hb and any evaluated oxygenation or oxygen extraction parameter.

Cohort-Level Longitudinal Changes

Over the first three postnatal weeks, Hb decreased significantly across all evaluated contrasts (all q < 0.001; Fig. 3a). In contrast, FHbF showed no significant cohort-level longitudinal change (Fig. 3b). Among oxygenation parameters, cStO2 decreased significantly over time (T2-T1, q = 0.008, T3-T1, p = 0.044; Fig. 3c). Changes in oxygen extraction were significant only before FDR adjustment, with both cFTOE and pFOE increasing over time. Discussion

This prospective observational study examined whether HbF parameters are related to NIRS-derived tissue oxygenation in preterm and term neonates during the early postnatal weeks. Cross-sectional analyses did not identify significant associations between FHbF or HbFc and NIRS parameters at individual time points. However, longitudinal change-on-change analyses provided additional insight into within-infant adaptation. A decline in FHbF during the first two postnatal weeks was associated with increasing cStO2 and decreasing cFTOE, whereas changes in Hb were not associated with these outcomes. This suggests that postnatal changes in hemoglobin composition may contribute to cerebral oxygenation dynamics beyond hemoglobin concentration alone.

Cerebral Oxygenation

The observed change-on-change associations between FHbF and cerebral oxygenation metrics are physiologically plausible and likely reflect heterogeneous hemoglobin transitions during the first two postnatal weeks. Although FHbF is expected to decline with maturation, trajectories were not uniform across the cohort, with some neonates showing stable or even increasing FHbF (Fig. 2b) [18]. Among neonates with declining FHbF, cStO2 increased while cFTOE decreased, consistent with replacement of HbF by HbA and a rightward shift of the ODC, which reduces oxygen affinity and facilitates tissue oxygen unloading [1, 4, 19]. Under stable cerebral metabolic demand and preserved cerebrovascular adaptation, this shift may allow cerebral oxygen requirements to be met with a smaller arterial-venous saturation difference, resulting in higher venous-weighted cStO2 and lower cFTOE (online suppl. 2-Fig. S1) [20]. Conversely, infants with increasing or persistently higher FHbF demonstrated lower cStO2 and higher cFTOE, compatible with a greater extraction gradient in the setting of higher hemoglobin-oxygen affinity [3, 5]. Although FHbF did not change significantly at cohort level, within-infant changes were associated with cerebral oxygenation changes, reflecting individual postnatal adaptations rather than a uniform cohort-wide trend (online suppl. 2, Fig. S2c).

Previous studies have highlighted the role of HbF in neonatal cerebral oxygenation. During immediate fetal-to-neonatal transition in preterm neonates, lower total HbFc has been associated with lower cStO2 and higher cFTOE [21]. In the present study, correlation analyses showed no significant associations between HbFc and either cStO2 or cFTOE. This difference may reflect the physiological context: during immediate transition, SpO2 is still rising and pulmonary gas exchange, cardiac output, and cerebrovascular autoregulation may not be established. In contrast, during the first two postnatal weeks, SpO2 is usually maintained within the target range of 90–95%, and, in the context of preserved cerebral autoregulation, cStO2 and cFTOE may not correlate with HbFc or FHbF. However, longitudinal changes in FHbF may induce shifts in the ODC and thereby influence accompanying changes in cStO2 (online suppl. 2, Fig. S1).

Since physiological changes in FHbF and NIRS parameters have not been studied previously, our findings can only be compared with RBC transfusion studies. In a recent study, very preterm neonates receiving adult A-RBC transfusions, resulting in a FHbF decline, demonstrated higher cStO2 and lower cFTOE compared with neonates receiving cord blood transfusions, in whom FHbF increased [22]. This aligns with our findings, although our cohort captured physiological FHbF changes without transfusion. However, without within-infant change-on-change analyses, it remains uncertain whether differences in cStO2 can be causally attributed to ODC. Positive pre-transfusional FHbF and cStO2 correlations in that cohort, may reflect altered cerebrovascular autoregulation in the presence of anemia and greater prematurity, in contrast to our cohort of clinically stable more mature neonates without anemia.

Although the effect size of physiological FHbF decline on cStO2 was relatively small, with each 1% decrease in FHbF corresponding to a 0.19% increase in cStO2 and 0.22% decrease in cFTOE, larger FHbF changes, induced by RBC transfusion, may have more clinically relevant effects. Consistently, A-RBC transfusion studies in anemic preterm neonates reported higher cStO2 and lower cFTOE [23, 24]. Transfusions cause abrupt changes in hemoglobin concentration and composition, whereas our study reflects gradual endogenous FHbF change under relatively stable clinical conditions. Despite this difference, directionally similar NIRS-derived changes support the plausibility of FHbF-related shifts in cStO2 and cFTOE.

The physiological relevance of FHbF for cerebral oxygenation should be interpreted cautiously. We do not suggest that FHbF is a dominant determinant of cStO2, but rather a modifying factor within established contributors, including arterial oxygen content, Hb, cerebral blood flow, and cerebral metabolic demand [10]. NIRS-derived cStO2 reflects the balance between cerebral oxygen delivery and consumption, not mitochondrial oxygen delivery directly. Thus, cStO2 and cFTOE should be interpreted as markers of cerebral oxygen supply-demand balance. Although Hb declined substantially, it was not significantly associated with cStO2 or cFTOE, suggesting that hemoglobin composition, rather than Hb concentration alone, may contribute to early postnatal cerebral oxygenation changes.

Clinical Implications

The observed associations between physiological FHbF changes and cerebral oxygenation are hypothesis-generating and may be relevant to vulnerable populations. Extremely preterm neonates often receive A-RBC transfusions, causing abrupt hemoglobin composition shifts that may exceed immature cerebrovascular autoregulatory capacity. Although causality cannot be inferred, these findings support further investigation of transfusion-related hemoglobin changes using cerebral NIRS.

Peripheral Oxygenation

In contrast to cerebral oxygenation, no significant longitudinal changes in peripheral tissue oxygenation or oxygen extraction were observed. Since change-on-change analyses were not feasible because of missing data, FHbF-related effects on peripheral tissue compartment remain unclear.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include the prospective design, repeated paired measurements, and within-infant analytical approach minimizing inter-individual confounding. Limitations include the modest sample size, not inclusion of infants with birth weight <1,500 g, limiting generalizability to very low birth weight, very preterm, or critically ill neonatal populations. Reduced data availability at later time points precluded later change-on-change analyses. Moreover, FHbF was estimated by capillary co-oximetry without repeat or reference-method confirmation. Therefore, preanalytical and analytical variability may have influenced individual FHbF trajectories. Finally, although HbF and HbA differ slightly in near-infrared absorption characteristics, previous neonatal data suggest that the resulting error is small and negligible [25]. Accordingly, findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in less stable neonatal populations exposed to the RBC transfusions.

Conclusion

In this prospective observational study, within-infant postnatal FHbF changes were associated with concurrent cerebral oxygenation and oxygen extraction changes in stable preterm and term neonates without red blood cell transfusion. FHbF decline was associated with increased cStO2 and decreased cFTOE, whereas FHbF increase showed opposing associations. Confirmation in larger cohorts of extremely preterm neonates is needed.
