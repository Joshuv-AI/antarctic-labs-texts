Parental Attachment and Mental Health Literacy in Young Adults: The Mediational Role of Emotional Regulation on Well‐Being and Psychopathological
Authors: Catarina Pinheiro Mota, Cristina Peixoto Alves, Daisy Araújo Rodrigues, Mariagiulia Galluzzo, Margarida Alves, Mónica Costa, Margarida Simões, Inês Relva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEntering higher education can represent significant challenges to the adaptation of young students. Supportive family relationships and mental health literacy can act as protective factors, favoring regulatory mechanisms that promote adaptive experiences. This study aims to analyze the role of attachment to parents in well‐being and psychopathological symptoms, as well as the role of mental health literacy in these outcomes, testing the mediating role of emotional regulation in the above associations. The sample consisted of 2258 young adults aged 18 to 35, collected using a random probabilistic method. The measures for data collection were a demographic questionnaire, the Psychological Well‐Being Manifestation Measure Scale, the Affect Regulation Checklist, the Mental Health Literacy Scale, and the Short Form Version of the Father and Mother Attachment Questionnaire. The results reveal indirect effects of emotional regulation on the association between parental attachment and well‐being, as well as with psychopathological symptoms. Similarly, there are positive associations between mental health literacy and well‐being and psychopathological symptoms in young students. The results will be discussed in light of attachment theory, emphasizing individual and relational resources in promoting well‐being and mental health among university students. The study's practical implication involves designing interventions that bolster parental engagement while concurrently fostering student autonomy.

Introduction

Attachment theory is characterized by the development of significant emotional bonds with an attachment figure during childhood, which are maintained throughout the life cycle and constitute a source of protection, comfort, and support (Bowlby 1988). This behavior is considered an integral part of human nature, necessary and relevant in establishing emotional bonds with others (Bowlby 1988). According to the author, significant attachment figures can be regarded as secure bases because of the continuity and intensity of the care provided. Bowlby also points out that in significant emotional relationships, dynamic internal working models are constructed from childhood through early relational experiences with parents or primary caregivers, corresponding to representations of the self, others, and the world. Thus, positive interactions with significant figures of affection can facilitate positive self‐representations and promote autonomy and exploration of the external relational context (Mikulincer and Shaver 2016). Secure attachment supports healthy development, as young people feel confident and safe, making them more open to developing new relationships and facing new challenges outside the family environment (Bowlby 1988).

Emotional experiences throughout development are particularly relevant in light of the transition processes that characterize adolescence and young adulthood. Attachment patterns are appropriate and can be reorganized; however, stability from childhood to adulthood can have a direct impact on the establishment of new relationships throughout life (Fraley 2002). In this sequence, attachment transfer occurs, with attachment behavior being dynamic (Fraley 2002). Adolescence and young adulthood represent a period of transition characterized by changes and challenges, with an emphasis on processes of separation‐individuation (Arnett 2016; Claro and Mota 2019; Sagone et al. 2023). Separation‐individuation is a process that develops from childhood onwards, allowing children and young people to build increasing autonomy through personal differentiation (Arnett 2016; Morais and Mota 2024). Adolescence and young adulthood are characterized by refocusing, during which emerging adults transition from dependent adolescents to independent young adults (Granja and Mota 2018; Tanner 2006). In this sense, behavior that was once regulated by others may be progressively replaced by self‐regulation, requiring greater independence, self‐regulation, and autonomy (Granja and Mota 2018; Tanner 2006). Throughout this period, the quality of the emotional bond is particularly relevant to the separation‐individuation process, as young people face emotional, social, and academic challenges (Arnett 2016).

Emerging adults with a more positive perception of the quality of emotional relationships tend to report higher psychological well‐being and lower levels of psychological distress (Mendoza et al. 2019). Psychological well‐being is a relevant component of young people's quality of life and a pressing psychosocial indicator (Massé et al. 1998), enabling them to face difficulties, contributing to increased levels of happiness and a greater predisposition to achieve life goals and objectives (Aldbyani et al. 2025). Psychological well‐being is typically characterized by indicators of self‐esteem, self‐control, sociability, balance, happiness, and contextual involvement, rather than merely the absence of psychopathological symptoms (e.g., anxiety and depression) exhibited by the individual (Massé et al. 1998). The quality of attachment may play a crucial role in the well‐being of emerging adults, as secure attachment facilitates greater adaptability in young adults when facing difficulties (Plantade‐Gipch et al. 2023). Secure relationships allow young people to have positive experiences and develop socio‐emotional skills, contributing to greater well‐being (Valarezo‐Bravo et al. 2024). Bohn et al. (2020) found an association between the quality of attachment relationships and well‐being, specifically in terms of communication, trust, and happiness. Similarly, Granja and Mota (2018) observed that young adults who experienced an environment characterized by trust, clear communication, respect, and appreciation developed greater psychological well‐being. Valarezo‐Bravo et al. (2024) found that secure attachment to parents is associated with a higher state of well‐being.

Additionally, the quality of emotional bonds can promote adaptive emotional regulation in individuals throughout their lives (Bowlby 1969). Bowlby (1969) emphasized that the quality of attachment is intrinsically linked to emotional control and maturation, whereas dependence, insecurity, and inhibition of individuality seem to increase difficulties in emotional regulation. Gross (2014) defines emotional regulation as the automatic or controlled, conscious or unconscious process by which individuals experience or express their emotions. Emotional regulation involves changes in one or more aspects of emotion, the situation that causes it, attention, evaluations, subjective experience, and behavior (Plantade‐Gipch et al. 2023). Claro and Mota (2019) found a positive association between attachment to parents and emotional regulation, specifically between separation anxiety and inhibition of exploration and individuality, and difficulties in emotional regulation in young adults. In this sense, a relationship on the basis of trust and security can facilitate adaptive emotional regulation strategies (Claro and Mota 2019; Henschel et al. 2020). In turn, a study conducted by Ferraro and Taylor (2021) with 269 university students found an association between insecure attachment and difficulties in emotional regulation, namely in the identification, processing, and communication of emotional experiences.

Similarly, adaptive emotional regulation can contribute to higher levels of well‐being, with greater ability to regulate emotions increasing the likelihood of young adults developing skills such as concentration, self‐acceptance, autonomy, and personal fulfillment (Plantade‐Gipch et al. 2023). In this sense, the ability to manage emotions adaptively in challenging contexts promotes the adoption of adaptive coping strategies, leading to greater well‐being (Martínez‐Líbano et al. 2025). Hu et al. (2014) highlighted an association between emotional regulation and well‐being, suggesting that the use of adaptive emotional regulation strategies can strengthen both well‐being and life satisfaction. Similarly, Kraiss et al. (2020) found that the grouping of adaptive emotional regulation strategies was positively associated with well‐being. Note that the literature has reported some controversy in the discussion of the implications of sex on the well‐being of university students. Some studies suggest that there are no significant differences between females and males in terms of well‐being (Anker et al. 2025; Mattelin et al. 2025; Sigmundsson et al. 2025; Urbón et al. 2025). However, other studies have reported significant differences in some dimensions of psychological well‐being, with males scoring higher on self‐acceptance and autonomy (Matud et al. 2019; Yudiani et al. 2024). Since this is a significant gap, it is important to address it to understand its implications for young people in a university context.

Emotional regulation also seems relevant for dealing adaptively with young people's challenges, as negative emotional states predict difficulties adapting to university life (Campos et al. 2024). The transition to university life presents young adults with social, academic, and personal challenges, which tend to highlight difficulties (Kilinç and Kendirkiran 2025). In this sense, in some situations, university can represent a risk context for the development of psychopathology, especially in students with greater difficulties in adapting, being more susceptible to developing mental health difficulties (Elkin et al. 2025; Massano‐Cardoso et al. 2024). Throughout their journey at university, many students are unable to identify emotional distress and the symptoms of the most common mental disorders, such as anxiety and depression, which tend to delay their seeking help (Elkin et al. 2025).

In this sense, mental health literacy becomes important for early diagnosis and intervention (Kilinç and Kendirkiran 2025). Mental health literacy is defined by Jorm et al. (1997) as the knowledge and beliefs about mental disorders that facilitate their recognition, management, and prevention. This concept includes recognizing symptoms of mental disorders, understanding available treatments, and seeking help (Kilinç and Kendirkiran 2025). Mental health literacy enables the adoption of coping strategies, such as seeking help, which are associated with greater life satisfaction and psychological well‐being (Reyes and Hocson 2025), as well as the prevention of mental disorders (Shichen et al. 2025). Specifically, awareness of mental health resources plays a key role in happiness, as it can provide confidence to seek help in situations of greater vulnerability, reducing stress and promoting resilience (Elkin et al. 2025). On the other hand, a lack of mental health literacy can contribute to stigma surrounding people who face mental health difficulties (Goda et al. 2025; Neto et al. 2021). Stigma stems from fears and misconceptions about mental health, which can lead to exclusion, difficulties in recovery, and difficulties in accessing and seeking mental health services, and can be countered by improving mental health literacy (Edward 2025). Young people with greater awareness and knowledge about mental health recognize the benefits of early intervention and treatment, which reduces barriers to seeking help and accepting treatment (Kilinç and Kendirkiran 2025). Wei et al. (2022) analyzed the implications of a mental health literacy intervention in the transition to higher education, finding that students who benefited from the intervention showed improvements in mental health knowledge, a decrease in stigma related to mental illness and perceived stress, as well as an increase in positive attitudes towards seeking help.

Perceived emotional distress has also been described differently according to sex (Amaro et al. 2024; Massano‐Cardoso et al. 2024; Mota et al. 2024). A study conducted by Massano‐Cardoso et al. (2024) with university students found significantly higher average levels of anxiety and stress in females compared to males. Similarly, Mota et al. (2024) identified lower levels of anxiety in males. In turn, Amaro et al. (2024) observed a higher prevalence of perceived depressive and anxiety symptoms in female university students. Literature reports this evidence with cultural issues where usually internalizing symptoms are associated with females (e.g., sadness, low impulsivity, anxiety, and depression). In contrast, males tend to have higher externalizing symptoms (Matos et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2025).

Studies addressing mental health literacy among young adults are scarce in the literature, specifically in Portuguese universities. Studies conducted in other countries suggest the need for preventive interventions and mental health promotion in the university context (Shichen et al. 2025; Teixeira et al. 2022). In this sense, mental health literacy can promote the development of adaptive coping and emotional regulation strategies, early recognition of psychopathological symptoms, and seeking professional help (Reyes and Hocson 2025; Shichen et al. 2025). Therefore, it is essential to implement intervention programs to enhance mental health literacy among university students, equipping them with the knowledge and skills necessary to prevent the development of mental health difficulties (Kilinç and Kendirkiran 2025; Ozturan and Kocakaya 2023; Shichen et al. 2025). Consequently, promoting mental health and well‐being in universities is fundamental to academic success and resilience (Gambolò et al. 2025).

This study is part of an action research project entitled YOUNG(in)_Mental_Health_UTAD—Prevention and Promotion of Mental Health and Well‐Being in University Students Based on Participatory Methodology, developed at the University of Trás‐os‐Montes and Alto Douro (UTAD) in Portugal. It aims to develop targeted interventions focused on the needs of young university students. It adopts an active and participatory methodology, enabling collaborative work with students. The action plan comprises activities that span various levels and areas of activity, on the basis of the stepped care model, which aims to provide each individual with the healthcare they need in the most appropriate form and intensity. This model encompasses intervention proposals spanning mental health prevention and promotion to interventions in situations of severe risk (Bailey et al. 2022).

Objectives

The main objectives of the present study are to test the role of attachment to parents in psychological well‐being and psychopathological symptoms of young people, and the role of mental health literacy in relation to psychopathological symptoms and psychological well‐being. The study also analyses the mediating role of emotional regulation in the association between attachment to parents and psychological well‐being, as well as in psychopathological symptoms of young people. The role of sex is controlled in psychological well‐being and psychopathological symptoms.

Method

Participants

The sample includes 2258 young adults (64% women and 36% men) who attend university, aged 18 to 35 (M = 20.53; SD = 2.90). The young comprise qualifications ranging from 1st‐year undergraduate to PhD, with 26.9% attending the first year of a bachelor's degree, 26.9% were attending the second year of a bachelor's degree, 24.4% were attending the third year of a bachelor's degree, 4.6% were attending the fourth year of a bachelor's degree, 13.7% were attending the first year of a master's degree, 2.7% were attending the second year of a master's degree, and 0.8% were attending PhD. Most participants (92.8%) are satisfied with the subject they are attending. More than half of young adults (75.5%) left their parents' homes to attend university. A few of them (11.4%) are currently receiving psychological/psychiatric treatment, and 30.3% have received it in the past. Approximately 22.5% of young people report having taken or currently taking medication for a psychological condition. Additionally, 63.9% indicated that they were aware of the university's psychological support services (Table 1).

Measures

The sociodemographic variables include demographic variables such as sex, age, and area of residence. Furthermore, it assessed key dimensions of participants' academic trajectories: academic performance, identification with the chosen subject, adaptation to higher education, satisfaction with the university experience, and engagement in extracurricular and recreational activities. The measures also collected information regarding family background, social involvement, lifestyle habits, and prior psychological support.

Psychological Well‐Being Manifestation Measure Scale (WBMMS) (Massé et al. 1998; Portuguese adaptation by Monteiro et al. 2012). It is a self‐report instrument designed to assess individuals' level of psychological well‐being, on the basis of the expression of behavioral, emotional, and cognitive indicators associated with adaptive psychological functioning. It is composed of 25 items distributed across six dimensions: Happiness (e.g., “I felt mentally balanced”), Sociability (e.g., “I laughed easily”), Control of self and events (e.g., “When faced with complex situations, I was able to resolve them clearly”), Social involvement (e.g., “I was curious and interested in all sorts of things”), Self‐esteem (e.g., “I felt that others liked me and appreciated me”), and Balance (e.g., “I lived at a normal pace and did not overindulge”). However, in the present sample, the second‐order confirmatory factor analysis indicated that all subscales contributed similarly to a common factor. Thus, the unidimensional composite version of the scale was adopted, per the principles of parsimony and the structural validity of the construct (Kline 2023). All items are positively worded and scored on a five‐point Likert scale, ranging from (1) “Never” to (5) “Almost Always”. Higher total scores reflect higher perceptions of psychological well‐being. In the present sample, the internal consistency of the unidimensional structure, assessed using Cronbach's alpha, was considered adequate (α = 0.95). The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed a good fit to the model: χ 2 (264) = 2089.78, p < 0.001; χ 2/gl = 7.72, CFI = 0.95; TLI = 0.94, RFI = 0.93, IFI = 0.95, NFI = 0.94, SRMR = 0.04, and RMSEA = 0.06.

Affect Regulation Checklist (ARC) (Moretti 2003; Portuguese adaptation by Santos et al. 2025). This is a self‐report instrument designed to assess emotional regulation processes through 12 items distributed across three dimensions: Control (e.g., “I find it difficult to control my feelings”) and Suppression (e.g., “I keep my feelings to myself”), which reflect indicators of maladaptive emotional regulation, and Adaptive reflection (e.g., “Thinking about why I have different feelings helps me learn about myself”), which refers to an adaptive component of emotional regulation. Each dimension is rated on a five‐point Likert scale, ranging from (1) “Does not describe me at all” to (5) “Describes me very well”. The results show globally adequate levels of internal consistency for the three dimensions: Control (α = 0.80), Suppression (α = 0.75), and Adaptive reflection (α = 0.77). It is important to note that item 11, which was reverse‐worded, was removed because of unsatisfactory performance in the model, compromising the quality of the factor structure (Kline 2023). The CFA supported the four‐factor model with acceptable fit indices: χ 2(37) = 316.71, p < 0.001, χ 2/gl = 8.56, CFI = 0.96; TLI = 0.95, RFI = 0.94, IFI = 0.96, NFI = 0.96, SRMR = 0.03, and RMSEA = 0.06.

Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS) (O'Connor and Casey 2015; Portuguese adaptation by Neto et al. 2021). This self‐report scale is designed to assess individuals' knowledge and understanding of topics related to mental health, through 35 items distributed across six dimensions: Recognition of disorders; Knowledge of risk factors and causes; Knowledge of self‐treatments; Knowledge of available professional help; Knowledge of how to seek mental health information; and Attitudes that promote recognition and appropriate self‐seeking. However, only those dimensions demonstrating satisfactory structural validity were retained due to identified weaknesses in internal consistency and the inadequacy of specific dimensions in the CFA (Kline 2023). The present study includes: Recognition of mental disorders (e.g., “How willing would you be to employ someone if you knew they had a mental illness”), Knowledge of how to seek mental health information (e.g., “I am confident using the computer or telephone to seek information about mental illness”), and Attitudes that promote recognition and appropriate self‐seeking (e.g., “Knowledge on generalized personality disorder”). The Recognition of disorders dimension is measured using a four‐point Likert scale, with response options ranging from (1) “Very unlikely” to (4) “Very likely”. In turn, the response scales for the dimensions Knowledge of how to seek mental health information and Attitudes that promote recognition and appropriate self‐seeking consist of five levels, ranging from (1) “Strongly Disagree” to (5) “Strongly Agree”, and (1) “Definitely Unavailable” to (5) “Probably Available,” respectively. It should be noted that, as they are negatively worded, items 20 through 28 are reverse‐scored. The final score is calculated by summing the responses to all items. Psychometric analyses yielded satisfactory Cronbach's alpha coefficients for all dimensions under investigation: Recognition of disorders (α = 0.84), Knowledge of how to seek mental health information (α = 0.83), and Attitudes that promote recognition and appropriate self‐seeking (α = 0.89). The results of the CFA indicated a good model fit to the empirical data, as demonstrated by the following fit indices: χ 2(15) = 76.99, p < 0.001, χ 2/df = 5.13, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, RFI = 0.98, IFI = 0.99, NFI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.03, and RMSEA = 0.04.

Short Form Version of the Father and Mother Attachment Questionnaire (FMAQ) (Matos and Costa 2001; Portuguese adaptation by Nunes et al. 2019). It is a self‐report questionnaire that aims to assess the quality of attachment of young people to their father and mother. It consists of 15 items divided into three dimensions: Quality of the Emotional Bond, Separation Anxiety, and Inhibition of Exploration and Individuality. The dimension Quality of the Emotional Bond (e.g., “My parents know me well.”) refers to the importance attributed by the individual to parental figures; the Separation Anxiety dimension (e.g., “I'm always anxious to be with my parents”) encompasses the individual's perception of anxious experiences and fear related to separation from the attachment figure and the Inhibition of Exploration and Individuality dimension (e.g., “My parents hardly listen to me”) It concerns the individual's perception of constraints to personal expression and exploitation. All dimensions range in a 6‐point Likert‐type response scale (from 1—“Strongly disagree” to 6—“Strongly agree”). In the present study, the instrument presented adequate psychometric characteristics, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.91/0.94 (mother/father) in the Quality of the Emotional Bond dimension; 0.73/0.75 (mother/father) in the Separation Anxiety dimension, and 0.75/0.80 (mother/father) in the Inhibition of Exploration and Individuality dimension, in the maternal version. The CFA showed adequate adjustment indices for the maternal version, χ 2(83) = 655.56; p < 0.001; χ 2/gl = 7.90; CFI = 0.96 TIL = 0.95, RFI = 0.95, IFI = 0.96, NFI = 0.96; SRMR = 0.04, and RMSEA = 0.05, and in the paternal version, χ 2(83) = 585.436, p < 0.001, χ 2/gl = 7.053, CFI = 0.97, TIL = 0.97, RFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.97, NFI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.04, and RMSEA = 0.05.

The Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI) (Derogatis 1982; Portuguese adaptation by Canavarro 1999). It is a self‐report instrument that aims to assess the type of psychopathological symptomatology, not allowing any type of diagnostic formulation. It consists of 53 items and consists of 8 dimensions; however, in the present study, we chose to use only the Anxiety and Depression scales. The Anxiety dimension (e.g., “Having terror or panic attacks”) consists of finding indicators such as nervousness and tension, as well as symptoms of generalized anxiety, panic attacks, and cognitive components that include apprehension and somatic symptoms of anxiety. In the Depression dimension (e.g., “Feeling hopeless about the future”), it presents several indicators of clinical depression, namely, symptoms of affect and dysphoric mood, loss of vital energy, lack of interest in life and motivation. All dimensions vary on a 5‐point Likert‐type response scale (from 0 – “Never” to 4 – “Very often”), where the individual is asked to rate the degree to which each problem listed has bothered him or her during the last week. In the present study, the instrument presented adequate psychometric characteristics, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.88 in the Anxiety dimension and 0.89 in the Depression dimension. The confirmatory factor analysis showed adequate adjustment indices, χ 2(49) = 520,101, p < 0.001; χ 2/gl = 10.614, CFI = 0.97, TIL = 0.96, RFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.97, NFI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.03 and RMSEA = 0.07.

Procedures

The UTAD Ethics Committee has approved the current research project – CE‐UTAD (Ref. Doc77‐CE‐UTAD‐2024), and all the research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the Oviedo Convention, the General Data Protection Regulation, and the European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity (ALLEA). All directors from the different UTAD schools/departments were contacted by email to coordinate the administration of the questionnaire in person. This collection was carried out from September 2024 to March 2025. During data collection, a brief overview of the research project was presented. Data were collected online through the LimeSurvey platform, ensuring voluntary participation, informed consent, anonymity, and data confidentiality. The order of the instruments in the research protocol was randomly inverted to minimize fatigue‐related response bias. Each administration lasted approximately 30 min. The researchers supervised the completion of the questionnaire. Participants and the institutions involved did not receive any reward or remuneration for their participation. All participants signed an online informed consent form that ensured compliance with the principles of voluntariness, ethics, and confidentiality. The questionnaire return rate is around 80% of requests.

Data Analysis

The present study is quantitative, cross‐sectional, and correlational, and its general objective is to verify the relationship between the variables under study and to corroborate the results with previous investigations by the scientific community (Marôco 2018). Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29. After introducing the data into the program, the sample was cleaned to remove missing values and outliers. Multivariate outliers were identified using the Mahalanobis distance (Marôco 2018). The internal consistency of the instruments was also assessed using Cronbach's alpha. Confirmatory factor analyses of the instrument models were performed in AMOS (IBM SPSS AMOS, version 29) to assess their adequacy, using the maximum likelihood estimation method. To verify the adequacy of the model for the data of the present research, measures were used to evaluate the model's adjustment. The reference values for acceptable adjustment values of NFI, TIL, and CFI were ≥ 0.90, and the values of RMSEA and SRMR were < 0.10 (Kline 2023).

Parametric statistical analyses were then used, as the assumptions of normality were ensured (Marôco 2018). The statistical analysis of the data was carried out through preliminary analysis (frequencies, means, and standard deviation). Finally, a mediation analysis was carried out using the AMOS program to assess the indirect effect of the independent variables (Mental Health Literacy and Attachment to the Father and Mother) on the dependent variables (Psychological Well‐being, Anxiety, and Depression) through the mediating variable (Emotional Regulation), controlling for sex (dummy variable) in the model. Despite the model's complexity and the high number of parameters to be estimated, the sample size is sufficient to allow for an accurate analysis. The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.4, considering the type of analysis planned for the study, with an effect size of d = 0.05, a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.95, which predicted a minimum of 513 participants. This estimate is largely surpassed by the sample size under study.

The Mediating Role of Emotional Regulation in the Relationship Between Attachment to Parents and Well‐Being and Psychopathological Symptoms

Path analyses were conducted to test the mediation between the independent variable (quality of bond to the father and mother), the mediating variable (emotional regulation), and the dependent variables (psychological well‐being and psychopathological symptoms). Parental attachment was analyzed separately for fathers and mothers, although included in the same model, as this allows us to understand the role of each parental figure separately. The results of the structural equation modeling analyses revealed positive associations between the quality of the emotional bond with the mother (β = 0.24, p < 0.001) and the father (β = 0.21, p < 0.001) and psychological well‐being. Conversely, negative associations were observed between separation anxiety towards the mother (β = −0.10, p = 0.02) and the father (β = −0.11, p = 0.02), as well as between inhibition of exploration and individuality with respect to the maternal figure (β = −0.07, p = 0.01) and psychological well‐being.

In addition, the results revealed predictive effects of separation anxiety toward the mother on anxiety (β = 0.20, p < 0.001) and depression (β = 0.17, p < 0.001). Inhibition of exploration and individuality to the mother also showed positive associations with anxiety (β = 0.12, p < 0.001) and depression (β = 0.13, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the results indicated an initial negative association between the quality of the emotional bond with the mother and both anxiety (β = −0.14, p < 0.001) and depression (β = −0.18, p < 0.001). Similarly, negative associations were observed between the quality of the emotional bond with the father and anxiety (β = −0.07, p < 0.001) and depression (β = −0.06, p < 0.001). The inhibition of exploration and individuality toward the father was removed from the model, as it proved to be a variable with no predictive value in the dependent and mediating variables, and thus did not fit the model.

Following the introduction of the mediating variable, the effects of inhibition of exploration and individuality, as well as separation anxiety in relation to the mother, on psychological well‐being lost their significance in the model. The results also indicate that the strength of the association between the quality of the father's emotional bond (β = 0.16, p < 0.001) and the mother's (β = 0.10, p < 0.001) and psychological well‐being decreased in magnitude. The association between inhibition of exploration and individuality in relation to the mother and anxiety (β = 0.07, p < 0.001) and depression (β = 0.07, p < 0.001) also decreased in magnitude in the model. The same was observed for the quality of the emotional bond established with the father figure in relation to anxiety (β = −0.04, p < 0.001) and depression (β = 0.08, p < 0.001). The results also highlight a reduction in the magnitude of the association between separation anxiety toward the mother and anxiety (β = 0.12; p < 0.001) and depression (β = 0.10, p < 0.001). The quality of the emotional bond with the mother also showed a significant reduction in magnitude in the final model, both regarding the relationship with anxiety (β = −0.09, p < 0.001) and with depression (β = −0.14, p < 0.001) (Figure 1). The dimension inhibition of the father's exploration and individuality was removed, as it did not play a significant role in the model (Kline 2023).

Conceptual framework.

The results indicate the existence of a total negative mediation of the control (β = −0.05, p < 0.001) and suppression (β = −0.02, p < 0.001) dimensions of emotional regulation in the association between inhibition of exploration and individuality in relation to the maternal figure and psychological well‐being. It was also possible to observe total negative mediations of the control (β = −0.08, p < 0.001) and suppression (β = −0.02, p < 0.001) dimensions in the association between separation anxiety in relation to the mother and psychological well‐being. There were also two positive partial mediations between the variable inhibition of exploration and individuality in relation to the mother and depression, through the emotional regulation dimensions control (β = 0.06, p < 0.001) and suppression (β = 0.02, p < 0.001). Positive partial mediations were also found between the variable inhibition of exploration and individuality towards the maternal figure and anxiety (control: β = 0.07, p < 0.001; suppression: β = 0.02, p < 0.001). Analysis of the model also shows that the control dimension has indirect effects on the association between the quality of the emotional bond with the father and psychological well‐being, through a positive partial mediation (β = 0.02, p < 0.001) (Table 3) (Figure 2).

The Predictive Role of Mental Health Literacy in Well‐Being and Psychopathological Symptoms

In the present model, some additional predictive effects were observed. It was found that the recognition of disorders of the Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS) positively predicts the size of the anxiety (β = 0.03, p = 0.03). In turn, the dimension of knowledge of how to seek mental health information positively predicts anxiety (β = 0.03, p = 0.037), as well as psychological well‐being (β = 0.18, p < 0.001). Regarding the attitudes that promote recognition and appropriate self‐seeking, it was found to positively predict anxiety (β = 0.10, p < 0.001) and depression (β = 0.05, p = 0.002) (Table 3) (Figure 2).

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to examine the predictive role of parental quality of relationships on the psychological well‐being, anxiety, and depressive symptoms of university students. Additionally, we aimed to investigate the mediating effect of emotional regulation on these associations. Furthermore, we investigated the predictive contribution of mental health literacy on well‐being, as well as on anxiety and depressive symptoms self‐reported by young adults.

The results indicate that the quality of the emotional bond established with both parents is positively associated with psychological well‐being. Conversely, maternal inhibition of exploration and individuality, as well as separation anxiety in relation to both parents, emerge as negative predictors of young university students' psychological well‐being. These findings suggest that relationships characterized by consistent support and security from both parents, maintained throughout emerging adulthood, tend to foster higher levels of psychological well‐being. This interpretation is supported by empirical evidence showing that parental relationships marked by warmth, availability, and responsiveness are associated with greater psychological adjustment in young adults, which, in turn, translates into higher levels of happiness and life satisfaction (Bohn et al. 2020; Marrero‐Quevedo et al. 2019; Navaneetham and Kanth 2022). Thus, and in line with attachment theory, the quality of the parental bond seems to reinforce the perception of self‐efficacy and support the construction of a resilient personal identity, capable of resulting in increased levels of psychological well‐being among young adults (Bowlby 1988; Fraley 2002; Marrero‐Quevedo et al. 2019; Mendoza et al. 2019; Moreira et al. 2021; Navaneetham and Kanth 2022; Plantade‐Gipch et al. 2023). In contrast, young people's recognition of maternal behaviors that restricted their exploratory initiatives and high levels of overprotection by both primary caregivers tended to be associated with less favorable assessments of well‐being. In line with the results presented, a study conducted by Calvo et al. (2022) with a sample of young adults showed that lower levels of psychological well‐being are associated with insecure attachment styles, characterized by high anxiety indicators. This evidence suggests that relational insecurity, marked by fear of abandonment and difficulty in trusting the availability of parental figures, can compromise the emotional balance and overall perception of well‐being of young adults (Calvo et al. 2022). Similarly, perceptions of constraints around exploratory behaviors on the part of the mother, as well as the level of support for autonomy provided by both caregivers, capable of raising indicators of emotional dependence, seem to condition the expression of well‐being among young people in the university (Marrero‐Quevedo et al. 2019; Mendoza et al. 2019; Mikulincer and Shaver 2016).

The results also indicated that the quality of the emotional bond established with both parents is negatively associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms. In contrast, separation anxiety and the inhibition of individuality exploration in relation to the maternal figure are positively associated with the manifestation of anxiety and depressive symptoms. The quality of the affective bond maintained with both parents during young adulthood may represent a relevant protective factor for mental health, providing an emotional security base that fosters self‐regulation (Mak et al. 2021; Navaneetham and Kanth 2022; Nunes et al. 2025; Reis et al. 2025). Parental relationships characterized by consistent support, emotional availability, and validation of individual experiences create a secure relational context that can promote adaptive processing and emotional validation, thereby mitigating the intensity and frequency of anxious and depressive symptoms (Rivers et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2022). In turn, overprotective and autonomy‐inhibiting parental behaviors, manifested through excessive levels of supervision and control, prove to be particularly detrimental to the adjustment of young adults (Mendoza et al. 2019). Studies by Navaneetham and Kanth (2022) align with the results presented, mentioning that high levels of parental control, both behavioral and psychological, are associated with exacerbated symptoms of anxiety and depression, contributing to a lower perception of mental health.

The analysis of the results identified relevant indirect effects between the quality of attachment to parents and the psychological well‐being of university students. Considering the mediational models, the findings reveal a positive association between separation anxiety from the mother and emotion regulation (control and suppression). A similar association was observed between the inhibition of exploration of individuality by the mother and emotion regulation skills. In contrast, negative associations emerged between the quality of the emotional bond with the father and the control dimension of emotion regulation and between the quality of the emotional bond with the mother and both the control and suppression dimensions. Therefore, parental relationships characterized by higher levels of inhibition of exploration and individuality, and separation anxiety appear to be positively associated with increases in maladaptive indicators of emotion regulation. Conversely, the quality of the emotional bond with parental figures appears to limit maladaptive tendencies towards emotional control and suppression. In line with the literature, different attachment styles have been associated with individual differences in the capacity for emotion regulation (Bowlby 1969; Mikulincer and Shaver 2016; Tammilehto et al. 2022; Valarezo‐Bravo et al. 2024). Interactions with maternal figures characterized by high separation anxiety and excessive control regarding the inhibition of exploration of individuality tend to exacerbate feelings of devaluation, as well as conditioning the effectiveness of young people's emotional regulation, leading them to have difficulties in controlling their emotions and resorting to emotional suppression tools that are poorly adaptive (Claro and Mota 2019; Tammilehto et al. 2022). This result may be because young students not only display maladaptive strategies for dealing with emotional distress but also increased tendencies to avoid experiencing emotions associated with vulnerability (Claro and Mota 2019; Ferraro and Taylor 2021). Conversely, the quality of the emotional bond established with parents appears to constitute a protective factor in the emotional regulation of young people (Claro and Mota 2019; Valarezo‐Bravo et al. 2024). Parental relationships characterized by security, care, and emotional availability appear to foster more constructive and effective mechanisms for impulse control and problem‐solving, greater clarity in the identification of emotions, as well as adaptive emotional regulation strategies (Claro and Mota 2019; Fraley 2002; Mikulincer and Shaver 2019; Moreira et al. 2021; Plantade‐Gipch et al. 2023). Thus, quality parental bonds can act as a resource capable of reducing difficulties surrounding emotional regulation (Medeiros et al. 2022), favoring more adaptive responses to stress and contributing to higher levels of psychological well‐being (Sagone et al. 2023). On the other hand, when the parental relationship is less responsive and secure, young people may develop a regulatory style focused on emotional restraint and avoidance, which, although functional in specific contexts (Tammilehto et al. 2022), tends to weaken their perception of well‐being (Calvo et al. 2022; Chervonsky and Hunt 2017; Kraiss et al. 2020; Marrero‐Quevedo et al. 2019; Mendoza et al. 2019; Sagone et al. 2023). In view of the above, it is important to emphasize that emotional regulation is not only an intrapersonal resource but also a process deeply influenced by relational experiences, with the potential to sustain or compromise psychological well‐being throughout young adulthood (Ferraro and Taylor 2021; Mendoza et al. 2019; Sagone et al. 2023).

The results also confirm that emotional regulation mediates the relationship between the quality of the emotional bond with both parents and anxiety and depressive symptoms. More specifically, the quality of the emotional bond appears to indirectly affect the manifestation of anxiety and depressive symptoms through emotional control. Furthermore, the mediating role of emotional regulation (suppression and control) is evident in the association between the quality of the emotional bond with the mother figure and the presence of anxiety and depressive symptoms. These results corroborate previous evidence that secure parental bonds, characterized by consistent support, affective validation, and emotional availability, favor the development of adaptive regulation strategies, such as cognitive revaluation, impulse control, and adjusted emotional expression, capable of promoting more adaptive emotional functioning (Bowlby 1988; Mikulincer and Shaver 2019; Moreira et al. 2021). In contrast, lower‐quality parental relationships tend to be associated with less functional regulatory patterns, such as emotional suppression and difficulties with emotional control, which can undermine the effective management of affective responses and adaptive problem‐solving (Aldao et al. 2010; Gross 2014). The literature further indicates that deficits in emotional recognition and difficulties in emotion regulation are linked to a higher prevalence of mental health problems, including depression (Li et al. 2019) and anxiety (O'Toole et al. 2019), as well as other psychopathological conditions that impair adaptation and well‐being (Plantade‐Gipch et al. 2023). Accordingly, the findings of the present study indicate that the quality of the parental bond serves as an indirect protective factor against anxiety and depressive symptoms by reducing the use of maladaptive emotion regulation strategies that tend to intensify internalizing symptoms. In this regard, promoting adaptive emotion regulation strategies within the university context emerges as a key preventive approach, contributing to the mitigation of anxiety and depressive symptoms in university populations.

The results indicate that emotional regulation exerts a partial and positive mediation in the relationship between separation anxiety and psychopathological symptoms (anxiety and depression). This mediation occurs exclusively through maladaptive strategies of emotional control and suppression, which also mediate the association between separation anxiety and the inhibition of individuality exploration in relation to the maternal figure. According to the literature, young people with higher levels of separation anxiety, often characterized by frequent security‐seeking, a pronounced need for validation, and heightened sensitivity to rejection, tend to experience greater difficulties in identifying and accepting their own emotions (Bowlby 1988; Ferraro and Taylor 2021; Henschel et al. 2020). At the same time, these young people experience more intense and prolonged negative affective states, preferentially resorting to maladaptive emotional self‐regulation strategies such as rumination and catastrophizing (Henschel et al. 2020; Marganska et al. 2013). Similarly, parental relationships characterized by autonomy inhibition and restriction of individuality exploration tend to contribute to feelings of worthlessness and inefficacy, negatively impacting the consolidation of emotional regulation strategies among youth (Bowlby 1988; Claro and Mota 2019). Empirical evidence indicates that difficulties in emotional regulation constitute a cross‐cutting risk factor for various psychopathological conditions (Plantade‐Gipch et al. 2023), particularly regarding the manifestation of anxiety and depressive symptoms (Hong et al. 2022; O'Toole et al. 2019). Thus, the results of the present study support a model in which separation anxiety and the inhibition of exploration and individuality within the context constitute relational factors that impair emotional regulation, promoting the adoption of strategies centered on rigid control and suppression of emotional expression. The use of maladaptive emotional regulation strategies, in turn, appears to increase vulnerability to the development of anxiety and depressive symptoms. These findings underscore the importance of developing intervention programs to strengthen regulatory competencies and foster emotional autonomy among university students.

Regarding the objective of testing the role of mental health literacy in relation to psychopathological symptoms, it was possible to observe that the attitudes that promote recognition and appropriate self‐seeking positively and significantly predict the dimension of anxiety and the dimension of depression. In view of these results, it is possible to state that when university students demonstrate significant attitudes toward the recognition of disorders and engage in help‐seeking behaviors, they seem to be more attentive and aware of present anxiogenic and depressive symptoms. In addition, it is verified that when university students are able to correctly identify characteristics of disorders, they tend to present anxiogenic symptoms. These interpretations are supported by empirical evidence showing that the influence of depressive and anxiety symptoms is present in the behavior of young students when seeking help (da Conceição et al. 2024). In this sense, it is important to address this type of symptom in interventions that aim to promote and encourage seeking professional help (da Conceição et al. 2024). These results are further supported by the study by Magaard et al. (2017), which found that individuals are most likely to seek professional help when they believe they can accurately assess their mental health status and understand the benefits of seeking help.

Regarding the dimension of knowledge of how to seek mental health information, it was found that it positively and significantly predicts anxiety symptoms. Given this result, it is possible to say that when university students pay greater attention to their experiences, have greater knowledge about mental health and ways of accessing content of this nature, they can present anxiogenic symptoms. This result is in line with the study carried out by Afolayan (2022), which showed that university students have high levels of anxiety when looking for information, given the barriers they face, namely, information resources, the search for information itself, identification of the theme they are looking for, and issues related to computers and the internet. Anxiety is considered fundamental to the process of seeking information; therefore, young students exhibit a high demand for information (Afolayan 2022). In this sense, anxiety can manifest itself during the process of seeking information, which on the one hand implies that they are more attentive to their mental health, but on the other hand, it may have repercussions in the form of negative feelings, such as frustration, palpitations, restlessness, and fear (Afolayan 2022). In turn, the dimension of knowledge about seeking mental health information positively and significantly predicts the dimension of psychological well‐being. Given this result, when young university students show increased attention to their experiences, greater knowledge about mental health, and how to access information on this topic, they tend to have a higher level of psychological well‐being. This result corroborates previous evidence that mental health literacy and mental health information‐seeking behavior can play a crucial role in promoting university students' mental health and mental health awareness (DeBate et al. 2018; Kutcher et al. 2016; Seboka et al. 2022). Studies have also shown that young university students' use of technology to seek information about mental health has proven very useful (Chen et al. 2020; Coughlan et al. 2021; Gazibara et al. 2020).

Finally, it was found that the covariate sex has a positive and significant effect on psychological well‐being. Given this result, it is possible to conclude that, in the present study, young male university students tend to have higher levels of psychological well‐being than female students. Previous studies have shown that differences in psychological well‐being between the sexes have been associated with cultural aspects, discrimination, or gender‐based violence, which consequently lead to higher levels of stress and mental overload more significantly in females (Krishnakumar et al. 2018; Van Droogenbroeck et al. 2018). This exacerbation of symptoms in female individuals stems from a tendency to internalize symptoms more (Matos et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2025), as well as from their ability to recognize and express symptoms more easily (Braz‐José et al. 2022). A similar association was observed in the study by Amaro et al. (2024), which found better mental health among males. Likewise, Aguiar et al. (2024), who aimed to verify the impact of the confinement resulting from COVID‐19 on the mental health and psychological well‐being of higher education students in a Portuguese context, found that females had higher levels of stress, anxiety, and depression, which was reflected in a lower appreciation of their own psychological well‐being.

In turn, the covariate sex predicts, in a negative and significant way, anxiety and depression symptoms. In view of this result, it is possible to state that female higher education students tend to manifest a greater propensity to experience anxiogenic and depressive symptoms when compared to males. This result may be explained by cultural factors related to differences in the manifestation of internalizing and externalizing symptoms by sex (Graves et al. 2021; Knapstad et al. 2021; Wickramasinghe et al. 2023). This evidence is reported by Matos et al. (2017) and Sun et al. (2025), who demonstrate that females tend to exhibit greater internalizing symptoms, whereas males report greater externalizing symptoms. On the basis of scientific evidence, females experience several physiological reactions throughout their lives, including hormonal fluctuations, which directly affect their mood and stress response (Cardoso et al. 2024). In addition, females tend to find it easier to express their feelings and recognize depressive and anxious symptoms (Braz‐José et al. 2022). On the other hand, males are more likely to develop aggressive behaviors, irritability, and put themselves in risky situations (Mohammadi et al. 2023). This result will also in line with previous studies in Portuguese university students and denotes a higher prevalence of perception of depressive and anxiogenic symptoms in female university students, as well as better mental health in male students (Aguiar et al. 2024; Amaro et al. 2024; Massano‐Cardoso et al. 2024; Mota et al. 2024).

Practical Implications, Limitations, and Suggestions for Future Studies

This research offers notable contributions to advancing scientific knowledge of the association among attachment to parents, mental health, and self‐regulatory processes among young university students, with significant practical implications for higher education. The results highlight the protective role of parental relationships in the adaptive functioning of young university students by promoting intrinsic self‐regulation skills that favor indicators of well‐being and mental health. Thus, this study serves as a starting point for developing awareness‐raising initiatives for parental figures to enhance their available and consistent involvement in the transition and continuity of young people's university trajectory, while also promoting their autonomy and individuality. In addition, it highlights the importance of implementing structured programs to develop emotional regulation skills in the university context, incorporating adaptive coping and stress management strategies for the student body.

In Southern European contexts, where parenting tends to be more protective and helicopter‐oriented, the influence of parental attachment on students' self‐regulation and well‐being may be amplified. Strong cultural norms that value close family bonds can enhance the benefits of supportive parental involvement during university transitions; however, heightened control may complicate efforts to foster autonomy. These findings underscore the importance of culturally sensitive initiatives that strike a balance between parental guidance and the promotion of independence, thereby ensuring that involvement does not become overly protective.

The contributions of the present study also underscore the role of mental health literacy, which is innovative in Portugal, as a factor that can enhance young people's sensitivity to identifying internalizing symptoms, which, in turn, may lead to a greater demand for professional support. In this regard, it is considered relevant to allocate resources to safeguard the mental health of university students by increasing the recruitment of specialized professionals, namely psychologists and psychiatrists, within higher education institutions (Conrad and Riba 2021; Cardoso et al. 2024). Furthermore, it is recommended that promotion and prevention programs be implemented that emphasize the active role of mental health literacy, through workshops and digital resources designed to foster the early identification of signs of psychological distress, reduce stigma, and encourage help‐seeking behaviors (Kilinç and Kendirkiran 2025; Shichen et al. 2025). These measures should be integrated into universities' institutional policies and operationalized through multidisciplinary programs involving professors, psychologists, and other relevant professionals within the academic community (Kilinç and Kendirkiran 2025). It is also imperative to invest in the training of both teaching and non‐teaching staff to enhance their knowledge and sensitivity to the early recognition of mental health symptoms and risk factors, thereby enabling more rapid referral processes (Bruce et al. 2025; Khan et al. 2018). In this way, the goal is to foster more protective university environments that strengthen students' internal resources, enabling them to respond effectively to the challenges inherent to the university context and to pursue academic trajectories oriented towards excellence. The present study aims to contribute positively to expanding knowledge about mental health and reinforce the need to demystify the process of seeking psychological help. In this sense, it aims to contribute to a better understanding of the phenomena and symptoms related to university students.

However, it is pertinent to point out some limitations inherent to this research, namely the lack of equality in relation to sex, which does not allow for the generalization of results in relation to this variable; the questionnaires were filled out in an online format, and in some cases did not allow the control of the emergence of possible doubts; it was a cross‐sectional study and, therefore, did not allow the establishment of cause‐effect relationships; the protocol being considerably long and time‐consuming and leading to possible fatigue or boredom effects; the exclusive use of self‐report instruments and the impossibility of making clinical diagnoses with any rigor, since the instruments used only test the symptomatic dimension. It is also considered important that, in future research, samples include more male participants to increase the representativeness of the sample; it would be interesting to replicate the study in a clinical sample to understand better the relationship between the variables studied and to compare the results between them, as well as to invest in longitudinal studies to monitor relevant indicators over time.
