Breaking the Barrier of Tumor Hypoxia: Oxygen-Enhancing Nano Biomaterials in Cancer Therapy
Authors: Xinran Wen, Lufang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor hypoxia is a fundamental hallmark of the solid tumor microenvironment (TME) that severely impairs the efficacy of oxygen-dependent treatments, such as photodynamic therapy (PDT), and drives therapeutic resistance. Overcoming this biological barrier is critical for improving clinical outcomes. This review comprehensively summarizes recent advancements in oxygen-modulating nanobiomaterials designed to tame the hypoxic TME and sensitize tumors to multimodal therapies. We systematically evaluate two primary nanomedicine strategies: exogenous oxygen delivery systems (including hemoglobin-based carriers, perfluorocarbons, and metal-organic frameworks) and in situ oxygen-generating catalysts (such as catalase, solid peroxides, and photocatalytic nanomaterials). Furthermore, nanotechnology-driven approaches for vascular normalization and enhancing oxygen diffusion are discussed. By focusing on material design, we elucidate how stimuli-responsive and actively targeted nanocarriers achieve precise, on-demand TME regulation. Crucially, we explore how these oxygen-enhancing platforms synergize with conventional treatments to reverse therapeutic resistance, enabling robust integrated regimens that combine PDT with chemotherapy, photothermal therapy (PTT), and gas therapy. Finally, we outline current translational challenges, such as nanoparticle stability, deep tumor penetration, and biosafety—and provide perspectives on developing intelligent, multifunctional nanomedicines to definitively break the barrier of tumor hypoxia. To provide a forward-looking paradigm, this review uniquely highlights the disruptive integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in nanomedicine design, single-atom catalysts (SACs) for oxygen-independent Type I PDT, and covalent photosensitizers.Keywords: tumor hypoxia, oxygen-enhancing nanomaterials, photodynamic therapy, metal–organic frameworks, catalase-based nanoplatforms, perfluorocarbons, multifunctional nanocarriers

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, characterized by profound complexity, heterogeneity, and formidable therapeutic resistance. While conventional treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy form the cornerstone of clinical oncology, their efficacy is frequently compromised by dose-limiting systemic toxicity, poor drug solubility, rapid clearance, and a lack of tumor specificity. To address these needs, nanomedicine has emerged as a transformative paradigm. They significantly enhance the pharmacokinetics and bioavailability of therapeutic agents, utilize the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect for passive tumor targeting, and can be functionalized with specific ligands for active cellular uptake. Furthermore, stimuli-responsive nanocarriers enable precise, on-demand drug release triggered by specific cues within the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby maximizing local therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target adverse effects.

The structural and functional attributes of nanomaterials make them exceptionally well-suited for advancing minimally invasive modalities such as photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT).1 PDT and PTT rely on the light-triggered activation of photosensitizers (PSs) and photothermal agents (PTAs) to generate cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) or localized hyperthermia, creating advantages in specificity and low systemic toxicity.2–4 However, many conventional PSs and PTAs are intrinsically hydrophobic, prone to aggregation-caused quenching in aqueous physiological environments and lack natural tumor selectivity. Nanocarriers perfectly resolve these limitations by encapsulating hydrophobic agents to preserve their photoactivity, extending their systemic circulation half-life, and facilitating deep tumor penetration. More importantly, nanoplatforms allow for the co-delivery of multiple agents, such as chemotherapeutics, specific kinase inhibitors, or oxygen generators, creating multifunctional synergistic systems that single-molecule drugs cannot achieve.

Despite the promise of nanotherapeutics, the TME presents a highly dynamic and hostile niche that significantly impedes treatment outcomes. A fundamental hallmark of solid tumors is hypoxia, a condition driven by rapid neoplastic proliferation that outstrips the aberrant and dysfunctional local vascular supply, which is estimated to affect over 50% of solid malignancies.5 The seminal work in 1955 first demonstrated diffusion-limited hypoxia, revealing a maximum oxygen diffusion distance of approximately 145 µm.6 The molecular understanding of this phenomenon identified and cloned the hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) in the 1990s,7 a transcriptional regulator of oxygen homeostasis. The profound oxygen deprivation in tumors is not solely a physical diffusion issue but is deeply rooted in complex metabolic reprogramming. While the shift toward aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) is well-documented, hypoxia and HIF-1α stabilization also drive massive alterations in lipid metabolism to support rapid tumor growth and survival.8,9 These intricate metabolic adaptations exacerbate local oxygen consumption, fortify the tumor’s antioxidant defenses, and create a severely immunosuppressive and therapy-resistant microenvironment.

Consequently, hypoxia inherently paralyzes oxygen-dependent treatments, particularly PDT, which strictly relies on the availability of molecular oxygen to generate singlet oxygen.10–15 Given these profound biological and metabolic barriers, there is an urgent need for innovative nanotechnological strategies designed to tame the hypoxic TME. In this review, we comprehensively examine recent advances in nanomaterial-based platforms engineered to alleviate hypoxia through exogenous oxygen delivery or in situ oxygen generation. Furthermore, we explore multimodal synergistic therapeutic combinations, such as PDT integrated with PTT, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, aiming to provide a definitive perspective on the design principles of oxygen-modulating nanomedicines for highly precise and effective cancer therapy.

Oxygen Delivery Methods in Tumor Therapy

To address hypoxia, various strategies have been developed to enhance oxygen content in tumors or to leverage the hypoxic environment for synergistic therapeutic effects.16,17 Specifically, the rapid development of nanotechnology has provided versatile platforms to effectively modulate the TME. Current oxygen-modulating strategies primarily fall into three categories: delivering pre-loaded exogenous oxygen via nanocarriers, generating oxygen in situ through catalytic nanomaterials, and enhancing natural oxygen diffusion by normalizing tumor vasculature.

Exogenous Oxygen-Supplying Nanomaterials

One direct approach to alleviate tumor hypoxia is the exogenous delivery of oxygen (O2) to the tumor site using specialized nanomaterials. These nanocarriers transport pre-loaded O2 or O2-carrying compounds to the hypoxic TME, increasing the local oxygen concentration and thereby improving the efficiency of oxygen-dependent PDT. These materials typically load oxygen through two main mechanisms: physical dissolution (eg., trapping O2 within highly fluorinated hydrophobic cores) or reversible chemical coordination (eg., binding O2 via transition metal centers in metalloproteins or porous frameworks). During systemic circulation, these nanoplatforms must stably retain O2 to prevent premature leakage. Upon accumulation in the tumor site, the payload release is typically triggered by the steep oxygen partial pressure gradient between the carrier and the severe hypoxic TME, or by specific local stimuli such as the acidic pH and external light irradiation. Currently, several distinct classes of materials are widely employed for this purpose.

Hemoglobin (Hb) has been utilized as an O2 delivery carrier. For instance, Wang et al used Zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc)-loaded Hb microspheres (HbMs) which were shown to yield more singlet oxygen (1O2) and greater photocytotoxicity on HeLa cells.18 Indocyanine green (ICG) and Hb co-delivery systems for CT-26 tumors demonstrated significant anti-tumor effects and a 100% survival rate in mice.19 Other Hb-based systems using Chlorine6 (Ce6) like SRF@Hb-Ce6 for 4T1 tumors shown negligible tumor progression20 and [PHC]PP@HA Ce6 with Hb for PC-3 cells have high rates of tumor cell apoptosis (97.17%),21 respectively. Red blood cells (RBCs) and nano-RBCs, often combined with photosensitizers like Ce6 or Methylene blue (MB), have also been explored for their O2-carrying capabilities, showing significant anti-tumor effects in various tumor models. For example, AmmRBCs with Methylene blue demonstrated a significant anti-tumor effect on 4T1 tumors, and their O2-carrying capability was almost similar to Hb in PBS solution, and notably superior when pretreated with H2O2.22

Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs) are designed to store O2 by exploiting the gas capability of metal ion bonds. Gao et al fabricated a nanoplatform named O2@UiO-66@ICG@RBC constructed for PDT in hypoxic tumors, where UiO-66 (a zirconium (IV)-based MOF) stored O2 (Figure 1a). Under 808 nm laser irradiation, ICG produces 1O2, which degrades the RBC membranes and accelerates O2 release from UiO-66, leading to effective tumor elimination in MCF-7 tumor-bearing mice.23

Perfluorocarbons (PFCs) are another class of O2 carriers that present high stability in organisms and exhibit higher O2 solubility compared to Hb (Figure 1b). Examples include PFH@PEG-F54-BODIPY for A375 tumors, which resulted in significant ablation,24 and PHD@PM loading Sinoporphyrin sodium for 4T1 tumors, which led to complete elimination of subcutaneous tumors.25 Additionally, O2@PFOB@PGL NPs with Porphyrin were shown to eliminate HT-29 tumors.26

In situ Oxygen-Generating Catalysts

While traditional oxygen delivery strategies rely on exogenous oxygen sources which may suffer from premature leakage, a more innovative approach involves generating oxygen directly within the TME through catalytic reactions. This method leverages the endogenous substrates present in the TME, enabling a more efficient and localized oxygenation process to overcome the limitations posed by hypoxia. Based on their distinct catalytic triggers, these in situ oxygen-generating systems can be broadly logically categorized into three pathways: decomposing overexpressed endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), exploiting the acidic TME using solid peroxides, or employing exogenous light for water splitting.

The first fundamental strategy utilizes catalase (CAT), an enzyme capable of decomposing H2O2 into oxygen. This strategy specifically capitalizes on the hallmark oxidative stress of solid tumors, where H2O2 is often significantly elevated due to aberrant metabolic activity. Nanoplatforms designed to incorporate catalase, such as HA-CAT@Ce6 and CAT-THPP-PEG, have demonstrated excellent tumor-targeting capabilities27,28 (Figure 1c). These systems have been shown to significantly inhibit tumor growth, likely due to the combined effects of enhanced oxygenation and increased ROS production. Such approaches take advantage of the endogenous H2O2 present in the TME to catalyze the release of oxygen, thereby improving the efficacy of oxygen-dependent therapies like PDT.

Moving beyond biological enzymes, a second promising class involves the use of solid peroxides, which release oxygen exploiting the acidic pH of the TME, a hallmark of solid tumors, to facilitate oxygen release. Hf-MOF-MgO2/DNA nanoplatforms, for example, have been shown to induce significant damage and apoptosis in tumor cells through this mechanism.29 Similarly, CaO2-based nanoparticles, such as PCN-224-CaO2-HA with TCPP and CaO2/B1/NH4HCO3 liposomes, have demonstrated impressive tumor suppression and even tumor regression, highlighting the therapeutic potential of these systems in combating tumor hypoxia.30

In addition to exploiting endogenous chemical triggers like H2O2 and acidity, a third avenue utilizes external physical stimuli, specifically the photocatalytic decomposition of water. Certain nanomaterials, when exposed to light, can catalyze the breakdown of water into oxygen, offering a direct means of overcoming hypoxic conditions. Nanomaterials like R-NCNP (Carbon Nitride) and Ti3C2/g-C3N4-TPP have shown promising results in this regard, with R-NCNP significantly reducing tumor volume in 4T1 models, and Ti3C2/g-C3N4-TPP inducing extensive necrosis in MCF-7 cancer cells.31,32 These photocatalytic systems offer a dual benefit: they directly address tumor hypoxia while simultaneously enhancing the efficacy of light-based therapies like PDT.

Beyond traditional enzymatic and photocatalytic systems, the emergence of single-atom catalysts (SACs) represents a quantum leap in the design of in situ catalytic nanomedicines. Unlike conventional nanoparticles whose catalytic activity is confined to surface atoms, SACs feature isolated metal atoms anchored on solid supports, offering maximum atom-utilization efficiency and unique electronic structures33 The intrinsic catalytic performance of SACs is highly governed by the finely tuned coordination environment of these isolated metal centers, which efficiently traps electrons and lowers the activation energy for radical or oxygen formation. By precisely regulating the local electronic states of the metal atoms, these advanced platforms can robustly catalyze the conversion of abundant endogenous substrates into oxygen or ROS.

In summary, in situ oxygen-generating nanomaterials represent a powerful strategy to alleviate tumor hypoxia. By either catalyzing the decomposition of endogenous H2O2 or utilizing photocatalytic reactions, these systems are able to generate localized oxygen within the TME, thereby improving the efficacy of various oxygen-dependent treatments and offering new possibilities for enhancing cancer therapy.

Vascular Normalization and Oxygen Diffusion Assistance

Strategies focused on direct oxygen delivery and in situ oxygen generation primarily aim to increase the availability of oxygen within the TME, while another way to enhance oxygen diffusion is worth paying attention to. Approaches that target vascular normalization and oxygen diffusion enhancement address the structural and physiological barriers that hinder oxygen penetration. These strategies work by improving the condition of tumor vasculature and the ability of oxygen to diffuse deeper into the hypoxic regions of tumors.34,35

One effective approach involves hypoxia normalization, where certain nanomaterials or nano-sensitizers are used to alter the tumor’s hypoxic conditions. For example, hypoxia-responsive polymeric vesicles such as poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly (methacryloyl metronidazole) (PEG-b-PMNZ) have been designed to destabilize in the presence of low oxygen levels, enabling targeted drug release at the tumor site.36 This response not only enhances the therapeutic action of the delivered drug but also contributes to alleviating hypoxia, thereby improving the effectiveness of chemoradiotherapy. By normalizing the hypoxic TME, these strategies also enhance the responsiveness of tumors to oxygen-dependent therapies like PDT and radiotherapy.37 Alongside these responsive carriers, nanomaterials with intrinsic catalytic properties, such as cerium oxide nanoparticles (CNPs), can actively facilitate vascular normalization.38 By utilizing surface Ce3⁺/Ce4⁺ redox cycling to modulate local oxygen dynamics and stabilize HIF-1α, CNPs catalytically mitigate severe hypoxia and assist in remodeling abnormal vascular networks.

Building upon this localized vascular normalization, broader TME remodeling plays a crucial role in improving oxygen diffusion and enhancing overall treatment efficacy. TME is characterized by a range of hostile factors, including acidosis, high interstitial pressure, and abnormal vasculature, all of which create significant barriers to oxygen distribution. One key factor that can aid in overcoming these barriers is the EPR effect,39 where nanoparticle-based photosensitizer (NP-based PSs) passively accumulate at tumor sites due to the leaky blood vessels and impaired lymphatic drainage commonly found in tumors.40 NPs with a size smaller than 100 nm are particularly effective in exploiting this phenomenon.41

To further improve the effectiveness of oxygen diffusion within the TME, nanotechnology can be employed to optimize the behavior of nanoparticles in the tumor site. Strategies such as hydrophilization of NPs using polyethylene glycol (PEG), the hitchhiking of NPs on blood cells or albumin, and the camouflaging of NPs with immune or blood cell membranes have all shown potential in prolonging the circulation time of NPs and improving their accumulation at the tumor site.42,43 These modifications not only help in reducing macrophage-mediated clearance but also minimize off-target accumulation, ultimately leading to more efficient modulation of the TME.44 Such strategies help in enhancing oxygen diffusion by improving the delivery of both oxygen and therapeutic agents to the tumor, addressing the complex challenges posed by the TME.

To summarize, vascular normalization and TME remodeling are critical strategies in improving oxygen access to tumors. Through the combination of nanomaterial engineering and innovative targeting strategies, these approaches can significantly enhance the efficacy of oxygen-dependent therapies by overcoming the structural barriers that typically limit oxygen penetration in solid tumors (Figure 1d).

Novel Nanomaterials and Delivery Systems

Significant advancements in nanotechnology have paved the way for novel strategies to overcome tumor hypoxia and enhance the efficacy of PDT. Nanocarriers offer numerous advantages in cancer treatment due to their ability to improve drug properties and target delivery.

Advantages of Nanocarriers

Nanocarriers significantly enhance the solubility, stability, and bioavailability of therapeutic agents. For instance, bovine serum albumin (BSA)-based nanomedicines like HA@IR780@KU55933@BSA (HIKB) can protect photosensitizer IR780 from photodecomposition, maintaining stability under light exposure. Nanoparticles also exhibit prolonged systemic circulation, facilitating enhanced drug accumulation at the tumor site, as seen with HIKB NPs which maintain a presence in the bloodstream for up to 96 hours post-injection. This prolonged circulation helps overcome rapid systemic clearance, a common issue with free drugs. Furthermore, nanocarriers can improve tumor targeting and TME responsiveness, allowing for precise and controlled drug delivery.45

Tumor Targeting Strategies

Effective tumor targeting is crucial for maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target toxicity.46 Nanomaterials employ various strategies to achieve this:

Active Targeting

This strategy leverages the overexpression of specific receptors on tumor cells.47 By functionalizing nanocarriers with ligands that bind to these receptors, drug accumulation at the tumor site can be significantly improved.48 Examples for lung cancer cells and other cancer models include: CD44: This cell surface glycoprotein receptor is overexpressed in a majority of cancer cells, including lung cancer cells.49 Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a common ligand used to target CD44. HA-functionalized nanoparticles have shown enhanced cellular internalization and improved tumor accumulation in various models, leading to significant reduction in tumor volume. Epidermal Growth Factor Receptors (EGFRs): These are transmembrane glycoproteins overexpressed in non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs).50 Cetuximab (CTXmab), an EGFR ligand, has been used to functionalize nanoparticles for improved drug delivery to tumors.51 Folate Receptors (FRs): Cancer cells often overexpress FRs to meet their high demand for folic acid, essential for rapid proliferation.52 Folic acid can be used as a targeting moiety to enhance nanoparticle accumulation in tumors.53 Transferrin Receptors (TFRs): Lung cancer cells overexpress TFRs to acquire iron for their metabolic needs. Transferrin (TF) or its agonists can be used to target these receptors, improving tumor accumulation of nanotherapeutics.54

Additionally, bacteria-mediated tumor homing has emerged as a cutting-edge active targeting strategy to overcome the physical barriers of the TME. Unlike conventional nanoparticles that primarily rely on passive accumulation or ligand-receptor interactions, certain facultative anaerobic bacteria exhibit a natural tropism for the hypoxic and necrotic regions of solid tumors. Recent advances highlight the profound potential of harnessing these bacteria as living delivery vehicles. For instance, integrating nanomaterials with tumor-homing bacteria can significantly enhance localized drug delivery, facilitate microbial niche editing, and exert robust immunomodulatory effects.55 This biohybrid approach perfectly leverages the hypoxic TME, transforming a therapeutic barrier into a highly specific guiding signal for synergistic cancer therapies.

Passive Targeting

This strategy relies on the EPR effect characteristic of tumor vasculature.56 Tumors often have leaky blood vessels and impaired lymphatic drainage, leading to preferential accumulation of nanoparticles (typically 20–200 nm in size) within the tumor tissue. For example, IKB NPs showed increased fluorescence intensity in tumor tissues compared to free IR780, which was attributed to the EPR effect.

Cell Membrane Biomimetic Delivery

This approach involves coating nanoparticles with cell membranes (eg., RBC membranes) to impart properties similar to natural cells, thereby improving tumor targeting and immune evasion. RBC membrane coating can significantly extend the systemic circulation half-life and reduce uptake by the reticuloendothelial system (RES). AmmRBCs, for instance, were designed with an RBC membrane coating to achieve prolonged blood circulation and enhanced tumor accumulation for self-oxygenated PDT.57 Similarly, O2@UiO-66@ICG@RBC uses RBC membranes to avoid immune clearance. This strategy has shown promise in improving the spatial distribution and therapeutic efficacy of nanoparticles in lung cancer models.58 Erythrocyte leveraged chemotherapy (ELeCt), a form of RBC hitchhiking, involves adsorbing drug-loaded nanoparticles onto RBC membranes ex vivo to facilitate their delivery to target organs like the lungs, where high shear stress induces detachment and localized accumulation.59 This strategy has been shown to effectively inhibit lung metastasis progression and improve animal survival.60 Immune cell membrane coating, such as T-cell membrane-coated nanoparticles, can enhance tumor infiltration and provide synergistic therapeutic effects by carrying various functional molecules like LFA-1, FasL and PD-1.61

TME Responsive Nanomaterials

Designing nanomaterials that respond to specific cues within the TME enables precise and on-demand drug release, enhancing specificity and reducing systemic toxicity.

PH-Responsiveness: The tumor microenvironment is typically acidic (pH 5.6–6.8) due to anaerobic metabolism and lactate accumulation.62 pH-responsive materials undergo charge reversal or conformational changes in this acidic environment, facilitating enhanced cellular uptake and controlled drug release at the tumor site.46 For example, HA modified BSA nanoparticles can promote drug release in an acidic environment, minimizing off-target toxicity. Micelles that exhibit charge transformation from negative to positive at acidic TME pH have shown enhanced cellular uptake, nuclear localization, and significant reduction in tumor burden and metastasis.

ROS-Responsiveness: Tumor cells often exhibit elevated levels of ROS.63 While excessive ROS can induce cell death, tumor cells also upregulate antioxidant systems, leading to a reductive intracellular environment.64 ROS-responsive materials can be designed to release their cargo in response to these conditions.

A covalent photosensitizer, MBTP-PA, is designed to react with intracellular thiol compounds such as cysteine and glutathione (GSH), disrupting the antioxidant system and alleviating hypoxia (Figure 2).65,66 This reaction-based therapy not only enhances PDT efficacy but also induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) through ferroptosis and pyroptosis, which are both highly effective even under hypoxic conditions.67,68 MBTP-PA nanoparticles have been shown to eliminate primary tumors and inhibit distant tumors with minimal side effects in vivo.

Redox-responsiveness: Redox-responsive systems are particularly relevant given the reductive state of the TME. Nanoparticles with disulfide linkages in their backbone can degrade in the presence of GSH, leading to controlled drug release and enhanced therapeutic efficacy.69 Dual pH and redox-responsive nanoparticles have been developed to achieve sequential drug release, deep tumor penetration, and reversal of multidrug resistance, further enhancing tumor-specific accumulation.

Integrated Multi-Stimuli Responsive Nanoplatforms: Building upon these individual responsive strategies, there is a growing emphasis on advanced, multi-stimuli TME-responsive nanoplatforms capable of integrating photodynamic, photothermal, and chemodynamic therapies (CDT) into a single, synergistic modality. For instance, recent studies have developed highly efficient systems such as the M-HMnO2@ICG platform designed for the multimodal treatment of cervical cancer.70 This platform utilizes hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide loaded with the photosensitizer ICG and is camouflaged by HeLa cell membranes to ensure precise homologous targeting. Upon entering the acidic TME, the HMnO2 carrier responsively degrades to achieve on-demand ICG release. Crucially, this microenvironment-triggered degradation self-generates O2 to relieve local hypoxia, while simultaneously consuming intracellular GSH to amplify the oxidative stress required for CDT. Under near-infrared laser irradiation, the efficiently accumulated ICG exerts a synergistic PDT and PTT effect. Such integration of TME-responsive PDT, PTT, and CDT provides a robust and comprehensive paradigm for overcoming tumor barriers and maximizing combinatorial therapeutic efficacy.

Multifunctional Integrated Nanoplatform Design

To address the complex heterogeneity and immunosuppressive microenvironment of tumors, the future of nanomedicine lies in multifunctional integrated nanoplatforms.71 By combining multiple therapeutic and targeting modules within a single “all-in-one” system, this approach overcomes the spatiotemporal limitations of administering free drugs separately. Typically, these platforms integrate oxygen delivery or in situ generation modules72 with hypoxia-resistant PSs73 and other synergistic therapies (eg., chemotherapy, PTT, gas therapy, or ATM kinase inhibitors like KU55933), all guided by precise TME-responsive targeting elements.

A representative paradigm of this integration is demonstrated by Nie et al, who designed a MOF-coated MnO2 nanosheet system for the co-delivery of doxorubicin and a self-activated DNAzyme.74 In the tumor microenvironment, the MnO2 nanosheets degrade to release oxygen and Mn2+ ions. This not only alleviates hypoxia but also provides the required Mn2+ cofactor to in situ self-activate the DNAzyme, acting in synergy with the released doxorubicin. Another prominent example is the V-M-K-T vesicle system, which integrates a hypoxia-activated prodrug (TPZ) with an oxygen-depleting agent (KP372-1) and an Akt inhibitor (MK-2206) for enhanced radiosensitization.75,76 Such integrated platforms illustrate how nanomaterials can utilize multi-step biochemical reactions to overcome TME-associated resistance mechanisms, promoting robust anti-tumor responses more effectively than traditional monotherapies.

Synergistic Strategy Platforms

To maximize therapeutic efficacy and address the multifaceted challenges posed by tumors, particularly hypoxia, researchers are developing synergistic platforms that combine PDT with other therapeutic modalities.79 Compared with monotherapies, combining PDT with other treatment modalities—such as chemotherapy, photothermal therapy, and immunotherapy—can harness their respective advantages while compensating for individual limitations. This synergistic approach not only enhances the therapeutic efficacy of PDT and reduces side effects on healthy tissues, but also addresses critical challenges of monotherapies, including tumor resistance and metastasis.80,81

PDT Combined with PTT

This approach leverages the heat generated by PTT to enhance the efficacy of PDT and vice-versa, often mediated by a single or double laser irradiation (Figure 3).77,82 Nanoscale covalent organic framework (NCOF)-based platforms like VONc@COF-Por showed high 1O2 quantum yield and photothermal conversion (55.9%) under red LED and 808 nm laser irradiation, effectively inhibiting MCF-7 tumor cell proliferation and metastasis.83 Tph-DMTP-COP nanoparticles also exhibited excellent effects under 650 and 808 nm laser irradiation with a 34.88% photothermal conversion efficiency.84 Pd nanosheet-holey Pd NSs (H-Pd NSs) with high photothermal conversion efficiency (35.1%) further enhanced PDT by catalyzing H2O2 decomposition into O2, achieving a 99.7% tumor inhibition rate in a 4T1 tumor model.85 To provide a more intuitive overview of these dual-mode approaches, Table 2 highlights representative synergistic nanoplatforms that integrate PDT with PTT. Synergistic Strategy Platforms for PDT with PTT and Mechanisms

Despite these remarkable preclinical outcomes, the translation of integrated PDT/PTT platforms requires overcoming critical constraints. Dual-laser systems complicate clinical operation and often suffer from mismatched tissue penetration depths between different wavelengths. Furthermore, while mild PTT relieves hypoxia, excessive hyperthermia can trigger severe vascular collapse and localized thrombosis, paradoxically exacerbating oxygen deprivation and completely shutting down subsequent PDT efficacy.

PDT Combined with Chemotherapy

PDT can be synergized with chemotherapy, with some strategies even leveraging the hypoxia created by PDT to activate chemotherapy drugs.86 For example, an AQ4N@CPC-FA nanoplatform combined high targeting with PDT and HAPs chemotherapy, significantly inhibiting tumors in MCF-7 xenograft mice (Figure 4).87 Similarly, a liposome nanocomplex containing TPZ and PS IR780 utilized the hypoxia environment formed during PDT to activate TPZ chemotherapy for breast cancer.88

PDT Combined with Immunotherapy

PDT alone may have insufficient efficacy in hypoxic environments, but combining it with immunotherapy can compensate for this. ROS induced by PDT can activate pyroptosis, which is a promising stimulus for anti-tumor immunotherapy due to its minimal invasiveness and spatiotemporal control.78,89 Strategies exploiting ROS under hypoxic conditions focus on inducing pyroptosis affect.

Pyroptosis Induction: Hemin-derived carbon nanodots (HNCDs) encapsulated with BSA to form HB nanoparticles can induce pyroptosis of tumor cells even under extremely hypoxic conditions (2% O2). This process is due to HB nanoparticles’ excellent Type I photodynamic activity, modulated by their single atomic Fe–N4 center, which synergistically alters energy levels and electron transfer to convert Type II photodynamic processes to Type I. HB nanoparticles induced nearly 86.18% cell death under both hypoxic and normoxic conditions, significantly outperforming Type II PDT agents like PB nanoparticles under hypoxia.90 This strong pyroptosis inducers evoke a significant anti-tumor immune response, effectively restraining tumor growth and lung metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) mice.

Co-delivery for Pyroptosis and Apoptosis: HIKB combines IR780-mediated PDT with an ATM inhibitor (KU55933) to elicit pyroptosis and apoptosis for TNBC therapy. HIKB nanoparticles demonstrate specific cellular internalization and mitochondrial co-localization in TNBC cells, while minimizing uptake by normal cells.91–93 Upon laser irradiation, HIKB significantly enhances 1O2 production and triggers pyroptosis via the GSDME-mediated pathway. In vivo studies showed that HIKB + Laser treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice and exhibited good biosafety with no significant organ damage.

Type I Combined with Type II PDT

This combination is another strategy to enhance treatment effects in hypoxic environments. A 2D heterostructure, Ti3C2/g-C3N4-TPP, activated by NIR light, reduced O2 to superoxide radical (O2−•) and generated hydroxyl radical (•OH) through exciting holes, realizing Type I PDT via electron transfer. Concurrently, this process decomposed H2O to provide abundant O2 for Type II PDT to produce 1O2. The combination of 670 nm and 808 nm laser irradiation resulted in a tumor cell survival rate as low as 27%, significantly lower than single-wavelength treatments.94

PDT Combined with Gas Therapy

Some platforms combine PDT with the release of therapeutic gases. For example, CyI-DNBS was developed for synergistic PDT and SO2 gas therapy.95 This kind of PS can be taken up by cancer cells and react with GSH, generating SO2 for gas therapy, through which the activated PS CyI-OH produce 1O2 under red light irradiation.

Synergistic Ion-Interference and Stimulus-Responsive Therapies

To further broaden the horizon of combined therapy strategies, recent advancements have explored the synergistic integration of stimulus-responsive ROS generation (such as ultrasound or phototherapy) with metal ion-interference therapy. Certain metal-based platforms, notably calcium copper phosphate nanomaterials (Ca19Cu2(PO4)14), have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in inducing tumor cell death through this synergistic mechanism.96 Under physical stimulation like ultrasound, these nanomaterials efficiently degrade to synergistically release copper and calcium ions into the tumor microenvironment. The released Cu2+ acts as a potent catalyst for Fenton-like redox reactions, generating abundant ROS that severely amplify intracellular oxidative stress and initiate mitochondrial damage. Concurrently, the burst release of Ca2+ triggers massive intracellular calcium overload, which regulates the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore and significantly accelerates membrane depolarization. This dual-ion synergistic mechanism provides an innovative, drug-free combined therapeutic strategy that robustly activates the intrinsic apoptotic pathway and successfully circumvents conventional chemoresistance in highly invasive tumors.

Conclusion and Prospects

Hypoxia within the TME remains a fundamental barrier to the success of oxygen-dependent therapies, particularly PDT. This review has summarized recent advances in oxygen-enhancing nanobiomaterials—including exogenous oxygen carriers, in situ oxygen-generating catalysts, and strategies to normalize tumor vasculature and improve oxygen diffusion—that have shown considerable potential for alleviating tumor hypoxia. We have also highlighted tumor-targeting strategies, stimuli-responsive nanocarriers, and multifunctional platforms that integrate PDT with chemotherapy, photothermal therapy, gas therapy, and immunotherapy to achieve synergistic anticancer effects.

The Conclusion of Different Nanomaterials and Clinical Translation Difficulty

While traditional cancer therapies have established a robust foundation for modifying the TME via oxygen delivery, recent therapeutic progress demands more precise, multi-scale engineering strategies, meanwhile reduce off-target toxicity, thereby paving the way for safer and more effective clinical applications. Main directions include size optimization, surface coating, targeted moieties conjugation and multifunctional compartmentalization.103 Next generation nanomedicines must transition from passive oxygen-delivering carriers toward active, multi-component therapeutic systems. These advanced platforms integrate generative AI, single-atom catalytic medicine, target covalent inhibition, and programmed cell death pathways to achieve enhanced therapeutic efficacy and systematic biosafety.

AI-Driven Molecular Engineering and Adaptive Dosimetry

The integration of AI transforms cancer nanomedicine from empirical, trial-and-error workflows toward highly predictable, data-driven optimization. At the molecular level, deep generative networks, including RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN, and AlphaFold3, to enable the de novo design of high-affinity protein binders with Kd values below 10 nM in a matter of weeks.102,104,105 At the clinical level, machine learning models, such as artificial neural networks and gradient boosting regression trees, are being deployed to recover tissue optical properties from real-time light measurements with an error rate of less than 6%. This computational efficiency allows treatment planning algorithms like PDT-SPACE to re-optimize diffuser power allocation dynamically. Clinically, automated photonics platforms like the DigiLum system utilize these closed-loop ML algorithms to standardize intraoperative PDT and eliminate operator-dependent variability. However, predictive accuracy remains heavily dependent on the quality and breadth of training datasets. Current algorithms often struggle to fully simulate unpredictable in vivo biological complexities, such as dynamic protein corona formation and subsequent immune clearance.

Oxygen-Independent Catalysis and Regulated Cell Death Crosstalk

SACs offer a promising oxygen-independent alternative. SACs feature uniform active sites, identical coordination environments, and high atom-utilization efficiency. The intrinsic mechanism of ROS generation by SACs is highly dependent on the finely tuned coordination environment of the isolated metal centers, which can efficiently trap electrons and lower the activation energy for radical formation.106 A prominent example is the development of cobalt single-atom catalysts with specific coordination environments (such as Co-N4-x-Cx).33 By precisely regulating the local electronic states of these metal atoms, SACs can robustly catalyze the conversion of oxygen or abundant endogenous substrates into highly toxic ROS. The clinical translation is primarily bottlenecked by their high surface energy, which makes them highly prone to aggregation. Mass-producing uniform coordination environments at an industrial scale remains immensely difficult, a challenge further compounded by their largely uncharted long-term in vivo toxicokinetics.

Biorheological Targeting and Biomimetic Barriers

To cross complex biological barriers, biomimetic strategies—including cell-membrane-camouflaged nanoparticles and RBC hitchhiking—have emerged as key translation-ready platforms. Specifically, the ELeCt platform utilizes non-covalent nanoparticle assembly on the RBC surface, extending blood circulation up to 16.6-fold and achieving a 10-fold higher drug delivery to metastatic lungs through shear-dependent cell deformation in capillary beds.60 To resolve the logistical and safety challenges of ex vivo cell manipulation, in situ RBC-hitchhiking platforms, such as i-Bind, use polyphenol functionalization to enable spontaneous, stable attachment of nanoparticles directly in the flowing bloodstream.

Addressing Barriers and Regulatory Milestones

Despite the preclinical success of multi-component systems, significant Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) barriers hinder clinical translation. Maintaining control over nanoscale-specific critical quality attributes (CQAs), such as particle size, polydispersity index, surface charge, and drug loading, is highly challenging during industrial scale-up.107 Small variations in these CQAs can drastically alter in vivo biodistribution and safety, as demonstrated by the clinical failure and discontinuation of DepoCyt due to long-term sterility and reproducibility issues.108

To bridge this translational gap, future development must utilize Quality by design methodologies to systematically map formulation and manufacturing variables. Combining continuous-flow microfluidics with AI-driven predictive modeling will enable the precise and reproducible scale-up of nanoparticle formulations. Furthermore, regulatory bodies must transition from conventional pharmaceutical paradigms to harmonized, science-driven frameworks that explicitly account for nanoscale behavior and nano-specific CQAs. While biomimetic systems represent the nearest-term clinical milestones due to their documented biocompatibility, advanced platforms like SACs represent longer-term milestones that require robust, scalable synthesis protocols and systematic in vivo toxicokinetic profiling.
