Antibiotic Cocktail: An Excellent Tool to Probe Physiology by Perturbing Gut Microbiota: A Mice Model
Authors: Swati Sagarika Panda, Tanya Pattnaik, Palok Aich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotics have become an excellent tool for understanding the role of gut microbes. While the effect of antibiotic treatment on adults is studied, its impact during adolescence is unclear. In the current study, we treated C57BL/6 mice with an antibiotic cocktail composed of nine antibiotics from weaning (3 weeks old) until they became young adults (10 weeks old). We investigated the effect of antibiotic treatment (1) on gut microbiota composition, (2) on the brain by studying the behavior, Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Adrenal (HPA axis), neuroinflammation, neurotransmitters, neuronal health, and appetite regulators, and (3) on systemic circulation by recording changes in the stress hormones, insulin resistance, and metabolic profile. In the gut, we found that the antibiotic treatment significantly increased the Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria while decreasing the Bacteroidetes phylum. In the brain, we observed HPA axis activation, elevated proinflammatory response, decline in neurotrophins, neurotransmitter abundance, and appetite regulators expression, which could be linked with behavioral changes. We found increased insulin resistance and altered metabolite profiles in the peripheral system. Moreover, our association study highlights the role of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria in altering the host behavior, brain function, and systemic circulation. Altogether this study demonstrates, how prolonged antibiotic exposure during adolescence disrupts gut microbiota and is associated with physiological and behavioral alterations through gut peripheral system and brain interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00284-025-04409-5.

Introduction

The discovery of antibiotics was a significant milestone in health science. While known for their ability to treat infections, reckless antibiotic use harms health [1]. Continuous antibiotic usage disrupts the gut microbial population. Gut microbiota monitor health by regulating the physiology and behavior of the host [2–4]. Several factors can influence the gut microbiota population, the overuse of antibiotics being the primary one [5]. The antibiotic composition, treatment duration, and method of administration determine the extent of gut microbiota perturbation.

The connection between the gut and brain is widely established [6, 7], however the precise communication pathways that mediate this bidirectional interaction remain poorly understood Research suggest that one of the primary pathways linking microbiota and the brain involves systemic communication. This communication encompasses interactions within the Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Adrenal (HPA) axis, cytokines, neurotransmitters, and the crosstalk of metabolites [8]. However, Bercik et al. highlighted that the antibiotic-induced perturbation of gut microbiota may affect the brain independently of neurotransmitter and cytokine exchanges [9]. The contradiction in these observation may be due to the use of individual antibiotics or smaller combinations of antibiotics which often exhibit narrow-spectrum activity, targeting only specific bacterial groups. Therefore, a comprehensive cocktail comprising antibiotics with diverse mechanisms of action and spectra is essential for accurately studying microbiota-driven physiological, behavioral, and neurochemical outcomes.

Previous research revealed that different combinations of antibiotics could affect gut–brain function in distinct ways [10]. While some studies have explored the general neurological effects of individual antibiotics, there is a lack of detailed investigation into their region-specific impacts. To our knowledge, no previous research has examined the effects of a combination therapy involving multiple antibiotics on specific brain regions such as the prefrontal cortex (PFC), hippocampus, and hypothalamus.

Various regions of the brain are engaged in distinct functions. The hippocampus, responsible for cognitive function and emotional responses, has been extensively studied for neurodegeneration following antibiotic treatment. However, the impact of antibiotics on the prefrontal cortex, a critical area for emotional control, remains inadequately understood. The hypothalamus, an essential modulator of the HPA axis and has a role in appetite regulation, has also not been thoroughly examined in the context of antibiotic-driven microbiota perturbation.

With the rise of psychiatric cases in adolescence due to the overuse of antibiotics, it becomes crucial to understand the impact of antibiotic treatment on the gut–brain axis. We treated freshly weaned mice (3-week-old) with the antibiotic cocktail and continued till the mice reached adulthood (10-week-old). The weaning age of mice is typically 3 weeks, while 10-week-old mice are classified as young adulthood. Additionally, 6-week-old mice are analogous to 11-year-old humans, representing the pubertal stage. Therefore, to comprehensively examine the effects of antibiotic treatment during critical developmental phases, from weaning through puberty to early adulthood, we selected the 3-week to 10-week age range for our experiments. To assess the progressive impact of antibiotics, we sacrificed mice and collected samples at 3, 4, 6, 8, and 10 weeks of age. In the current study, we focused on understanding the antibiotic treatment effect on (1) gut microbiota profile, (2) behavior, (3) brain chemicals and markers, (4) stress response, (5) insulin resistance, and (6) metabolite profile. This report is the first to show the progressive effect of antibiotics on the gut, brain, and systemic metabolic profile during mice's mid-developmental (week 3 till week 10, weaned to young adult) stage.

Materials and Methods

Animal Housing and Experimental Condition

We conducted experiments with animals according to the National Centre for Animal Research and Experimentation, School of Biological Sciences, NISER, Bhubaneswar, India. Briefly, we housed the C57BL/6 male mice at room temperature (22 ± 3 °C) with 55 ± 5% humidity. We split the mice into two groups. The control group received the autoclaved (sterile) drinking water, while the treatment group received the autoclave drinking water mixed with an antibiotic cocktail (Supplementary Fig. 1). As antibiotic cocktails demonstrate greater efficacy in targeting a broader spectrum of bacteria compared to single antibiotics [11], in our study we used an antibiotic cocktail consisted of nine antibiotics, i.e., Bacitracin (1 mg/ml), Ceftazidime (100 μg/ml), Ciprofloxacin (125 μg/ml), Gentamicin (170 μg/ml), Metronidazole (100 μg/ml), Neomycin (100 μg/ml), Penicillin (100 U/ml), Streptomycin (50 μg/ml), Vancomycin (50 μg/ml). The antibiotics in the cocktail were so chosen that they can perturb gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria to establish a microbe-compromised gut environment or microbial dysbiosis. To avoid the fluctuation in physiology due to the estrous cycle of female mice, we have included only male mice in our study. We provided ad libitum access to food and water to both the control and antibiotic-treated groups. We used three different sets of mice for our research, ensuring that their experimental conditions, housing, age, and strain remained consistent (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

Food Intake Measurement

We monitored the mice throughout the entire experimental timeline, from 3 to 10 weeks, to estimate their food consumption. We used a professional digital tabletop scale (India) to measure the food intake. Each week, we calculated the food intake manually by measuring the food loss per cage and then dividing this amount by the number of mice in each cage.

Behavior Test

Antibiotic treatment has been reported to affect behavior. We conducted four behavioral tests, namely the Open Field Test (OFT), Elevated Plus Maze Test (EPM), Forced Swim Test (FST), and Marble Burying Test (MBT), in both the control and treatment groups. We performed the behavioral test of the control and treated group at 3, 4, 6, 8, and 10 weeks. For each behavior test, we used a different set of mice (Supplementary Fig. 1a). After each behavioral experiment, we euthanized the mice. The details of the methods are described as follows.

Open Field Test (OFT)

We performed the OFT to assess the locomotor activity in mice. We used a box of 50 cm in length and 50 cm in width. We conducted the test by following the protocol described previously [12]. Briefly, we first cleaned the apparatus with ethanol. After evaporation of ethanol, we put the mice at the center of the apparatus for 5 min and recorded the video using a Panlab Videotracker (Harvard Apparatus, USA). We repeated the ethanol washing step after every mouse exposure to the OFT apparatus. We measured the total time spent at the center by each mouse. If the time spent at the center and distance traveled by mice decreases that indicates an anxiety behavior in mice. The number of mice used for OFT is ten.

Elevated Plus Maze (EPM)

We conducted an EPM test to measure the anxiety levels in mice. Following the previously described protocol, we performed the test. We placed each mouse from both the control and treated groups at the center of the apparatus. Every mouse was exposed to the apparatus for 5 min, and we recorded the video using the Panlab video tracker (Harvard Apparatus, USA). We washed the fecal pellet and the urine of mice from the EPM apparatus with ethanol after each mouse experiment. We repeated the experiment for 10 mice. We measured the time spent in the open arm and the distance traveled by the mice to assess the level of anxiety.

Forced Swim Test (FST)

We conducted the FST to evaluate the depression levels in mice. We took a transparent cylindrical container with a length of 25 cm and a width of 1 cm. We filled it with water following established procedures [13]. We put the mice in the water for 6 min and recorded the video using a camera. The last 5 min were taken for the analysis as in the first minute mice were hyperactive [14]. During the test, we measured the time of immobility and mobility. We took 5 mice for the FST. This immobility time serves as an indicator of depression in rodents.

Marble Burying Test (MBT)

We performed the MBT to measure anxiety in the mice following the protocol described elsewhere [15]. In brief, we prepared the cage with bedding and 16 marbles organized in a 4 × 4 manner. We put a mouse into the cage. After 30 min, we removed the mice from the cages and counted the marbles buried in at least two-thirds of their portion. We considered 10 number of mice for MBT.

Sample Collection

We used mice from set 1 to study microbiome and brain activity, where the number of mice used was three. We sacrificed the mice from both the control and treatment groups by cervical dislocation. We collected the sample from the 4-, 6-, 8-, and 10-week-old age groups. We collected the blood by heart punching. As described previously [12], we isolated the serum from the blood by centrifuging at 1600 × g for 10 min and stored for future use.

Similarly, we isolated the whole brain from the mice and washed it with ice-cold PBS. Next, we separated the prefrontal cortex (PFC), hippocampus (HP), and hypothalamus (HT) carefully by using the ice-chilled plate. We kept the sample in the RNA liv (Himedia, India) for the RNA extraction and stored it at − 20 °C. We snap-froze the brain tissues to extract whole metabolites for metabolomics study. We used the cecal content for microbiome and metabolome analyses.

Isolation of Cecum DNA and Microbiome Analysis

We used the supplied QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit to isolate DNA from the cecal content of mice. We determined the isolated DNA quality using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis.

We next proceeded with the sequencing of 16S rRNA at AgriGenome Pvt. Ltd. (India), adhering to the protocol described elsewhere [16]. We targeted the V3–4 region of the 16S rRNA gene utilizing the primer sequences described in the Supplementary Table 1. Subsequently, we used the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit (USA) to prepare the amplicon library, which was then sequenced on the Miseq Illumina platform. The quality of the Fastq files was checked, followed by adapter trimming and the removal of duplicates. Paired-end reads were stitched together using the FLASH tool. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were generated using the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology-1 (QIIME1) pipeline with the UCLUST algorithm. For taxonomic classification, we used the SILVA database. As the sample size was modest, we used Microsoft Excel (USA) to calculate the relative abundance of the taxa.

RNA Isolation and Gene Expression Study

We took 25–30 mg of PFC, hippocampus, and hypothalamus. We extracted the RNA from the manufacturer's instructions for the RNA miniprep purification kit (Himedia, India). We checked the RNA quality using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. We prepared the cDNA from the extracted RNA using the Affinity Script One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Agilent, USA) by following the previously described protocol [17]. Next, we performed the qRT-PCR to know the gene expression. We added the reaction mixture (Master Mix, forward primer, reverse primer, cDNA, and nuclease free water) into a 0.1 ml MicroAMP Fast 96 well plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and thoroughly mixed all the reagents using a plate centrifuge (Neuation, India). We used the GoTaq qPCR Master Mix containing the SYBER green (Promega, USA). The primers used in the current study are listed in the Supplementary Table 1. After preparing the reaction mixture, we proceed with the qRT-PCR run on the QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Massachusetts, USA). Each cycle of amplification consisted of denaturation (95 °C for 2 min), Annealing, and Extension (60 °C for 30 s). After the amplification, we repeated the cycle 40 times and obtained the Ct value. Alongside the gene of interest, we also ran the housekeeping gene (GAPDH) for normalization. For analysis, we first normalized the Ct values of both the control and treated samples to their respective GAPDH values. We then compared the differences between the control and treatment groups and calculated the fold change using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Here, the sample size was three.

Measurement of Blood Fasting Glucose and Insulin Level

We used the mice from set 3 to measure the blood fasting glucose and insulin level. We kept the mice of the control and treatment groups under overnight fasting. We removed the food and water from the cages. After the fasting, we collected the blood from the tail vein of each mouse. We measured the blood glucose level using the Accu-Check Instant S Glucometer (Roche, Switzerland). Using the commercially available kit, we measured the fasting insulin level from serum (Raybiotech, USA). After measuring the fasting blood glucose and insulin level, we calculated the insulin resistance using the following method.

ELISA Assay

We collected the serum from the blood of mice, as described in the previous section. Next, we measured the serum corticosterone and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) per the manufacturer's instructions for the commercially available kit (Elabscience, USA).

Serum Metabolomics Study

We took 50 μl of serum into a 2 ml vial and added 1 ml of 1:1 (v/v) ice-chilled ethanol:methanol with 0.1% formic acid to the sample. We also added 5 μl of internal standard to the sample. After vortexing, we placed the sample at − 20 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, we centrifuged the sample at 14,000 × g for 15 min with a set temperature of 4 °C. Next, we collected the supernatant and put it into a new tube.

Further, we concentrated the supernatant with the help of a vacuum concentrator (Thermo Scientific, USA). Following the evaporation, we added 50 μl of 5:95 (v/v) of acetonitrile and water mixture and transferred the whole amount to the vial for the LC–MS analysis. We used ExionLC UHPLC (Sciex, USA) for the metabolomics study. We used acetonitrile and water as the mobile phase to separate metabolites from the serum sample. Both mobile phases contained 0.1% of formic acid. The gradient used for the elution is shown in Table 1. Our experiment used a C18 column with a diameter of 4.6 mm (Phenomenex, USA) and ran the gradient at the flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. All other parameters remained constant, as described previously [12]. We ran the sample in both the positive and negative modes. After the 20-min run, the data were processed through PeakView software (AB sciex, USA).

We used MS Dial Software [18] with the NIST mass spectroscopy library (USA) to obtain peak area information. The parameters we used to identify the metabolites are described in Supplementary Table 2. Following the extraction of metabolites with their respective area, we did a comparative analysis across all the time points. We consider only metabolites that are either significantly altered or uniquely present in the treatment group. We used three mice samples for metabolomics study.

Measurement of Neurotransmitters

We used LC–MS/MS for the quantification of neurotransmitters in the PFC. Initially, we created a stock solution (1 mg/ml) containing glutamate, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and norepinephrine (NE) using a solvent containing 0.1% formic acid. Subsequently, we diluted the stock with acetonitrile and water to prepare different standards of Glutamate, GABA, and NE.

Next, we prepared the samples by homogenizing 20 mg of PFC and adding a mixture of ice-chilled ethanol and methanol (1:1, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid. Following the addition of the internal standard, the sample was incubated at − 20 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 14,000 × g and 4 °C for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected. We dried the supernatant using a vacuum concentrator (Thermo Scientific, USA) and resuspended it in 50 μl of mobile phase (a mixture of acetonitrile and water). We transferred the solution to the vial for further analysis. Instrument parameters were maintained as described in the previous section. The flow rate for glutamate and GABA was set at 0.5 ml/min, while for norepinephrine (NE), the flow rate was adjusted to 0.8 ml/min. The gradient used for the run is detailed in Table 2.

We employed the Multiple Reactions Monitoring (MRM) mode to detect eluted analytes. We set the depolarization potential for glutamate, GABA, and NE as 70, 50, and 80 while the collision energy was set to 15, 20, and 25, respectively. The optimal parameters were maintained per the previously described protocol [12]. We processed spectra using MultiQuant software of version 3.0.3 (Sciex, USA). With the help of Microsoft Excel, we generated the standard curve and calculated the concentration of each neurotransmitter.

Statistical Analysis

We used GraphPad Prism version 8.0 for all statistical analysis. We conducted two-way ANOVA to assess differences across all measured parameters including gut microbiota composition, behavioral responses, hormone concentrations, gene expression, and neurotransmitter levels between the control and antibiotic-treated groups. Following significant ANOVA results, we conducted Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test as a post hoc analysis to identify specific group differences. The t-values reported correspond to the test statistics from these post hoc comparisons and reflect the magnitude of difference between groups at individual time points. We performed multiple pairwise Pearson correlation analyses between key parameters, using an R-value threshold of 0.7 to identify robust associations. We represented the data as mean ± SEM. The F-values reported are accompanied by their degrees of freedom. The P value of ≤ 0.05 is represented as *, while the P value of ≤ 0.01, ≤ 0.001, and ≤ 0.0001 as **, ***, and ****. unless stated otherwise.

Results

Antibiotic Cocktail Treatment Altered the Gut Microbiota Composition

Antibiotics have the potential to change the gut microbiota status [16]. As previously reported the nine-antibiotic cocktail has the potential to alter the physiology of the mice as well to deplete the microbiota population significantly as compared to the untreated control group of mice [17]. Now to see the effect of our antibiotic cocktail on the gut microbiota composition, we used 16S rRNA sequencing of cecal samples of both the control and treated mice using next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology. We observed that the impact of antibiotics significantly affects the Proteobacteria treatment (F1, 20 = 224, P < 0.0001), Actinobacteria (F1, 20 = 114.7, P < 0.0001), and Bacteroidetes (F1, 20 = 39.00, P < 0.0001). Also a significant interaction was observed between the treatment and time for Proteobacteria (F4, 20 = 25.19, P < 0.0001), Actinobacteria (F4, 20 = 10.80, P < 0.0001), and Bacteroidetes (F4, 20 = 5.287, P = 0.004, Fig. 1A, C, D). Here, F1, 20 refers to the F-statistic with 1 degree of freedom between groups and 20 degrees of freedom within groups, reflecting the variation attributable to antibiotic treatment relative to variation within the sample. From the post hoc test, we found an increase in the Proteobacteria at 6- (t = 11.42), 8- (t = 9.824), and 10-week (t = 9.517) antibiotic-treated mice (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1c). However, the Actinobacteria population increased significantly at 4-week age (P = 0.0476, t = 2.868) and remained high at 6- (t = 7.843), 8- (t = 6.100), and 10-week (t = 7.137) aged mice following the antibiotic treatment (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1d). Conversely, the population of Bacteroidetes significantly decreased in mice treated with antibiotics at 6-week (P = 0.003, t = 4.034), 8-week (P = 0.0012, t = 4.475), and 10-week of age (P = 0.0005, t = 4.845, Fig. 1a). We have not seen significant difference in Firmicutes level between the control and treatment group F4, 20 = 0.842, P = 0.5144 (4-week: P > 0.9999, t = 1.257; 6-week: P = 0.1230, t = 2.431; 8-week: P > 0.9999, t = 1.197; 10-week: P > 0.9999, t = 0.5008, Fig. 1b). Here, the t-value represents the calculated statistic from the post hoc comparison, reflecting the magnitude of difference between groups at each time point. The relative abundance for other phyla cumulatively was below 8%, which was negligible as compared to the other four major phyla. We validated genus-level gut microbiota composition. Within the Proteobacteria phylum, major genera such as Pandoraea (F1, 20 = 214.5, P < 0.0001), Acinetobacter (F1, 20 = 775.3, P < 0.0001), and Sphingomonas (F1, 20 = 778.7, P < 0.0001) exhibited an increase in abundance due to antibiotic treatment (Fig. 1e–g). Furthermore, the primary genera under the Actinobacteria phylum, including Corynebacterium-1 (F1, 20 = 166.4, P < 0.0001) and Nocardioides (F1, 20 = 195.2, P < 0.0001), showed an upward trend in abundance followed by the antibiotic treatment (Fig. 1h, i). In contrast, we observed a decrease in the of Odoribacter abundance (F1, 20 = 19.52, P = 0.0003), a genus within the Bacteroidetes phylum, in the 6- (P = 0.045, t = 2.884), 8- (P = 0.223, t = 2.142), and 10-week mice (P = 0.0005, t = 4.806) treated with antibiotics compared to time-matched control group mice (Fig. 1j). From the interaction between time and treatment, we found that Pandoraea (F4, 20 = 17.83, P < 0.0001), Acinetobacter (F4, 20 = 74.03, P < 0.0001), Sphingomonas (F4, 20 = 108.9, P < 0.0001), Corynebacterium-1 (F4, 20 = 10.89, P < 0.0001), Nocardioides (F4, 20 = 14.83, P < 0.0001), Odoribacter abundance (F4, 20 = 4.122, P = 0.013) also significantly altered.

Antibiotic Treatment Induced Alterations in Mice Behavior

Antibiotic treatment can alter behavior. We assessed the mice behavior in both the control and treatment groups at 3, 4, 6, 8, and 10 weeks using the OFT, EPM, FST, and MBT to measure stress-related behavior. Our observation showed that the antibiotic treatment affected the OFT in the mice (F1, 73 = 14.36, P = 0.0003). In the OFT, we observed that the time spent at the center of the open arm remained unchanged in 3-week (P ≥ 0.9999, t = 0.000) and 4-week (P ≥ 0.9999, t = 0.1353) mice in both the control and treatment groups. However, at 6- (P = 0.2106, t = 2.069), 8- (P = 0.0527, t = 2.625), and 10 weeks (P = 0.0026, t = 3.633), there was a gradual decrease in the time spent at the center by the antibiotic-treated mice (Fig. 2a). We also measured the distance traveled by the mice during the open field exposure, which was significantly decreased due to the antibiotic treatment (F1, 90 = 11.10, P = 0.0013, Fig. 2b). The time spent at the center (F4, 73 = 3.008, P = 0.023) was significantly altered due to the interaction between the time and treatment as well, however we have not found any significant in the distance traveled by the mice in the OFT apparatus (F4, 90 = 1.810, P = 0.133). Although a decline in the distance traveled by antibiotic-treated mice was observed, post hoc analysis revealed a significant decrease, specifically in the 10-week-old antibiotic-treated mice (P = 0.0063, t = 3.330). We conducted the EPM test to assess anxiety levels, which were also significantly affected due to the antibiotic treatment (F1, 72 = 41.37, P < 0.0001) and due to the interaction between both treatment and time (F4, 72 = 5.812, P = 0.0004). The declining trend in the time spent in the open arm was observed at 6 weeks (P = 0.0301, t = 2.830), 8 weeks (P = 0.0008, t = 3.986), and 10 weeks (P ≤ 0.0001, t = 5.952) in mice undergoing antibiotic treatment (Fig. 2c). Similarly, the distance traveled in the EPM apparatus decreased in the antibiotic-treated mice (Fig. 2d). The FST, designed to assess depressive behavior in mice, showed a consistent decrease in mobility (F1, 35 = 73.45, P < 0.0001) and increase in the immobility time (F1, 37 = 47.78, P < 0.0001) in antibiotic-treated mice at all-time points (Fig. 2e, f). In the study of interaction between antibiotic treatment and time, the mobility time (F4, 35 = 6.085, P = 0.0008) and immobility time (F4, 37 = 4.960, P = 0.002) also found to be altered. The MBT was performed to evaluate the effect of antibiotic treatment on anxiety levels in mice, also altered due to both antibiotic and time effect (F4, 82 = 4.341, P = 0.003) as well as only antibiotic treatment (F1, 82 = 19.67, P < 0.0001). The post hoc test indicated a significant increase in the number of marbles buried at 8 (P = 0.0024, t = 3.640) and 10 weeks (P ≤ 0.0001, t = 4.583) compared to their respective control groups (Fig. 2g).

Antibiotic Treatment Affected the HPA Axis, Insulin Resistance, and Inflammation

Based on our behavioral study and neuronal health assessment findings, we thought that prolonged antibiotic treatment may induce stress. Given that the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis is activated in response to stress [13], our subsequent investigation involved measuring corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) levels in the hypothalamus, as well as corticosterone and ACTH levels in the serum of both control and treated mice. CRH expression was found to be altered significantly due to the interaction between the treatment and time (F4, 20 = 12.78, P < 0.0001). We found that the expression of CRH was highest in the 6-week mice (P < 0.0001, t = 5.772) following antibiotic treatment. However, its expression significantly decreased in the 8- (P = 0.0773, t = 2.647) and 10-week (P = 0.0198, t = 3.255) antibiotic-treated groups compared to their control groups (Fig. 3a). In contrast, we observed a significant increase in ACTH levels in the serum of mice after the antibiotic treatment (F1, 20 = 56.91, P < 0.0001). Post hoc analysis revealed that particularly at 8- (P = 0.0020, t = 4.239) and 10-week (P < 0.0001, t = 8.963) antibiotic-treated mice, there is a significant increase in the ACTH level (Fig. 3b). Similar to ACTH, corticosterone followed the same trend (F1, 20 = 147.0, P < 0.0001), which is known for its role in regulating glucose homeostasis (Fig. 3c). We found that fasting glucose levels (F1, 20 = 34.37, P < 0.0001), insulin (F1, 20 = 45.04, P < 0.0001), and insulin resistance (F1, 20 = 42.26, P < 0.0001) increased due to the antibiotic-treated mice (Fig. 3d–f). When we analyzed the effect of interaction between the treatment and time, we found the serum glucose (F4, 20 = 9.257, P = 0.0002), insulin (F4, 20 = 6.222, P = 0.002), and insulin resistance (F4, 20 = 12.11, P < 0.0001) altered significantly. The post hoc analysis results revealed that both serum fasting glucose and insulin levels increased in the antibiotic treatment group at 8- (glucose: P = 0.0212, t = 4.171; insulin: P = 0.0014, t = 5.334) and 10-week age points (glucose: P = < 0.0001, t = 7.109; insulin P = 0.0005, t = 5.832, Fig. 3d, e). Moreover, insulin resistance displayed a similar trend to serum glucose and insulin levels, with elevated levels observed in the 8- (P = 0.0005, t = 5.838) and 10-week (P < 0.0001, t = 7.434) aged mice subjected to antibiotic treatment (Fig. 3f).

Since the HPA axis activation and neuroinflammation are interconnected [13], our next goal was to examine the expression of inflammatory markers in the PFC, hippocampus, and hypothalamus. We observed a significant upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes TNFα (PFC: F1, 20 = 75.71, P < 0.0001; Hippocampus: F1, 20 = 90.35, P < 0.0001; Hypothalamus: F1, 20 = 32.26, P < 0.0001) and IL1β (PFC: F1, 20 = 126.1, P < 0.0001; Hippocampus: F1, 20 = 39.64, P < 0.0001; Hypothalamus: F1, 20 = 21.55, P = 0.0002) expression at all the three part of the brain (Fig. 3g). The interaction between the time and treatment shown to affect the TNFα expression (PFC: F4, 20 = 10.88, P < 0.0001; Hippocampus: F4, 20 = 18.43, P < 0.0001; Hypothalamus: F4, 20 = 13.16, P < 0.0001) and IL1β (PFC: F4, 20 = 38.72, P < 0.0001; Hippocampus: F4, 20 = 3.69, P < 0.020; Hypothalamus: F4, 20 = 6.648, P = 0.001). From the post hoc study we observed that in mice undergoing antibiotic treatment from 6- (TNFα: P = 0.0020, t = 4.232; IL1β: P = 0.0214, t = 3.221), 8- (TNFα: P < 0.0001, t = 5.644; IL1β: P < 0.0001, t = 14.45), and 10 weeks of age (TNFα: P < 0.0001, t = 8.224; IL1β: P < 0.0001, t = 7.850). In the hippocampus, we observed the same trend as PFC in 6- (TNFα: P = 0.0004, t = 4.961; IL1β: P = 0.0058, t = 3.785), 8- (TNFα: P = 0.0003, t = 5.027; IL1β: P = 0.0096, t = 3.568), and 10 weeks (TNFα: P < 0.0001, t = 10.67; IL1β: P = 0.0004, t = 4.901, Fig. 3). In the hypothalamus, this upregulation of proinflammatory markers was significant in the 8- (TNFα: P = 0.0003, t = 5.089; IL1β: P = 0.0008, t = 4.624) and 10 weeks (TNFα: P < 0.0001, t = 7.675; IL1β: P = 0.0002, t = 5.156) of antibiotic-treated groups compared to the control group (Fig. 3g–l). On the contrary, due to both treatment and time, the anti-inflammatory marker IL10 exhibited an increase in expression in PFC (F4, 20 = 12.81, P < 0.0001). At 4 weeks of antibiotic treatment IL10 expression increase at PFC (P = 0.0002, t = 5.140), followed by a gradual decrease to reach the minimum till they became 10-week-old (P = 0.0060, t = 3.769, Fig. 3g–l). However, no significant changes were observed in IL10 expression in the hippocampus and hypothalamus of the brain due to the only treatment effect (Hippocampus: F1, 20 = 0.6272, P = 0.4377; Hypothalamus: F1, 20 = 20.21, P = 0.0002) as well as due to the interaction of treatment and time effect (Hippocampus: F4, 20 = 2.062, P = 0.124; Hypothalamus: F4, 20 = 1.291, P = 0.307).

Alteration in the Neurotransmitters and Neuronal Health Following the Antibiotic Treatment

Since the PFC is connected to limbic regions and helps regulate brain function and behavior [12], our focus was to investigate the status of neurotransmitters in the PFC. Levels of glutamate (F1, 20 = 30.27, P < 0.0001), GABA (F1, 20 = 323.7, P < 0.0001), and norepinephrine (F1, 20 = 5.438, P = 0.0303) were found to be altered due to antibiotic treatment. The interaction effect between time and treatment was also found to be altered in glutamate (F4, 20 = 7.883, P = 0.0006), GABA (F4, 20 = 62.38, P < 0.0001), and norepinephrine (F4, 20 = 7.966, P = 0.0005). We observed that the glutamate concentration remained unchanged between the control and treated groups during 4 (P > 0.9999, t = 0.05267) and 6 weeks (P = 0.1787, t = 2.252). However, in the 8- (P = 0.0154, t = 3.366) and 10-week (P < 0.0001, t = 6.738) age groups of mice, the glutamate concentration significantly decreased in the antibiotic-treated group (Fig. 4a). Further, we noted a significant decrease in GABA concentration in 6- (P < 0.0001, t = 7.866), 8- (P ≤ 0.0001, t = 12.84), and 10-week (P < 0.0001, t = 18.59) old mice subjected to antibiotic treatment. However, we did not observe any changes in the 4-week age group (P > 0.9999, t = 0.9321, Fig. 4b). For norepinephrine (NE) concentration, we observed an increase in the 4-week age group of mice treated with antibiotics (P = 0.0727, t = 2.677). However, NE concentration gradually decreased and reached its lowest point in the 10-week-old mice within the antibiotic treatment group (P = 0.0006, t = 4.773, Fig. 4c).

Antibiotic treatment itself was the stimulus, as it has been shown to influence brain function and behavior, including anxiety-like phenotypes. Therefore, we examined the cFOS and BDNF activity in the brain. The cFOS is a marker for neuronal activities, while BDNF is a marker for neuronal health. The BDNF expression in the PFC in the antibiotic-treated group significantly decreased at 10 weeks (P = 0.0178, t = 3.302) compared to the control group (Fig. 4d, g). In the hippocampus, this reduction was more pronounced at 8 weeks (P = 0.0108, t = 3.518) and 10 weeks (P = 0.0021, t = 4.219) in the treated group (Fig. 4e, h). Antibiotic Treatment and the Appetite Regulation

After observing increased stress levels in the brains of the antibiotic-treated group and considering literature that suggests a connection between stress and feeding behavior [19], our next step was to investigate food intake by the mice and feeding markers' expression in the brain's hypothalamus. Among the 10 weeks of mice (P = 0.0066, t = 3.456), those subjected to antibiotic treatment significantly reduced food intake compared to the control group of the same age. However, we did not find substantial differences between the control and treatment groups in any other age category (F1, 40 = 0.9023, P = 0.3479, Fig. 5a). Regarding gene expression, we noted that the NPY and POMC expression significantly altered due to the antibiotic treatment (NPY: F1, 20 = 36.29, P < 0.0001; POMC: F1, 20 = 121.3, P < 0.0001) and due to the interaction between the time and treatment (NPY: F1, 20 = 1117.5, P < 0.0001; POMC: F1, 20 = 42.50, P < 0.0001). The NPY expression was initially elevated in the 4-week-old mice subjected to antibiotic treatment (P < 0.0001, t = 21.59). We observed a significant decrease in the NPY expression in the 8- (P = 0.0136, t = 3.419) and 10-week (P = 0.0002, t = 5.301) age groups under the same treatment. However, antibiotic treatment did not affect NPY expression in 6 weeks (P > 0.999, t = 0.6044, Fig. 5b). The expression pattern of AgRP followed the same trend as NPY (Fig. 5c). POMC (P = 0.0487, t = 2.858) and CART (P = 0.7029, t = 1.535) expression decreased in 4-week mice following antibiotic treatment. Subsequently, POMC showed a noticeable increase in the 6- (P = 0.0005, t = 4.811), 8- (P ≤ 0.0001, t = 10.14), and 10-week (P ≤ 0.0001, t = 12.54) age groups. At the same time, CART exhibited a rise in the 8- (P = 0.0743, t = 2.665) and 10-week (P = 0.0019, t = 4.263) age groups in the antibiotic-treated mice compared to their time-match control group (Fig. 5d, e).

Antibiotic Treatment Altered the Systemic Metabolism

IR serves as a critical link between metabolic and neuronal health. Therefore, after detecting alterations in the gut microbiota, brain function, and IR, we aimed to assess the systemic metabolites profile. Following the antibiotic treatment, we observed significant changes in the serum metabolite profile as compared to the time-matched control mice group. In the 8-week mice, antibiotic treatment led to increased levels of Allantoic acid, Melibiose, Cystathionine, and Glyceric acid in the serum. In 10-week-old mice, there was an upregulation of Histamine, 2-Oxopentanedioic acid, and 5-Hydroxy-l-tryptophan. Conversely, Cis-5, 8, 11-Eicosatrienoic acid and 12-Ketodeoxycholic acid were downregulated in the serum of 10-week-old antibiotic-treated mice. Association Among Gut Microbiota, Behavior, Neuronal Health Markers, HPA Axis Regulators, IR, Neuroinflammation, Neurotransmitters, and Appetite Regulators

The association study identified correlations among various parameters regulating the gut–brain axis. The EPM and FST results showed a negative association between corticosterone. The time spent at the center of the OFT apparatus, time spent at the open arm of the EPM, and mobility time in the FST demonstrated positive associations with cFOS expression in the PFC and BDNF expression in the hippocampus and hypothalamus regions. These associations indicate that when the stress increases, there is an alteration in the brain's cFOS and BDNF activities and behavior. ACTH and corticosterone exhibited a negative correlation with cFOS and BDNF activity. Both corticosterone and ACTH were positively correlated with insulin resistance.

On the other hand, the proinflammatory expression in the brain is positively associated with IR, while only the IL10 expression at PFC is negatively associated with IR. Furthermore, most proinflammatory markers' expression in the brain negatively correlated with cFOS and BDNF activities while positively correlating with corticosterone and ACTH concentrations in the serum. Among the neurotransmitters, only norepinephrine was positively associated with the expression of neuronal health markers while negatively associated with the inflammatory markers in the brain. Orexigenic markers, i.e., NPY, demonstrated a positive correlation, while anorexigenic markers (POMC and CART) negatively correlated with cFOS and BDNF. Additionally, POMC and CART exhibited a positive relationship with HPA axis regulators, insulin resistance, and several inflammatory markers in the brain (Supplementary Table 3).

The gut microbiota played a pivotal role in these gut–brain axis regulatory processes. Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria were negatively associated with behavioral test parameters, while Bacteroidetes showed a positive association. Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria were also positively associated with corticosterone, ACTH, insulin resistance, and proinflammatory markers in the brain. Only POMC demonstrated a positive association among appetite regulators with the abundance of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria. The multiple correlation analysis suggested a stronger correlation between the phylum (Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria) and corticosterone level with the inflammatory marker (IL1β), anorexigenic marker (POMC), and IR.

Discussion

The results revealed that the nine-antibiotic cocktail can potentially disrupt neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic pathways. Therefore, prolonged treatment with a nine-antibiotic cocktail could disrupt the gut–brain communication in the development of mouse. We observed that the antibiotic cocktail (1) caused a significant alteration in gut microbiota composition, (2) increased the stress response, (3) induced an inflammatory response, (4) reduced neurotransmitter production, neuronal health, and appetite, and (5) altered serum metabolites and inducing IR, which altogether could contribute to induce anxiety and depressive behavior. Additionally, we found an association between antibiotic-induced perturbed gut microbiota, altered brain function, and host behavior.

Different antibiotic cocktail has different potential to perturb the gut microbiota structure and function. Chen et al. observed that treatment with a nine-antibiotic cocktail resulted in a more significant disruption of the gut microbiota (approximately 2-log reduction) than the widely used standard antibiotic cocktail for gut microbiota depletion [20]. The present study represents the first exploration into the impact of this specific antibiotic cocktail on the brain's peripheral system and its association with the microbiome composition to provide valuable insights into gut–brain communication.

Gut microbiota is dynamic and varies under different conditions. After weaning, we observed a predominance of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes in the control groups across all age categories, consistent with previous findings [21]. However, as the antibiotic treatment progressed, the Bacteroidetes population gradually replaced by the Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria phyla, which is corroborated with the previous studies [22]. The antibiotic treatment disrupted the anaerobic conditions in the gut, which could lead to a potential dominance of facultative anaerobes such as Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria in our study. Concerning the 3-week-old mice and the respective control group, these three phyla changed overtly at 6 weeks. They maintained almost the same trend in the 8- and 10-week antibiotic-treated mice, suggesting that the prolonged antibiotic treatment exerts a more pronounced effect on the gut microbiota than the short-term treatment. At the genus level, the increase in the Pandoraea, Acinetobacter, Sphingomonas from the Proteobacteria phylum, Corynebacterium, Nocardiosis from Actinobacteria, and decrease in the Odoribacter from Bacteroidetes phylum following antibiotic treatment, providing support for our phylum-level observations (Fig. 1d–i). Additionally, although our study consisted of a 7-week antibiotic treatment, we have not observed any restoration pattern in the gut microbiota abundance and composition, proving this antibiotic cocktail's efficacy.

Various studies have demonstrated abnormal behavior in germ-free and gut microbiota-perturbed mice. The decreased exploratory activity observed in the antibiotic-treated group during the OFT at 8 and 10 weeks of age (Fig. 2a, b) suggests the presence of anxiety-like behavior. Similarly, results from the EPM and MBT suggested potential anxiety induction following antibiotic treatment. The decrease in the mobility time in the FST indicated helpless behavior, a possible induction of depression in the 8- and 10-week antibiotic-treated mice (Fig. 2e, f). The behavior tests cumulatively suggest that long-term antibiotic treatment could exacerbate anxiety and depressive behaviors in rodents. We found a negative association between the abundance of the Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria phyla while a positive association of Bacteroidetes with the result of OFT, EPM, and FST, suggesting that different microbes in the gut regulate the behavior of the host differently (Supplementary Table 3). The increase in the Acinetobacter, Corynebacterium and decrease in Odoribacter phylum in the antibiotic group also support the link between the gut microbiota and the stress induction [23, 24].

The HPA axis, activated in response to stress, produces and balances hormones necessary to manage stress levels. We observed the highest expression of CRH in 6-week-old antibiotic-treated mice, indicating the initiation of the HPA axis during physiological stress. However, later, CRH decreased, possibly due to elevated concentrations of corticosterone and ACTH, inhibiting CRH expression through a feedback mechanism. We found a positive association between the Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria with the corticosterone and ACTH concentration in the serum, suggesting their role in the stress response. Hence, we confirmed that antibiotic-induced gut microbiota perturbation could disrupt the neuroendocrine system and activate the stress response. The perturbation in the gut microbiota and HPA axis could affect different functions in the host, of which immune regulation is the major. The increase in the stress hormone could induce inflammation in the brain. Hence, our study shows a positive association of the serum stress hormones with the expression of proinflammatory markers in the different brain regions. The gram-negative bacteria have the potential to induce inflammation. We also observed a positive association between Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria phyla with the TNFα and IL1β expression in the brain.

In contrast, Bacteroidetes showed a positive correlation with glutamate and a negative correlation with IL1β and TNFα, implying their potential beneficial role in regulating psychiatric disorders. Existing literature indicates that Pandoraea, Acinetobacter, and Sphingomonas from the Proteobacteria phylum are associated with inflammatory responses. Our findings are the first to suggest that these genera may also be linked to neuroinflammation [25–27] as both maintain a proportional relationship. Acinetobacter also has been shown to elevate corticosterone levels and contribute to inflammation, aligning with our observations [28]. Additionally, Corynebacterium from the Actinobacteria phylum has been reported to increase the expression of proinflammatory cytokines. This finding correlates with our observed proportional relationship between Corynebacterium abundance and neuroinflammation [29]. Furthermore, activation of the HPA axis and subsequent production of stress hormones are known to upregulate Nocardia abundance, which may explain the significantly higher levels of Nocardia observed in the 8- and 10-week antibiotic treatment groups [30].

The PFC, hippocampus, and hypothalamus are key brain regions in stress response. Exploring the impact of antibiotic treatment on these three brain regions represents a novel aspect of our study. The expression of cFOS and BDNF significantly decreased in the antibiotic-treated group at all three parts of the brain after the antibiotic treatment, suggesting a decline in neuronal activity and health. The positive correlation between the behavioral tests and the activity of cFOS in the PFC and BDNF in the hippocampus and hypothalamus could potentially result from the region-specific prevalence of specific markers within their respective brain regions. The negative association between the expression of cFOS and BDNF with cortisol also suggests a decrease in neuronal function and growth during stress.

Moreover, we found a connection between IL1β and TNFα in the brain and the activity of cFOS and BDNF, indicating an interlink between neuronal health and neuroinflammation that could impact host behavior. The positive correlation between Bacteroidetes and the negative correlation of Proteobacteria with the expression of cFOS and BDNF is consistent with prior research [31, 32]. Jiang et al. demonstrated an elevated population of Actinobacteria in individuals with depression, aligning with our findings from the association study [33].

Gut microbiota synthesizes neurotransmitters, which are the significant components in gut–brain communication. PFC controls the limbic area by regulating neurotransmitters. To understand their impact on observed behavior and molecular changes in the brain, we assessed glutamate, GABA, and NE concentrations in the PFC. No alterations were observed in the 4 weeks of antibiotic-treated mice, suggesting the inefficacy of short-term antibiotic treatment on neurotransmitters. Conversely, long-term treatment reduces the glutamate concentration, which could affect the host's behavior. As the BDNF regulates the production of glutamate in the PFC, the decrease in BDNF expression could also contribute to the decline in the glutamate concentration in the PFC [34]. A reduction in glutamate concentration may lead to an imbalance between excitatory (Glutamatergic) and inhibitory (GABAergic) mechanisms, which may cause behavioral deficits. The deficit could be one of the reasons why we found an inverse relation between glutamate and stress behavior from our association study. Similarly, norepinephrine is positively associated with neuronal health markers and negatively related to inflammatory markers, supported by the previous literature [35, 36].

Stress is linked with metabolic regulation. In our study, the prolonged antibiotic treatment could cause IR, which may arise due to stress. Consistent with previous findings, we also identified a robust positive association between corticosterone and ACTH concentrations with insulin resistance, IR [37]. We found that the POMC and CART, known to suppress the appetite, are positively associated with IR. This observation suggests that antibiotic-induced stress could influence metabolic parameters differently. The positive association between Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria with IR and POMC proves the role of gut microbiota in regulating metabolic parameters during stress conditions. The multiple correlation analysis showed that Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria are associated with neuroinflammation, suggesting that these two significant phyla could regulate different parameters of gut–brain communication (Table 4). We also observed a positive association between the expression of POMC and CART with the proinflammatory cytokine markers (IL1β, TNFα). The association suggests that activating inflammatory pathways could disturb appetite regulation in the brain, and hence, we observed a decline in appetite during the prolonged antibiotic treatment. The decreased appetite may be linked to the weight loss observed in 8- and 10-week-old antibiotic-treated mice, as reported previously [17]. Also, in the serum, the high abundance of histamine, 5-hydroxy-tryptophan, 12-keto deoxycholic acid, and cis-5, 8, 11-eicosatrienoic acid, Melibiose supports the complex interplay involving the endocrine system, metabolic regulation, and neuroinflammation in response to antibiotic-mediated stress [38–41] (Table 3).

Conclusion

Previously, we reported that prolonged exposure to nine-antibiotic cocktails could stimulate the formation of beige fat within the inguinal and perigonadal depots, which may aid in weight loss [17]. The present study reveals that these specific nine-antibiotic cocktails induced gut microbial disruption and showed their consequences on behavior, brain function, and host metabolism. We observed that the prolonged antibiotic treatment increased the Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria phyla, positively associated with the observed stress-like behavior, HPA axis activation, neuroinflammation, and insulin resistance. On the other hand, these two phyla negatively regulate neuronal health and appetite. The altered gut–brain communication following the antibiotic treatment may be due to the enhanced level of Allantoic acid, Melibiose, Cystathionine, Glyceric acid, Histamine, 2-oxopentanedioic acid, 5-Hydroxy-l-tryptophan, 12-Ketodeoxycholic acid, and Cis-5, 8, 11-Eicosatrienoic acid in the serum. The association study highlights the pivotal roles of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria in the intricate interplay among endocrine, metabolic, and neuronal circuits. Our earlier and present observations imply a connection between brain–gut–adipose communication, warranting deeper exploration [42].

We conducted correlation analyses to examine associations between changes in the gut microbiota and various host health parameters which have a clinical significance. The observed alteration of the gut microbiota in response to antibiotic treatment suggests the potential for developing microbiome-targeted therapies that could complement existing treatment strategies. Moreover, the identified microbial shifts and their association with host neurological outcomes may serve as valuable biomarkers for disease progression or treatment response. However, further experimental studies are necessary to establish causality and validate our findings. As we were interested in the effect of antibiotic treatment on males, we have included only male mice in this study. However, it is also essential to include female mice to understand their physiology and behavior comprehensively. In this study, we utilized a nine-antibiotic cocktail to induce significant perturbations in the gut microbiota. Our use of 16S rRNA sequencing effectively captured the overall microbial composition, however future studies using shotgun metagenomic sequencing would enable deeper insights into microbial functions, and strain-specific dynamics more comprehensively. While our findings demonstrated the effectiveness of the antibiotic cocktail in altering the gut microbial community, it is essential to acknowledge that antibiotics can also exert systemic effects beyond the gut.

Additionally, we evaluated the translational aspect of our mouse model to humans, acknowledging the differences in microbial composition, abundance, and dynamics between the two species. Numerous studies have highlighted both positive and negative impacts of antibiotic cocktails on overall host health. Given these varied outcomes, it is crucial to understand how this antibiotic cocktail affects the entire body. To achieve this, further research is needed to elucidate how different antibiotics influence gut microbiota and host physiology.
