BRAF mutations and targeted therapy in unclassified pericytic tumors: insights from genetic analysis and clinical response
Authors: Hana Golan, Moshe Leitner, Marilena Vered, Gahl Greenberg, Rinat Yacobi, Hilla Tabibian-Keissar, Amos Toren, Bella Bielorai, Gideon Rechavi, Ninette Amariglio, Iris Barshack, Ran Yahalom, Ariel Hirschhorn, Michal Yalon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPericytic tumors are a group of soft tissue neoplasms characterized by a hemangiopericytoma (HPC)-like pattern. The underlying genetic lesion of pericytic neoplasms is still obscure; however, recent advances in molecular pathology have enabled a mechanistic understanding and accurate diagnosis of these tumors. Nevertheless, a subset of unclassified pericytic tumors continues to present substantial diagnostic challenges, potentially impacting clinical decision-making and therapeutic strategies.MethodsUsing Ion AmpliSeq Comprehensive Cancer Panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) assay, we searched for HPC-like-associated mutations in a newborn female with a congenital large sublingual unclassified pericytic tumor that recurred after resection and was refractory to chemotherapy treatment. Further analysis of a series of other tumors with distinctive pericytic features was performed via Sanger sequencing and the results were validated via the auto genomics INFINITI® and Biocartis Assays.ResultsA BRAF V600D mutation was identified in the tumor tissue of the newborn patient. Treatment with the BRAF inhibitor (BRAFi) dabrafenib resulted in a dramatic response and regression of the tumor. Sequencing of 15 additional HPCs revealed the presence of a BRAF V600E mutation in 6 samples.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that BRAFi may be a useful therapy for unclassified pericytic tumors. Genetic testing for BRAFV600 mutations and other actionable oncogenic variants should be part of the evaluation for pericytic neoplasms, especially with the currently available targeted drug therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14595-x.Keywords: Pericytic tumors; Hemangiopericytoma-like; BRAF mutation; BRAF inhibitors, targeted therapy; Tumor-agnostic biomarker

Introduction

Pericytic tumors are a distinctive group of neoplasms that originate from pericytes and morphologically share a distinctive perivascular pattern of growth [1]. Historically, these tumors were grouped with vascular (endothelial) tumors characterized by a hemangiopericytoma (HPC)-like pattern [2]. Hemangiopericytoma (HPC) is an uncommon neoplasm that was originally described in 1942 by Stout and Murray as a tumor composed of perivascular contractile cells that often assume a staghorn configuration [3]. However, the ovoid tumor cells of HPC are rather nondescript and may cause confusion with those of other soft tissue tumors. Indeed, over the past few decades, HPC has become the preferred label for several unrelated lesions that share a hemangiopericytic vascular pattern, including myopericytoma/myofibroma [1]. In the 2002 WHO classification, it was acknowledged that most lesions formerly known as HPCs show no evidence of pericytic differentiation and, instead, are fibroblastic in nature and form a morphological continuum with solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) [2, 4, 5].

Currently, most HPCs are recognized as examples of SFTs, but there is still a diagnostic challenge concerning other pericytic tumors that mimic many mesenchymal and nonmesenchymal unrelated tumor entities [6]. The diagnostic ambiguity in these groups of tumors clearly has important implications for choosing the optimal treatment strategy. This issue is even more significant in young children and infants, in whom these are extremely rare entities, with little knowledge about the etiology, prognosis, and optimal therapeutic strategy [7]. Moreover, in cases where the tumor is unresectable, treatment with chemotherapy or radiotherapy may be associated with immediate and late sequelae and is not recommended specifically for young infants. Owing to the recent widespread application of next-generation sequencing (NGS) in pathology, significant advances have been made in the understanding of pericytic, myoid, and myofibroblastic tumors. The identification of PDGFRB alterations in myopericytoma [8], ACTB-GLI fusions in pericytoms [9], SRF-RELA fusions in a subset of cellular myofibromas/myopericytomas [10], and gene fusions involving the NOTCH gene family and BRAF/KRAS mutations in glomus tumors [11] allows a more accurate diagnosis of these neoplasms [12]. Furthermore, some molecular alterations can serve as targets for rational therapy regardless of the exact pathologic diagnosis. Here, we present a case of congenital locally invasive and life-threatening sublingual unclassified pericytic tumor in an otherwise normal female infant. A significant sustained response was obtained via targeted therapy with BRAFis. Further analysis of a series of other tumors with distinctive pericytic and HPC-like features revealed frequent BRAF V600E mutations. Our findings might shed light on the biological characteristics of pericytic tumors and offer innovative approaches for the diagnosis and treatment of this group of rare neoplasms.

Materials and methods

Tumor samples

Tissue for genetic analysis of the index patient was obtained from resected tongue tissue preserved as a formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) block that was originally diagnosed as infantile HPC with chondroid differentiation and was identified as a pericytic tumor. Additional pericytic tumor samples that were selected for molecular evaluation were diagnosed as HPC NOS, benign HPC or malignant HPC according to the World Health Organization classification criteria. H&E-stained slides of FFPE tissues were revised by a pathologist to confirm the diagnosis and select a representative tumor area. Parental informed consent from the index case was obtained and documented. All studies were performed according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approval was obtained from the Sheba Medical Center Institutional Review Board.

Molecular genetic studies for the detection of BRAF somatic mutations

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from 20 to 25 4-µm thick sections of FFPE tumor tissue biopsies via Qiagen columns (51306, Hilden, Germany). Next, 50 µl of proteinase K (40 mg/ml) was added to the ATL buffer, and the samples were incubated at 56 °C overnight. The same amount of proteinase K was added in the morning, and the samples were incubated at 56 °C for 1 h. The samples were then incubated at 90 °C for 1 h. DNA was precipitated by adding AL and alcohol, and the samples were incubated at 20 °C for 1 h. The rest of the protocol was performed as described by the manufacturer.

Ion ampliseq comprehensive cancer panel (CCP) analysis

Genomic mutations were analyzed using the Ion AmpliSeq CCP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) assay, which provides all-exon coverage of 409 oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. All procedures were performed, and the reagents used were in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, DNA was extracted from paraffin sections containing areas with at least 90% tumor cells via Qiagen columns as described above. Twenty nanograms of genomic DNA (measured with a Qubit®) were used for sequencing library preparation. Libraries were analyzed via the Ion Proton sequencing system, and the results were processed via Variant Analysis™ software from Ingenuity® Systems from QIAGEN.

Sanger sequencing

Orthogonal method analysis of the BRAF V600 target region was performed via PCR amplification followed by Sanger sequencing. The primers used were designed to amplify the 236 bp region of the BRAF gene exon 15 coding sequence surrounding the mutation region. Both the forward and reverse strands were sequenced with the following forward and reverse primers: F- 5’- GCTTGCTCTGATAGGAAAATGAG– 3’ and R- 5’- TAACTCAGCAGCATCTCAGGG– 3’. The cycling conditions (after the initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 min) were 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s for 40 cycles, followed by a final elongation at 72 °C for 2 min. The amplified products were purified via a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Cat. No. 28104, QIAGEN). The samples were sequenced at Hylabs laboratories.

Validation of BRAF mutations

Validation of BRAF V600 mutations was performed by autogenomics INFINITI® or the Biocartis Idylla System (Biocartis) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All the genomic analyses were performed at the Sheba Cancer Research Center, Tel Hashomer, Israel.

Results

Index subject medical course

A term female neonate weighing 3970 g was born to a 40-year-old woman, gravida 2, para 2, mother at 42 weeks gestation. The pregnancy was uneventful, and prenatal screening did not reveal any abnormalities. At birth, a protrusive tongue was noted (Fig. 1a and b). Intraoral examination revealed considerable swelling of a rubbery consistency in the floor of the mouth, with an imminent potential for airway obstruction. The remainder of her physical examination was unremarkable.

(a + b) Congenital protrusive tongue of a female newborn

Ultrasound performed on the first day of life revealed a sublingual heterogeneous mass with multiple dilated vascular spaces (Fig. 2a). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a 4.1 × 2.7 × 4.9 cm infiltrative vascular mass occupying the base of the tongue that was not readily amenable to surgical resection (Fig. 2b). The results of the metastatic workup (MRI of the brain, MRA of the neck vessels, chest CT and Tc bone scan) were negative.

(a) US performed on the first day of life revealed a sublingual heterogeneous mass with multiple dilated vascular spaces. (b) MRI of the face revealed an infiltrative vascular mass occupying the base of the tongue

As the initial diagnosis was infantile hemangioma, treatment with oral propranolol 2 mg/kg/day was started. However, the tumor continued to grow, as demonstrated by physical examination and MRI, after 2 weeks of treatment.

Index subject histopathology

An open biopsy at 4 weeks of age followed by subtotal resection of the tumor at 8 weeks of age demonstrated a yellow fibrotic lesion displacing the floor of the mouth superiorly. Light microscopic evaluation of H&E-stained sections (Fig. 3a-d) revealed an ill-defined infiltrating hypercellular tumor (Fig. 3a) with hamartomatous features composed of areas reminiscent of infantile fibromatosis combined with areas of infantile HPC. A staghorn pattern of blood vessels was demonstrated in solid areas (Fig. 3a), and the predominant pattern of expansion was that of anastomosing bands of tumor cells infiltrating the striated muscle (Fig. 3b). Lesional cells were closely related to blood vessels (Fig. 3c), and mitotic figures could also be identified (Fig. 3d).

(a-e) Light microscopic evaluation of H&E-stained sections showing infiltrating hypercellular tumors with a staghorn pattern of blood vessels (a) and tumor cells infiltrating the striated muscle (b). Lesional cells are closely related to blood vessels (c). Mitotic figures are shown (d). Immunohistochemical staining (e + f) revealed that the cells were positive for CD34 (e), and 20–25% of them were Ki-67 positive (f)

Immunohistochemical staining revealed that the cells were positive for CD34 (Fig. 3e), CD99 and vimentin, partly for calponin, and weakly and focally positive for alpha smooth muscle actin (αSMA). The cells were negative for AE1/AE3 (pan cytokeratin), S100, bcl2, caldesmon and beta-catenin, and 20–25% of them were Ki-67 positive (Fig. 3f). Immunostaining for ALK, c-kit, and PDGFR was noncontributory, and FISH analysis for ETV6 rearrangement was negative. RT‒PCR analysis of NAB2-STAT6 gene fusions performed at the Michigan Center for Translational Pathology as previously described [13] was negative. Several diagnoses have been suggested, including infantile HPC, infantile fibromatosis, infantile myofibroma or fibrous hamartoma of infancy. External revisions of the pathology that were performed at the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Department of Pathology and the Vanderbilt University Medical Center Department of Pathology included intramuscular spindle cell neoplasm, pericytic/myopycytic in type, with no convincing features of malignancy.

Identification of candidate somatic mutations

As the tumor continued to progress over 3 months of follow-up and chemotherapy with four cycles of vincristine (0.05 mg/kg/dose), actinomycin-D (0.05 mg/kg/dose) followed by nine cycles of methotrexate (1 mg/kg/dose) and vinblastine (0.17 mg/kg/dose) resulted in a minimal response, somatic and germline genomic analyses were performed via the Ion AmpliSeq CCP assay. Three genetic variations were detected in three different genes: BRAF V600D, NTRK1 (P.R568C; P.R574C; P.R388C) and SMARCA4 (P.R421L). The mutations were clearly somatic and were found only in the tumor and not in the peripheral blood, which was also analyzed. INFINITI™ Analyzer analysis and Sanger sequencing analysis confirmed the presence of a BRAF V600D mutation.

BRAFi treatment course

At the age of 15 months, the patient started treatment with dabrafenib at a pediatric dose of 4.5 mg/kg/day. The drug was provided for compassionate use by GlaxoSmithKline Pharmaceuticals Ltd and later by Novartis Pharma AG and was given after approval was obtained from the Sheba Medical Center Institutional Review Board. Within the first 3‒4 weeks of treatment, a significant reduction in the tumor mass was noted during the clinical examination. This observation was later confirmed by an MRI conducted 4 months after treatment initiation (Fig. 4a and b), with a continued response in the following months. Cessation of therapy by the family one year after institution of treatment resulted in rapid regrowth of the residual mass (Fig. 4c). However, the tumor responded to retreatment with dabrafenib and regressed once again, resulting in a small stable residual mass (Fig. 4d). The patient continues to receive dabrafenib for almost 11 years, with decreasing doses to the current dose of 2 mg/kg. Throughout the treatment course, timely cardiologic, ophthalmologic, dermatologic and endocrinologic follow-up was conducted, with no significant adverse events observed except for mild episodes of panniculitis that did not require reduction or cessation of BRAFi therapy.

MRI scans obtained before (a) and 6 months after (b) initiation of treatment with BRAFi demonstrated a significant reduction in tumor size. Cessation of therapy resulted in rapid regrowth of the residual mass (c). Upon resuming treatment with dabrafenib, marked tumor regression was demonstrated once again (d)

BRAF mutations in pericytic samples

As little is known about the incidence and biological role of BRAF mutations in pericytic tumors, we next investigated whether other tumors with a similar diagnosis harbor BRAF V600 mutations. A computerized record search identified 29 patients who were diagnosed with pericytic tumors/HPCs at the Sheba Medical Center between 2001 and 2016. Genomic DNA was extracted from 15 FFPE samples that were available for further evaluation. Prior to DNA extraction, histopathological revision of H&E-stained FFPE tissues was performed.

Using PCR followed by Sanger sequencing of the BRAF gene, we found that 5 of the 15 samples that were diagnosed as pericytic tumors/HPCs harbored the heterozygous BRAF V600E mutation in exon 15 of the BRAF gene (Fig. 5a and b). As a control, WT BRAF and two samples of melanoma tissue were used as negative (Fig. 5c) and positive (Fig. 5d) controls, respectively, for BRAFWT/V600E. In one case analysis was performed via the Ion AmpliSeq CCP assay demonstrating heterozygous BRAF V600E mutation. The results were further validated via the INFINITI™ Analyzer or Biocartis assay.

Using Sanger sequencing of the BRAF exon, we found that 5 of the 15 samples diagnosed with HPC harbored the heterozygous BRAFWT/V600 mutation in the exon 15 hotspot T1799A of the BRAF gene sequence. Representative results are shown (a + b). As controls, a sample with BRAFWT and a sample of melanoma tissue were used (c + d, respectively), which presented negative (c) and positive (d) results for BRAFWT/V600E

Overall, we identified BRAF V600 mutations in 44% of the samples (7/16 samples, including the index case) from 16 different patients who were diagnosed with pericytic tumors/HPCs in various locations and in different age groups. Interestingly, the V600E variant was found in 6 samples, and V600D in the sample from the index patient (Table 1, Supplementary files 1–3).

Discussion

Recent advances in molecular techniques in soft tissue pathology, including the widespread application of next-generation sequencing (NGS), have led to considerable progress in our understanding of mesenchymal tumors. Recognition of the genetic signatures of these neoplasms not only clarifies the pathogenesis of these entities but also improves diagnosis and therapy. Our work demonstrates how the identification of a specific mutation, not previously described in a disease entity, can change the therapeutic approach and medical course in resistant patients. Moreover, it may help in handling other similar cases in a group of rare diseases.

In the case discussed, a newborn female presented with a congenital, life-threatening infiltrative sublingual mass that was difficult to classify. Notably, the tumor was unresectable and demonstrated significant resistance to conventional chemotherapy, further complicating its management and underscoring the urgent need for alternative therapeutic approaches. The identification of the BRAF V600D mutation in tumor tissue via next-generation sequencing led to the initiation of BRAFi therapy, which resulted in an almost complete regression of the tumor within a brief period. Given the potential morbidity associated with surgical resection and the risk of relapse upon BRAFi discontinuation, this therapy could serve as a bridge to surgery, potentially optimizing treatment outcomes and reducing operative risks. Screening of 15 additional samples from patients diagnosed with similar pericytic tumors/HPCs revealed that 44% of these neoplasms (7 of 16 samples, including the index case) harbored BRAF V600 mutations. HPC diagnosis has been extensively used in the past 40 years. Currently, according to the 2020 WHO classification of soft tissue tumors [1], myopericytoma, myofibroma, angioleiomyoma and glomus tumors are classified as pericytic (perivascular) neoplasms. It seems that many tumors previously diagnosed as HPC, including some tumors analyzed in our study, fall within the spectrum of HPC-like/pericytic neoplasms. Therefore, in these circumstances, identification of a BRAFV600 mutation can help in distinguishing between tumor types and, importantly, can support therapeutic decisions, as demonstrated in our case.

The principle of precision oncology is based on the concept that medical therapies should focus on the predictive molecular biomarker rather than the histotype [14, 15]. Since 2017, the FDA has granted seven tumor-agnostic approvals for six drugs that target a variety of molecular alterations rather than specific tumor types including microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), high tumor mutation burden (TMB-H), neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase (NTRK) gene fusions, and BRAF V600E mutations [16, 17]. This paradigm shift is particularly significant for patients with refractory or rare cancers. In our case, identification of a BRAF mutation and subsequent targeted therapy resulted in a significant clinical response. This case illustrates the importance of thoughtful integration of clinical, histopathologic, and molecular data, which act synergistically to support accurate diagnosis. It also underscores the value of broad molecular profiling in pediatric tumors and the need for expert input in evaluating complex cases. While individual case reports may be anecdotal, each one contributes to the growing body of evidence that molecular characterization can be essential for guiding therapy, particularly in rare pediatric tumors with limited or uncertain treatment options. Sharing these successes underscores the value of such testing, encourages its application in challenging cases, and ultimately advances treatment approaches for similarly affected children. Key strategies for tumor-agnostic therapy trials include basket trials enrolling patients based on biomarkers [18, 19], adaptive designs, and Bayesian models for early detection of non-responsive subgroups hereby reducing risks and increasing trial flexibility. Early biomarker validation, companion diagnostics integration, and collaboration with regulatory and health technology asses (HTA) agencies are crucial for optimizing trial outcomes and regulatory success [16]. The BRAF protein, a key component of the RAS–RAF–MAPK signaling pathway, regulates cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and survival. BRAF mutations, particularly V600E, can lead to constitutive pathway activation and neoplastic transformation [20, 21]. The V600E mutation, resulting from a T > A transversion at nucleotide 1799, causes a valine to glutamic acid substitution at codon 600, increasing BRAF activity 500-fold [22]. This and other BRAF mutations (V600K, V600D, V600R) produce RAS-independent monomers sensitive to BRAF inhibitors [23, 24]. Accurate classification of BRAF mutations is crucial for guiding future treatment strategies in affected patients. BRAF mutations occur in 3.9–15% of human cancers, with the highest prevalence in hairy cell leukemia and metanephric adenoma (90–100%) [25–27]. The 44% frequency of BRAF V600 mutations in our HPC-like/pericytic neoplasms cases is comparable to other BRAF-mutated tumors including melanomas, ameloblastomas, Erdheim-Chester disease, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, papillary thyroid carcinomas and ganglioglioma, in which the prevalence is between 40 and 80% [28–33] yet BRAF V600E mutations remain less common in other cancers [34, 35]. Interestingly, BRAF V600E mutations have been reported in approximately 6% of glomus tumors [11], whereas their prevalence in myopericytoma remains less well characterized [36, 37]. Given the overlapping perivascular myoid lineage of glomus tumors and other pericytic neoplasms, our finding of a 44% BRAF V600E mutation rate in unclassified HPC-like/pericytic neoplasms raises the possibility that certain BRAF-mutant HPC-like tumors may lie along a continuum with glomus tumors. This observation highlights the evolving diagnostic classification of pericytic neoplasms, where precise subclassification remains challenging.

To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to report the occurrence of BRAF mutations in pericytic/HPC-like neoplasms. In our study, the older patients were diagnosed with the common V600E mutation, whereas the youngest index patient had a BRAF V600D mutation. The rare BRAF V600D mutation, requiring two nucleotide changes, has been reported in limited cases of soft tissue tumors, pediatric LCH, metastatic melanoma, pediatric brain tumors, and melanoma cell lines [38–41]. Unlike V600E, it has not been observed in hematologic neoplasms, benign melanocytic nevi, or other solid tumors [42, 43]. Furthermore, almost no cases of congenital tumors with BRAF mutations have been reported. There have been only a few cases of congenital tumors with BRAF V600 mutations described in melanoma [44], one case of undifferentiated spindle cell sarcoma [38] and one case of cutaneous LCH [45]. While several targeted molecular therapies have demonstrated efficacy in tumors harboring V600E and V600K mutations, V600D mutations have not been specifically evaluated in a clinical setting. However, several preclinical studies suggest that BRAFV600D mutations are likely to be sensitive to vemurafenib [46] and dabrafenib [47]. Our index case is most likely the first and youngest child reported to have been successfully treated for a tumor harboring the BRAF V600D mutation with a targeted BRAFi, resulting in a significant radiological response and dramatic clinical improvement. Thus, in the absence of alternative therapeutic options, BRAFis likely to play a key role in the management of patients with V600D mutations, even infants and young children.

Our patient has received dabrafenib monotherapy for over 11 years without significant adverse effects or secondary cutaneous malignancies, contrasting with observations in adult melanoma patients [48]. This aligns with reports in children with Langerhans cell histiocytosis on long-term BRAF inhibitor therapy, potentially reflecting differences in disease pathophysiology [49]. The absence of secondary skin tumors may be attributed to the lack of paradoxical MAPK pathway activation in BRAF wild-type cells or pre-existing UV-induced RAS mutations [48]. Additionally, the gradual tapering of the drug dosage to 2 mg/kg, lower than in typical adult melanoma regimens, may contribute to the reduced risk of secondary malignancies. Currently, the optimal duration of BRAF inhibitor (BRAFi) therapy and its impact on clinical outcomes upon discontinuation remain unclear. Retrospective studies have shown a heterogeneous risk of progression (20–100%) following BRAFi discontinuation after 1–3 years of treatment [50]. Even after complete radiological response, minimal residual disease may drive relapse, with progression risks of 35% and 71% after complete and partial responses, respectively [51, 52]. The correlation between initial BRAFi treatment duration and response durability is controversial, with some studies reporting no association [53], while others suggest a strong correlation impacting response strength and durability after discontinuation [54]. Due to the lack of established biomarkers guiding BRAFi treatment duration, therapy is often continued indefinitely until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity occurs [55]. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) of BRAF V600E has shown correlation with disease activity and may serve as a potential biomarker for treatment decisions [56]. Further research is needed to address these critical issues and develop evidence-based guidelines for BRAFi therapy management. Our index patient experienced rapid progression of the tumor when treatment was discontinued, with a good response and resumption of treatment. Considering this, no further attempts have been made to discontinue the treatment. She has been receiving dabrafenib for almost 11 years without adjusting the dose to the weight gained. To the best of our knowledge, this is the longest reported duration of treatment with BRAFis in a pediatric patient exceeding previously documented treatment periods of 8.8 years in melanoma [57] and 7 years in pediatric low-grade glioma [58]. However, it is important to note that long-term continuous BRAF/MEK inhibitor therapy is standard in melanoma management. Consequently, many adult melanoma patients may have received similarly extended treatment durations, although these cases may not be systematically documented in the literature. Interestingly, unlike melanoma and other high-grade malignancies, which frequently develop adaptive resistance to BRAFi, our patient showed no evidence of resistance despite prolonged treatment. This observation aligns with the tendency of low-grade tumors and those driven by a single pathological pathway, such as low-grade gliomas and Langerhans cell histiocytosis, to exhibit less frequent resistance to targeted inhibitors [59–61]. This phenomenon warrants further investigation in similar cases to elucidate the mechanisms underlying long-term treatment efficacy.

Conclusions

The widespread use of NGS-based molecular diagnostic approaches and the integration of morphology and genetics have led to the characterization and treatment of different histotypes based on their genetics. Our results revealed BRAFV600 mutations in a subset of HPC-like tumors, a finding that has not been previously reported. In addition, for the first time, we describe prolonged and successful treatment of a congenital HPC neoplasm harboring the rare BRAFV600D mutation with a BRAFi. Our patient’s response to targeted BRAF inhibition was exceptional and highlights promise for future use in pericytic tumors with this mutation, particularly in patients in whom the morbidity associated with surgical resection or aggressive chemotherapy is considered unacceptable. We propose that genetic testing for BRAFV600 mutations and other actionable oncogenic variants should be part of the evaluation of HPC-like and pericytic neoplasms, especially with the currently available targeted drug therapies. Additional studies would help elucidate the frequency and role of BRAF V600 mutations, specifically BRAF V600D mutations, in the pathogenesis of the clinically and pathologically heterogeneous forms of pericytic/HPC-like and other soft tissue neoplasms.
