Life satisfaction in later life: the role of health, social support, and social exclusion in Türkiye
Authors: Kübranur Çebi Karaaslan, Rümeysa Bayar, Ali Öztürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExamining the factors affecting life satisfaction among older adults in Türkiye is crucial for both scientific inquiry and social policy development. Population ageing in Türkiye has accelerated in recent years, and the increasing proportion of individuals aged 65 and over has begun to significantly reshape the demographic structure. Life satisfaction is a fundamental public health indicator that predicts disease development, psychosocial health status, mortality risk, and healthcare utilisation. Dynamics unique to Türkiye, such as the transformation of family structures and the growing tendency to live alone, necessitate a comprehensive examination of the factors affecting life satisfaction among older adults.MethodsThe dataset used in this study is the microdata from the Türkiye Older Adults Profile Survey, conducted by the Turkish Statistical Institute and nationally representative. A total of 10,348 individuals aged 65 years and over were included in the study. The dependent variable of the study is the life satisfaction status. To identify the socio-demographic, health status and functionality, social support and living arrangement, psychosocial well-being, and social exclusion factors affecting life satisfaction among older adults, the binary logit regression method was employed.ResultsKey determinants such as health, social support, and economic conditions play a significant role in shaping life satisfaction among older adults. The findings indicate that positive physical and mental health enhance life satisfaction, whereas loneliness, social isolation, exposure to violence, and exclusion have adverse effects. Furthermore, satisfaction with life and religious beliefs contribute positively, and attitudes toward ageing and acceptance of life stages also influence overall satisfaction.ConclusionsIn addition to reflecting individual well-being, life satisfaction can be improved through policy interventions aimed at expanding social support systems, facilitating access to healthcare services and home care, promoting social participation, and developing age-friendly environments. Such interventions may help reduce issues such as loneliness, exclusion, and violence, thereby improving the quality of life of older adults.Keywords: Older adults, Life satisfaction, Türkiye, Quality of life, Social support, Health, Psychosocial status

Background

Neugarten, Havighurst, and Tobin developed the Life Satisfaction Index (LSI-A) to assess life satisfaction among older adults, examining it through five distinct indicators [1]. The first indicator, which evaluates the tendency to enjoy daily activities, emphasises Zest versus Apathy, analysing the degree to which an individual approaches life with interest and enthusiasm, actively pursues their goals, and maintains curiosity. Next, Resolution versus Resignation relates to how individuals face challenges, their acceptance of personal responsibility, and whether they feel at peace when reflecting on their life journey and reconciling with the past. As the gap between an individual’s desired and actual self-diminishes, their satisfaction tends to increase. This indicator specifically measures this through the Congruence Between Desired and Achieved Goals [2]. argued that an individual’s overall life satisfaction or dissatisfaction results from complex comparisons among their past, present, and future expectations.

Another contributor to life satisfaction is a positive self-concept, which focuses on how an individual perceives themselves, their abilities, and their personality traits. Similarly [3], Campbell et al. (1976) described life satisfaction as an internal evaluative experience in which a person compares their current state with their expected or desired state. Finally, Mood Tone relates to the individual’s general state of mind, whether they are free from anxiety, cheerful, and successful. In his study [4], life satisfaction as a spiritual experience in which the individual undergoes mystical insights and enlightenment, achieving peace or communicating with a greater meaning.

Although life satisfaction encompasses multifaceted and both cognitive and physical parameters, Ed Diener defines life satisfaction as how an individual approaches their personal life, whether they appreciate their life, and how they compare it to the standards they believe they should meet [5, 6]. In other words, life satisfaction serves as an indicator of the overall quality of a person’s life [7]. Elevated levels of these parameters suggest that the older adults possess high life satisfaction and are content with their existence. Nonetheless, geography and culture continue to influence the determinants of life satisfaction. For instance, age exhibits a positive correlation with life satisfaction in the United States, whereas it shows a negative correlation in European countries [8].

This research identifies pathways that influence the life satisfaction of older adults. Good physical health and functioning support self-decision-making and enhance autonomy by enabling older adults to perform daily activities independently and to report high levels of life satisfaction [9]. Self-determination theory explains this through autonomy, competence and relatedness [10]. However, this theory goes further in terms of older adults, stating that autonomy is not simply the ability to act independently; it is more about `the ability to make choices and have preferences considered. Because objective independence and subjective autonomy do not have the same meaning. If an older adult in need of physical assistance is engaged in decision-making processes, they may perceive a greater sense of autonomy, which can enhance their overall life satisfaction [11]. Conversely, an older adult who is physically independent and in good health yet persistently feels subjected to external pressures or social exclusion may experience a diminished sense of autonomy, resulting in decreased life satisfaction [12]. Therefore, social support can also be accepted as a major contributor to life satisfaction as it aids in alleviating negative effects such as depression, loneliness, and social exclusion [13]. Loneliness can be mitigated through the support of neighbours, access to emergency assistance, community engagement, and healthcare services [14]. These measures help to reduce the adverse effects of loneliness, which can also alleviate mental health issues or depression, recognised as the most significant factors influencing life satisfaction among older adults [15]. It has been observed that when older adults experience low levels of stress and depression, coupled with robust social support and good health, the adverse impact of depression on life satisfaction diminishes [16]. Also exposed to violence, social exclusion, or feelings of meaninglessness in life place creates a heavy psychosocial burden on older adults and damages their emotional and social attachment and decreases their life satisfaction. Therefore, social connections, enjoyable leisure activities, and family support play a vital role in enhancing life satisfaction [17].

In addition to mental health and social support, financial independence and economic security are imperative for the life satisfaction of older adults [18]. Elevated income levels and improved living conditions serve to mitigate the adverse effects of detrimental environmental factors that could otherwise compromise their well-being and satisfaction [19]. An elevated income level facilitates the fulfilment of basic needs and enhances life satisfaction; the impact is more significant at lower income levels, and also increases autonomy by providing greater freedom of choice. Moreover, personality traits, educational level, and demographic factors are elements that independently explain life satisfaction. Life satisfaction is influenced not only by external factors but also by individuals’ responses to and handling of situations. Under identical physical conditions, two individuals may encounter markedly different levels of satisfaction; one perceives loss as a threat, whereas the other views it as an opportunity for reorganisation [20]. Extraversion and low neuroticism are regarded as the strongest non-health determinants in most urban older adults` groups [21]. High self-esteem and consistent, productive, or consumption-oriented leisure activities also support life satisfaction [22]. Additionally, literacy and cognitive ability are positively correlated with life satisfaction, especially in very elderly samples [23]. Literacy and cognitive skills enable older adults to manage their daily lives independently, increase access to financial and health literacy, reduce stress associated with illiteracy, and enhance autonomy, thereby contributing positively to active ageing [24, 25]. Furthermore, geographical factors significantly influence life satisfaction, as the experience of ageing in rural areas differs from that in urban settings owing to the distinct cultural and physical characteristics of urban environments [26]. The differences shape the characteristics of autonomy as well as the overall experience of life satisfaction.

This study, conducted in Türkiye, a country with an increasingly ageing population, aims to investigate the factors affecting life satisfaction among older adults in Türkiye. For the first time in Türkiye, the Turkish Statistical Institute conducted the Türkiye Older Persons Profile Survey in 2023, which included a variety of questions to establish a profile of older adults in the country. The focus of this study encompasses a variety of factors, including sociodemographic factors, health status and functioning, social support and lifestyle, psychosocial well-being and social exclusion. By examining these factors, the study aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the determinants of life satisfaction among older adults. Importantly, this study is the first to examine the factors affecting life satisfaction among older adults using the Türkiye Older Persons Profile micro dataset.

Data and methods

Study population and dataset

The dataset utilised for this study comprises microdata derived from the Türkiye Older Persons Profile Survey. This survey was conducted under a bilateral protocol signed on 10 May 2023 between the Turkish Statistical Institute (TURKSTAT) and the General Directorate of Disability and Elderly Services of the Ministry of Family and Social Services. The fieldwork was carried out by TURKSTAT across Türkiye from 23 October to 18 December 2023. The target population consists of individuals aged 50 years and older living in private households in Türkiye, excluding the institutional population such as nursing homes, dormitories, prisons, and military facilities. The sampling frame was constructed using the Address-Based Population Registration System, supported by the National Address Database. A two-stage cluster sampling design was employed. In the first stage, clusters consisting of approximately 100 household addresses with at least one individual aged 50 or older were selected with probability proportional to size. In the second stage, 10 households were systematically selected from each sampled cluster. The survey is representative at the national level and across 12 NUTS-1 regions. Sampling weights provided by TurkStat were applied in the analysis. These weights incorporate design weights, non-response adjustment, calibration, and trimming, resulting in a final weighting factor [27]. All analyses were conducted using sampling weights provided by TURKSTAT, which incorporate design weights, non-response adjustment, and calibration. All reported coefficients and marginal effects are based on survey-weighted estimates. The study included only participants aged 65 and above. Perceptual and subjective questions incorporated into the survey were evaluated only if the participants answered them directly. Consequently, data from 10,348 older adults were included in the analyses.

Dependent variable

The dependent variable used in the study is the life satisfaction of older adults. This variable was measured by the question, “Overall, are you satisfied with your life? (Yes; No).” The categorical distribution of the dependent variable is presented in Fig. 1.

A total of 89.3% of older adults reported being satisfied with their lives, while 10.7% stated that they were not satisfied.

Statistical analysis

Excel was used to organise the dataset, and Stata 16 was used for statistical analysis. Given the binary nominal categorical structure of the dependent variable, the binary logit regression model, one of the discrete choice models, was employed in the study. Sampling weights provided by the Turkish Statistical Institute were applied, and standard errors were computed accounting for the complex survey design. For the assumption of multicollinearity among explanatory variables, variance inflation factors were used, and the traditional threshold of 5 was used for all variables. VIF values below 5 indicate that there is no multicollinearity problem [28, 29]. Due to the nature of discrete choice models, quantitative interpretations of the coefficients will be made through average marginal effects [30].

To test the multicollinearity assumption, variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated. All VIF values for the independent variables in the model were below 5, indicating that multicollinearity is not an issue. The established model as a whole was found to be statistically significant (P < 0.0001).

Marginal effects will be evaluated by taking into account 1%, 5% and 10% significance levels. The study results indicate that older adults residing in eastern Türkiye exhibit a 4.4% lower level of life satisfaction than their counterparts in western Türkiye. Additionally, upon analysis of sociodemographic indicators, it was observed that life satisfaction tends to increase with age, and older male adults are 3.7% less likely than older female adults to life satisfaction. Receiving social assistance is associated with a 3.2% lower predicted probability of reporting life satisfaction among older adults.

Regarding health status and functional indicators, older adults with good and moderate overall health exhibit 4.9% and 3.9% higher life satisfaction, respectively, compared to those experiencing poor health. Those who are able to go out independently are 5.6% more likely to express life satisfaction than those who are unable to do so. Additionally, older adults receiving home care services are 7.7% less likely to report satisfaction with their lives than those not receiving such services.

Examining the results concerning social support and lifestyle indicators, older adults living alone are 2.6% less likely to report life satisfaction compared to those residing with others. An increase in life satisfaction correlates with the number of sources from which support can be obtained. Older adults who receive assistance from their neighbours are 2,8% more likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who do not. Additionally, older individuals who have someone available to call for help during emergencies are 3.7% more likely to express life satisfaction than those who lack such support. Moreover, older adults who perceive their home environment as safe when alone are 3.2% more likely to report satisfaction with their lives than those who do not feel secure.

Older adults who plan to live in a nursing home in the future are 6.3% less likely to be satisfied with life compared to those who believe they will live with their children or close relatives. Older individuals interested in religious matters are 2.9% more likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who are not. Those who have been insulted or mistreated by family members are 6.8% less likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who have not.

In terms of psychosocial well-being and social exclusion indicators, the results show that older adults who believe life is meaningless are 7.7% less likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who do not share this view. Older adults who are frequently bored are 7.1% less likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who are not. Older adults who often feel anxious are 4.1% less likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who do not experience such feelings. Conversely, older adults who, when reflecting on their lives as a whole, consider themselves generally happy are 8.7% more likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who do not. Additionally, older adults who believe they have achieved their life goals are 4.2% more likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who do not.

Older adults who perceive those matters concerning themselves are beyond their control exhibit a 2.4% lower likelihood of life satisfaction compared to those who do not hold this belief. Conversely, older persons who believe that the national government provides protection and implements effective measures against their social exclusion are 3.9% more likely to report satisfaction with their lives than their counterparts without this belief. Additionally, older individuals who perceive that society treats older adults poorly are 2.9% less likely to express life satisfaction than those who do not share this perception. Furthermore, older adults who have experienced any form of violence—whether physical, sexual, economic, or psychological—from family members or acquaintances are 8.1% less likely to be satisfied with their lives than those who have not encountered such violence.

Discussion

The observed disparity in life satisfaction between older adults residing in eastern versus western Türkiye is primarily associated with differences in economic and urban infrastructure. Older adults living in rural eastern areas often face limited access to urban amenities, lower income levels, and less urban development compared to their western counterparts, factors that are likely associated with lower life satisfaction [31]. Additionally, geographical variations, disparities in educational attainment, and differences in per capita gross national income underscore the east-west divide, are associated with lower levels of well-being of this vulnerable demographic [26]. Being unable to access basic urban needs such as health care, effective public transport, recreational areas, and social centres underscores that while life satisfaction has universal traits, it is also significantly influenced by regional and cultural urban contexts. Older adults are more reliant on urban functions to sustain independent living and uphold their autonomy. Consequently, residing in inefficient urban regions or disadvantaged areas may diminish the life satisfaction of older adults [32–34].

Contrary to common research assumptions, the increase in life satisfaction with age may be interpreted in light of to the fact that older adults residing in Türkiye tend to be more content than demanding when compared to their European counterparts. As age advances, feelings of gratitude for one’s current circumstances, a reduction in demands and life expectations, and a desire for healthy living can be explained by the satisfaction paradox, which suggests a positive outlook towards one’s present situation. This phenomenon is characterised by a subjective evaluation that lags behind objective economic realities, with individuals finding peace through gratitude for what they currently have [35]. The 2015 Rural Ageing Analysis report for the TR82 Region, conducted by the North Anatolia Development Agency (KUZKA), found that older adults without income sources expressed satisfaction with their lives despite financial hardships [36]. This evidence indicates that older adults are generally content, and their life satisfaction increases naturally over time. Although it may be argued that older adults with limited economic resources can find contentment, those receiving social assistance are associated with lower levels of life satisfaction compared to those who do not. While feelings of contentment and gratitude contribute to inner peace, the absence of adequate material support can diminish the quality of life. As age-related physical decline hampers individuals’ ability to work and generate income, financial hardship is associated with poorer daily living and overall life satisfaction [37].

One primary factor contributing to lower life satisfaction among older men compared to older women is that women tend to maintain stronger social relationships and bonds [38]. Moreover, when examining social roles, it is evident that men’s emphasis on work, career, and societal status—paired with the loss of meaning and purpose following retirement—further diminishes their life satisfaction [39]. Conversely, women’s greater ability to adapt more rapidly to widowhood has a lesser impact on their overall well-being [40]. Furthermore, after retirement, men are observed to lose their societal status, and their perceived loss of masculinity may also impact their overall life satisfaction [41, 42].

Another key indicator of life satisfaction is overall health and functional ability [43]. In Türkiye, older adults in good health report higher life satisfaction than those in poorer health, supporting this trend. Additionally, those who can go out independently tend to report greater life satisfaction than those who cannot, emphasising the positive effect of autonomy on daily activities. As ageing is accompanied by a decline in health, dependence on others for routine activities such as grocery shopping, visiting parks, and domestic tasks can lead to stress and increased anxiety. Therefore, maintaining good health enhances autonomy in decision-making and physical activity, which in turn has a greater impact on the life satisfaction of older adults [44, 45]. Additionally, this study indicates that older adults in Türkiye who receive home care support tend to have lower life satisfaction compared to those who do not. This may be attributed to increased fragility and poorer health conditions, which can diminish perceived life satisfaction by reducing autonomy and increasing dependence on others [46].

Regarding health, psychosocial factors and indicators of social exclusion also influence life satisfaction in older adults. Research indicates that perceiving life as meaningless—an attitude that amplifies the negative psychological impacts of ageing—is associated with a lower likelihood of experiencing life satisfaction, compared to individuals who do not hold this view. This is because, although numerous external factors contribute to life satisfaction, overall well-being is fundamentally linked to an individual’s perception and interpretation of their own life [47]. Chronic boredom and persistent anxiety are associated with reduced life satisfaction. In contrast, individuals who reflect on their lives holistically and consider themselves generally happy, having achieved their personal goals, tend to report higher levels of satisfaction. Conversely, individuals who believe that matters concerning their personal control are beyond their influence are less likely to feel satisfied with their lives than those who do not share this belief. In this context, the fear of losing control over one’s life and the perception of diminished capacity for independent action are associated with lower levels of life satisfaction.

Understanding that an individual’s visibility within society influences their outlook on life, the belief that the state safeguards older adults and implements effective measures against their social exclusion tends to increase their life satisfaction compared to those who do not share this view. Conversely, poor treatment of older adults, which can lead to social marginalisation and ageism, diminishes their satisfaction with life. Historically, ageing was associated with wisdom and held a respected position at the core of society; however, in the modern era, older adults are often marginalised, alienated, or regarded as irrelevant [48]. State-enacted social policies serve as evidence that older adults remain integrated into society, emphasising their continued social inclusion rather than exclusion.

It is unequivocally apparent that older adults who have not experienced any form of violence—be it physical, sexual, economic, or psychological—or abuse from family members or acquaintances exhibit a higher level of life satisfaction compared to those who have. Nonetheless, this finding also highlights the converse issue: the prevalence of violence against older adults. Regardless of demographic factors such as age or gender, violence invariably exerts a detrimental effect on an individual’s perceived quality of life. Critical areas for further investigation include identifying specific types of violence and formulating targeted policy interventions. The marginalisation of older adults—particularly those who constitute vulnerable populations and lack the physical or mental capacity for self-defence—due to societal structures such as retirement, social exclusion based on age, or familial marginalisation, exacerbates their susceptibility to violence. Living alone may represent a significant initial factor contributing to this increased vulnerability. As our study highlights, living alone reduces life satisfaction, while having more people to turn to for support increases it. Being able to get help from neighbours, having someone to call for help in an emergency, or feeling safe at home when alone are factors that positively influence older adults’ life satisfaction.

As social networks diminish with advancing age, individuals may experience increased loneliness, reduced ability to interact socially within their environment, difficulties in adapting to societal changes, and challenges in obtaining personalised support [49]. These factors can ultimately lead to depression and emotional distress, potentially resulting in mental health crises. The retirement, widowhood, absence of children, or having children who reside far away can further contribute to social isolation and psychological ailments such as depression among older adults [50]. As previously noted, key determinants of life satisfaction include mental well-being, the pursuit of a meaningful existence, and social engagement. The decline in autonomous management of daily activities, often associated with biological ageing, directs older adults toward institutional care or communal living arrangements rather than independent residence.

This research indicates that older adults contemplating relocation to a care facility tend to report lower life satisfaction compared to those intending to remain with their children or close relatives. This suggests that directing older adults toward institutional care may negatively impact their well-being, underscoring the significance of ageing in place. It is feasible to support the ageing process within the existing environment by maintaining spatial and social stability [51]. Ensuring urban and residential spaces are conducive to ageing, along with fostering strong social connections with neighbours and the community, can enhance life satisfaction among older adults [52]. Social interactions and intergenerational engagement positively influence psychological well-being, thereby promoting overall life satisfaction [53].

This study, conducted in Türkiye, identifies religious engagement among older adults as a significant factor in life satisfaction. Findings indicate that seniors interested in religious matters tend to report greater life satisfaction compared to those who are not. Religion offers a structured social environment that fosters a sense of belonging and connection [54, 55]. Activities such as attending religious gatherings, socialising with peers of similar age and perspectives, and participating in routine religious practices promote social interaction and engagement in communal spaces. Consequently, individuals maintain their identity as active members of society. Additionally, religious involvement supports positive mental health by cultivating hope, acceptance, and a sense of divine support, thereby mitigating stress and depression. It also alleviates anxiety related to mortality, which tends to increase with age [56]. Within religious communities, recognising older individuals as wise and esteemed enhances their perceived societal value, thereby contributing to their overall well-being [57, 58].

Conclusion

Life satisfaction is a complex concept involving multiple interrelated factors, especially among vulnerable populations. This study examines the life satisfaction of older adults in Türkiye, highlighting key determinants, including health, social support, and economic conditions. The findings indicate that positive physical and mental health enhance life satisfaction, whereas loneliness, social isolation, violence, and exclusion have adverse effects. Additionally, contentment and religious beliefs contribute positively, and attitudes towards ageing and acceptance of life stages also influence overall satisfaction. While life satisfaction reflects individual well-being, targeted policy interventions—such as expanding social support systems, providing comprehensive health and home care, promoting social participation, and developing age-friendly environments—can mitigate issues such as loneliness, exclusion, and violence, thereby improving the quality of life for older adults.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in its use of a nationally representative dataset covering the entire older adult population in Türkiye, which elevates the observed associations from mere statistical estimates to robust empirical patterns and provides baseline evidence for life satisfaction of older adults in the Turkish context. Because the large sample size enhances the statistical robustness of the analysis and facilitates the examination of a broad range of individual characteristics. Moreover, the study employs a multidimensional analytical framework by concurrently considering sociodemographic factors, health and functional status, social support, psychosocial well-being, and social exclusion, thus offering a comprehensive perspective on the determinants of life satisfaction in later life. On the other hand, there are also some limitations. All variables were measured using self-reported data, which may be subject to reporting bias. Furthermore, life satisfaction and several explanatory variables were captured using single-item, dichotomous measures, thereby limiting variability and reducing measurement precision. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Additionally, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, reverse causality cannot be entirely discounted, particularly with regard to psychosocial variables such as social exclusion, loneliness, and depressive symptoms. It is also plausible that lower life satisfaction may influence individuals’ perceptions of their social relationships and overall well-being.
