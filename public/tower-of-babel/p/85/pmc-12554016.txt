Letter to the Editor: Impact of the Prosigna assay on neoadjuvant treatment decision-making in patients with early-stage HR-positive/HER 2-negative breast cancer: a single-center prospective observational study
Authors: Y. Wei, K. Li, J. Lv, C. Wei
Published: 2025

We read with interest the prospective observational study by Corti et al. investigating the impact of the Prosigna assay on neoadjuvant treatment decisions in early-stage hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor2-negative breast cancer.1 The authors report that Prosigna testing altered treatment plans in 35.2% of patients and improved physician and patient confidence. While the study addresses an important clinical question, several methodological and interpretative aspects warrant further scrutiny.

Firstly, the Prosigna assay is Food and Drug Administration-cleared only for post-operative specimens, yet this study utilized core biopsies obtained before surgery.2 Although previous retrospective data support technical feasibility, the single-center design and small cohort (n = 54) limit broader applicability. Crucially, 55.5% (n = 30) of patients underwent concurrent Oncotype DX testing, which may have confounded treatment decisions. While the authors note high concordance between assays in 50% of cases, the potential influence of Oncotype DX on clinician choices cannot be dismissed. For neoadjuvant applications, validation in larger, multi-center cohorts without parallel genomic testing is needed.

Secondly, the study did not evaluate surrogate immunohistochemical (IHC) subtypes (e.g. Ki67-based luminal classification) against Prosigna’s intrinsic subtyping. Given that Ki67 is not routinely tested in United States pathology workflows, this omission is understandable but represents a missed opportunity to contextualize Prosigna’s added value. If Prosigna is to guide neoadjuvant therapy, its superiority over IHC-based classifiers must be established—particularly since IHC is more accessible and cost-effective. Additionally, the lack of association between Residual Cancer Burden and Risk of Recurrence (ROR) groups (P = 0.21) raises questions about Prosigna’s ability to predict chemosensitivity, a key rationale for its neoadjuvant use.

Thirdly, the authors suggest Prosigna could ‘spare chemo-resistant patients from unnecessary chemotherapy’ by identifying luminal A tumors. ROR-low tumors, however, constituted only 18.5% (n = 10) of the cohort, all luminal A, and just 20% (n = 2) of these patients had treatment de-escalation. Conversely, 75% of changes in ROR-high patients involved escalation to chemotherapy. This risks overtreating indolent cancers, especially as long-term survival data are absent. Prosigna’s prognostic (not predictive) nature means its utility for predicting chemotherapy benefit remains unproven in the neoadjuvant setting.

This study provides preliminary evidence that Prosigna may influence neoadjuvant decision-making. Its impact, however, appears driven predominantly by reclassifying intermediate-risk patients (45.5% change rate), highlighting the uncertainty in this subgroup. To advance clinical adoption, future work should: compare Prosigna with IHC-based classifiers (e.g. Ki67) in randomized trials; incorporate long-term survival outcomes to assess whether assay-directed changes improve prognosis; and validate ROR subgroups in cohorts receiving uniform modern therapies (e.g. CDK4/6 inhibitors + endocrine therapy). Until prospective trials (e.g. OPTIMA,3 NeoTAILOR4) confirm survival benefits, Prosigna’s role in neoadjuvant settings remains exploratory.
