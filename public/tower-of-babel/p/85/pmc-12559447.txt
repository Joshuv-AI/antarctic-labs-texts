Reduced Pressure Ionization Enhances Native Mass Spectrometry of Proteins and Protein Complexes
Authors: Jake P Violi, Chong Zhang, William A Donald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNative mass spectrometry (MS) enables the analysis of protein interactions in complex biological mixtures. However, nonvolatile salts and buffers commonly present in such samples can cause ion adduction, peak broadening, and reduced signal intensity. Reducing the pressure surrounding the ionization emitter significantly improves native MS performance under these challenging conditions. Signal enhancements of up to 20‐fold were observed with nanoscale emitters, and up to 7‐fold with microscale emitters in high‐salt solutions. Protein ions remained detectable in solutions containing up to 300 mM NaCl, unlike ambient pressure ionization. High signal‐to‐noise was observed for the DDB1:DCAF1 complex at 50 nM using reduced pressure ionization, whereas no readily assignable signal was detected at ambient pressure, demonstrating its utility for detecting tightly bound complexes at trace levels. Coupling to native ion mobility mass spectrometry showed that arrival time distributions and collision cross sections did not depend significantly on the pressure used, indicating that structural information is preserved. These results show that reduced pressure ionization improves native MS performance under conditions that typically suppress signal at ambient pressure, such as high salt or low analyte concentration. The method is compatible with both nano‐ and microscale emitters and requires only minor modifications to existing instrumentation. Reduced pressure ionization expands the range of conditions accessible by native MS and is expected to enable automated, high‐throughput workflows in structural proteomics, biopharmaceutical characterisation, and protein‐ligand interaction studies.Keywords: Ion mobility spectrometry, Native mass spectrometry, Protein complexes, Reduced‐pressure ionization, Trace protein analysis

Introduction

Native mass spectrometry (MS) is increasingly recognized as an important approach for elucidating the composition and interactions of proteins and protein complexes.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] By forming protein ions using electrospray ionization from aqueous solutions at near‐neutral pH, noncovalent interactions can be maintained for sensitive and rapid detection by MS from heterogenous samples.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Native MS can reveal detailed insights into the composition,[ 4 ] stoichiometry,[ 5 ] interaction dynamics,[ 6 , 7 ] structural properties,[ 8 ] and functional characteristics of protein complexes[ 9 ] under conditions that resembles their native environment. For instance, native MS has been used to reveal insights into viral capsid assembly,[ 10 ] membrane protein interactions,[ 11 ] and large multi‐subunit complexes.[ 12 ] However, one of the key challenges of native MS is the presence of non‐volatile salts, buffers, and ligands, which are often essential for maintaining protein stability, enzyme activity, or metal cofactor binding in biologically relevant solutions.[ 13 , 14 , 15 ] The presence of these species can adversely affect native MS analysis by causing ion suppression, introducing chemical noise, and forming nonspecific protein adducts with nonvolatile compounds.[ 13 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] While native MS can be used to analyze highly complex biological samples, its effective implementation remains challenging.

Reducing salt adduction and improving resolution is of fundamental importance in native MS for investigating protein‐ligand interactions,[ 1 ] protein complexes,[ 2 ] and other biomolecular assemblies.[ 19 ] To address these challenges, four primary strategies have been used: nanoscale ionization emitters, which produce smaller droplets that limit co‐solvation of nonvolatile species and thereby reduce adduction;[ 16 , 17 , 18 ] buffer loading with volatile electrolytes such as ammonium acetate to displace nonvolatile cations;[ 7 , 15 ] size‐based separations (e.g., size exclusion chromatography) to separate non‐volatile components;[ 20 , 21 ] and in‐source activation to remove adducts via collisional activation.[ 9 , 21 ] Nanoscale emitters have been shown to improve desolvation and minimize salt adduction for proteins,[ 16 , 17 ] protein‐small molecule complexes,[ 18 ] nucleic acid assemblies,[ 22 , 23 ] protein–protein interactions,[ 5 , 16 , 17 ] and to screen thousands of natural products simultaneously for protein binding.[ 20 ] Recently, Agasid and co‐workers used native MS in sodium‐rich solutions with nanoscale emitters to reveal sodium dependent ligand‐induced changes and enhanced ligand binding to G protein‐coupled receptors,[ 24 ] providing critical insights that are challenging to obtain with standard methods. However, nanoscale emitters are prone to clogging and operate at sub‐nanoliter flow rates, posing challenges to their integration into high‐throughput, online native MS workflows.[ 25 , 26 ] Moreover, size‐based separation methods can disrupt solution binding equilibria. Although in‐source ion activation can reduce adduction, it may perturb specific noncovalent interactions,[ 27 ] potentially resulting in an underrepresentation of binding events. Developing complementary approaches to further improve resolution and signal quality should be beneficial in tackling the increasingly complex and heterogeneous samples of growing interest in native MS.

In native MS, nanoscale ionization emitters can reduce the adduction of nonvolatile salts to protein ions by producing smaller initial droplets.[ 1 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] At low protein concentrations, these smaller droplets are more likely to contain either one protein molecule or none. Thus, the smaller the initial droplets, the fewer nonvolatile species are available to adduct to the protein once the volatile solvent evaporates, resulting in higher effective resolution for detecting proteins and protein complexes and higher signal.[ 16 , 17 , 18 ] One strategy to further reduce droplet sizes and improve desolvation is reduced pressure electrospray ionization (ESI), which has been applied to denatured proteins, peptides,[ 28 , 29 ] and small molecules[ 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] but has not been reported for use in native MS. In principle, reduced pressure ionization could benefit native MS by producing smaller initial droplets and enhancing ion desolvation, thereby reducing salt adduction and improving protein ion detection from solutions with high concentrations of nonvolatile salts and buffers.

To explore the utility of reduced pressure ionization in native MS, we examined its ability to enhance protein ion signals under conditions that typically suppress protein detection due to nonvolatile components. Using custom 3D‐printed reduced pressure ionization chambers that attach to commercial mass spectrometers, the system reaches operating pressure within seconds. The effects of reduced pressure ionization on signal and salt adduction were evaluated using both nano‐ and microscale emitters with proteins and protein complexes, including from solutions containing up to 300 mM NaCl, mimicking some of the highest salt concentrations found in biological environments.[ 34 , 35 ] These are conditions under which ambient pressure native MS can often fail to yield detectable protein ions, emphasizing the need to explore reduced pressure as an approach for overcoming limitations in the analysis of solutions containing high concentrations of nonvolatile components.

Results and Discussion

Enhancing Sample Delivery and Mitigating Clogging of Narrow‐Bore Emitters in Native MS

In Figure 1a, a diagram of a custom 3D‐printed reduced pressure chamber is shown, which was designed to interface with the ambient pressure inlet of an ESI mass spectrometer. The chamber is fabricated from a heat‐resistant, nonporous polymeric material, ensuring a seal that enables the reduction of pressure to 200–333 mbar (depending on the instrument that is used) through vacuum suction provided by the mass spectrometer inlet while withstanding the high temperatures from the heated inlet to the MS. A feedthrough is used to integrate a nanoelectrospray ionization (nESI) emitter tip into the chamber, and two ports are included: i) one equipped with a leak valve to adjust the pressure and ii) the other connected to a gauge for real‐time monitoring of the chamber pressure. The chamber reliably reaches stable vacuum conditions within 5 s of being mounted on the mass spectrometer, attaining pressures as low as ∼333 and ∼200 mbar respectively for instruments with capillary‐skimmer (LTQ Orbitrap XL) and capillary‐RF focusing (Q Exactive UHMR) ion sources. The capillary entrances of both MS instruments have similar outer diameters, enabling the same reduced pressure chamber to be used interchangeably without modification. Moreover, the inlet capillaries for both systems have essentially the same inner diameters (i.d.), but the shorter capillary on the UHMR (∼58 mm vs. ∼102 mm) reduces the conductance limit, enabling lower pressures despite similar pressures measured in the first stage of differential pumping (∼1.6–1.9 mbar). Given the use of 3D‐printing, the chamber can be rapidly customized and, in principle, readily adapted to a range of MS platforms. Over the past 6 months, multiple users within our laboratory have consistently used reduced pressure ionization to acquire hundreds of native mass spectra of different proteins and protein complexes.

Insets in (b) and (f) show optical microscope images of the emitter tip taken immediately before and after spectra acquisition, confirming enhanced sample delivery to the tip by use of reduced pressure ionization. c) Fold‐change increase in the signal and e) full width half maximum (FWHM) of the most abundant charge state as function of the ionization pressure for both emitter tip inner diameters. Error bars denote ± one standard deviation from triplicate measurements.

As an example, reduced pressure ionization has been effective in facilitating sample delivery, providing an advantage in reducing issues with clogging that are associated with nanoscale emitter tips in native MS.[ 26 , 36 ] In Figure 1b, a microscope image of an nanoelectrospray ionization emitter with an inner tip diameter of ∼500 nm that was deliberately misloaded with an exaggeratedly large air bubble (∼4 mm long) between the solution and the emitter tip is shown. Smaller air bubbles can readily clog narrow‐bore emitters. The aqueous solution in the emitter contained 5 µM of a recombinant truncated receptor region of Kelch like family member 7 (KLHL7) in 120 mM ammonium acetate. KLHL7 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase involved in protein degradation pathways and is linked to neurodegenerative diseases.[ 37 ] At ambient pressure, no protein ions were detected owing to inadequate sample delivery to the tip of the emitter (Figure 1b). However, reducing the pressure of the chamber surrounding the emitter creates a pressure differential that pulls the solution toward the tip. For example, using a pressure of ∼733 mbar, abundant protein ion signals corresponding to the 12+, 11+, and 10+ charge states of KLHL7 were readily detected (Figure 1d) with high signal‐to‐noise (S/N) ratios (e.g., ∼540 for 11+). These charge states are consistent with native mass spectra of proteins of similar molecular weights (∼35.1 kDa), such as Apolipoprotein E (34.4 kDa) as reported previously.[ 8 ] When the pressure was returned to ambient, signal intensity for protonated KLHL7 decreased by approximately 20‐fold (Figure 1f). Following the experiment, a microscope image of the emitter indicated that the air bubble had migrated, allowing the solution to fully reach the tip of the ionization emitter, resolving the clogging issue (Figure 1f). The charge state distribution was comparable but slightly lower at ambient pressure vs at 733 mbar, consistent with reduced pressure not causing significant activation of protein ions under these conditions.

As pressure decreased from ambient to 733 mbar, signal intensity increased monotonically by up 20‐fold at 733 mbar compared to ambient pressure for the narrower bore emitter tips (∼500 nm i.d.) and were up to 7‐fold higher for the wider bore emitters (∼1.3 µm i.d.; Figure 1c). The enhanced ion signal observed under reduced‐pressure conditions arises from improved desolvation dynamics and more efficient ion formation. Lower ambient pressure increases solvent volatility and accelerates solvent evaporation, leading to smaller initial ESI droplets.[ 32 , 38 ] These smaller droplets, in turn, yield higher ionization efficiencies and better ion transmission,[ 32 , 38 ] consistent with theoretical predictions from vacuum ESI models developed in the context of small molecules and ion thruster propulsion systems.[ 39 , 40 ] These effects appear to also hold in the context of native mass spectrometry, as evidenced by the increased signal intensity observed here with reduced pressure ionization. Additionally, pressure differentials across the emitter tip may increase solution flow to the tip, further enhancing ion signal by improving sample delivery.

Notably, the extent of adduction, likely corresponding to adventitious sodium and acetate from the electrolyte solution, decreased as pressure was reduced from ambient to 667 mbar (Figures 1e and S1), resulting in narrower peaks for the charge states of KLHL7. For example, the full width half maxima of the most abundant charge state of KLHL7 decreased by ∼35 ± 2.5% as the pressure decreased from ambient pressure to 667 mbar for the wide‐bore emitters, while nanoscale emitters exhibited a more moderate peak narrowing of ∼7.9 ± 4.3% (Figures 1e and S1). The use of reduced pressure is expected to produce smaller initial ESI droplets, resulting in droplets containing either no protein or a single protein molecule at low protein concentration, reducing the availability of nonvolatile salts to adduct to the protein during solvent evaporation. This would result in narrower peaks and higher resolution, particularly for the larger bore emitters which have large initial droplets under ambient conditions. Overall, reduced‐pressure ionization offers enhanced ion signal (up to 20‐fold) and improved resolution in native MS and can also mitigate clogging from misloaded tips that are associated with narrow‐bore emitters.

To assess the stability of reduced pressure ionization, a solution containing 5 µM of bovine carbonic anhydrase II (bCAII) in 100 mM ammonium acetate was continuously analysed with reduced pressure ionization for 30 min. In Figure S2, the signal of the most abundant charge state (10+) of bCAII is plotted versus acquisition time for three replicate emitter tips at 733 mbar. The protein ion signal relative standard deviation is less than 10% for three replicate emitter tips at an ionization pressure of 733 mbar, demonstrating that reduce pressure does not result in high signal variability over relatively long aquisition times. While the reduced pressure chamber can reach pressures as low as 200–333 mbar, ion signal becomes unstable and is typically lost at pressures below ∼667 mbar. Two factors may contribute to this loss. First, at lower pressures, ion mobility increases, which can lead to greater plume expansion and higher ion velocities that may reduce ion transfer efficiency into the narrow inlet to the mass spectrometer. Second, solvent evaporation from the Taylor cone may exceed the delivery rate from the emitter tip, disrupting the stability of the electrospray. Both effects are expected to become more pronounced at lower pressures. No evidence of electrical discharge was observed down to the lowest pressures accessible with this setup, as confirmed by the absence of current spikes, visible arcing, or the emergence of new low‐mass ions.

Native Mass Spectrometry with Wider‐Bore Emitters from Solutions Containing High Concentrations of Nonvolatile Salts and Buffers

To investigate the ability of native MS to directly analyze proteins from solutions containing high concentrations of nonvolatile salts and buffers using larger bore emitters, we examined the effects of reduced pressure ionization with ∼1.3 µm emitter tips under both ambient and reduced pressure. Larger emitter tips are more readily compatible with higher throughput and automated applications such as online desalting,[ 25 ] separation,[ 41 , 42 , 43 ] or chip‐based infusion systems.[ 44 ] Native mass spectra were obtained for bCAII and a recombinant truncated receptor region of zyg‐11 related cell cycle regulator (Zer1) in solutions containing 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, and 500 mM ammonium acetate. Zer1 is a ubiquitin ligase that facilitates protein degradation by “tagging” target proteins with ubiquitin, a post‐translation modification that signals proteasomal degradation.[ 45 ] Native mass spectra were collected at both ambient pressure (Figure 2a,b) and at 733 mbar (Figure 2c,d). At ambient pressure, the spectra for bCAII and Zer1 have elevated baselines, with overlapping chemical noise from ionic salt clusters and heavily adducted, unresolved protein ion charge states (Figure 2a,b). Nonvolatile species can readily adduct to the protein ions, spreading protein ion signal across multiple detection channels and suppress ionization. Upon applying reduced pressure, bCAII and Zer1 ions are readily detected in relatively low charge state distributions consistent with native‐like mass spectra (Figure S3). Although some ionic salt clusters remain, they are now distinct and assignable and do not obscure the protein ions (Figure 2c,d). These results demonstrate that reduced pressure ionization in native MS can be used to detect protein ions from solutions containing high concentrations of nonvolatile salts and buffers using micron‐sized emitter tips without the use of nanoscale emitters.[ 16 , 17 , 18 ]

Reduced Pressure Ionization Increases the Limit of Nonvolatile Salt Concentrations in Native MS

To increase the limits of nonvolatile salt concentration in native MS, nanoscale emitter tips were integrated with buffer loading[ 7 , 15 ] using 1000 mM ammonium acetate, nanoscale ion emitters with inner tip diameters of ∼500 nm, and reduced pressure ionization. This approach was applied to solutions containing up to 300 mM NaCl, which is double the highest nonvolatile salt concentration previously reported for protein signal detection using nanoscale emitters.[ 16 , 17 ] Solutions containing 10 µM of bCAII and a recombinant truncated receptor region of Kelch like family member 20 (KLHL20) containing 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, and 1000 mM ammonium acetate were analyzed under both ambient and reduced pressure conditions. KLHL20, like KLHL7, is an E3 ubiquitin ligase involved in protein degradation pathways. However, KLHL20 plays a broader role in cellular homeostasis and stress response than KLHL7.[ 46 ] At ambient pressure, the resulting mass spectra of solutions containing bCAII and KLHL20 have no detectable protein ions (Figure 3a,b). However, by lowering the pressure of the ionization chamber to 733 mbar, protein ions for both bCAII and KLHL20 were readily detected (Figure 3c,d). The protein ion charge state distributions were native‐like and comparable to those obtained at ambient pressure without the addition of nonvolatile salt and buffer (Figure S4). These results demonstrate that integrating nanoscale emitters, buffer loading, and reduced pressure ionization enables protein detection in native MS at very high concentrations of nonvolatile salt (300 mM NaCl) and buffer (20 mM Tris), which should expand the range of conditions under which native MS can be applied.

Native Mass Spectrometry of a Protein Formed from Diluted Storage Buffer Containing High Concentrations of a Nonvolatile Salt, Buffer, and Phosphine‐Based Reductant

While ammonium acetate is commonly used as an electrolyte for native MS, many proteins are stored in specialised buffers to prolong stability in solution,[ 13 ] which is particularly the case for many recombinant proteins that are often kept in specialised buffers post‐purification including the recombinant truncated receptor region of Kelch‐like ECH‐associated protein 1 (KEAP1) used in the present study. KEAP1 is a critical regulatory protein involved in the cellular response to oxidative stress.[ 47 ] A common KEAP1 storage buffer contains: 50 mM HEPES/NaOH, 250 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP. Proteins such as KEAP1 are typically buffer exchanged into a volatile electrolyte (e.g., ammonium acetate) prior to native MS, and spectra are acquired with minimal delay to avoid protein instability. Buffer exchange can risk affecting protein conformations and result in the loss of protein‐ligand interactions. Here, KEAP1 in its storage buffer was diluted threefold with water to a final concentration of 17 mM HEPES, 83 mM NaCl, and 333 µM TCEP and directly analyzed by native MS. When using ∼500 nm emitter tips at ambient pressure, the resulting spectra showed a broad peak corresponding to a protein with unresolvable charge states likely owing to the nonspecific adduction of buffer components to the protein ion (Figure 4a). However, by lowering the ionization pressure, distinct KEAP1 protein ions became readily detectable (Figure 4b), with charge state distributions consistent with native mass spectra (Figure S5). For each charge state of the KEAP1 protein ion series, the peaks are broad owing to the adduction of multiple HEPES molecules and sodium ions (Figure S6). Overall, this data indicates that reduced pressure ionization can facilitate the native MS analysis of proteins diluted directly from their specialized storage buffers containing high concentrations of nonvolatile salts and reducing agents.

Native MS analysis of a) and b) KEAP1 using ∼500 nm i.d. emitter tips in diluted protein storage buffer (17 mM HEPES / NaOH, 83 mM NaCl and 333 µM TCEP) at a) ambient pressure and b) 733 mbar. a) Inset is a scanning electron micrograph of an emitter tip with i.d. of ∼500 nm.

Reduced Pressure Ionization Retains Protein:Protein Interactions and Lowers Detection Limits

To examine the effect of reduced pressure ionization on protein:protein interactions, a truncated recombinant DDB1:DCAF1 (DNA Damage‐Binding protein 1:DDB1 and CUL4 Associated Factor 1) complex was analyzed at three different pressures (ambient pressure, 867, and 733 mbar) and seven concentrations of the protein complex (10, 5, 2.5, 1, 0.5, 0.1, and 0.05 µM). The DDB1:DCAF1 heterodimer complex represents a portion of the full‐length DCAF1 complex, which is responsible for substrate recognition and modulates various cellular functions, particularly those involved in cell division processes.[ 48 ] For more standard protein concentrations used in native MS (2.5‐10 µM) with ambient pressure ionization, the most abundant ion series corresponded primarily to the intact DDB1:DCAF1 complex, with a lower abundance ion signal corresponding to free DDB1, and no signal detectable for free DCAF1 (Figure 5a,f). Reducing the ionization pressure to 733 mbar results in essentially the same relative distribution of intact DDB1:DCAF1 to DDB1 indicating that any dissociation of the protein complex resulting from reducing the ionization pressure is minimal (Figure 5d). The charge state of the intact DDB1:DCAF1 complex increases by an average of less than one charge state at 733 mbar compared to ambient pressure (Figure 5a,d), consistent with data for other proteins analyzed using reduced pressure ionization, which typically results in a slight increase in charging or remains unchanged. The increase in signal intensity with reduced pressure was consistent across different concentrations (Figure 5c). At 867 mbar, the signal was roughly 2‐2.5 fold higher than at ambient pressure, while at 733 mbar, the intensity increased by approximately 4‐fold compared to data acquired at ambient pressure for concentrations at which ion signal was readily detected using ambient pressure ionization (0.50 to 10 µM; Figure 5c). Reduced pressure ionization enabled the DDB1:DCAF1 complex to be assignable at concentrations as low as 100 and 50 nM (Figure 5e), which was not readily possible with ambient pressure ionization (Figure 5b,c) under these conditions.

Native MS analysis using ∼500 nM i.d. emitters of a), b), d), and e) the protein complex DDB1:DCAF1 at a) and d) 2.5 µM and b) and e) 50 nM at a) and b) ambient pressure and d) and e) 733 mbar. c) Protein complex fold change at two reduced pressure (867 and 733 mbar) over concentrations ranging from 10 to 500 nM and complex absolute intensity at 100 and 50 nM at two reduced pressures. f) Ratio of complex to total protein signal over several concentrations ranging from 10 to 50 nM at ambient pressure, 867 and 733 mbar. c) and f) Error bars denote ± one standard deviation from triplicate measurements.

In native MS, obtaining the binding affinities of strongly bound complexes in the low nanomolar range can be challenging.[ 1 ] At the concentrations typically used in native MS (1 to 10 µM), high‐affinity complexes are often fully saturated, leaving only a small fraction of unbound protein subunits, limiting the ability to accurately determine the equilibrium dissociation constant (K d). However, reduced pressure ionization lowers detection limits, which may enable the more effective quantitation of strong protein‐protein interactions. The DDB1:DCAF1 complex is a tightly bound heterodimer with an unreported binding affinity. The K d for the DDB1:DCAF1 complex can be approximated by assuming that DDB1 and DCAF1 subunits are ionized and detected with the same efficiencies, although this assumption often does not hold true for different proteins. To avoid issues related to saturation, the lowest concentrations where binding between subunits is not near full saturation (50, 100, and 500 nM) were used, as more than 10% of the complex appears partially dissociated in solution at these concentrations (Figure 5f). Based on measurements at 50, 100, and 500 nM, the average K d value for the complex is 17.7 nM with a standard deviation of 6.6 nM. However, this value depends strongly on the ionization efficiency of DDB1 and DDB1:DCAF1. For example, if DDB1 ionizes 80% more efficiently than DDB1:DCAF1, the estimated K d could be as high as 180 nM. Conversely, if DDB1:DCAF1 has an 80% higher ionization efficiency, the K d could be as low as 0.48 nM. An alternative method to estimate K d involves measuring the absolute abundance of the DDB1:DCAF1 complex across increasingly dilute concentrations, then fitting a sigmoid curve to the data to determine the concentration at which 50% of the complex dissociates. This approach provides a lower limit for K d as the sensitivity of the instrument contributes to a natural signal reduction at lower concentrations. Using this method, a K d value of 124 nM was obtained. Overall, reduced pressure ionization enhances detection limits and can facilitate the more direct measurement of the dissociation constants of strongly bound protein interactions, covering a broader range of physiologically relevant concentrations.

Reduced Pressure Ionization Native Ion Mobility Mass Spectrometry

To evaluate the generality of reduced‐pressure ionization across instrument platforms, we adapted our 3D‐printed chamber to the Waters Synapt XS, which features a skimmer–skimmer (Z‐spray) source and travelling wave ion mobility spectrometry (TWIMS). Native MS data were collected for bCAII across a range of source pressures (5 µM protein in 100 mM ammonium acetate) for emitter tips with inner diameters of ∼500 and ∼1,300 nm. Signal intensity increased by up to ∼3.5 fold for the ∼1,300 nm emitters and ∼5.3 fold for the ∼500 nm emitters (Figure S7), similar to the improvements observed with the capillary‐RF focusing interface (UHMR). The peak widths narrowed by ∼58 ± 5.5% for ∼1,300 nm emitters and by ∼60 ± 5.9% for the ∼500 nm emitters at reduced pressure (Figure S7). Spectral resolution improvements at reduced pressure were higher on the Synapt XS compared to capillary interfaces, potentially owing to less efficient desolvation from lower source temperatures and the absence of a long heated capillary resulting in broader peaks with atmospheric pressure ionization (FWHM of 5.3 ± 0.3 for the LTQ‐XL vs 9.3 ± 0.9 for the Synapt XS for ∼500 nm emitters, and 8.3 ± 0.2 for the LTQ‐XL vs. 15.9 ± 1.6 for the Synapt XS for ∼1300 nm emitters). Measured arrival time distributions for both ambient pressure ionization and reduced pressure ionization (733 mbar) are shown in Figure S8 and Table S2 for the most abundant charge states of ubiquitn (6+), cytochrome c (7+), and bCAII (10+). CCS values at reduced pressure were obtained via a calibration procedure from drift tube IMS measurements (refer to Supporting Information Methods).[ 49 ] The arrival time distribution peak centroids and calculated CCS values remained within 10% as the ionization pressure was reduced, consistent with native‐like protein structures being preserved under reduced pressure conditions. These findings also indicate that reduced‐pressure ionization enhances signal and resolution for the skimmer–skimmer (Z‐spray) interface with minimal effects on protein ion structures based on TWIMS data collected at pressures as low as 733 mbar.

Conclusion

Reduced pressure ionization significantly improves native MS performance by addressing key limitations including peak broadening from salt adduction, signal loss in high‐salt solutions, and emitter clogging with narrow‐bore tips. Using nanoscale emitters, protein ion signal increased up to 20‐fold with narrower peaks and improved S/N ratios. Reduced pressure ionization also enabled detection of proteins from solutions containing up to 300 mM NaCl and 20 mM Tris, conditions in which ambient pressure ionization resulted in no detectable protein signal. Proteins were also detected from solutions with up to 150 mM NaCl using wide‐bore emitters (∼1.3 µm i.d.), unlike under ambient pressure ionization. The method preserves noncovalent protein interactions at low nanomolar concentrations (50 nM), enabling more reliable estimation of binding affinities without relying as substantially on assumptions about ionization efficiency, as demonstrated for DDB1:DCAF1. Coupling to native ion mobility MS confirmed that protein ion arrival time distributions (and collision cross sections) did not depend strongly on the pressure used, indicating that structural information is preserved under these conditions. These results demonstrate that reduced pressure ionization is a versatile and robust method for native MS, improving detection limits, resolution, and mitigating clogging.

The method was shown to be compatible with multiple ion source designs (capillary‐skimmer, capillary‐RF focusing, and skimmer–skimmer) across two major instrument manufacturers, and its 3D‐printed design should permit straightforward adaptation to other instruments. Emitter alignment and sample loading are currently manual processes aided by the chamber's viewing port, highlighting opportunities for automation. Integration with online protein separation and automated sample injection is underway to support high‐throughput native MS, leveraging the enhanced signal and increased tolerance to nonvolatile salts achieved with wide‐bore emitter tips under reduced pressures. Enhanced desolvation and reduced adduction of nonvolatile specices at moderate pressures with wide‐bore emitters may also improve native MS for membrane protein analysis[ 50 ] and multiplexed protein‐ligand screening applications.[ 20 ] This approach is expected to expand the use of native MS and native ion mobility MS for analyzing increasingly complex heterogenous biochemical samples and facilitate integration into automated, high‐throughput[ 25 , 51 , 52 ] workflows.
