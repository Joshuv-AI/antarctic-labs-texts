Real-world prescribing patterns and early weight and blood pressure outcomes of GLP-1–based therapies: a retrospective observational study in the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Salma Almemari, Nour Almulla, Yasmin Alsarraf, Ghada Badri, Nelofar Sami Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (RA) and the dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 RA tirzepatide has increased rapidly for the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity. This study aimed to describe real-world prescribing patterns, patient characteristics, and observational short-term changes in weight and blood pressure among adults receiving GLP-1 RA and dual GIP/GLP-1 RA in a tertiary academic healthcare setting in the United Arab Emirates.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included adult patients prescribed GLP-1 RA or tirzepatide (dual GIP/GLP-1 RA) between January 2022 and December 2024. Electronic health records and pharmacy dispensing databases were reviewed. Prescription trends were standardized using monthly treatment units. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were described, and short-term changes in body weight and blood pressure were assessed where follow-up data were available. The mean treatment duration among patients with follow-up data was 6.99 months. An exploratory multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age group, sex, BMI category, treatment group, and HbA1c category was performed to evaluate factors associated with achieving clinically meaningful weight loss (≥ 5%).ResultsA total of 238 patient records and 1,744 dispensing records were analyzed. Utilization of GLP-1–based therapies increased by 371.7% over the study period, with a marked shift toward tirzepatide as the predominant agent. Most patients had obesity (83.6%), type 2 diabetes mellitus (89.0%), and multiple cardiometabolic comorbidities. Among patients with paired follow-up data, the mean percentage weight reduction was 3.91% (SD 7.32; 95% CI 2.70 to 5.12), and the mean treatment duration was 6.99 months (SD 3.69). Weight responses were heterogeneous, reflecting substantial interindividual variability in patient weight outcomes, with 35.6% achieving ≥ 5% weight loss and 25.1% experiencing weight gain. After adjustment for age group, sex, BMI category, treatment group, and HbA1c category, no independent association was observed between treatment group and achieving ≥ 5% weight loss.ConclusionIn this tertiary care setting, prescribing of GLP-1–based therapies expanded rapidly, accompanied by heterogeneous short-term changes in weight and blood pressure observed in routine clinical practice. These findings highlight the need for longer-term, multicentre studies to evaluate the real-world effectiveness, safety, and population-level impact of GLP-1 RA and dual GIP/GLP-1 RA.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02273-3.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and obesity are closely linked metabolic disorders with major clinical and economic impact. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), about 11.1% of adults worldwide were living with diabetes in 2024 (crude prevalence), and more than 90% of them had T2DM. The number of adults with diabetes is expected to increase to 853 million by 2050 [1]. Obesity-related insulin resistance is closely linked to dyslipidaemia, hypertension, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and early cardiovascular disease [2] These conditions impose a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems through increased healthcare utilization, long-term pharmacotherapy, and management of complications.

The burden of obesity and T2DM is particularly high in the Middle East and North Africa region, including the United Arab Emirates [3]. This increases economic burden and pressure on healthcare systems and creates a growing need for effective long-term treatments for metabolic disease. Alongside these trends, market analyses report continued growth in the use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA) in the region, reflecting their expanding use for both diabetes and obesity management [4].

Incretin hormones play an important role in regulating postprandial blood glucose. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is released from intestinal L-cells in response to food intake and increases glucose-dependent insulin secretion, reduces glucagon release, slows gastric emptying, and increases satiety [5]. In people with T2DM, the overall incretin effect is reduced, mainly due to reduced responsiveness to glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), while the action of GLP-1 remains largely preserved. This understanding led to the development of incretin-based therapies, particularly GLP-1 RA, as glucose-lowering treatments with additional metabolic effects [5].

Recent expert perspectives highlight that GLP-1–based therapies exert benefits beyond glycaemic control and weight reduction, supporting their broader role in cardiometabolic disease management [6]. GLP-1 receptors are expressed in multiple tissues, including the cardiovascular system, which may underlie these pleiotropic effects [7]. Large cardiovascular outcome trials have demonstrated cardiovascular safety and reduced major adverse cardiovascular events, as well as slower progression of kidney disease, in patients with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk [8]. Notably, these benefits appear consistent across body mass index categories, suggesting mechanisms beyond weight loss alone [8].

The treatment options within incretin-based therapy have changed rapidly in recent years. Long-acting GLP-1 RA, such as semaglutide, has shown greater glucose and weight reduction than daily formulations in both diabetes and obesity populations [9]. More recently, tirzepatide, the first dual GIP/GLP-1 RA, has been introduced. By targeting two incretin pathways, tirzepatide represents a further development in this drug class and has become an important option for patients who do not achieve metabolic targets with existing therapies [10]. At the same time, GLP-1–based therapies are increasingly used for obesity treatment in people without diabetes. Randomized trials and meta-analyses have consistently shown weight loss with GLP-1 RA in overweight and obese individuals without diabetes, supporting their use for long-term weight management [11]. As obesity increasingly begins in childhood and adolescence, emerging evidence on GLP-1 RA for weight management in younger populations reflects the expanding scope of incretin-based therapies beyond traditional adult indications [12].

Despite strong evidence from clinical trials, the effectiveness of GLP-1–based therapies in real-world practice may differ. Factors such as patient differences, treatment exposure over time, dose titration, access to care, and healthcare system constraints can influence outcomes. Large observational studies and electronic health record analyses have shown rapid growth in GLP-1 RA prescribing, along with wide variation in indications for drug use [13]. Socioeconomic factors, insurance coverage, and access to specialist care further affect real-world use and may limit equal access to these therapies [14]. In addition, supply constraints and prescribing restrictions associated with newer agents may delay treatment initiation and limit consistent availability in routine clinical practice [15].

Differences in cardiovascular risk between men and women, together with the under-representation of women in many clinical trials, highlight the importance of studying treatment patterns in diverse real-world populations [16].

Although the global use of GLP-1–based therapies has increased rapidly, data on real-world prescribing patterns and early clinical outcomes in the Middle East remain limited, particularly in healthcare settings managing populations with a high burden of cardiometabolic disease. Existing studies have largely focused on clinical outcomes or prescribing trends in isolation, with limited integration of pharmacy dispensing data and clinical outcomes within a single healthcare system. Moreover, data from the UAE and comparable regional settings remain scarce.

Understanding how incretin-based therapies are used in routine practice, including drug selection, dose escalation, and short-term changes in weight and blood pressure, is important for situating trial evidence in a real-world clinical context and supporting informed decision-making in regional healthcare settings. Accordingly, this descriptive observational study was conducted to characterize prescribing trends, patient characteristics, and short-term changes in weight and blood pressure among adults receiving GLP-1–based therapies at a tertiary academic hospital in Ajman, United Arab Emirates.

Primary objective

To describe prescribing trends of GLP-1–based therapies and baseline patient characteristics in a real-world clinical setting.

Secondary objectives

To assess observational short-term changes in weight and blood pressure, characterize patterns of weight response, and explore factors associated with clinically meaningful weight loss.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Thumbay University Hospital, Ajman, United Arab Emirates, a tertiary-level academic teaching hospital affiliated with Gulf Medical University. The hospital functions as a major referral centre for metabolic and endocrine disorders in the Northern Emirates and delivers care across multiple specialties, including endocrinology, internal medicine, cardiology, and bariatric services. The institution provides outpatient care with a high outpatient volume and a demographically diverse adult population, serving patients from Ajman and neighbouring emirates. Its role as a tertiary academic hospital ensures standardized prescribing protocols, centralized pharmacy dispensing, and uniform electronic health record documentation, enabling consistent capture of real-world prescribing patterns and early clinical outcomes. This setting allows evaluation of real-world practice within a stable healthcare system, with reduced variability related to differences in data capture and prescribing practices across institutions.

Electronic health records (EHRs) and pharmacy dispensing databases were reviewed to describe prescribing trends and patient characteristics, and to assess observational short-term changes in weight and blood pressure associated with GLP-1–based therapies between January 2022 and December 2024. The study was conducted using data derived primarily from outpatient records and linked pharmacy dispensing databases. Pharmacy records represent medications dispensed to patients rather than prescriptions written or administered. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed.

Study population

All adult patients, defined as individuals aged ≥ 18 years, prescribed a GLP-1 RA or the dual GIP/GLP-1 RA tirzepatide during the study period were eligible. In this study, the term “GLP-1–based therapies” is used as an umbrella term encompassing both GLP-1 RAs and the dual GIP/GLP-1 RA tirzepatide. Specifically, the GLP-1 RAs captured in this analysis were semaglutide (Ozempic® for diabetes and Wegovy® for obesity) and liraglutide (Saxenda®), while tirzepatide was represented by the brand Mounjaro®. All agents were prescribed for either glycaemic control in T2DM or weight management, in accordance with routine clinical practice. Where relevant, analyses were stratified by individual drug to account for pharmacological differences within this broader GLP-1–based therapy category.

Based on the prescription of GLP-1–based therapy for adults, 238 patient records were included for analysis. In parallel, 1744 pharmacy dispensing records were reviewed to assess prescription volume and temporal trends. The dispensing dataset comprised multiple dispensing events per patient and was analysed separately from patient-level clinical outcomes. Patients were included if they had T2DM, obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), or were prescribed GLP-1–based therapy for weight management as part of routine clinical care. Records with incomplete or missing key baseline or outcome data were excluded from respective analyses. Missing follow-up measurements primarily reflected patients with a single recorded visit or early treatment discontinuation, both of which are common in real-world datasets. Patients with only a single recorded visit or without follow-up measurements were excluded from outcome analyses but retained in descriptive analyses. The number of patients included in each analysis varied depending on data completeness. Patients with available paired weight measurements were included in outcome analyses, while regression and comparative analyses were conducted using complete-case datasets based on the availability of covariates and treatment group information. The number of records excluded due to missing baseline or follow-up data is reported in the Results section.

Patients who switched between GLP-1–based therapies during follow-up were analysed within the same cohort, with exposure defined based on the agent recorded at follow-up.

As this was a retrospective study, all eligible patients during the study period were included, without a priori sample size calculation or randomization.

Data collection and variables

A structured data extraction form was used to collect demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity), BMI, comorbidity burden, diabetes status, insurance coverage, and prescribed GLP-1 agent and dose. The data extraction form was internally validated through pilot testing and consistency checks prior to full data extraction. EHR and pharmacy datasets were linked at the patient level using unique patient identifiers.

Clinical outcomes included changes in body weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) from baseline to the most recent follow-up, where available. Baseline values were defined as the last recorded measurements prior to initiation of GLP-1–based therapy, and follow-up values as the most recent measurements after treatment initiation. The interval between these time points was considered treatment duration and represents short-term follow-up (median ~ 7 months).

Detailed longitudinal dose titration patterns were not analysed; however, the prescribed dose at available time points was captured.

Data were anonymized and coded before analysis.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were prescription trends of GLP-1–based therapies, standardized using monthly treatment units (MTUs; 28-day equivalents). MTUs were calculated as standardized 28-day treatment equivalents based on dispensed quantities and were not adjusted for dose strength or dosing frequency. Each dispensing unit (e.g., a single pen or an equivalent pack) was standardized to a 28-day treatment unit for analysis. Secondary outcomes included baseline patient characteristics and observed short-term changes in weight and blood pressure among patients with follow-up data.

Percentage weight change (%) was calculated. Clinically meaningful weight loss was defined as ≥ 5% reduction from baseline body weight, with ≥ 10% weight loss analysed as a secondary threshold. Weight change was categorized into predefined groups (weight gain, minimal change, and graded weight loss categories) to describe the distribution.

An exploratory subgroup analysis was performed to compare patients with weight gain (< 0%) with those achieving ≥ 10% weight loss.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 29. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized as mean with standard deviation (SD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), and as median with interquartile range (IQR) where distributions were skewed. Changes in weight, percentage weight, SBP, DBP, and treatment duration were analysed among patients with paired baseline and follow-up data. Normality was assessed using graphical and statistical methods. Independent-samples t-tests were used for normally distributed variables, while Mann–Whitney U tests were used for skewed variables. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the Pearson chi-square test. Fisher’s exact test was used where appropriate. Analyses comparing treatment groups were performed at the molecule level, grouping semaglutide formulations (Ozempic and Wegovy) together and comparing them with tirzepatide (Mounjaro). Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with achieving clinically meaningful weight loss (≥ 5%). Predictor variables were selected based on clinical relevance, and all variables were entered simultaneously (enter method). The primary model included age group, sex, BMI category, treatment group, and HbA1c category. A sensitivity analysis was performed, replacing HbA1c with diabetes status. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the exploratory subgroup analysis among patients with extreme weight responses, patients with intermediate responses were excluded. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between groups using Pearson’s chi-square test.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approvalwas obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Gulf Medical University (Ref. no. IRB-COM-STD-60-Jan-2025). Informed consent was waived due to the study’s retrospective design. All data were handled in accordance with institutional data protection and confidentiality policies. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

A total of 311, 838, and 1,467 MTUs of GLP-1-based therapies were prescribed in 2022, 2023, and 2024, respectively, representing an overall increase of 371.7% over the three-year period (Table 1). In 2022, Ozempic accounted for 79.4% of all GLP-1 prescriptions, while Mounjaro represented a smaller proportion (20.6%). By 2023, a marked shift in prescribing patterns was observed within this cohort, with Mounjaro becoming the predominant GLP-1 agent, accounting for 60.5% of MTUs, compared with 38.4% for Ozempic. This trend continued in 2024, with Mounjaro accounting for 65.6% of all GLP-1 MTUs, representing a 1,404.7% increase from 2022.

Ozempic prescriptions increased steadily over the study period, rising by 63.2% between 2022 and 2024, although its relative share of total GLP-1 prescriptions declined from 79.4% to 27.5%. Wegovy prescriptions were first observed in 2023 and increased in 2024, accounting for 6.9% of total MTUs, indicating an emerging uptake trend.

Table 2 shows a dose-wise comparison of Mounjaro, Ozempic, and Wegovy prescriptions standardized by MTUs. Mounjaro prescriptions demonstrated a clear shift from initiation doses (2.5–5.0 mg), which accounted for the majority of prescriptions in 2022, toward higher maintenance doses (≥ 7.5 mg) by 2024. Notably, doses ≥ 7.5 mg accounted for more than 47% of MTUs in 2024, reflecting a shift toward higher prescribed doses over time.

Ozempic prescribing showed a progressive shift toward the 1.0 mg dose, which accounted for two-thirds of MTUs by 2024, reflecting an increasing proportion of prescriptions at this dose over time. Wegovy prescriptions were first observed in 2023 and increased in 2024, with uptake across both initiation and maintenance doses, reflecting increasing adoption for obesity management.

Patient characteristics

Baseline characteristics of patients prescribed GLP-1–based therapies are summarized in Table 3. The cohort comprised 238 patients, predominantly middle-aged adults (79.5% aged 31–60 years). Men accounted for 55.5%, and most patients were of Arab ethnicity (62.9%). A high burden of obesity was observed, with 83.6% of patients classified as obese, while 89.0% had T2DM. Cardiometabolic comorbidities were common, including dyslipidaemia (69.3%) and hypertension (41.6%), and nearly one-third of patients had three or more comorbid conditions. Semaglutide and tirzepatide accounted for the majority of GLP-1–based therapies prescribed, with tirzepatide being the most frequently prescribed agent in this cohort (52.5%). These baseline characteristics reflect a population with a high burden of cardiometabolic disease, relevant for interpreting subsequent treatment outcomes.

Among patients with paired baseline and follow-up data (n = 143), the mean weight reduction was 4.29 kg (SD 7.96; 95% CI 2.97 to 5.61), corresponding to a mean percentage weight reduction of 3.91% (SD 7.32; 95% CI 2.70 to 5.12). Median weight reduction was 3.40 kg (IQR − 0.10 to 7.00), and median percentage weight reduction was 3.46% (IQR − 0.09 to 6.86), indicating a right-skewed distribution and substantial interindividual variability (Table 4).

Mean systolic blood pressure decreased by 3.59 mmHg (SD 14.05; 95% CI 1.26 to 5.91), while mean diastolic blood pressure decreased by 1.01 mmHg (SD 11.29; 95% CI − 0.86 to 2.87). Median reductions were 3.00 mmHg (IQR − 6.00 to 12.00) for SBP and 1.00 mmHg (IQR − 6.00 to 9.00) for DBP.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis included n = 143 patients, of whom 51 (35.7%) achieved ≥ 5% weight loss, providing an adequate number of events for the variables included in the model, ensuring stability (Table 6).

No significant association was observed between treatment group and achieving ≥ 5% weight loss. Tirzepatide use was not associated with higher odds of achieving ≥ 5% weight loss compared with semaglutide (adjusted OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.29–1.34, p = 0.225). Similarly, age group, sex, BMI category, and HbA1c were not significantly associated with weight loss outcomes.

In a sensitivity analysis that replaced HbA1c with diabetes status, the findings remained unchanged, with no significant associations observed.

In an exploratory subgroup analysis restricted to patients with extreme responses (n = 52), age was significantly associated with outcome group. Patients aged ≥ 46 years were more likely to experience weight gain, whereas younger patients were more likely to achieve ≥ 10% weight loss (63.9% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.006). No statistically significant differences were observed for sex, BMI category, diabetes status, HbA1c category, or treatment group (Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study conducted at a tertiary academic hospital in the UAE, we observed a rapid expansion in the prescribing of GLP–1–based therapies between 2022 and 2024, accompanied by heterogeneous short-term changes in body weight and blood pressure among patients with available follow-up data. These findings offer practical insight into how evidence from clinical trials and market entry of newer incretin-based agents are translated into routine clinical care, while also highlighting the variability of early clinical responses outside controlled trial environments. Overall, the results describe observed prescribing behaviour and early clinical changes rather than comparative effectiveness between GLP-1–based agents.

Expansion and shift in prescribing patterns

One prominent observation from this study was the 371.7% increase in total GLP-MTUs prescriptions over three years, with a pronounced shift from semaglutide toward tirzepatide as the predominant agent. Tirzepatide prescriptions increased by 1,404.7% between 2022 and 2024, accounting for nearly two-thirds of all GLP-1 MTUs by 2024, whereas semaglutide, despite a modest absolute increase, declined substantially in relative share. Similar patterns of rapid uptake of newer incretin-based therapies have been reported internationally, reflecting the expansion of therapeutic indications beyond glycaemic control to include obesity management and cardiometabolic risk reduction [17]. In a large population-based analysis of post-approval prescribing in the United States, tirzepatide dispensations rose sharply after market entry, increasing to 12.3% of all glucose-lowering medication prescriptions by late 2023 and outpacing other agents in both prevalent and incident use, highlighting how the clinical landscape of incretin therapy is rapidly shifting in routine practice [18]. Industry-level analyses have likewise identified GLP-1 RA and dual incretin agents as major growth drivers in endocrine and metabolic therapeutics, providing a broader context for the prescribing shifts observed in this tertiary care setting (IQVIA, 2023) [19]. These comparisons are provided for contextual interpretation and are not intended to imply equivalence in patient populations, prescribing environments, or treatment exposure between this single-centre cohort and larger international studies.

Published real-world data from the UAE and wider Arab region remain limited, but the few available regional reports support the relevance of our findings. A retrospective audit from the Dubai Diabetes Center described clinically meaningful reductions in HbA1c and weight over one year among patients treated with GLP-1 agonists in the UAE, while a more recent real-world study from an Arab population reported that tirzepatide was associated with significant reductions in HbA1c and body weight in routine practice. These regional data, although differing in study populations, exposure durations, and designs, support the broader observation that incretin-based therapies are increasingly integrated into routine metabolic care in the region [20, 21].

The introduction and subsequent uptake of Wegovy during the latter part of the study period further underscores a growing clinical emphasis on obesity-focused indications. Although Wegovy accounted for a smaller proportion of total MTUs than semaglutide and tirzepatide, its rapid increase after introduction is consistent with the evolving role of GLP–1–based therapies in weight management rather than in diabetes alone. The presence of Wegovy dispensing in 2023 indicates that obesity-focused semaglutide was already in use in this setting during the study period. Thus, the dataset itself provides direct evidence of local availability and utilization, independent of external launch timelines.

Dose escalation and treatment intensification

Dose-wise analyses showed a progressive shift toward higher prescribed doses over time for both semaglutide and tirzepatide. For tirzepatide, initiation doses predominated in 2022, whereas by 2024 higher maintenance doses (≥ 7.5 mg) accounted for more than 47% of MTUs, reflecting a shift toward higher prescribed dose categories over time. A similar pattern was observed with semaglutide, with the 1.0 mg dose accounting for approximately two-thirds of prescriptions by 2024. These real-world patterns are consistent with stepwise titration strategies commonly employed in clinical practice to manage tolerability during dose escalation. Randomized controlled trials have reported dose-dependent metabolic effects of incretin-based therapies under standardized conditions, including incremental reductions in weight and blood pressure at higher doses [22]. However, the controlled environments, strict eligibility criteria, and prolonged follow-up in clinical trials differ substantially from real-world settings, underscoring the importance of interpreting dose-escalation patterns alongside pragmatic outcome data.

Patient profile and clinical complexity

The population receiving GLP–1–based therapies in this cohort was characterized by a high burden of obesity (83.6%), type 2 diabetes (89.0%), and cardiometabolic comorbidities with dyslipidaemia (69.3%) and hypertension (41.6%) being highly prevalent. This profile is consistent with reports indicating that GLP-1 receptor agonists are more commonly prescribed among individuals with elevated metabolic and cardiovascular risk [23]. The predominance of middle-aged adults further reflects the demographic distribution of obesity and diabetes burden in the region, where incretin-based therapies are frequently used in routine clinical practice [3]. A small proportion of patients received GLP–1–based therapy in the absence of formal diabetes or obesity diagnoses, suggesting use for weight management outside formal diagnostic categories. While numbers were limited, this observation mirrors evolving real-world practice patterns reported in other settings [18].

Heterogeneous weight-loss outcomes

Among patients with available follow-up data (n = 143), approximately one-third achieved clinically meaningful weight loss (≥ 5%), whereas one-quarter experienced weight gain, and nearly 40% had minimal or no meaningful weight loss. When assessed as continuous outcomes, the mean percentage weight reduction was modest, and the interquartile range extended from slight weight gain to clinically meaningful weight loss, highlighting substantial interindividual. No statistically significant differences were observed between semaglutide and tirzepatide in short-term weight outcomes, consistent with comparative analyses.

The observation that a substantial proportion of patients experienced weight gain despite therapy is clinically important and aligns with real-world variability seen outside trial settings. In routine practice, early weight gain or limited response may reflect multiple factors, including shorter treatment exposure, incomplete dose escalation, reduced adherence, early discontinuation, and individual biological responsiveness. In our cohort, treatment duration among patients with paired follow-up data was approximately seven months, which remains substantially shorter than the duration over which maximal weight-loss effects are typically observed in pivotal trials.

An exploratory analysis of extreme responders suggested a potential influence of age on short-term weight response. This observation should be interpreted cautiously, given the small sample size and exploratory design; it is consistent with the possibility that early response to incretin-based therapies may vary by age in routine clinical practice. Evidence from controlled trials helps contextualize this variability. A large model-based meta-analysis of 55 placebo-controlled trials reported that age modifies weight-loss responses observed in clinical trials, with attenuated responses in older individuals, while baseline BMI and sex were inconsistent predictors [24]. Importantly, maximal weight-loss effects in pivotal trials were generally observed after approximately 52 weeks of therapy, a duration that exceeds the follow-up available for many patients in routine practice. This temporal aspect likely contributes to the modest and heterogeneous short-term outcomes observed in the present study.

Our subgroup analysis comparing patients with weight gain with those achieving ≥ 10% weight loss supports this interpretation. Age was the only factor significantly associated with extreme outcomes, with older patients more likely to experience weight gain. No significant differences were observed with respect to treatment group, diabetes status, BMI, or glycaemic control. These findings suggest that the determinants of early response in routine practice are likely multifactorial and not fully explained by baseline clinical variables alone.

Supporting evidence and conflicting evidence should both be acknowledged. Randomized trials have consistently shown greater weight reduction with tirzepatide than with semaglutide under controlled conditions [19]. By contrast, real-world studies from Arab and non-Arab settings have shown clinically meaningful weight loss with these therapies alongside substantial heterogeneity in response, likely reflecting differences in adherence, titration, prior therapy, follow-up duration, and patient selection [21, 25].

These observations describe short-term changes in routine clinical practice and should not be interpreted as comparative effectiveness between GLP-1–based agents.

Blood-pressure responses

Blood-pressure changes showed a heterogeneous pattern. Reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure were observed in roughly one-third of patients with paired measurements, whereas approximately half showed minimal change, and a smaller subset experienced increases. These findings are broadly consistent with randomized evidence reporting modest average reductions in blood pressure with GLP-1 RA, with considerable interindividual variability. A meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials reported mean systolic and diastolic reductions of approximately 3.4 mmHg and 1.0 mmHg, respectively, without strong associations with baseline demographic or metabolic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, HbA1c, weight, and BMI) [26]. Mechanistic reviews suggest that such effects may be mediated by natriuresis, endothelial function, and sympathetic modulation, but typically result in modest clinical reductions, particularly in real-world settings [27].

The presence of patients with elevated blood pressure despite treatment likely reflects pragmatic factors such as short follow-up, intercurrent changes in antihypertensive therapy, variable adherence, and the multifactorial nature of blood pressure regulation. As a change from baseline was not modelled using time-updated covariates, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than mechanistic.

Sex, age, and access-related considerations

In this cohort, neither sex nor age was independently associated with achieving short-term weight loss. Trial-level evidence suggests that sex-based differences in GLP-1–associated weight loss are modest, with females showing approximately 1 kg greater weight loss, and becoming more apparent with greater overall weight reduction [28]. Narrative reviews report inconsistent sex differences in glycaemic and blood pressure responses [29]. Real-world factors related to treatment exposure and healthcare access may further influence observed outcomes. Prior population-based studies have documented sex-related differences in cost-related medication nonadherence [30] and persistent socioeconomic disparities in GLP-1 RA use despite insurance coverage [31], which may contribute to variability in real-world outcomes.

Implications and interpretation

Overall, this study shows that utilization of GLP–1–based therapy expanded rapidly in this tertiary care setting, while short-term clinical changes observed in routine practice were modest and heterogeneous. Observed short-term outcomes in this real-world setting were more modest than those reported in controlled clinical trials, consistent with findings from large observational studies reporting small absolute differences between agents [32]. These observations underscore the importance of cautious interpretation of short-term outcomes and highlight the need for longer follow-up, standardized outcome assessment, and additional data on treatment exposure and patient-level behavioural factors to better contextualize these findings. At a health-system level, this study provides health-system-level insight into how GLP-1–based therapies are adopted, escalated, and utilized in routine tertiary care, with implications for service planning and resource allocation. These findings support the need for longer-term, multicentre real-world studies to contextualize early observations. Clinically, these findings may help set realistic expectations regarding early changes in weight and blood pressure with GLP-1–based therapy.

Strengths

This study has several important strengths. First, it provides real-world evidence on prescribing patterns and early clinical outcomes of GLP-1–based therapies from a tertiary academic hospital in the UAE, where published data remain limited. The combined use of electronic health records and pharmacy dispensing data allowed evaluation of both patient-level characteristics and population-level prescription trends, providing a comprehensive view of routine clinical practice.

Second, the three-year study period enabled evaluation of temporal changes in prescribing behaviour, including the rapid uptake of tirzepatide and the emergence of Wegovy for obesity management. Standardization using MTUs facilitated comparisons across drugs, doses, and time.

Third, the inclusion of a clinically complex population with a high burden of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiometabolic comorbidities enhances the clinical relevance of the findings by reflecting patients commonly treated in tertiary care rather than highly selected trial populations.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this was a retrospective observational study conducted at a single tertiary academic centre, limiting generalizability to other healthcare settings and precluding causal inference. Although analyses were adjusted for key demographic and metabolic factors, residual confounding is likely. As treatment was not randomly assigned, unmeasured factors such as medication adherence, lifestyle changes, dose adjustments, and clinician prescribing preferences may have influenced the observed outcomes.

Second, follow-up weight and blood pressure measurements were available for only a subset of patients. This may have reduced the precision of estimates and introduced selection bias, as patients with follow-up data may differ systematically from those without.

Third, the analysis focused on short-term outcomes and did not permit assessment of longer-term weight trajectories, durability of blood pressure changes, glycaemic control, or cardiovascular outcomes.

The analysis did not include change-from-baseline modelling adjusted for baseline values, which may influence the interpretation of treatment effects. Potential indication and channelling bias may also be present, given that treatment allocation may have been influenced by clinical characteristics or prescribing preferences. Stratified analyses by treatment indication (diabetes vs. obesity) were not performed, and prescribing appropriateness or adherence to clinical guidelines was not assessed.

Although the single-centre design limits generalizability, it enabled consistent prescribing practices, centralized pharmacy data, and standardized documentation, strengthening internal consistency. The exploratory regression analysis should be interpreted cautiously, as it was not designed to fully explain the multifactorial determinants of short-term weight change in routine clinical practice. The subgroup analysis of extreme responders was also exploratory and based on a relatively small sample size.

Recommendations and future directions

Future studies should use prospective or longitudinal designs with regular follow-up to better understand how weight and blood pressure change over time with GLP-1–based therapies in routine clinical practice. Collection of data on treatment duration, dose escalation, medication exposure, and accompanying lifestyle interventions would help clarify factors influencing variability in observed outcomes.

Multicentre studies across different healthcare settings in the UAE and the wider region are also needed to contextualize findings and assess how health-system factors and access to care influence treatment use and outcomes. Clinically, the variability in early response underscores the importance of individualized patient counselling, realistic expectation setting, and regular follow-up when initiating GLP-1–based therapy. These observations may inform the design of future regional studies evaluating longer-term outcomes across diverse healthcare settings.

Future research should examine patient perceptions and attitudes toward GLP-1–based therapies, as treatment beliefs, concerns about adverse effects, cost, and evolving social perceptions may influence treatment acceptance, adherence, and real-world utilization in the region [33].

Conclusion

In this real-world study, GLP-1–based therapies were associated with modest short-term reductions in weight and blood pressure, with substantial variability in response. No significant differences were observed between semaglutide and tirzepatide in early outcomes, and no strong baseline predictors of clinically meaningful weight loss were identified. These findings highlight the heterogeneous nature of treatment response in routine clinical practice and underscore the importance of individualized patient management and longer-term follow-up.
