Inhibition of Galectin-3 by ADSC-derived exosomes after cavernous nerve injury ameliorates erectile dysfunction by modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory profibrotic cascades
Authors: Ruiyu Li, Guanbo Wang, Chen Feng, Tongxiang Diao, Shuai Liu, Qiang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCavernous nerve injury (CNI) is a prominent etiological factor in the development of erectile dysfunction (ED). Nevertheless, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of CNI-induced ED (CNI-ED) are not fully elucidated. Galectin-3 (Gal-3), an indicator of inflammation and fibrosis, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of arteriogenic ED. In this study, we investigate the role of Gal-3 in CNI-ED and explore the potential mechanisms by which adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (ADSC-Exo) ameliorate CNI-ED.MethodsWe established a bilateral CNI (BCNI) rat model and evaluated erectile function using intracavernous pressure (ICP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and infrared ray thermography (IRT). The expression level of Gal-3 was measured in the major pelvic ganglia (MPG), penile corpus cavernosum, and cultured cells, with its expression modulated by lentiviral vectors. A combination of experimental approaches, including western blot, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometry analysis, were employed to investigate the role of Gal-3 in the progression of CNI-ED. Additionally, the potential molecular mechanisms by which ADSC-Exo ameliorates CNI-ED were explored.ResultsOur findings indicated that the expression of Gal-3 is significantly upregulated in the MPG and penile corpus cavernosum of BCNI rats. This upregulation was accompanied by oxidative stress and activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. Following lentiviral knockdown of Gal-3, erectile function in BCNI rats was improved. Moreover, ADSC-Exo transplantation inhibited Gal-3 expression and the related inflammatory profibrotic cascades.ConclusionsCollectively, this study demonstrates that upregulation of Gal-3 promotes the pathogenesis of CNI-ED by triggering oxidative stress and inflammatory profibrotic cascades and highlights the therapeutic potential of ADSC-Exo in restoring erectile function via Gal-3 inhibition.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04749-w.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) is a common clinical issue for which satisfactory treatment options remain elusive [1]. Cavernous nerve injury (CNI), a specific subset of PNI, frequently occurs following radical pelvic surgeries and uniquely affects erectile function [2, 3]. According to a global multicenter study, CNI-induced erectile dysfunction (CNI-ED) accounts for over 14% of all erectile dysfunction (ED) cases [4]. Prolonged denervation leads to morphological changes in penile corpus cavernosum, resulting in poor response to first-line medications [5, 6]. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CNI-ED are not yet fully understood, hampering the development of effective treatment strategies.

CNI leads to reduced penile blood perfusion and oxygen content. Chronic hypoxia in corpus cavernosum triggers oxidative stress, resulting in the atrophy and apoptosis of smooth muscle cells, and ultimately causing irreversible fibrosis [2, 7]. The interplay of inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis contributes to the progression of CNI-ED [8–10]. Multiple studies have documented the increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress markers, and fibrosis indicators after BCNI [8–10]. Matsui et al. discovered that there was a rise in pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in the major pelvic ganglion (MPG) after BCNI [10]. Mounting evidence indicates that inflammation, especially chronic inflammation, is strongly linked to fibrosis [11]. Inflammatory profibrotic cascades are intricate biological processes and significant contributors to ED following CNI. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate how to modulate inflammatory profibrotic cascades resulting from CNI.

Galectin-3 (Gal-3) plays a crucial role in fibrosis and inflammation in the heart, kidneys, and lungs [12–15]. Elevated Gal-3 levels have been shown to correlate with heart failure and its severity [13]. Inhibition of Gal-3 after myocardial infarction prevents progressive fibrosis by modulating the inflammatory profibrotic cascades. Furthermore, elevated Gal-3 levels were linked to heightened inflammation and fibrosis in heart lesion regions, whereas Gal-3 deficient mice showed resistance to heart inflammation [16]. The role of Gal-3 in ED has recently garnered attention. One study reported elevated Gal-3 levels in the corpus cavernosum of diabetic rats [17]. Moreover, our group found that inhibition of Gal-3 could significantly ameliorate arteriogenic ED in rats [18]. However, at present, there are no studies on the role of Gal-3 in CNI-ED.

In recent years, the use of adipose mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (ADSC-Exo) for the treatment of ED has garnered increasing attention [19–21]. ADSC-Exo has shown positive effects on nerve regeneration and neurodegenerative diseases [20, 22]. Compared to exosomes derived from other mesenchymal stem cell sources, such as those from bone marrow or umbilical cord, ADSC-Exo are more accessible and have higher yields [23]. In addition, their strong targeting capacity and immunomodulatory properties make ADSC-Exo well-suited for the treatment of complex tissue injuries [24]. We previously used thermosensitive hydrogels coupled with ADSC-Exo to significantly repair CNI [20]. Similarly, Wang et al. developed a novel hydrogel spray for restoring erectile function [25]. However, the intraoperative application of ADSC-Exo presents challenges such as limited repeatability and duration of effect, and the mechanisms by which ADSC-Exo promotes nerve repair have not been thoroughly explored. In our study, intracavernous injection of ADSC-Exo was selected to investigate its mechanism for ameliorating CNI-ED.

Based on the above findings, we hypothesize that Gal-3 may play a significant role in the development and progression of CNI-ED. To test this hypothesis, we constructed a BCNI rat model and observed changes in CN repair and erectile function after injecting lentivirus and ADSC-Exo into the penile corpus cavernosum. The primary aim of this study is to explore the role of Gal-3 in CNI-ED and the potential molecular mechanisms by which ADSC-Exo treatment ameliorates CNI-ED.

Methods

Animals and experimental design

A total of 60 male 12-week-old Sprague‒Dawley rats were used in this study. All rats were sourced from Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. The animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Shandong Provincial Hospital (NO.2022-117). The rats were kept at the Shandong Provincial Hospital animal house and randomly allocated to standard cages at a temperature of 22–24 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle and an unlimited supply of food and water. The random number table method was used to group the animals. Different experimenters were in charge of rat modeling experiments, experimental grouping, and evaluation of experimental results. The grouping of animal experiments is detailed in Fig. S1. This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Isolation and characterization of adipose mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) and ADSC-Exo

In accordance with earlier reports, ADSCs were isolated and cultivated [26]. Once the cells reached about 90% confluence, they were passaged and used at passage three. ADSC-Exo were isolated and characterized as previously reported [20]. ADSCs were cultured to approximately 80% confluence and incubated in serum-free medium for 24 h. The cell supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 50 min, and the resulting supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 90 min to isolate exosomes. The pellets were resuspended in PBS, centrifuged again, and stored at − 80 °C. The dimensions of purified exosomes were measured by the NanoSight LM10 nanoparticle tracking device (Malvern Instruments), and protein concentrations were determined using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kits. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging was performed with a Libra 120 microscope to examine exosome morphology, and exosomal markers CD63, CD9, and TSG101 were detected by western blotting.

Rat model of BCNI and erectile function evaluation

The BCNI rat model was constructed as mentioned before [27]. SD rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (35 mg/kg). In the sham group, the prostate was exposed through a midline abdominal incision without further surgical intervention. Animals were excluded from this study if they died before the conclusion of the experiment. However, no samples were excluded during the data analysis. Erectile function and histological changes in the penile corpus cavernosum and MPG were evaluated 30 days postoperatively. As previously described, intracavernous pressure (ICP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were measured [28]. Rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital, and a heparinized catheter was inserted into the carotid artery for MAP monitoring. A 23-gauge heparinized catheter was placed in the corpus cavernosum to measure ICP. Cavernous nerve stimulation was applied (5 V, 25 Hz, 60 s), and data were recorded using LabChart 8.0 (ADInstruments, Australia). After sample collection, the animals were euthanized using an overdose of pentobarbital sodium solution while their heart rate and respiratory rate were monitored.

Infrared ray thermography

Previous research detailed the use of a FLIR T540 thermal imager (FLIR Systems, Boston, MA) to track changes in penile temperature during rat erections [18]. Rectal temperature was recorded using a thermometer.

In vivo tracking of ADSC-Exo

According to the producer's instructions, ADSC-Exo were labelled with DiR dye (Invitrogen, D12731). Two days after surgery, all rats received a single intracavernous injection. For the BCNI + ADSC-Exo group, exosomes (100 μg of protein) were extracted with 100 μL PBS and delivered via intracavernous injection, while the control group was given the same volume of PBS. The intracavernous injection method was performed as previously described [21]. Briefly, 100 μL of solution was injected into the corpus cavernosum with a rubber tourniquet applied at the penile base, which was removed after 2 min. Following the injection, rats were observed using Perkin Elmer (Caliper) IVIS Spectrum In Vivo System.

Extraction and characterization of corpus cavernosum smooth muscle cells (CCSMCs) and schwann cells (SCs)

CCSMCs were extracted and cultured as previously reported and identified with calponin and α-SMA [29]. Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. provided the rat SCs (RSC96 cells). To verify their identity, immunofluorescence staining was performed using myelin basic protein (MBP) and S-100. Fig. S2 displays the categorization and handling of the cellular experiments.

Construction of lentivirus and cell transfection

Genechem Company (Shanghai, China) supplied lentiviral vectors for Gal-3 knockdown (Gal-3KD), Gal-3 overexpression (Gal-3OE), and their respective negative controls (Gal-3NC). Rno-miR-200c-5p mimics were designed by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China).

CCSMCs and SCs (1 × 105/well) were seeded in 6-well plates. These cells were then transfected with Gal-3KD and Gal-3OE (multiplicity of infection, MOI = 50) and corresponding Gal-3NC, respectively, according to the manufacturer's protocols. Cells were exposed to a lentivirus solution for 12 h, then collected and screened with 2 μg/ml puromycin to obtain stable cell lines with Gal-3 overexpression and knockdown.

Apoptosis induction and cell viability assay

The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, C0037, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was utilized to evaluate the viability of CCSMCs and SCs across various groups. Cells were cultured in 96-well plates. Following the specific treatments, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was introduced to the cell cultures at set time points. Following a 2-h incubation period, the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded with a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis was used to assess the levels of cell apoptosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) as previously described [29].

In vitro cultivation and neurite outgrowth measurement of CN/MPG

As previously described, the bilateral CN/MPG complexes were isolated from rats and cultured in vitro [30]. The CN/MPG were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6): I. Negative control (NC) group; II. hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) group: CN/MPG was treated with H2O2; III. H2O2 + ADSC-Exo group (ADSC-Exo group): CN/MPG was treated with H2O2 and ADSC-Exo (100 μg/mL). Following intervention, neurite outgrowth was observed using an inverted phase-contrast microscope.

Corpus cavernosum explant culture and sprouting assay

The rat corpus cavernosum tissues were dissected into small pieces of 1–2 mm3 and placed in a 6-well cell culture plate coated with Matrigel. The Matrigel was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min to facilitate the process of polymerization. Afterwards, 3 mL of serum-free Dulbecco modified Eagle medium (DMEM) was added to each well. The cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humid environment with 5% CO2. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM, with medium changes every 2 days. The grouping and treatment of the corpora cavernosa explants were consistent with CN/MPG. Following a period of 4 days, phase-contrast microscopy was employed to capture images, and the variations in the areas of sprouting cell growth were analyzed using Image J software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

To examine the expression of specific proteins, paraffin-embedded penis sections along with CCSMCs and SCs slides were used for immunofluorescence. The sections were left to incubate overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies including Gal-3 (1:1000; Abcam), MyD88 (1:500; Proteintech), NF-κB p65 (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology), TGF-β1 (1:500; Proteintech), α-SMA (1:500; Abcam), and nNOS (1:200; Abcam). Afterward, suitable secondary antibodies were chosen for additional incubation. Ultimately, the sections were examined using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), and the relative fluorescence intensity was semi-quantitatively analyzed with Image J software.

Masson trichrome staining

As previously mentioned, Masson trichrome staining was employed to measure the proportion of smooth muscle to collagen in the corpus cavernosum. The percentage of the stained area was quantified using Image J software.

Western blotting

For the following protein content analysis, rat tissues and cells were utilized and broken down using RIPA lysis buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) with added protease inhibitor mixtures (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Transfer the supernatant after high-speed centrifugation. Protein levels were determined using a BCA assay kit from Beyotime, China. Equal quantities of protein from various tissues or cell extracts were fractionated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently moved to PVDF membranes. Following the application of 5% fat-free milk to the membranes, they were incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies targeting Gal-3 (1:5000; Abcam), TLR4 (1:2000; ABclonal), MyD88 (1:1000; Proteintech), NF-κB p65 (1:3000; Cell Signaling Technology), TGF-β1 (1:2000; Proteintech), Desmin (1:100000; Abcam), α-SMA (1:10000; Abcam), Collagen I (1:1000; Abcam), HIF-1α (1:1000; Abcam), nNOS (1:1000; Abcam) and β-actin (1:2500; Proteintech). Following incubation with primary antibodies, suitable secondary antibodies added and left at room temperature for 1 h. The relative intensity of protein bands was assessed using Image J software with the corresponding β-actin as the control bands.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM analysis, fresh CN was cut into slices with the thickness no more than 1 mm. The sections were reserved in 0.1 M PBS with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for four hours. Subsequently, they were immersed in 0.1 M PBS with 1% osmium tetroxide for two hours at ambient temperature. The pieces were dried out and processed with propylene oxide. Subsequently, the slices were trimmed into very thin Sects. (60–80 nm) and treated with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Ultimately, the tissue's ultrastructural characteristics were documented using a transmission electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After sample collection of corpus cavernosum tissue, the levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and Collagen III were measured using ELISA kits (Jingmei Biotech, China). All operational steps were performed strictly according to the instructions.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The 3’UTR of Lgals3 containing wild-type or mutated miR-200c-5p binding sites was cloned into the pmirGLO reporter vector. Reporter constructs and miR-200c-5p mimics or negative control were co-transfected into HEK-293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA), and relative luciferase signal is represented by the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to that of Renilla.

Statistical analyses

To guarantee precise outcomes, each test was conducted thrice, and the data shown here were the averages derived from the three separate experiments. Prism 8 was used to analyze the data, which was displayed as means ± standard deviation. Comparisons among normally distributed data were conducted using either a t-test or one-way ANOVA, followed by the Tukey test. All comparisons were deemed statistically significant if the p-value was less than 0.05 (p < 0.05).

Results

Gal-3 expression was up-regulated in BCNI rats

Erectile function was evaluated 30 days after surgery. Compared with the sham group, BCNI rats showed a significant decrease in max ICP/MAP and total ICP (area under the curve) values (Fig. 1A and S3A). Similarly, infrared ray thermography (IRT) revealed that penile temperature during erection was noticeably lower in the BCNI group compared with the sham group (Fig. 1B and S3B). Masson trichrome staining indicated that the reduction in the smooth muscle to collagen ratio of the corpus cavernosum in the BCNI group (Fig. 1C and S3C). Significant differences in protein expression between the sham and BCNI groups were shown by western blot analysis. Specifically, the expression levels of Gal-3, TLR4, and NF-κB p65 in the corpus cavernosum and MPG of the BCNI group were significantly higher than those in sham group (Fig. 1D, E and S3D). Gal-3 expression was upregulated in CCSMCs and SCs after H2O2 intervention

CCSMCs were identified by immunofluorescence using Calponin and α-SMA markers (Fig. S3E). SCs were identified by immunofluorescence using MBP and S100 markers (Fig. S3E). Previous studies have confirmed that CNI leads to increased oxidative stress levels in the MPG and corpus cavernosum of rats [7, 10]. To simulate an oxidative stress environment, H2O2 was used to treat cells in vitro. Appropriate concentrations and durations of H2O2 treatment for CCSMCs and SCs were determined by treating the cells with different concentrations of H2O2. Based on the results of the CCK-8 assay and previous study [29], a final concentration of 200 μmol/L H2O2 for 12 h was selected for intervention (Fig. S3F). Compared with the negative control (NC) group, the fluorescence intensity of Gal-3 in CCSMCs and SCs significantly increased after H2O2 treatment (Fig. 1F, G and S3G, H). These findings indicate that the intervention of H2O2 upregulates the expression of Gal-3 at the protein level in CCSMCs and SCs.

Gal-3 inhibition enhances erectile function in BCNI rats.

BCNI consistently resulted in ED, with the BCNI group showing the lowest max ICP/MAP ratio (Fig. 2D). The erectile function in the BCNI + Gal-3NC group showed no significant difference from the BCNI group (Fig. 2D, E). Masson staining indicated that knockdown of Gal-3 significantly improved penile corpus cavernosum fibrosis in BCNI rats (Fig. 2C, F). Downregulation of Gal-3 after BCNI modulates the inflammatory profibrotic cascades

After 4 weeks of treatment, the mechanisms by which modulating Gal-3 expression improves erectile function in BCNI rats were explored using western blotting and immunofluorescence. Western blotting was used to analyze the expression levels of Gal-3, TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway proteins, pro-inflammatory factors, and phenotypic transformation-related proteins in corpus cavernosum (Fig. 3A). The expression levels of Gal-3, TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB p65, and TGF-β1 were significantly higher in the BCNI and BCNI + Gal-3NC groups compared with the NC group (P < 0.05). However, transfection with Gal-3KD notably downregulated Gal-3 expression and reversed the activation trend of the inflammatory pathway (Fig. 3A, B). Under CNI pathological conditions, CCSMCs undergo phenotypic transformation from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype [31]. Compared with the sham group, the BCNI group showed a significant decrease in contractile phenotype markers α-SMA and Desmin, and a significant increase in synthetic phenotype markers HIF-1α and Collagen I (Fig. 3A, B). Similarly, downregulation of Gal-3 notably inhibited the phenotypic transformation of CCSMCs. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of Gal-3, MyD88, NF-κB p65 showed similar trends to western blot (Fig. 3C, D). ELISA was performed to quantify the levels of selected cytokines and fibrosis markers in corpus cavernosum tissue. Compared with the Sham group, the IL-6, TNF-α, and Collagen III levels in the BCNI and BCNI + Gal-3NC groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 3E). As shown in Fig. 3E, IL-6, TNF-α, and Collagen III levels in the BCNI + Gal-3KD group were significantly lower than those in the BCNI group. ADSC-Exo enhances erectile function after BCNI

The effects of ADSC-Exo treatment on the recovery of erectile function are illustrated in Fig. 6. ADSC-Exo was characterized by particle size analysis, TEM, and western blotting (Fig. S4). Successful injection of ADSC-Exo into the penile corpus cavernosum was confirmed using the IVIS Lumina imaging system (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B–E, ICP and IRT assessments demonstrated a significant decline in erectile function in the BCNI group, whereas ADSC-Exo treatment notably improved erectile function. Masson staining indicated that the smooth muscle/collagen ratio was significantly increased in the BCNI + ADSC-Exo group compared with the BCNI group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6F, G). These findings are consistent with previous studies, suggesting that ADSC-Exo can improve erectile function in BCNI rats, presenting a promising therapeutic option for CNI-ED. Additionally, TEM employed to investigate the morphological alterations in myelinated fibers of the CN (Fig. 6H). Compared with the Sham group, the BCNI group showed evident reduction in myelin fragments and intact axons in CN sections (Fig. 6I, J). Nevertheless, after ADSC-Exo treatment, a few regenerated axons were seen in the CN, exhibiting reduced dimensions and thinner myelin sheaths. Quantitative analysis of CN axon density revealed a decrease after BCNI, with increased axon density after ADSC-Exo treatment (P < 0.05, Fig. 6I). Discussion

Prostate cancer (PCa) is currently the most commonly diagnosed cancer among men in 118 countries [32]. Radical prostatectomy (RP) is the primary treatment for early-stage localized PCa, with a reported postoperative ED incidence of 81.2% [33]. Despite the advancement of nerve-sparing techniques, postoperative ED remains unresolved. The pathogenesis of CNI-ED is closely linked to penile corporal hypoxia and fibrosis resulting from prolonged denervation.

Extended periods of reduced blood flow and hypoxia can initiate chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, leading to fibrosis of the penile corpus cavernosum [34]. The relationship between inflammation and fibrosis is complex and intertwined. Corpus cavernosum fibrosis typically begins with inflammation, which activates fibroblasts to secrete cytokines, stimulating the synthesis and accumulation of the extracellular matrix (ECM). TGF-β1 is considered a pivotal molecule in the development of corpus cavernosum fibrosis following CNI [35]. Furthermore, oxidative stress is also a critical pathway in the fibrosis of corpus cavernosum. The persistent inflammatory and profibrotic environment result in excessive production of ROS, which in turn, exacerbates the progression of fibrosis. Oxidative stress triggers the activation of TGF-β1, which further increases ROS production, establishing a positive feedback loop [36]. In summary, the inflammatory profibrotic cascades are intricate biological processes which play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of CNI-ED.

Galectin-3 (Gal-3) plays a crucial role in nerve regeneration and inflammation and serves as a major mediator of organ fibrosis [22, 37]. Inhibition of Gal-3 has been shown to modulate the inflammatory profibrotic cascades following myocardial infarction [38]. Nevertheless, the impact of Gal-3 on CNI-ED has not been thoroughly documented, and its underlying mechanism requires more investigation. In this study, we report for the first time that Gal-3 expression is significantly elevated in the penile corpus cavernosum and MPG of BCNI rats compared with the Sham group. Given the role of Gal-3 in oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis, we hypothesize that the upregulation of Gal-3 may contribute to CNI-ED by activating the inflammatory profibrotic cascades.

To validate this hypothesis, we constructed lentiviruses to regulate Gal-3 expression. Compared to Gal-3 inhibitors, genetic intervention enables sustained and stable modulation of expression, offering deeper insights into underlying mechanisms. Mechanistically, suppressing Gal-3 expression reduced H₂O₂-induced ROS production and reversed the activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway and TGF-β1. In addition, knockdown of Gal-3 inhibited the phenotypic transformation of CCSMCs. Intracavernous injection of Gal-3 knockdown lentivirus significantly ameliorated corpora cavernosa fibrosis and improved erectile function in BCNI rats. Consistent with our findings, Wang et al. demonstrated that using the Gal-3 inhibitor MCP improved erectile function in a rat model of arteriogenic ED [18]. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments revealed that upregulation of Gal-3 activates the inflammatory profibrotic cascades. Therefore, inhibition of Gal-3 provides a novel strategy for the treatment of CNI-ED.

Following nerve compression, although the nerve may appear structurally intact, its transmission is temporarily impaired, leading to structural changes in penile tissue. The main challenge in treating CNI-ED is how to promote nerve regeneration and prevent penile corpus cavernosum fibrosis following CNI [39]. ADSC-Exo shows therapeutic potential in nerve regeneration following PNI. Among various stem cell-derived exosomes (SC-Exo), ADSC-Exo are more accessible and have stable production [23]. Moreover, Pomatto et al. have reported that ADSC-Exo have superior efficacy in promoting wound healing compared to bone marrow-derived SC-Exo [40]. Our previous study demonstrated that applying ADSC-Exo combined with a thermosensitive hydrogel at the site of CNI promotes cavernous nerve repair and improves erectile function [20]. However, the therapeutic mechanisms of ADSC-Exo have not been explored in depth. In this study, our findings indicate that ADSC-Exo therapy has the potential to enhance erectile function in rats after BCNI by stimulating the regeneration of injured corpus cavernosum tissue and CN/MPG. ADSC-Exo exihibit therapeutic effects primarily through their bioactive cargo, including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and proteins [41, 42]. Our findings indicate that the suppression of Gal-3 by ADSC-Exo is likely mediated by their cargo of miR-200c-5p. As a subclass of non-coding RNAs, lncRNAs carried by ADSC-Exo have been shown to mitigate inflammatory responses and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine levels at injury sites, thereby promoting tissue repair [41]. Future studies will investigate whether these lncRNAs also contribute to the downregulation of Gal-3 expression. Inhibition of Gal-3 expression and inflammatory profibrotic cascades after BCNI is one of the mechanisms by which ADSC-Exo ameliorates CNI-ED, providing evidence for the clinical application of ADSC-Exo.

In summary, our study demonstrates that upregulation of Gal-3 promotes the pathogenesis of CNI-ED by triggering oxidative stress and inflammatory profibrotic cascades and highlights the therapeutic potential of ADSC-Exo in restoring erectile function via Gal-3 inhibition. Despite the expected outcomes being attained, there are still some limitations in our research. First, we did not use a Gal-3 inhibitor for further validation. In future studies, in vivo experiments involving a Gal-3 inhibitor will be conducted to strengthen the mechanistic credibility of our findings. Additionally, the role of macrophages in the inflammatory profibrotic cascades following CNI was not investigated. Previous studies have demonstrated predominant increases in M1 macrophages in the MPG following BCNI, and Gal-3 plays a pivotal role in regulating macrophage polarization [10, 43]. Based on these findings, we plan to further elucidate the function of macrophage polarization in CNI-ED and ADSC-Exo therapy through immunological analysis and detection of macrophage markers. Third, the long-term efficacy and dosing strategies of ADSC-Exo also warrant further exploration. Ultimately, we intend to corroborate the results of this research using large animal models to facilitate the clinical translation of ADSC-Exo therapy.

Conclusion

To conclude, in the present study, we reported that Gal-3 plays a significant role in the development of CNI-ED. Moreover, the results also indicated that inhibition of Gal-3 after CNI ameliorates ED by modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory profibrotic cascades. More importantly, the mechanism by which ADSC-Exo ameliorates CNI-ED is also related to the inhibition of Gal-3. Thus, we unraveled that the inhibition of Gal-3 is a promising therapeutic strategy for CNI-ED.
