Connection between attention, executive function, episodic memory, and adaptive functioning in young adults with Down syndrome
Authors: Kayla Kristensen, Kumari Anshu, Emily K Schworer, Audra Sterling, Benjamin Handen, Ruth Y Litovsky, Sigan L Hartley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:This study examined associations between attention, working memory, inhibition, episodic memory, and adaptive functioning in young adults with Down syndrome (DS).Methods:Forty-seven adults with DS (19–30 years) completed measures of attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory within two studies. Study partners completed the Vineland-3, an informant-report of adaptive functioning. Hierarchical regressions were used to examine associations between attention, working memory, inhibition, episodic memory, and Vineland-3 scores.Results:Participants with DS with an IQ in the mildly and moderately below average range had comparable levels of daily living and socialization skills but differed in communication. All adaptive functioning scores were lower for those with an IQ in the severely below average range. Working memory and episodic memory were positively associated with all Vineland-3 scores except socialization. Inhibition was positively associated with communication and overall adaptive skills. Attention was positively associated with overall adaptive skills.Conclusions:Attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory are associated with the adaptive functioning of adults with DS. Important connections exist between memory and communication and daily living skills, as well as inhibition and communication skills. Future research should explore environmental adaptations for supports in these areas (e.g. assistive technology).Keywords: Adaptive function, Down syndrome, cognition, executive function, everyday living skills, attention, memory, inhibition

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) is among the most common chromosomal conditions in the United States and involves a full or partial third copy of chromosome 21 (Mai et al. 2019). About 5,000 infants with DS are born each year in the United States (de Graaf, Buckley, and Skotko 2015, 2016; de Graaf et al. 2017), and the number of individuals with DS in the United States increased from 49,923 in 1950 to 220,186 in 2018 (de Graaf, Buckley, and Skotko 2015, 2016; de Graaf et al. 2017). Life expectancy has also increased from 26 years in 1950 to close to 60 years today for individuals with DS, largely due to improvements in early life medical care (Bittles et al. 2007; de Graaf, Buckley, and Skotko 2015, 2016; de Graaf et al. 2017). Given the greater number of individuals with DS living into adulthood, there is a critical need to understand how to support and enhance adaptive functioning skills during this life stage to maximize participation in everyday life activities. Adaptive functioning refers to the conceptual, practical, and social skills that individuals use in daily life (Schalock et al. 2000). Optimizing adaptive behavior has been identified as a key aspect of quality of life for adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD) (Balboni, Mumbardó-Adam, and Coscarelli 2020). The goal of the current study was to understand how cognitive abilities – specifically attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory – foster or hinder adaptive functioning outcomes for young adults with DS.

Adaptive functioning is commonly assessed in the skill domains of communication (e.g. ability to understand and use verbal and non-verbal communication in everyday life), socialization (e.g. ability to respond and relate to others in everyday life), and daily living (e.g. ability to complete everyday life tasks related to dressing, meals, household chores, and community engagement) (Sparrow, Cicchetti, and Saulnier 2016; Tassé, Luckasson, and Schalock 2016). On average, individuals with DS demonstrate relative strengths in the adaptive functioning domains of socialization and daily living skills and weaknesses in communication skills (Dressler et al. 2015; Ghezzo et al. 2014; Hamburg, Lowe, et al. 2019; Spiridigliozzi et al. 2019; Tomaszewski et al. 2018). However, there is within-group variability in adaptive functioning levels among individuals with DS (Schworer, Esbensen, Nguyen, et al. 2022). Prior studies indicate that adaptive functioning is related to, but also distinct from, overall intellectual level or intelligence quotient (IQ) (Dakopolos et al. 2024; Tassé, Luckasson, and Schalock 2016; Van Deusen et al. 2024). Adaptive functioning is contextual and dynamic. For example, adaptive skills can differ based on setting and communication partner, such as in the school or home (Sabat et al. 2020). Lee et al. (2025) describe associations between community participation of adults with DS and additional environmental contextual factors like parental and family involvement, availability of support services, amenities, financial resources, progressive disability policies, and supportive attitudes of family, friends, and colleagues. Associations have also been found between adaptive skills and specific cognitive abilities, including attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory (Edgin, Pennington, and Mervis 2010; Jacola et al. 2014; Onnivello et al. 2022; Will, Schworer, and Esbensen 2021).

To date, investigation of associations between specific cognitive abilities and adaptive functioning has largely come from studies of children and adolescents with DS. Attention refers to the cognitive ability to selectively concentrate and maintain attention on specific information. For adolescents with DS, challenges with attention have been linked to lower adaptive functioning (Jacola et al. 2014). Executive functioning abilities including working memory (i.e. ability to hold and manipulate information) and inhibition (i.e. the ability to override initial impulses to direct attention and behaviors) are critical for completing goal-directed behavior in everyday life. In studies of children with DS, important associations have been documented between executive functioning and adaptive skills. Working memory has been associated with communication adaptive skills (Onnivello et al. 2022; Sabat et al. 2020; Will, Schworer, and Esbensen 2021) and daily living adaptive skills (Van Deusen et al. 2024). Inhibition has been associated with socialization adaptive skills (Onnivello et al. 2022; Will, Schworer, and Esbensen 2021) and daily living adaptive skills (Van Deusen et al. 2024). Episodic memory, which involves the recollection of new information or experiences, has also been shown to be critical for everyday life functioning in adolescents with DS, specifically with an association between adaptive skills and episodic memory (Edgin, Pennington, and Mervis 2010).

To our knowledge, only a handful of published studies have examined the association between attention, executive functioning, episodic memory, and adaptive functioning in young adults with DS, and each of these studies also includes adolescents in their participant sample (Edgin, Pennington, and Mervis 2010; Hocking, Byer, and Lee 2024). In a sample of 32 adolescents and young adults with DS (aged 15–35 years), Hocking, Byer, and Lee (2024) found a positive association between parent-reported executive functioning skills and overall adaptive functioning. Edgin, Pennington, and Mervis (2010) examined the relation between attention, working memory, and episodic memory, and adaptive functioning in a sample of 27 adolescents and young adults with DS. Higher episodic memory performance was related to a higher level of overall adaptive functioning.

The goal of the current study was to examine the association between attention, working memory, inhibition, episodic memory, and adaptive functioning in a sample of 47 young adults with DS (aged 19–30 years). This relationship has been examined most frequently in younger cohorts in DS, with some investigation in samples that include both adolescents and young adults. Investigation is warranted in adulthood considering the growing population of adults with DS, discrepancies in available supports for children and adults with DS (e.g. availability of ongoing specialty center support as individuals age as reviewed in Santoro et al. 2021), and the importance of individualized supports to promote quality of life for adults with DS (e.g. Jevne, Kollstad, and Dolva 2021). Based on prior evidence of the importance of attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory for everyday living skills in children and adolescents with DS (Jacola et al. 2014; Onnivello et al. 2022; Sabat et al. 2020; Will, Schworer, and Esbensen 2021), we hypothesized young adults with DS with higher attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory performance would have a higher adaptive functioning level.

Methods

Participants

Participants included 47 adults with DS (19–30 years) who were enrolled in two separate studies that overlapped in measures of attention, working memory, inhibition, episodic memory, and adaptive functioning. The first study (cohort 1) was focused on examining hearing and cognition in young adults with DS (n = 18). The second study (cohort 2) was a multi-site study on the natural history of Alzheimer’s disease in adults with DS (n = 29). Participants from these studies were included in analyses based on the following criteria: (1) aged 18–30 years, (2) confirmed genetic diagnosis of DS (trisomy 21, mosaicism, or translocation); (3) regular use of 2-to 3-word spoken phrases, (4) had a mental age of at least 3 years based on standardized IQ testing; (4) English was their primary language; (5) no concerns about dementia symptoms.

Protocol

In both studies, the participant with DS and a study partner participated in a multiday study visit that involved the collection of data on attention, working memory, inhibition, episodic memory, and adaptive functioning, along with other study procedures. Participants in cohort 1 completed the study protocol described in Kristensen et al. (2022). Participants in cohort 2 completed the study protocol described in Hartley et al. (2017).

Measures

Attention

Participants completed the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fourth Edition – Digit Span Forward and Corsi Block Tapping Forward (WISC-IV; Wechsler 2003) tasks. These measures have been used in prior research with adults with DS (e.g. Hartley et al. 2017). Participants were asked to repeat a series of digits in the same order. For Corsi Block Tapping Forward, 9 blocks were arranged in an irregular pattern, and the examiner tapped the blocks in a sequence. Participants were instructed to tap the blocks in the same order. Higher scores on both Digit Span and Corsi Block Forward indicate higher attention skills. Digit span and block tapping forward tasks have acceptable feasibility, test-retest reliability, and low practice effects in DS (Schworer, Esbensen, Nguyen, et al. 2022).

Working memory

Working memory was assessed using the WISC-IV – Digit Span Backward and Corsi Block Tapping Backward (Wechsler 2003) tasks. These assessments have been included in prior studies with adults with DS (e.g. Anshu et al. 2024; Hartley et al. 2017; Kristensen et al. 2022). Similar digit span and block tapping tasks show negligible practice effects and some evidence for test-retest and convergent validity (Schworer, Esbensen, Nguyen, et al. 2022). For Digit Span Backward, participants were asked to repeat spoken numbers in reverse order (beginning at 2-digit span and increasing in length until the discontinue rule was reached). For Corsi Block Tapping Backward, 9 blocks were arranged in an irregular pattern. The examiner tapped the blocks in a sequence, and participants were instructed to tap the blocks in the reverse order. Higher scores on both Digit Span and Corsi Block Backward indicate higher working memory skills.

Inhibition

The Cat/Dog Stroop task (Ball et al. 2008) involves having participants first name pictures of cats and dogs as quickly as they can (naming trial) and then repeat the task but label cats as ‘dogs’ and dogs as ‘cats’ (switch trial). The primary variable used in analyses was the ratio of number of correct responses during the switch trial divided by the switch trial time. Higher scores indicate stronger inhibition abilities. Prior research on individuals with DS has shown that the Cat/Dog Stroop task has low floor effects and demonstrates convergent validity with other assessments of inhibition (Soltani, Schworer, Amin, et al. 2023).

Episodic memory

Episodic memory was assessed using the Rivermead Behavioural Memory Test, Third Edition (RBMT-3; Wilson et al. 2008). The RBMT-3 Picture Recognition task is appropriate for assessment of memory in adults with DS (Esbensen, Hoffman, et al. 2017). Participants were shown 15 pictures and asked to name them. After a 20-minute delay, the testing phase pictures were presented with foils, and participants were asked to point to the pictures seen previously. Higher scores indicate greater memory abilities in everyday situations.

The modified Cued Recall Test (mCRT) was also used to test episodic memory (Bazydlo et al. 2021; Devenny et al. 2002; Krinsky-McHale et al. 2022). Participants completed a learning phase, in which 12 black and white line drawings were presented with a semantic cue (e.g. ‘tool’ for the picture of a screwdriver). After the learning phase, participants were asked to recall all of the learned objects. The semantic cue was provided for any item not initially recalled. The total score for this measure includes items that were freely recalled and also items that were recalled when the cue was provided. Three trials were presented for a maximum total score of 36, with a higher score indicating greater memory abilities. The mCRT has adequate score range in adults with DS and is correlated with other measures of memory (Devenny et al. 2002; Krinsky-McHale et al. 2022).

Intellectual quotient (IQ) level

Participants in cohort 1 completed the Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test-Second Edition (KBIT-2; Kaufman and Kaufman 2004), and participants in cohort 2 completed the Stanford–Binet, Fifth Edition abbreviated battery IQ (SB-5; Roid 2003). Both measures have been used with adults with DS in prior research (e.g. Hamburg, Lowe, et al. 2019; Hamburg, Rosch, et al. 2019; Handen et al. 2025; Hocking, Byer, and Lee 2024; Kristensen et al. 2022; Shields et al. 2023). Estimates of intellectual quotient (IQ) level were coded: 1 = Mildly below average (IQ composite 50 and above), 2 = Moderately below average (35–49; if IQ composite at floor, verbal or nonverbal age equivalent ≥4), or 3 = Severely below average (IQ composite at floor and verbal and nonverbal age equivalent <4).

Adaptive functioning

A study partner completed the Vineland-3 (Sparrow, Cicchetti, and Saulnier 2016), a standardized measure of adaptive skills, which is appropriate for use with adults with DS (e.g. Dakopolos et al. 2024; Hamburg, Lowe, et al. 2019; Handen et al. 2025). For cohort 1, the parent/caregiver form was used, and for cohort 2, the interview form was used. Both versions provide standard scores of Communication, Daily Living Skills, Socialization, and a combined Adaptive Behavior Composite. Standard scores range from 20 to 140, with higher scores demonstrating higher independent functioning.

Sociodemographic data

Participant age (coded in years) and biological sex were reported by the participant’s study partner. Type of DS was based on karyotyping (trisomy 21, mosaicism, or translocation). See Table 1 for participant characteristics.

Data analysis plan

All statistical analyses were conducted in R using RStudio (version 4.4.2; R Core Team 2022). Descriptive statistics were computed to examine the distribution of study variables. Independent sample t-tests were performed to evaluate differences between studies in outcomes. As IQ is known to be related to adaptive functioning, a one-way ANOVA was performed to assess how adaptive functioning differs across IQ categories. Bivariate Spearman correlations were examined among all variables to determine covariates for regression models and to avoid multicollinearity between predictors. Age was included as a covariate in all models as chronological age was negatively associated with the daily living skills domain. Study cohort was also included as a covariate in all models because it was associated with the socialization domain and adaptive composite, and because the two cohorts differed in Vineland-3 forms. These correlations were adjusted for multiple comparisons and were no longer significant after corrections; however, cohort was retained in all regression models to account for systematic between-cohort differences. See Supplementary Table S1 for details.

To evaluate the robustness of the findings to potential between-cohort differences in Vineland-3 scoring related to the use of different forms across cohorts, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which Vineland-3 outcomes were standardized within cohort before rerunning the regression models. Plots from these sensitivity analyses are shown in Supplementary Figures 1–4.

To determine whether attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory explained unique variance in adaptive functioning above and beyond IQ level, we conducted hierarchical multiple regression. In Step 1, the base model included IQ level, age, and study; in Step 2, attention, working memory, inhibition, or episodic memory measures were added in separate models. Improvement in model fit was evaluated via model comparisons (ΔR2 and F-tests). To reduce the variance inflation factor and to limit the number of predictors in Step 2, we specified four model categories based on our key predictors: (1) Attention model: An attention composite score was created by averaging the z-scored WISC-IV Digit Span Forward and Corsi Block Tapping Forward scores. (2) Working memory model: A working memory composite score was created by averaging the z-scored WISC-IV Digit Span Backward and Corsi Block Tapping Backward scores. (3) Inhibition model: From the Cat/Dog Stroop test, inhibition was indexed as the ratio of number of correct responses to trial time on the incongruent trial. (4) Episodic Memory model: A memory composite score was computed by averaging the z-scored RBMT-3 Picture Recognition raw score and mCRT total score. Across all models, all predictors had variance inflation factors < 2.5, indicating no multicollinearity concerns.

IQ and adaptive functioning

As shown in Figure 1, IQ level was significantly related to all four Vineland-3 scores. One-way ANOVA revealed significant group differences for the Vineland-3 domains (communication: F(2,44) = 13.09, p < 0.001; daily living skills: F(2,44) = 14.34, p < 0.001; and socialization: F(2,44) = 10.73, p < 0.001) and adaptive composite (F(2,44) = 18.29, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons showed that adaptive skills were significantly lower in participants who had an IQ in the severely below average range as compared to in the mildly (communication: p < 0.001; daily living skills: p < 0.001; socialization: p < 0.001; adaptive composite: p < 0.001) and moderately (communication: p = 0.010; daily living skills: p < 0.001; socialization: p = 0.002; adaptive composite: p < 0.001) below average range across all Vineland-3 domains and the adaptive composite. There were also significant differences between the participants with IQs in the mildly below average range versus moderately below average range for the Vineland-3 communication domain (p = 0.013) but not in the daily living skills domain (p = 0.364), socialization domain (p = 0.196), or adaptive composite (p = 0.072).

Attention, working memory, inhibition, episodic memory, and adaptive functioning

The following section reports findings from the hierarchical regression models examining the contribution of attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory in predicting Vineland-3 scores, above and beyond the base model that included IQ group, age, and study.

Attention

Adding the attention composite into the regression model yielded improvements in explained variance beyond the base model for the Vineland-3 adaptive composite (ΔR2 = 0.048, F(1,37) = 4.39, p = 0.043). The attention composite did not explain additional variance beyond the base model for the Vineland-3 socialization or daily living skills domains (ps >0.12). Overall, a higher attention composite score was significantly related to a higher Vineland-3 adaptive composite score (Figure 2; β = 6.22, SE = 2.97, p = 0.043) after adjusting for covariates. The attention composite was not significantly associated with the Vineland-3 communication (p = .072), daily living skills (p = .148), or socialization (p = .125) domains.

Relationships between attention composite score and adaptive functioning measures. (A) Communication domain. (B) Daily Living Skills domain. (C) Socialization domain. (D) Adaptive composite score.

Working memory

The working memory composite explained additional variance beyond the base model for the Vineland-3 adaptive composite (ΔR2 = 0.062, F(1,37) = 5.82, p = 0.021), communication domain (ΔR2 = 0.086, F(1,37) = 6.30, p = 0.017), and daily living skills domain (ΔR2 = 0.075, F(1,37) = 5.86, p = 0.021). However, the working memory composite did not significantly explain new variance in the Vineland-3 socialization domain (ΔR2 = 0.018, F(1,37) = 1.33, p = 0.256). Overall, a higher working memory composite score was significantly positively associated with a higher Vineland-3 adaptive composite (Figure 3; β = 7.10, SE = 2.94, p = 0.021), communication domain (β = 9.51, SE = 3.79, p = 0.017), and daily living skills domain (β = 7.92, SE = 3.27, p = 0.020) scores, after adjusting for covariates. No significant association was found between the working memory composite and the Vineland-3 socialization domain (β = 5.22, SE = 4.53, p = 0.256).

Relationships between working memory composite score and adaptive functioning measures. (A) Communication domain. (B) Daily Living Skills domain. (C) Socialization domain. (D) Adaptive composite score.

Inhibition

The inhibition measure accounted for additional variance beyond the base model for the Vineland-3 adaptive composite (ΔR2 = 0.050, F(1,37) = 4.50, p = 0.041) and communication domain (ΔR2 = 0.113, F(1,37) = 8.75, p = 0.005), but not the Vineland-3 daily living skills or socialization domains (ΔR2 = 0.012, F(1,37) = 0.84, p = 0.364; DR2 = 0.019, F(1,37) = 1.40, p = 0.244). Overall, the inhibition score was significantly positively associated with the Vineland-3 adaptive composite (Figure 4; β = 12.8, SE = 6.02, p = 0.041) and communication domain (β = 21.9, SE = 7.42, p = 0.005), after adjusting for covariates. In contrast, the inhibition composite was not significantly related to the daily living skills (p = 0.364) or socialization domains (p = 0.244).

Relationships between inhibition score and adaptive functioning measures. (A) Communication domain. (B) Daily Living Skills domain. (C) Socialization domain. (D) Adaptive composite score.

Episodic memory

The episodic memory composite explained significant additional variance beyond the base model for the Vineland-3 adaptive composite (ΔR2 = 0.071, F(1,37) = 6.76, p = 0.013), communication (ΔR2 = 0.120, F(1,37) = 9.44, p = 0.004), and daily living skills domains (ΔR2 = 0.079, F(1,37) = 6.25, p = 0.017), but not for the socialization domain (ΔR2 = 0.005, F(1,37) = 0.398, p = 0.532). Overall, higher scores on the episodic memory composite were significantly positively related to a higher Vineland-3 adaptive composite (Figure 5; β = 8.33, SE = 3.20, p = 0.013), communication domain (β = 12.4, SE = 4.03, p = 0.004), and daily living skills domain (β = 8.95, SE = 3.58, p = 0.017) score, after adjusting for covariates. There was not a significant association between the episodic memory composite and the socialization domain score (β = 3.18, SE = 5.04, p = 0.532).

Relationships between episodic memory composite score and adaptive functioning measures. (A) Communication domain. (B) Daily Living Skills domain. (C) Socialization domain. (D) Adaptive composite score.

Combined attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory model

To assess the unique contributions of each cognitive composite score while accounting for shared variance, we included the attention, working memory, and inhibition composite scores and episodic memory composite score in a combined model adjusting for age, study, and IQ group. The combined model significantly improved explained variance (beyond the base model) for the Vineland-3 adaptive composite (ΔR2 = 0.116, F(4,34) = 2.88, p = 0.037), and communication domain (ΔR2 = 0.194, F(4,34) = 4.16, p = 0.008), and had a trend-level improvement in explained variance for the daily living domain (ΔR2 = 0.114, F(4,34) = 2.25, p = 0.084). The combined model did not explain additional variance beyond the base model for the socialization domain (ΔR2 = 0.046, F(4,34) = 0.792, p = 0.539). When predicting Vineland-3 communication domain scores, there was a trend-level positive association with inhibition (β = 14.0, SE = 7.67, p = 0.077) and episodic memory (β = 7.52, SE = 4.35, p = 0.093). Similar trends were seen for the Vineland-3 daily living skill domain (β = 6.88, SE = 4.05, p = 0.099). In the combined model, none of the attention, working memory, and inhibition composites nor the episodic memory composite was a significant predictor of the Vineland-3 socialization domain or adaptive composite (all ps > 0.17).

Sensitivity analyses using within-cohort standardized Vineland outcomes

Sensitivity analyses using within-cohort standardized Vineland outcomes yielded a broadly similar pattern of findings to the primary analyses (Supplementary Figures 1–4). Findings for working memory and episodic memory were unchanged. Findings for attention and inhibition were also broadly similar, although their associations with the adaptive composite were somewhat weaker.

Discussion

Efforts to build adaptive functioning in order to increase independence and participation in everyday life are among the top factors that drive high quality of life for young adults with DS and their family members (Jevne, Kollstad, and Dolva 2021; Santoro et al. 2022). The goal of the current study was to understand the extent to which attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory are related to differences in adaptive functioning among young adults with DS. This information has important implications for the design of adaptive functioning supports and services leading up to and throughout adulthood, as attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory are cognitive skills that can be improved through skill training and through the use of aids and supports (Jevne, Kollstad, and Dolva 2021; White et al. 2023). Increased understanding of profiles of attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory can also inform future design and study of contextual supports to increase adaptive skills, such as individualized assistive technology and augmentative and alternative communication strategies.

Our findings support prior evidence that IQ is related to adaptive functioning level, but that these are separate constructs that do not directly align (Tasse et al. 2016). Indeed, in our sample, young adults with DS who had IQs in the mildly or moderately below average range had higher adaptive functioning than individuals with IQs in the severely below average range. However, young adults with IQs in the mildly versus moderately below average range did not differ in their daily living skills, socialization skills, or in their overall Vineland-3 adaptive composite. Thus, intellectual level in and of itself does not determine the level of independence in everyday life for a young adult with DS. Instead, adaptive functioning is shaped by a variety of factors, including specific cognitive skills, such as attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory, as well as contextual factors (e.g. Holyfield et al. 2023; Sabat et al. 2020, Lee et al. 2025, Landuran et al. 2023, Babb et al. 2020) identified in prior research, which is beyond the scope of the current study. The current study’s findings have implications for supporting cognitive skill in individual interventions and policy level-efforts aimed to promote independence among young adults with DS and continues to highlight the need for further study of contextual factors impacting the adaptive skills of adults with DS.

In support of previous findings (Dakopolos et al. 2024; Onnivello et al. 2022; Sabat et al. 2020; Will, Schworer, and Esbensen 2021) for children and adolescents with IDD, attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory were related to adaptive functioning after adjusting for IQ level for young adults with DS. Higher performance on attention, working memory, and inhibition and in episodic memory were related to a higher overall level of adaptive functioning (i.e. Vineland-3 adaptive composite). When considering different aspects of adaptive behavior, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory were also related to higher communication skills. Working memory and episodic memory were also related to higher daily living skills. Thus, the cognitive ability to hold and manipulate information, tune out irrelevant information, and remember information appears critical for helping young adults with DS communicate with others and complete everyday tasks around the house and in the community. Interestingly, none of the attention, working memory, or inhibition domains nor the episodic memory domain was related to adaptive functioning in the area of socialization after adjusting for IQ level. Non-cognitive factors, such as theory of mind or emotion regulation skills, as well as environment and skills of communication partner may play a greater role in shaping the social functioning of adults with DS than attention, executive function, and episodic memory. Attention was also only related to the overall adaptive behavior composite, which may be attributed to the moderate correlation with adaptive functioning with no singular adaptive subdomain driving this association.

Overall, findings suggest that interventions aimed at improving attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory may be a meaningful pathway for enhancing the adaptive functioning of young adults with DS, especially in the areas of communication and daily living skills. Recently, there have been successes in tailored approaches to intervention with individuals with DS and specific working memory targets (Costa, Purser, and Passolunghi 2015; Lanfranchi et al. 2024). Additionally, assistive technology supports and augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) approaches, which pair auditory and visual presentation of language to support word learning and retrieval may have particular benefit for adults with DS and should continue to be explored as a pathway to increase independence in adulthood (e.g. Holyfield et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2025, Landuran et al. 2023, Babb et al. 2020).

The current study has a number of strengths and limitations. The study is among the few to describe the connection between attention, working memory, inhibition, episodic memory, and adaptive functioning in young adults with DS and used standardized measures commonly used in clinical practice and research settings. However, the study was limited by a relatively small sample size and age range. Findings reflect patterns seen in young adults with DS who regularly use multi-word phrases and have a mental age greater than 3 years but may not be generalizable to the full population of individuals with DS. Future work should examine the connection between cognitive domains and adaptive functioning in a broader age range, including younger developmental periods. A further limitation is that adaptive functioning was assessed using different Vineland-3 forms across cohorts. Although study cohort was included as a covariate in the primary analyses to account for systematic between-cohort differences, and sensitivity analyses using within-cohort standardized Vineland outcomes yielded a broadly similar pattern of results, the use of different Vineland-3 forms should still be considered when interpreting results. The study was also cross-sectional, meaning that time-ordered interpretations about the connection between attention, working memory, inhibition, memory, and adaptive functioning cannot be made. Future work should examine these relationships longitudinally to address the directionality of these connections for individuals with DS across the lifespan.

In conclusion, findings indicate that attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory have important connections to adaptive functioning for young adults with DS, and in particular, communication and daily living skills that are above and beyond IQ level. Findings add evidence to the literature advocating for the importance of screening individuals with DS for co-occurring conditions that impact attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory, such as obstructive sleep apnea and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (e.g. Chen, Spanò, and Edgin 2013; Esbensen, Hooper, et al. 2017; Joyce et al. 2020; Soltani, Schworer, and Esbensen 2023). There is a need for research on interventions that can build attention, working memory, inhibition, and episodic memory skills and/or compensate for these challenges (e.g. environmental and communication partner supports, assistive technology, AAC strategies, etc.) to promote optimal independence and participation in everyday life for young adults with DS.
