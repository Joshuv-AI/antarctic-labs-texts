Fit of three-unit anterior fixed dental prosthesis frameworks fabricated by different manufacturing techniques
Authors: Gürel Pekkan, Hilal Gülgezen Aydın, Süleyman Hakan Tuna, Canan Hekimoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis in vitro study aimed to evaluate different fabrication techniques for anterior cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr) three-unit fixed dental prostheses (FDPs) and their effects on the fit of maxillary central incisor and canine abutments.Materials and methodsFive fabrication techniques were evaluated as the experimental groups: manual wax carving and casting (Cast), milled wax and casting (CAW), 3D-printed polymers and casting (3DP), direct metal laser sintering (DMLS), and selective laser melting (SLM). Three-unit FDP frameworks were designed for the prepared maxillary central incisor and canine teeth, and Co-Cr specimens were fabricated (n = 10). Marginal (MDV), chamfer (CDV), axial (ADV), incisal (IDV), and overall discrepancy values (ODV) were evaluated using the silicone replica technique (SRT) and statistically analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)(α = 0.05).ResultsFabrication technique significantly affected measurements (p < 0.001), while tooth type did not. No differences in CDV, ADV, or IDV were found between canine and central incisor (P > 0.05). The 3DP technique showed higher MDV, CDV, and ODV than others (P < 0.05), and higher ADV than DMLS and SLM (P < 0.05). For IDV, 3DP had higher values in canine teeth compared to all techniques, and in central teeth compared to Cast, DMLS, and SLM (P < 0.05).ConclusionSLM showed a more favorable overall discrepancy than the lost-wax technique. Additionally, DMLS offers a comparable and viable alternative for fabricating anterior 3-unit FDP frameworks.

Introduction

Despite the increased utilization of all-ceramic restorations in recent decades, metal-ceramic fixed dental prostheses (FDPs) persist in their widespread application as replacement for lost natural teeth. Cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr) alloys are favored for the metal frameworks in FDPs due to their superior mechanical properties and cost-effectiveness compared to precious metals, and titanium alloys [1]. The predominant technique employed in the fabrication of metal alloy restorations is the conventional lost wax technique [2]. The casting of Co-Cr alloys presents considerable challenges owing to their elevated melting points, considerable hardness, and limited ductility [3]. Recent advancements have enabled the integration of conventional casting methods with computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies. Contemporary approaches employ three-dimensional (3D) -printed polymers or CAD/CAM wax blocks. Wax patterns are fabricated either subtractive manufacturing or 3D printing, followed by casting using conventional techniques [4]. Once the design is finalized, frameworks can be fabricated using Selective Laser Melting (SLM) or Selective Laser Sintering (SLS). These techniques utilize a high-energy carbon dioxide laser to fuse layers of Co-Cr metal powder. Direct Metal Laser Sintering (DMLS), a variant of SLS, incrementally constructs the framework, thereby enhancing density and mechanical strength [5].

The marginal and internal fit of a restoration is essential for its biomechanical strength, as optimal seating enhances fracture resistance [6, 7]. Inadequate marginal and internal fit may result in microleakage, which can promote secondary caries, cement dissolution, and periodontal inflammation in tooth-supported restorations [8]. The fit of dental restorations is influenced by the type of tooth, even when identical fabrication techniques are employed [9, 10]. Despite the widespread use of CAD/CAM and additive manufacturing techniques for fixed dental prostheses, comparative data focusing specifically on anterior tooth-supported cobalt–chromium three-unit frameworks remain limited. Most existing studies have primarily investigated posterior regions or implant-supported restorations, where biomechanical and anatomical conditions differ substantially. Therefore, the effect of contemporary fabrication techniques on the marginal and internal fit of anterior tooth-supported three-unit FDPs has not been sufficiently clarified. This study aimed to address this gap by systematically comparing conventional and digital manufacturing techniques under standardized in vitro conditions. The null hypothesis posited that the technique of constructing a Co-Cr metal framework would not significantly influence the fit of a three-unit anterior FDP. The second null hypothesis asserts that there will be no differences among tooth types regarding overall, marginal, chamfer, axial, and incisal fit within the frameworks, irrespective of the fabrication technique used.

Materials and methods

The specimen size was calculated using power analysis software (G*Power 3.1.9.7) with an alpha level of 0.05, an effect size ranging from 0.50 to 0.79, and a statistical power of 80%. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 10 specimens per group (n = 10, effect size = 0.52) was required. Accordingly, a total of 50 Co-Cr 3-unit FDP metal frameworks were fabricated utilizing five different manufacturing techniques (Fig. 1). Five groups were evaluated (n = 10 per group): Cast, CAW, 3DP, DMLS, and SLM.

Specimen design and master model preparation

The maxillary master model was made utilizing a sintering device (Formiga P 110, EOS GmbH, Krailling, Germany) with fine polyamide powder (Polyamide 12, PA 2105; EOS GmbH) after scanning a typodont. The master model was replicated using vinyl polysiloxane impressions to fabricate stone casts for the conventional casting group, whereas the remaining groups were digitized using a laboratory scanner and fabricated via CAD/CAM workflows. Three-unit FDPs were fabricated for prepared dies utilizing segmented master models. The left central incisor and canine were prepared for the anterior FDP. The abutment teeth were prepared by reducing the occlusal and incisal surfaces by 1.5 to 2 mm and the axial surfaces by 1 to 1.5 mm and creating approximately a 6-degree angle. A dental surveyor (Dental Surveyor; Unident Instrument Pvt. Ltd., India) was used to assess the preparations and prevent undercuts. After final adjustments, impressions were taken from the master model based on the assigned procedure for each group. The specimens were fabricated using similar parameters and methods across different techniques, consistent with those utilized in a prior study [10].

Fabrication techniques and group allocation

In all specimens except the conventional casting group, a laboratory scanner (DentalWings 7 Series; DentalWings, Montreal, Canada) digitized the master model, followed by framework design (DWOS crown and bridge CAD software, DentalWings). The coping thickness was set at 0.5 mm, with connection areas established at 6 mm². The cement gap was 70 μm, starting 0.5 mm above the margin edge in all specimens. For CAD/CAM-based groups, the same master model was digitized using the same laboratory scanner and design parameters were kept constant across groups (coping thickness, connector area, and cement space settings). Nevertheless, technique-specific manufacturing steps and post-processing procedures may introduce variability that cannot be fully standardized. In the conventional casting group (Cast), the cement space was obtained by applying four layers of 15 μm thick (Durolangold; DFS Diamon) and one layer of 10 μm thick die spacer (Durolanblue; DFS Diamon) to the abutment teeth. To standardize the pontic and connector components of the framework, these parts were milled from wax blocks (Alliance Wax Disk; Almadent, İzmir, Turkey) according to standardized designs and subsequently attached to wax crowns using an electric waxing unit (Hot 2 Touch; Dentalfarm, Torino, Italy). The cement gap was standardized digitally for all CAD/CAM-based groups and by die spacer application for the conventional casting group. Although these approaches aimed to achieve comparable cement thickness, minor variations cannot be completely excluded.

Specimens in the DMLS group were fabricated using the manufacturer-recommended powder (SP2: EOS SP2; Turku, Finland) with a layer thickness of 20 μm on a DMLS machine (EOS M 270; EOS GmbH). Similarly, specimens in the SLM group were fabricated using the manufacturer-recommended powder (Remanium® star; Dentaurum, Ispringen, Germany) with a layer thickness of 20 μm on an SLM machine (Concept Laser Mlab; Concept Laser GmbH, Lichtenfels, Germany). CAD/CAM 3D-printed polymer (3DP) specimens were fabricated from a translucent polymeric material (Fusia RF080; DWS Srl, Thiene, Italy) using a laser stereolithography machine (Digital Wax 028D; DWS Srl) with a layer thickness of 40 μm. Subsequently, these specimens were sprued, invested, and cast from Co-Cr alloy (Kera C; Eisenbacher Dentalwaren ED GmbH) following conventional casting procedures. Some specimens exhibited significant discrepancies attributable to the photosensitivity and shrinkage of the polymeric material; consequently, new specimens were fabricated to replace those missing within the groups.

Impressions of the master model were obtained using vinyl polysiloxane (VPS) (Hera Moldastone CN; Heraeus Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) to fabricate plaster models for the conventional casting specimens. Removable die models were fabricated utilizing the Pindex system (ED-Laser; Dentalfarm Srl, Turin, Italy). Wax patterns were prepared by melting modeling wax (GEO Dip gelb; Renfert GmbH, Hilzingen, Germany) in a melting unit (Hotty; Renfert GmbH). Following preheating and wax elimination at 950 °C, Co-Cr ingots (Kera C; Eisenbacher Dentalwaren ED GmbH, Main, Germany) were melted in a ceramic crucible (Bego Fornax; Bego, Bremen, Germany) and cast using an induction casting machine (INF 2010; Mikrotek Dental, Ankara, Turkey) at approximately 1400 °C. The specimens were examined under a microscope at 5× magnification, and those exhibiting casting defects were excluded. All surfaces were sandblasted with 110 μm alumina particles (Korax; Bego, Bremen, Germany) at a 90° angle for the outer surfaces and inner occlusal surfaces, and at a 45° angle for the remaining inner surfaces, from a distance of 2 cm under a pressure of 4 bar for one minute.

Silicone replica technique and discrepancy measurements

The marginal and internal fit of the frameworks was assessed utilizing the silicone replica technique with VPS materials of varying colors and viscosities. Initially, a low-viscosity VPS material (Fit Checker Advanced White; GC Corp., Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the copings, which were then seated onto the dies under finger pressure. Subsequently, a vertical load of 40 N was exerted on the teeth for one minute using a digital dynamometer (Digital Force Gage SH-500; Geratech Ltd., Hong Kong, Taiwan). All seating procedures were performed by the same operator to minimize operator-related variability; however, intra-operator variability inherent to in vitro procedures may not be entirely eliminated.

After removal, the interior of the copings was filled with a light-body VPS material (Betasil Light Vario; Müller-Omicron GmbH & Co., KG, Lindlar, Germany). Silicone replicas were sectioned in the mesiodistal and buccolingual directions (Fig. 2). Marginal and internal discrepancy measurements were conducted at 100× magnification using a stereomicroscope (AZ100M motorized multi-purpose zoom microscope; Nikon Corp., Kanagawa, Japan) (Fig. 3). Measurements were taken at 30 points for each specimen, including four locations in the marginal, chamfer, and axial regions, with two points from the mesio-distal and bucco-lingual sections and three points in the incisal area (Fig. 4) [11]. The overall discrepancy value (ODV) was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the marginal (MDV), chamfer (CDV), axial (ADV), and incisal (IDV) discrepancy values obtained for each specimen. The mean value of the measurement data from each region was calculated, and statistical analysis was conducted.

Statistical analysis

A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to investigate the effects of fabrication technique (3DP, Cast, CAW, DMLS, SLM), tooth type (canine, central), and their interaction on discrepancy values (ODV, MDV, CDV, ADV, and IDV). While the assumption of normality was met, Levene’s test indicated a violation of the homogeneity of variances assumption (P < 0.001). Therefore, a Bootstrap procedure with 2000 resamples and a 95% confidence interval was employed to evaluate group differences in the ANOVA analyses [12].

Results

Skewness and kurtosis values of the discrepancy values were examined to assess the normality of the distribution. The absolute skewness values ranged within ± 2, and kurtosis values were within ± 7 (Table 1). Considering the large sample size, the data were deemed to be normally distributed [13]. Regarding MDV, CDV, ADV, IDV, and ODV, the fabrication technique exhibited a statistically significant main effect (P < 0.001). Conversely, tooth type did not demonstrate a statistically significant main effect for MDV (P = 0.160), CDV (P = 0.281), ADV (P = 0.180), IDV (P = 0.867), or ODV (P = 0.069). Additionally, the interaction effect between the fabrication technique and tooth type was not statistically significant for MDV (P = 0.190), CDV (P = 0.427), ADV (P = 0.707), IDV (P = 0.913), or ODV (P = 0.343). Effect sizes (partial η²) were reported to indicate the magnitude of the observed differences (Table 2).

An analysis of estimated marginal means, adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, revealed that for both the CAW and DMLS techniques, the mean MDV of the canine was significantly higher than that of the central incisor (P < 0.05). Additionally, for both the canine and central incisor, the 3DP technique exhibited a significantly higher MDV compared to all other fabrication techniques (P < 0.05). No significant differences in CDV, ADV, or IDV were observed between canine and central incisor across all fabrication techniques (P > 0.05). For both types of teeth, the 3DP technique demonstrated a significantly higher CDV than all other techniques (P < 0.05). Furthermore, for canine, the CAW and DMLS techniques resulted in higher CDV than the SLM technique (P < 0.05). For both canine and central incisor, the 3DP technique exhibited a significantly higher ADV compared to the DMLS and SLM techniques (P < 0.05). Specifically, in canine, the Cast and CAW techniques demonstrated higher ADV than the SLM technique (P < 0.05). In central incisor, the CAW technique showed higher ADV than the DMLS technique (P < 0.05). Regarding incisal discrepancy values (IDV), the 3DP technique yielded significantly higher measurements for canine than all others (P < 0.05). Similarly, for central incisor, the 3DP technique yielded significantly higher IDV than the Cast, DMLS, and SLM techniques (P < 0.05). Within the DMLS technique, the overall discrepancy value (ODV) for canine was significantly higher than that for central incisor (P < 0.05). Furthermore, for both tooth types, the 3DP technique resulted in significantly higher ODV than all others (P < 0.05). For canine, the SLM technique exhibited significantly smaller ODV than to all others (P < 0.05). Lastly, for central incisor, the Cast technique demonstrated higher ODV than the SLM technique (P < 0.05) (Table 3) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the fabrication technique significantly influences the internal and marginal fit of 3-unit FDPs’ Co-Cr alloy frameworks. Consequently, the first null hypothesis, which posited that the technique of constructing a Co-Cr metal framework would not significantly influence the fit of a three-unit anterior FDP, is rejected. Furthermore, irrespective of the fabrication technique employed, discrepancy values were not affected by tooth type. Thus, the second null hypothesis, which proposed that there are no differences among tooth types regarding overall, marginal, chamfer, axial, and incisal fit within the frameworks regardless of the fabrication technique employed, is accepted. This finding aligns with previous studies, although it contrasts with a study suggesting that tooth type may influence the marginal and internal fit of restorations [14–16].

The selective laser melting technique demonstrated the lowest values of ODV. The superior fit observed in laser-melted frames can be attributed to the inherent precision and selectivity of the laser melting/sintering technique, along with the rapid solidification of the Co-Cr powder in thin layers, which effectively minimizes the alloy’s shrinkage potential [17]. Differences among the DMLS, SLM, and casting groups may be explained by factors such as wax pattern shrinkage, high solidification expansion, and the high thermal expansion coefficient of the molten metal. It is well established that multi-stage processes are more susceptible to errors [3, 18]. In conventional casting, marginal fit may be compromised due to shrinkage of impression materials or plaster and deformation of the wax pattern. In contrast, automated techniques such as laser melting and sintering eliminate the need for molds or plaster models by enabling the direct fabrication of metal frameworks, thereby reducing potential sources of error. Although cast crowns have demonstrated long-term durability and the lost wax technique remains a standard method for restoration fabrication among many clinicians, the present study found that the conventional lost wax technique exhibited discrepancies below 120 μm in all evaluated areas [19]. This technique has yielded favorable results with gaps distributed homogeneously across the framework.

No significant difference in ODV was observed between the DMLS and SLM techniques for the central incisor, which serves as the abutment for the Co-Cr alloy 3-unit FDP framework. However, the SLM technique demonstrated superior results for the canine tooth. This discrepancy appears to be attributable to measurements taken in the marginal and chamfer regions. While central teeth exhibited no differences between the two techniques, the observed variation in canine teeth suggests that material shrinkage may have occurred in the DMLS technique, potentially due to the larger surface area of the canine compared to the central incisor. In this study, both DMLS and SLM techniques utilized metal powders with layering thickness of 20 μm; nevertheless, the processes applied to these powders differ. In selective laser sintering, thermal imbalances arise from the sintering of powder particles heated by a CO2 laser beam within the build chamber, resulting in shrinkage. Total shrinkage comprises material shrinkage, process shrinkage, and thermal shrinkage, all of which impact dimensional accuracy and surface quality. Factors contributing to shrinkage in selective laser sintering include powder characteristics and process parameters such as scanning speed, scanning interval, layer thickness, laser power, delay time, and bed temperature. Additionally, crystallization induces molecular reorganization into a more compact structure, which may further contribute to material shrinkage [20, 21].

Across all groups, the incisal region demonstrated the highest numerical values regarding restoration fit. This phenomenon can be attributed to the existence of residual powder within the melt zone, resulting from exposure to environmental heat during the production processes of DMLS and SLM techniques. Such melting leads to inconsistencies, particularly in smaller and more complex structures. Notably, previous studies have reported that the highest mean discrepancy values across all frameworks occur in the incisal region. [22, 23] It is assumed that the inadequacy of the internal and marginal gaps hinders the proper seating of the restoration, consequently leading to an increased gap in the incisal/occlusal region [24].

A fundamental challenge in additive manufacturing is the potential for distortion caused by shrinkage after curing, particularly when utilizing thin layer thicknesses [25]. Techniques that reduce the number of fabrication steps have been demonstrated to enhance the fabrication of metal frameworks with better fit [18]. However, there remains a scarcity of research investigating the accuracy of metal frameworks fabricated using stereolithography (SLA) technology [26, 27]. 3D printing offers several advantages over conventional techniques, including accelerated restoration fabrication and the ability to save and reutilize CAD data. Since SLA utilizes photosensitive resin, post-processing procedures such as cleaning and support removal are necessary [28]. Consistent with previous findings, this study demonstrated that the 3DP group exceeded clinically acceptable limits in all regions except the axial, and exhibited the greatest differences compared to the other groups [26]. This discrepancy is attributed to volumetric shrinkage, a significant challenge associated with light-sensitive resins [29]. It is assumed that a reduction in the axial gap leads to incomplete seating of the framework, the presence of gaps in the occlusal and chamfer regions, and reduced marginal fit. Prior studies have reported deviations in the dimensional accuracy of 3D-printed casting molds relative to their intended specifications, which may explain the observations in the 3DP group in the present study [27, 30]. In this study, casting molds were printed with a layer thickness of 40 μm; by contrast, another study reported favorable outcomes using 10 μm layers with the same equipment [30]. In additive manufacturing, models are constructed by sequentially depositing layers, with each layer’s thickness potentially inducing a phenomenon known as layer error or the stair-stepping effect. Furthermore, deformation of mold components may occur during polymerization due to thermal effects and other factors affecting interlayer bonding. Damage incurred during the removal of support material post-printing can further exacerbate marginal discrepancies [27].

Except for the IDV in the central teeth, CAW samples exhibited values below 120 μm across all groups. This inconsistency observed in the incisal region may be attributed to the limitations of CAD/CAM milling systems. The milling process is affected by the drill diameter, which tends to flatten the contacted areas on the cusps. This effect restricts accurate geometric reproduction and may lead to inadequate milling of sharp internal angles [31]. No significant differences were observed between the CAW and Cast techniques, except for the MDV. The CAW technique demonstrated superior marginal fit for central and canine teeth; however, overall fit was comparable between the two techniques. Based on these findings, it can be concluded that specimens fabricated using the CAW technique exhibited a more homogeneous distribution of internal and marginal gaps. This outcome may be attributed to the challenges associated with preparing wax patterns [32]. Furthermore, the CAW technique demonstrated comparable outcomes to DMLS in terms of ODV. However, DMLS exhibited lower gap values in the axial region and higher values in the marginal region. These findings suggest that the DMLS technique results in incomplete seating of the restoration and increased marginal gaps, which may be attributed to dimensional shrinkage in the axial region.

A potential limitation of this type of study is the absence of details concerning if internal adjustments were performed before measurement, which could affect the results. Moreover, manual correction techniques have been shown to significantly enhance marginal fit [33]. The removal of the oxide layer enhances the fit, likely by eliminating occlusal surface irregularities attributable to the presence of the oxide layer [34]. In this study, the inner surfaces of the specimens were subjected solely to sandblasting and the removal of casting nodules under microscopy, without any further modifications. Across all techniques and measurement regions, except for DMLS and SLM techniques in the axial region, internal gap exceeded the designed cement gap (70 μm) in all frameworks. The amount of cement gaps influences restoration seating by affecting cement thickness; as an increased cement space may decrease marginal discrepancies; it may also lead to large internal gaps that adversely affect the mechanical properties of the cement [35]. Based on prior research, this study utilized a cement gap of 70 μm [36]. This parameter may have influenced the fit data [37]. Alterations in internal fit can generate stresses that potentially compromise restoration retention [25]. Previous investigations have reported a wide range of values for internal and marginal discrepancies [8, 38, 39]. The variability in fabrication and evaluation parameters complicates the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the superiority of any particular fabrication technique. It has been reported that no technique exhibits a significant advantage over others regarding internal and marginal fit [38]. Consistent with these findings, the results of this study do not indicate the superiority of any individual technique. However, it is noteworthy that the DMLS and, particularly, SLM techniques have yielded favorable outcomes and represent viable alternatives to traditional casting for the fabrication of Co-Cr frameworks in anterior three-unit FDPs. These techniques facilitate the rapid and precise fabrication of restorations, as reported in previous studies [38, 39].

Restoration fit is determined by measuring the discrepancies between the substructure surface and the teeth at various points. In this study, vertical marginal misfit was used as the primary criterion for evaluating the marginal fit of three-unit anterior FDPs. Previous research has demonstrated that vertical misfits are the least amenable to adjustment after the fabrication of the metal framework [40]. Although horizontal marginal misfits, such as crown extensions, can be partially adjusted under appropriate laboratory conditions and intraorally, vertical misfits can only be compensated for by cement, which is susceptible to dissolution over time. Therefore, vertical marginal misfit holds greater clinical significance and represents a more critical parameter for assessing marginal fit [8].

There is no universally established threshold defining the clinically acceptable degree of misfit. Traditionally, the clinical threshold for the marginal and internal fit of crowns has been established at 120 μm [41]. However, this threshold may have been updated in contemporary prosthodontics and digital dentistry. It has been reported that restorations fabricated using the lost wax technique exhibit a mean marginal gap of 71.6 μm, whereas those fabricated with CAD/CAM systems demonstrate a mean marginal gap of 103.21 μm [42]. Another systematic review indicated that restorations fabricated with CAD/CAM technology exhibited a mean marginal misfit ranging from 10 to 110 μm and a mean internal misfit ranging from 23.5 to 154 μm. Furthermore, the majority of studies reported misfit values below 80 μm, suggesting that CAD/CAM techniques are capable of achieving misfit values within this range [43].

Currently, standardized guidelines for assessing fit accuracy are lacking. The silicone replica technique offers the advantage of preserving restorations during evaluation, simplicity and effectiveness; however, as a two-dimensional technique, it is limited by the finite number of cross-sections and is unable to evaluate the fit of margins that are excessively overextended or underextended [22, 44, 45]. Despite these limitations, numerous researchers have demonstrated the reliability of the silicone replica technique and have employed it as a measurement method [23, 46]. The location and number of measurement points are critically important for accurately evaluating restoration fit. In the present study, measurements were obtained at 15 points on each tooth to enhance the precision of the results.

The authors emphasize the importance of achieving consensus on the standardization of methodologies to accurately evaluate restoration fit. In the DMLS, SLM, CAW, and 3DP groups, cement gap was defined using software to ensure standardization. Conversely, in the lost-wax casting technique utilized a die spacer to establish the intended cement gap. However, it remains unconfirmed whether this gap precisely measures 70 μm, representing a potential limitation of the present study.

The effect of ceramic veneering on the internal and marginal fit of porcelain-fused-to-metal restorations remains a subject of ongoing debate [23, 30]. Previous studies have yielded inconsistent findings; some report that repeated firing enhances marginal fit, others suggest it reduces fit, while some observe no significant effect [23, 46, 47]. However, veneering has been demonstrated to increase the gap between the restoration and the model [46]. Consequently, the present study focused solely on measurements of frameworks without veneering.

This study ensured the restorations fit correctly during SRT by applying a 40 N vertical force with the dynamometer tip to the incisal middle third of the pontics. The force was applied along the FDPs’ long axis, however, off-axis loading forces may have generated changes in the restorations’ fit. This in vitro study is limited by its inability to fully replicate the moisture and thermal conditions inherent to the oral cavity. Previous research has demonstrated that thermomechanical aging can increase internal gaps in Co-Cr frameworks fabricated using both traditional casting and computer-aided manufacturing techniques [48]. Future studies should prioritize in vivo validation of these findings by evaluating the effects of variables such as occlusal loading and thermal cycling on the fit of restorations produced through various fabrication techniques.

To the authors’ knowledge, no previous study has compared the restoration fit values across different anterior tooth types in either FDPs or crowns. Previous studies evaluating the fit of fixed dental prostheses mainly focused on fabrication or impression methods and prosthesis design complexity, without directly assessing different anterior abutment tooth types. In these investigations, central incisor and canine teeth were not analyzed as independent variables, and fit outcomes were primarily attributed to manufacturing workflows or material processing stages [49–51]. A study examining digital and analog workflows further suggested that abutment tooth geometry may influence accuracy at a broader level, particularly when comparing anterior and posterior preparations [52]. In contrast, the present study specifically compared maxillary central incisor and canine abutments and found no significant differences in any discrepancy parameter under standardized in vitro conditions, indicating that anterior tooth type alone may not be a decisive factor for fit outcomes.

This study has several limitations. The silicone replica technique is sensitive to material thickness variations and provides two-dimensional measurements based on sectioned replicas rather than a complete three-dimensional assessment. Cement space standardization relied on software parameters and die spacer application, which may not precisely reproduce clinical cement thickness. The use of a single master model may limit external validity, and operator-dependent variables during scanning and fabrication could not be entirely eliminated. In additive manufacturing workflows, factors such as print anisotropy, build orientation, and post-curing or thermal post-processing may influence dimensional accuracy and, consequently, fit outcomes. These process-related variables were not independently assessed in the present study and should be considered when interpreting the findings and their potential clinical translation. Finally, point-based measurements may not capture localized discrepancies across the entire surface, and future studies using 3D methods (e.g., micro-CT or digital 3D gap mapping) could provide more comprehensive assessment. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and cannot be directly extrapolated to clinical conditions.
