Modulation of splicing factors SF3B1 and SRSF1 by polypurine reverse Hoogsteen hairpins affects the splicing pattern of estrogen receptor α in breast cancer cells
Authors: Ester López-Aguilar, Judith Cullell, Ana Delgado, Laia Monserrat, Violeta Serra, Carlos J. Ciudad, Véronique Noé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple mRNA splice variants for the human estrogen receptor α (ERα) with one or more skipped exons have been identified, and some encode isoforms with modified functions compared with the wild-type 66 kDa protein. Our aim was to evaluate the effect of Polypurine Reverse Hoogsteen Hairpins (PPRHs) targeting the splicing factors SF3B1 and SRSF1 in breast cancer cells to analyze the potential changes in ERα splicing pattern. Two PPRHs against SF3B1 and three against SRSF1 were designed, and their cytotoxic effect was evaluated in vitro. HpSF3B1 against the SF3B1 promoter and HpSRSF1 against SRSF1 exon 3 decreased the protein levels of their respective targets in MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Inhibition of either SF3B1 or SRSF1 in these cells altered the inclusion/skipping of several ERα exons and decreased the protein levels of ERα isoforms. Changes in ERα splicing variants were also observed in the ZR-75-1 cell line. Additionally, modulation on mRNA and protein levels upon transfection with both PPRHs was also detected in patient-derived xenograft cells. Finally, we showed that combining PPRHs targeting SF3B1 or SRSF1 with Tamoxifen or 4-hydroxytamoxifen reduced MCF-7 cell viability in an additive or synergistic manner, respectively. Our results indicate that SF3B1 and SRSF1 participate in the generation of different ERα splicing variants and their inhibition by PPRHs could have therapeutic potential in the treatment of estrogen receptor positive breast tumors.

Introduction

Splicing is essential for eukaryotic gene expression. For more than 90% of human genes, this process can occur simultaneously through alternative pathways, generating multiple splice products from a single gene with a direct impact on cell homeostasis, tissue specificity, and organ development [1, 2]. Furthermore, when genes are mis-spliced, dysfunction of biological networks can lead to developmental and neurological disorders, muscular dystrophies, and cancer [3–7]. In cancer, aberrant alternative splicing is considered a hallmark of the disease, since it is involved in key processes such as cell survival, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [8, 9].

The splicing process is catalyzed by the spliceosome molecular machinery which comprises six small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs), U1-6. Among splicing regulators, the Splicing Factor 3B subunit 1 (SF3B1) [10] and Serine/arginine-Rich Splicing Factor 1 (SRSF1) are recurrently altered in cancer [11, 12]. SF3B1, the biggest component of the U2 snRNP, is frequently mutated or overexpressed in several malignancies, including breast cancer, where its upregulation is associated with lymph node metastasis [13]. SRSF1 is an oncogenic splicing factor frequently overexpressed in solid tumors, and it is directly regulated by MYC, which can modulate cell growth, apoptosis, and differentiation by modifying the expression of its target genes [14].

Estrogen receptor α (ERα) is a nuclear receptor whose interaction with estradiol (E2) induces estrogen-dependent transcriptional pathways involved in cell cycle regulation, the proliferation of sexual tissues and tumorigenesis. ERα is a key player in the development of breast tumors, overexpressed in around 70% of breast cancer cases and constitutes the target of endocrine therapeutic strategies for this type of tumor. ERα is composed of six functional domains [9, 15, 16]. Its full-length 66 kDa isoform (ER66) is the main mediator of the canonical estrogen signaling, whereas other truncated or alternatively spliced variants missing some of these domains, such as ER52 (lacking the N-terminus), ER46 (lacking the C-terminus) or ER36 (lacking both N and C-terminus) display other functions associated with altered estrogen responses, endocrine resistance and more aggressive tumor progression. The relative balance between the full-length ER66 and shorter isoforms is emerging as an important factor in response to endocrine therapy and tumor behavior [17, 18].

Some small molecules target the spliceosome and could have a potential application in the context of cancer treatment. Among them, the chemical inhibitor Pladienolide B blocks the conformational change of SF3B1 during the formation of the A complex, which has an inhibitory effect on RNA splicing in vitro [19, 20]. However, in a cellular context, introns exhibit differential sensitivity to this inhibitor, which results in intron retention and/or exon skipping rather than a general inhibition of splicing [21, 22]. Some derivatives of the drug such as E7107, even reached phase I clinical trials, but further development was discontinued due to severe ocular adverse events [23, 24]. Another derivative, H3B-8800, orally bioavailable and effective against tumors with SF3B1 mutations [25], is currently in clinical trials. Other approaches such as antisense or decoy oligonucleotides have also been explored to modulate RNA splicing in a potentially therapeutic way [26, 27]. Our research group developed the Polypurine Reverse Hoogsteen Hairpins (PPRHs), formed by two antiparallel polypurine stretches linked by 5 thymidines, which adapt a hairpin structure through reverse Hoogsteen bonds. The polypurine sequence of each PPRH is designed to bind specifically to a complementary polypyrimidine strand located in the gene of interest through Watson and Crick bonds. Consequently, it causes the displacement of the complementary DNA strand, leading to the inhibition of transcription and gene silencing. The use of PPRHs has been validated for several cancer-related genes and its efficacy both in vivo and in vitro has been demonstrated [28]. Some of the targeted genes are BIRC5, PD-1/PD-L1, MYC, KRAS or HER-2 [29–33]. Recently, we extended the application of our technology to the PCSK9 gene as a therapeutic approach for cardiovascular diseases [34] or against SARS-CoV-2 as a treatment for Covid19 infection [35].

Our aim was to design specific PPRHs targeting the splicing factors SF3B1 and SRSF1. We hypothesized that they might be implicated in ERα mRNA alternative splicing. We evaluated the effect of these therapeutic oligonucleotides on human breast cancer cell lines and cells obtained from patient derived xenografts. Finally, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of PPRHs against SF3B1 and SRSF1 in combination with the antiestrogen drug Tamoxifen and its active metabolite 4-hydroxytamoxifen.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

MCF-7 and ZR-75-1 human estrogen receptor positive breast cancer cell lines were grown in Ham’s F12 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS (GIBCO, Invitrogen, Barcelona, Spain) and penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma Aldrich, Madrid, Spain) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were detached with 0.05% trypsin (Merck Life Science S.L.U., Madrid, Spain) for expansion and harvesting.

Patient derived tumor cells

Patient-derived tumor cells (PDXCs) were isolated from patient-derived xenografts grown in the facilities of the Vall d’Hebron Institute of Oncology, Barcelona, Spain. PDX519 breast cancer cells were isolated following the specifications reported in [36] and plated in DMEM medium (GIBCO, LifeTechnologies, Paisley, UK), supplemented with 2% FBS (GIBCO, Invitrogen, Barcelona, Spain), 10 µg/mL of Rock inhibitor (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 3 ng/mL of EGF (Prepotech, USA), 5 µg/mL of insulin (Merck), 1 µg/mL of hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich), 1x of B-27 (GIBCO, LifeTechnologies, Grand Island, NY, USA), 30 µg/mL of gentamycin (GIBCO, LifeTechnologies, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 15 ng/mL of fungizone (GIBCO, Paisley, UK).

Design of PPRHs

PPRHs targeting the genes encoding for the splicing factors SF3B1 and SRSF1 were designed using the Triplex-Forming Oligonucleotide Target Sequence Search software (https://utw10685.utweb.utexas.edu/tfo/). We searched for polypurine stretches with more than 20 nucleotides in length, with a maximum of 3 pyrimidine interruptions, and a minimum G content of 40%. The polypurine-rich sequence candidates were analyzed using BLAST to confirm their specificity and to avoid off-target effects. The structure of PPRHs consists of two-stranded mirror repeats of the selected polypurine stretches, running in an antiparallel orientation, linked by a five-thymidine loop and intramolecular reverse Hoogsteen bonds. We also designed two negative controls, HpSC, a scrambled sequence PPRH, and HpWC, a hairpin structure with intramolecular Watson & Crick bonds instead of reverse Hoogsteen bonds.

All the designed PPRHs were synthetized as non-modified oligodeoxynucleotides by Merck (Haverhill, UK), resuspended in sterile Tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; Sigma-Aldrich, Madrid, Spain) at a concentration of 100 µM and stored at − 20 °C.

Transfection of PPRHs

MCF-7 cells (10,000–30,000), ZR-751 cells (100,000) and PDX519 cells (200,000) were plated in 6-well dishes and transfected with the PPRHs at different final concentrations (100–250 nM) by mixing them with N-[1-(1,2-Di-(9Z-octadecenoyl)−3-trimethylammoniumpropane methyl sulfate (DOTAP, Biontex, Germany) as previously described [33].

Cell incubations with Pladienolide B, Tamoxifen and 4-hydroxytamoxifen

Pladienolide B (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Heidelberg, Germany), Tamoxifen and 4-hydroxytamoxifen were all dissolved in DMSO for incubations in MCF-7 cells (10,000–30,000) at different concentrations.

Cell viability

Cell viability was determined by MTT assays five days after transfection. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm in a Varioskan LUX Plate Reader (ThermoFisher, Barcelona, Spain). Results were expressed as the percentage of cell survival.

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Life Technologies, Madrid, Spain) following the manufacturer’s specifications from either control cells, PPRH-transfected or treated with Pladienolide B. Quantification of RNA was determined by measuring its absorbance at 260 nm using a Nanodrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Barcelona, Spain).

Analysis of ERα splicing variants

cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription at 42 °C for 1 h, in a reaction mixture containing 1 µg of total RNA. PCR amplification was performed in two-phase mixtures separated by paraffin wax. The lower phase contained 5 µL 5× GC buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), 0.2 mM dNTPs and 500 ng of each primer (Merck, Haverhill, UK). The upper phase contained 5 µL 5× GC buffer, 2.5 units of OneTaq (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and 5 µL of cDNA. PCR reactions involved an initial step at 94 °C (3 min), followed by 30 cycles of a denaturation step at 94 °C (30 s), an annealing step at 59 °C (1 min) and an elongation step at 68 °C (1 min), with a final extension step at 68 °C (5 min). The amplified products corresponding to the inclusion/skipping of each exon in ERα mRNA were visualized by electrophoresis in 5% acrylamide gels in 1×TBE.

For each exon, a specific pair of primers was designed, in which the forward primer was complementary to a sequence in the upstream exon to be detected, and the reverse primer complementary to a sequence in the downstream exon to be detected. Thus, the inclusion or skipping of a specific exon led to the amplification of two PCR products of different sizes, and the difference in size corresponded exactly to the exon analyzed. The location, names and sequence of the primers’ sets, and size of the amplified products are shown in Table 2. As an endogenous control, the mRNA corresponding to the APRT gene was also amplified.

Western blot analyses

Total protein extracts from breast cancer cells or PDXCs were obtained after transfection or treatment with PDB, in 100 µL of RIPA buffer (20 mM H3PO4 pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM MgCl2), supplemented with NaF 25 mM, PMSF 5 mM, and cOmplete™ Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Lysates were centrifuged (12,000 x g at 4 °C for 10 min).

Protein extracts (20–50 µg) were resolved in 4–12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred to PVDF membranes using Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad, California, USA). Membranes were probed for 1 h with primary antibodies against SF3B1 (1:100 dilution; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Heidelberg, Germany), SRSF1 (1:100 dilution; Invitrogen, Rockford, IL, USA), ERα (ERα N-terminal 1:500 dilution, EMD Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany; ERα C-terminal 1:500 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), tubulin (1:100 dilution, EMD Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) or β-Actin (HRP-linked; 1:5000, A3854, Merck Life Science SLU, Madrid, Spain). Signals corresponding to tubulin or β-Actin were used to normalize the results. Secondary antibodies anti-rabbit (1:2000 dilution, Agilent Technologies, Glostrup, Denmark) or anti-mouse (1:2000 dilution, Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, Heidelberg, Germany) were incubated with the membranes for 1 h at RT. Chemiluminescence was detected with Image Quant LAS 4000 mini (GE Healthcare, Barcelona, Spain) and results were quantified by densitometry using the Image-Quant TL software v 10.2 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Barcelona, Spain). Results are expressed as the percentage of the normalized control signal.

RT-qPCR Analyses

The expression levels of ERα target genes GREB1, TFF1 and Cyclin D1 were determined in RNA samples from PDX519 breast cancer cells transfected with HpSC, HpSF3B1-Pr or HpSRSF1-Ex3 by RT-qPCR, using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Hs00536409_m1 for GREB1; Hs00907239_m1 for TFF1; Hs00765553_m1 for Cyclin D1 and Hs00427620_m1 for TBP used as endogenous control; Thermofisher Scientific, Madrid, Spain). Quantification was performed using the ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, California, USA). All data are shown as the mean ± SEM of three independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance. Differences were considered significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Effect of on ERα splicing variants in MCF-7

PPRHs targeting SF3B1 were designed against its promoter region (HpSF3B1-Pr) and intron 1 (HpSF3B1-I1). For SRSF1, PPRHs targeted either its promoter region (HpSRSF1-Pr), intron 1 (HpSRSF1-I1) or exon 3 (HpSRSF1-Ex3). HpSC and HpWC were used as negative controls. An initial screening was performed to evaluate the effect on cell viability of all the PPRHs transfected at 100 nM in MCF-7. Among them, HpSF3B1-Pr and HpSRSF1-Ex3 showed the most significant reduction in cell viability, by 32% and 33%, respectively (Fig. 1A), and were selected for further experiments. The analyses of protein levels of both targets upon PPRH transfection revealed that HpSF3B1-Pr reduced SF3B1 expression by 79% (SEM ± 8.15), while HpSRSF1-Ex3 decreased SRSF1 expression by 66% (SEM ± 2.79) relative to the control (Fig. 1B).

To evaluate the result of downregulating the abovementioned splicing factors on ERα variants, we conducted a series of RT-PCR reactions to determine whether each ERα exon has been included and/or skipped in the corresponding mRNA transcript (Fig. 1C). Inhibition of SF3B1 reduced the inclusion of ERα exons 1 and 3. Silencing of SRSF1 reduced the inclusion of ERα exons 1, 2, 3, 5 and 7. Except for exon 3, for which an increase in its skipped form was observed, the decrease in exon inclusion did not occur concomitantly with an increase in the corresponding skipped form. The changes observed in the inclusion/skipping of the different ERα exons upon HpSF3B1-Pr and HpSRSF1-Ex3 transfection were specific to ERα pre-mRNA since APRT mRNA levels remained unchanged in these conditions (Fig. 1C). Additionally, HpSC was used as a negative control.

The effect of PPRH-mediated inhibition of SF3B1 and SRSF1 was also explored on the different ERα protein isoforms present in MCF-7 cells (Fig. 1D). ER66 decreased by 42% (SEM ± 2.08) when SF3B1 was inhibited and by 57% (SEM ± 1.76) when SRSF1 was targeted. ER52 also decreased by 58% (SEM ± 2.79) upon transfection of HpSF3B1-Pr and by 77% (SEM ± 2.36) upon transfection of HpSRSF1-Ex3. The levels of ER46 were much lower than ER66 and ER52 in MCF-7 control cells and appeared almost undetectable in PPRH-transfected cells.

Effect of Pladienolide B and Characterization of ERα splicing variants in MCF-7

To further confirm the involvement of SF3B1 on the generation of ERα splicing variants, MCF-7 cells were treated with the chemical inhibitor Pladienolide B (PDB). Incubations with increasing concentrations of PDB (1–10 nM) resulted in a progressive reduction in cell viability (Fig. 2A). The inclusion of all ERα exons was decreased after a 24 h exposure to the drug at 2 and 10 nM, except for exons 3 and 4, while the inhibitor increased the skipping of exons 2, 5, and 6 (Fig. 2B). These effects were more pronounced at 10 nM. ER66 protein levels decreased by 70% (SEM ± 2.00) and ER52 by 40% (SEM ± 6.35) at this concentration of PDB (Fig. 2C).

Effect of PPRHs against splicing factors SF3B1 and SRSF1 in ZR-75-1 cells

The ZR-75-1 breast cancer cell line was used to evaluate the changes in the inclusion/skipping of ERα exons upon silencing of SF3B1 and SRSF1 in a different cell line and to optimize the concentration of PPRHs and DOTAP. This step was necessary to establish an appropriate range for the application of the PPRH technology in PDXCs, which require a high number of cells. The number of ZR-75-1 cells was set to 100,000 per well for both viability and mRNA expression assays, and three different concentrations of PPRHs were tested (100, 200 and 250 nM).

Dose-response analyses were performed to evaluate the effect of increasing PPRH concentrations on cell viability, while maintaining a 1:100 ratio between PPRH and DOTAP, over a 5-day incubation. As the concentration of HpSF3B1-Pr and HpSRSF1-Ex3 gradually increased to 250 nM, a clear reduction in cell viability was observed, by 88% and 92%, respectively (Fig. 3A). HpSC at 250 nM did not produce a significant reduction in cell viability and was used for comparative purposes in the analysis of splicing variants. Transfection of ZR-75-1 cells with 100 nM of the PPRHs had no effect in the inclusion of most ERα exons. In contrast, changes in the inclusion of ERα exons 1, 4, 5, 6 and 8 were evident at 200 nM of HpSF3B1-Pr. At 250 nM of this PPRH, the inclusion of exon 4 and 6 was significantly reduced. Transfection of HpSRSF1-Ex3 affected the inclusion of exons 4, 5, 7 and 8 at both 200 and 250 nM. The skipped products of exons 5 and 6 were decreased in these conditions (Fig. 3B). The relative low levels of ERα protein in ZR-75-1 cells did not allow its detection in our experimental conditions.

Effects of PPRHs against the splicing factors SF3B1 and SRSF1 in PDXCs

Patient-derived xenograft cells (PDXCs) were used to investigate alterations in ERα splicing variants induced by PPRH-mediated downregulation of SF3B1 and SRSF1 in a cellular model that reflected more closely tumor heterogeneity. Cell viability was assessed in PDXC519 upon PPRH transfection at 250 nM and was expressed relative to the HpSC condition, since this cellular model exhibited high sensitivity to the transfection procedure. Changes in cell viability caused by HpSF3B1-Pr were not significant whereas HpSRSF1-Ex3 decreased cell viability (Fig. 4A). Downregulation of SF3B1 and SRSF1 was determined at the protein level for both targets. HpSF3B1-Pr led to a 52% (SEM ± 6.94) reduction in SF3B1 levels, while HpSRSF1-Ex3 transfection resulted in a 44% (SEM ± 3.49) decrease in SRSF1 in PDXC519 (Fig. 4B).

Included and skipped forms for each ERα exon were analyzed using the RT-PCR approach (Fig. 4C). Both HpSF3B1-Pr and HpSRSF1-Ex3 led to a reduction in the inclusion of all ERα exons. Exons 1 and 5 were the most affected by PPRH transfection, as evidenced by the absence of both, the included and skipped forms. In the case of exon 3, an increase in its skipped form was observed compared to HpSC following transfection with HpSRSF1-Ex3.

ER66 protein levels were reduced by 27% (SEM ± 3.60) and 79% (SEM ± 3.12) following treatment with HpSF3B1-Pr and HpSRSF1-Ex3, respectively. In addition, changes in the expression of other ER isoforms generated through alternative splicing were also observed. Protein levels of ER52, ER46, and ER36 were markedly downregulated in response to the modulation of both splicing factors. ER52 levels decreased by 24% (SEM ± 3.63) and 49% (SEM ± 3.01) upon SF3B1 and SRSF1 inhibition, respectively. ER46 expression was reduced by up to 13% (SEM ± 2.56) and 58% (SEM ± 3.32) in these same conditions. ER36, that could be detected in this cell model, was affected by 45% (SEM ± 4.87) and 57% (SEM ± 4.81), respectively (Fig. 4D). The consequences of SF3B1 and SRSF1 silencing on ERα downstream targets were evaluated for three representative ER target genes, TFF1, GREB1 and Cyclin D1, by RT–qPCR. Inhibition of SRSF1 and SF3B1 mediated by PPRH transfection reduced TFF1 and Cyclin D1 mRNA levels by around 25% and 40%, respectively, whereas GREB1 expression showed a decrease of 12% in these conditions (Fig. 4E).

Effect of the combination of PPRHs and Tamoxifen or 4-hydroxytamoxifen on MCF-7 cells

A combinatorial study was performed to determine whether PPRHs and Tamoxifen and its active metabolite 4-hydroxytamoxifen could potentiate their cytotoxic effect. First, cell viability was assessed upon incubation of MCF-7 cells with increasing concentrations of Tamoxifen (TAM) (Fig. 5A). Then, TAM (0.5–1 µM) was used in combination with PPRHs (100–150 nM). TAM at 0.5 µM reduced cell viability by 12%, whereas its co-incubation with PPRHs at 100 nM decreased viability by more than 50%. At 1 µM, TAM alone reduced viability by 36%, while its combination with PPRHs against SF3B1 or SRSF1 at 100 nM led to a reduction of 66% and 68%, respectively. The combination of 1 µM TAM with 150 nM PPRHs decreased cell viability by 80% (Fig. 5B & C). The Combination Index (CI) was obtained by the Chou-Talalay method using the CompuSyn software (https://www.combosyn.com/). Synergy is determined by CI values under 0.90, while CI values between 0.90 and 1.10 are indicatives of an additive effect [37]. The CI values for combinations of HpSF3B1-Pr or HpSRSF1-Ex3 with TAM were all between 0.90 and 1.10, indicating an additive effect.

Since 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) is 30 to 100 times more active than Tamoxifen in inhibiting breast cancer cell growth [38], we next tested the combination of 4-OHT with either HpSF3B1-Pr or HpSRSF1-Ex3 on cell viability, using increasing concentrations of 4-OHT (10–100 nM) and 100 and 150 nM of each PPRH. The CI values for HpSF3B1-Pr with 4-OHT were between 0.3 and 0.5, and between 0.6 and 0.8 for HpSRSF1-Ex3, indicating in both cases a synergistic effect for those combinations (Fig. 5D & E).

Discussion

Breast cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide. Approximately 70% of tumors are ERα-positive and eligible for endocrine therapy. However, primary and acquired resistance to endocrine therapies continues to limit clinical success [28, 39], underscoring the need for novel therapeutic strategies that modulate estrogen signaling beyond classical receptor blockade. The existence of multiple ERα isoforms may contribute to endocrine resistance, influencing estrogen signaling, drug response, and, in some cases, promoting more aggressive tumor phenotypes.

Splicing factors SF3B1 and SRSF1 are involved in breast cancer progression, although their role in regulating ERα splicing remains largely unexplored. To date, only three splicing factors have been directly implicated to ERα alternative splicing [40]. HMGA1a promotes the generation of the ERα46 isoform in MCF-7 cells by modulating splice site selection, thereby influencing estrogen-dependent signaling [41]. BARX2 has been proposed to regulate ERα46 expression, with a preferential effect on the 46 kDa isoform and potential implications for tumor invasiveness. Finally, the NPE3-3 rat homolog facilitates alternative splicing of ERα, contributing to the production of isoforms that may affect tumor progression [42]. These findings suggest that ERα isoform is tightly controlled by splicing regulators and may affect breast cancer behavior.

Given the key role of SF3B1 and SRSF1 in splicing regulation and the limited knowledge of their impact on ERα mRNA variants, this study investigated whether silencing of these two splicing factors using PPRHs could alter the pattern of ERα variants. The data reveal substantial differences in ERα exon inclusion/skipping across MCF-7, ZR-75-1, and PDXCs, emphasizing the heterogenicity between these models [43]. Our results indicate that downregulation of SF3B1 and SRSF1 using PPRHs affected ERα exon inclusion/skipping in a cell-, dose- or exon-specific manner, highlighting that each tumor context may respond differently to splicing modulation. Furthermore, since SF3B1 and SRSF1 silencing affect skipping and/or inclusion of several exons at the same time in ERα transcripts, it cannot correlate with the downregulation of a specific ERα protein isoform.

Previous studies have shown that SF3B1 knockdown using shRNA in MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75-30 breast cancer cells suppressed proliferation by 50–80% and reduces SF3B1 protein levels by 60–90%, with RNA-seq analyses revealing increased exon skipping as the predominant splicing alteration [15]. Similarly, in T cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia cell lines, which highly overexpress SF3B1, its silencing by shRNAs or treatment with E7107 induced near-complete protein depletion and significantly decreased proliferation, observing once more that exon skipping is the main splicing change detected by paired-end RNA sequencing [44]. In line with these findings, the present work suggests that SF3B1 plays a prominent role in regulating the splicing machinery that contributes to ERα transcript generation. Treatment with Pladienolide B has been reported to increase retention of sororin introns 1 and 2 in MDA-MB-231 and Hs578T cells, and to promote the expression of pro-apoptotic isoforms of MCL-1 and BCL-X in chronic lymphocytic leukemia cells, without affecting splicing in normal lymphocytes, supporting a cell type-specific sensitivity to SF3B1 inhibitors [45, 46]. In MCF-7 cells, PDB induced a pattern of ERα splicing changes different from those caused by HpSF3B1-Pr transfection. This observation is particularly relevant, as it demonstrates that PPRHs provide a new tool to target the same splicing factor through a non-overlapping mechanism. Moreover, PPRHs can be used at concentrations up to 100 nM without dramatically affecting cell viability over a 5-day period, whereas PDB significantly reduces viability even at very low concentrations.

The PDX-model PDXC519 showed the clearest response to SRSF1 silencing: HpSRSF1-Ex3 led to pronounced alterations in ERα splicing patterns and significant reductions in its protein levels, effects that were also observed in MCF-7 and ZR-75-1 cells. Consistently, other studies employing gene therapy tools have reported that SRSF1 knockdown using shRNA inhibits proliferation in MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and T47D cells, achieving up to 85% decrease in SRSF1 mRNA levels and at least a two-fold change in exon in inclusion/exclusion ratios in around ten target genes [47]. An alternative approach, employing two decoy RNA oligonucleotides to block SRSF1 activity in U87MG and MDA-MB-231 cells, increased exon inclusion in U2AF1 and MKNK2, while reducing exon inclusion in INSR and USP8 [23]. In A549 lung cancer cells, exon exclusion was the most frequent event upon downregulation of SRSF1 with a specific siRNA, as determined by exon-junction microarray data, leading the authors to propose that SRSF1 mainly functions as a splicing repressor in these cells [48]. Altogether, these results indicate again that downregulation of SRSF1 affects exon inclusion/skipping of targeted genes that appear to be specific to the cell context used in the experimental approach.

Furthermore, we described that the alteration in ERα splicing variants and its protein expression upon silencing of splicing factors SF3B1 and SRSF1 in the PDX model led to the downregulation of several ERα target genes, such as GREB, TFF1 and Cyclin D1 [49].

Endocrine therapy is the first line of treatment for ERα positive breast tumors. This includes selective estrogen receptor modulators and degraders, and aromatase inhibitors, either as single agents or in combination with chemotherapy. Tamoxifen, a selective inhibitor of ERα, is a first-generation breast cancer drug approved in 1977 by the FDA for the treatment of breast cancer [50]. Since resistance to hormone therapy develops in 30%–50% of ERα-positive cancer patients, the utilization of combination therapies with Abemaciclib, aromatase inhibitors, or Fulvestrant is a common strategy to avoid the development of resistant tumors. Here we provide a possible alternative for this approach by combining PPRHs with either Tamoxifen or its active metabolite 4-hydroxytamoxifen. Our results in MCF-7 cells support this hypothesis, since cells are more sensitive to both Tamoxifen and 4-hydroxytamoxifen when combined with PPRH transfection, which leads to additive or synergistic effects on cytotoxicity. The synergy between RNA splicing inhibitors, such as H3B-8800 and PDB, and cancer therapy has been shown to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy or to overcome resistance when combined with immune checkpoint blockade therapy and BCL inhibitors, respectively [51].

Nowadays, targeting RNA splicing represents a promising path for cancer therapy. A variety of natural products and derivative molecules target the SF3B complex, and other approaches are being developed to target UA2F2, spliceosome transition from A to B complexes, and post-transcriptional modifications of different splicing factors. Several of these strategies are currently in clinical trials [51]. Here we describe the application of the PPRH technology as a novel strategy to downregulate a specific splicing factor. We show that HpSF3B1-Pr and HpSRSF1-Ex3 can be used as a silencing tool either for mechanistic explorative purposes or for therapeutic usages. Additionally, the possibility of transfecting PDXCs with PPRHs enables the direct assessment of ERα splicing and its regulation by specific splicing factors in patient-derived samples. Furthermore, the synergistic effect observed with 4-hydroxytamoxifen suggests a potential therapeutic combination for ERα-positive breast tumors, which could be particularly advantageous given PPRH stability, low immunogenicity, and ability to modulate specific gene networks involved in tumor progression [30, 31, 52].

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations that point to future research directions. Our analyses relied on RT-PCR determinations and known estrogen receptor isoforms, so broader methods such as RNA sequencing could reveal wider effects on splicing. In addition, more models are needed to address tumor heterogeneity. While our findings are promising in cell-based and ex vivo systems, in vivo studies are necessary to assess their therapeutic potential as well as the long-term effects of PPRH-mediated gene silencing. And finally, the improvement of delivery methods for gene therapy tools will be crucial for their clinical applications.
