Surgical outcomes of cable plate fixation in treatment of Vancouver type B1 and type C periprosthetic femoral fractures: a retrospective case series
Authors: Cengiz Şen, Taha Kızılkurt, Mehmet Demirel, Ahmet Müçteba Yıldırım, Yavuz Sağlam, İrfan Öztürk
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to investigate the mid-to-long-term surgical outcomes of open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) using a hybrid locking plate/cable technique for the treatment of Vancouver type B1 and type C periprosthetic femoral fractures (PPFs) in a consecutive group of patients from a single tertiary referral center.Methods:Twenty-five patients (25 PPFs; 17 female, 8 male) in whom a Vancouver type B1 or type C PPF was diagnosed and treated by a hybrid locking plate/cable technique from 2005 to 2016 were included in the study. Patients’ functional status was categorized into 4 groups based on the Harris Hip Score (HHS) at the final follow-up: 70 = poor result; 70-80 = fair; 80-90 = good, and 90-100 = excellent. Intra- and postoperative complications were also recorded. PPF union was defined clinically as the patient’s ability to bear full weight with or without assistance and radiographically as the presence of a callus bridging the fracture. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to the Vancouver classification and type of fixation regarding the HHS and time to union.Results:The mean age was 57 ± 16.6 (range, 17-82) years at the time of the primary hip replacement and 64 ± 18.7 (range, 24-88) years at the time of PPF. The mean follow-up was 5.6 ± 3.3 (range, 2-14) years from primary procedure to PPF and 6.5 ± 4.1 (range, 3-15) years following PPF. There were 7 type B1 and 18 type C PPFs. At the final follow-up, the mean HHS was 71 ± 7.74 (range, 57-89). According to HHS, functional results were poor in 8 patients, fair in 14 patients, and good in 3 patients. No major intra- or postoperative complications were noted. Fracture union was achieved in all patients without complications at an average of 13 ± 4.9 (range, 6-24) weeks. In subgroup analysis, while no significant differences were observed in the HHS (P = .87 for the Vancouver type, P = .96 for the type of fixation), time to union differed among groups. Time to union was significantly shorter in type B1 than in type C PPFs (P = .006). Time to union was considerably shorter in the uncemented group compared to the cemented one (P = .017).Conclusion:Adding cables to the locking plate can provide adequate stability to preserve fracture alignment and achieve bony union in Vancouver type B1 and C PPFs. Although union can be achieved by ORIF in such patients, a longer union time may be required for PPFs in the setting of a cemented femoral stem or Vancouver type C.Level of Evidence:Level IV, Therapeutic study.

Introduction

Periprosthetic fracture (PPF) of the femur after total hip arthroplasty (THA) continues to increase in frequency.1 As a result, a rising number of primary and revision arthroplasties are performed annually due to patients’ increasing age and fragility.2 The incidence of PPFs has been reported to range between 0.5% and 4% following primary THA but can rise up to 4% after revision THA procedures.3 Management of PPFs is demanding due to the complexity of the surgical procedure, which requires both revision arthroplasty and trauma surgery expertise. Comorbidities such as previous surgery, osteoporosis, and altered functional status of patients can make PPF management more challenging even in the best hands.4

Nowadays, the Vancouver classification is the universally recognized classification scheme used to guide the treatment choice by orthopedic surgeons.5,6 This classification considers the location of the fracture relative to the stem, prosthesis stability, and bone loss. If the prosthesis is loose (type B2, B3), there is general agreement that the femoral component should be revised with or without adjuvant plate and/or strut fixation (Table 1). However, managing PPFs developing around (type B1) or below the tip of a well-fixed femoral stem (type C) remains controversial. Although open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is the recommended treatment option for such PPFs, using a proper implant that can achieve adequate proximal fixation in osteoporotic bone without damaging the cement mantle or femoral stem is a significant concern.3,7 Numerous fixation techniques for Vancouver type B1 and type C PPFs have been developed, including lateral plates, screws, wires, cables, and allografts or combinations. Nevertheless, the literature is relatively scarce and uncertain which fixation technique is optimal.8-10

In recent years, locking plates have emerged as a viable alternative to conventional plates for the fixation of PPFs because of their several advantages, such as minimal disruption of the periosteal blood supply and enhanced stability provided by a fixed-angle construct.11 Nonetheless, the efficacy of locking plate fixation for treating type B1 and C PPFs remains controversial due to its high failure rates and increased nonunion risk with variable results.12,13

The high failure rates of locking compression plates in treating such PPFs may be because implant stability is only evaluated based on the Vancouver classification. Furthermore, proximally inserted unicortical locking screws might not provide enough resistance to pull out, even if the stem is well-fixed in type B1 and C PPFs. Accordingly, when locking plates are preferred to fix these fractures, we consider that cables should be utilized routinely as a supplement to prevent failure of the construct. This study hypothesized that the hybrid locking plate/cable technique could provide sufficient stability to maintain fracture alignment and achieve bony union in this challenging group of patients.

This study aimed to report mid-to-long-term surgical outcomes of ORIF with a hybrid locking plate/cable technique for managing Vancouver type B1 and type C PPFs in a consecutive group of patients from a single tertiary referral center.

Material and Methods

A retrospective chart review was conducted on all patients who presented with PPFs to our department. As the treatment strategies for these fractures were established based on the Vancouver classification (Table 1), only type B1 or type C fractures were considered for ORIF. Only a hybrid locking plate/cable system was employed for the ORIF of such fractures during the study period. The locking plate can offer enhanced stability provided by a fixed-angle construct, and the strength of the system can be augmented by passing cables around the plate and the bone, particularly over the proximal femur, where the presence of a prosthesis does not allow satisfactory screw fixation.

Inclusion criteria for the study were: a diagnosis of Vancouver type B1 or type C PPF; treatment with a hybrid locking plate/cable system; a minimum follow-up of 36 months; complete medical records and radiographic images; and willingness to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria were: a diagnosis of pathological fracture or malignant neoplasm; coexisting neuromuscular disorders; nonambulatory patients; multi-trauma patients; lost to follow-up; inadequate medical and radiological data; and unwillingness to participate in the study.

Based on the above eligibility criteria, 27 consecutive patients diagnosed with Vancouver type B1 or type C PPF following THA and treated with a hybrid locking plate/cable technique from 2005 to 2016 were identified retrospectively. After excluding 2 patients (one was lost to follow-up and the other had insufficient medical records), the remaining 25 patients (25 PPFs; 17 female, 8 male) who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study and invited to a final follow-up appointment. Before data collection, approval from the Istanbul University’s institutional review board (approval no. 190619/date: 2020) was obtained, and all participants provided informed consent. All the patients analyzed in the study sustained isolated PPF after a low-energy trauma.

Study protocol

Clinical outcome measures

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic database, including age at the time of the primary hip replacement procedure, age at the time of PPF, gender, follow-up durations, and comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, or hypertension). Furthermore, femoral component design (metaphyseal-fitting or diaphyseal-fitting stems) and fixation type (cemented or cementless) were recorded.

The Harris hip score (HHS) was measured at the final follow-up.14 Patients’ functional status was categorized into 4 groups based on the HHS: <70 = poor result; 70-80 = fair; 80-90 = good, and 90-100 = excellent. Intra- and postoperative complications were also recorded.

Radiographic outcome measures

All radiographic assessments were done by the same experienced arthroplasty surgeon who was blinded to patients’ clinical information, hence eliminating inter-observer variability but not intra-observer variability. PPFs were radiographically classified according to the Vancouver classification. On immediate preoperative radiographs at the time of PPF, the stability of cementless femoral stems was evaluated using criteria developed by Engh et al,15 and the stability of cemented stems was assessed using criteria developed by Harris et al.16

In the evaluation of technical aspects of the locking compression plate (LCP) application, the plate span ratio and the plate screw density were calculated on postoperative radiographs. The plate span ratio is used to determine the ideal plate length, which is the quotient of plate length divided by overall fracture length.17 The plate screw density is used to determine the number of screws inserted, which is the quotient formed by the number of screws inserted and the number of plate holes.17

PPF union was defined clinically as the patient’s ability to bear full weight with or without assistance and radiographically as the presence of callus bridging the fracture on both anteroposterior and lateral views. Delayed union, loss of reduction, and malunion were reviewed. Time to union following PPF was also recorded.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to the Vancouver classification and type of fixation regarding the HHS and time to union following ORIF with the hybrid technique.

Operative technique

All surgical procedures were performed under spinal anesthesia by 2 orthopedic surgeons with expertise in arthroplasty and trauma, who have performed 200 cases per year for the last 2 decades in a tertiary referral center. The patient was placed in a lateral decubitus position and draped with an antimicrobial Ioban drape. None of the operations was executed using minimally invasive techniques. Before incision, standard antibiotic prophylaxis was administered (2 g of cefazolin intravenously or clindamycin if the patient was sensitive to penicillin). A straight lateral skin incision was performed, and the vastus lateralis was split to gain access to the femur.

Fracture reduction was performed under direct vision to restore the functional anatomy (length, alignment, and rotation) in meta- and diaphyseal fractures. After the length was corrected by manual traction, the axial alignment was controlled with an image intensifier in both planes. The rotational alignment was then controlled clinically. Later, a hybrid fixation system with a lateral locking compression plate (LCP; Synthes, Switzerland or Smith and Nephew, USA or TST, Türkiye) and cables (Smith and Nephew, USA) were utilized in all the patients to accomplish secure fixation. While applying the LCP, the general techniques described for inserting these devices were strictly followed up.17-19 One of the most critical steps in applying LCP is choosing plates of the proper length, which relies on the fracture pattern and the plate loading. The plate loading, which can be affected by both the plate length and the insertion of screws, should be kept as low as possible to avoid fatigue failure due to cyclic loading. Accordingly, the ideal plate length was based on the plate span ratio recommended as more than 2-3 for comminuted fractures and higher than 8-10 for simple fractures.17 The number of screws inserted was based on the plate screw density empirically recommended as less than 0.5.17

While a 5 mm femur diaphysis straight LCP was applied for the fixation of type B1 PPFs, a 5 mm distal femoral anatomical LCP was used for type C PPFs. In the fixation of type B1 PPFs, the plate was stabilized with a mean of 2 bicortical locked screws (range: 1-3 screws) and a mean of 2 unicortical locked screws (range: 0-3 screws) in the proximal part of the femur. The device was fixed distally with a mean of 4 bicortical screws (range: 4-5 screws). In the fixation of type C PPFs, the plate was fixed proximally with a mean of 4 bicortical screws (range: 2-4 screws) and a mean of 1 unicortical screw (range: 0-3 screws). The device was fixed distally with a mean of 5 bicortical screws (range: 5-7 screws). After placing the proximal and distal screws on the plate, mostly in locked and rarely in cortical mode, the construct stability was augmented by passing a mean of 4 cables (range: 3-5 cables) in type B1 PPFs and a mean of 3 cables (range: 2-3 cables) in type C PPFs around the plate and the bone, particularly over the proximal femur where the presence of a prosthesis does not permit satisfactory screw fixation (Table 2).

No strut or other allografts were used (Figures 1, 2, and 3). After the final fluoroscopy examination, the incision was appropriately sutured in layers.

Postoperative management protocol

Tramadol and paracetamol drugs were administered for postoperative pain management for at least 3 days in the hospital and prescribed after discharge. Antimicrobial prophylaxis with cefazolin was postoperatively administered for 48 hours. Enoxaparin (40 mg) was subcutaneously administered for thrombosis prophylaxis during hospitalization, and after discharge, only oral acetylsalicylic acid (100 mg) was given until full mobilization. Patients were rehabilitated with restricted weight bearing for the first 6 weeks. Patients began progressing to full weight bearing based on their tolerance with 2 crutches or a walker. Range of motion exercises and muscle strength physical therapy were started on the second postoperative day. Patients are generally released from physiotherapy, and using a cane at 3 months is allowed to progress to full activity. All patients were assessed clinically and radiographically every 6 weeks until the ability for painless full weight bearing with or without aids and radiographic evidence of callus formation or bridging trabeculae on both anteroposterior and lateral radiographs.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics such as mean values, standard deviation, and frequencies were used for the sociodemographic data and other clinical variables. The distribution of the variables was measured using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. An independent Student’s t-test was used in the comparative analysis of quantitative independent variables. A paired Student’s t-test was used to compare the score at 2 points for HHS. A P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical outcomes

Twenty-five PPFs of 25 patients (17 female, 8 male) were analyzed. The mean age was 57 ± 16.6 (range, 17-82; 95% CI: 50.27-65.20) years at the time of the primary hip replacement and 64 ± 18.7 (range, 24-88; 95% CI: 56.75-72.03) years at the time of PPF. The mean follow-up was 5.6 ± 3.3 (range, 2-14; 95% CI: 3.10-7.67) years from primary procedure to PPF and 6.5 ± 4.1 (range, 3-15; 95% CI: 4.29-8.49) years following PPF (Table 3).

According to the Vancouver classification, there were 7 type B1 and 18 type C PPFs. The fracture configurations were short oblique in 7 patients, transverse in 9 patients, and long oblique in 9 patients. In the primary THAs, the femoral component was metaphyseal-fitting anatomical femoral stems in 15 hips (5 cemented, 10 cementless) (Bi-Metric™ [Biomet, Inc., Warsaw, IN] in 5 hips]; Synergy™ [Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA] in 6 hips; and Spectron™ [Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA] in 4 hips). Additionally, the femoral component was diaphyseal-fitting stems in 10 hips (5 cemented, 5 cementless) (Echelon™ [Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA]) (Table 3 ).

At the final follow-up, the mean HHS was 71 ± 7.74 (range, 57-89; 95% CI: 68.04-75.25). According to HHS, functional results were poor in 8 patients, fair in 14 patients, and good in 3 patients. No major intra- or postoperative complications were noted. Superficial wound infection was encountered in 2 patients (8%), and both were treated with culture-based antibiotics and dressing (Table 3 ).

Radiographic outcomes

Fracture union was achieved in all patients without complications at an average of 13 ± 4.9 (range, 6-24; 95% CI: 10.93-16.71) weeks. On preoperative radiographs at the time of PPF, all the femoral stems were evaluated as stable based on the criteria of both Eng et al and Harris et al.15-16 No patients suffered from loss of reduction and malunion (Figures 1, 2, and 3). The mean plate screw density was 0.60 ± 0.07 (range, 0.42-0.76; 95% CI: 0.58-0.63), and the mean plate span ratio was 6 ± 1.92 (range, 3-10; 95% CI: 5.28-6.76).

Subgroup analyses

According to the Vancouver classification, the mean time to union was 9 ± 3.09 weeks (range, 6-13; 95% CI: 6.63-11.94) in type B1 patients and 14.47 ± 4.67 weeks (range, 8-24; 95% CI: 12.0818.69) weeks in type C patients. Time to union was significantly shorter in type B1 than in type C PPFs (P = .006). The mean HHS was 70 ± 7.4 (range, 57-79; 95% CI: 61.90-79.66) in type B1 and 69 ± 5.62 (range, 60-80; 95% CI: 65.84-74.04) in type C patients. No significant difference was observed in the HHS between groups (P = .87; post-hoc power: 0.11, and effect size: 0.2). The mean plate screw density was 0.57 ± 0.09 (95% CI: 0.48-0.64) in type B1 patients and 0.62 ± 0.06 (95% CI: 0.59-0.65) in type C patients (P = .196). The mean plate span ratio was 6.14 ± 2.79 (95% CI: 4-8.25) in type B1 patients and 6 ± 1.57 (95% CI: 5.26-6.77) in type C patients (P = .142) (Tables 2 and 4).

There were cemented femoral stems in 10 hips and cementless stems in 15 hips. According to the type of fixation, the mean time to union was 15.5 ± 5.93 (range, 6-24; 95% CI: 10.67-18.03) weeks in the cemented group and 10.86 ± 3.09 (range, 6-15; 95% CI: 8.7613.57) weeks in the uncemented group. Time to union was significantly shorter in the uncemented group compared to the cemented one (P = .017). The mean HHS was 72 ± 7.74 (range, 60-88; 95% CI: 65.78-78.45) in the cemented group and 71 ± 8.01 (range, 57-89; 95% CI: 67.06-79.87) in the uncemented group. No significant difference was determined in the HHS between cemented and uncemented groups (P = .96; post-hoc power: 0.08 and effect size: 0.12). The mean plate screw density was 0.60 ± 0.08 (95% CI: 0.55-0.65) in type B1 patients and 0.61 ± 0.07 (95% CI: 0.57-0.64) in type C patients (P = .343). The mean plate span ratio was 6 ± 2.05 (95% CI: 4.83-7.27) in type B1 patients and 6.06 ± 1.90 (95% CI: 5.11-7) in type C patients (P = .467) (Tables 2 and 4).

Discussion

The success of surgical treatment for PPFs after THA depends on several factors, including bone quality, stem loosening, the patient’s health status, and the surgeon’s experience. Internal fixation is the first-line treatment for PPFs occurring around (type B1) or just below (type C) the tip of a stable femoral stem, as proposed by the Vancouver classification.5,6 The primary issue when performing internal fixation for such fractures is using a proper implant to ensure sufficient proximal fixation in osteoporotic bone without damaging the cement mantle or femoral stem.3,7 Numerous fixation techniques, including lateral plates, screws, wires, cables, allografts, and combinations, have been developed. Nonetheless, to our knowledge, the literature regarding the optimal fixation technique is limited and unclear.8-10 Accordingly, the current study aimed to assess mid- to long-term surgical outcomes of a hybrid locking plate/cable technique in treating Vancouver type B1 and type C PPFs in a consecutive group of patients from a single tertiary referral center.

Cable plate systems have evolved as a standard technique for PPF fixation over the last 3 decades. Since the introduction of the first designs called the Ogden plate (Zimmer, Warsaw, IN) and Dall-Miles plates, several modifications have been developed to optimize plate fixation to the femoral stem using cables or screws.3 Although these conventional cable plate systems could confer favorable clinical outcomes, the number of revisions for nonunion was relatively high.20-22 Locking plates have recently emerged as a viable alternative to conventional plates because of their several advantages, which include minimal disruption of the periosteal blood supply and enhanced stability provided by a fixed-angle construct. In a critical review of 12 clinical studies addressing locking plate fixation for 135 cases of Vancouver B1 PPFs, Graham et al (2013)11 concluded that outcomes could be enhanced if the fracture site was not secured with locking screws, preserving the soft tissue envelope and adding cortical strut grafts or cables with unicortical screws in the case of poor bone quality.

According to our literature review, variable results regarding the efficacy of locking plate fixation for treating type B1 and C PPFs have been reported. Buttaro et al (2007)12 reported a high failure rate (6/14) in their case series of 14 patients with a type B1 PPF treated with a locking compression plate. In a systematic review of 333 patients with type B1 PPFs, Dehghan et al (2014)13 compared the most commonly used fixation strategies. They found that ORIF with locking plates alone had a significantly higher nonunion rate than cable plate or compression plate systems (9% vs. 9%, P = .02). In contrast, in a more recent systematic review (2016) of 1571 cases with PPFs after THA, Stoffel et al23 reported an 11.9-fold increased risk of nonunion for nonlocking plates compared with locking compression plates, despite analyzing all Vancouver fracture forms. In light of the literature mentioned above, we postulate that the reason for the high failure rates of locking compression plates in treating types B1 and C is that implant stability is only evaluated based on the Vancouver classification. Furthermore, proximally inserted unicortical locking screws may not provide adequate pull-out resistance despite their ability to preserve the cement mantle or femoral stem. When locking plates are preferred to fix these fractures, we consider cables should be utilized routinely as a supplement. Our results supported the hypothesis that the hybrid locking plate/cable technique could provide sufficient stability to maintain fracture alignment and achieve bony union in Vancouver type B1 and C PPFs.

Cables have been associated with a “nonbiologic” stigma for several decades, but the findings from the current study have shown that if used appropriately, they can be effective instruments for fixing PPF. Nonetheless, when inserting cables, care must be taken to minimize soft tissue stripping, and they should be supported by unicortical or polyaxial locking screws.11,24 Several published case series indicated favorable outcomes with locking compression plates from various manufacturers for treating type B1 PFFs, with a mean time to fracture recovery varying from 3 to 6 months.22,25-27 In another study including patients with Vancouver type B1 and C fractures, Chakravarthy et al4 reported a mean fracture union time of 4.8 months. In accordance with the literature, in our case series, the fracture union was accomplished for types B1 and C patients at a mean of 9 ± 3.09 (range, 6-13) weeks and 14.47 ± 4.67 weeks (range, 8-24) weeks, respectively.

One of the most critical aspects of this study is the long-term follow-up data presented, which is longer than that of most studies cited above. Another significant and novel aspect is the subgroup analyses examining the effects of Vancouver PPF and fixation types on time to union and functional status. Neither PPF nor fixation type significantly impacted the final follow-up HHS, yet significant relationships were determined regarding union time. Although all the patients achieved clinical and radiographic union after ORIF, cemented and type C PPFs, separately, showed a longer time to union. Furthermore, poor functional results were obtained for 8 patients in the present case series. This may be attributed to the high morbidity of the revision surgery and low functional status before PPF surgery.

When interpreting the results of this study, several significant limitations must be considered. The major limitations were its retrospective nature and limited sample size due to the rarity of PPFs. Another limitation was the lack of a control group of patients with PPFs undergoing internal fixation with a locking compression plate alone. However, our institutional approach was to perform hybrid fixation using cables to obtain sufficient stability. Furthermore, the current study is a surgeon-dependent study where 2 different surgeons performed all the operations. This may have biased the results because of the surgeon’s preference and experience. It’s also important to note that this is a nonrandomized, observational study, meaning there might be several hidden, confounding factors and potential selection biases in the data. Finally, although the HHS used in the study to assess patients’ functional status is a reliable and validated scoring system,28 the reliabilities of the Engh score and Harris classification for the evaluation of femoral stem stability could be debated. Nonetheless, there is evidence in the literature that both systems are reliable when used by a single, experienced arthroplasty surgeon as in the present study.29,30 Despite these limitations, our study is one of few addressing long-term surgical outcomes of hybrid locking plate and cable technique in treating Vancouver type B1 and C PPFs. The data obtained from the study reflect unique observations of a specific group of patients.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that adding cables to the locking plate can provide adequate stability to preserve fracture alignment and accomplish bony union in Vancouver type B1 and C PPFs. With the hybrid locking plate/cable technique, successful clinical and radiographic outcomes with a low complication rate can be expected when treating patients with Vancouver type B1 and C PPFs at long-term follow-up. However, poor functional outcomes can be anticipated in this challenging group of patients. It should also be noted that although union can be achieved by ORIF in such patients, a longer union time may be required for PPFs in the setting of a cemented femoral stem or Vancouver type C. The authors believe the hybrid locking plate/cable technique should be added to the orthopedic surgeon’s armamentarium and considered an effective tool for managing Vancouver type B1 and type C PPFs. This study’s small sample size and retrospective nature limit the generalizability of the results, mainly obtained from subgroup analyses. Thus, future research should be conducted prospectively with larger samples and control groups to address these issues.
