Distinct directional patterns of somatosensory interhemispheric interactions in expert pianists and nonmusicians
Authors: Tatsuya Kato, Masato Hirano, Sachiko Shiotani, Shinichi Furuya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySkilled bimanual actions require the nervous system to process sensory information from both hands while preserving hand-specific information. How interhemispheric somatosensory interactions are organized during bimanual hand movements, and whether their directional patterns differ in individuals with extensive bimanual expertise, remains unclear. Here, we compared bidirectional somatosensory interactions between pianists and nonmusicians using paired-pulse nerve stimulation combined with passive robotic finger movement. In nonmusicians, movement-evoked responses to nondominant-hand movements were reduced when the dominant hand moved simultaneously. Paired-pulse stimulation revealed direction-dependent modulation of early somatosensory evoked responses. Behaviorally, dominant-hand movement selectively impaired perceptual discrimination in the nondominant hand, with interference magnitude correlating with early interhemispheric interactions. In pianists, early responses did not provide evidence for a group-consistent directional asymmetry, whereas perceptual interference occurred for both hands. These findings suggest that expert pianists show reduced group-level directional bias at early somatosensory processing stages, with bilateral interactions emerging at the perceptual level.

Introduction

Humans excel at coordinating their two hands in ways far complex beyond simple mirror movements. Skilled bimanual actions, such as piano performance, require simultaneously coupling the hands to achieve coherent timing while preserving the inter-manual independence needed for rapid, hand-specific movements.1,2,3 To satisfy these opposing demands of unity and independence, the brain relies on intense interhemispheric communication via the corpus callosum, which enables flexible exchange of sensory and motor information between the hemispheres.4,5,6,7,8,9,10,11 Consistent with this view, skilled bimanual performers, such as pianists, show structural enlargement and enhanced functional connectivity of the corpus callosum,12,13,14,15,16,17 suggesting that interhemispheric communication may be organized differently in this population.

In the motor domain, commands generated during bimanual movements must ultimately be routed to each hand without inter-mutual interference, even during tightly coordinated actions.18,19,20 This independence is achieved in part through augmented interhemispheric inhibition between the primary motor cortices,21,22,23 a hallmark of expert bimanual performers such as pianists.17,24 In contrast, the somatosensory system faces a more fundamental challenge. Somatosensory inputs from both hands reach the cortex in tightly overlapping time windows from the outset. These concurrent inputs must be integrated to fine-tune bimanual timing, force, and mutual alignment, yet they must remain distinguishable so that proprioceptive errors can be correctly assigned to the appropriate hand during rapid movements. Moreover, excessive cross-talk or asymmetric sensory communication between hemispheres,25,26,27,28,29,30 particularly when biased toward the dominant hand, could potentially blur hand-specific feedback and affect motor control. Thus, during skilled bimanual movement, the somatosensory system is required to strike a delicate balance, segregating information from each hand while integrating bilateral signals in an unbiased fashion. However, while interhemispheric communication of motor signals has been extensively studied,31,32,33,34,35 it remains poorly understood how bilateral somatosensory inputs from the two hands interact during bimanual movements, whether such interactions show directional bias, and how early cortical interactions relate to perceptual interference between the hands in expert pianists and musically untrained individuals (i.e., nonmusicians).

To examine directional patterns of intermanual somatosensory communication in expert pianists and nonmusicians, we measured early EEG responses during passive finger movement and paired-pulse nerve stimulation, and assessed how contralateral hand movement affected perceptual discrimination. By incorporating passive movements delivered with a custom-built exoskeleton robot hand,36 this approach allowed us to examine somatosensory processing under bimanual movement conditions. This paradigm extends the insights gained from conventional electrical nerve stimulation at rest.37,38,39,40,41 Here, we report that pianists and nonmusicians exhibited distinct patterns of intermanual somatosensory communication. Neurophysiologically, early somatosensory responses to electrical stimuli and passive finger movements revealed significant direction-dependent interhemispheric interaction in nonmusicians. In contrast, pianists did not show evidence for a group-consistent directional asymmetry in these early responses. Behaviorally, contralateral movement impaired perception bilaterally in pianists, whereas in nonmusicians, interference was more evident for nondominant-hand perception. Regression analysis revealed a significant relationship between neurophysiological interhemispheric interaction and behavioral intermanual interference only in nonmusicians. Together, these results suggest reduced group-level directional bias in early somatosensory interhemispheric interactions in expert pianists under the present experimental conditions. The dissociation between early responses and perceptual interference raises the hypothesis that early somatosensory processing may be less directionally biased in expert pianists, while bilateral interactions can still emerge at the perceptual level.

Results

Direction-dependent modulation of movement-evoked responses in nonmusicians but not evident in pianists

We initially investigated interhemispheric interaction in somatosensory processing by comparing cortical responses to passive unimanual and bimanual finger movements, using EEG and a custom-made exoskeleton robot hand (Figure 1). During EEG recordings, each finger was moved passively by the exoskeleton robot hand. A single passive motion consisted of 100 ms of flexion (0–45°) followed by 100 ms of extension (45–0°) of the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint of a finger. Consecutive movements were separated by 250 ms with a slight time jitter (±25 ms), and the passive movements were repeated a total of 250 times per finger in a randomized order. In the unimanual condition, only one hand moved while the other remained at rest; in the bimanual condition, both hands moved with different random sequences. Since EEG responses from consecutive movements largely overlap, we used a regression-based linear deconvolution method to disentangle and reconstruct EEG responses for each finger movement.42 Figure 2 illustrates the impact of deconvolution on the estimated cortical responses (event-related potentials, ERPs) evoked by movements of the right or left index finger during the bimanual condition. The traditional ERP estimation method, which averages epochs aligned to finger movement onsets, yielded atypical waveforms (Figure 2A). These waveforms exhibit phasic fluctuations at approximately 4 Hz, suggesting the presence of overlapping ERPs triggered by temporally close finger movements. Additionally, the bilateral distribution of the initial positive component (P1) topography suggests that simple epoch averaging did not adequately differentiate ERPs elicited by right-versus left-hand movements. In contrast, the linear deconvolution enhanced ERP estimation (Figure 2B). The resulting waveform is free of phasic fluctuations, shows no activity during the pre-stimulus period, and exhibits a lateralized topography. However, if somatosensory information interacts between hemispheres, passive movement of one hand should influence ERPs in the other. We analyzed these data using a linear mixed-effects model. Within-subject factors, condition, hand, and finger, as well as their interactions, were included as fixed effects. Interhemispheric interaction and directional asymmetry during peripheral nerve stimulation in nonmusicians but not evident in pianists

Peripheral electrical nerve stimulation synchronously activates many afferent fibers, yielding somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs) with highly stable early latencies across trials. This temporal precision, especially in a paired-pulse paradigm with an interstimulus interval matching callosal conduction time, allows interhemispheric interactions between somatosensory cortices to be probed more selectively than with movement-evoked responses.37,38,39,40,41 To further probe the neural mechanisms underlying intermanual somatosensory communication, we recorded SEPs elicited by electrical stimulation to the ulnar nerve. The ulnar nerve was chosen because it primarily innervates the intrinsic muscles of the second to fifth digits, which play a key role in MCP joint control43,44 and in fine finger movements such as those required for piano performance. Our previous work demonstrated that pianists show selective adaptation in ulnar- but not median-nerve related somatosensory responses.45 The stimulation consisted of both single pulses and paired pulses, that is, a conditioning stimulus to the opposite hand preceded the test stimulus with a 20-ms interstimulus interval, a latency at which interhemispheric interaction via the corpus callosum is thought to occur.37,38,41 In the rest condition, the exoskeleton robot hand did not move at all; in contrast, in the bimanual condition, electrical pulses were delivered while the robot produced passive bimanual movements (the movements were identical to those described in the previous section). A total of 300 stimuli were delivered for each hand (i.e., 150 single pulses and 150 paired pulses) in both conditions. The SEPs were estimated by using linear deconvolution.

The SEPs exhibited clear early N20-P25 components (see Figure 4A), which are thought to originate from the primary somatosensory cortex. Figure 4B illustrates the peak-to-peak amplitude of these N20-P25 components of the single-pulse SEP across both conditions and hemispheres, where “hemisphere” refers to the side contralateral to the wrist receiving the test stimulus. A linear mixed-effects model (group∗condition∗hemisphere+(1+condition | participant ID), with a Student-t family) revealed a significant main effect of the condition (χ2 = 10.74, p = 0.001) as well as group×hemisphere interaction (χ2 = 8.46, p = 0.004) on the single-pulse SEP amplitude. We evaluated interhemispheric somatosensory communication (IHC) by subtracting the N20-P25 peak-to-peak amplitudes of the single-pulse SEP from those of the paired-pulse SEP (Figure 4C).37 The IHC was tested using a linear mixed-effects model with a Student-t error distribution. The model included fixed effects of group, direction (i.e., dominant-to-nondominant and vice versa), condition, and their interactions with a covariate for single-pulse SEP amplitude and a random intercept for participant (here, we did not include the random slopes of the within-subject factors since including those effects led to a singular fit). The analysis revealed a significant group × direction interaction (χ2 = 10.66, p = 0.001) as well as a main effect of the condition (χ2 = 4.80, p = 0.028). Because the group × direction interaction was significant, post hoc comparisons were conducted after marginalizing over conditions. Post hoc tests revealed a significant direction-wise difference in the IHC in nonmusicians (estimated difference: −0.420, CI [−0.686–0.155], p = 0.002) but not in pianists (0.14, CI [−0.091–0.370], p = 0.370). We further found a significant (and a trend of) group difference in the IHC (ND-to-D: −0.335, CI [−0.590–0.079], p = 0.010; D-to-ND: 0.225, CI [−0.025–0.475], p = 0.078). A significant difference in the amplitude between the single-pulse and paired-pulse conditions was evident only in nonmusicians (rest condition: D-to-ND, 0.402, CI [0.115–0.689], p = 0.006; ND-to-D, 0.063, CI [−0.216–0.341], p = 0.660; bimanual condition: D-to-ND, 0.145, CI [−0.010–0.390], p = 0.244; ND-to-D, −0.355, CI [−0.604–0.107], p = 0.0051) but not in pianists (rest condition: D-to-ND, 0.218, CI [−0.027–0.463], p = 0.081; ND-to-D, 0.205, CI [–0.019–0.429], p = 0.073; bimanual condition: D-to-ND, −0.12, CI [−0.343–0.103], p = 0.291; ND-to-D, −0.172, CI [–0.068–0.412], p = 0.161). We further assessed the stability of the group × direction effect using a leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) analysis. In each iteration, one participant was removed, the same robust mixed-effects model was refitted, and the group × direction coefficient was extracted. The coefficient retained the same sign in all refits, suggesting that the observed effect was not attributable to any single participant. At the group level, nonmusicians showed direction-dependent differences in interhemispheric interactions, with significant differences between D-to-ND and ND-to-D. By contrast, in pianists, paired stimulation did not significantly modulate SEP amplitudes relative to single stimulation in either direction, providing no clear evidence for a group-consistent directional asymmetry in this measure (see Figure S1).

Perceptual integration of bilateral somatosensory information

To examine whether intermanual somatosensory communication is reflected in perceptual performance during bimanual movement, we measured somatosensory discrimination thresholds during robot-induced passive finger movements. Participants wore the exoskeleton robot hands on both sides, which passively flexed the MCP joints and delivered one of two predetermined five-finger sequences (A: 5-4-2-3-1, B: 5-3-2-4-1; numbers represent thumb to little finger). After each passive sequence, participants performed a two-alternative forced-choice task (A vs. B) using foot buttons. The inter-movement interval (i.e., sequence tempo) was adaptively adjusted to estimate the discrimination threshold at 80% accuracy. Each hand was tested in separate sessions. In each hand, there were two conditions: an unimanual condition, where the passive sequential movements were delivered only to the target hand, and an interference condition, where the contralateral hand received randomized passive finger movements in addition to the target-hand movements. Each condition consisted of 50 trials, for a total of 100 trials in each session. The order of the two conditions was randomized in a trial-by-trial manner. The session order was counterbalanced, with a brief break between hands. If somatosensory processing is separate between hands, perception remains unchanged regardless of interference from the contralateral hand. Conversely, if intermanual somatosensory communication occurs, perception during interference would be impaired compared to the unimanual condition.

We analyzed the discrimination threshold using a linear mixed-effects model (threshold ∼ group×condition×hand+(1+hand+condition|participantID), with a Student-t error distribution). The model revealed a significant main effect of Condition (χ2 = 13.17, p = 0.0002) and a significant group × hand interaction (χ2 = 5.536, p = 0.018). In contrast, the three-way interaction between group, hand, and condition did not reach the conventional level of significance (χ2 = 3.484, p = 0.062), and the group × condition interaction was also not significant (χ2 = 2.745, p = 0.097). Because the pattern of results was complex, with a trend toward a three-way group × hand × condition interaction, together with a significant group × hand interaction and a significant main effect of condition, we conducted post hoc analyses to further characterize the effect of condition within each group × hand combination. Specifically, we tested the effect of condition separately for each hand in each group. In nonmusicians, simultaneous contralateral movements specifically impaired discrimination for the nondominant hand (Figure 5A right; estimated difference: −0.161, CI [−0.254–0.067], p = 0.0008, unimanual:149.64 ± 58.59 ms, bimanual: 207.88 ± 75.33 ms), while perceptual discrimination threshold with the dominant hand remained consistent across conditions (−0.072, CI [−0.171–0.028], p = 0.157, unimanual:239.85 ± 222.75 ms, bimanual: 214.17 ± 103.06 ms). In contrast to the neurophysiological findings, pianists showed interference effects on discrimination performance for both hands (Figure 5A left; D-hand: −0.19, CI [−0.298–0.089], p = 0.0003, unimanual:95.92 ± 43.17 ms, bimanual: 149.00 ± 77.09 ms; ND-hand: −0.111, CI [-0.210–0.012], p = 0.0288, unimanual:119.68 ± 52.76 ms, bimanual: 174.60 ± 142.77 ms). To assess whether these behavioral effects were driven by individual participants, we performed within-group leave-one-subject-out analyses using Gaussian mixed-effects models with the same fixed- and random-effects structure (see Figure S2). In each refit, we excluded one participant from the corresponding group and extracted the condition effect from estimated marginal means within each group × hand combination. In nonmusicians, the 95% CI for the condition effect included zero in all 15 refits for the dominant hand, but was consistently negative in all 15 refits for the nondominant hand. In pianists, the condition effect was consistently negative in all 15 refits for the dominant hand. For the nondominant hand, the point estimate remained negative in all refits, and the 95% CI was negative in 14 of 15 refits; in the remaining refit, the CI only marginally included zero (estimate = −0.103, 95% CI [−0.207, 0.001]). Thus, the LOSO analysis supported the stability of the main behavioral pattern. These findings indicate asymmetric perceptual interference in nonmusicians, with a larger effect on nondominant-hand perception, broadly paralleling the directionality observed in early somatosensory responses. Finally, we examined the relationship between behavioral and neural measures of intermanual somatosensory communication. The difference in the discrimination threshold between the interference and unimanual conditions (i.e., ΔPT) was modeled according to the average difference in the P1 amplitude of ERP evoked by the passive finger movements (average across all fingers) between the bimanual and unimanual conditions (i.e., IHCPM, see Figure 3), and the difference in the N20-P25 amplitudes between the paired-pulse and single-pulse SEP evoked by the nerve stimulation in both rest and bimanual conditions (i.e., IHCES_rest, IHCES_bimanual, see Figure 4). We also added a categorical predictor for the hand to remove the effect of D- or ND-hand on the ΔPT and a random intercept for participant, as the data included two points per participant. Thus, the overall model was as follows in the Wilkinson formula: ΔPT ∼ IHCPM + IHCES_rest + IHCES_bimanual + hand + (1|participant). To reduce the influence of outliers on the regression, we used a Student-t error distribution (ν = 4). The model was created separately for each group. In nonmusicians, we found that IHCES_Bimanual (coefficient = −0.248, CI [−0.486–0.102], p = 0.041), as well as the hand (coefficient = 0.499, CI [0.0728–0.926], p = 0.022), were significant predictors of the ΔPT, while neither IHCPM (coefficient = −0.159, CI [−0.501–0.182], p = 0.691) nor IHCES_rest (coefficient = 0.184, CI [−0.017–0.386], p = 0.072) showed a significant effect. By contrast, in pianists, none of these factors significantly explained the ΔPT (hand: coefficient = −0.101, CI [−0.632–0.43], p = 0.708; IHCPM: coefficient = 0.213, CI [−0.022–0.448], p = 0.076; IHCES_rest: coefficient = −0.157, CI [−0.440–0.126], p = 0.277; IHCES_bimanual: coefficient = 0.195, CI [−0.081–0.471], p = 0.166). Figure 5B showed the relationship between IHCES_bimanual and the ΔPT for each hand and each group, with other variables held at their average. We further assessed the stability of the IHCES_bimanual–ΔPT relationship using LOSO analyses with Gaussian mixed-effects models. In each refit, one participant from the corresponding group was excluded, and the coefficient for IHCES_bimanual was extracted (see Figure S3). In nonmusicians, the coefficient remained negative in all 15 refits, and its 95% CI was negative in 13 of 15 refits; in the remaining two refits, the CI only marginally included zero, while the point estimates remained negative. In pianists, the 95% CI included zero in all 15 refits. Thus, the association between IHCES_bimanual and ΔPT was relatively stable in nonmusicians but not evident in pianists. These findings suggest that, in nonmusicians, variation in paired-pulse SEP modulation during bimanual movement was associated with variation in behavioral somatosensory interference. In pianists, this association was not evident under the present experimental conditions.

Discussion

Our findings reveal distinct features of intermanual somatosensory processing in expert pianists and nonmusicians at both neural and behavioral levels. By combining movement-evoked and stimulation-evoked EEG responses with a behavioral perception task, we found a dissociation between the two groups. Nonmusicians showed group-consistent directional bias in early somatosensory processing and perceptual interference, whereas pianists did not show clear evidence for group-level paired-pulse modulation or directional bias in early somatosensory responses, despite bilateral perceptual interference. This pattern raises the possibility that early somatosensory processing in expert pianists is less directionally biased, while bilateral interactions can still emerge at the perceptual level.

Early somatosensory interhemispheric communication and its directional biases

A central methodological strength of this study is the use of a custom-built exoskeleton robot (Furuya et al., 2025), which enabled us to examine interhemispheric somatosensory communication (IHC) not only during conventional peripheral nerve stimulation at rest, but also during passive movements and movement-embedded electrical stimulation. This matters because real bimanual behaviors are temporally dense: somatosensory events occur in rapid succession and inevitably overlap in time. The combination of bimanual passive movements and a paired-pulse stimulation paradigm is well-suited for probing callosal interactions between primary somatosensory cortices (S1), as supported by prior work showing that paired-pulse effects at around 20 ms have been used to probe interactions compatible with transcallosal transmission times.38 Importantly, whereas previous research typically assessed IHC in only one direction (dominant to nondominant or vice versa),37,38,39,40,41 our bilateral stimulation and movement design allowed us to characterize bidirectional patterns of interhemispheric interactions in expert pianists and nonmusicians.

In nonmusicians, both movement-evoked responses and SEPs showed directional-dependent effects. Specifically, movement-evoked responses showed reduced responses to nondominant-hand movement during simultaneous dominant-hand movement, while paired-pulse SEPs showed direction-dependent modulation between the two stimulation directions. This directional imbalance is comparable to previously reported dominance-related asymmetries in motor and sensory domains. For example, Duque and colleagues showed that interhemispheric inhibition between primary motor cortices becomes asymmetric immediately before initiating a dominant-hand movement, but not a nondominant-hand movement, suggesting that manual dominance shapes the directionality of interhemispheric interactions.46 For early somatosensory responses, some studies have reported differences between stimulation of the dominant and nondominant hands,25,27 although others have found no such laterality effects.28,29 In our data, the N20–P25 amplitudes of the contralateral hemisphere did not differ when stimulating the dominant versus the nondominant hand. A similar pattern was observed for movement-evoked responses. The lack of clear differences in unilateral responses suggests that these directional effects are unlikely to arise solely from differences in hemispheric responsiveness. Such direction-dependent processing during bimanual actions aligns with everyday behavioral experience: the dominant hand typically performs more precise or fine-grained operations, whereas the nondominant hand often provides complementary stabilization (e.g., peeling an apple). This suggests that the observed neural asymmetry may support functional specializations that emerge from habitual manual use.

Pianists, by contrast, did not show evidence for group-consistent directional modulation in early somatosensory responses. Given that pianists exhibit greater callosal structural volume and enhanced interhemispheric functional connectivity,12,13,14,15,16,17 this pattern should not be interpreted simply as reduced anatomical and functional connectivity. Instead, our findings indicate a distinct pattern of somatosensory processing, with one possible interpretation being that early interhemispheric interactions are more strongly gated or less consistently direction-biased at the group level. Such a mechanism, if confirmed, could help maintain hand-specific somatosensory information during rapid bimanual actions.

Perceptual integration of bilateral somatosensory information

Behavioral results complemented these neurophysiological findings. In nonmusicians, contralateral random finger movements impaired perceptual discrimination for the nondominant hand but not the dominant hand, mirroring the directionality observed at the physiological IHC pattern. This pattern suggests that dominance-weighted intermanual influence is expressed not only in neural responses but also in perceptual consequences. Moreover, regression analyses showed that variation in paired-pulse SEP modulation during bimanual movement was associated with variation in perceptual interference, suggesting a relationship between early interhemispheric interactions and later somatosensory perception.

In pianists, perceptual interference emerged for both hands, despite the absence of evidence for group-consistent interhemispheric interactions and directional asymmetry in early physiological responses. This dissociation raises the hypothesis that reduced group-level directional bias in early responses does not preclude bilateral interactions at the perceptual level. One possible interpretation is a hierarchical account in which early S1 responses may preserve hand-specific information (i.e., minimizing low-level cross-talk), while integration of inputs from both hands may occur at subsequent processing stages to support unified perceptual outcomes (Figure 6). Conceptual hypothesis of somatosensory processing during unimanual and bimanual movements

During unimanual movements, somatosensory inputs from each hand are processed independently and give rise to hand-specific perception. During bimanual movements, inputs from the two hands are integrated after initial processing. In nonmusicians, asymmetric interhemispheric interactions arise at these early stages via the corpus callosum, leading to degraded perception of ND-hand movements. In pianists, early responses did not provide clear group-level evidence for interhemispheric interactions or directional bias in the present measures, while bilateral perceptual interference suggests that interactions between the two hands may emerge at later perceptual stages.

This hierarchical account may help explain the group difference in the directionality of interference. Nonmusicians exhibit asymmetric perceptual interference, in which early-stage modulation of nondominant-hand responses may contribute to dominance-weighted perceptual representations. Pianists, in contrast, show less group-consistent interhemispheric interaction and directional bias in early responses while still showing bilateral perceptual interference (Figure 6). Notably, this account does not assume group differences in the perceptual processing of somatosensory inputs; instead, perceptual outcomes may be related to differences in early-stage signal modulation. Given that no significant relationship was found between interhemispheric interactions in somatosensory perception during bilateral movements and performance on perceptual discrimination tasks, it cannot be ruled out that pianists may have a different somatosensory perception compared to non-musicians.

In summary, our findings show that interhemispheric processing of somatosensory inputs differs between expert pianists and nonmusicians in the present cohort. In nonmusicians, somatosensory interhemispheric communication is directionally biased, with effects more evident for nondominant-hand responses and perception during bimanual interference. In contrast, pianists did not show evidence of group-consistent directional asymmetry in early responses; nevertheless, bilateral perceptual interference suggests that interactions between the hands can emerge at the behavioral level. These findings not only provide a framework for studying sensory lateralization but also highlight expert pianists as a model for probing variations in somatosensory processing.

Limitations of the study

The sample size was modest, data were collected at a single center, and the pianist cohort represents a specialized population; therefore, the generalizability of the findings requires validation in larger and independent cohorts.

Although the present study provides a comprehensive characterization of IHC across multiple somatosensory inputs, several limitations warrant future investigation. First, movement-related sensory gating may have contributed to the observed effects. However, we did not observe systematic differences in SEP amplitude or gating between D and ND hands, and we included SEP amplitude as a covariate in the LME to account for its potential influence on the results. These considerations suggest that such effects are unlikely to be the primary driver of our findings, although they cannot be entirely excluded. Second, our neural measurements primarily capture interactions at the level of S1. Higher-order somatosensory and parietal regions, which are central to sensory integration and perceptual decision-making,47,48,49,50,51 were not directly assessed. Future research on experimental/analytical designs capable of illuminating sensory processing with later latencies could be critical for clarifying how the integration of bimanual information unfolds across processing stages.

Last but not least, an interesting open question concerns how intermanual somatosensory communication varies with individual traits and task-specific factors. For example, individuals with more balanced use of the two hands, such as ambidextrous populations, may exhibit reduced asymmetry in interhemispheric interactions; however, whether such patterns resemble those observed in expert pianists remains unclear. In addition, different domains of expertise impose distinct sensorimotor demands across the two hands. For instance, in string instrument performance, the non-dominant hand is often required to achieve high spatial and force precision, which may lead to relatively enhanced somatosensory processing associated with that hand,17,52 whereas piano performance requires more balanced and interdependent bimanual control. Similarly, other expert populations such as esports players, who engage in asymmetric yet highly coordinated bimanual actions, may exhibit distinct patterns of interhemispheric interaction depending on task-specific requirements. Finally, longitudinal training designs would provide an important framework to further characterize whether these interaction patterns change with sensorimotor experience. Together, these approaches may help dissociate the contributions of hand-use balance and task-specific demands to intermanual somatosensory communication.

Experimental model and study participant details

Fifteen healthy pianists (10 females, 22.1±2.6 y/o, mean±SD) and fifteen healthy nonmusicians (8 females, 22.9±2.7 y/o, mean±SD) participated in this study. All pianists majored in piano performance at a musical conservatory and/or had extensive and continuous private piano training under the supervision of professional pianists and/or piano professors. Nonmusicians had no experience of practicing/playing any musical instrument for more than five years. The sample size (n = 15 per group) was determined based on our previous studies in similar paradigms,36,45,59 in which comparable group differences between pianists and nonmusicians were detected with samples of approximately this size. We acknowledge that this sample size is modest, and therefore complemented the primary analyses with robust mixed-effects models and leave-one-subject-out stability analyses for the main effects. All participants gave their written informed consent before participating in this study. All experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the ethics committees of the Sony Corporation (approval no. 22-18-001). To account for handedness effects, all data were realigned based on individual participants’ dominant hand (D-hand), with one pianist and one nonmusician being left-handed and the others right-handed. This reclassification defined two hemispheric categories: the dominant hemisphere (D-hemisphere, contralateral to the dominant hand (D-hand)) and the nondominant hemisphere (ND-hemisphere, contralateral to the nondominant hand (ND-hand)).

Method details

Neurophysiological experiments

General procedure

This experiment aimed to quantitatively assess interhemispheric interaction of somatosensory processing by combining an custom-build exoskeleton robot hand with electrical stimulation. This approach enables us to investigate bilateral somatosensory processing during controlled passive bimanual movement. EEG recordings were conducted under four different experimental settings: rest, passive D-hand, passive ND-hand, and passive bimanual movements. Each setting comprised five separate sessions, and the order of all sessions across the four settings was randomized across participants. In the rest setting, participants kept relaxed while the robot held the MCP joints of the fingers at 0 degrees of flexion. During the passive movement settings, the robot passively moved the fingers in either the D- or ND-hand, or in both hands while participants remained at rest. The finger order was randomized, and the inter-stimulus interval was set at 4 Hz (i.e., 250 ms). In each recording session, electrical stimulation was delivered to the ulnar nerves of both D- and ND-hands at 2 Hz, concurrently with finger movements during the passive movement sessions (Figure 1). The timing of the passive movement and electrical stimulation was synchronized to the shared system clock, but an intentional temporal jitter (±25 ms) was introduced independently for each modality (see the relevant sections below for details). This temporal jitter was designed to enhance the separation of overlapping responses in subsequent linear deconvolution analysis, as described in detail below.

EEG recording and preprocessing

EEG signals were recorded from the entire head using 64 electrodes, according to the international 10/20 system (actiCHamp plus and actiCAP snap, BrainProducts, Germany; impedance <10 kΩ). Participants were instructed to remain relaxed and keep their eyes closed throughout the EEG recordings in order to minimize visual input and potential gaze-related influences on somatosensory responses. The reference electrode was placed at Fz, and the sampling rate was set to 1 kHz. Offline analysis was performed using EEGLAB53 and a custom-made MATLAB script. For offline analysis, the EEG signals were preprocessed through high-pass filtering at 1 Hz, line noise removal (EEGLAB cleanLineNoise function), and the removal of bad channels that had channel-wise correlations with all other channels lower than 0.85 (EEGLAB clean_artifact function). Subsequently, we applied artifact subspace reconstruction (ASR)54 to further clean the data. For ASR, we used the clean_artifact function implemented in EEGLAB (burst criterion: 20; burst rejection: on, flatline criterion: 5, and window criterion: 0.25). After applying the ASR, the EEG signals were interpolated and re-referenced to the common average. The data were then submitted to independent component analysis (ICA) using an adaptive mixture independent component analysis (AMICA) algorithm. This step was preceded by a principal component analysis, which reduced the rank of the dataset according to the number of interpolated channels. Components extracted from the ICA were classified using a machine learning algorithm implemented in the EEGLAB IClabel function.55 We removed components for which the probability of muscle activity, eye movement, heartbeat, line noise, or channel noise exceeded 70%.

Passive movement stimulation

The robot hand passively moved the fingers in a randomized order at an inter-movement interval of 250 ms (i.e., 4 Hz) with a jitter of ±25 ms. During the recording session, each passive movement involved the flexion of the MCP joint from 0 to 45 degrees over a duration of 100 ms, followed immediately by the extension of the same joint back to 0 degrees, also taking another 100 ms. In each recording session, each finger movement was performed 50 times in randomized order (i.e., 250 finger movements per session across five fingers), resulting in a total of 250 movements per finger across the five sessions. For the unimanual passive movements, one of the robot hands moved the fingers while the other fixed the MCP joints of the fingers in a neutral position at 0 degrees of flexion. For the bimanual passive movement, both robot hands passively moved the fingers of both hands with an independent randomized finger sequence and independent jitter.

Electrical nerve stimulation

An electrical pulse (a rectangular pulse of 1 ms duration) was applied to the ulnar nerve at the wrist of each hand using a constant current stimulator (DS4, Digitimer, Inc. and DS7A, Digitimer, Inc.) via a paired bar-type electrode in order to evoke somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs) and assess somatosensory interhemispheric communication (IHC). The ulnar nerve was selected because it mainly innervates the hand intrinsic muscles corresponding to the 2nd–5th digits, which play a key role in MCP joint control.43,44 Our previous work45 also demonstrated expertise-related plasticity in somatosensory functions, specifically in the ulnar nerve, but not in the median nerve. The stimulus intensity was set at 0.9 times the motor threshold, the lowest electrical current intensity that evokes a visible twitch of the first dorsal interosseous muscle, measured before the experiment for each participant and each hand (pianists, R-hand: 2.66±0.98mA, L-hand: 3.00±1.54mA; nonmusicians, R-hand: 3.40±1.17mA, L-hand: 3.98±1.93mA, mean±SD). There were four stimulation types: (1) single-pulse stimulation of the D-hand, (2) single-pulse stimulation of the ND-hand, (3) paired-pulse stimulation with the ND-hand as the conditioning and the D-hand as the test (ND-to-D), and (4) paired-pulse stimulation with the D-hand as the conditioning and the ND-hand as the test (D-to-ND). In paired-pulse stimulation, the inter-stimulus interval between the first and second stimuli was set at 20 ms, based on previous studies using this interval to probe interhemispheric somatosensory interactions.37,38 A single recording session included 30 stimuli for each type (i.e., a total of 150 stimuli were applied for each type over five sessions), and the stimulus interval was set at a mean of 500 ms (i.e., 2 Hz) with a jitter of ±25 ms.

Linear deconvolution

To address the temporal overlap of cortical responses evoked by electrical stimuli and passive finger movements, EEG signals were analyzed using a regression-based linear deconvolution approach. The detailed mechanism of this method is described in the previous paper.42 In brief, this method models continuous EEG data as a linear summation of event-related responses, allowing isolation of ERPs generated by passive finger movements and SEPs elicited by electrical stimulation. The design matrix included two primary event categories: (1) passive movement of individual fingers (i.e., thumb, index, middle, ring, and little), and (2) electrical stimulation under the four stimulation types described above. Each predictor was temporally expanded within defined windows: passive movement responses were modeled from -0.1 to 0.6 s relative to movement onset, while electrical stimulation responses were captured from -0.05 to 0.2 s relative to the stimulus onset. Typically, the duration of the response evoked by passive movement was longer than that evoked by electrical stimulation, so the time window was set longer for ERPs than that for SEPs. The window size was determined based on our previous study.45 Data from the five repeated sessions per stimulation and movement condition were concatenated in time prior to deconvolution.

ERP and SEP analysis

For analysis of ERPs evoked by passive finger movements, the peak amplitude of the first positive component was measured (i.e., the maximum value within a 50-200 ms window relative to the movement onset), which typically occurred approximately 100 ms after finger movement onset. For visualization purposes, a topographical map of the group-averaged ERP was obtained using the “topoplot” function implemented in the EEGLAB. For the SEP, the peak-to-peak amplitudes between the N20 and P25 components were calculated for both single-pulse and paired-pulse conditions. To reduce potential contamination from volume conduction artifacts in paired-pulse responses, single-pulse SEP waveforms were temporally aligned and subtracted from their paired-pulse counterparts. For example, paired-pulse SEP in the D-to-ND condition was corrected by subtracting single-pulse SEP evoked by isolated D-hand stimulation.37,38 IHC was quantified as the difference in N20-P25 amplitudes between paired-pulse and single-pulse conditions (IHC = paired-pulse amplitude - single-pulse amplitude). Positive and negative IHC values indicate facilitation-like and suppression-like SEP modulation, respectively. For both SEPs, signals were averaged across left-hemisphere electrodes (CP3/C3) and right-hemisphere electrodes (CP4/C4) to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio. Additionally, we applied a high-pass filter at 20 Hz to correct for slow drifts induced by residuals of passive finger movements.

Perceptual test

We assessed the threshold of the inter-movement interval (i.e., the time interval between the onsets of successive finger movements) at which participants could discriminate between two sequences. Participants wore the exoskeleton robot hand36 on both hands. The robot hands were designed to passively move the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints of the fingers. During the test, the robot passively flexed the finger joints in one of the predetermined sequences (5-4-2-3-1 or 5-3-2-4-1; 1: thumb, 2: index finger, 3: middle finger, 4: ring finger, 5: little finger) at a constant angular velocity of 450°/s. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed. After the passive movement, participants were asked to press one of the two foot buttons to identify the sequence in which the robot hand had moved their fingers (two-alternative forced-choice task). The inter-movement interval varied across trials. The time interval was determined using the ZEST method60 to measure the perceptual discrimination threshold. Specifically, the threshold was defined as the time interval at which participants could discriminate the sequence order with 80% accuracy. A Gaussian distribution with a mean of 150 ms and a standard deviation of 3 was used as the prior distribution. The psychometric function was modelled using a Weibull function of the formy=δ·γ+(1−δ)·(1−(1−γ)·exp(−10∧(β·(x−x_threshold)))),where x represents log10 contrast relative to threshold, γ is corresponding to the chance level (0.5), δ (the fraction of trials on which the observer responds blindly) was set to 0.02, and x_threshold was chosen such that the function reached y = 0.8 at threshold. During the ZEST procedure, the slope parameter β was set to 3.5. After each trial, the probability density function was updated based on the participant’s discrimination response and the time interval used in that trial. The time interval at the next trial was then determined as the mean of the updated posterior probability density function. After the experiment, we recomputed the posterior probability density functions with different slope values (β ranging from 0.5 to 10 in steps of 0.01) to identify the best-fitting model accounting for the data, and computed the threshold based on this model.

The perceptual test consisted of two conditions: (1) the unimanual condition, in which only one hand was moved by the robot, and (2) the interference condition, in which the contralateral hand fingers were also flexed in a randomized order simultaneously with the test hand. The perceptual test was conducted for the D- and ND-hands in separate sessions. Prior to the test, participants were instructed to focus exclusively on identifying the movements of the hand being tested. They were explicitly informed that the opposite hand might occasionally move during the task (i.e., the interference condition); however, they were advised to disregard such movements and continue to focus solely on the designated hand. We hypothesized that intermanual somatosensory interactions would be reflected in a deterioration of perceptual threshold in the interference condition compared with the unimanual condition. Thus, the degree of deterioration was used as a behavioral index of somatosensory interhemispheric communication. Each condition (unimanual, interference) included 50 trials presented in a randomized order. Before the test, participants practiced five trials for each condition (10 trials in total) with a fixed tempo (2Hz, i.e., a 500 ms interval) and were informed whether their response was correct or incorrect. No feedback was provided during the main test. The D- and ND-hand sessions were separated by a 5-minute break, and the order of the tested hands was counterbalanced across participants.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using linear mixed-effects models implemented in the glmmTMB package in R56 (https://www.r-project.org), using a Student-t error distribution (ν = 4) with an identity link to reduce sensitivity to outliers. We treated the group (i.e., pianists and nonmusicians), direction (or just hemisphere/hand), condition (unimanual and bimanual conditions for the ERPs; rest and bimanual conditions for SEPs; unimanual and interference conditions for the perception test), and interaction between these factors as the fixed effects. The random-effects structure included random intercepts for participants, as well as random slopes for within-subject factors at the participant level. When the maximal random-effects structure led to convergence problems or singular fits, the random-effects structure was simplified by removing the variance components responsible for the instability, and the most complex converging model was retained for inference. Type-III Wald chi-square tests were used to assess fixed effects using the ANOVA function implemented in the car package.57 Multiple comparisons were performed based on the estimated marginal means calculated using the emmeans package.58 The degrees of freedom of these comparisons were computed using the Kenward-Roger method, while p-values were adjusted using Tukey’s method. For key group-level effects and neural-behavioral associations, we additionally performed leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) analyses by refitting the same model after excluding one participant at a time and assessing the stability of the relevant coefficient or estimated marginal contrast.
