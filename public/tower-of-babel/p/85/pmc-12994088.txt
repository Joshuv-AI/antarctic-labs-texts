Comparative study of thermal and non-thermal sterilization on the physicochemical properties, microstructure, texture and flavor quality of avocado purée
Authors: Famei Cai, Muhammad Aaqil, Li Zhuang, Zekun Shi, Ruobing Cheng, Yujie Zi, Yang Tian, Cunchao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study compared five sterilization methods; pasteurization (P; 90 °C, 5 min), microwave sterilization (MS; 600 W, 3 min), high-pressure processing (HPP; 450 MPa, 10 min), gamma irradiation (GI; 8 kGy), and electron-beam irradiation (EBI; 10 kGy) to evaluate their effects on the physicochemical, microstructure, texture, volatile profile, and microbial safety of avocado purée (AP). All treatments ensured microbial safety and increased free polyphenols by 5.0–92.86%, with vitamin C retention ranging from 17.93% to 93.82%. Thermal treatments (P, MS) caused microstructural disruption, enlarging particle size, increasing hardness and adhesiveness. In contrast, non-thermal methods better preserve microstructural integrity and textural stability. Volatile analysis showed substantial aroma losses after P and MS, whereas MS and EBI enhanced fruity–floral notes, and HPP and GI retained the characteristic fresh avocado aroma. Overall, non-thermal treatments, particularly HPP and EBI provided superior quality retention, demonstrating strong potential for high-value industrial processing of avocado purée.

Introduction

Yunnan Province is the leading avocado-producing region in China and constitutes the central hub of the country's avocado industry. Avocados are widely appreciated for their creamy texture and distinctive flavor profile, and they contain substantial levels of unsaturated fatty acids, dietary fiber, potassium, vitamins, and numerous phenolic compounds (Salazar-López et al., 2020). Owing to these nutritional attributes, avocados exhibit multiple health-promoting functions, including cardiovascular protection, modulation of gut microbiota and gastrointestinal health, and notable antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities (James-Martin, Brooker, Hendrie, & Stonehouse, 2024; Thompson et al., 2021). With the rising global interest in healthy diets and functional food ingredients, consumer demand for avocado-based products has expanded considerably in recent years. However, the ready-to-eat nature and rapid postharvest deterioration of fresh avocados restrict their processing versatility. Transforming avocados into purée offers a practical solution, enabling their incorporation into bakery items, beverages, infant foods, and ready-to-eat formulations. Within this processing chain, sterilization is a critical unit operation, as it ensures microbial safety while simultaneously influencing the final microstructure and textural properties. For example, thermal treatments may alter viscosity and stability through pectin solubilization or degradation and induce substantial microstructural modifications, whereas non-thermal approaches such as high-pressure processing (HPP) can achieve microbial inactivation with minimal disruption to particle integrity, thereby retaining fresh-like characteristics (Xing et al., 2023). Consequently, identifying a sterilization strategy that guarantees safety while preserving nutritional and sensory quality is essential for producing high-quality avocado purée (AP).

In traditional thermal processing, pasteurization (P) remains one of the most widely applied preservation techniques in the fruit and vegetable juice/beverage industry due to its proven effectiveness in achieving microbial and enzymatic inactivation (Petruzzi, Campaniello, Speranza, Corbo, Sinigaglia, & Bevilacqua, 2017). Within the framework of HACCP systems, key processing parameters, particularly time–temperature combinations can be precisely monitored and validated as critical control limits to ensure product safety and regulatory compliance (Duan et al., 2023). However, despite its microbiological efficacy, thermal processing may adversely affect product quality. Heat exposure can promote degradation of natural pigments (e.g., chlorophylls and carotenoids), accelerate lipid oxidation, and modify volatile aroma compound profiles, ultimately leading to undesirable changes in color attributes and sensory characteristics (Polak, Kalisz, & Kruszewski, 2024). Microwave sterilization (MS), by contrast, induces rapid volumetric heating through the oscillation of polar molecules within the microwave field, allowing efficient microbial inactivation while potentially better preserving nutritional quality. However, issues related to heating uniformity, spatial temperature gradients, and batch-to-batch variability still warrant systematic evaluation. Over the past decade, non-thermal sterilization technologies, including HPP and ionizing radiation methods such as gamma irradiation (GI) and electron-beam irradiation (EBI) have attracted increasing attention for their ability to better maintain product color, flavor, and structural integrity. HPP inactivates microorganisms through isostatic pressure at near-ambient temperatures, thereby reducing the extent of lipid oxidation and facilitating retention of fresh flavor notes (Wei, Lee, Kang, Tsai, & Arakawa, 2025). Ionizing radiation, including GI and EBI, destroys microbial DNA by generating ion pairs and reactive species, achieving effective sterilization while minimizing heat-induced degradation (Chaudhary, Kumar, Kumar, Singh, & Dhiman, 2024). Previous studies have shown that ionizing irradiation not only ensures microbial safety but also supports the preservation of nutritional components, color stability, and sensory attributes (Yoon et al., 2022). These technological advances provide diverse routes for developing green, safe, and high-quality fruit and vegetable products.

Sterilization method selection greatly influences the nutritional composition, textural attributes, and sensory characteristics of plant-based products. Thermal sterilization can modify fatty acid composition, increasing unsaturated fatty acids and intensifying bitterness in avocado pulp (Thomya et al., 2025). Moreover, heat-induced modifications to pectin structure can substantially impact rheological behavior, gel stability, and mouthfeel (J. Liu et al., 2020). Compared with P, MS has been shown to better preserve chlorophyll and ascorbic acid, delay color deterioration, and markedly inhibit microbial proliferation (Qu et al., 2021). Although both P and HPP effectively reduce microbial load, P typically causes a notable reduction in volatile compounds and a considerable loss of aroma, whereas HPP exhibits superior retention of nutrients, antioxidant potential, and sensory properties (Sawicki, Jabłońska, Starowicz, Szmatowicz, Latocha, & Błaszczak, 2025). For instance, Niu et al. (2022) demonstrated that HPP-treated passion fruit jam maintained higher levels of color compounds, sugars, organic acids, and volatiles compared with pasteurization and ultra-high-temperature treatment. Likewise, Nam, Ramakrishnan, & Kwon (2019) reported that 1 kGy EBI extended the shelf life of citrus fruits by 15 days without altering their physicochemical profile. Further evidence from Hu et al. (2025) showed that 10 kGy EBI significantly enhanced the concentrations of functional ingredients in Angelica sinensis, indicating that radiation may facilitate the release of bioactive components and elevate medicinal value without impairing volatile composition. GI has also been reported to increase total phenolics content, improve color characteristics, and enhance antioxidant activity in some fruits and vegetable products (e.g., mango juice) (Naresh, Varakumar, Variyar, Sharma, & Reddy, 2015); although the effects are generally dependent on the product matrix and irradiation dose. For example, in onion bulbs, low-dose gamma irradiation during storage showed no obvious negative effects on texture, color, or overall sensory quality (Kavita, Adiani, & Mishra, 2024).

Collectively, these findings underscore the diverse impacts of sterilization on plant-based matrices and highlight the need for systematic evaluation across different product types.

Although numerous studies have examined sterilization technologies in fruit and vegetable preservation, most have evaluated single matrices or isolated quality attributes such as microbial safety, selective nutrient retention, or limited textural parameters. Far fewer investigations have addressed how these technologies influence the multidimensional quality of high-fat matrices such as avocado purée. In particular, the mechanisms by which sterilization-induced microstructural alterations affect nutrient retention, water distribution, textural behavior, and volatile release remain poorly understood, leaving a clear gap in the structure–function knowledge of avocado-based products.

To address this gap, the present study systematically evaluated the effects of five sterilization methods: pasteurization (P, 90 °C, 5 min), microwave sterilization (MS, 600 W, 3 min), high-pressure processing (HPP, 450 MPa, 10 min), gamma irradiation (GI, 8 kGy), and electron-beam irradiation (EBI, 10 kGy) on the physicochemical properties, nutritional attributes, microstructure, texture, and volatile profile of avocado purée containing 2% cold-pressed oil. Physicochemical parameters (pH, color, vitamin C, free polyphenols) and microbial safety (total viable counts, molds, yeasts, Escherichia coli) were quantified. Microstructural changes were assessed using CLSM and particle-size analysis, while texture, rheology, and LF-NMR provided complementary insights into structural and water-distribution dynamics. Volatile changes were characterized using an E-nose and HS-SPME/GC-MS. Overall, this study offers a mechanistic understanding of how sterilization technologies govern texture–flavor retention in avocado purée and provides scientific guidance for enhancing product quality and industrial performance.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade. The primary reagents included trichloroacetic acid (Chengdu Kelong Chemical Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China), vitamin C (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), anhydrous sodium carbonate (Tianjin Youpu Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China), and Folin–Ciocalteu phenol reagent (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Methanol was purchased from Chengdu Jinshan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China), and 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane was obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All reagents were sourced from certified commercial suppliers and used as received without further purification.

Preparation and processing of AP

Fresh avocados were sourced from Menglian Dai, Lahu, and Wa Autonomous County, Pu’er City, Yunnan Province, China. The AP preparation and storage sampling procedures were adapted from a previously reported avocado puree study (Uzunlu, 2024), with modifications. The treatment conditions used in this study were selected based on preliminary trials and equipment operating characteristics. Uniformly sized fruits were washed to remove surface impurities, peeled, sliced, and blended with 2% (w/w) cold-pressed avocado oil. The mixture was then homogenized using a 60-mesh colloid mill to obtain avocado purée (AP). The purée was dispensed into high-temperature-resistant aluminum foil pouches (350 g; n = 30), vacuum-sealed, and allocated to six treatment groups: (i) control (C, no sterilization); (ii) pasteurization (P), 90 °C for 5 min in a water bath with intermittent agitation; (iii) high-pressure processing (HPP), 450 MPa for 10 min (HPP.L1-600/5); (iv) gamma irradiation (GI), ^60Co at 8 kGy (Yunnan Yuanhe Technology Co., Ltd., China); (v) microwave sterilization (MS), 600 W for 3 min (AL-IMC2 intelligent microwave synthesis/extraction system); and (vi) electron-beam irradiation (EBI), 10 MeV/20 kW at 10 kGy. All samples were stored at 4 °C. For each treatment, one pouch was used for flavor and quality evaluation, while the remaining four were analyzed for physicochemical and microbiological attributes on days 0, 7, 14, and 21 (day 0 representing the processing day).

Determination of physicochemical and nutritional quality

Measurement of pH and color

The pH value was determined using a PHS-3C pH meter, and the color attributes L⁎, a⁎, and b⁎ were measured using a colorimeter (Model CM-5). The total color variation (ΔE) during storage was calculated according to the following equation:ΔE=L1∗−L0∗2+a1∗−a0∗2+b1∗−b0∗2

Determination of Vitamin C

The total vitamin C content (ascorbic acid plus dehydroascorbic acid) was analyzed using the 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) method (Raj, Chakraborty, & Rao, 2019). A 100 g sample of AP was blended with an equal weight of pre-chilled 2% (w/w) oxalic acid for 2 min. The resulting homogenate (40 g) was diluted to 100 mL with 1% oxalic acid, mixed thoroughly, and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The obtained supernatant was treated with activated charcoal (0.5 g/25 mL), vortexed, allowed to stand, and then filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. The filtrate was combined with 2% thiourea, followed by derivatization with 2% DNPH at 37 °C for 3 h in the dark. After cooling, the mixture was reacted with 85% sulfuric acid. Following 30 min of incubation at room temperature, the absorbance was recorded at 520 nm (n = 3). The vitamin C concentration was determined from a standard calibration curve (y = 0.1983× + 0.0864, R2 = 0.9902) and expressed as mg/100 g of sample.

Determination of free polyphenol content (FPC)

A 2 g of sample was extracted with 20 mL of methanolwater (80:20, v/v) in a 50 mL centrifuge tube. The mixture was subjected to low-temperature ultrasonication for 60 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. For quantification, 0.2 mL of the extract was combined with 1.0 mL of Folin Ciocalteu reagent, 0.8 mL of saturated Na₂CO₃ solution, and 2.0 mL of distilled water, followed by incubation in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a microplate reader (n = 3). The free polyphenol content was determined from a gallic acid calibration curve (y = 1.1538× + 0.111, R2 = 0.9926) and expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of sample (mg GAE/g).

Microbial analysis

The total aerobic mesophilic bacteria, yeasts and molds, and Escherichia coli in the AP samples were enumerated following standard microbiological procedures. A 1 g portion of each sample was serially diluted in sterile distilled water to obtain dilutions of 10−1, 10−2, and 10−3. Appropriate dilutions were plated on Plate Count Agar (PCA) for total aerobic mesophiles, Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) for yeasts and molds, and Violet Red Bile Agar (VRBA) for E. coli. Incubation conditions were as follows: PCA plates at 36 ± 1 °C for 48 h, PDA plates at 25 ± 0.2 °C for 5 days, and VRBA plates at 35 ± 1 °C for 4 h followed by incubation at 44 ± 1 °C for an additional 48 h. Colony-forming units (CFU) were recorded and expressed as log₁₀ CFU per gram of sample (log₁₀ CFU/g).

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

Samples (∼100 mg) were incubated with 1 mL of a mixed dye solution containing Calcofluor White (CW, 10 μM), SYTO 9 (5 μM), and Nile Red (10 μM) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, the samples were rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then mounted on glass slides and the cell-wall polysaccharide network, lipid droplets, and nucleic acid structures of the AP were observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (STELLARIS 5, Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Particle size assessment

A 5 g of sample was suspended in 100 mL of distilled water (without ultrasonication) and evaluated using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Malvern Panalytical, UK) with the refractive index adjusted to 1.51. The particle size distribution (PSD) and surface area weighted mean diameter (D[3,2]) were subsequently recorded.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis

The transverse relaxation time (T₂) was measured using a low-field NMR spectrometer (NMI20-060H-I, Niumag Analytical Instrument Co., Suzhou, China) operated with a Q-CPMG pulse sequence. The experimental settings were as follows: sampling frequency, 250 kHz; operating frequency, 21 MHz; radiofrequency (RF) delay, 0.03 ms; recycle delay, 3000 ms; number of scans, 16; echo time, 0.2 ms; number of echoes, 3000; and echo-peak offset, 0.02 ms. The acquired data were processed using multi-exponential inversion (MultiExp Inv Analysis) to determine the T₂ component distributions and their relative proportions.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

The texture analysis was conducted following the procedure described by (D. Liu et al., 2025) using a texture analyzer (TA.XT or TA.XT Plus; Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) operated at ambient temperature. A 20 g of samples were placed in 50 mL standard cups and compressed with a P25 probe at a test speed of 3 mm s−1 to 33.3% of the sample height, followed by a return speed of 33.3 mm s−1. The parameters of hardness, adhesiveness, cohesiveness, and springiness were recorded.

Rheological properties

The rheological characteristics of AP were analyzed using an MCR 92 rotational rheometer (NETZSCH–Kinexus, Germany) equipped with a parallel-plate system (PP25, 25 mm diameter, 1000 μm gap). The apparent viscosity was determined within a shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1, and the viscosity value at 50 s−1 was selected for comparison. The dynamic viscoelastic behavior was examined after establishing the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) by performing an amplitude sweep test (0.01–10% strain, 1 Hz). Subsequently, frequency sweep measurements (0.1–10 Hz, 0.1% strain, 25 °C) were carried out to determine the storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), and damping factor (tan δ).

Electronic nose analysis

The 5 g of AP was sealed in a 20 mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 30 °C for 30 min prior to analysis. The volatile profile was determined using an electronic nose (Fox 3000, Alpha MOS, France) with clean air as the carrier gas. Both the detection and sensor-cleaning phases were set to 200 s, with a sampling flow rate of 400 mL/min. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each treatment.

HS-SPME GC-MS analysis

Frozen samples (−80 °C) were cryogenically ground in liquid nitrogen and vortex-mixed. Each 500 mg portion was placed into a headspace vial containing 2 mL of saturated NaCl solution and 20 μL of internal standard solution (10 μg/mL). The vials were agitated at 60 °C for 5 min, followed by headspace extraction using a 120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS SPME fiber for 15 min. The collected analytes were thermally desorbed at 250 °C for 5 min and separated on a DB-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent J&W Scientific) using helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The injector temperature was set to 250 °C, with a solvent delay of 3.5 min. The oven temperature program was as follows: an initial temperature of 40 °C (held for 3.5 min), increased at 10 °C/min to 100 °C, then at 7 °C/min to 180 °C, and finally at 25 °C/min to 280 °C (held for 5 min). Electron-impact ionization (70 eV) was employed, with the ion source, quadrupole, and transfer line temperatures maintained at 230 °C, 150 °C, and 280 °C, respectively. The MS was operated in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode to enable targeted analysis; consequently, full-scan mass spectra of individual analytes were not acquired under this analytical workflow (Yuan et al., 2024). Compound annotation was achieved by combining retention time (RT) data with selected diagnostic ions typically comprising one quantifier ion and two to three qualifier ions according to the established analytical method and reference database. Quantification (or semi-quantification) was conducted based on the integrated peak area of the designated quantifier ion (Yuan et al., 2022). Accordingly, compound identification in the present study was not based on conventional full-scan spectral library matching scores. To enhance the transparency, reproducibility, and analytical traceability of the SIM-based methodology, representative total ion chromatograms (TICs), characteristic fingerprint spectra, and extracted ion chromatograms, including peak integration profiles for selected compounds, are presented in the Supplementary Material. To evaluate the contribution of each volatile compound to the overall aroma characteristics, the relative odor activity value (ROAV) was used in this study. Compounds with ROAV ≥1 were defined as key aroma-active substances, whereas those with 0.1 ≤ ROAV <1 were regarded as aroma-modifying components (C. Yi, Li, Zhu, Liu, & Quan, 2021). Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n ≥ 3), and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0.1, with significance defined at p < 0.05. All figures and graphical outputs were generated using OriginPro 2024.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical and nutritional quality

This study conducted a systematic comparison of the physicochemical properties and nutritional quality of AP processed using five sterilization methods (P, HPP, GI, MS, and EBI) (Table 1 and Fig. 1). The results demonstrated that sterilization treatments markedly influenced the nutritional constituents and physicochemical characteristics of the purée. The pH of AP increased slightly following P and HPP treatments (p < 0.05). However, the GI, MS, and EBI groups exhibited decreased pH values, likely due to the release of free organic acids triggered by free-radical reactions or matrix disruption, as well as the formation of acidic degradation compounds. Throughout storage, all treatment groups showed a gradual decline in pH; however, the rate of decrease was slower in sterilized samples compared with the untreated control, suggesting enhanced stability conferred by sterilization. Color parameters were also strongly affected by sterilization. Increases in L* and a* values across treatments may be attributed to cellular disruption and alterations to heat-sensitive constituents during processing. During storage, GI-treated samples consistently maintained the highest L* values. The L* value of the EBI group initially increased and subsequently declined, whereas P- and HPP-treated samples remained relatively stable over time. In contrast, MS-treated samples showed a pronounced reduction in L*, indicating greater susceptibility to color deterioration. All sterilization treatments led to reductions in b* values, aligning with previous observations of pigment degradation during thermal or oxidative processing of fruit and vegetable matrices (Pénicaud, Achir, Dhuique-Mayer, Dornier, & Bohuon, 2011). The P, HPP, and EBI treatments effectively mitigated enzymatic browning and avoided excessive thermally- or irradiation-induced pigment loss, resulting in comparatively moderate reductions in b* (Sarantakou, Andreou, Paraskevopoulou, Dermesonlouoglou, & Taoukis, 2023). Conversely, the localized overheating associated with MS and the elevated reactive oxygen species generated during GI were more likely to accelerate carotenoid and chlorophyll degradation, yielding more substantial decreases in b* values. Sterilization also significantly increased ΔE values, with HPP causing the least overall color change with P producing the largest ΔE value (14.12). Vitamin C content showed clear treatment-dependent responses. Relative to the untreated sample (14.93 mg/100 g), thermal sterilization caused substantial losses, with the P group exhibiting the greatest decline (8.19 mg/100 g). Non-thermal methods produced markedly lower vitamin C degradation, and HPP demonstrated the highest retention (93.82%). During 21 days of storage, vitamin C levels declined across all treatments, indicating ongoing oxidative degradation, consistent with prior findings (Wu et al., 2021). Thermal sterilization promotes vitamin C cleavage and oxidation, whereas irradiation accelerates oxidative loss through reactive oxygen species formation. Sterilization also influenced free polyphenol content (FPC). Relative to the untreated group, the sterilized samples exhibited a 5.0–92.86% increase in FPC, and FPC levels continued to rise throughout storage. Notably, the P-treated samples showed significantly higher FPC than all other treatment groups. This increase may reflect the combined effects of thermal sterilization and limited microbial fermentation in disrupting the cell structure, thereby reducing cell-wall-bound polyphenols and enhancing the release of free, bioactive phenolic compounds (X. Liu et al., 2024).

Collectively, these findings indicate that non-thermal sterilization methods exert comparatively milder effects on the physicochemical and nutritional attributes of AP, with HPP yielding the greatest retention of color and vitamin C. However, thermal treatments were more effective at increasing free polyphenol content, underscoring the distinct mechanistic influences of thermal and non-thermal sterilization on nutrient stability and matrix interactions.

Microbial analysis

The microbial analysis revealed that all five sterilization treatments exhibited effective bactericidal activity compared with the control (Table S1). On day 0, pasteurization (P) produced the lowest total colony count (1.50 log CFU/g), whereas after 21 days of storage at 4 °C, gamma irradiation (GI) resulted in the lowest count (1.01 log CFU/g). All treatments effectively inhibited molds and yeasts; however, an increase in microbial counts was observed in all sterilized groups, except for the P group, consistent with the findings of Sethukali et al. (2025). Escherichia coli was not detected in any samples throughout storage. In summary, all five sterilization methods can effectively reduce microbial populations.

In the P and MS groups, blue fluorescence appeared as irregular fragments, dispersed speckles, or discontinuous streaks, demonstrating a significant loss of structural continuity within the polysaccharide network. This was accompanied by pronounced lipid droplet aggregation. These changes are consistent with the high temperatures involved in thermal sterilization, which promote depolymerization of cell-wall polysaccharides and denaturation of membrane-associated proteins, ultimately causing notable cell-wall collapse and enhanced lipid coalescence (Cheng, Sun, Huang, Feng, Zhou, & Cen, 2013). GI- and EBI-treated samples also exhibited more fragmented structures than the control; however, portions of sheet-like or short band-like polysaccharide regions remained observable, and the lipid droplets were still distinguishable as individual particles. This reflects the mode of action of ionizing radiation, wherein reactive oxygen species generated during irradiation oxidize membrane lipids and proteins, producing structural damage that is considerable yet less intense than that induced by high-temperature treatments. In contrast, the HPP-treated samples retained a microstructure more closely resembling that of the control. Larger and more continuous, blue-labeled polysaccharide regions remained visible, and lipid droplets exhibited a relatively uniform dispersion pattern. This structural preservation aligns with the mechanism of high-pressure processing, which primarily modifies membrane permeability without causing extensive destruction of the cell-wall polysaccharide matrix. Taken together, the extent of structural alteration among the sterilization treatments followed the order: thermal treatments (P, MS) > ionizing irradiation (GI, EBI) > HPP, highlighting the comparatively gentle impact of HPP on the microstructure of AP.

Particle size distribution

As shown in Fig. 2B–C, all AP samples exhibited a dominant particle-size peak within the 10–100 μm range, corresponding to the typical cell-size distribution in avocado-based matrices. The untreated control (C group) displayed the highest peak intensity (6.48% at 66.9 μm) and the narrowest distribution, indicating a uniform and intact native tissue structure. Following sterilization, disruption of cell integrity led to varying degrees of particle fragmentation or aggregation across treatments. Thermal treatments (P and MS) shifted the particle-size distribution toward larger diameters and significantly increased the D[3,2] values (25.53 μm and 24.63 μm, respectively). This expansion in particle size suggests enhanced flocculation or aggregation involving both cellular fragments and lipid droplets. These changes are likely attributable to heat-induced pectin solubilization, structural rearrangements within the polysaccharide matrix, and strengthened inter-particle interactions. Similar phenomena have been reported in thermally processed apple purée, where heating promoted aggregation and broadened the particle-size profile (Rinaldi, Langialonga, Dhenge, Aldini, & Chiavaro, 2021). In contrast, HPP treatment resulted in a reduction in the D[3,2] value and an increased proportion of fine particles. This indicates that high-pressure processing can effectively refine particles through mechanical disruption of dispersed phases and tissue fragments. Such trend aligns with previous studies demonstrating reductions in the particle size of high-pressure-treated apple juice (J. Yi, Kebede, Kristiani, Grauwet, Van Loey, & Hendrickx, 2017). EBI exerted comparatively modest effects on particle-size distribution. Notably, GI-treated samples exhibited the most pronounced reduction in particle size, which may be attributed to radiation-induced free radical formation causing chain scission of polysaccharides such as pectin, thereby weakening the particle network and promoting depolymerization (Silva, Villar, & Pimentel, 2012). Overall, gamma irradiation produced the smallest particle sizes among the treatments, followed by high-pressure processing, both contributing to a comparatively more stable colloidal system.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analysis

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) offers a rapid, non-destructive approach for characterizing aqueous phases and evaluating the molecular binding states within complex food matrices. In this system, the transverse relaxation time (T₂) serves as an indicator of the internal structural environment of the purée, reflecting hydrogen proton mobility and interactions with surrounding macromolecules (D. Liu et al., 2024). Generally, a longer T₂ corresponds to greater molecular freedom and weaker water–matrix associations. Three distinct relaxation components were identified: T₂₁ (0.1–10 ms), representing tightly bound water; T₂₂ (10–100 ms), corresponding to immobilized water entrapped within the purée matrix; and T₂₃ (100–1000 ms), associated with freely mobile water.

As shown in Fig. 3E–F, the T₂₃ peak constituted the predominant proportion across all treatment groups, indicating that free water was the major water fraction in AP and played a central role in determining the product's overall fluidity. Relative to the control, P, HPP, and GI treatments reduced the T₂₁ peak while increasing the T₂₂ and T₂₃ peak areas. Conversely, MS and EBI treatments increased T₂₂ but decreased T₂₃. These shifts collectively suggest that all sterilization methods altered the physical structure of the purée by weakening water–macromolecule interactions (particularly with fibers), thereby inducing water redistribution among bound, immobilized, and free states. Notably, MS produced the most pronounced structural modifications, showing the highest T₂₂ and the lowest T₂₃ peak areas. This pattern may be attributed to microwave-induced structural rearrangements, in which localized heating facilitates the conversion of a portion of free water into more immobilized forms, reflecting greater confinement within the disrupted matrix.Fig. 3Texture profile and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analysis: (A) hardness; (B) adhesiveness; (C) springiness; (D) cohesiveness; (E) T₂ relaxation profile; (F) stacked bar chart of relative water content.Fig. 3

Texture profile analysis

Textural properties are critical determinants of sensory perception, and sterilization treatments can markedly influence mechanical attributes such as hardness, elasticity, viscoelastic behavior, and cohesiveness, thereby shaping consumer acceptance. As shown in Fig. 3A–D, P treatment substantially increased the hardness and adhesiveness of AP while producing the lowest cohesiveness among all groups. In contrast, HPP- and GI-treated samples exhibited only moderate increases in hardness, and their elasticity and cohesiveness were comparable to or slightly higher than those of the untreated control. The MS- and EBI-treated samples displayed the highest cohesiveness and elasticity, indicating distinct structural responses to these processing methods.

These textural modifications can be explained by differences in the underlying physicochemical mechanisms. Pasteurization involves exposure to elevated temperatures, which promotes protein and pectin denaturation as well as macromolecular aggregation. Such thermally induced restructuring forms a denser, more rigid network capable of resisting compression and increasing adhesiveness, but simultaneously reduces the purée's capacity to recover after deformation, thereby lowering cohesiveness (Nawaz et al., 2021). By comparison, high-pressure processing reinforces the gel-like network more moderately through compression of cellular structures and pressure-induced redistribution of water at subambient temperatures, resulting in a more balanced textural profile. The localized, volumetric heating generated during microwave treatment and the partial crosslinking initiated by irradiation further enhance physical entanglement and weak intermolecular bonding among dispersed particles. These effects increase the resilience and structural integrity of the matrix, enabling MS- and EBI-treated samples to rebound more readily after compression and maintain higher elasticity and cohesiveness.

Rheological properties

As shown in Fig. 4, all AP samples demonstrated typical shear-thinning behavior, characterized by decreasing viscosity with increasing shear rate. Despite this shared rheological pattern, substantial differences emerged among sterilization treatments. P and MS treatments markedly increased the viscosity of the purée, thereby enhancing resistance to flow. This trend is consistent with previous observations that thermal processing can promote the structural consolidation and the viscosity of plant-based matrices. In contrast, the viscosity curves of the HPP and GI groups were considerably lower than the untreated control, indicating weakened structural networks and improved flowability. These rheological patterns corresponded closely with the microstructural findings. Thermal treatments (P and MS) caused pronounced cell-wall polysaccharide rupture and extensive particle aggregation, which translated macroscopically into increased viscosity and reduced ease of flow. As EBI caused the least disruption to cell-wall continuity and particle connectivity, its rheological profile remained most similar to that of the untreated purée.

An electronic nose system was applied to characterize and differentiate the overall aroma profiles of AP subjected to distinct sterilization treatments. Based on HS-SPME/GC–MS analysis, a total of 1529 volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were detected across the six treatment groups. Alcohols constituted the predominant chemical class, representing 26.51% of total VOC content. Due to their low odor thresholds and characteristic aromatic and green sensory attributes, alcohols make a substantial contribution to the aroma quality of AP (Lomelí-Martín, Martínez, Welti-Chanes, & Escobedo-Avellaneda, 2021). This pattern reflects the thermal volatilization and degradation of aroma-active compounds, as well as heat-induced disruption of cellular structure and water distribution, which collectively accelerate the loss of volatile constituents during thermal processing (Wu et al., 2021). Principal component analysis (PCA) further elucidated treatment-induced variations in VOC profiles. ROAV analysis of volatile organic compounds in AP

By screening the key aroma-active compounds with ROAV ≥1 in Table S3, it was determined that the dominant aroma characteristics of AP were primarily shaped by lipid oxidation–derived aldehydes and ketones (e.g., 2,4-undecadienal, 2,4-nonadienal, 1-nonen-3-one, 1-octen-3-one, and 3-octen-2-one), fruity and floral compounds (β-damascone; butanoic acid, 3-methyl-, 2-phenylethyl ester), sulfur-containing volatiles (3-cyclohexene-1-methanethiol, α,α,4-trimethyl-), and pyrazines (2-methoxy-3,5-dimethylpyrazine). In the control sample, 3-cyclohexene-1-methanethiol (ROAV = 100), 2,4-undecadienal (74.00), 2-methoxy-3,5-dimethylpyrazine (30.12), and β-damascone (8.21) emerged as the major contributors to the characteristic fresh, creamy, roasted, and fruity nuances of AP.

Sterilization treatments substantially reshaped this key aroma landscape. P treatment markedly reduced the intensity of several major contributors; 2,4-undecadienal decreased to 13.36, β-damascone to 1.55, and 1-nonen-3-one to 0.85—leading to a notable decline in fresh, creamy, roasted, fruity, and floral sensory attributes. In contrast, HPP and GI preserved aroma characteristics more effectively and remained closer to the control, retaining multiple critical aldehydes, ketones, and fruity/floral contributors (HPP: 2,4-undecadienal 66.18, 2,4-nonadienal 3.91, β-damascone 6.83; GI: 2,4-undecadienal 66.59, 2,4-nonadienal 2.22, β-damascone 7.09). Conversely, MS and EBI substantially enhanced the contribution of several key compounds. For both treatments, 2,4-undecadienal reached a ROAV of 100. β-Damascone exhibited pronounced increases (19.23 in MS and 18.62 in EBI), while butanoic acid, 3-methyl-, 2-phenylethyl ester also rose to 2.29 and 2.39, respectively. Notably, both MS and EBI showed declining trends for the key sulfur compound 3-cyclohexene-1-methanethiol (74.61 in MS and 31.49 in EBI). These shifts suggest that MS and EBI promote the formation or enhanced contribution of selected esters, ketones, and aldehydes, potentially through thermally or radiatively induced transformations and volatilization dynamics (Yan et al., 2025).

Overall, P treatment diminished the contribution of most key aroma compounds, resulting in a less complex and less characteristic flavor profile. In contrast, MS and EBI enhanced fresh, creamy, and fruity sensory attributes, while HPP and GI largely maintained the original aromatic signature of AP.

To further elucidate how specific volatiles contributed to overall aroma perception, correlation analyses were conducted between electronic-nose sensor responses and the twelve key aroma-active compounds identified via HS-SPME/GC–MS. As shown in Fig. 7D, sensors PA/2, P40/1, P10/2, P10/1, T30/1, and LY2/LG exhibited significant positive correlations (|r| > 0.4, p < 0.05) with pyrazines, ketones, and selected esters—including hexanethioic acid S-methyl ester, 1-octen-3-one, 2-methoxy-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, and 1-nonen-3-one. Additionally, thiol compounds can form sulfides or sulfates on metal oxide films, reducing conductivity changes and weakening sensor sensitivity (Yang et al., 2025).

Conclusion

This study comparatively evaluated the effects of different sterilization technologies on the physicochemical properties, microstructure, texture, aroma profile, and microbial safety of AP. Thermal treatments (P and MS) caused the most pronounced quality changes, including greater color change, vitamin C loss, viscosity increase, and microstructural disruption, whereas non-thermal treatments, particularly HPP and EBI, better preserved structural integrity, nutritional quality, and desirable texture. Although all treatments effectively ensured microbial safety, their effects on volatile compounds differed substantially. Notably, HPP best preserved the characteristic fresh aroma profile of avocado and remained most like the untreated sample. Overall, these findings support the use of non-thermal sterilization, especially HPP and EBI, as promising strategies for balancing microbial safety and quality retention in minimally processed avocado-based products.
