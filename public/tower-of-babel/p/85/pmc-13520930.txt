Suspected hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies: clinical signs and electrodiagnostic findings in eleven dogs
Authors: Valentina Buffagni, Nicola Gasparinetti, Nicla Furiani, Pier Luigi Attili, Filippo Belforti, Teresa Gagliardo, Gualtiero Gandini, Stefania Gianni, Stefano Biffani, Ezio Bianchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is limited information available regarding the electrodiagnostic studies (EDX) features of hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSANs) in dogs.Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo retrospectively describe the clinical and electrodiagnostic findings of dogs with suspected HSAN and to assess whether comprehensive EDX can assist in disease classification and prognostication.AnimalsEleven client-owned dogs with early-onset distal sensory loss.MethodsMulticentric retrospective observational study (2008-2025). Inclusion criteria were distal sensory loss before 1 year of age, acral mutilations or analgesia of the toes, and complete EDX. Results of sensory nerve conduction studies (SNCS) and cord dorsum potential (CDP) were compared with normal values and with control dogs using an exact permutation test.ResultsTwo electroclinical patterns emerged. Pattern 1 included dogs with acral mutilation, preserved gait, and recordable but significantly reduced sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitudes, sensory nerve conduction velocity (NCV), and CDP amplitudes (P < .05). Pattern 2 included dogs with tetraparesis and proprioceptive deficits; SNAPs and CDPs were absent in most nerves tested, and motor nerve conduction studies (MNCS) showed reduced compound muscle action potential amplitudes or reduced motor NCV, or both. Dogs in pattern 2 were euthanized within 12 months, whereas 5 of the 6 pattern 1 dogs showed stable or slowly progressive disease.Conclusions and clinical importanceComprehensive EDX, including SNCS and CDP, identifies sensory and possible concomitant motor dysfunction in dogs with suspected HSAN and differentiates distinct electroclinical patterns associated with prognosis.Keywords: CDP, distal sensory loss, electrodiagnostic studies, sensory neuropathy, SNCS

Introduction

Hereditary peripheral neuropathies are neurodegenerative disorders of the peripheral nervous system.1 In human medicine, these rare disorders are classified into 3 main categories depending on the degree of involvement of motor, sensory, or autonomic nerve fibers: hereditary motor neuropathies (HMNs), hereditary motor and sensory neuropathies (HMSNs), and hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSANs).1,2 Among the 8 clinically heterogeneous forms of HSAN, sensory or autonomic deficits predominate, but concomitant motor alterations have also been reported.1,3 The clinical hallmarks of HSAN are reduced pain perception and subsequent injury.1,4

Congenital insensitivity to pain (CIP) is an inherited disorder with a clinical presentation similar to HSAN, making it an important differential diagnosis.1,4,5 The differences between CIP and HSAN are that in the former, the clinical signs are congenital, whereas in the latter they appear later in life, and that while CIP selectively affects small fibers, HSAN causes sensory loss across all somatosensory modalities (pain, temperature, vibration, touch, and proprioception).1,4 The overlap between some of the 7 forms described of CIP and certain subtypes of HMSN and HSAN can make it challenging to differentiate these diseases solely on the basis of clinical signs.1,4

Reported cases of HSAN have been documented in both purebred and mixed-breed dogs.2,3 However, only 3 causal genetic variants have been identified to date,6–8 compared to the several variants in at least 20 genes reported in humans.1,3 The first variant is an inversion disrupting reticulophagy regulator 1 (RETREG1) in Border Collies and Border Collie crosses.5,6,9 The second is a missense variant also affecting RETREG1 in a family of mixed breed dogs5,7 and in German spitz.10 The third variant is a regulatory single-base substitution in a lincRNA upstream of the GDNF (glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor) gene in Pointers, English Springer Spaniels, and French Spaniels.5,8

Reduced or absent distal nociception in the limbs is a feature of all forms of HSAN in dogs.3 Depending on the form, loss of nociception can be associated with self-mutilation, proprioceptive deficits, ataxia, motor impairments, and autonomic disorders.3,5–20

Electrodiagnostic studies (EDX), and sensory nerve conduction studies (SNCS) in particular, play a key role in correctly diagnosing HSAN in human medicine.4 Sensory nerve conduction studies show alterations in HSAN and HMSN, but are generally normal in HMN or CIP.4,21 This test actually evaluates large-diameter, fast-conducting sensory fibers, which are typically affected in HSAN but normal in CIP.4,22

Electromyography (EMG), motor nerve conduction studies (MNCS), and F-waves are EDX that assess motor fiber function. F-waves are a purely motor event that is used primarily to evaluate the proximal and radicular portions of the motor fibers.23 Electromyography, MNCS, and F-waves are particularly affected in HMSN compared to HSAN and are therefore important for differentiating between these 2 conditions.1,22

Electrophysiological findings in HSAN typically include reduced or absent sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) and reduced sensory nerve conduction velocity. Other possible features in some subtypes of HSAN I, II, and VI include spontaneous abnormal activity (SAA) on EMG and abnormal MNCS.4,24,25

There are few reports of dogs with HSAN that include EDX,3,6,7,11,12,15,16 and none of these involves a test that selectively evaluates sensory fibers, the cord dorsum potential (CDP). Indeed, since the recorded potential originates from the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, the CDP only investigates the sensory fibers of the peripheral nerves, including the dorsal roots.23 Moreover, no report focusing on electrodiagnostic findings in dogs affected by HSAN has been published in the veterinary literature. This study aimed to retrospectively evaluate the clinical signs and EDX of dogs suspected of having this rarely described condition. A related objective was to evaluate the use of clinical findings and comprehensive EDX results for classifying HSAN in dogs and providing prognostic information.

Materials and methods

Animals

This is an observational retrospective multicentric study. The study included dogs showing signs of distal sensory loss that appeared before the age of one. Specifically, only dogs showing signs of self-mutilation of the paw or analgesia of the toes, that had undergone a neurological examination by an ECVN diplomate or resident, or by a veterinarian with at least 5 years’ experience at a neurology referral center, were enrolled.

Additional inclusion criteria comprised undergoing EDX, which involved EMG, as well as MNCS, repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS), F-waves, SNCS, and CDP of at least 1 peripheral nerve. Furthermore, dogs with skin lesions had to have undergone a specialist dermatological consultation in order to rule out primary skin conditions as the cause of the lesions.

Data extracted from the medical records included breed, age, sex, physical examination, days elapsed between the onset of signs of neurological disease and EDX, results of neurological examination, electrodiagnostic findings, and, when performed, results of nerve and muscle biopsies. Information regarding dogs follow-up was collected by contacting owners or referring veterinarians by telephone. Information was collected in particular about the onset, progression, and response to treatment of self-mutilation, gait abnormalities, regurgitation, and urinary incontinence.

Electrodiagnostic studies

Electrodiagnostic studies were performed, under general anesthesia, by a board-certified neurologist (E.B., Dip. ECVN) using commercially available electrodiagnostic equipment (Myoquick, Micromed S.p.A., Treviso, Italy). Electromyographic recordings were performed on the epaxial, appendicular, and cranial muscles on one side of the body. Motor nerve conduction studies, RNS, SNCS, F-waves, and CDP recordings of the sciatic-tibial and ulnar nerves were also carried out.

In some dogs, SNCS of radial, peroneal, and saphenous nerves was also performed. Specific details on the EDX’s techniques are reported in the Appendix.

Other tests

After EDX, the collection of biopsies of a mixed nerve (peroneal nerve) or a sensory nerve (caudal sural cutaneous nerve), or both, and a muscle (tibialis cranialis muscle) was performed in some cases. Muscle biopsies were collected from a muscle that was not tested during the EMG. A genetic test for the RETREG1 gene variant responsible for HSAN in Border Collies and Border Collie crosses was performed on 1 dog.

Statistical analysis

Differences between cases and controls for sensory nerve conduction velocity (NCV), sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude, and CDP amplitude were assessed using an exact permutation test for the difference in means. More statistical analysis details are reported in the Appendix.

Results

Animals

Eleven dogs were included in the study, of which 4 were Miniature Pinschers (36%), 1 was a Bernese Mountain Dog (9%), and 6 were mixed breeds dogs (55%). Among the 6 mixed breed dogs, there was 1 Border Collie and 2 Miniature Pinscher mix-breeds. There was no close genetic relationship between the included dogs. However, a more distant relationship cannot be ruled out, as no pedigree analysis was carried out. Five of the dogs were intact females (45%) and 6 were intact males (55%). Considering the clinical presentation and the results of EDX of the enrolled dogs, 2 patterns emerged (pattern 1 and pattern 2). Pattern 1 included the 4 Miniature Pinschers and the 2 Miniature Pinscher mixes. Pattern 2 included the Bernese Mountain Dog and the remaining 4 mixed breeds, including the Border Collie mix (Table 1).

History and clinical finding

All the dogs exhibited clinical signs of neurological dysfunction compatible with distal sensory loss before the age of 1 (mean 5.5 months, range 3-8 months). Skin wounds on the digits were present in all dogs. Four of the dogs, all of which belonged to pattern 1, had mutilated at least one of their phalanges (Figure 1).

Images of the distal part of the right thoracic (A) and right pelvic (B) limb of a Miniature Pinscher of pattern 1 group showing signs of acral self-mutilation.

The main clinical signs observed in the 6 dogs (6/11, 55%) in pattern 1 were self-mutilation and sustained licking of the paws, resulting in wounds to the digits (Figure 1). Four of these dogs had signs in their pelvic limbs only, while 2 had signs in all 4 limbs (Table 1; Video S1).

The remaining 5 dogs (5/11, 45%), which constituted the pattern 2 group, had skin lesions that were considered a consequence of the abnormal gait and insensitivity to pain (Video S2). One dog of this group had concomitant orthopedic diseases (right hip and intertarsal luxation), while another developed digit and metatarsal osteomyelitis as a result of severe skin lesions.

All the dogs included in the study underwent specialist dermatological consultations, during which primary skin diseases were ruled out. The age at which the neurological examination and electrodiagnostic evaluation were performed ranged from 7 to 32 months. During the neurological examination, the mental status and behavior of all the dogs appeared normal.

All the dogs in pattern 1 had a normal gait. Of these dogs, 2 had reduced or absent postural reactions in pelvic limbs.

All the dogs in pattern 2 presented with tetraparesis. Of these, 1 was nonambulatory, 4 were ambulatory, and 3 showed spontaneous knuckling of the pelvic limbs. Reduced or absent postural reactions were present in all of these dogs in both the front and pelvic limbs (Table 1).

No abnormalities were detected in the cranial nerves of the dogs included in the study. All 11 dogs exhibited reduced or absent nociception distal to the carpus and tarsus and reduced or absent withdrawal reflexes of all limbs. The patellar and perineal reflexes were intact in all the dogs.

Electrodiagnostic findings

On EMG, spontaneous abnormal activity (SAA) was recorded in 10 dogs. In the pattern 1 group, 5 dogs exhibited sparse and mild (1+) SAA in epaxial and appendicular muscles and 1 had absent SAA. All 5 of the dogs in the pattern 2 group had moderate (2+) to severe (4+) SAA, which was only observed in the distal muscles of the front (extensor carpi radialis, flexor carpi ulnaris, and palmar interossei) and the pelvic (tibialis cranialis, gastrocnemius, and plantar interossei) limbs (Table 2; Figure 2).

In the pattern 1, the MNCS of the tibial and ulnar nerves were characterized by normal compound muscle action potential (CMAP) and motor NCV in 5 dogs. The CMAP amplitude was reduced in 1 dog, but the motor NCV was normal. All the 5 dogs in the pattern 2 had reduced CMAP amplitudes (Figure 2). Three of these dogs also exhibited motor NCV reduction (Table 2).

In all dogs included in the study, RNS was normal. The F-waves for the tibial and ulnar nerves were not recordable in 3 dogs in pattern 1. Of the remaining 3 dogs in this group, 2 exhibited reduced F-waves persistence, with 1 displaying a minimum latency higher than expected based on limb length. One dog had normal F-waves. F-waves could not be recorded in any of the 5 dogs in pattern 2 (Figure 2).

In pattern 1 dogs, SNAP could be recorded in all the nerves evaluated during SNCS, except for 1 tibial nerve. It was observed that the tibial nerves of 3 dogs in this group with recordable SNAP exhibited a reduction in amplitude and a reduced sensory NCV. The amplitude of the SNAP of the tibial nerve was normal in the other 2 dogs, but the sensory NCV was reduced. Of the 4 ulnar nerves evaluated in this group, 3 exhibited reduced SNAP amplitude and all 4 reduced sensory NCV. The SNCS of the peroneal nerve was also evaluated in 1 dog of pattern 1. In this dog, the SNAP amplitude and sensory NCV were reduced. Cord dorsum potential was recordable in all the tibial and ulnar nerves evaluated in dogs in pattern 1. The CDP of the tibial nerves of all the dogs in this group had a higher onset latency than expected (Table 3; Figure 3).

All 5 dogs in the pattern 2 group had the SNCS of the tibial and ulnar nerves evaluated. The SNCS of the saphenous and the radial nerves were evaluated in 3 dogs and 1 dog, respectively. Apart from 1 ulnar nerve that exhibited a recordable, albeit reduced-amplitude, SNAP, and a reduced sensory NCV, SNAP could not be recorded in any of the other evaluated nerves of this group of dogs. Other tests

Biopsies of the peroneal nerve and the tibialis cranialis muscle were performed in 5 dogs. In 3 of these, the caudal sural cutaneous nerve was also collected. The peroneal nerve of the only dog in the pattern 1 group evaluated showed axonal degeneration of some small- and medium-diameter myelinated fibers. This dog also showed mild signs of neurogenic muscle atrophy. The nerve biopsies of the other 4 dogs (pattern 2) revealed an extensive loss of myelinated fibers. This loss of myelinated fibers was evident in both the peroneal and caudal sural cutaneous nerves. Muscle biopsies of these dogs showed moderate to severe signs of neurogenic muscle atrophy. The mixed breed Border Collie was homozygous for the RETREG1 variant, which is described as the causal variant of HSAN in Border Collies and Border Collie crossbreeds.6,9

Treatment and outcome

For all dogs, follow-up information was available until death/euth-anasia or for at least 2 years after diagnosis. Before referral for the neurological consultation, 5 dogs received meloxicam (0.1 mg/kg q24h, PO; Meloxoral, Dechra Veterinary Products S.r.l., Turin, IT). Due to lack of improvement, tapering dose of prednisolone (0.5-1 mg/kg q24h, PO; Prednicortone, Dechra Veterinary Products S.r.l., Turin, IT) was administered in 2 dogs and discontinued several weeks before referral. All dogs underwent short-term or long-term treatment with gabapentin (10 mg/kg q8h; Gabapentin Teva Pharma, Teva Italia S.r.l., Milan, IT), the benefits of which were unclear. Seven dogs received different types of antibiotics for skin ulcers or osteomyelitis infections, which were secondary to self-mutilation or knuckling of the paws. The dogs of pattern 1 group were maintained with bandages or E-collar to avoid further self-mutilation.

All dogs in pattern 2 were euthanized within 12 months of being examined neurologically. The Bernese Mountain Dog was euthanized 1 year after diagnosis due to deterioration in its gait and concomitant multidrug-resistant osteomyelitis. The mixed breed Border Collie and another mixed breed dog developed megaesophagus. They were both euthanized approximately 6 months after diagnosis, following recurrent episodes of aspiration pneumonia which compromised their quality of life. Another mixed breed dog was euthanized a few months after the neurological consultation, due to deterioration in ambulation. The last dog developed urinary incontinence 1 year after the neurological consultation and was euthanized 3 months later due to recurrent episodes of cystitis.

Of the dogs in pattern 1, 1 dog was euthanized at the owner request 13 months after diagnosis for the inability to be kept without muzzle and bandages. Two dogs were lost to follow-up approximately 2 years after diagnosis. The remaining 3 dogs died of natural causes or diseases unrelated to sensory neuropathy. The owners of 2 of these dogs reported an apparent reduction over time of the tendency to self-mutilate.

Discussion

This retrospective multicentric study describes the clinical presentation and electrodiagnostic features of a cohort of dogs suspected of having HSAN, a rare condition that remains poorly characterized in veterinary medicine.2,3

The predominant features of the dogs included in our study—distal loss of nociception, proprioceptive deficits, self-mutilation, reduced spinal reflexes, and autonomic dysfunction—closely resemble those described in both human HSAN and previously reported cases in dogs.1–3,6–20 Congenital insensitivity to pain (CIP),4,5 the main differential diagnosis of HSAN, was considered unlikely in our cases due to an apparent lack of clinical signs at birth, as well as frequent proprioceptive and motor deficits and alterations at EDX.4 In fact, only nociceptive fibers are usually lost in humans and dogs affected by CIP, and the clinical manifestations of this dysfunction are present from birth.4,5

As reported in HSAN in human patients the degree of sensory, motor, and autonomic involvement varied between individuals.1,2 This ranged from dogs with preserved gait and proprioception19,20 to those with severe tetraparesis and suspected autonomic signs, such as urinary incontinence and regurgitation, the latter of which being caused by megaesophagus.2,7 In both humans and dogs, the presence of motor deficits can raise doubts as to whether the neuropathy should be classified as HSAN or HSMN.1,6 Because lack of pain sensation in the extremities of the limbs, which is regarded as one of the principal signs of HSAN,1 is not generally reported in dogs with HSMN,2 we established this as an inclusion criterion for our cases, without excluding subjects who also exhibited motor deficits.

Two electroclinical patterns emerged in particular in our population. Dogs classified as pattern 1 exhibited acral mutilation and absent nociception alongside usually preserved proprioception, with no signs of autonomic involvement. Of these 6 dogs, 4 were Miniature Pinschers and 2 were Miniature Pinscher crossbreeds. The clinical presentation was similar to that previously reported in Miniature Pinschers,20 Pointers,11,19 Spaniels,8,17 Fox Terriers,3 and German Spitz.10

Pattern 2 dogs showed a loss of both nociception and proprioception, but not self-mutilation. This form was similar to those observed in Long-haired Dachshunds,14,15 Jack Russell Terriers,18 and Border Collies.6,9,12,13,16 A notable difference is the self-mutilation of the toes reported in some Border Collies and, in 1 Long-haired Dachshund, of the genitals. The inversion disrupting RETREG1 gene responsible for HSAN in Border Collies and Border Collie crosses did indeed affect one of the dogs of pattern 2 group.6

During the follow-up period, 3 of these dogs showed signs of suspected autonomic nervous system involvement, such as regurgitation or urinary incontinence.

The electrodiagnostic findings were consistent with the observed clinical signs. Apart from 1 dog with an absent tibial SNAP, all the other dogs in pattern 1 on SNCS exhibited a reduction in SNAP amplitude or a reduced sensory NCV, or both. Similarly, the onset latency of the CDP of the tibial nerve was greater than the expected latency based on limb length.26 No such findings have ever been reported in the very limited number of similar cases that have undergone EDX. The 2 dogs (a Fox Terrier and a Pointer Puppy) that underwent SNCS had a normal sensory NCVs.3,11 The SNAP amplitude was normal in the Pointer and only mildly reduced in the Fox Terrier.3,11 The CDP recording was not performed in these subjects. The difficulties in interpreting these test results are well known.23,26,27 This is due to variability in sensory potential amplitude (both SNAP and CDP) even in normal subjects, and possible inaccuracy in measurement of sensory NCV when the stimulus and recording electrodes are close together.23,26,27 Therefore, to strengthen our findings, we compared the recorded values with those of the normal tibial nerves of a group of dogs of a similar size and limb length. Significant reductions were confirmed in the mean amplitude of both the SNAP and the CDP, as well as in the mean sensory NCV of the tibial nerve. As was seen in previous similar cases, which were characterized by normal EMG and MNCS,3,12 these tests were normal or only mildly affected in dogs in the pattern 1 group. Similar electrodiagnostic and clinical findings have been reported in type II HSAN in humans.1

The dogs in the pattern 2 group, except for one, showed no recordable SNAP or CDP which suggests a greater degree of degeneration in the sensory pathways.23,26,27 Abnormal SNCS with often not recordable SNAP were previously reported in affected Border Collies,12,13,16 Long-haired Dachshunds,15 Jack Russell Terrier,18 and mixed breed dogs.7 No data are available regarding the CDP of these dogs. At EMG, moderate to severe SAA of the distal appendicular muscles was recorded in pattern 2 dogs. Based on these findings, concomitant length-dependent axonopathy of motor fibers can be suspected.28 At MNCS, a reduction in CMAP amplitude was observed in all dogs in this group. Two dogs also exhibited reduced motor NCV suggesting the possible presence of concurrent demyelination.23

Similar electrodiagnostic findings are reported in type I HSAN of humans.1 In earlier reports, EMG and MNCS were variably affected in dogs with HSAN that showed similar clinical signs.6,7,9,12,16

All the dogs in our study had normal RNS. Except for 1 dog, they either had absent F-waves or F-waves characterized by decreased persistence. While the normal RNS results are not unexpected given that this is a functional test of the neuromuscular junction,23 the alterations detected in the F-waves of HSAN dogs are somewhat surprising. Indeed, F-waves should not be affected by abnormalities in sensory fibers, since they represent a purely motor event.23 In fact, the only case of HSAN in the previous veterinary literature that had undergone F-waves testing was found to have normal latency.13 Abnormalities in F-waves recordings have occasionally been reported in humans with HSAN as a consequence of concurrent motor fiber involvement.29 Considering the evidence of impaired motor fiber function revealed by EMG and MNCS, a similar mechanism is a plausible explanation for the F-waves alterations observed in dogs in pattern 2. It is harder to interpret the lack of F-waves recordings in some dogs in pattern 1, given the minor changes seen in EMG and MNCS. For these subjects, it is possible to hypothesize that the absence or reduced persistence of F-waves is caused either by myelin damage to the proximal motor fibers or ventral roots of the spinal nerves, resulting in conduction block, or by reduced excitability of the lower motor neurons.23

The electroclinical pattern also appeared to influence the outcome. Most of the dogs in pattern 1 were reported to have stable signs of the neurological disease over time. Nevertheless, since the follow-up information was collected by telephone, the possibility of a slow worsening of neurological signs (eg, the onset/aggravation of proprioceptive deficits) cannot be ruled out. However, considering the absence of autonomic signs as well, the overall data suggest a more benign phenotype than dogs of pattern 2, which is consistent with previous reports.3,10,17

The clinical and electrodiagnostic findings in the pattern 1 dogs, which mainly indicate nociceptive dysfunction and only marginally show proprioceptive and motor abnormalities, are consistent with the neuropathological findings in published cases of Miniature Pinscher and Pointer dogs that underwent complete necropsies.11,20 These forms are commonly classified as neuronopathies based on histopathological findings, as they are characterized by degeneration of the primary nociceptive neurons in the dorsal root ganglia.2,11,20 Secondary degeneration and loss of sensory fibers in the peripheral nerves, dorsal nerve roots, and in the periphery of the lateral and ventral funiculi, together with neuronal loss, diffuse fibrosis, and spongiosis in the laminae of the dorsal horns of the spinal cord in the lumbosacral area were reported in these dogs.11,20 Similarly to the previously reported case of a Miniature Pinscher with HSAN, the only Miniature Pinscher in the pattern 1 group that underwent histopathology of peripheral nerves showed fiber loss, particularly of small and medium myelinated fibers.20

Rapid clinical deterioration and poor outcomes, including euthanasia due to a decline in quality of life, was characteristic of dogs of pattern 2. The presence of a more diffuse process involving both proprioceptive and nociceptive sensory fibers, as well as more severe degeneration of motor fibers, as demonstrated by EDX, together with frequent autonomic nerve fiber involvement, can explain the worse prognosis for dogs in pattern 2. Dogs with similar clinical phenotypes that have undergone nerve biopsies and necropsies have shown varying degrees of myelinated fiber loss and Wallerian degeneration in sensory, autonomic, and mixed nerves.11–14,17 In our study, of the 4 dogs in pattern 2 that underwent nerve biopsies, a moderate to severe loss of myelinated fibers was present in both sensory and mixed nerves, alongside moderate signs of denervation of muscle fibers. Two of the dogs also displayed signs of demyelination, confirming the MNCS findings. Peripheral nerve demyelination has been previously reported in Long-haired Dachshunds with HSAN.14

Miniature Pinscher and Miniature Pinscher crossbreeds were overrepresented in our study. Apart from one Border Collie crossbreed, none of the other breeds recognized as being affected by HSAN—such as some hunting dog breeds—were present among the enrolled dogs.3,11,17 This result probably reflects the canine population in the geographical area in which the referral clinics that contributed to the study operate. It seems to support the hypothesis that this condition is present in Miniature Pinscher bloodlines, at least in some southern European countries, as previously suggested.3,20

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective and multicentric design. The inclusion of cases from 8 veterinary neurology referral centers over a 17-year period precluded standardization of clinical assessments and diagnostic protocols apart from EDX, which were all performed by one author and with the same equipment. Moreover, it complicated the process of obtaining detailed outcomes for several cases. The outcome information was based solely on telephone interviews with owners or referring veterinarians and was therefore often incomplete.

The relatively small number of cases included is another weakness of our study. This is despite the study’s long investigative period and the large number of centers involved. The findings confirm the low incidence of HSAN in dogs, consistent with the limited number of documented clinical cases in veterinary literature.6–20 The global prevalence of HSAN in human medicine is unknown; however, it has been reported to affect around 4% of neuropathy patients in the United Kingdom.1,4

The known causal variants responsible for HSAN in dogs involve the RETREG1 and GDNF genes.6–8 The dogs in our study were not tested for these variants, with the exception of the Border Collie crossbreed that tested positive for the RETREG1 gene variant reported in Border Collies and Border Collie crossbreeds.6,9 Moreover, no genetic analysis was conducted to determine the variant causing these cases. This information would have confirmed the genetic basis of these dogs’ neuropathies, establishing genotype–phenotype correlations and increasing our knowledge of HSAN in dogs.

In human medicine, certain codified and validated tests are used to identify and quantify autonomic dysfunctions, which are useful for correctly classifying the type of HSAN present.30 Autonomic function was not specifically assessed in our cases as similar autonomic function testing is mostly unavailable for dogs.31 Although we cannot rule out other pathogenic mechanisms, clinical findings such as urinary incontinence and megaesophagus suggested autonomic involvement in some dogs in the pattern 2 group.

Another limitation of this study is that nerve biopsies were performed on only 5 dogs, and only 1 dog in the pattern 1 group underwent such an investigation. No dorsal root ganglion biopsies or complete necropsies were available for any of the dogs. If more comprehensive histopathological investigations had been available, they could have provided useful information to better characterize these pathologies.

Conclusions

Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy in dogs is characterized by early-onset distal sensory loss with variable motor and autonomic involvement. This study shows that electrodiagnostic testing is a useful add-on to neurological examinations for evaluating these disorders.

Sensory nerve conduction studies and CDP recordings, in particular, reveal sensory pathway dysfunction and can help to distinguish between different disease patterns. In fact, as demonstrated by some of our cases, SNCS and CDP are altered even when the disorder appears to affect only nociceptive fibers clinically.

Dogs with abnormal but preserved sensory responses on SNCS and CDP appear to have a more benign, nonprogressive or slowly progressive course. In contrast, dogs with absent sensory responses show rapid progression and a poor outcome. A comprehensive electrodiagnostic assessment can, therefore, represent a valuable tool for supporting diagnosis, improving clinical classification, and providing prognostic information in dogs with suspected HSAN, along with other investigations like histopathology and, when available, genetic tests.

Further prospective studies incorporating systematic genetic investigations are warranted to better define the pathophysiology and classification of this rare neuropathy.

Electrodiagnostic studies (EDX)

Electrodiagnostic studies (EDX) were performed using commercially available electrodiagnostic equipment (Myoquick, Micromed S.p.A., Treviso, Italy).

All EDX procedures were performed on dogs under general anesthesia. Induction was achieved using methadone (0.1-0.2 mg/kg intramuscular; Semfortan, Dechra Veterinary Products S.r.l., Turin, IT) in conjunction with dexmedetomidine (2-4 μg/kg intramuscular; Dexdomitor, Vetoquinol Italia S.r.l., Forlì-Cesena, IT). Fifteen minutes later, an intravenous catheter was placed, and propofol (1-5 mg/kg, Proposure, Boehringer Ingelheim Animal Health Italia S.p.A., Milan, IT) was administered intravenously. Then, an endotracheal tube was placed, and anesthesia was maintained with an inspired fraction of 2% isoflurane (Isoflon, Zoetis Italia S.r.l., Rome, IT).

During EDX rectal temperature was monitored, and heating pads were used if necessary to maintain the dog’s temperature between 37 °C and 38 °C. A neuromuscular blocker (Cisatracurium, 0.1 mg/kg slow intravenous bolus; Cisatracurio, Mylan Pharma, Milan, IT) was administered before recording SNCS and CDP.

Disposable concentric needle electrodes (Spes Medica S.r.l., Genova, Italy) were used for EMG recordings. Monopolar needle electrodes (disposable monopolar needle electrode, Spes Medica S.r.l., Genova, Italy) were used for electrical stimulation and recording of the other electrodiagnostic tests. For EMG recordings, the “ground” electrode (E0) was placed on a bony prominence; for the other EDX, between the stimulating and recording electrodes.

Electromyography was used to evaluate the epaxial spinal muscles of the lumbar and cervical areas, as well as the muscles of the head, front limbs, and pelvic limbs. The muscles tested in the head were the orbicularis oculi, the masseter and the temporalis, as well as the muscles of the tongue. The muscles tested in the front limb were the supraspinatus, the triceps brachii, the biceps brachii, the extensor carpi radialis, the flexor carpi ulnaris, and the palmar interossei. The muscles tested in the pelvic limbs were the quadriceps femoris, semitendinosus, tibialis cranialis, gastrocnemius, and plantar interossei.

A brief burst of insertional activity due to the needle stimulating the muscle fibers during insertion, followed by the absence of spontaneous electrical activity, except for physiological endplate potentials, was consistent with a normal EMG recording.23 The presence of spontaneous abnormal activity (SAA), such as fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves, and complex repetitive discharges, was consistent with an abnormal EMG recording. The density on the screen and spread across the muscle were used to grade SAA from 0 to +4 for each muscle.32

After EMG, MNCS were performed on the sciatic-tibial and ulnar nerves. The active recording needle (E1) was inserted into the plantar and palmar interossei muscles and the reference electrode (E2) was inserted subcutaneously on the distal plantar and palmar surface of the paw.23,33 Proximal stimulation of the sciatic-tibial nerve was performed at the level of the trochanteric fossa. For the ulnar nerve, stimulation was performed at the level of the medial region of the elbow.23,33 Distal stimulation of the sciatic-tibial nerve was performed over the tarsus and of the ulnar nerve over the medial region of the carpus.

At each stimulation point, the electrodes were placed 10-20 mm apart, with the cathode positioned distal to the anode.23,33 Stimulation was achieved using 0.1 ms rectangular pulses at a supramaximal intensity.

The following variables were evaluated at MNCS: motor NCV, CMAP amplitude (peak-to-peak), CMAP duration (from initial deviation of the CMAP from baseline to its final return to baseline), and CMAP morphology23,33.

Repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS) was performed by keeping the recording and stimulating needle electrodes in the same position as for distal stimulation for the tibial and ulnar nerve MNCS.23 Trains of 9 supramaximal stimuli at 2 and 3 Hz were applied.23,34 The RNS was considered normal if the CMAP amplitudes and areas did not decrease by more than 10% between the first and fifth stimulations.34

The F-waves were elicited by keeping the recording and stimulating needle electrodes in the same position as for distal stimulation of the tibial and ulnar nerve MNCS, but with the anode and cathode inverted. Two trains of 10 consecutive supramaximal stimulations were applied, and their corresponding responses were recorded. The minimum F-waves latency for each train was measured and compared with the expected latency obtained from the following equations23:

Sciatic–tibial nerve expected minimum F-waves latency = 3.45 + 0.33 × Limb length (in centimeters, from the greater trochanter to the fingertips).

Ulnar nerve expected minimum F-waves latency = 6.03 + 0.22 × Limb length (in centimeters, from the cranial angle of the shoulder blade to the fingertips).

The percentage of trials with detectable F-waves (defined as F-waves persistence) was evaluated for each train. This was calculated by dividing the number of recorded F-waves by the number of stimulations. Persistence was considered normal if it fell within the range of 80%-100%.35

Sensory nerve conduction studies (SNCS) were performed after the F-waves. The stimulating electrodes were inserted subcutaneously in the autonomous sensory area of the tibial (plantar surface of the paw), ulnar (palmar surface of the paw), radial (dorsal surface of thoracic limb paw), peroneal (dorsal surface of the paw, proximal to the metatarsophalangeal joint), or saphenous (medial surface, proximal to the knee) nerves.25,34 The recording electrodes were left in the same positions used for the distal stimulation of the tibial nerve and for the proximal stimulation of the ulnar nerve during MNCS.27,36 For the radial nerve, the recording electrodes were placed on the lateral surface of the elbow (caudal to the medial epicondyle of the humerus). 27 For the peroneal nerve, the recording electrodes were placed on the caudolateral surface of the knee (on the lateral surface of the lateral head of the gastrocnemius muscle).27,36 For the saphenous nerve, the recording electrodes were placed on the medial surface of the proximal third of the thigh (close to the femoral artery).23,27 Stimuli of supramaximal intensity were applied. Recordings were digitally averaged until a SNAP, characterized by an initial small positive wave followed by a larger negative wave, was detected.23 If a SNAP potential was obtained, the recording was repeated to check the reproducibility of the potential. The amplitude was measured from the peak of the positive deflection to the top of the negative peak.23,36 Amplitude of SNAP and sensory NCV were compared with reference ranges reported in the literature.36,37

The cathode and anode positions for stimulating the CDP of the tibial and ulnar nerves were the same as for the F-waves. The CDP for tibial nerve was recorded by placing the E1 electrode on the midline between the spinous processes of the fourth and fifth lumbar vertebrae and for the ulnar nerve at the level of the dorsal lamina of T1.23,26 The E2 electrode was placed subcutaneously 20-30 mm laterally on the side opposite to the stimulated limb.23,26 Similarly to the SNCS, supramaximal intensity stimuli were used. The recording obtained with the signal averaging technique was repeated twice to verify its reproducibility.

When present, the CDP was characterized by a main negative wave, followed by a prolonged positive wave.26,38 For the tibial nerve, the CDP amplitude, measured from baseline to the peak of the negative potential,23 and the onset latency were evaluated. The recorded onset latency of CDP for the tibial nerve was compared with the expected onset latency based on the limb length, which was estimated using the following equation23:

CDP (hock stimulation) expected onset latency = −1.194 + (0.014 × pelvic limb length) (mm)

Only the recordability of ulnar nerve CDP was evaluated due to the lack of normal reference ranges for amplitude and onset latency for this potential in this nerve.

Statistical analysis

Given the homogeneity of breed and size among the 6 dogs in pattern 1, we performed a statistical analysis to compare the SNCS and CDP amplitude results for the tibial nerve of these dogs with those of a control group of normal dogs with similar limb length. The control group consisted of 6 dogs that underwent SNCS and CDP of the tibial nerve of the normal limb for comparison with the pathological contralateral limb. To be included in the control group, the dogs had to have a similar hind-limb length to that of the affected dogs, measured from the greater trochanter of the femur to the tip of the middle toe. They also had to be under 10 years of age.
