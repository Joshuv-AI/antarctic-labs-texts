Living rent free in your head: resident memory T cells
Authors: Hanna N Degefu, Sierra A Kleist, Pamela C Rosato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) are a distinct subset of memory T cells that persist long-term within non-lymphoid tissues and provide rapid, localized immune protection. The central nervous system (CNS) was among the first sites in which these TRM were detected, but only recently has their importance in brain immune surveillance been fully appreciated. Despite their relative low abundance, brain TRM can protect against reinfection, contribute to neuropathology, and participate in neurologic diseases. This review provides an overview of what is currently known about TRM in the brain, with a focus on CD8+ T cells, identifies major knowledge gaps, and highlights implications for human brain TRM biology. Addressing these gaps will help contextualize brain TRM in health and disease and guide strategies to harness or modulate them for vaccination and neuroimmune therapies.

Introduction

Memory T cells play a vital role in protection against reinfections. Historically, memory T cells were classified into 2 subsets based on their expression of secondary lymphoid organ (SLO) homing molecules and levels of effector molecule production; central memory T cells (TCM) express high levels of CCR7 and CD62L and primarily recirculate through blood, lymph, and SLOs, while effector memory T cells (TEM) express high levels of integrins and chemokine receptors, allowing for recirculation through nonlymphoid tissues in addition to the blood and lymph.1 The discovery of a third memory T cell subset, which remains in peripheral tissues without recirculating, overturned the prevailing view that T cells only transiently surveil tissues. Early studies provided hints of these resident memory T cells (TRM), detecting T cells with distinct phenotypes in non-lymphoid tissues long after the resolution of infection.2–5 This concept of tissue residency was solidified by concurrently published reports characterizing TRM as a distinct population from their circulating counterparts.6,7 Both of these studies used transplant models to demonstrate that memory T cells found within non-lymphoid tissues (skin and small intestine) did not re-enter circulation during steady-state conditions and were not replenished by T cells in the circulation or surrounding tissue. Subsequent studies used long-term depletion of circulating cells,8,9 intravenous antibody labeling,10 and parabiosis surgery to show that the majority of T cells within non-lymphoid tissues are resident.11

The first indication of T cell residency in any non-lymphoid tissue came from studies in the brain,2,5 which was also among the first tissues in which bona fide TRM were formally described.12,13 Despite this, the concept of brain TRM did not gain much traction until relatively recently. This is perhaps due to the historical view that the immune-privileged status of the central nervous system (CNS) restricted T cells from actively trafficking into and surveilling the brain. With the re-discovery of meningeal lymphatics and the growing appreciation of T cell involvement in neurologic processes, brain TRM have now become a burgeoning area of active investigation. In this review, we highlight what is currently known about TRM in the brain, with a focus on CD8+ T cells, highlight needed areas of investigation, and speculate on human brain TRM biology.

TRM in mouse and human brain

Much of our understanding of murine brain TRM has been derived from studies using infection models. The earliest evidence of TRM-like cells in the mouse brain came from a study showing the persistence of CD8+ T cells following intracranial influenza infection, long after viral clearance.2 These cells were localized in the parenchyma and expressed CD69, which at the time was viewed as an indicator of antigen engagement, but is now a commonly used marker of TRM. Subsequent studies over a decade later defined bona fide brain CD8+ TRM, distinct from circulating T cells, as CD69+ CD103−/+ after local infection.12,13 Since then, murine brain TRM have been identified following diverse CNS infections,8,14–21 peripheral infections and vaccinations,9,22–26 and in the context of CNS autoimmunity and neurodegeneration.27–30 CD8+ T cells with TRM signatures have also been identified in human postmortem brains with no neurologic disease,31,32 in the context of multiple sclerosis (MS),32–34 neurodegenerative diseases,32,35 neurocognitive disorders,36 and COVID-19.37 Despite identification of these cells across species, many questions remain about CNS TRM biology including their functional diversity, how anatomic niches within the CNS may influence this diversity, and how mouse studies translate to human TRM biology.

Brain TRM functions

A hallmark of CD8+ TRM across all tissues is their “sensing and alarm” functions that allow for rapid detection of reinfections leading to local immune infiltration and activation. First demonstrated in barrier tissues (skin and female reproductive tract), TRM reactivated via cognate antigen were shown to produce inflammatory cytokines interleukin 2 (IL-2), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), and interferon γ (IFN-γ) and chemokines CCL3 and CCL4, which promoted the recruitment and activation of other adaptive and innate immune cells,38–40 and entry of circulating antibody into tissues.41 Indeed, several murine models in diverse tissues have shown that TRM can protect from reinfections,6,8,39,42–48 and even prevent tumor growth and recurrence49–53 without assistance from recruited circulating memory T cells, emphasizing their potent capabilities as the first line of defense.

Similar “sensing and alarm” functions have recently been described for CD8+ brain TRM. Upon reactivation with cognate antigen, these cells recruit and activate monocytes, including macrophages and dendritic cells, and stimulate microglia, which upregulate expression of CD122, MHC-II, CD11c, and CD86.54,55 TRM reactivation also increases infiltration of other immune populations, including natural killer cells, CD4+ T cells, regulatory T cells, and B cells within the brain.55 Collectively, brain CD8+ TRM confer protection against intracranial reinfections8,9,12,20 which, in some contexts, is achieved through production of IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, and perforin.8,9,17,20 While their protective role upon reinfection is well established, the extent to which TRM contribute through direct killing of infected cells remains incompletely understood. In a lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) infection model, viral clearance was dependent on the production of perforin, suggesting cytotoxicity was required.8 Additionally, intracranial injection of peptide-loaded antigen-presenting cells resulted in direct killing of target cells, indicating that brain TRM are capable of cytotoxicity.12,55 Despite this, studies have demonstrated that viral control in the CNS can occur through noncytolytic mechanisms mediated by cytokines.56–59 For example, control of VSV infection in CNS neurons was not dependent on direct interactions between infected neurons and T cells, but instead relied on noncytolytic control mediated by interactions with microglia cross-presenting viral antigen to CD8+ T cells.60 Therefore, while brain TRM do have the ability to directly kill target cells, it is still unclear how common this is during viral infection of the CNS where excessive cytotoxicity could cause irreversible damage to non-regenerating neurons.

Less studied is the role of TRM activated in an antigen-independent, bystander manner, and how these cells contribute to immune responses. Bystander-activated T cells become stimulated in a T cell receptor (TCR)-independent manner by cytokines present during inflammation or infection. These cells are capable of producing inflammatory cytokines and even killing cells in an MHC-independent manner through stress receptor-ligand interactions.61 For example, in the lung TRM can be bystander-activated in response to inflammatory stimuli, viral infection, or bacterial products, which can potentiate inflammatory responses and resolve infection.62,63 In contrast, one study found that bystander-activated brain TRM were insufficient to protect against a CNS viral rechallenge.8 While this suggests bystander-activated TRM in the brain may not contribute to protection from infection, it is unclear how they may impact neurologic diseases. For example, a recent study found that physiological microbial exposure, through co-housing laboratory mice with pet store mice or sequential infection, resulted in a significant increase in circulating and resident CD8+ T cells in the brain. Notably, the presence of T cells in the brains of sequentially infected mice was associated with increased duration of experimentally-induced seizures.23 While this suggests that brain TRM can influence neurologic diseases, further investigation is needed to elucidate the bystander functions of TRM in the brain across different disease contexts.

In contrast to mice, little is known about the function of TRM in the human brain. One study found that CD8+ TRM from human brain tissue stimulated in vitro produced IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GM-CSF.32 While this study was performed in vitro, it demonstrates that human brain TRM are capable of producing cytokines, and therefore may perform “sensing and alarm” functions in vivo. In support of this, resident-like T cells specific for common viral infections have been identified in human brain tumors, and following incubation of tumor slices with cognate antigen, genes associated with TRM functions were upregulated, including IFN-γ and chemokines.64 Moreover, higher frequencies of T cells expressing the canonical TRM marker, CD103, have been correlated with improved survival in patients with glioblastoma, suggesting resident-like T cells in brain tumors may contribute to an anti-tumor response.65 Although these findings point to parallels between TRM in mouse models and humans, T cells in human tissues remain difficult to study due to limited accessibility of donor tissue and technical constraints, and much is still unknown about how these cells function within the human CNS.

While microbe-specific TRM are protective against infection, they have also been implicated in worsened neurological inflammatory disorders and neurodegenerative diseases. For example, interactions between microglia and CD8+ TRM specific for neuropathic viruses have been shown to drive gliosis and synapse elimination resulting in cognitive dysfunction.16,21,66 Peripheral infections may also seed the brain with TRM that can contribute to diseases later in life. For example, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection earlier in life has been shown to increase the risk of developing multiple sclerosis (MS), a demyelinating autoimmune disorder.67 MS is characterized by an increase in the frequency of CD8+ T cells in the CNS,34 but the exact role of TRM in disease progression remains unclear. One human brain study found TRM in close contact with EBV-infected B cells in patient post-mortem samples, suggesting they may play a protective role in controlling infection while inadvertently contributing to pathology through sustained inflammation.68 In addition, aging studies have found that T cells expressing residency and activation markers accumulate in the CNS and interact with microglia, which can potentiate post-ischemic neuroinflammation and drive axon degeneration leading to cognitive and motor decline.28,69 Overall, TRM appear to have context-dependent effects in the brain, sometimes providing protection and other times contributing to pathology.

Revisiting brain TRM establishment and specificity

Given the ability of brain TRM to mediate both protection and pathology, understanding the signals that regulate their establishment will be essential for guiding strategies to either promote or limit their formation and maintenance in a disease-specific manner. The most evident way to seed CNS tissues with TRM is via local infection. Numerous studies have shown that direct infection of brain tissue, via intracranial infections or peripheral infections with neurologic dissemination, generates TRM in mice. These include acute viral infections (adenovirus, attenuated LCMV, vesicular stomatitis virus, West Nile virus, and Zika virus),8,9,12–16,30,66,70,71 chronic/latent infections (herpes simplex virus 1, LCMV Clone 13, murine cytomegalovirus, murine polyomavirus, and Theiler’s murine encephalomyelitis virus),18–21,54,72–77 bacterial infections (attenuated Listeria monocytogenes),78 and parasitic infections (Toxoplasma gondii)17,79,80 (Fig. 1A). These numerous studies highlight the common convention that CNS antigen and inflammation are required for brain TRM establishment, however, this notion has recently been challenged. Mouse studies have shown that gut microbiota,81 physiological microbial exposure,23 peripheral infections,9,22,24,25,82 or even immunization,9,26 can establish TRM in the brain (Fig. 1A). Indeed, more reductionist approaches support brain TRM establishment in the absence of CNS infection or even inflammation; brain TRM form under conditions of homeostatic proliferation in RAG−/− mice25 and from in vitro activated T cells transferred into naïve mice.75 While local antigen recognition at the site of infection can increase TRM numbers, particularly CD103+ TRM,9,12,25 these recent studies emphasize that this is not a requirement.

These murine studies raise the possibility that, as in mice, peripheral immune events can contribute to brain TRM seeding in humans (Fig. 1B). Evidence for this includes detection of T cells specific for the common peripheral infections influenza A, EBV, and cytomegalovirus in human primary and metastatic brain tumors,64,83,84 and EBV-specific T cells in patients with Alzheimer’s disease85 and MS.68 Moreover, following CD19-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy for peripheral B cell leukemias, brain TRM were detected in patient cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) 28 d post-infusion.86 Collectively, these mouse and human studies highlight a major knowledge gap—what is the specificity of TRM in the healthy human brain? This could conceivably encompass T cells for viral antigens, self-antigens, tumor-associated peptides or even cryptic epitopes generated through noncanonical translation or altered antigen processing.87 One way to identify antigen-specific T cells is through staining with tetramers that have defined peptide-MHC epitopes. This technique, however, requires live cells and given the low frequency of T cells in the CNS, can make acquiring sufficient amounts of tissue challenging. Fresh tissue can be obtained from neurosurgical procedures such as epilepsy resections, peri-adjacent tissue from brain tumor removals, or diagnostic biopsies, though these samples may be limited in size and may be affected by disease-related changes in the immune microenvironment. An alternative source is postmortem brain tissue, however obtaining tissue within a short postmortem interval is often challenging. TCR-based analyses using fixed or frozen brain samples offer an alternative for assessing antigen specificity, but they depend on known TCR–antigen pairs and may miss novel or brain-restricted specificities. In short, determining the true specificity of human brain TRM will require methods that combine high-resolution specificity mapping with access to suitable tissue.

Location of TRM in the CNS

While there are numerous studies to support the existence of TRM in the brain, it is important to note their relatively low density compared to other tissues. A recent study that combined literature searches and computational analysis provided estimates of immune cell density across the human body, including the brain, where CD3+ T cells were approximated at about 105 cells per gram of brain tissue.88 These densities are on par with those estimated in the heart, eyes, connective tissue and adipose tissue, but about 25-fold less than in reproductive and internal organs (eg ovaries, testes, salivary gland and pancreas), and roughly 200-fold lower than in barrier tissues such as the lungs, skin, and small intestine.88 As T cells surveil tissues by cell-cell contact, these numbers suggest that the precise location of TRM, for example at brain barrier interfaces or near sites of latent/chronic infection, could be critically important for their protective function. Even so, these numbers also highlight the notable presence of T cells in the brain—with the estimate of 105 cells/g tissue,88 and an average brain weight of 1.5 kg,89 the adult human brain is home to roughly 150 million T cells.

T cell infiltration within the brain is shaped by the tightly regulated and anatomically complex structure of the CNS. The CNS is enveloped by the meninges, a barrier tissue that consists of three layers: the dura, arachnoid, and pia mater.90 The dura mater is attached to the skull and contains blood and lymphatic vessels. Beneath the dura, the pia and arachnoid (collectively known as leptomeninges) are separated by the subarachnoid space through which CSF circulates. The CSF is a protective fluid produced mainly by the choroid plexus, which is also important for nutrient transport and waste clearance in the CNS. The subarachnoid space also contains arterial vessels that penetrate the CNS parenchyma, giving rise to perivascular spaces, which serve as a site of CSF fluid exchange. The vasculature found within the leptomeninges is characterized by a specialized endothelium that forms the blood–brain barrier (BBB), restricting molecular and cellular movement between the blood and CNS. Although such barriers limit immune cell entry into the parenchyma, specialized structures like circumventricular organs found in the midline of the brain lack conventional BBBs, enabling them to serve as potential entry points for lymphocytes and other immune cells into the CNS.91

TRM in CNS border regions

The choroid plexus is a secretory circumventricular organ located in the cerebral ventricles that can serve as a route for lymphocyte migration from the blood into the CNS. In mice, CD8+ T cells with a TRM phenotype have been detected in the choroid plexus under both homeostatic and disease conditions, including infection, and accumulate with age in mice.2,15,17,69,81,92 Moreover, in neurodegenerative disease models such as Alzheimer’s disease, CD8+ TRM have been shown to interact with choroid plexus epithelial cells, influencing neuroinflammatory responses.93 Human postmortem analyses similarly identify CD8+ TRM populations in the choroid plexus.81,94,95 TRM have also been identified in other circumventricular organs, such as the area postrema and the subfornical organ in both mouse and human studies.81 Despite their identification in most circumventricular organs, very few studies provide quantitative and phenotypic comparisons of CD8+ T cells across these regions and adjacent parenchyma. Given their permeable vasculature and distinct stromal and epithelial cell composition, these compartments are likely to impose unique retention cues and functional programs on TRM.

Beyond the ventricular interfaces, perivascular spaces form a distinct niche within the CNS. In healthy human white matter, Smolders et al. used laminin co-staining to delineate vessel basement membranes and showed that the majority of brain T cells reside in the perivascular spaces.31,32 Similarly, an independent human study using positional mapping also found about 75% of CD8+ T cells were located in perivascular spaces.96 In human MS brain, quantitative histology showed CD8+ T cells are also predominantly perivascular space-localized with a TRM profile.34 Functionally, depletion of circulation-accessible perivascular CD8+ cells did not impair protection mediated by parenchymal TRM in murine infection models, indicating compartmentalized roles for perivascular and parenchymal populations.9 As these perivascular TRM reside at the junction of blood vessels and brain parenchyma, and sit within the CSF, they may represent a more malleable population, poised to infiltrate the brain or re-enter circulation; however, the stability and migration dynamics of perivascular TRM are unclear.

The meninges form a specialized immune interface at the CNS border, supporting T cell surveillance and local immune responses. In mice, the dural venous sinuses are key routes for antigen trafficking as well as hubs for TRM establishment in the dural meninges.97 A complementary human atlas of the leptomeninges that mapped diverse stromal and immune populations showed that meningeal T cells are mostly CD8+ and display features of TRM.98 Meningeal CD8+ TRM have also been detected in young and aged mice69,81,97 and are observed in human dura and leptomeninges in the absence of inflammatory stimuli.81,95 Some studies suggest that there may be differences in meningeal versus parenchymal brain TRM. For example, following intracranial LCMV infection, meningeal TRM displayed higher proliferation (Ki-67) and pSTAT5 expression compared with parenchymal counterparts, and a higher frequency of Listeria-specific TRM in the parenchyma expressed CD103 than in the meninges.8,9 Collectively, mouse and human data establish meninges as an active immunological interface that harbor TRM with distinct phenotypes; however, it is still unclear how these meningeal TRM may differ functionally from TRM in other brain compartments.

TRM in brain parenchyma

T cells were long thought to be scarce in the brain parenchyma due to restrictive CNS barriers, with most immune populations confined to border regions such as the meninges or choroid plexus. However, numerous mouse studies have demonstrated that CD8+ TRM can establish and persist within the parenchyma following local or peripheral infections.2,8,9,12,14,16,17,19,22–24 In humans, CD8+ TRM have been documented within the white matter parenchyma in both healthy individuals and in the context of neurodegenerative diseases.29,31,32,34,95,99 In disease states, parenchymal TRM frequently localize near sites of pathology, such as plaques and lesions, where they may contribute either to clearance of damaged cells/aggregates or to bystander tissue injury via cytotoxic activity and release of proinflammatory cytokines.34,100 As these TRM reside among sensitive and non-replenishable cells such as neurons, their functions are likely different from TRM in border regions, however, this remains to be elucidated.

TRM in CSF

CSF flows continuously from its primary source, the choroid plexus, through perivascular spaces and the subarachnoid space. Although CSF is a fluid compartment, studies report CD8+ T cells with TRM-like features in the CSF of healthy donors,95,101 patients with neurodegenerative disorders35,101 and patients receiving CAR T cell therapy.86 For example, one study found that CSF collected by lumbar puncture from healthy donors contained CD8+ T cells which expressed transcriptional signatures associated with residency (eg CD69) and cytotoxicity (eg granzymes A and K).101 This is consistent with a recent study which profiled intrathecally sampled, CSF-derived T cells from post-mortem donors and found that most CD8+ T cells expressed markers indicative of residency including CD69 and CD103.95 While these studies clearly demonstrate that CD8+ T cells in human CSF exhibit a TRM phenotype, they also reveal an important knowledge gap; what are the migratory dynamics of memory T cells in homeostatic CSF, and more fundamentally, can a T cell be truly resident within a fluid compartment?

TRM diversity

Between-tissue heterogeneity

Although TRM share core features, they also reflect tissue-specific cues that enable them to adapt and persist in specialized environments.102 Factors expressed by TRM, such as chemokine receptors, transcription factors, cell surface receptors, and adhesion molecules, can vary depending on the tissue environment. For example, in mice, epithelial tissues such as skin and salivary gland are dominated by a TRM population expressing the integrin CD103, while TRM in liver and kidney typically lack this integrin.103,104 Additionally, some transcription factors are uniquely expressed in specific tissues, such as the transcription repressor hypermethylated in cancer 1 (Hic1), which was found to be uniquely upregulated in intraepithelial lymphocyte (IEL) TRM.104,105 There have been relatively few studies directly comparing CD8+ TRM in the CNS with those in peripheral organs. High expression of the immune checkpoint receptor PD-1 was initially considered a defining feature of brain CD8+ TRM,19,32,76 but similar expression patterns have also been observed in other tissues.106–108 Direct cross-tissue comparisons are needed to establish whether brain TRM possess characteristics truly unique from TRM in other tissues.

Within-tissue heterogeneity

TRM can also exhibit heterogeneity within the same tissue. A widely observed feature of intra-tissue diversity, evident across nearly all organs, is the division of CD69+ TRM into CD103+ and CD103- subsets. This can be driven by tissue compartmentalization as seen with CD103+ TRM in intraepithelial versus subepithelial lung niches109 and dermal versus epidermal skin layers.110 Distinct transcriptional programs can also drive functionally distinct TRM subsets within the same tissue such as the skin111 or small intestine.112 This heterogeneity has recently been resolved on a microanatomic level in the small intestine, where distinct TRM populations form along the crypt–villus axis driven by TGFβ and CXCR3 signaling.113 T cell heterogeneity has also been described in the CNS in the setting of chronic antigen; Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of hypothalamic CD8+ T cells in an autoimmunity mouse model identified distinct clusters of TRM, including those expressing markers of exhaustion, type I IFN-responsiveness and populations with high cytotoxicity molecules like granzyme B, indicating possible functional specialization.29 An IFN-responsive population was also identified by single-cell RNA sequencing from the brains of mice with latent Toxoplasma gondii infection, in addition to stem-like and cytokine-expressing populations.80 While these studies highlight the diversity of T cells in the brain, it remains unclear if these populations are unique to settings of chronic/persistent antigen, and/or if they possess distinct functions. Further, it’s unknown if this TRM diversity is also represented in location-specific heterogeneity, given that the brain’s different macro-and micro- anatomic regions create distinct local environments where TRM could adapt in niche-specific ways. Supporting this, a recent human post-mortem study found phenotypic differences between CD8+ T cells in distinct CNS compartments, with parenchymal CD8+ T cells typically CD69hi CD103+, and border compartments such as the meninges and choroid plexus harboring a CD69lo CD103+ subset, hinting at location-specific diversity in CNS TRM populations.95

Summary

Brain TRM are key defenders of the CNS, providing rapid protection against viral and other infections. Yet major gaps remain regarding brain TRM biology; we lack a clear picture of their functional diversity, how that diversity maps onto CNS niches (eg parenchyma, meninges, perivascular spaces), and how mouse data translates to humans (Fig. 2). Open questions include antigen specificity in the healthy human brain, the frequency and consequences of TRM cytotoxicity during infection, and noncytolytic or regulatory roles across diseases. Foundational issues, such as whether true residency can occur in fluid compartments like CSF and whether brain TRM are uniquely distinct from TRM in peripheral tissues, also remain unresolved (Fig. 2). Filling these gaps is essential for contextualizing brain TRM in health and disease and ultimately harnessing them for protective or therapeutic interventions.
