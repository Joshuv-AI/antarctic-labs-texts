First trimester urine glyphosate concentrations and gestational diabetes in nulliparas: a nested case-control study
Authors: David M Haas, Kevin Moss, Hani Faysal, Lynn M Yee, Robert M Silver, William A Grobman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnvironmental exposures, such as pesticides, during pregnancy have been associated with several adverse pregnancy outcomes, including miscarriage and shorter gestational length at birth. However, their relationship with gestational diabetes (GDM) is uncertain. The objective of this study was to analyze first-trimester urinary herbicide concentrations for their associations with GDM.MethodsThis was a nested case-control study analysis of the Nulliparous Pregnancy Outcomes Study- Monitoring Mothers-to-be (nuMoM2b) study. The study included participants at the three midwestern study sites. We analyzed individuals according to whether they developed GDM. Urinary herbicide concentration at the upper quartile was the exposure of interest. To assess the association of first-trimester urine glyphosate and metabolites with GDM, conditional logistic regression was used for matched pairs models. To test whether the association of herbicides with GDM was modified by BMI, an interaction term of herbicide (dichotomous variable) * BMI was included.ResultsThe 118 cases of nuMoM2b participants with GDM were matched with 118 randomly selected age-matched controls who had no adverse pregnancy outcomes. Glyphosate concentrations were above the limit of detection (LOD) in 93.6% of analyzable samples. Background

Environmental exposures during pregnancy have been associated with several adverse pregnancy outcomes [1]. For example, pesticides are associated with adverse reproductive and pregnancy outcomes in both animal models and human pregnancies [2]. These adverse outcomes include miscarriage and shorter gestational length [3].

While animal data documenting adverse pregnancy and offspring outcomes, including obesity and metabolic changes, of herbicide pesticides have been available for some time [4, 5], specific human data are more recent. The herbicide glyphosate is the most used crop herbicide worldwide. It has been found in the urine of over 90% of pregnant individuals studied, there are multiple possible exposure routes [3, 6, 7]. Exposure to glyphosate has been noted to be higher in individuals with obesity, and obesity can modify the effect of glyphosate on some health outcomes [8, 9]. Additionally, urinary glyphosate has been associated with a higher risk of diabetes and glucose dyshomeostasis in the American general adult population [10–12]. Although glyphosate has been associated with some adverse pregnancy outcomes, the relationship to gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is uncertain. As glyphosate is a potential endocrine disrupting chemical [13], impact on GDM is plausible, as it is an endocrine disorder. Animal data have demonstrated a relationship between glyphosate exposure and multiple endocrine and sex differentiation abnormalities [14–16].

The objective of this study was to analyze human first-trimester urinary herbicide concentrations and test for associations with GDM. The robust collection of covariates and well characterized phenotypes of the Nulliparous Pregnancy Outcomes Study- monitoring mothers-to-be (nuMoM2b) cohort provide an ideal population to fill this gap in knowledge.

Methods

This was a nested case-control study analysis of the nuMoM2b study. The full methods of the nuMoM2b study are published elsewhere [17]. Briefly, 10,038 nulliparous pregnant people were recruited in the first trimester of pregnancy between October 2010 and September 2013 at eight geographically diverse study sites in the United States. Inclusion criteria were pregnant people who were attending one of the affiliated obstetric clinics, planned to deliver their baby at one of the clinic site hospitals, had a viable singleton gestation, were between 6 weeks + 0 days gestation and 13 weeks + 6 days gestation (based on documented ultrasound crown-rump length measurement), and had no previous pregnancy that lasted ≥ 20 weeks based on self-report. Exclusion criteria included maternal age < 13 years, history of ≥ 3 spontaneous abortions, likely fatal fetal malformation evident before enrollment, known fetal aneuploidy, assisted reproduction with a donor oocyte, multifetal reduction, participation in an intervention study that was anticipated to influence maternal or fetal outcomes, previous enrollment in nuMoM2b, plan to terminate the pregnancy, or inability to provide consent. Participants had study visits between gestational weeks 6 weeks + 0 days and 13 weeks + 6 days (V1), 16 weeks + 0 days and 21 weeks + 6 days (V2), 22 weeks + 0 days and 29 weeks + 6 days (V3), and during the delivery hospitalization (V4). At each study visit, a trained research team member administered several questionnaires and collected biological samples, including urine. After pregnancy completion, trained and certified chart abstractors verified pregnancy and newborn outcome data. All participants gave written informed consent and the institutional review boards of each study center approved the study.

The population selected for this analysis included participants who had urine collected at V1 who were recruited from the three centers in the Midwest region (Indiana, Illinois, Ohio). These sites were chosen because they were in a more agricultural region, and we hypothesized more likely to have pesticide exposure. We excluded any participants with pre-existing diabetes of any type. Selected cases were all participants at these sites who had GDM. GDM was diagnosed clinically during the course of the pregnancy using standard recommended clinical screening and diagnostic criteria [18]. Control participants were selected from participants at the same three sites who did not have any adverse pregnancy outcomes (preterm birth, hypertensive disorder of pregnancy, stillbirth, or GDM) and were matched with cases on a 1:1 basis based on maternal age (± 2 years). When multiple controls were possible for a given case, the control participant was selected by simple randomization. We knew at the start that there were 131 participants at the three sites who were diagnosed with GDM. Estimating an odds ratio of 2.2 for GDM with high glyphosate levels from the literature, a 1:1 match would provide over 95% power for the analysis.

All urine specimens had been frozen at -80 degrees Celsius since collection. Identified specimens were retrieved from the long-term storage facility at Fishers Bioscience (Rockville, MD). Urine samples were sent to the Laboratoire du Centre de Toxicologie du Québec (CTQ) for analysis. Glyphosate, glufosinate, and their metabolites aminomethylphosphonic acid (AMPA) and 3-[hydroxy(methyl)phosphinoyl] propionic acid (MPPA) were analyzed using validated methods [19, 20]. Limits of detection for each chemical were: 0.08 µg/L for glyphosate and glufosinate, 0.09 µg/L for AMPA, and 0.10 µg/L for MPPA. Briefly, liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry quantification was performed using a Waters Xevo TQ-XS tandem/triple quadrupole mass spectrometer coupled to an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class System (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Calibration curves were developed using urine matrix from human volunteers with confirmed low analyte content that was spiked with standard solutions over a linear range of 0.1–50 µg/L for all analytes. Urine samples with analyte levels below the LOD were imputed using LOD/√2. Specific gravity was measured at the time of analyte assessment using a digital handheld refractometer. Urine analyte concentrations were adjusted for hydration status using specific gravity by applying the O’Brien method and adjusting for covariates [21]. Hydration correction was achieved by dividing the analyte value by the ratio of the observed specific gravity to the predicted specific gravity. We focused the analyses on glyphosate instead of the degradation product AMPA.

Individual demographic characteristics were compared by case versus control status. Conception season was calculated from estimated due date derived from first-trimester ultrasounds for study participants. Seasons used traditional calendar cutoffs (Spring = March 21-June 20; Summer = June 21-September 20; Autumn = September 21-December 20; Winter = December 21-March 20). Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. Educational attainment was dichotomized at college education or above. Statistical significance was determined using chi-square for categorical variables and t-test or nonparametric tests for continuous variables where appropriate. We dichotomized each herbicide at the upper quartile to create a binary variable. This analytic method is typically used in these types of studies as it lends itself to potentially useful clinical cutoff values in later work. We further analyzed glyphosate as a continuous variable exposure. To assess the association of each herbicide with GDM, we used conditional logistic regression for matched pairs models, including covariates that were significant in the pairwise comparison.

Because of the known association of BMI and GDM, we explored whether the association of herbicides with GDM was modified by BMI. Additionally, it has been shown that obesity modifies the impact of glyphosate on other health outcomes such as osteoarthritis [9], and that exposure to glyphosate is higher in obese individuals [8]. To accomplish this, we included an interaction term of herbicide (dichotomous variable) * BMI (continuous variable) in these regression models. We also tested BMI as a dichotomous variable at 30 kg/m2. Because the interaction tests the effect of herbicide at the y-intercept, which would normally be when BMI = 0.0 kg/m2, we centered BMI at different BMI levels from 25 to 40 by subtracting a constant from BMI (BMI = BMI-30 for example). This example would test the effect of elevated herbicide where BMI = 30 kg/m2. Because of low numbers of participants at the extremes of BMI, we evaluated the effect of elevated herbicide for BMI’s ranging from 25 kg/m2 to 40 kg/m2. Utilizing a subtraction constant of BMI = 30 provided the best model fit.

All analysis were performed using SAS V9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). P values < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Of 10,038 participants in the parent study, 236 were selected for this analysis. (Supplemental Fig. 1) There were 131 cases of Midwest nuMoM2b participants with GDM (4.7% incidence), but only 118 had available urine specimen data. These were matched with 118 randomly selected age-matched controls who had no adverse pregnancy outcomes. Table 1 displays the demographic characteristics of the individuals with GDM and controls. Participants in the GDM group were less likely to be Non-Hispanic White (26.3% vs. 40.7%; p = 0.02), had a higher BMI (30.7 vs. 26.1; p < 0.01), were less likely to have graduated from college (55.1% vs. 72.0%; p = 0.007), and were more likely to have conceived in the autumn (26.5% vs. 12.9% for other seasons combined; p = 0.0009). Despite cases and controls being matched by age within 2 years, there was a difference in mean maternal age at study entry, with those developing GDM being younger (29.9 vs. 31.8 years old; p = 0.02, Table 1). BMI and BMI category were associated with GDM. The distribution of BMI category in the two groups is displayed in Supplemental Table 1. Fewer participants in the GDM group had BMIs in the healthy or underweight categories compared to controls (29.6% vs. 55.9%, p = 0.0001).

Table 2 displays the median log-transformed, specific gravity adjusted analyte concentration comparisons between cases and controls. Supplemental Fig. 2 displays the histogram of concentration distributions for glyphosate, AMPA, and MPPA, demonstrating left skewed distributions. There were no differences in the median concentrations of glyphosate, or AMPA between those with and without GDM. There was a significant difference noted for MPPA but only 13.6% of participants had values above the LOD so this was not clinically relevant. Thirty participants (25.9%) with GDM were in the upper glyphosate concentration quartile, compared to 24.6% of those without GDM (p = 0.82). Twenty-five participants (21.4%) with GDM were in the upper AMPA concentration quartile, compared to 28.2% of those without GDM (p = 0.23). Ten participants (8.6%) with GDM were in the upper MPPA concentration quartile, compared to 18.6% of those without GDM (p = 0.02).

The distribution of GDM cases in those with high glyphosate concentrations by obesity diagnosis is presented in Table 3. Nearly all individuals (90%) with both obesity and high glyphosate were diagnosed with GDM. Compared to those who were not obese and had lower glyphosate concentrations, the risk of GDM was significantly elevated, but with wide confidence intervals (RR 48.37, 95% CI 4.33-540.24). The crude interaction p-value for glyphosate in the 75th percentile and BMI was 0.008. The p-value for interaction of glyphosate as a continuous variable and BMI was 0.02. In the adjusted conditional logistic regression for matched pairs model, when the interaction term was included, participants with glyphosate in the upper quartile had significantly higher odds of developing GDM (odds ratio (OR) 3.27, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.04–10.3, Table 4), and the interaction OR (for example with BMI centered at 30 kg/m2) was 1.23 (95% CI 1.02–1.47). In the model, higher maternal age was associated with lower odds of GDM (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68–0.98) and ever-smoking was associated with higher odds of GDM (OR 2.89, 95% CI 1.08–7.73). Additionally, compared to conceiving the nuMoM2b pregnancy in the winter, conceiving in the spring (OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.14–0.95) or summer (OR 0.27, 95%CI 0.09–0.79) were associated with lower odds of being in the GDM group. Supplemental Table 2 displays the conditional logistic regression models for AMPA and MPPA, neither of the main effect models were associated with GDM. However, the interaction of AMPA (a metabolite of glyphosate) and BMI was significant (p = 0.02) showing that BMI is an effect modifier for elevated AMPA. The adjusted ORs for GDM by elevated AMPA for each BMI centering value show increasing odds, however, these odds ratios are only significant when BMI was 39 kg/m2 or higher. Interactions with race, season, and age were not significant (p = 0.93, 0.96, and 0.99, respectively). The interaction of MPPA and BMI was not significant.

Discussion

In this case-control study nested within the cohort of individuals who participated in the nuMoM2b study in the Midwest, participants whose urinary glyphosate was in the upper quartile of concentrations had higher odds of developing GDM if they also had a first trimester BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. We found an interaction between glyphosate and BMI, demonstrating higher risks for GDM for those with high glyphosate concentrations as BMI increased. This association is concerning, particularly given the obesity epidemic in many places worldwide [22]. This synergistic effect is similar to other studies showing that risk factors added to BMI demonstrated increased risk for GDM [23]. Our results are similar to other findings in nonpregnant individuals that BMI modifies the association of glyphosate with glucose dyshomeostasis, particularly in obese individuals [8, 10].

We found that nearly all participants had detectable levels of glyphosate and its metabolite AMPA. Given that the three recruitment sites in the Midwest were mainly urban and suburban, this finding is important, as it demonstrates the ubiquitous nature of this herbicide. Exposure to glyphosate is prevalent even in nonagricultural areas. This finding is consistent with other studies which found high rates of glyphosate detection in human pregnant individuals [3, 6, 7]. With mounting evidence of increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes with higher exposure to glyphosate, the near ubiquitous exposure should be carefully examined and public health efforts should focus on limiting its use.

We did not find any specimens with glufosinate, and very few had its metabolite MPPA. This finding is similar to findings in a study of pregnant individuals from Canada where glufosinate and MPPA were found in only 1% and 6% of samples, respectively [24]. Animal studies suggest that prenatal exposure to glufosinate is associated with changes in gut microbiome and potential behavioral abnormalities [25]. Our findings of low rates of detectable glufosinate may indicate low potential for exposure among individuals receiving care at these enrollment sites.

Our observation, that higher urinary glyphosate concentrations were associated with higher risk of GDM in humans, has not been reported before. Data from NHANES demonstrated that higher glyphosate exposure in non-pregnant individuals was associated with type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome [26, 27]. A study of self-reported household pesticide use among individuals who had developed GDM did not demonstrate a relationship with glyphosate use [28]. Another study found lower mean glyphosate concentrations in pregnant individuals who had diabetes, but included participants with both type 1 and 2 diabetes in the outcome, and did not adjust for other characteristics [6]. Other studies focused on other adverse pregnancy outcomes but did not report GDM [3, 7]. Our finding of higher age of participants being associated with lower rates of GDM was inconsistent with other studies [18]. Further work to better understand this result in the context of the other findings will be ongoing in a currently recruiting study [29].

The finding that higher glyphosate exposure in the first trimester conveys higher risk of GDM, particularly among those with higher BMI, is notable. If the findings of adverse effects on human health are confirmed, recommendations to avoid or decrease exposure will be needed. However, how this should impact clinical care and recommendations is unclear. One study in pregnant individuals in Idaho found that increasing organic food consumption was associated with reduced exposure to pyrethroid insecticides [30]. However, it is unclear if the same intervention would reduce exposure to glyphosate. Organic farming techniques may reduce dietary exposures from herbicides [31]. At this time, limiting use of chemicals with potentially harmful effects may be the most effective way to decrease exposure [32]. This would likely focus on policy changes rather than individual clinical counseling recommendations [33]. Recent findings that populations of color bear a disproportionately higher burden of environmental health hazards highlight the importance of investigating these outcomes and a multifaceted approach to environmental health and justice [34, 35].

The offspring of the nuMoM2b cohort have not been studied for long-term adverse outcomes related to different pregnancy exposures, including herbicides. Given recent evidence documenting adverse neurodevelopmental and cancer outcomes from glyphosate exposure in both animals and humans, child follow up of pregnancy cohorts is imperative [4, 36–39].

Given the nearly universal exposure to some levels of glyphosate and its AMPA metabolite, future research should also focus on ways to reduce the exposure, as our findings of higher rates of GDM in those in the upper quartile of concentrations attest. Discovering ways to keep concentrations lower in the body, particularly during gestation, may be a way to mitigate some of the risk. Additionally, the interaction with BMI bears further study into the mechanisms that may contribute to that finding. Some animal data demonstrate some concentration of glyphosate in adipose tissues [40]. With a larger database of herbicide concentrations and pregnancy outcomes, future studies could explore clinically meaningful concentration cutoff values that could be used for risk stratification.

We found that compared to conceiving the pregnancy in the winter, pregnancies conceived during the spring and summer, typically heavier herbicide spraying seasons, had lower odds of developing GDM in the adjusted model. The influence of spray season on pregnancy outcomes bears added investigation, as concentrations have been found to differ in pregnant individuals based on spray season [41]. We did not find an interaction of herbicide concentrations with season of conception in this study, but will continue to explore this in a larger cohort [29].

Our study may be the first to document an association of high levels of glyphosate exposure with GDM, including the interaction with BMI. Additionally, the nuMoM2b study is a well-characterized pregnancy cohort with adjudicated outcomes and robust biospecimens. The dose response showing that the odds of GDM increase as BMI increases is similar to that which has been seen with glyphosate concentrations and adult metabolic syndrome [27].

Our study is limited by its sample size, which may have led to somewhat wide confidence intervals. The sample size studied is, however as large or larger than other non-population-based studies. Increasing the number of cases or controls could increase precision of our estimates. Our participants were limited to the three centers in the Midwest, with most participants having an urban or suburban residence, potentially impacting the generalizability to other geographic regions. Due to differences in number of participants at sites and small cell sizes, we were unable to adjust for site. With larger studies, we would plan to adjust for possible site differences. While the exposure to glyphosate was nearly universal, we were able to demonstrate that upper quartiles of adjusted urinary concentrations were associated with increased GDM risk.

Conclusions

High concentrations of urinary glyphosate were associated with GDM for pregnant individuals at BMI values ≥ 30 kg/m2. We found a positive interaction between glyphosate and BMI. This adds to the increasing body of literature documenting increased risks of human adverse pregnancy outcomes from glyphosate exposure. Finding ways to mitigate this exposure may be important for healthy pregnancies and offspring.
