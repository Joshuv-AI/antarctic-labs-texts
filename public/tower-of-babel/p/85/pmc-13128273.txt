The interferon-stimulated gene product HERC5 inhibits human LINE-1 retrotransposition with an ISGylation-independent mechanism
Authors: Kei Nishimori, Ahmad Luqman-Fatah, Yuzo Watanabe, Mari Takahashi, Takuhiro Ito, Fuyuki Ishikawa, Tomoichiro Miyoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMobilization of Long INterspersed Element-1 (LINE-1 or L1) compromises genome stability and can cause sporadic genetic diseases. Accordingly, cells have evolved multiple mechanisms to restrict L1 retrotransposition. Several interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) that interact with cytoplasmic L1 ribonucleoproteins (RNPs), which contain the L1-encoded proteins ORF1p and ORF2p, have been identified as suppressors of L1 retrotransposition. We previously reported that the ISG protein HECT and RLD domain containing E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase 5 (HERC5) efficiently inhibits L1 retrotransposition. While HERC5 is known to restrict numerous viruses through ISGylation, how HERC5 inhibits L1 remains to be elucidated. Here, we show that HERC5 inhibits L1 retrotransposition through an ISGylation-independent mechanism. HERC5 interacts with L1 RNA and selectively reduces ORF1p levels in a manner that requires the full-length ORF1p expression. We further demonstrate that HERC5 decreases L1 translation efficiency and alters L1 RNP composition. Our comparative analysis further suggests that HERC5 may have acquired its L1-inhibitory function during the evolution of the small HERC family. These findings uncover a previously unidentified mechanism by which an ISG protein associates with and inhibits L1 and suggest a role for HERC5 as an evolutionarily adapted restriction factor that expands the repertoire of cellular defenses against retrotransposons.

Introduction

Human genome sequencing revealed that the protein-coding regions cover only a small portion of the genome at <2% [1, 2]. In comparison, nearly half of the genome is made up of transposable elements [1–3]. Among them, Long INterspersed Element-1 (LINE-1 or L1) comprises ∼17% of the human genome [1, 2]. There are >500 000 L1 copies in the human genome; however, only ∼80–100 full-length L1 copies are estimated to be retrotransposition-competent L1s (RC-L1s) [4–6].

RC-L1s are ∼6 kb in length and contain a 5′ untranslated region (UTR), two open reading frames (ORF1 and ORF2), and a 3′ UTR [3, 7]. L1s are transcribed from an internal RNA polymerase II promoter within the 5′ UTR [8–11]. ORF1 encodes a ∼40 kDa protein (ORF1p) with RNA-binding and nucleic acid chaperone activities [12, 13]. ORF1p forms a homotrimeric structure and is essential for L1 retrotransposition [14, 15]. ORF2 encodes a ∼150 kDa protein (ORF2p) possessing endonuclease (EN) and reverse transcriptase (RT) activities, both of which are required for L1 retrotransposition [16–19]. In the cytoplasm, ORF1p and ORF2p preferentially bind to L1 RNA in cis and form L1 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes [20–23]. L1 RNPs then gain nuclear access; however, the mechanism remains unclear [24]. In the nucleus, ORF2p EN activity introduces a single-strand endonucleolytic cleavage at 5′-TTTTT/AA-3′ (the slash indicates the ORF2p cleavage site) and related variants of that consensus sequence in genomic DNA, exposing a 3′-OH group [16, 25–27]. The T-rich sequence, with the liberated 3′-OH group, base-pairs with the L1 RNA poly(A) tail and provides a primer for reverse transcription of L1 cDNA from L1 RNA [22, 28, 29]. Subsequently, L1 cDNA is integrated into the genome through a process called target-site primed reverse transcription (TPRT) [30–32].

De novo retrotransposon insertions can disrupt genes or alter gene expression, potentially leading to sporadic human diseases [33, 34]. To date, more than 100 L1-mediated retrotransposition events have been implicated in disease-causing mutations [35, 36], including L1 insertion into F8 (hemophilia A), APC (colon cancer), and SVA insertion into FKTN (Fukuyama congenital muscular dystrophy) [37–39]. In addition to insertional mutagenesis, recent studies have suggested that retrotransposon intermediates, including L1 cDNA, L1 RNA, and EN-induced DNA breaks, can contribute to the development of autoimmune diseases, cancer progression, and cellular senescence [40–46]. To maintain genomic stability, host cells have evolved multiple defense mechanisms to suppress L1 retrotransposition and its expression (e.g. Apolipoprotein B mRNA-editing enzyme catalytic polypeptide 3 [APOBEC3], zinc-finger antiviral protein [ZAP or ZC3HAV1], and Moloney leukemia virus type 10 protein [MOV10]) [47–53]. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which host factors regulate potentially pathogenic L1 activity is expected to provide insights into future preventive medicine and novel therapeutic strategies for multiple diseases.

To identify the host proteins involved in the regulation of L1 retrotransposition, we previously performed immunoprecipitation of wild-type ORF1p and an RNA-binding mutant (RBM) of ORF1p, followed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) and label-free quantification (LFQ) analysis [54]. We identified helicase with zinc finger 2 (HELZ2), HECT and RLD domain containing E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase 5 (HERC5), and 2′–5′-oligoadenylate synthetase-like (OASL) as ORF1p interactors, and overexpression of these factors significantly inhibited L1 retrotransposition [54]. HELZ2 suppresses L1 retrotransposition by recognizing the L1 5′ UTR and reducing L1 RNA levels [54]. However, the molecular mechanism by which HERC5 inhibits L1 remained unclear.

HERC5, originally identified as cyclin E-binding protein 1 (Ceb1), is a member of the HERC family, which is characterized by the presence of a homologous to the E6-AP carboxyl terminus (HECT) domain and one or more RCC1-like domains (RLDs) [55–57]. HERC5 is an interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) product and a major E3 ligase for ISGylation [58]. ISGylation is a ubiquitin-like post-translational modification in which ISG15, a ubiquitin-like 15 kDa protein containing two ubiquitin-like domains, is covalently conjugated to target substrates through an E1 activating enzyme (UBA7/UBE1L), an E2 conjugating enzyme (UBE2L6/UbcH8), and E3 ligases (HERC5, TRIM25, and ARIH1), similar to ubiquitin modification [58–64]. HERC5 is a ∼116 kDa protein and the HECT domain, especially the catalytic cysteine at position 994, is essential for ISGylation [58, 65]. Additionally, the RLD domain interacts with polysomes, facilitating co-translational ISGylation of newly synthesized viral and host proteins, thereby playing an important role in innate immunity [66, 67]. HERC5 inhibits various viruses, including influenza A virus (IAV), human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), and Ebola virus through an ISGylation-dependent mechanism [68–70]. Although an ISGylation-independent mechanism has also been reported for the inhibition of HIV-1 and Ebola virus [71, 72], the detailed mechanism remains largely unknown.

In this study, we elucidated the molecular mechanism by which HERC5 inhibits L1 retrotransposition. Our results revealed that HERC5 suppresses L1 retrotransposition independently of ISGylation. We also found that the HERC5 RLD domain binds to L1 RNPs, potentially via RNA undergoing translation, leading to a decrease in L1 translation and L1 RNP formation. Finally, comparative analysis across the small HERC paralogs suggests that HERC5 acquired the ability to inhibit L1 during the evolution of the small HERC family.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture conditions

HEK293T, HeLa-JVM [19, 73, 74], and N2102Ep Clone 2/A6 cell lines were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Nissui, Tokyo, Japan or Shimadzu Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 10% (volume/volume [v/v]) fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Amarillo, Texas, United States; Capricorn Scientific, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany; or MP Biomedicals, California, United States), 0.165% (weight/volume [w/v]) NaHCO3 (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), 100 U/ml of penicillin G (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, United States), 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Sigma–Aldrich), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Sigma–Aldrich). HeLa-HA [73, 74] cells were grown in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) (Gibco) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 0.165% (w/v) NaHCO3, 100 U/ml penicillin G, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1 × MEM non-essential amino acids solution (Nacalai Tesque). In the retrotransposition assay for mouse L1spa, TGF21, ORFeus-Mm, and zebrafish ZfL2-2, HEK293T cells were grown in DMEM high glucose medium (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (Gibco), 0.165% (w/v) NaHCO3, 100 U/ml penicillin G, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine. The cell lines were grown at 37°C in 100% humidified incubators supplied with 5% CO2. The absence of Mycoplasma spp. was confirmed with VenorGeM Classic Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Sigma–Aldrich). STR genotyping was performed to confirm the identity of HeLa-JVM, HeLa-HA, HEK293T, and N2102Ep cells by BEX (Tokyo, Japan).

Virus transduction

HeLa-JVM and HEK293T cells were plated on a 35 mm dish (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, United States) at 3 × 105 cells/dish density in DMEM. On the next day (day 0), the cells were transfected with 2 µg DNA (1 µg of shRNA plasmid [pLKO.1-TurboGFP or pALAF034], 0.5 µg of pMD2.G, and 0.5 µg of psPAX2) using 6 µl of FuGENE HD (Promega, Wisconsin, United States) in 200 µl of Opti-MEM (Gibco) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. On the following day (day 1), the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM. On day 2, the virus-containing medium was collected and filtered through a 0.45 µm filter (Merck Millipore, Massachusetts, United States). The filtered virus medium was used to transduce HeLa-JVM and HEK293T cells.

For virus transduction, cells were plated in a six-well plate at 2 × 105 cells/well density in DMEM. The next day (day 0), the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 200 µl of the virus suspension and 8 µg/ml polybrene (Merck Millipore). On day 1, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM. On day 2, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 2 µg/ml puromycin (Sigma–Aldrich). The medium was changed daily until all of the uninfected control cells died (typically day 5 post-transduction). The transduced cells were cultured in DMEM containing 1 µg/ml puromycin and used for subsequent analyses.

Retrotransposition assays

Retrotransposition assays in cultured cells were performed as described previously with some modifications [19, 49, 54, 76, 78, 79, 84, 87]. Unless stated otherwise, HERC5 (WT or mutants) was ectopically expressed by transient transfection; thus, the mutant experiments are not rescue assays in a HERC5-deficient background.

For the retrotransposition assay using the pEBNA overexpression vector in HEK293T cells (Fig. 1C), we used an mEGFPI-based retrotransposition assay [78]. HEK293T cells were plated in six-well plates (Greiner, Frickenhausen, Germany or Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 2 × 105 cells/well density in DMEM. On the next day (day 0), the cells were transfected with 1 µg DNA (0.5 µg of cepB-gfp-L1.3, cepB-gfp-L1.3RT[-], or cepB-gfp-L1.3RT[-] intronless and 0.5 µg of pEBNA control, pKN025 [HERC5 WT], pKN026 [HERC5 ∆RLD], pKN027 [HERC5 ∆HECT], pKN028 [HERC5 C994A], or pALAF024 [MOV10]; MOV10 served as a positive control) using 3 µl of FuGENE HD in 100 µl of Opti-MEM according to the manufacturer’s protocol. On the following day (day 1), the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM without antibiotics. On days 2 and 5 post-transfection, the transfected cells were selected with 10 µg/ml Blasticidin S HCl (Sigma–Aldrich). The cells were collected on day 7 post-transfection to determine the percentage of EGFP-positive cells out of 30 000 cells using a flow cytometer, BD Accuri C6 Plus with FACSAccuri C6 Plus software version 1.0.23.1 (BD Bioscience, New Jersey, United States). The percentage of the resultant EGFP-positive cells was normalized to the transfection efficiency measured by cepB-gfp-L1.3RT(-) intronless EGFP-positive cells, as shown in Fig. 1B. Each transfection was carried out with at least two technical replicates.

For the retrotransposition assay using the pCMV-3Tag-9 overexpression vector in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B), we used an mEGFPI-based retrotransposition assay [78]. Results

HERC5 overexpression inhibits L1 retrotransposition independently of ISGylation

We previously showed that HERC5 interacts with ORF1p and its overexpression inhibits L1 retrotransposition in HeLa-JVM and HEK293T cells [54]. To examine the contribution of each domain to HERC5 overexpression-mediated inhibition of L1 retrotransposition, we generated HERC5 expression constructs in the pEBNA and pCMV-3Tag-9 vectors, including HERC5 wild-type (WT), an RLD domain-deleted mutant (∆RLD), a HECT domain-deleted mutant (∆HECT), and an ISGylation-defective catalytic mutant (C994A) (Fig. 1A). We investigated the effects of these mutants on L1 retrotransposition by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with an empty vector (control), HERC5 WT, ∆RLD, ∆HECT, C994A, or MOV10 (positive control)-expressing plasmid together with cepB-gfp-L1.3, which expresses an engineered human L1.3 containing the mEGFPI retrotransposition indicator cassette (Fig. 1B, see Materials and methods) [54, 76]. Unless stated otherwise, HERC5 (WT or mutants) was ectopically expressed by transient transfection; thus, experiments using HERC5 mutants represent overexpression rather than rescue experiments in a HERC5-deficient background. L1 retrotransposition efficiencies were determined by measuring the percentage of EGFP-positive cells using flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. S1A).

Overexpression of HERC5 WT and C994A similarly reduced L1 retrotransposition efficiency by ∼90%, and ∆HECT by ∼85% compared to the control, suggesting that HERC5 overexpression inhibits L1 retrotransposition independently of its ISGylation activity (Fig. 1C). However, ∆RLD overexpression had no significant effect, suggesting that the RLD domain is indispensable for L1 inhibition in HEK293T cells. We also examined expression levels of HERC5 and its mutants and found that the pEBNA vector expressed these mutants at levels nearly comparable to WT (Fig. 1D).

We also assessed L1 retrotransposition efficiency with the pCMV-3Tag-9 vector. In this context, HERC5 WT and C994A overexpression reduced L1 retrotransposition efficiency by ∼80%, ∆HECT by ∼30%, while ∆RLD did not significantly reduce L1 retrotransposition efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S1B, top). The overexpressed protein levels from pCMV-3Tag-9 of C994A were still comparable to WT, suggesting that the point mutation did not affect protein stability; however, ∆RLD and ∆HECT showed lower protein levels than WT (Supplementary Fig. S1B, bottom). The diminished repression effect of L1 retrotransposition observed with ∆HECT mutant expressed from pCMV-3Tag-9 is likely due to the less stable protein expression. We observed a similar trend of L1 inhibition by HERC5 in HeLa-JVM cells when HERC5 was overexpressed from pCMV-3Tag-9 (Supplementary Fig. S1C): ∆RLD and ∆HECT did not significantly affect L1 retrotransposition, while HERC5 WT and C994A similarly reduced L1 retrotransposition efficiency by ∼50% compared to the control, suggesting that ISGylation-independent inhibition by HERC5 is conserved across cell types.

To test whether endogenous HERC5 inhibits L1 retrotransposition, we knocked down HERC5 using siRNA or shRNA in HEK293T and HeLa-JVM cells, respectively. HERC5 siRNA-treated HEK293T cells showed a slight, though not significant, increase in retrotransposition efficiency compared to the control knockdown cells (Fig. 1E, left). We speculate that this may be due to low endogenous HERC5 levels, and therefore, the knockdown had a minimal effect on L1 retrotransposition. To increase endogenous HERC5 levels, we treated HEK293T cells with interferon-α (IFN-α) at 100 U/ml, as this concentration is sufficient to induce HERC5 expression to almost plateau level (Supplementary Fig. S1D). As expected [50], IFN-α treatment increased HERC5 levels and reduced L1 retrotransposition efficiency (Fig. 1E and F; IFN-α [−] versus [+]). Comparing the retrotransposition efficiencies of the IFN-treated cells to those of the untreated cells (IFN-treated/untreated ratio) suggested that HERC5 knockdown modestly but significantly increased L1 retrotransposition efficiency in IFN-α-treated cells (Fig. 1E, right; siHERC5 versus siControl; see also Fig. 1F).

In HeLa-JVM cells, HERC5 knockdown increased L1 retrotransposition ∼3-fold compared to the control knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S1E). A side-by-side comparison suggested that the endogenous HERC5 levels are higher in HeLa-JVM cells than in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. S1F), which may explain the minimal effect of HERC5 knockdown in untreated HEK293T cells. Alternatively, HERC5 may be the dominant ISG that potentially inhibits L1 in HeLa-JVM cells, as it was significantly enriched in our previous mass-spectrometry analysis of L1 RNPs [54].

TRIM25 and ARIH1 have also been reported as ISG15 E3 ligases in the canonical ISGylation pathway [59, 60, 62, 63]. Although TRIM25 and ARIH1 are expressed in HEK293T cells [90], they were not detected or enriched in the previous analysis of ORF1p-associated complexes in HEK293T cells [91, 92], suggesting that they are not significant candidates for cytoplasmic L1 RNPs. However, to test whether the ISGylation pathway is important for L1 regulation, we knocked down ISG15 using siRNA and measured the L1 retrotransposition efficiency with or without IFN-α treatment in HEK293T cells to confirm whether ISGylation is not required for L1 inhibition by HERC5. In contrast to HERC5 knockdown, under both IFN-untreated and treated conditions, ISG15 knockdown did not show significant differences in L1 retrotransposition efficiency (Supplementary Figs S1G and S1H). These results suggest that ISGylation does not affect the L1 retrotransposition, supporting our finding that HERC5 suppresses L1 retrotransposition in an ISGylation-independent manner.

HERC5 overexpression reduces ORF1p but not L1 RNA levels

To elucidate the mechanism by which HERC5 overexpression inhibits L1 retrotransposition, we examined its effects on L1 RNA and ORF1p. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with a plasmid (pTMF3) expressing full-length L1 whose expression is augmented by a CMV promoter and either an empty vector or a HERC5 expression plasmid (Fig. 4A). RT-qPCR analysis, using a primer pair that specifically recognizes the T7 gene 10 epitope-tag sequence in the L1 plasmid, revealed no significant change in L1 RNA levels upon HERC5 overexpression (Fig. 4B). In contrast, as previously reported [54], HELZ2 overexpression led to a ∼80% reduction in L1 RNA levels compared to the control. This suggests that HERC5 does not significantly affect L1 RNA levels.

Next, we analyzed ORF1p levels with HERC5 overexpression by western blotting and flow cytometry in HEK293T cells. The western blot analysis demonstrated that HERC5 overexpression markedly decreased ORF1p levels compared to the control (Fig. 4C). Using an EGFP-tagged ORF1p construct (pVan583) and flow cytometry, we found that HERC5 overexpression reduced both the ratio of EGFP-positive cells and the median fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4D). In contrast, these effects were not observed when solo EGFP was expressed from pKN033 (Fig. 4E), suggesting that HERC5 specifically downregulates ORF1p (the actual and relative EGFP ratios of Fig. 4D and E are shown in Supplementary Table S2).

We also examined L1 expression level using L1-encoding plasmids without the CMV promoter (pKN042_pTMF3_∆CMVp) or the neomycin reporter cassette (pTM132_pTMF3[-]neo), respectively. As expected, the L1 RNA expression level in the control from pKN042 was ∼40% compared to pTMF3 (data not shown). Consistent with the previous result (Fig. 4B), HERC5 did not reduce the L1 RNA level compared to each control (Supplementary Fig. S2A); however, both ORF1p levels from pKN042 and pTM132 were decreased by HERC5 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S2B). These data suggest that L1 ORF1p, not RNA levels, is reduced by HERC5 overexpression, which may not target the CMV promoter or the neomycin reporter cassette.

Consistently, HERC5 knockdown increased ectopically expressed ORF1p levels with or without IFN-α treatment in the HEK293T cell line (Supplementary Fig. S2C). We also tested the ORF1p level in the N2102Ep cell line, which expresses a high endogenous ORF1p amount [93, 94]. HERC5 knockdown also increased endogenous ORF1p levels (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Taken together, our overexpression and knockdown data support the conclusion that HERC5 inhibits L1 retrotransposition by lowering ORF1p levels.

The RLD domain is required for ORF1p reduction

To identify the HERC5 domain responsible for reducing ORF1p, we measured ORF1p-EGFP levels by flow cytometry in HEK293T cells co-transfected with the L1 plasmid (pVan583) and either HERC5 WT or the mutant constructs. HERC5 WT significantly reduced both the ratio of EGFP-positive cells and the median fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4F), consistent with the western blot result in Fig. 4C. Conversely, ∆RLD did not show a significant reduction in the ratio of EGFP-positive cells relative to the control (Fig. 4F, left, and Supplementary Table S2). Although the median EGFP intensity in ∆RLD-transfected cells was lower than that in the control, the reduction was smaller than that with HERC5 WT or the other mutants (Fig. 4F, right), suggesting that the RLD domain is important for ORF1p reduction. Notably, both ∆HECT and C994A mutants retained the ability to reduce both the ratio and median intensity to levels comparable to WT, suggesting that the HECT domain and its ISGylation E3 ligase activity are dispensable for ORF1p reduction.

Deletion of the RLD domain alters HERC5 subcellular localization

We analyzed the subcellular localization of HERC5 and its mutants, and their effects on ORF1p expression, using immunofluorescence staining. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with a full-length L1 plasmid expressing FLAG-tagged ORF1p (pJM101/L1.3FLAG) and either the empty vector, HERC5 WT, or its mutants. In the control, cytoplasmic ORF1p foci were observed, consistent with previous reports [23, 49, 54]. HERC5 WT expression markedly reduced the ORF1p signal, and it was localized exclusively in the cytoplasm. In contrast, ∆RLD was detected in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, and the ORF1p signals were detected in cells expressing ∆RLD, further suggesting that the RLD domain is important for reducing ORF1p levels (Supplementary Fig. S2E). ∆HECT and C994A mutants, similar to WT, also localized in the cytoplasm and reduced ORF1p expression. These results imply that the RLD domain contributes to HERC5 cytoplasmic localization and is important for its access to L1 RNPs. In a previous study [54], ORF1p foci were detectable in ∼50% of transfected cells even under HERC5 overexpression; in contrast, here we did not clearly detect ORF1p foci by immunofluorescence. A likely explanation for this discrepancy is a cell line difference: the previous study used HeLa-JVM cells, whereas we used HEK293T cells in this study. Because HERC5-mediated L1 inhibition seems to be more robust in HEK293T compared to HeLa-JVM (Supplementary Figs S1B versus C), ORF1p levels in HEK293T were likely reduced below the detection threshold, precluding visualization of ORF1p foci.

HERC5 overexpression regulates ORF1p and its downstream ORF2p

We further investigated which part of L1 is recognized by HERC5 using plasmids expressing different regions of L1. First, to test whether the L1 ORF sequences are targeted by HERC5, we replaced the L1 coding region with the luciferase ORF. However, we found that the luciferase protein level was not reduced by HERC5, suggesting that the L1 ORFs are important for HERC5 activity (Supplementary Fig. S3A). In addition, HERC5 overexpression reduced ORF1p levels expressed from an L1 construct without the 5′ UTR (pTMF3_∆5UTR) to a level comparable to ORF1p from the full-length L1 construct (pTMF3), suggesting that the 5′ UTR is dispensable for ORF1p level reduction. Next, we co-expressed HERC5 with monocistronic ORF1p, monocistronic ORF2p, or full-length bicistronic L1. HERC5 overexpression reduced monocistronic ORF1p but not monocistronic ORF2p levels (Fig. 2A and B). In contrast, ORF2p from the bicistronic L1 construct was reduced together with ORF1p (Fig. 2C). These results suggest that HERC5 specifically recognizes the ORF1 sequence or protein for inhibition and consequently affects the downstream ORF2p expressed from the same construct.

To further test this hypothesis, we investigated the effects of HERC5 on Alu retrotransposition in the presence or absence of ORF1p. Alu is a non-autonomous retrotransposon comprising ∼11% of the human genome [3]. Unlike L1, Alu retrotransposition does not strictly require L1-derived ORF1p but depends on ORF2p [29, 84, 95, 96]. pKN040 expresses Alu and the full-length bicistronic L1, while pTM489 expresses Alu and monocistronic ORF2p lacking ORF1p (Fig. 2D and E, respectively). We then measured Alu retrotransposition efficiencies with these two plasmids in the HeLa-HA cell line, which is permissive for Alu retrotransposition [73, 74]. HERC5 overexpression reduced Alu retrotransposition efficiency driven by the bicistronic L1 (pKN040) by ∼50% relative to the control (Fig. 2D). In contrast, HERC5 overexpression had no detectable effect on Alu retrotransposition induced by monocistronic ORF2p (pTM489) (Fig. 2E). These findings strongly support our hypothesis that HERC5 specifically reduces ORF1p levels, and that ORF2p is affected only when expressed downstream of ORF1. We also confirmed that HERC5-mediated L1 inhibition was conserved in HeLa-HA cells (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

L1 sequence is not required for HERC5-mediated L1 inhibition

To assess the L1 sequence required for HERC5 inhibition, we used synthetic human L1 (L1 ORFeus)-expressing plasmids derived from pDA093_tet-ORFeus_SBtet-RN [45]. The L1 ORFeus contains codon-optimized DNA and RNA sequences that differ by ∼25% from the human L1.3 ORF sequences at the nucleotide level but encode ORF1p and ORF2p, which are ∼99% identical to those of L1.3 at the amino acid level. We assessed retrotransposition efficiency and ORF1p levels of L1 ORFeus when co-transfected with HERC5. While HERC5 overexpression less efficiently reduced L1 ORFeus retrotransposition efficiency and ORF1p levels at high amounts of the ORFeus plasmids (>0.063 µg), a stronger suppressive effect was observed at lower plasmid amounts (<0.031 µg) (Fig. 2F and G). Because L1 ORFeus is codon-optimized and expresses a higher protein amount compared to WT L1.3, the reduced suppression effect of HERC5 on L1 ORFeus at a higher plasmid amount might be underestimated [97]. We also confirmed that HERC5 does not affect EGFP levels at low EGFP protein amounts (Supplementary Fig. S3C and Supplementary Table S2). Thus, we concluded that HERC5 also inhibits the codon-optimized L1 ORFeus retrotransposition efficiency whose L1 sequence is different from L1.3, suggesting that L1 RNA or DNA sequences may not be required for HERC5 inhibition.

The RLD domain interacts with L1 RNA independently of ORF1p

We next performed co-immunoprecipitation of FLAG-tagged ORF1p with HERC5 variants to determine which domain of HERC5 is important for the interaction with ORF1p (Fig. 5A, top). HERC5 WT, ∆HECT, and C994A were co-immunoprecipitated with FLAG-tagged ORF1p, whereas ∆RLD was not, indicating that the RLD domain is essential for the ORF1p–HERC5 interaction (Fig. 5A, bottom and Supplementary Fig. S4A). Similarly, immunoprecipitation of FLAG-tagged HERC5 also showed that the HERC5 and ORF1p interaction depends on the RLD domain (Supplementary Fig. S4B). The endogenous HERC5-ORF1p interaction was also observed in N2102Ep cells, suggesting that this interaction is not due to the overexpression system (Supplementary Fig. S4C).

Because ORF1p together with ORF2p binds L1 RNA in cis to form L1 RNPs [20–23], we asked whether HERC5 interacts with ORF1p directly or through L1 RNA. To address this, we performed co-immunoprecipitation with FLAG-tagged ORF1p followed by RNase A treatment, which degrades single-stranded RNA. RNase A treatment substantially reduced the ORF1p–HERC5 interaction, suggesting that RNA mediates the interaction (Fig. 5B). To confirm the association of HERC5 with L1 RNA, we used the RNA-binding-deficient ORF1p mutant (hereafter referred to as RBM, which contains three point mutations (R206A, R210A, and R211A) [15, 54]. Because RBM is unable to bind L1 RNA, testing its interaction with HERC5 could provide insight into whether HERC5 primarily interacts with ORF1p or L1 RNA (Supplementary Fig. S4D, top). RBM ORF1p binding to HERC5 was notably reduced compared to the WT ORF1p–HERC5 interaction (Supplementary Fig. S4D, bottom). However, the RBM ORF1p–FLAG immunoprecipitation efficiency was lower than that of the WT ORF1p–FLAG, likely because RBM ORF1p fails to bind L1 RNA and thus does not recruit other ORF1p molecules via RNA. These immunoprecipitation results further suggest that HERC5 interacts with L1 RNA or its-binding proteins rather than directly with ORF1p.

HERC5 may interact with L1 RNA via polysomes

To further confirm the association of HERC5 with L1 RNA, we performed RNA-IP. FLAG-tagged HERC5 WT and ∆RLD were immunoprecipitated, and the co-immunoprecipitated L1 RNA levels were measured by RT-qPCR. HERC5 WT significantly co-immunoprecipitated L1 RNA, whereas ∆RLD did not, suggesting that HERC5 interacts with L1 RNA via the RLD domain (Fig. 5C, left). We also found that HERC5 associates with GAPDH mRNA, a process that depends on the RLD domain (Fig. 5C, right). Since the HERC5 RLD domain has been reported to interact with polysomes [66], we consider that the interaction of HERC5-L1 RNA or GAPDH mRNA may also be mediated by polysomes, and HERC5-L1 RNA interaction is not specific for its RNA sequence.

To test this possibility, FLAG-tagged HERC5 was immunoprecipitated and subsequently treated with 50 mM EDTA, which destabilizes the 80S ribosome [98, 99]. After EDTA treatment, the interaction of HERC5-L1 RNA or -GAPDH mRNA was markedly reduced (Fig. 5D, left and middle); however, this treatment did not reduce the ORF1p and L1 RNA interaction, which is known to be direct [15, 21, 100] (Fig. 5D, right). These data suggest that HERC5 may interact with L1 RNA via polysomes. Of note, while HERC5 interacts with GAPDH mRNA, it does not reduce GAPDH protein levels (Figs 4C and 2A–C, Supplementary Figs S2B–D and S3A). However, HERC5 overexpression decreases ORF1p levels, suggesting that RNA interaction alone is insufficient, and additional mechanisms are required for HERC5-mediated regulation of ORF1p.

Full-length ORF1p is required for HERC5-mediated repression of ORF1p

Given that HERC5 was suggested to interact with L1 RNA via polysomes, we examined whether full-length ORF1p translation is necessary for HERC5 inhibition. We constructed a range of ORF1p mutants to disrupt full-length ORF1p translation with minimal changes on the sequence: a canonical start codon mutant (M1A), a premature stop codon mutant (R49*), and a frameshift mutant (688_689insA). Since it was supposed to be difficult to directly quantify the ORF1p mutant levels under HERC5 overexpression, to quantitatively measure the effects of HERC5 overexpression, we replaced ORF2 with hrGFP-C and utilized it as a reporter instead of the truncated ORF1p (Fig. 5E, top). As expected, the GFP reporter from the downstream of the WT ORF1p was reduced to approximately half by HERC5 overexpression (Fig. 5E), consistent with the reduction observed for EGFP-conjugated ORF1p level (Fig. 4D) and ORF2p (Fig. 2C). In contrast, HERC5 did not significantly reduce the GFP levels from the downstream of the other ORF1 mutants (Fig. 5E, bottom left) and GFP itself (Fig. 5E, bottom right, and Supplementary Table S2), suggesting that HERC5-mediated suppression requires the full-length ORF1p.

To assess whether these ORF1p mutants produce stable proteins, we performed the western blotting using antibodies against ORF1p (recognizes N-terminus 35–44 amino acids of full-length ORF1p [101]) and the T7 epitope tag at the C-terminus. The predicted truncated peptides translated from the ORF1 mutants are shown in Supplementary Fig. S4E (see the figure legends). The protein reduction by HERC5 was the most evident in ORF1p WT, while the truncated ORF1p mutant proteins were not so affected by HERC5, suggesting that HERC5-mediated ORF1p suppression requires the full-length ORF1p (Supplementary Fig. S4E).

We further examined the interaction between HERC5 and the ORF1 mutant RNAs. We immunoprecipitated HERC5-3 × FLAG and measured co-immunoprecipitated ORF1 mutant RNAs by RT-qPCR. HERC5 interacted with all ORF1 mutant RNAs at a comparable level with the ORF1 WT L1 RNA (Supplementary Fig. S4F). These results suggest that HERC5 associates with L1 RNAs even without full-length ORF1p, but only exerts the suppressive effect when the full-length ORF1p is expressed.

L1 RNP formation is not required for HERC5 inhibition

The hypothesis that full-length ORF1p is required for L1 suppression by HERC5 led us to consider the possibility that L1 RNP formation may be important for HERC5-mediated suppression. To test whether HERC5 targets an L1 RNP-specific structure, we checked RBM ORF1p expression level when co-expressed with HERC5. Intriguingly, as reported previously [54], HERC5 overexpression also reduced RBM ORF1p to a level comparable to WT ORF1p (Fig. 5F). This result indicates that L1 RNP formation is not essential for HERC5 to regulate L1, although HERC5 requires full-length ORF1p for protein regulation (see “Discussion” section).

HERC5 overexpression reduces L1 translation efficiency

Our results suggest that HERC5 interacts with L1 RNA and modulates ORF1p levels. We hypothesized that HERC5 regulates ORF1p levels either by promoting protein degradation or by suppressing translation. To distinguish these two possibilities, we first treated HEK293T cells with CHX, a global mRNA translation inhibitor, to block de novo protein synthesis and monitored the remaining ORF1p levels with or without HERC5 overexpression (Fig. 6A). After CHX treatment, no significant difference in ORF1p levels was observed between conditions with and without HERC5 overexpression, suggesting that HERC5 does not measurably affect ORF1p stability and may instead inhibit its translation (Fig. 6B and C). We therefore performed polysome profiling to examine the translation efficiency of L1. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with HERC5 and L1 constructs and treated with CHX to arrest ribosomal elongation on mRNA prior to cell lysis. Cell lysates were then centrifuged in 15%–60% (w/v) sucrose density gradients to separate monosome and polysome fractions (Fig. 6D). For each fraction, we calculated the enrichment ratios (HERC5/control) for L1 and GAPDH RNAs. Under the HERC5 overexpression condition, L1 RNA accumulated in the 80S and light polysome (first polysome) fractions and was reduced in the heavier polysome fractions (Fig. 6E). In contrast, the enrichment ratios for GAPDH mRNA were unchanged by HERC5, suggesting that HERC5 selectively reduces L1 translation efficiency, thereby reducing ORF1p levels.

We also performed immunoprecipitation of FLAG-tagged HERC5 WT and C994A followed by LC–MS/MS analysis to identify potential cofactors (Supplementary Fig. S5A). FLAG-tagged HERC5 WT and C994A were efficiently immunoprecipitated with anti-FLAG antibody (Supplementary Fig. S5B). The analysis revealed that both HERC5 WT and C994A co-purified with multiple translation-related proteins (Supplementary Fig. S5C and Supplementary Table S1), consistent with a previous report showing that HERC5 interacts with polysomes via its RLD domain [66]. Collectively, these results suggest that HERC5 potentially forms a complex with translation-associated proteins that dampen L1 translation. Nonetheless, further studies are required to elucidate the cofactors involved and to determine how HERC5 specifically targets L1 ORF1p for translational regulation (see “Discussion” section).

HERC5 alters the composition of RC-L1 RNPs

We next asked whether HERC5 overexpression alters the composition of retrotransposition-competent L1 RNPs (RC-L1 RNPs), which contain both ORF1p and ORF2p, by reducing ORF1p. To obtain RC-L1 RNPs that contain both ORF1p and ORF2p, we purified the FLAG-tagged ORF2p using an anti-FLAG antibody and analyzed the ORF1p and ORF2p levels. In the input lysates, the ORF1p/ORF2p ratio was not significantly altered by HERC5 overexpression, suggesting that HERC5 reduces both ORF1p and ORF2p levels to a similar extent (Fig. 6F, right top). In contrast, in the immunoprecipitated RC-L1 RNP fractions, HERC5 overexpression further reduced the ORF1p/ORF2p ratio by ∼60%, suggesting that HERC5 also decreases ORF1p incorporation into RC-L1 RNPs in addition to translational repression (Fig. 6F, right bottom) (see “Discussion” section). We also attempted to observe ORF1p and ORF2p co-localization by immunofluorescence microscopy; however, ORF2p-positive cells were infrequently observed under our conditions to allow for robust quantification (Supplementary Fig. S5D).

HERC5 overexpression inhibits human and mouse retrotransposons but not a zebrafish retrotransposon

To examine whether the retrotransposition inhibition by HERC5 extends to other retrotransposons, we tested two mouse L1 elements and one synthetic L1 element (L1spa; TF subfamily, and ORFeus-Mm; a synthetic L1 element derived from L1spa, TGF21; GF subfamily) and a zebrafish L2 element (ZfL2-2). In mouse L1spa, ORFeus-Mm, and TGF21 retrotransposition assays, HERC5 significantly reduced retrotransposition efficiencies compared to controls (Fig. 3A–C). In contrast, HERC5 did not reduce the zebrafish ZfL2-2 retrotransposition efficiency (Fig. 3D). This may reflect the lack of ORF1 in ZfL2-2, which contains a single ORF encoding EN and RT domains similar to L1 ORF2 [83, 102] (Fig. 3E). These data further support the notion that HERC5 specifically inhibits L1 retrotransposition through full-length ORF1p modulation and that its inhibitory effect is conserved across different L1s and species.

Human HERC5 may have acquired an inhibitory mechanism against L1 during evolution

The human small HERC family consists of four members: HERC3, HERC4, HERC5, and HERC6 [103, 104]. We examined whether other small HERC family proteins can suppress human L1 retrotransposition. Phylogenetic analysis of the small HERC family showed that HERC4 is the most ancient paralog, followed by HERC3, HERC6, and HERC5 [104]. The small HERC family is suggested to have evolved >595 million years ago (Mya); HERC3 may have arisen by duplication of HERC4 between ∼476 and 595 Mya, and HERC6 and HERC5 may have subsequently emerged ∼430 Mya and ∼413 Mya, respectively (Fig. 3F) [104]. The retrotransposition assay showed that HERC4 and HERC6 did not inhibit L1 retrotransposition, whereas HERC5 significantly inhibited it (Fig. 3G). This suggests that L1 suppression activity is unique to HERC5 among human small HERC family proteins.

We also tested whether mouse HERC6 (mHERC6) inhibits human L1 retrotransposition because, unlike in humans, mHERC6 functions as the ISGylation E3 ligase instead of HERC5 [105]. It has been suggested that mice have lost HERC5, but the ISGylation E3 ligase activity is conserved in HERC6 (Fig. 3F) [105]. Human HERC6 (hHERC6) did not inhibit human L1 retrotransposition; however, mHERC6 efficiently inhibited human L1 retrotransposition (Fig. 3G). This result suggests that mHERC6 has acquired L1 suppression activity after the human–mouse divergence. We propose that the small HERC family may have independently evolved mechanisms to restrict L1 retrotransposition (see “Discussion” section).

Discussion

HERC5 has been reported to exert antiviral activity against many viruses, primarily through an ISGylation-dependent mechanism [57]. To date, several reports have suggested ISGylation-independent activities of HERC5, including inhibition of HIV-1 and Ebola virus; however, the detailed underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood [71, 72]. In this study, we demonstrate that HERC5 overexpression inhibits retrotransposition of endogenous retroelements independently of ISGylation through previously unreported mechanisms involving suppression of translation and modulation of RC-L1 RNP formation.

We show that the RLD domain of HERC5 is required for this ISGylation-independent suppression of L1 retrotransposition (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S1B, C, and G). Immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated that HERC5 interacts with ORF1p and L1 RNA via the RLD domain (Fig. 5A–C and Supplementary Fig. S4A–D), which is important for reducing the ORF1p level (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. S2E). HERC5 has also been shown to interact with polysomes and act on multiple mRNAs, inducing ISGylation on nascent peptides [66, 67]. Our results suggest that HERC5 may interact with L1 RNA via polysomes, and this binding does not appear to be limited to L1 RNA alone (Fig. 5C and D). However, HERC5 exerts translational suppression on specific targets, such as ORF1p; it does not suppress GAPDH, GFP, luciferase, or even monocistronic ORF2p (Figs 4C, E, 2A–C, G, and 4F, and Supplementary Figs S2B–D, S3A, C, and S4E). Moreover, we found that HERC5 reduces ORF1p levels only when full-length ORF1p is translated (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. S4E). We tested two possibilities of HERC5 specificity: L1 RNP formation and L1 RNA sequence, and found that both are dispensable. HERC5 repressed ORF1p expression even when RNP formation is impaired (Fig. 5F) and can also inhibit L1 ORFeus retrotransposition (Fig. 2F).

Based on these data, we hypothesize that newly synthesized full-length ORF1p peptides are likely required by HERC5. One hypothesis is that HERC5 suppresses its translation in cooperation with associated factors that target the full-length ORF1p polypeptide during translation. Our previous mass spectrometry analysis and this study identified several translation-related proteins (e.g. LARP1/4, RACK1, and IFRD2) as common factors detected in the ORF1p and HERC5 complexes, suggesting that these factors may cooperatively and specifically suppress ORF1p translation with HERC5 [106–108] (Supplementary Table S3). Alternatively, ORF1p expression may induce factors that enhance HERC5 function. L1 has been reported to activate the interferon response [54]. In addition, ORF1p overexpression activates transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling by altering PIN1 localization, thereby changing gene expression [109]. The upregulated factors under this condition could work with HERC5 to form a feedback loop that reduces ORF1p translation. We also should note that the truncated ORF1p mutants tested in this study expressed lower levels than full-length ORF1p (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Therefore, it cannot be ruled out that these truncated forms failed to sufficiently induce the expression of such HERC5 mediators.

Polysome profiling analysis revealed that the L1 RNA enrichment was reduced in heavier polysome fractions, suggesting that HERC5 may decrease L1 translation efficiency (Fig. 6E). To our knowledge, this is the first report of translational repression activity of HERC5. Importantly, we did not observe upregulation of global translational-inhibition markers such as phosphorylation of eIF2α and PKR by HERC5 overexpression (data not shown), suggesting that HERC5 acts specifically on L1 translation. Given the enrichment of L1 RNA in the 80S and lighter polysome fractions (Fig. 6E), HERC5 may induce ribosome stalling during translation of L1 RNA. Moreover, because HERC5 reduces ORF1p in the RC-L1 RNPs (ORF2p IP fraction) (Fig. 6F), we hypothesize that HERC5 binding to L1 RNPs may also slow down a shift to RC-L1 RNP maturation. Clearly, further studies will be important to elucidate these mechanisms.

Previous studies have demonstrated that HERC5 inhibits Ebola virus and HIV-1 independently of ISGylation. Although the mechanism by which HERC5 inhibits Ebola virus remains unclear, HERC5 suppresses HIV-1 replication by preventing the nuclear export of unspliced HIV-1 genomic RNA [71]. The CRM1/Ran-GTP complex mediates the nuclear export of HIV-1 RNA by interacting with the HIV-1 Rev protein, which binds to the intronic Rev-response element (RRE) within the unspliced HIV-1 RNA. This unspliced HIV-1 RNA export pathway is disrupted by HERC5 in an RLD domain-dependent manner [71, 110, 111]. Whether this mechanism affects other unspliced RNAs remains unclear; however, it is less likely to apply to L1 RNA for several reasons. First, HERC5 was initially identified as an L1 inhibitor through our previous proteomics approach, which identified the cytoplasmic L1 RNP complex via ORF1p immunoprecipitation [54]. Second, HERC5 is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm, not near the nuclear membrane, where the CRM1/Ran-GTP complex is found (Supplementary Fig. S2E). Third, we did not observe suppression of proteins translated from unspliced transcripts such as EGFP, luciferase, and monocistronic ORF2, except for ORF1-containing transcripts (Figs 4E, 2B, and Supplementary Fig. S3A). Fourth, there is no significant sequence conservation between HIV-1 and L1. Lastly, the identified HERC5 interactors did not include Ran and CRM1 proteins (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Table S1). These observations suggest that HERC5 reduces ORF1p expression via a mechanism distinct from the previously reported pathway targeting HIV-1.

In ISGylation, a post-translational modification in which ISG15 is conjugated to substrate proteins [57], not only HERC5 but also other ISGylation components, such as ISG15 and the E2 enzyme UbcH8 [58, 62], are required. Under normal conditions, the expression levels of these proteins are typically low, resulting in limited ISGylation activity. In contrast, ISGylation-independent functions of HERC5 may not require other components and thus can be exerted even in cells without interferon stimulation. In addition to the antiviral effects mentioned above, ISGylation-independent roles of hHERC5 and mHERC6 have been implicated in mitochondrial metabolism in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and in morphogenesis of the seminal vesicle, respectively [112, 113], suggesting that HERC5 and its orthologous proteins are broadly involved in various processes beyond immune responses. Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) data analyses show that HERC5 is highly expressed in the testis [55, 114, 115]. Furthermore, a single-cell transcriptome analysis revealed high levels of HERC5 expression in human male and female primordial germ cells (PGCs), though not in the inner cell mass (ICM) [116]. Notably, ISG15 expression levels in the testis remain relatively similar to those in other tissues, also maintaining low levels even in PGCs and ICM cells, suggesting that HERC5 also functions independently of ISGylation in the testis and PGCs [114, 116]. Because L1 is highly expressed in germ cells and de novo L1 insertions are inherited, multiple pathways suppress L1 in the germline [117–119]. The relatively high expression of both L1 and HERC5 in the testis and PGC suggests that HERC5 may act as a guardian of the germline genome by restricting de novo L1 insertions independently of ISGylation. While interferon responses are generally low in the early stages of embryonic development [120, 121], the expression of certain ISGs, including HERC5, is high, suggesting that HERC5 may play an important role in the developing germline. In addition to previously characterized mechanisms such as RNA degradation (piRNA) and protein degradation (TEX19) [122, 123], a multilayered host defense system, including translational regulation by HERC5, may have evolved in mammals to protect the genome from retrotransposons.

Among the human small HERC family members examined, only HERC5 overexpression suppressed L1 retrotransposition (Fig. 3G), whereas mouse HERC6 (mHERC6), but not human HERC6 (hHERC6), also exhibited inhibitory activity, suggesting that this function was acquired in the mouse lineage after the divergence from humans. Consistent with this idea, the RLD domains of hHERC5 and mHERC6, particularly the N-terminal ∼100 amino acids, appeared to have undergone positive selection in mammals to gain the ability to interact with polysomes [66, 67, 71, 104]. Taken together, these findings suggest that small HERC proteins may have coevolved the capacity to inhibit L1 retrotransposition. Intriguingly, the APOBEC3 gene cluster, comprising ISGs that inhibit both L1 and Alu, is thought to have coevolved with endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) [124, 125], which is conceptually similar to our hypothesis. Although evolutionary relationships between viruses and ISGs have been extensively investigated, the coevolutionary dynamics between retrotransposons and ISGs have received much less attention. Future research may further shed light on the evolutionary interplay between these elements.

Overall, our study suggests that HERC5 interacts with L1 RNA and selectively inhibits ORF1p translation and L1 retrotransposition in a manner that requires full-length ORF1p expression. While previous studies on HERC5 have primarily focused on its canonical role in ISGylation, our study suggests HERC5 possesses multiple mechanisms beyond ISGylation. Future investigations will be required to elucidate the ISGylation-independent activities and to further characterize its structural and functional features during translation.
