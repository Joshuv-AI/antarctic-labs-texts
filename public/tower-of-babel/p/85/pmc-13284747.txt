Sex differences in the association of adverse childhood experiences with brain and cognition along a continuum of risk for Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Mateja Perović, Natalie A Phillips, Gillian Einstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWomen make up two‐thirds of people with Alzheimer's disease (AD). Research has focused on biological explanations for this sex difference, while contributions of psychosocial risk factors are less well understood.METHODSWe examined sex differences in the effects of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) on late‐life cognition in groups along a continuum of AD risk: cognitively unimpaired controls (CU; n = 128), subjective cognitive decline (SCD; n = 113), mild cognitive impairment (MCI; n = 241), and AD (n = 77), from the Canadian Consortium on Neurodegeneration in Aging Comprehensive Assessment of Neurodegeneration and Dementia (COMPASS‐ND) study.RESULTSWomen reported more ACEs than men. There were negative associations between ACEs and hippocampal and prefrontal cortical volumes in CU men and prefrontal volumes in men with SCD. In MCI, ACEs were linked to poorer executive function and associative memory in women. No ACE‐related effects were found in AD.DISCUSSIONACEs may have enduring effects on late‐life cognition that differ between men and women and vary by cognitive status.Keywords: adverse childhood experiences, cognitive aging, dementia risk, early life adversity, sex differences

BACKGROUND

There are well‐established sex differences in Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk and prognosis: Women comprise two‐thirds of persons with AD 1 and are more likely to convert from the high‐risk mild cognitive impairment (MCI) state to AD than men. 2 , 3 Once AD is diagnosed, women have been shown to experience more rapid cognitive decline than men, 4 as well as stronger associations between AD‐related brain pathology and clinical AD presentation. 5 Many biological explanations have been proposed to account for these sex differences in AD prevalence and progression, including loss of estradiol over the course of spontaneous menopause, 3 surgical menopause, 6 , 7 and greater effects of genetic risk factors in women. 8 However, psychosocial contributing factors to the greater AD risk in women remain understudied.

One such factor is having a history of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), such as abuse or neglect. Mixed‐sex cohorts indicate that early life adversity is associated with worse memory 9 , 10 and executive function, 10 , 11 while meta‐analyses suggest that, even in the absence of post‐traumatic stress disorder, a history of traumatic experiences in childhood is associated with lower hippocampal volume in adulthood. 12 , 13 Prefrontal cortical gray matter volumes are also reduced in adults exposed to physical 14 and emotional 15 maltreatment in childhood. Moreover, ACEs have been associated with higher likelihood of receiving a dementia diagnosis. 16 , 17

However, a recent review of studies on early life adversity and late‐life cognition revealed few sex‐disaggregated analyses, 18 despite the well‐documented sex differences in reports of ACEs. Specifically, most studies on ACE prevalence found that women were more likely to report ACEs, 19 , 20 , 21 while some studies found similar overall likelihood of experiencing ACEs for both sexes but with women more likely to report multiple ACEs. 22 There are also sex differences in the types of ACEs reported by women and men, with women being more likely to report sexual abuse. 23

Given the known sex differences in dementia risk and prevalence, as well as the impact of early life stress on cognition and brain health, this study undertook an extensive examination of the effects of ACEs and sex on brain structure and cognition along a continuum of risk for AD: in cognitively unimpaired controls, subjective cognitive decline (SCD), MCI, and AD. SCD refers to self‐reported cognitive decline in the absence of objective cognitive impairment as measured by standardized neuropsychological tests, 24 and MCI, commonly considered prodromal AD, 25 is characterized by persistent cognitive decline detectable through objective measures and corroborated by an informant. 26 People with SCD are at greater risk of MCI and of progression from MCI to dementia. 24 Critically, sex differences in cognitive aging vary across these clinical groups. 3 , 4 , 27 To delineate potential contributions of ACEs to sex differences in both healthy and pathological aging, taking note of the variance in sex differences by AD risk level, we examined sex differences in the association of ACEs across a range of cognitive states. We took this approach to avoid assuming uniform effects of ACEs with respect to sex across the AD continuum, potentially allowing the identification of cognitive status and sex‐specific patterns.

We examined both neuroimaging and behavioral data. Hippocampal and prefrontal white and gray matter volumes were analyzed as ACEs have been consistently associated with lower hippocampal 12 , 13 and prefrontal 14 , 15 volumes in adult mixed‐sex cohorts. Furthermore, both regions have been strongly associated with AD‐related pathology and cognitive decline. 28 , 29 We predicted that women would have higher ACE scores than men and that higher scores would be associated with lower hippocampal and prefrontal cortical volumes as well as lower cognitive performance on tasks associated with these brain regions: episodic memory and executive function, respectively.

METHODS

Data source

We analyzed data from the Canadian Consortium on Neurodegeneration in Aging (CCNA) Comprehensive Assessment of Neurodegeneration and Dementia (COMPASS‐ND) study, 7th release. 30 , 31 COMPASS‐ND is an ongoing longitudinal observational study funded by the government of Canada through the Canadian Institutes of Health Research. Canadians aged 60 to 90 who currently have or are at risk of developing different types of dementia were recruited for COMPASS‐ND (for the 7th release, between June 8, 2016, and June 29, 2022). Recruitment for COMPASS‐ND was conducted in over 30 sites across Canada, including memory clinics, stroke clinics, movement disorder clinics, behavioral neurology clinics, and academic and private research groups. Recruitment was done through flyers and mailing lists, as well as direct advertisement by clinicians at memory clinics and by researchers with existing participant pools of older adults. COMPASS‐ND collects clinical, neuropsychological, neuroimaging, and fluid biomarker data with the aim of determining risk factors and early markers of dementia. For this study, we focused on the cohorts at varying levels of risk of AD.

Participants

Inclusion criteria for the COMPASS‐ND study comprised either unimpaired cognition or presence of subjective or objective cognitive impairment, proficiency in English or French, ability to attend multiple study sessions, and having a study partner who could take part in the study as a co‐participant providing corroborative information. The general exclusion criteria included the presence of other chronic brain diseases (e.g., chronic static leukoencephalopathy, malignant tumor, multiple sclerosis), ongoing substance abuse, symptomatic stroke within the previous year, a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score equal to or under 13, and inability to undergo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning.

The assessment protocol comprised up to five visits, completed within a 12‐week window. Visit order and structure have been described in detail in a prior publication characterizing the COMPASS‐ND study design. 30 Briefly, visit 1 included eligibility confirmation, collection of sociodemographic and lifestyle information, and a comprehensive clinical assessment; visit 2, additional physical and neurological clinical examinations, diagnostic reassessment conducted by the study site physician, and biological sampling (blood, saliva, urine, and buccal swab); visit 3, administration of a neuropsychological battery; 32 visit 4, a 3T MRI scan; and visit 5, a lumbar puncture.

Notably, participants underwent multiple rounds of cognitive testing. First, during the initial clinical assessments (visits 1 and 2), which included clinical judgment and administration of cognitive screening tests (Table 1). Then, during an extensive neuropsychological evaluation (visit 3) that was independent of initial cognitive screening, findings from the full neuropsychological evaluation, combined with clinical assessment data, were used to categorize participants into final diagnostic groups for research purposes (classification criteria are summarized in Table 1, adapted from Phillips et al. 32 ). Screening criteria and group definitions are summarized in detail in published COMPASS‐ND documentation, including a general study protocol 30 and a detailed description of the neuropsychological evaluation. 32

Adverse childhood experiences

The Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE) was used to assess self‐reported childhood history of verbal, physical or sexual abuse, neglect, or household dysfunction. 33 ACE scores ranged from 0 to 10, with each participant's score representing the number of reported adverse experiences out of the following: verbal abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, family not loving, neglect from parents, parents separated or divorced, mother abused, substance abuse by household member, household member mentally ill/suicidal, household member went to prison (further details on operationalization can be found in Supplementary Materials). We also report the percent of participants with “high ACEs” (4 or more).

Neuroimaging

All scans were collected using 3T MRI scanners. The sequences were standardized across scanners throughout Canada and analyzed in accordance with Canadian Dementia Imaging Protocol, 34 a harmonized protocol for image acquisition available for GE, Phillips and Siemens scanners (parameters for acquisition of 3D T1‐weighted images: https://www.cdip‐pcid.ca/), including a common procedure for qualification, quality control, and quality assurance. We focused our analysis on hippocampal and prefrontal white and gray matter volumes.

Neuropsychological tests

Memory tasks

The Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) 35 and the Face‐Name Test 32 were analyzed as measures of memory function. RAVLT is a test of verbal learning and memory that measures both the immediate and delayed recall of a list of 15 nouns (read five times). Face‐Name Associative Memory Test is an associative memory measure, considered one of the most sensitive predictors of MCI to AD progression. 36 Participants are shown pairs of names and faces and asked to remember them, with their recall measured both immediately and after a delay. Recognition of the face‐name pairs is also tested after a delay. For both tasks, we analyzed the delayed measures as they are considered more indicative of hippocampal memory formation than the immediate measures. 37 , 38 Raw scores for both tasks were used in the analyses, with higher scores indicating better performance (RAVLT max score = 15; Face‐Name max score = 9).

Executive function tasks

The Color‐Word Interference Test (CWIT) of the Delis–Kaplan Executive Function System 39 and the WAIS‐III Digit Span task 40 were analyzed as measures of prefrontal/executive function. In CWIT, participants were presented with red, green, and blue squares and asked to say the names of the colors as quickly as possible without making mistakes (color naming trial). Participants were then shown a list of words (“red,” “green,” “blue”) printed in black ink and asked to read them as quickly as possible without making mistakes (word reading trial). In the inhibition trial, participants were shown the same words but printed in incongruent colors and asked to name the ink color. Finally, participants were presented with the words “red,” “green,” and “blue” printed in red, green, or blue ink, with half of the words printed within boxes. They were asked to say the color of the ink, unless the word was printed in a box, in which case they were asked to read the word itself (inhibition/switching trial). Number of errors and completion times in seconds were recorded for each trial, with higher scores indicating more errors and longer times (color and word trials max time = 90 s; inhibition and switching trials max time = 3 min). Age‐corrected scaled scores were calculated for the time to complete each of the four conditions. 32 In the Digit Span task, the experimenter read a series of numbers the participant then repeated. In digit Span Forward, the participant repeated them exactly as heard, while in Digit Span Backward, the participant repeated them in reverse order. The digit sequences gradually increased in length, from 4 to 9 for Digit Span Forward and from 3 to 8 for Digit Span Backward. The longest string that the participant repeated correctly was recorded for each trial (forward, backward), and the two scores were summed (max score = 17) and age‐scaled – longer strings indicated better performance.

Psychosocial measures

We assessed the effects of sex and ACEs on several psychosocial measures known to be affected by ACEs 33 , 41 and that themselves are associated with cognition 42 , 43 in order to (1) characterize the cohort and (2) account for possible confounding factors. Quality of life was measured using the Quality of Life—AD Scale. 44 The scale consisted of 13 four‐point items (ranging from “poor” to “excellent”) and asked participants to rate the following domains: physical health, energy, mood, living situation, memory, family, marriage, friends, chores, fun, money, self, and life as a whole. A social support index was calculated as an overall function of the four subscales of the Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Scale. 45 The scale consisted of 12 five‐point response items (ranging from “none of the time” to “all of the time”), and participants indicated how often they had various types of social supports available (e.g., “someone you can count on to listen when you need to talk”). Finally, depression was measured using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), 46 a scale consisting of 30 yes/no items (e.g., “Do you often feel helpless?”). Scores of 0 to 9 suggest that there is no depression present; 10 to 19 indicate likelihood of mild depression; 20 to 30 indicate likelihood of severe depression.

APOE status

Finally, participants were genotyped for the presence of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele, which is strongly associated with the risk of familial and sporadic AD onset and progression, particularly in women. 8 Samples were collected using the Affymetrix UK Biobank Axiom array chip and analyzed at the Mt. Sinai Clinical Genomics Centre in Toronto.

STATISTICAL ANALYSES

Data were analyzed using R statistical software 4.0.5, using base R functions.

Cohort characterization. Continuous demographic characteristics (MoCA, quality of life, depression, social support) were analyzed using general linear models and predicted by an interaction between sex and group (model = lm[dependent variable ∼ sex * group]), where group = control, SCD, MCI, and AD. Models were refit using different reference groups (through releveling of the group factor) to capture all group comparisons. The presence of the APOE ε4 allele between groups and between sexes in each group were compared using chi‐squared (χ 2) tests. ACEs were analyzed separately for the total cohort and each group: The total number of ACEs as a continuous score was predicted by sex using general linear models. To identify potential covariates for the main analyses, demographic variables shown to vary by ACEs in previous literature – quality of life, depression, and social support – were modeled as a function of an interaction between ACE and sex while accounting for group (model = lm[dependent variable ∼ ACE * sex + group]). Quality of life and social support were included as covariates in the cognitive and neuroimaging analyses due to significant associations with ACEs.

Primary outcomes. Hippocampal and prefrontal cortical volumetric measures were analyzed using linear models and predicted by an interaction of sex and the number of reported ACEs as a continuous measure, with total intracranial volume, age, APOE status, social support, quality of life, and education as covariates (model = lm[dependent variable ∼ ACE * sex + total intracranial volume + age + APOE status + social support + quality of life + years of education]). Models were run separately for each group. Similarly, cognitive performance was analyzed using linear models and predicted by an interaction of sex and the number of reported ACEs, with age, APOE status, social support, a quality of life, and education used as covariates (model = lm[dependent variable ∼ ACE * sex + age + APOE status + social support + quality of life + years of education]). Extreme outliers (>3 SD ± M) in the cognitive and brain measures were removed from analyses. False discovery rate (FDR) was used to correct for multiple comparisons.

Sensitivity checks and supplementary analyses. Several sensitivity checks were performed to validate the COMPASS‐ND group classifications, specifically to evaluate confidence in the AD group definition, which was made based on clinician judgment rather than biomarker status. Aβ42/40 ratio and phosphorylated tau 181, plasma biomarkers diagnostic of AD, were compared between groups (Figures S1and S2). Additionally, an analysis predicting hippocampal volume, a sensitive measure of AD‐related neurodegeneration, by diagnostic group while controlling for intracranial volume was performed in order to confirm whether volumes were lower in the AD group (Figure S3). The results of all sensitivity checks are outlined in Supplementary Materials. Briefly, all analyses were indicative of valid AD group classification. Also included in the Supplementary Materials is an analysis of neuroimaging regions of interest predicted by an interaction of group and sex, controlling for total intracranial volume (Figures S4–S6).

RESULTS

Participants

Out of 1010 total participants in the CCNA COMPASS‐ND study, 7th release, 559 were included in the study classed by diagnostic group (controls = 128, SCD = 113, MCI = 241, AD = 77; the excluded participants belonged to other clinical groups, e.g., Parkinson's disease). Age ranged between 57 and 91 across the four cohorts and participants were predominantly highly educated, with 15 years of education on average across all cohorts. Most (88.4%) participants were tested in English (Suppl. Continuous demographic characteristics were analyzed using linear models and modeled by an interaction between sex and group (model = lm[dependent variable ∼ sex * group]). There were no differences in mean age, years education, and quality of life between groups or sexes (all p > 0.05). Expected group differences emerged in terms of MoCA scores. While there were no differences between controls and participants with SCD (p = 0.175), both participants with MCI (b = −3.72, SE = 0.39, t[550] = −9.55, p < 0.001) and AD (b = −9.87, SE = 0.6, t[550] = −16.57, p < 0.001) performed worse than controls. Participants with MCI (b = −3.16, SE = 0.41, t[550] = −7.73, p < 0.001) and AD (b = −9.31, SE = 0.61, t[550] = −15.3, p < 0.001) also performed worse than participants with SCD, and participants with AD performed worse than those with MCI (b = −6.14, SE = 0.59, t[550] = −10.35, p < 0.001). There were no sex differences in MoCA scores (p = 0.972).

Similarly, there were no sex differences in terms of depression (GDS) scores, but the cognitively impaired groups reported more depressive symptoms than the controls (MCI: b = −3.77, SE = 0.63, t[542] = −5.99, p < 0.001; AD: b = −3.31, SE = 0.98, t[542] = −3.37, p < 0.008), and participants with MCI reported more depressive symptoms than those with SCD (b = −1.91, SE = 0.66, t[542] = −2.89, p < 0.004). The only group difference that emerged in terms of social support was between women in the control and SCD groups, with control women having a higher social support index than women with SCD (b = 6.15, SE = 2.92, t[518] = 2.1, p = 0.036).

Group differences in terms of APOE status were analyzed using χ 2 tests. As expected, the ε4 allele was more common in the group with AD than in controls (χ 2[1, N = 205] = 12.9, p = 0.003), SCD (χ 2[1, N = 190] = 20.09, p < 0.001) or MCI (χ 2[1, N = 318] = 8.21, p < 0.004). Participants with MCI were also more likely to carry an ε4 allele than participants with SCD (χ 2[1, N = 354] = 6.29, p < 0.012). At the level of the entire cohort, there were no sex differences in APOE carrier status, but more women than men in the SCD group were ε4 carriers(χ 2[1, N = 113] = 4.12, p = 0.042).

ACEs

The total number of ACEs was predicted by sex using general linear models. Reports of individual ACEs (e.g., physical abuse, verbal abuse) between the sexes were compared using χ 2 tests; however, these were only analyzed in the overall cohort and in the MCI group due to small numbers of individual ACE reports in the SCD and AD groups. Neglect from parents and abuse of the mother were also not individually analyzed in the MCI cohort due to low numbers (expected number of observations not >5 per cell for χ 2).

In the overall cohort, women reported more ACEs (b = −0.38, SE = 0.14, t[557] = −2.66, p = 0.008) and were more likely to report physical abuse (χ 2[1, N = 554] = 4.3, p = 0.038). In the MCI group, women also reported more ACEs (b = −0.45, SE = 0.21, t[239] = −2.12, p = 0.035) and were more likely than men to report both physical abuse (χ 2[1, N = 239] = 4.79, p = 0.029) and growing up with a mentally ill or suicidal family member (χ 2[1, N = 237] = 7.19, p = 0.007) (Figure 1). Although the numbers of people with defined high‐ACE burden (≥4) were too low for meaningful statistical analysis, higher percentages of women with high‐ACEs were observed across all four groups.

Quality of life, depression, and social support were modeled as a function of an interaction between total number of ACEs and sex (model = lm[DV ∼ ACE * sex + group]). There were no significant effects on depression, but there was a significant negative main effect of ACEs on quality of life (b = −0.58, SE = 0.21, t[555] = −2.79, p = 0.005) and social support (b = −2.21, SE = 0.58, t[519] = −3.77, p < 0.001). Because of this, quality of life and social support were covaried in the analyses of brain volume and cognitive performance.

Neuroimaging

Hippocampal and prefrontal cortical volumetric measures were analyzed using linear models and predicted by an interaction of sex and total number of ACEs, with total intracranial volume, age, APOE status, social support, quality of life, and education used as covariates (model = lm[dependent variable ∼ ACE * sex + total intracranial volume + age + APOE status + social support + quality of life + years of education]). Models were run separately for each group. In controls, sex and ACEs significantly interacted to predict hippocampal (b = −264, SE = 99, t[81] = −2.66, p = 0.009, αFDR = 0.06) and frontal cortical gray matter volumes (b = −8309, SE = 3490, t[80] = −2.38, p = 0.02, αFDR = 0.11), with smaller volumes with higher ACEs in men only (Figure 2). The SCD cohort also showed significant interactions between sex and ACEs that indicated effects of ACEs specific to men: larger frontal gray matter (b = 4540, SE = 1790, t[89] = −2.54, p = 0.013, αFDR = 0.08) and smaller white matter volumes (b = −4528, SE = 2012, t[88] = −2.25, p = 0.027, αFDR = 0.13) with higher ACEs (Figure 3). The effects on gray matter should be interpreted with caution given the presence of significant sex differences in frontal cortical gray matter volume in the SCD cohort (lower in men than women; Supplementary Materials). In the MCI and AD cohorts, there were no effects of the number of reported ACEs or sex on brain volumes (all p > 0.05).

Cognitive performance

Cognitive performance was analyzed using linear models and predicted by an interaction of sex and ACEs, with age, APOE status, social support, quality of life, and education used as covariates (model = lm[dependent variable ∼ ACE * sex + age + APOE status + social support + quality of life + years of education]). In controls and SCD, there were no effects of sex or ACEs on cognitive performance (all p > 0.05) other than women outperforming men in the delayed trial of the RAVLT in the SCD cohort (b = −1.61, SE = 0.75, t[96] = −2.13, p < 0.035, αFDR = 0.16). In MCI, women also outperformed men on RAVLT delayed (b = −2.95, SE = 0.58, t[211] = −5.1, p < 0.001, αFDR < 0.001). Women with MCI also had better delayed recall (b = −1.32, SE = 0.34, t[209] = −3.9, p < 0.001, αFDR = 0.002) and recognition (b = −1.11, SE = 0.3, t[207] = −3.73, p < 0.001, αFDR = 0.004) than men in the face‐name task but more switching errors than men on the CWIT (b = −1.09, SE = 0.54, t[193] = −2.04, p = 0.042, αFDR = 0.18). Also in MCI, sex and ACEs interacted to predict CWIT color‐switching time (b = −5.18, SE = 1.83, t[196] = −2.8, p = 0.005, αFDR = 0.04); women's but not men's times got slower with higher ACEs (Figure 4). Finally, multiple main effects of ACEs emerged in the MCI cohort (all shown in Figure 4). There was a main effect of ACEs on CWIT color‐reading time indicating slower times with both women (b = 1.1, SE = 0.27, t[209] = 3.9, p < 0.001, αFDR = 0.002) and men (b = 0.64, SE = 0.27, t[209] = 2.38, p = 0.018, αFDR = 0.11) as reference, a negative main effect of ACEs on digit span with women as reference (b = −0.3, SE = 0.15, t[210] = −2.01, p = 0.046, αFDR = 0.19), and a negative main effect of ACEs on delayed recognition with women as reference (b = −0.21, SE = 0.1, t[207] = −2.16, p = 0.032, αFDR = 0.15). These main effects may be interpreted as sex‐specific effects for the reference group (women). In the AD cohort, there were no associations of sex or the number of ACEs with any cognitive task (p > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

This study presents a comprehensive account of sex differences in ACE associations with brain and cognition along a continuum of AD risk. Consistent with prior literature, women reported more ACEs than men. 20 , 21 Furthermore, relationships between ACEs and outcomes of interest differed between men and women: Negative effects of ACEs on brain structure were seen in cognitively unimpaired men, with the effects of ACEs on women's cognition emerging in MCI. The AD group showed no effects of ACEs. These results point to nuanced effects of ACEs that vary by sex and cognitive status, diminishing at a high level of neurodegeneration characteristic of AD.

These findings suggest potentially greater ACE‐related sensitivity of brain structure in cognitively unimpaired men relative to women, as evidenced by declining hippocampal and prefrontal cortical volumes in the control cohort and effects on prefrontal cortical volumes in men with SCD. This is consistent with prior research in younger, cognitively unimpaired cohorts, which suggests that the effects of ACEs on hippocampal volume are either specific to men 47 , 48 or affect predominantly men. 49 Prior literature also suggests that psychological distress may affect cognition in older cognitively unimpaired men but not women 50 and that negative effects of early life adversity on prefrontal gray matter volume may also be specific to men. 51 , 52 Collectively, these findings point to a sex difference in the effect of ACEs on hippocampal and prefrontal cortical structure in cognitively healthy men throughout adulthood, with implications for late‐life brain health.

Conversely, effects of ACEs on women were reflected in cognitive performance and emerged in MCI. Women with MCI showed negative associations between ACEs and both executive function and associative memory. These findings align with evidence that women with MCI face greater risk of progression to AD than men, 2 including greater risk associated with psychological risk factors. 53 Furthermore, it has been suggested that verbal memory advantage in women, which was also observed in the current study, may mask risk of progression to AD. 3 Our findings suggest that ACEs may increase cognitive risk in women with MCI without affecting their verbal memory advantage, potentially further complicating accurate risk assessment in women with MCI. Longitudinal analyses are needed to directly assess the effects of ACEs on MCI progression in women.

Notably, women with MCI had significantly higher ACEs than men with MCI, which may further explain our findings. Higher ACE exposure may negatively affect the development or maintenance of cognitive reserve – the lifetime accumulation of cognitive, social, and physical experiences that help maintain cognitive function later in life. 54 Additionally, in the current study, women with MCI were more likely to report experiencing physical abuse and growing up with a mentally ill or suicidal family member. Prior work suggests that, while higher ACEs are associated with more negative psychosocial outcomes across age groups, 55 , 56 enduring effects of ACEs in adulthood differ by sex 56 and type of experienced ACEs. 55 While we were not able to assess the effects of individual ACEs due to sample size constraints, it is possible that differences in the types of ACEs experienced by women and men in our MCI cohort partially explain the more pronounced effects of ACEs on women with MCI.

In addition to the observed effects in women, people with MCI of both sexes showed an association of ACEs with slower color‐reading times on the CWIT. While not considered direct measures of executive function, reading times measure lower‐order functions necessary for executive function. Across the adult lifespan, errors on these low‐order measures are associated with worse processing speed and have been interpreted as lapses in executive function. 57 Prior studies indicated that ACEs were associated with worse executive function in mixed‐sex groups of both children 10 and adults. 11 While studies in younger cohorts have rarely examined sex differences, 58 those that did found that ACEs had consistently negative effects across sexes. 59 , 60 In concert with our findings, these studies indicate enduring effects of ACEs on executive function.

Finally, there were no effects of ACEs on brain or cognition in AD, potentially suggesting that the effects of ACEs become less prominent at a certain level of brain pathology. Alternatively, this pattern of results may either be evidence of survivor bias or indicative of worse memory for ACEs in people with AD. The latter interpretation would be in line with the fact that reports of high rates of ACEs were generally lower in our AD group, but this type of explanation has been called into question in prior work on ACEs in older populations, 61 as studies have shown memory for and a lasting impact of ACEs in old age, 62 including in cohorts with dementia. 17 Nevertheless, results in our AD cohort should be interpreted with caution.

Overall, the prevalence of ACEs across the current cohorts was low, corresponding to the general prevalence of ACEs in the Canadian 63 and US populations. 21 The fact that we still found notable effects of ACEs on brain structure and cognition despite the overall low reports of ACEs suggests that ACEs are an important risk factor to consider when assessing dementia risk. Notably, men consistently reported a restricted range of ACEs yet nevertheless showed a pattern of negative association between ACEs and brain structure, underscoring the importance of any ACE exposure. However, the current findings do not sufficiently capture the effects of very high ACEs (e.g., 4+, which are typically associated with more severe outcomes 64 ). This may explain why many of the smaller effects of ACEs in the current study did not survive corrections for multiple comparisons. Given the effects we see in the current low‐ to moderate‐ACE cohort, effects of ACEs on cognition in people who experienced severe early life adversity are likely more pronounced and need to be assessed in future studies.

The sex differences in the cognitive effects of ACEs observed in this study may be indicative of differential compensatory mechanisms in the aging brain, 65 potentially driven by sex differences in physiological and cellular responses to stress. 66 This may help explain why cognitively unimpaired men show structural vulnerability to ACEs while cognition remains intact. However, current neuroimaging data were not sensitive enough for further exploration of such potential mechanisms. Future work would benefit from leveraging more fine‐grained neuroimaging techniques to assess whether age‐associated changes in structural 67 , 68 and functional connectivity 67 , 68 are affected by ACEs in a sex‐specific manner suggestive of different compensation strategies.

Alternatively, our findings could reflect compounding psychosocial factors not captured in this study. For example, cumulative exposure to stress and adversity across the life course may amplify the effects of ACEs. Chronic stress during the menopausal transition is associated with white matter lesions 69 and increased risk of dementia and AD diagnosis later in life. 70 The risk of AD in women has not only been associated with chronic stress in general but also independently predicted by the number of psychosocial stressors reported over a 38‐year period. 70 Similarly, research considering both a history of ACEs and stressful life experiences in adulthood suggests that a history of ACEs combined with recent adversity significantly compounds the risk of psychiatric symptoms, 71 suggesting that early life stressors should not be considered in isolation in adult cohorts.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

The strengths of this study include a cohort spanning the dementia continuum, examining the effects of ACEs by sex, having well‐matched groups in terms of age and education despite diversity in cognitive status, accounting for APOE status in all analyses, and examining both neuroimaging and behavioral outcomes. The study's limitations include cross‐sectional design, small subcohorts – particularly in AD, lower numbers of men in the cognitively unimpaired groups and of women in the AD group, prefrontal volume measures not broken down by subregions, and lack of neuropathology data, which would have enabled full certainty in AD group classification. Future work could benefit from cohort sizes large enough to enable (i) an analysis of specific ACEs; (ii) examination of ACE‐associated effects on cognition in groups with more variance in education and socioeconomic status – low levels of which may magnify effects of ACE on cognition; and (iii) the analysis of the interplay between ACEs and biological factors known to affect sex differences in AD risk, such as estradiol levels, and APOE status. Furthermore, future studies would benefit from targeted recruitment of high‐ACE men.

CONCLUSIONS

This study represents a novel exploration of sex differences in the effects of ACEs along a continuum of risk for AD. ACEs were associated with brain and behavior, with effects depending on sex and cognitive status. Effects on the brain were seen in cognitively unimpaired men, with cognitive effects in both sexes, but predominantly in women, emerging in MCI – a pattern potentially suggestive of differential effects of ACEs on brain and cognitive reserve between the sexes. The effects of ACEs, regardless of sex, appeared attenuated at the high level of pathology characteristic of AD, indicating that ACEs may be a risk factor for cognitive decline and a potential contributing factor for AD risk but that they are unlikely to affect progression in AD itself. Our findings underscore the importance of considering early life psychosocial risk factors when studying sex differences in late‐life AD risk.
