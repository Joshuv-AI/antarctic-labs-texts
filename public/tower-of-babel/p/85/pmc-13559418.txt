Management of benign tracheal stenosis: rigid bronchoscopy versus serial cannulas—a retrospective cohort study from a tertiary center
Authors: Paola Palomares-Capetillo, Patzy Marcela Leal-Valenzuela, Marco Antonio Cruz-López, Enrique Josué Esquivel-Mendez, Luis Eduardo Jiménez-Pineda, Alan García-Grimaldo, Olivia Sánchez-Cabral, Héctor Olvera-Prado, Marco Antonio Iñiguez-García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBenign tracheal stenosis is increasingly recognized and diagnosed; however, its optimal management is not standardized, and no international guidelines are currently available. This study focuses on comparing the outcomes in terms of reintervention and complications between two different techniques for central airway dilation.MethodsA retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted including patients diagnosed with benign tracheal stenosis at a tertiary-care center between January 2024 and January 2025. Patients were treated by two departments—thoracic surgery and interventional bronchoscopy—using serial Rusch cannula (RC) dilatation or rigid bronchoscopy (RB), respectively.ResultsA total of 47 patients were included: 27 in the RC group and 20 in the RB group. Most stenoses were Cotton-Myer grade III in both groups. A history of orotracheal intubation (OTI) was present in 37 patients (24 RC vs. 13 RB). Median time to recurrence after the first dilatation was 16 days in RC and 23 days in RB (P=0.12). The RC group required fewer reinterventions (median 2 vs. 4; P<0.01), but had a higher complication rate (37% vs. 10%; P=0.02).ConclusionsRB was associated with fewer complications, supporting its safety profile. Although patients treated with serial cannulae required fewer reinterventions, this technique was associated with a significantly higher complication rate.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this retrospective cohort of patients with benign tracheal stenosis, rigid bronchoscopy (RB) was associated with a significantly lower complication rate compared with serial Rusch cannula (RC) dilation.

• Patients treated with serial RC required fewer reinterventions, while overall treatment success was similar between groups.

What is known and what is new?

• Endoscopic dilatation is commonly used in the management of benign tracheal stenosis, particularly in patients awaiting definitive surgery or those who are not surgical candidates. RB is considered a cornerstone of interventional airway management because it provides airway control, ventilation, and direct visualization during the procedure.

• Direct comparisons RB and serial RC dilatation are scarce. Our study demonstrates that RB is associated with fewer procedure-related complications, whereas serial RC dilation was associated with fewer reinterventions.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Selection of the dilatation technique should be individualized according to stenosis, characteristics, etiology, available resources, and operator expertise.

• Given its favorable safety profile, RB remains an important option for the endoscopic management of benign tracheal stenosis.

• Prospective multicenter studies are needed to better define the role of different dilatation techniques and to support the development of standardized treatment algorithms.

Introduction

Central airway obstruction can be classified as benign or malignant. Among the causes of benign stenosis are post-intubation tracheal stenosis (PITS) and prior tracheostomy, with a reported frequency ranging from 0.6% to 21%. Less frequent causes include idiopathic and stenosis secondary to autoimmune (vasculitis) or infectious processes (tuberculosis) (1).

Clinical presentation depends on the location and degree of stenosis and generally includes stridor, cough, dyspnea, sputum production, and occasionally hemoptysis (2).

Although the therapeutic use of bronchoscopes dates back more than one hundred years and interventional pulmonology has significantly evolved since the mid-20th century, no current management algorithm exists for tracheal stenosis across clinical scenarios, whether urgent or elective (3).

Endoscopic methods are sometimes used as bridge therapy before a definitive treatment, and in patients who are not surgical candidates, they become the only available option to maintain airway patency (3,4). Interventional bronchoscopy modalities include mechanical dilation with various devices (rigid bronchoscopes, cannulae, bougies), balloon dilation, laser therapy, cryotherapy, argon plasma coagulation, stent placement, and Montgomery T-tubes (5-8).

The only effective long-term definitive treatment, considered the gold standard, is tracheal surgery. However, this is not feasible for all patients with benign stenosis, making it essential to define optimal management for this subgroup (3-5).

The objective of this study was to compare recurrence rates, complications, and safety between rigid bronchoscopy (RB) dilatation and dilatation with serial Rusch cannulae (RC) in patients with benign tracheal stenosis.

We hypothesized that serial cannula dilation would be associated with higher complication rates and earlier restenosis compared to RB. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0742/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

A retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted including patients diagnosed with benign tracheal stenosis at a tertiary referral center between January 2024 and January 2025. Patients were treated only with RB or serial RC dilatation by two different departments: interventional bronchoscopy and thoracic surgery, respectively. Inclusion criteria were: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) confirmed diagnosis of benign tracheal stenosis; and (III) treatment with either RB or serial cannula dilation. Exclusion criteria included: (I) malignant airway stenosis; (II) incomplete medical records; and (III) prior definitive tracheal surgery before evaluation.

Data were obtained from electronic medical records, with prior approval from the Ethics and Research Committee of the National Institute for Respiratory Diseases, Mexico City (approval No. C60-22). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before enrollment. Patient confidentiality and anonymity were preserved in accordance with institutional and ethical standards throughout the study. Demographic characteristics, etiology, stenosis length, Cotton-Myer grading, recurrence interval, number of procedures before definitive treatment, and complications were compared.

Most patients in both groups presented emergently; thus, airway assessment and dilatation were performed first, followed by planning for definitive treatment. The number of interventions prior to definitive management and the interval in days until restenosis after the initial procedure were recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the number of reinterventions required after the initial dilatation procedure. Secondary outcomes included complications, time to restenosis after the first intervention, and treatment success, defined as described below.

Successful cases in the surgical group were defined as those taken to definitive tracheal surgery; in the bronchoscopy group, successes included patients who tolerated stenting or had successful stent removal without restenosis within 6 months.

Bias

Due to the retrospective design, selection bias may be present, as treatment allocation depended on the managing department rather than randomization. Additionally, differences in baseline characteristics and stenosis complexity between groups have influenced outcomes.

Study size

The sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients treated during the study period.

Surgical technique

RB technique

Dilations were performed under deep sedation, using direct visualization with a flexible bronchoscope (Olympus Q-190). Sequential monobloc rigid bronchoscopes (Karl Storz), ranging from 6.5 to 11 mm, were introduced from smaller to larger sizes depending on stenosis diameter (Figure 1).

Images of a patient with tracheal stenosis before (A) and after (B) dilation with rigid bronchoscopes.

Serial RC technique

First, airway evaluation was performed. Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) was administered. A laryngeal mask was placed and connected to an adapter to allow ventilation during introduction of a flexible bronchoscope (Olympus Q-190 or disposable equivalents). Once airway lumen and stenosis characteristics were determined, the laryngeal mask was removed, and under direct laryngoscopy the smallest appropriate cannula was inserted. Progressive dilatation was achieved with larger cannulae until an adequate airway lumen (80–90%) was obtained (Figures 2,3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using descriptive and comparative methods. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Comparisons between groups were made using the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when indicated. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

No multivariate analysis was performed due to the limited sample size.

Discussion

Endoscopic techniques for airway obstruction management have been used for more than 150 years (8-10). Since the introduction of tracheal reconstruction surgery in the 1970s, bronchoscopy has been reserved for selected cases considered inoperable due to stenosis characteristics or medical comorbidities.

Despite advances in interventional bronchoscopy and the emergence of novel techniques over the past four decades, no consensus or guideline exists today for the management of benign tracheal stenosis (1-3). Few studies compare the efficacy and safety of different airway dilatation techniques (11-16).

At our center, serial RCs are frequently used, particularly in emergency scenarios. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies directly comparing serial RC dilatation with RB in benign tracheal stenosis (8,10,17).

Advantages of serial cannula dilation include the potential use of local anesthesia and conscious sedation when combined with flexible bronchoscopy (18-20). However, RB allows simultaneous ventilation (21). RB also enables continuous airway visualization with a flexible bronchoscope, reducing the risk of laceration or perforation (22,23).

Although RB has traditionally been regarded as the cornerstone of endoscopic management for benign tracheal stenosis, substantial evidence supports the safety and effectiveness of other therapeutic modalities, including balloon dilation, thermal ablation techniques, cryotherapy, and combinations thereof. These approaches may be used individually or as part of a multimodal strategy to improve airway patency and reduce symptom recurrence. Nevertheless, only two dilation techniques were evaluated in the present study: RB in one cohort and serial mechanical dilation in the other (4,6,9,11,14).

Some authors describe non-occlusive balloons that permit ongoing ventilation during dilation (23), while others describe gentle dilation using the largest RB possible after laser resection, minimizing trauma from repeated insertions (24).

As reported in previous series, post-intubation stenosis was the most frequent etiology in both groups, although the RB group showed additional benign causes consistent con prior literature.

The literature clearly describes that the pathophysiology of benign tracheal stenosis varies according to its etiology. For example, stenosis associated with autoimmune diseases such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis typically involves the subglottic region, presents as a circumferential lesion, and predominantly affects the anterior tracheal wall. In contrast, post-intubation stenosis is usually membranous in nature and primarily involves the pars membranacea. These anatomical and pathophysiological differences may directly influence recurrence rates and, consequently, the number of procedures required for disease control (25).

Notably, seven RB patients had a history of OTI due to severe COVID-19 pneumonia versus one in the RC group (P<0.01). All COVID-related stenoses were complex, predominantly Cotton III, and most often affected the proximal trachea. These patients generally required ≥2 reinterventions but experienced no complications.

Trauma was another relevant OTI indication: 13 surgery-group patients had trauma, mostly moderate-to-severe traumatic brain injury, and 2 chest trauma cases. Mental health-related etiologies were also observed (anxiety, depression, substance use, suicide attempts).

Glikson et al. found that balloon dilation maintained airway patency longer than RB (P=0.3) (6). We hypothesized that patients treated with serial mechanical dilation would experience a shorter interval to restenosis than those undergoing RB, which served as the rationale for conducting this retrospective study. Previous reports have suggested that mechanical dilation achieves enlargement of the tracheal lumen through disruption of fibrotic tissue and, potentially, through additional injury to the tracheal wall caused by the radial and longitudinal forces applied during the procedure. Such trauma may trigger an inflammatory response and subsequent scar formation, thereby contributing to recurrent stenosis. In addition, serial mechanical dilation provides limited visualization of the distal tip of the dilator during advancement, which may increase the risk of airway injury or perforation. In contrast, although RB may also induce local trauma and inflammation, it offers continuous airway visualization and excellent procedural control, resulting in a favorable safety profile when performed by experienced operators (17,22,23).

Similarly, our study demonstrated—though without statistical significance—that RC dilation had shorter recurrence intervals, possibly due to greater trauma and inflammatory response generated by serial cannulae.

Patients in the bronchoscopy group required more reinterventions on average, likely due to planned surveillance related to stent placement, whereas many surgery-group patients underwent definitive tracheal repair sooner, reducing the need for repeated dilatations.

Several published series on the endoscopic management of benign tracheal stenosis have reported the average number of interventions required to achieve airway stabilization. In most studies, the mean number of procedures ranges from 2 to 4 per patient, although this number may increase to 5–7 interventions in cases of complex stenosis. In our cohort treated with RB, the mean number of reinterventions was 4 procedures per patient; however, one patient required as many as 11 interventions, which may be explained by the complexity of the lesion and its tendency to recur. Similarly, a recent study conducted in a Mexican population reported a total of 216 rigid bronchoscopies performed in 23 patients, corresponding to an average of 7 procedures per patient, a figure higher than that observed in our series (1,3,5,6,26,27).

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential selection and information bias. Second, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power and precluded multivariate analysis. Third, treatment allocation was not randomized, as patients were managed by different departments, which may have resulted in baseline differences between groups, including stenosis complexity. Additionally, heterogeneity in underlying etiologies and clinical presentation may have influenced outcomes.

Conclusions

Both RB and serial RC dilation are feasible and effective options for the management of benign tracheal stenosis. In our cohort, serial mechanical dilation showed a tendency toward shorter recurrence intervals, whereas RB provided greater procedural control and continuous airway visualization, supporting its role as a safe and versatile therapeutic modality.

Treatment selection should be individualized according to patient-specific factors, stenosis complexity, institutional resources, and operator expertise. Furthermore, early identification of candidates for tracheoplasty and timely surgical intervention may help reduce repeated procedures and associated complications.
