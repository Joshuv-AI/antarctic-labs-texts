Single-cell profiling of gastric cancer ascites reveals an epithelial-immune dual phenotype signature predictive for anti-PD-1 immunotherapy responses
Authors: Tianbing Xu, Muyang Yang, Jing Huang, Lisen Lu, Deqiang Deng, Xiujuan Shi, Yao Sun, Jonathan F Lovell, Zhenyu Lin, Yongfa Zheng, Honglin Jin
Published: 2026

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth most prevalent malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1–3]. Peritoneal metastasis (PM) constitutes a common dissemination route in GC, with many advanced GC patients developing malignant ascites (MA), which is characterized by abdominal distension, rapid clinical deterioration, and extremely poor prognosis [2, 4, 5]. Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has recently advanced GC treatment; however, its efficacy in patients with GC-associated peritoneal metastasis (GCPM) remains controversial [6, 7]. Encouragingly, phase III clinical trials, including CheckMate-649 and RATIONALE-305, demonstrated that ICB combined with chemotherapy significantly improves overall and progression-free survival in the presence of PM, reducing mortality risk by approximately 29% and 20%, respectively, in patients with GCPM (HR = 0.71 and 0.80) [8–10]. Nonetheless, considerable heterogeneity in treatment response persists, necessitating further exploration of the underlying immune regulatory mechanisms that influence therapeutic outcomes across patient populations.

Unlike solid tumor tissues or peripheral blood, GCPM represents a distinct “liquid tumor microenvironment” enriched with tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells, and their secreted factors. This milieu is characterized by the accumulation of immunosuppressive factors, frequent cell-cell interactions, and dynamic spatial remodeling [11]. Recent single-cell studies have revealed significant immunoplastcity and complex stromal-immune crosstalk in GCMA, underscoring its unique “liquid immune microenvironment” characteristics [10, 12, 13]. However, most investigations have focused on isolated cell types or particular tissue compartments, leaving a limited understanding of epithelial-immune cellular interactions within ascites and the functions of several uncharacterized cell populations. More importantly, the analysis of the immune microenvironment at single-cell resolution and the identification of novel cell subsets help identify key immune regulatory nodes and potential therapeutic targets, thereby providing a crucial basis for predicting immunotherapy responses and developing personalized treatment strategies [14].

In this study, we characterized the immune landscape of GCMA through single-cell transcriptomic and TCR/BCR repertoire analyses. Notably, we identified a previously undescribed epithelial-immune dual-phenotype cell (EIDPC) population in malignant ascites that co-expresses canonical epithelial and immune markers, exhibits moderate malignant features, and possesses potent immunomodulatory capacity. Integrative single-cell and developmental trajectory analyses revealed that EIDPCs likely originate from epithelial cells and undergo transcriptional reprogramming toward immune regulation, suggesting a novel mechanism underlying tumor immune evasion. Given the limited predictive value of conventional immunotherapy biomarkers in gastric cancer, we developed an immune response signature based on EIDPC core genes (IRS-EIDPC), which demonstrated robust predictive capacity for response to anti-PD-1 therapy and patient prognosis across multiple independent cohorts. Collectively, these findings establish EIDPCs as a critical cell population linking tumor malignancy with immune modulation and highlight their potential as both a predictive biomarker and a therapeutic target in gastric cancer.

Methods

Patient recruitment and sample collection

We enrolled 10 patients with GCMA who were either treatment-naïve or had not received therapy within two months before sampling (Additional file 2: Table S1). Ascites and peripheral blood samples were collected and processed under standardized conditions.

Cell lines

Human gastric adenocarcinoma (AGS), T-cell leukemia (Jurkat), and monocytic leukemia (THP-1) cell lines were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology. Cells were cultured under standard conditions in appropriate media supplemented with fetal bovine serum and antibiotics.

Co-culture and qPCR analysis

To evaluate the impact of immune cells on epithelial gene expression, AGS cells were co-cultured with Jurkat or THP-1 cells using 6-well Transwell inserts with PET membranes and 0.4-µm pores (TCS016006, BIOFIL). Specifically, 1.6 × 10⁶ Jurkat or THP-1 cells were seeded in the upper chamber, and 4 × 10⁵ AGS cells were placed in the lower chamber. After 16 h of co-culture, AGS cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted for reverse transcription. Gene expression was then quantified by qPCR using gene-specific primers. (Additional file 2: Table S2).

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed to characterize epithelial–immune phenotypes in both patient-derived ascites and transwell co-culture systems. Ascites-derived cells were stained with antibodies against epithelial and myeloid markers (EpCAM, CD14, and CD45) following Fc receptor blocking. In parallel, AGS cells from co-culture systems were isolated from the lower chamber and stained with a viability dye and antibodies targeting epithelial and immune markers (EpCAM, CD45, CD3, and CD14). Cells were subsequently fixed and analyzed on a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer. Standardized gating strategies were applied to resolve epithelial and immune cell populations.

Single-cell RNA-seq and V(D)J sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing and paired V(D)J profiling were performed using the MobiCube platform according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Single-cell suspensions were loaded for droplet-based capture, followed by reverse transcription, cDNA amplification, and library construction. Libraries were sequenced in paired-end mode.

Single-cell RNA-seq data preprocessing

Raw sequencing data were processed using the MobiVision workflow to generate gene-cell count matrices. Subsequent analyses were performed using the Seurat software [15]. Low-quality cells and potential doublet cells were excluded based on standard quality control criteria, including gene count, UMI count, and mitochondrial content. After filtering, high-quality single cells were retained for further analysis.

Gene expression data were standardized, and genes with high variability were identified for subsequent analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for dimensionality reduction, followed by visualization using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Batch effects across samples were corrected using harmony (Additional file 2: Table S1) [16]. Cells were clustered and annotated based on marker gene expression and reference sequence methods [17, 18].

V(D)J data analysis

Single-cell V(D)J data were processed using the MobiVision pipeline, and clonotypes were defined based on shared complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) nucleotide sequences. T-cell receptor (TCR) and B-cell receptor (BCR) repertoires were integrated with transcriptomic data to assess clonal expansion, diversity, and inter-tissue overlap. Clonal dynamics, including expansion, migration, and state transition, were quantified using established metrics [19].

Differentially expressed gene and pathway enrichment analyses

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified across groups using standard statistical methods. Functional enrichment analyses, including GO and KEGG, were performed to characterize the biological processes associated with each cell cluster. GSEA and GSVA were also conducted to assess pathway activity at the cell cluster and single-cell levels.

Functional gene signature scoring

We curated gene signatures from prior literature encompassing T cell states (e.g., naïve, cytotoxic, and regulatory) [20], macrophage polarization and function (e.g., M1, M2 polarization, angiogenesis, and phagocytosis) [21], and epithelial pathways (e.g., hypoxia, autophagy, and plasticity). Module scores were calculated to assess pathway activity across different cell populations.

Trajectory analyses

To reconstruct cellular differentiation dynamics, multiple trajectory inference methods were applied, including graph-based and pseudotime approaches [22–26]. By leveraging the consistency of results obtained from these different methods, we ensured the robustness of the inferred lineage relationships and state transitions.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell–cell communication networks were inferred based on ligand–receptor interactions using permutation-based statistical frameworks. Interactions between epithelial and immune cell subsets were analyzed and visualized using established computational tools.

Bulk transcriptomic data acquisition and processing

Bulk transcriptomic datasets and clinical information were obtained from public repositories (TCGA-STAD [27], GSE15459 [28], PRJEB25780 [29], GSE91061 [30], and GSE78220 [31]). Data preprocessing and normalization were performed using standard pipelines to ensure cross-cohort comparability.

Survival analysis

Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models. Risk stratification employed “surv_cutpoint” in survminer R package to determine optimal cutoffs via maximally selected rank statistics, providing unbiased, prognostically informative patient grouping despite uneven sample distributions.

Assessment of malignant potential

The malignant potential of epithelial cells and EIDPCs was evaluated using complementary approaches. Copy number variation (CNV) was inferred from transcriptomic data, and malignancy scores were calculated based on established gene signatures [32, 33]. Specifically, malignancy scores were computed with Seurat’s “AddModuleScore” function, applying curated gene signatures from Zhang et al., derived from the top 100 DEGs between TCGA-STAD tumor and normal tissues [34]. The malignancy score was defined as the difference between malignant and non-malignant module scores. Allele-specific CNV analysis was performed to further validate chromosomal alterations at single-cell resolution [35].

Signature construction and immunotherapy prediction

To construct a predictive gene signature associated with immunotherapy response, differential expression analysis was performed between responders and non-responders, followed by feature selection using machine learning algorithms. A composite score was derived to quantify patient risk. Immunotherapy response was further evaluated using immune checkpoint gene expression, TIDE analysis, and SubMap comparison with external cohorts [36, 37].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on the specific situation. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method combined with the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. The predictive performance of the models was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) and its corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the DeLong method. All statistical tests were two-sided; unless otherwise specified, p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell transcriptome map of the MA and normal control in GC patients

To systematically characterize the cell landscape within the ascitic tumor microenvironment of GC, we performed scRNA-seq on paired MA and PBMC samples from 10 patients who were treatment-naïve or had not received treatment within two months prior to sample collection (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Following stringent quality control and batch effect correction (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A–B, Additional file 2: Table S1), unsupervised clustering of 190,392 cells identified eight major cell types: T cells, T_NK cells, B cells, plasma cells, myeloid cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and platelets (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A–B). Each population was well-defined by canonical marker gene expression profiles (Additional file 1: Fig. S3C, Additional file 2: Table S3).

The atlas of T cells, B cells and myeloid cells in MA and PBMC in GC patients

To delineate the immunopathological characteristics of T cells in GCMA, dimensionality reduction and clustering identified 11 major T cell subclusters across paired MA and PBMC samples (Fig. 1A). Annotations were validated by canonical marker expression (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A–G, Additional file 2: Table S4) and functional scoring (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A–B, Additional file 2: Table S5) [20, 38, 39]. TCR clonality and migration analyses revealed pronounced clonal expansion in T03_CD8T and T05_CD4T subclusters (Fig. 1B, Additional file 1: Fig. S6A–C), with significant TCR sharing between MA and PBMC across patients (Additional file 1: Fig. S6D). STARTRAC analyses confirmed that T03_CD8T exhibited the highest migration and clonal expansion indices (Fig. 1C), and substantial TCR overlap between MA and PBMC was observed for T03_CD8T, T04_CD8T, and T05_CD4T (Additional file 1: Fig. S6E). TCR transition analysis suggested lineage progression from T02_CD8T to T03_CD8T, indicating differentiation from tissue-resident memory to terminal effector states (Additional file 1: Fig. S6F). Besides, the characteristics of B cells in GCMA were also analyzed. Clustering of B cells from MA and matched PBMC samples identified five distinct subclusters (Fig. 1D, Additional file 1: Fig. S7A–B). B cell receptor clonality analysis revealed clonal expansion predominantly in plasma and cycling plasma cell subclusters (Fig. 1E–F, Additional file 1: Fig. S8A–D), with BCR sequence overlap between MA and PBMC (Fig. 1G), indicating antigen-driven expansion of terminally differentiated effectors. Immunoglobulin isotype switching analysis showed that naïve, memory, and atypical memory B cells predominantly expressed unswitched IgM, whereas plasma and cycling plasma cells mainly expressed class-switched IgA and IgG, confirming expected class switch recombination during B cell activation (Fig. 1H, Additional file 1: Fig. S8E–G).

Integrative clustering of myeloid cells from GCMA and PBMC identified 11 distinct subsets (Fig. 2A, Additional file 1: Fig. S9A). Subset identities were validated by marker gene expression and cross-reference with published myeloid classifications (Fig. 2B, Additional file 1: Fig. S9B–D, Additional file 2: Table S6). Among DC clusters, M04_mregDC-LAMP3 uniquely exhibited comprehensive activation, with high expression of IL15, CD40, chemokines (CCL17, CCL19, CCL22), maturation markers (LAMP3, MARCKSL1), and migration regulators (CCR7, FSCN1, SLCO5A1) (Fig. 2C). Functional scoring revealed superior antigen presentation and regulatory functions, positioning this subset centrally in immune tolerance (Fig. 2D, Additional file 2: Table S7). Hallmark pathway GSVA confirmed broad biological activation (Fig. 2E). Trajectory inference suggested that M04_mregDC-LAMP3 differentiates from M02_cDC1-CLEC9A and M03_cDC2-CD1C subsets, indicating microenvironment-induced conversion of conventional DCs into LAMP3⁺ regulatory DCs in ascites (Fig. 2F). In addition to DC subsets, macrophage populations were also resolved, revealing polarization hierarchies in which M10_Mac-PLTP and M11_Mac-SPP1 exhibited classical M2-like phenotypes with elevated M2 gene expression scores compared to M08_Mac-IL1B and M09_Mac-C1QC (Additional file 1: Fig. S10A–B, Additional file 2: Table S8). These subsets were enriched in many tumor-supportive pathways (Additional file 1: Fig. S10C, Additional file 2: Table S9). Marker analysis showed that M11_Mac-SPP1 overexpressed TREM1, SIRPA, and CD47, while M10_Mac-PLTP expressed TREM2, FOLR2, SIRPA, and CD47, molecules associated with immune checkpoint functions and immune escape (Fig. 2G). Cell–cell communication analysis revealed interactions between these macrophages and tumor epithelial cells via complement pathways (C3, C5) and the CD47–SIRPA “don’t eat me” axis; additionally, M10 engaged TREM2 signaling, suggesting complex immunoregulatory roles (Fig. 2H–I). Survival analysis further demonstrated that M10 and M11 significantly stratified overall survival in gastric cancer patients, highlighting their potential prognostic value (Fig. 2J–K, Additional file 2: Table S10).

Together, these results elucidate how immunocyte subsets in ascites mediate immune suppression and tumor evasion, representing compelling therapeutic targets for interventions aimed at immunosuppressive immune cells.

Identification and characterization of novel epithelial-immune dual phenotype cells in GCMA

Through dimensionality reduction analysis of epithelial cells derived from GCMA, we identified a distinct subpopulation characterized by co-expression of canonical epithelial markers (EPCAM, KRT8, and KRT18) alongside typical immune markers (PTPRC, CD14, and C1QB), which we term epithelial-immune dual phenotype cells (EIDPCs) (Fig. 3A–B). This unique subpopulation was consistently detected across multiple patients, suggesting a recurrent and biologically relevant cellular state within the ascitic tumor microenvironment (Fig. 3C, Additional file 1: Fig. S11A–B). Differential expression analysis revealed that EIDPCs significantly upregulated a broad range of immune- and inflammation-associated genes, including S100A8, PTPRC, C1QB, and SRGN, distinguishing them from conventional epithelial cells (Fig. 3D). To further explore their transcriptional identity, we performed correlation analyses based on average gene expression profiles. Notably, EIDPCs exhibited moderate correlation with conventional epithelial cells (r = 0.47) but showed a remarkably strong correlation with macrophages (r = 0.62), whereas epithelial cells and macrophages displayed minimal similarity (r = 0.07), supporting the notion that EIDPCs may represent an intermediate state bridging epithelial and immune lineages (Fig. 3E).

To exclude the possibility that EIDPCs arose from technical doublets, we evaluated gene and unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts alongside computational doublet scores; no enrichment was observed within the EIDPC cluster, indicating their bona fide biological identity (Additional file 1: Fig. S12A–D). Flow cytometric analysis further validated the presence of EIDPCs (EPCAM⁺CD45⁺CD14⁺) in ascites samples from nearly all patients, confirming their existence at the protein level (Fig. 3F).

To assess the malignant potential of EIDPCs, we performed inferCNV analysis, which revealed a gradient of chromosomal instability: conventional epithelial cells exhibited the highest CNV burden, followed by EIDPCs with intermediate levels, whereas canonical immune populations (B cells, plasma cells, and M02 macrophages) displayed minimal genomic aberrations (Fig. 3G). Allele-specific CNV analyses further evidenced extensive chromosomal alterations in both epithelial cells and EIDPCs, corroborating their malignant epithelial origin (Additional file 1: Fig. S13). Consistently, malignancy scoring based on established gene signatures demonstrated that EIDPCs had significantly lower malignancy scores than conventional epithelial cells, accompanied by retained partial malignant epithelial signatures and reduced expression of non-malignant markers, further supporting their intermediate malignant status (Fig. 3H–I).

Collectively, these findings establish EIDPCs as a novel cell population in GCMA characterized by intermediate malignancy and a hybrid epithelial-immune phenotype. These observations suggest that EIDPCs may represent a transitional cell state within the ascitic microenvironment, although further functional studies are required to determine their biological roles.

Experimental validation of EIDPC origin through non-contact co-culture

To investigate whether epithelial-derived GC cells can acquire immune-associated phenotypes and to explore the potential mechanism underlying EIDPC formation, we performed in vitro co-culture experiments using a transwell system that prevents direct cell–cell contact. The GC cell line AGS (of epithelial origin) was cultured in the lower chamber, while Jurkat T cells or THP-1 monocytic cells were placed in the upper chamber, allowing exchange of soluble factors and extracellular vesicles without physical interaction (Additional file 1: Fig. S14A). Following co-culture, AGS cells exhibited significantly increased mRNA expression of immune-related genes, including PTPRC (CD45) and CD14, as measured by quantitative RT-PCR (Additional file 1: Fig. S14B–C). Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed that AGS cells co-cultured with Jurkat cells acquired surface expression of CD45 and CD3E (Additional file 1: Fig. S14D–E), whereas those co-cultured with THP-1 cells expressed CD45 and CD14 (Additional file 1: Fig. S14F–G). These findings demonstrate that gastric cancer cells of epithelial origin can ectopically express both generic immune markers (CD45) and lineage-specific immune molecules upon non-contact exposure to immune cells, recapitulating the dual epithelial-immune phenotype observed in EIDPCs in vivo.

The result suggests that the observed phenotypic acquisition not only results from cell–cell fusion events. Instead, they point to the involvement of soluble mediators, such as cytokines, exosomes, or other extracellular vesicles, as potential vehicles for transferring immune-associated molecular signals from immune cells to tumor cells. Collectively, these experimental data support the hypothesis that EIDPCs arise from epithelial tumor cells undergoing phenotypic adaptation within the immunosuppressive ascitic microenvironment, likely through paracrine signaling or vesicle-mediated intercellular communication that drives transcriptional reprogramming toward an immune-like state.

Transcriptional reprogramming of EIDPCs in GCMA reveals cellular plasticity mechanisms underlying tumor immune escape

Comprehensive transcriptomic and developmental trajectory analyses of epithelial cells and EIDPCs demonstrated that EIDPCs were significantly enriched in immune activation and inflammatory pathways, including lipopolysaccharide signaling, IFN-γ response, cellular localization, cytokine-mediated signaling, and MHC class II antigen processing (Fig. 4A). In contrast, classical epithelial cells predominantly enriched metabolic and stress adaptation pathways, such as glycolysis, growth inhibition, zinc ion homeostasis, cadmium response, and electron transport (Fig. 4A).

GSEA based on KEGG pathways further demonstrated that EIDPCs exhibited upregulation of chemokine signaling, TGF-β pathway components, extracellular matrix remodeling, T cell receptor signaling, and interleukin signaling, reflecting their potent immunoregulatory activity. Conversely, pathways associated with proliferation and fundamental cellular processes—including protein translation, mitotic cell cycle, DNA repair, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle metabolism, and TP53 signaling—were downregulated in EIDPCs, indicating a functional shift from proliferation to immune modulation (Fig. 4B). GSVA corroborated these findings, showing that epithelial cells were enriched for proliferation-associated programs, whereas EIDPCs displayed enhanced immune activation, signaling transduction, metabolic activity, and organelle function (Fig. 4C).

Module scoring further quantified phenotypic distinctions: epithelial cells scored higher in hypoxia adaptation, ferroptosis sensitivity, and proliferation, consistent with malignant tumor characteristics. In contrast, EIDPCs exhibited elevated plasticity, autophagy regulation, angiogenesis, MHC-II expression, cancer stem cell traits, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, supporting their immune regulatory and migratory capacities (Fig. 4D, Additional file 2: Table S11). Cytokine profiling revealed that epithelial cells expressed pro-growth factors such as VEGFA/B, PDGFC, EGF, and IL15, while EIDPCs expressed MHC-II molecules (HLA-DPA1, HLA-DRA) and immunomodulatory cytokines and chemokines, including CCL2, CCL20, CXCL8, IL10, IL1B, TGFB1, and TNF (Fig. 4E).

Trajectory inference using Monocle and CytoTRACE2 indicated that EIDPCs arise subsequent to epithelial cells, consistent with a process of cellular transformation (Fig. 4F–H). Pseudo-time functional enrichment analysis identified six distinct modules: EIDPCs were enriched for cytokine production, myeloid cell activation, and immune response pathways, while epithelial cells were enriched for ion transport, oxidative stress responses, and basal metabolic processes (Fig. 4I–J). Together, these findings highlight a transcriptional reprogramming event underlying the transition from classical epithelial cells to immune-regulatory EIDPCs, implicating this plasticity mechanism in tumor immune escape.

IRS-EIDPC scores can effectively predict anti-PD-1 immunotherapy response and prognosis

Given the heterogeneity of responses to anti-PD-1-targeted therapies in metastatic gastric cancer (mGC) and the suboptimal overall response rates, we investigated the association between EIDPC features and immunotherapy efficacy [29]. Immune checkpoint expression analysis revealed significantly higher expression of key molecules, including CTLA4, PD-1/PD-L1, and HAVCR2, in EIDPCs compared with classical epithelial cells, with HAVCR2 showing particularly pronounced elevation in the EIDPC subset (Fig. 5A).

Using a machine learning approach, we selected ten core genes to construct the IRS-EIDPC predictive model (Fig. 5B, Additional file 1: Fig. S15A). TIDE analysis of the TCGA-STAD cohort demonstrated significantly higher total TIDE scores, as well as elevated T-cell exclusion and immune dysfunction scores, in the high IRS-EIDPC group relative to the low-score group (p < 0.05), indicating enhanced immune escape mechanisms (Fig. 5C–F). SubMap cross-validation using published immunotherapy datasets revealed that the low IRS-EIDPC group exhibited stronger molecular similarity to anti-PD-1 treatment responders (all p < 0.05) (Fig. 5G–J).

To further validate the predictive value of the IRS-EIDPC score, we analyzed three independent cohorts comprising a total of 204 patients (148 non-responders and 56 responders) who received anti-PD-1 therapy, including PRJEB25780 (gastric cancer), GSE91061 (melanoma), and GSE78220 (melanoma). Across all cohorts, responders consistently showed significantly lower IRS-EIDPC scores than non-responders (all p < 0.05) (Fig. 5K–M). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses confirmed robust predictive performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.929, 0.693, and 0.728, respectively (Fig. 5N–P, Additional file 1: Fig. S15B–D). Survival analyses further validated the prognostic utility of the score, as patients with low IRS-EIDPC scores exhibited significantly longer overall survival in the GSE91061 and GSE78220 cohorts (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5Q–R).

In summary, the IRS-EIDPC score effectively captures the immune status of EIDPCs and demonstrates strong predictive value for anti-PD-1 therapy response and patient prognosis. These findings underscore its potential as a reliable biomarker for precise patient stratification and optimization of immunotherapy strategies in clinical practice.

Discussion

PM constitutes the predominant aggressive dissemination mode in advanced GC, with associated MA representing both advanced disease stage and a complex, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [10, 13, 40, 41]. Despite growing recognition of immune and stromal roles within the TME, comprehensive understanding of immune cell composition, states, and contributions to immune evasion in MA remains limited [42–44]. Here, we employed single-cell transcriptomics and immune repertoire profiling to dissect the immune landscape of gastric cancer-associated malignant ascites (GCMA). Our analysis revealed extensive remodeling of T cells, B cells, and myeloid lineages within this “liquid tumor microenvironment.” Notably, we identified a clonally expanded CD8⁺ T cell subset (T03_CD8T) that undergoes active recruitment and terminal differentiation in ascites (Fig. 1B–C, Additional file 1: Fig. S6A–F). In the myeloid compartment, a regulatory DC subset (M04_mregDC-LAMP3) was found to originate from conventional DCs under ascitic pressure, expressing high levels of immune checkpoints and chemokines that foster immune tolerance (Fig. 2C–F) [45, 46]. Furthermore, two immunosuppressive macrophage subsets (M10_Mac-PLTP and M11_Mac-SPP1) engaged the CD47-SIRPA and TREM2 signaling axes, reinforcing immune evasion (Fig. 2G–I, Additional file 1: Fig. S10A–C) [47–51]. Collectively, these findings illustrate a coordinated immunosuppressive network in GCMA that contributes to poor prognosis and immunotherapy resistance.

Beyond these immune alterations, we identified and characterized a previously undescribed epithelial-immune dual-phenotype cell (EIDPC) population, which corresponds to recently described dual-phenotype cells in solid tumors, suggesting a conserved tumor adaptation mechanism [52–54]. Cancer-associated macrophage-like cells (CAMLs) in NSCLC co-express myeloid and epithelial markers similarly to EIDPCs (Fig. 3A–F) [55], while the high proportion of CD45⁺ circulating tumor cells (CTCs) parallels EIDPC PTPRC (CD45) expression [56]. InferCNV and malignancy scoring revealed that EIDPCs possess an intermediate malignant phenotype—lower than classical epithelial cells but significantly higher than immune cells—supporting their identity as a transitional malignant subpopulation (Fig. 3G–I). Trajectory analyses (Monocle, CytoTRACE2) indicated that EIDPCs likely originate from epithelial cells and undergo transcriptional reprogramming toward immune regulation (Fig. 4F–H). This is corroborated by our in vitro non-contact co-culture experiments, where gastric cancer cells acquired CD45 and lineage-specific immune markers upon exposure to immune cell-derived soluble factors, recapitulating the EIDPC phenotype (Additional file 1: Fig. S14A–G). These findings point to paracrine signaling or extracellular vesicle-mediated transfer as potential mechanisms driving this phenotypic conversion.

Functionally, EIDPCs exhibited potent immunomodulatory capacity. They upregulated chemokine signaling, TGF-β pathways, MHC class II antigen presentation, and multiple immunomodulatory cytokines (CCL2, IL10, TGFB1, TNF), while downregulating proliferation-associated programs (Fig. 4A–E). They also expressed high levels of immune checkpoint molecules including CTLA4, PD-1/PD-L1, and HAVCR2 (Fig. 5A). This transcriptional signature suggests that EIDPCs may contribute to tumor immune evasion by adopting an “immune camouflage” strategy. Given the limited predictive value of conventional biomarkers for immunotherapy in gastric cancer, we developed the immune response signature of EIDPC (IRS-EIDPC) using a machine-learning approach. The 10-gene signature robustly predicted PD-1 inhibitor response and overall survival across multiple independent cohorts (gastric cancer AUC 0.929; melanoma AUC 0.693–0.728), with low IRS-EIDPC scores associated with significantly better outcomes (Fig. 5K–R). The IRS-EIDPC thus holds translational promise as a reliable biomarker for patient stratification.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample size of the MA cohort is relatively small, and while the IRS-EIDPC signature was validated in retrospective datasets, prospective validation in larger cohorts is warranted. The functional roles of EIDPCs are primarily inferred from transcriptomic and in vitro data; in vivo lineage-tracing and perturbation studies are needed to confirm their origin and immunomodulatory mechanisms. Finally, whether EIDPCs and the IRS-EIDPC score apply to other metastatic niches or cancer types remains to be explored.

Conclusions

This study systematically mapped the immune landscape of the GCMA microenvironment through single-cell transcriptomics and immune repertoire profiling, revealing extensive remodeling and plasticity across T cell, B cell, and myeloid lineages. We identified and validated a novel EIDPC subset characterized by moderate malignancy and potent immunoregulatory capacity. Based on the core EIDPC gene signature, we established the immune response signature IRS-EIDPC, which reliably predicts patient prognosis and anti-PD-1 treatment response across multiple independent clinical cohorts. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the ascites tumor immune microenvironment but also provide promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets for precision immunotherapy in GC.
