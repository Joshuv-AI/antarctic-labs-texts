Outcomes of chronic myeloid leukemia patients after therapeutic failure to conventional tyrosine kinase inhibitors and asciminib
Authors: Lucía Pérez-Lamas, Adrián Segura Díaz, Elvira Mora Casterá, Marta Hidalgo Soto, Antonio Paz Coll, Natalia De las Heras, Montse Cortes, Patricia Vélez Tenza, Magdalena Sierra Pacho, Alicia Senin Magan, Anna Angona, Carlos Cervero, Raquel de Paz, Araceli Salamanca Cuenca, Marta Santaliestra, Javier López Marín, Luis Felipe Casado, Valentín García-Gutiérrez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) who have failed conventional tyrosine kinase inhibitors (cTKIs) and asciminib constitute a complex group of patients with few therapeutic options. A retrospective descriptive multicenter observational study was carried out including patients with CML who had presented a therapeutic failure to ≥ 2 cTKIs and asciminib, and had received or were not candidates to ponatinib. Of the 19 patients enrolled, 8 patients failed asciminib due to intolerance and 11 due to resistance. The most common strategy for intolerant patients was to initiate a previously administered cTKI (6/8) with dose adjustments or symptomatic management of adverse events (AEs). Of the patients who failed due to resistance, only patients who underwent progenitor transplantation achieved profound long-term responses. A frequent strategy was to use ponatinib (4/11) in patients previously considered non-candidates, with poor responses in patients in whom dose reductions were used, and severe AEs in patients at standard doses. In the remaining patients, cTKIs and other agents (interferon, hydroxyurea, citarabine, busulfan) were used with poor responses. Patients who progressed to advanced stages had a dismal prognosis. With a median follow-up of 11.2 months, overall survival of the global cohort was 73%; 100% for intolerant patients, 71% for resistant patients and 25% for those who discontinue asciminib in accelerated/blastic phase.Keywords: Chronic myeloid leukemia, Conventional tyrosine kinase inhibitors, Resistance, Intolerance, Asciminib, Real world setting

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) patients who have failed two or more conventional tyrosine kinase inhibitors (cTKI) have limited therapeutic alternatives [1]. Until recently, ponatinib was the preferred option for patients failing cTKIs due to resistance; however, its substantial risk of vasoocclusive events limits its use in patients prone to cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. In intolerant patients, the most common strategy was to switch between the different available cTKIs, but given their shared mechanism of action, in many cases the appearance of cross-intolerance led to sequential failure of the different available cTKIs [3–5].

The recent approval of asciminib has provided an important therapeutic option for patients both resistant and intolerant to cTKIs [6, 7]. This drug is a first-in-class specific allosteric inhibitor of BCR::ABL1, that produces a selective binding to the myristoyl pocket of ABL1 kinase. Unlike other ATP-competitive TKIs, asciminib functions by mimicking the myristoylated peptide, restoring the kinase’s inactive conformation [8].

For patients who exhibit resistance to cTKIs, asciminib has shown efficacy in ATP binding site mutations, including the T315I mutation, allowing another effective and safe option to ponatinib [9]. Outcomes seem to be less impressive in patients who fail ponatinib because of resistance [10]. In patients in whom cTKIs fail due to unacceptable adverse events (AEs), asciminib has been shown to be more selective, as it avoids many of the “off-target” effects of cTKIs [11]. It has also been observed that there is no cross-toxicity for many of the AEs common with cTKIs [12].

Patients who have failed cTKIs and both asciminib and ponatinib, or who are not candidates for ponatinib due to cardiovascular risk, are in a difficult situation in which there are no comparative studies and clinical guidelines only advise early consideration of an allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (allo-HSCT), with the risks and toxicity that this entails, or inclusion of the patient in a clinical trial [2, 13]. The aim of this analysis is to describe the most common real-world treatment strategies in this setting, as well as to gain insight into the prognosis in this difficult-to-treat group.

Materials and methods

The study enrolled patients diagnosed with CML, aged 18 years or older, who had experienced therapeutic failure with asciminib, either due to resistance or intolerance. All patients had also received ponatinib or this drug was contraindicated due to cardiovascular comorbidity.

Patients were selected from a heavily polytreated cohort of 85 CML patients who received asciminib through a managed-access program provided by Novartis between July 2018 and March 2023 at different centers in Spain, with previously reported results [12]. From the overall cohort of 85 patients, those who failed asciminib were selected. Five patients who died from other causes unrelated to CML or its treatment were excluded (pharyngeal neoplasm, hepatic adenocarcinoma, skin ulcer with calciphylaxis, and two unknown causes). The remaining 61 patients (72%) of the extended cohort were still on asciminib at the time of analysis. The study was conducted retrospectively, with a descriptive, multicenter, and observational design.

Clinical data were collected from the medical chart by the responsible CML expert physician using the REDCap electronic database. Demographic data, co-morbidities, disease characteristics, previous treatments, AEs, reasons for discontinuation, treatments received after cTKIs/asciminib, response data and subsequent toxicity were included.

Response data was analyzed in peripheral blood samples with real time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) for the determination of BCR::ABL1. Samples were processed in EUTOS-accredited laboratories. Response analysis was performed at a minimum periodicity of 3 months. Response grades were established based on the European Leukemia Net (ELN) 2020 recommendations [2]. Mutation analysis was performed by Next Generation Sequencing in the patients in whom it was carried out.

Treatment failure to previous tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) was defined as resistance (established based on the ELN 2020 criteria [2]) or intolerance (unacceptable toxicity leading to discontinuation of the TKI). AEs were graded based on the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for AEs Version 5.0.

Results

Baseline data

Nineteen patients were recruited from 17 centers in Spain (Table 1). The mean age at diagnosis was 54.8 years (range 37–79), 47.4% were men and the rest were women. At diagnosis 18 (95%) patients were in chronic phase and one patient was in accelerated phase. The percentage of low, intermediate and high sokal score at diagnosis was one third for each group. The median number of previous lines was 5 (range 3–7). Regarding cTKIs, 84.2% (n = 16) had received imatinib, 94.7% (n = 18) dasatinib, 68.4% (n = 13) bosutinib and another 68.4% (n = 13) nilotinib. Almost half of the patients (47.4%) had received ponatinib (n = 9) and the rest were not considered candidates due to cardiovascular risk. Three of 19 patients (15.8%) had previously received an allo-HSCT. Median time from diagnosis to asciminib failure was 7.9 years (range 1.7–25.5). Median age at the time of therapeutic failure to asciminib was 64.4 years (range 41.6–87.6).

Eight out of 19 patients failed treatment due to intolerance and 11/19 due to resistance. Median time on asciminib was 5.6 months (range 0.7–40), slightly higher for resistant (6.3 months, range 1.4–31) vs. intolerant (5.5 months, range 0.7–41) patients.

In terms of best response achieved with asciminib; 4 patients achieved MMR or better, and 2 patients CHR. Overall, 7 patients maintained MMR, 7 CHR, 3 cytogenetic response and two maintained worse response than CHR. Among patients who discontinued due to resistance, only 2/11 (18.2%) patients achieved MMR or better vs. 5/8 (62.5%) in the intolerant ones. Six patients required dose reductions (3/8 intolerant vs. 3/11 resistant) and 9 patients required temporary interruptions (5/8 intolerant vs. 4/11 resistant).

Of the patients who discontinued due to resistance, four of them had progression to accelerated/blastic phase at the time of withdrawal. Of the intolerant patients, the most frequent reason for discontinuation was pancreatitis (50%, 4/8). The remaining causes were renal failure (1), pleural effusion (1), fatigue (1) and neurological symptoms (1). All the AEs that led to discontinuation were grade 3.

Only in 1 out of 19 patients new mutations were described during asciminib treatment; three variants in the ABL sequence; p.Tyr236His (variant allele frequency (VAF) 1%), p.Met343Ile (VAF 7%) and p.Asp391Asn (VAF 2%), all described in the same patient, that was among those who advanced to an accelerated phase.

The two patients who started treatment in accelerated phase managed to control their disease, one achieving CHR, maintaining treatment with asciminib until discontinuation due to new progression to accelerated phase after 31 months of treatment. The second patient maintained at all times a worse response than CHR, maintaining treatment for 12 months until discontinuation due to resistance.

Treatment following asciminib

Sixteen out of 19 patients received treatment after asciminib failure (Table 4; Fig. 1). The median number of lines was 1.26 (range 0–4). As mentioned, 42% of patients (8/19) failed treatment due to intolerance and 58% (11/19) due to resistance.

Failure due to intolerance

In the group of patients who failed asciminib due to intolerable AEs (Table 3), two patients did not require restart of treatment due to a sustained deep molecular response (MR) after a mean follow-up of 17.2 months (range 5.6–22.4). The rest (6/8) required treatment, which in all cases involved restarting a cTKI that they had already received (2 patients imatinib, 2 dasatinib, 2 nilotinib). In 50% (3/6) dose reductions and symptomatic treatment were used to control AEs, which in 2 out of 3 patients did not compromise efficacy, allowing to maintain MMR (in the third patient receiving imatinib 200 mg, with only three months of follow-up, CHR was not achieved). In another case, low-dose corticosteroid therapy was used for the management of pleural effusion, maintaining standard dose and achieving MMR.

Of the two remaining patients, one maintains CHR after 3 months of imatinib, with no improvement in response at last follow up. The second patient, among those intolerant to treatment, is the only individual necessitating an additional change in therapy. This patient, following one year of dasatinib 100 mg therapy, during which maintained CHR, required a change due to resistance, and subsequently enrolled a phase 1a/1b clinical trial involving the ELVN-001 molecule. All patients who failed asciminib due to intolerance are still alive and without progression to advanced stages at the last follow-up.

Failure due to resistance

Regarding the patients that required treatment change due to progression to advanced phases, one patient underwent allo-HSCT. The rest were not candidates due to age or clinical impairment (1 received dasatinib, 1 hydroxyurea (HU), 1 without treatment). Only the patient who underwent HSCT is alive more than one year after the procedure with a grade 5 MR. In the rest the median overall survival (OS) was less than 1 month.

Of the remaining resistant patients, 4/7 (57%) received ponatinib (1 of 4 had received it previously), 1 imatinib, 1 allo-HSCT and the other patient received cytarabine + interferon + HU. This last patient, after failure of hematologic control with cytarabine, interferon and hydroxyurea, restarted asciminib at a higher dose, increasing to 160 mg every 12 h without response.

Among patients who received ponatinib following asciminib, 2 out of 4 (50%) did so at reduced doses due to cardiovascular risk (15 mg and 30 mg, respectively). Both cases encountered difficulties in improving response, with one maintaining a CCR and the other exhibiting a response worse than CHR. In the other two cases where ponatinib 45 mg was initiated in patients not previously considered candidates, due to the absence of other therapeutic options, severe AEs were observed. One patient discontinued treatment due to grade 3 arterial hypertension, while the second patient experienced a grade 5 acute myocardial infarction, resulting in the patient’s death. Three of 7 patients required additional lines including change of TKI (asciminib, nilotinib, dasatinib) and other strategies (HU, busulfan, interferon, cytarabine).

In terms of response, only the patient undergoing HSCT maintained a mayor MR (1/7). One patient maintained a CCR, one patient a CHR, and the rest (4/7) were unable to improve response despite treatment (maintaining worse response than CHR at the end of follow-up). Two patients died (1 acute myocardial infarction, 1 digestive neoplasia).

With a median follow-up of 11.2 months, OS of the global cohort is 73%; 100% for intolerant patients, 71% for resistant patients and 25% for those in accelerated/blastic phase.

Discussion

Managing patients with failure to ≥ 2 cTKIs and asciminib remains challenging. Intolerant patients can be managed with dose reductions and symptomatic treatment, although with significant impact on their quality of life. In resistant patients there is currently, as suggested by the findings of this study, an unmet therapeutic need with poor survival outcomes.

Allo-HSCT provides a potent therapeutic tool with a high likelihood of curing the disease, however, it has a high morbimortality rate and many patients are not candidates due to age or comorbidities. In this study, this approach was the only one capable of yielding long-lasting deep responses when faced with therapeutic failure to cTKI and asciminib.

In resistant patients, we observed that rechallenging previously used TKIs leads to insufficient responses and poor outcomes. Mutations and resistance mechanisms that may arise in patients who receive sequential treatment with different cTKIs and asciminib can cause limited sensitivity to the remaining TKIs. The use of ponatinib at reduced doses in patients at risk of vasoocclusive events without prior optimal response does not appear to be effective and its use at standard doses in these patients may have fatal consequences.

In this scenario of need, multiple options are being explored in the context of clinical trials, and may provide better choices for these patients in the near future.

Combining asciminib with different cTKIs in patients who have previously shown resistance to ≥ 2 TKIs presents an appealing prospect. This approach offers the potential for dual targeting, effectively addressing established resistance to individual treatments, as demonstrated by in vitro studies [14]. Numerous studies are already investigating this strategy, both in refractory patients (NCT02081378) [15] and to improve treatment-free remission rate (NCT04838041, NCT04216563, NCT03578367) [16, 17].

Additionally, new third-generation TKIs are on the horizon. Olverembatinib, an ATP-binding site inhibitor of BCR::ABL1, obtained approval in China in November 2021. In the pivotal phase 1/2 trial, this inhibitor demonstrated a 56% MMR rate among 127 patients, with over 80% having received ≥ 2 lines of treatment and 60% harboring the T315I mutation. Notably, in accelerated phase patients, the MMR rate was 45% [18]. Trials for this same drug are also underway in the United States (NCT04260022) [19], as well as a phase III clinical trial in China (NCT05311943). Vodobatinib is also a third-generation TKI with limited off-target effects that has shown efficacy in patients treated with asciminib and ponatinib in a Phase I clinical trial, with the Phase 2 study still ongoing (NCT02629692) [20].

Other allosteric inhibitors of BCR::ABL1, such as TGRX-678 (NCT05434312) [21] or TERN-701 (NCT06163430), are also currently under investigation, both still in early clinical phases. Additionally, drugs within the small molecule category, like ELVN-001 (NCT05304377) [22] or KRT-232 (NCT04835584) [23], are being studied as part of another therapeutic strategy. Furthermore, CML is not excluded from the cell therapy revolution, with ongoing trials exploring the use of natural killer cell therapy (NCT04808115, NCT03348033) for chronic phase CML and CAR-T cell therapies for patients with advanced disease (anti CD123-CD33: NCT04156256, anti-CLL1-CD33 CAR-T: NCT03795779). There are also options for patients with advanced stages, combining ponatinib with azacitidine (phase II clinical trial PONAZA: NCT03895671) or with decitabine and venetoclax (NCT04188405) [24].

Many of these investigational strategies explore new pathways to treat CML, which are essential to bypass the mechanisms of resistance and progression generated with current therapies. Likewise, the toxicity profile of small molecules or cell therapy is diametrically opposed to that produced by TKIs. We await that in the future all these approaches will draw a map in which it will be possible to design the best therapeutic algorithm for each patient depending on the type of resistance, the patients’ comorbidities and their previous treatments on a case-by-case basis, providing hopeful alternatives for these patients.
