Candidate serum metabolite biomarkers of subclinical Haemonchus contortus infection in sheep
Authors: Hamza Jawad, Desiree Gellatly, Yaogeng Lei, Sean Thompson, Shima Borzouie, Olufemi Osonowo, John S Gilleard, Younes Miar, Seyed Ali Goldansaz, Ghader Manafiazar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParasitic infection is a major factor negatively affecting sheep production worldwide. The gold standard, fecal egg count, identifies only clinical stages after egg count establishment and shedding in the feces. However, detection at the sub-clinical phase could offer a critical window for strategic deworming and prevent production losses. Blood metabolites often perform as signalling molecules preceding major physiological events; thus, they could provide a prognosis of immunological alterations in the host. Therefore, we conducted a controlled longitudinal study to identify predictive biomarkers of Haemonchus contortus infection in 60 Rideau Arcott ewe lambs, analyzing 420 serum samples across seven timepoints (from one day pre-inoculation to 57-days post-inoculation [dpi]) using direct injection mass spectrometry and reverse-phase liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry custom assay. We identified a total of 22 unique metabolites as candidate biomarkers of parasitic infection pre- and post-drenching, ranging in area-under-the-receiver-operating-characteristic-curve (AU-ROC) values 0.69–0.94 (p < 0.05). The earliest candidate biomarkers were detectable at 7-dpi, the most promising pre-drenching biomarkers emerged at 21-dpi, while the highest AU-ROC value post-drenching was at 42-dpi. This is the first study to identify predictive blood biomarkers of H. contortus infection in Rideau Arcott ewe lambs, including the relevant mathematical models. Introduction

Parasitic infections and building resistance to anthelmintic drugs are considered one of the major challenges facing sheep production around the globe, with Haemonchus contortus being a particularly significant pathogen1. Haemonchus contortus inhabits the gastrointestinal (GI) tract of small ruminants and consumes a significant amount of blood, therefore preventing the animals from reaching their potential growth, and in severe cases, causing death2–4. After ingestion, H. contortus third-stage larvae (L3) develop in the host abomasum for 18 to 21 days without producing eggs (pre-patent period), then progress through the L4 stage and mature into egg-shedding adult worms (patent period)5. Sub-clinical infections during both periods cause hidden production losses through poor nutrient absorption5,6, decreasing growth by 25%7, and lowers live-weight gain by 30 g per day8, while reducing wool, milk, and lamb survival9. In some cases, moderate worm burdens < 2,000 persist as “silent” infections; these worms in the patent phase continue to shed eggs and contaminate pastures10. Sub-clinical infections can progress to clinical disease when larval burdens become excessive or when the host is immunocompromised.

The particular diagnostic challenge is that during the pre-patent period5, H. contortus remains undetectable by conventional diagnostic methods, particularly fecal egg count (FEC) approach. The FEC method is considered as a gold standard for detecting parasitic infection11,12. Moreover, during the sub-clinical infection phase, animals exhibit no obvious phenotypic signs of the infection. Due to economic losses during sub-clinical infection phases, many studies have emphasized the early infection detection by relying on parasite burden-dependent proxies such as occult fecal blood13,14, early hematological shifts15, or anti-larval antibodies16–18. However, each of these approaches shows variable sensitivity, which is confounded by diet and breed, and performs poorly under field conditions.

As the parasites propagate, the clinical manifestation of the signs, depending on parasite load and host immunity, becomes visible and measurable at the phenotypic level with the highest FECs2,6. These clinical signs mainly include anemia, weight loss or lack of weight gain, bottle jaw, reduced wool quality, lower milk production, abortion in pregnant ewes, and death of the infected host. In the absence of early prognosis, the parasite’s high fecundity (egg shedding) leads to infection transmission among the rest of the flock and contamination of the pasture19. To prevent infection complications, farmers perform a blind treatment of the flock using commercial anthelmintic drugs, assuming all animals are infected20. The lack of alternative drugs and the widespread use of similar anthelmintics (through dewormers and injections) have led to the development of drug resistance in the parasites over the years. Moreover, increasing the frequency of drug administration to make up for the reduced efficacy is further deteriorating treatment effectiveness and increasing consumer concern21. Hence, alternative detection methods of parasitic infection during the sub-clinical phase become more vital than ever before, providing precise data on individual animals for targeted and data-driven prevention of infection22.

Previous studies have shown that sheep parasites manipulate host metabolism at the molecular level22–24. The host undergoes significant metabolic changes across multiple biochemical pathways25, resulting in alterations to various metabolites, particularly in the blood26. Identification and quantification of these biological fluctuations in the blood could help identify predictive metabolite biomarkers of sub-clinical stage infection22. These small molecules (< 1500 Da) metabolites, collectively known as the metabolome, can be characterized using various metabolomics technologies27. Metabolite biomarkers are routinely used in biomedical settings to provide a prognosis of metabolic conditions such as cancer, diabetes, Alzheimer’s disease, and immune-related disorders28,29. This is while the application of metabolomics in livestock research, with a path to translating metabolite biomarkers into pen-side tools for non-invasive and rapid detection of economically important traits such as feed efficiency, growth potential, and milk production, has gained momentum27. While there are very few reports using metabolomics to investigate sheep parasitic infections22,23, to date, we have not found any study that explored candidate biomarkers of H. contortus sub-clinical infection while also providing mathematical equations to diagnose the infection condition. Furthermore, none have investigated the kinetics of the blood metabolome throughout the course of infection development.

Therefore, we constructed a longitudinal controlled experiment and manually infected ewe lambs with H. contortus to investigate the blood metabolome of infected sheep. We hypothesized that alterations in the blood metabolome of ewe lambs infected with H. contortus could be used to identify and quantify candidate biomarkers of sub-clinical parasitic infection. Hence, the objectives of this study included: (1) characterizing the baseline serum metabolome of healthy ewe lambs during the course of the experiment, (2) characterizing the serum metabolome of ewe lambs infected with H. contortus, (3) examining the serum metabolome of healthy and infected animals after anthelmintic drenching, and (4) identifying metabolites as candidate biomarkers of sub-clinical parasitic infection and constructing the relevant predictive models.

Results

Findings from this study are presented in two main areas. First, the parasitological findings demonstrate sub-clinical infection progression and deworming (drenching) efficacy, and the second section presents metabolomic findings.

Parasitological findings

The nemabiome sequencing analysis confirmed 100% H. contortus species presence, with no other GI nematode (GIN) species detected. The CT group maintained a parasite-free status (0.0 ± 0.0 EPG) throughout the trial. Parasite eggs were detected exclusively in the IN group starting from 21 dpi, increasing markedly (p < 0.001) by 28 dpi (Table 1). Subsequently, in the post-drench phase, significant divergence (p < 0.001) in FEC between IN-D and IN-ND was observed. Deworming of IN-D resulted in the complete eradication of eggs (0.0 ± 0.0 EPG) by 35 dpi, which was maintained through 56 dpi. The IN-ND group showed persistent parasite burden, peaking at 28 dpi before declining through 56 dpi, yet never achieving complete clearance.

Metabolomics findings

Metabolomic profiles in healthy sheep

Using targeted metabolomics DI/LC–MS/MS (direct injection mass spectrometry and reverse-phase liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, TMIC Prime®) approach, we identified and quantified 70 metabolites in serum from 60 ewe lambs across seven timepoints. These metabolites were classified into 15 classes following the ClassyFire ontology30, predominantly glycerophospholipids, carboxylic acids and derivatives, and sphingolipids (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Our first objective was to comprehensively identify and quantify the serum metabolome of healthy ewe lambs (CT-ND, n = 15) from baseline (day− 1) to 57 dpi (Supplementary Table S1). Between day − 1 to 57, sequential metabolomic t-test analysis revealed significant (p < 0.05) temporal changes in healthy ewe lambs (Supplementary Table S2). Student’s t-test results showed that metabolic changes were most pronounced in early timepoints comparisons, with day − 1 versus day 7 exhibiting the highest number of significantly altered metabolites. As the study progressed from day − 1 to 57 dpi, the magnitude and frequency of metabolic variations between consecutive timepoints gradually decreased (Supplementary Table S2).

The fold change analysis and volcano plot analysis highlighted significant (p < 0.05) changes in the concentration of indole acetic acid (IAA), p-hydroxyhippuric acid, HPHPA, and polyamines (spermidine, spermine) in the CT-ND group at several timepoints (Supplementary Table S2). The PLS-DA analysis also identified a similar group of metabolites in the top 15 metabolites list and generated statistically significant models (permutation n = 1000) for day 42 versus day 57 (p < 0.05) and a tendency for day 21 versus day 28 (p = 0.06) results (Supplementary Table S3). The metabolite concentration pattern in the CT-ND group revealed notable temporal patterns, with citrulline dominating at day − 1, 21 dpi, and 35 dpi (range 244.2–399.5 μM), while beta-hydroxybutyric acid peaked at 7, 28, 42, and 57 dpi (range 274.3–332.9 μM). Consistently, acylcarnitines maintained the lowest concentrations, with C7DC (range 0.02–0.03 μM) as the metabolite with the lowest concentration across all timepoints (Supplementary Table S1).

Metabolomic changes associated with H. contortus infection

After establishing longitudinal metabolomic profiles in CT-ND animals, we characterized the temporal impact of H. contortus infection on the ovine metabolome by identifying and quantifying the serum metabolome of infected ewe lambs (IN-ND, n = 15) from baseline (day − 1) to 57 dpi (Supplementary Table S4). Between day − 1 to 57, longitudinal t-test analysis of the infected group revealed significant (p < 0.05) alterations across several metabolite classes (Supplementary Table S2). Student’s t-test results of early pre-patent sub-clinical infection response (day − 1 vs. day 7) showed eight significantly altered metabolites, primarily involving short-chain acylcarnitines (C2, C3, C7DC). The most significant (p < 0.05) pronounced alterations occurred between day − 1 versus day 28 (19 metabolites) and between day 7 versus day 21 (14 metabolites). The period from 21 to 28 dpi showed 11 substantially (p < 0.05) altered metabolites (including citrulline, alpha-ketoglutaric acid, and several lysophosphatidylcholines) coinciding with the transition from pre-patent to patent phase of sub-clinical infection. Lysophosphatidylcholines emerged as the most consistently altered metabolite class, with lysoPC a C18:0 significantly changed in 5 of 9 temporal comparisons. In late (patent) stages of sub-clinical infection (day 42 vs. day 57), polyamines (serotonin, spermidine) showed significant (p < 0.05) alterations.

Metabolite concentration patterns in the IN-ND group were dominated by beta-hydroxybutyric acid across all timepoints, with concentrations declining from day − 1 (525.2 ± 304.9 μM) to 57 dpi (271.3 ± 144.6 μM) (Supplementary Table S4). Acylcarnitines such as C7DC (range 0.02–0.03 μM) exhibited the lowest concentration across all time points. The complete list of 70 metabolites with their concentrations is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

In both comparison approaches (CT vs. IN and CT-ND vs. IN-ND), the number of significant (p < 0.05) metabolites identified via t-test increased from 7 to 28 dpi, with a clear peak observed at 28 dpi in both approaches (Table 2). The CT versus IN comparison revealed more significant metabolites (p < 0.05) than the CT-ND versus IN-ND comparison at most timepoints. The CT versus IN t-test analysis showed 5, 15, and 20 significant metabolites at 7, 21, and 28 dpi, respectively, while the CT-ND versus IN-ND t-test results showed 10, 12, and 13 metabolites at these same timepoints. This trend continued post-drench (35, 42, 57 dpi), where CT versus IN analysis maintained a higher or comparable count of metabolites (Table 2).

Volcano plot analysis of CT-ND versus IN-ND identified PC aa C32:2, asymmetric-dimethylarginine, and PC ae C40:6 as significant (p < 0.05) on 35 dpi, and serotonin on 57 dpi. All univariate and multivariate statistical analyses comparing CT versus IN at 28 dpi yielded the most significant (p < 0.05) results in this study. Multivariate analysis confirmed the significance of the PLS-DA model for the CT versus IN group at 28 dpi, as validated by permutation testing (n = 1000; p < 0.05). Five metabolites (PC ae C40:6, Serotonin, Kynurenine, trans-Hydroxyproline, and IAA) showed the highest statistical significance (Q < 0.05), with PC ae C40:6, Serotonin, and Kynurenine additionally demonstrating the highest VIP scores (> 2). The top 15 differential metabolites between CT versus IN with VIP scores greater than 1 identified via PLS-DA multivariate analysis at 28 dpi are shown in Fig. 1.

Among the metabolites found to be significant (p < 0.05) in the t-test, several appeared frequently in one or both comparison groups (CT vs. IN and CT-ND vs. IN-ND) as shown in Table 2. Across all timepoints (7–57 dpi), LysoPC a C14:0, PC ae C40:6, and C2 (acetylcarnitine) each appeared at four out of six timepoints. SM(OH) C14:1 also appeared at four timepoints, exclusively in the CT versus IN group, with increased prominence during the later phase (28–57 dpi). Four metabolites, including trans-hydroxyproline (7, 28, 57 dpi), IAA (7, 21, 28 dpi), kynurenine (7, 21, 28 dpi), and lysoPC a C16:0 (28, 35, 42 dpi), were consistently detected at three distinct post-inoculation timepoints. Specifically, in the pre-drench phase, IAA and kynurenine were the most consistently detected metabolites (on 7, 21, 28 dpi). Metabolites such as C4 (butyrylcarnitine) and beta-hydroxybutyric acid were consistently detected on 7 and 21 dpi, citrulline and trans-hydroxyproline on 7 and 28 dpi, and C2 (acetylcarnitine), PC ae C40:6, PC aa C32:2, and LysoPC a C14:0 on 21 and 28 dpi.

The t-test analysis across experimental groups (CT-D vs. CT-ND, IN-D vs. IN-ND, and CT-D vs. IN-D) during the pre-drench and post-drench phases identified 4–9 significantly altered metabolites per time point (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S5). The t-test analysis of the post-drench phase revealed 5 to 15 metabolites per timepoint that appeared significantly (p < 0.05) only after drenching (Supplementary Table S5). Furthermore, each treatment comparison displayed distinct metabolite signatures in the post-drench phase, with specific compounds unique to individual comparisons and not detected in the other group comparisons (Supplementary Table S5).

The sequential self-comparison analysis of IN-D and CT-D groups revealed distinct recovery trajectories in the post-drench phase, as summarized in Supplementary Table 1 (concentrations), Supplementary Table 2 (t-test results), and Supplementary Table 3 (PLS-DA results). Discussion

This study provides several key insights into sub-clinical H. contortus infection in sheep and the metabolic candidate biomarkers that have the potential to predict the disease earlier, as compared to traditional detection methods. Our parasitological findings validated a controlled H. contortus sub-clinical infection model with confirmed FEC detection starting in IN animals at 21 dpi, aligning with well-established H. contortus pre-patent period5. The current study captured both pre-patent (18–21 dpi) and patent stages (> 21 dpi) of the H. contortus sub-clinical infection. The negative impacts of this sub-clinical infection on health and production parameters are reported in our companion study31. The IN group exhibited significantly (p < 0.5) reduced RBC counts from 21 to 57 dpi, decreased BW, and a tendency (p = 0.08) of lower average daily gain compared to the CT group. Anthelmintic efficacy was also confirmed by significantly (p < 0.05) improved RBC values in the IN-D group as compared to IN-ND after 35 dpi31.

The substantial individual FEC variation observed in IN (IN-D and IN-ND) animals reflects natural host-parasite interaction heterogeneity32, influenced by host genetics, immune responses, and microenvironmental factors affecting parasite establishment2. The infection trajectory (peaking at 28 dpi before naturally declining in IN-ND animals) demonstrates the development of partial host immunity against H. contortus22, consistent with documented host protective immune responses that develop over time4. The use of an anthelmintic-susceptible H. contortus strain resulted in complete parasite clearance in the IN-D group by 35 dpi, as confirmed by zero FEC33,34. Thus, provided a distinct advantage, enabling clear phenotypic differentiation between the pre-drench and post-drench phases.

Throughout the 57-day trial, significant temporal changes in 25 distinct metabolites out of 70 in healthy ewe lambs highlighted the normal physiological progression of the serum metabolome in growing lambs. The identification of the metabolic profile of healthy ewe lambs on a longitudinal scale was also crucial for the precise interpretation of infection-induced changes and for addressing a significant research gap by providing normal reference values for these metabolites in the ovine serum metabolome35. The variation in the healthy sheep metabolome from day − 1 to day 57 of the trial can be attributed to several possible physiological parameters, such as growth. Sheep with similar breed genetics but differing growth rates have shown differential metabolic profiles36. Moreover, the normal metabolite profiles are also influenced by physiological status, age, breed, and environmental conditions37, with recent studies confirming phosphatidylcholines and amino acids as indicators of sheep reproductive function38. Therefore, our findings highlight the importance of identifying context-specific (healthy and infected) metabolic changes.

Our study is a pioneer in reporting serum metabolomics kinetics during a longitudinal H. contortus sub-clinical infection, spanning six timepoints over a 57-day period. In the sequential analysis of the IN-ND group, substantial changes observed between day 7 versus day 21 timepoints coincided with L3 maturation stages5,15. The highest significant metabolic shifts occurred between day − 1 versus day 28, as 28 dpi coincided with the peak FEC. These huge metabolic perturbations at pre-patent and patent stages happened because both the developing L3 and adult worms damage the abomasal mucosa while secreting immunomodulatory molecules39, triggering a significant metabolic response22 and overall leads to reduced feed intake and altered digestion process40. Specifically, the changes in lysophosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylcholines indicate abomasal epithelial membrane damage41, alongside their role in repair mechanisms42. Altered metabolites related to the tryptophan pathway suggest disrupted gut microbiome function43–45.

Potential biological role of metabolic pathways and candidate biomarkers

The pre-patent phase of sub-clinical infection (7 dpi) primarily affects arginine biosynthesis, which is associated with immune responses and oxidative stress through nitric oxide pathways46–48, potentially exacerbating blood loss by damaging erythrocyte membranes49. At the peak FEC of the patent phase (28 dpi), metabolic alterations were also increased. The altered tryptophan metabolism suggested host immunomodulation via the kynurenine pathway50, concurrent with pronounced hematological changes (lower packed cell volume, RBC counts, and hemoglobin) in the IN compared to the CT group31.

Candidate biomarker panels showed progressively improved predictive power for differentiating IN from CT animals. The 7 dpi predictive panel was considered relevant despite slightly falling below our AUC ≥ 0.70 threshold due to its significance for detecting pre-patent sub-clinical infection. We assume that the higher parasite burdens in field conditions might yield stronger metabolic signals during the pre-patent period at 7 dpi. Future validation studies should examine other pre-patent timepoints (e.g., 10 or 14 dpi) to improve the predictive biomarker panel strength. The 21 dpi predictive panel captured the critical transition point when FEC detection begins, while the 28 dpi predictive model identified peak metabolic disruption during established sub-clinical infection.

Indole acetic acid levels were significantly higher in CT animals on 7 dpi compared to IN animals, while by 28 dpi, IN animals showed higher levels than CT animals. As a prominent candidate biomarker on early timepoints, IAA provides insights into host-parasite-microbiota metabolic crosstalk during parasite infections51. Derived from tryptophan metabolism by gut microbiota52, IAA indicates altered gut microbiome activity in response to larval and adult H. contortus in the abomasum43–45. This aligns with evidence that GI tract parasite infections disrupt the GI microbiome even in early larval developmental stages53,54. Indole acetic acid may help the host to combat the pathogens as it possesses antimicrobial properties55, modulates intestinal permeability56, and activates mucosal protection via IL-22 transcription57,58.

Lysophosphatidylcholines (LysoPC a C18:0 and LysoPC a C14:0) were significantly lower in the IN group compared to the CT group on 7 dpi and 21 dpi, respectively. These metabolites are key in cell signalling and epithelial membrane remodelling41. Similar membrane lipid alterations occur in other parasitic infections, like Fasciola hepatica (liver parasite) in sheep23. The importance of these metabolites stems from the H. contortus-induced damage to the abomasal epithelium and capillaries during penetration and blood feeding15. LysoPC a C14:0 reduction, likely reflects increased phospholipid metabolism and membrane restructuring during parasite activities in the abomasum, consistent with the findings of Silva-Neto et al.59. Our findings also align with those of Yichoy et al.60, who observed similar LysoPC C14:0 reductions in Giardia lamblia infections.

PC aa C38:0 and PC ae C40:6 (ether phospholipid) were significantly elevated in the IN group compared to the CT group on 21 dpi and 28 dpi, respectively. These phosphatidylcholines maintain cell membrane integrity61 and homeostasis during metabolic stress62,63. Their elevation suggests compensatory mechanisms during parasite damage and potential anti-inflammatory responses42,63. These findings align with altered phospholipids in sheep during GIN infection22 and in lentivirus-infected goats64, correlating with abomasal epithelial disruption in H. contortus pathophysiology. Ether phospholipids enhance epithelial membrane fluidity and protect against oxidative stress65,66, potentially defending against tissue and hematological damage characteristic of H. contortus infection49. Similar patterns of increased PC ae C40:6 were observed in Huntington’s disease ewe lambs67. Trans-hydroxyproline’s higher levels in the IN group compared to the CT group at 28 dpi suggest that H. contortus affects collagen in both the abomasal basement membrane and deeper connective tissues68,69. Trans-hydroxyproline and arginine metabolites modulate the host’s immune responses46–48.

The significant metabolites and their associated disrupted pathways in the pre-drench phase of our study primarily provide the knowledge foundation for developing innovative diagnostic tools, while also suggesting potential therapeutic research directions. Previous studies demonstrate that targeted supplementation with arginine, histidine, and cysteine enhances immunity against GIN infection70. Our identification of the disrupted arginine-citrulline axis at 7 dpi and compromised butanoate pathway at 21 dpi reveals specific metabolic signatures associated with sub-clinical infection stages. While rumen-protected arginine or citrulline71 and sodium butyrate72 have shown benefits in supporting host resistance; however, we cannot simply assume that supplementing these candidate metabolites will directly restore specific disrupted pathways during H. contortus infection. Such therapeutic applications require dedicated validation studies beyond the scope of this candidate biomarker discovery work. The systemic nature of affected metabolites and metabolic pathways demonstrates complex interactions between parasite damage, host defence, and overall compensatory adaptations25,73. Further validation studies are needed to confirm the direct causal relationships between candidate metabolites and the pathophysiology of H. contortus in sheep. Future research combining targeted metabolic pathway analysis with abomasal histopathology could enhance the diagnostic and therapeutic potential of these candidate biomarkers under field conditions.

Post-drench phase

The metabolic response to deworming in healthy animals revealed unexpected complexities that might have important implications for routine deworming practices in healthy livestock. The CT-D versus CT-ND group demonstrated significant drug-induced metabolic perturbations by a dramatic shift from a maximum of 6 altered metabolites pre-drench to 16 at the post-drench phase. The post-drench phase introduced several new metabolites, indicating that anthelmintics trigger extensive metabolic pathways in healthy animals. Specifically, significant phospholipid changes suggest direct drug interactions with cellular membranes, potentially affecting membrane fluidity and cellular signalling cascades74. The methylmalonic acid elevation specifically at 35 and 42 dpi provides evidence of temporary disruption of vitamin B12-dependent pathways as a direct consequence of anthelmintic exposure in healthy animals75. The emergence of multiple lysophosphatidylcholines (lysoPC a C20:3, lysoPC a C24:0) during the post-drench period, particularly at 42 dpi, may suggest that these compounds are part of the host’s adaptive response to xenobiotic stress76. These distinct metabolic signatures provide valuable insights for refining deworming protocols to minimize unnecessary deworming to avoid physiological disruption in healthy animals.

In contrast, the metabolic response to anthelmintic treatment in infected sheep followed distinct trajectories by affecting a few metabolites that reflect targeted recovery mechanisms. Two key metabolites (acetyl-ornithine and kynurenine) appeared consistently elevated in the IN-D group compared to the IN-ND group. They were absent in CT-D versus CT-ND analysis, revealing recovery mechanisms specific to previously infected animals. Acetyl-ornithine may play a role in nitrogen metabolism and tissue regeneration, potentially helpful for the abomasum internal membranes and functional recovery75,77, similar to its role in enhancing immunity in other stress conditions78,79. Kynurenine may contribute to ongoing immune system regulation with established anti-inflammatory properties50,80, suggesting continued healing processes despite the absence of active parasites in the animal body.

Spermidine was significantly lower in IN-D compared to IN-ND at 42 dpi, likely reflecting ongoing restoration of polyamine homeostasis following parasite elimination in IN-D animals. Polyamine pathways are critical in both parasite survival mechanisms and host inflammatory responses during parasitic infections81. Other metabolites, such as alpha-ketoglutaric acid, may contribute to energy metabolism and detoxification82, and IAA in gut microbiome restoration43–45.

Potential biological role of candidate biomarkers for post-drench assessment

Particularly revealing were the metabolic differences between CT-D and IN-D animals, which show that prior parasitic sub-clinical infection leaves lasting metabolic fingerprints after FEC clearance. Serotonin consistently differentiated between the CT-D and IN-D groups, aligning with Castello et al.83 finding of elevated plasma serotonin in Toxoplasma gondii-infected sheep, suggesting ongoing immune modulation during the recovery phase. Additionally, the presence of alpha-ketoglutaric acid indicated long-term energy metabolism adjustments, as it functions as a key Krebs cycle intermediate and indirect energy donor during catabolic stress recovery82.

The 35 dpi predictive panel showed a statistical tendency. The 35 dpi panel metabolites (methionine-sulfoxide and SM(OH) C14:1) were significantly lower in IN-D animals as compared to CT-D. Their presence suggests ongoing oxidative stress and abomasal membrane remodelling processes59,84,85. By 42 dpi, methionine sulfoxide alone showed strong discriminatory power with significantly lower levels in the IN-D group compared to the CT-D group. Methionine sulfoxide is an oxidized form of methionine, which is essential for maintaining methionine levels in ruminants86. Methionine exhibits more potent anti-inflammatory properties than its oxidized forms87. Lower methionine-sulfoxide levels suggest increased conversion via reductase activity88, likely enhancing anti-inflammatory response against residual effects of sub-clinical infection. In this case, the oxidative stress persists longer than the initial insult49. This is also because the delayed restoration of metabolic homeostasis is due to the extended recovery of abomasal structural and functional integrity. Machado et al.49 demonstrated that H. contortus infection creates a significant oxidative imbalance, characterized by increased stress markers and histologically confirmed abomasal damage, supporting our finding that oxidative processes remain active well after immediate parasite clearance. This aligns with previous research’s findings, showing that oxidative stress impacts small ruminants through multiple pathways, including altered metabolism and disrupted metabolic signalling89.

The 57 dpi predictive panel, combining SM(OH) C14:1 and C4 (butyrylcarnitine), indicates that even after an extended recovery time, previously infected animals maintain some distinct metabolic signatures. Both SM(OH) C14:1 and C4 levels were lower in the IN-D group compared to the CT-D group. Butyrylcarnitine serves as a key indicator of altered fatty acid metabolism and energy utilization patterns90, particularly during metabolic stress conditions in small ruminants91. The post-drench phase candidate biomarker-based predictive models, after validation, have the potential to help detect sub-clinical infection recovery in treated animals with normal FEC but lingering metabolic issues, identifying those needing further care. This is relatable to field conditions, where animals remain in low production after successful deworming, indicating that they require more nutritional support than just deworming.

Future studies should include H. contortus-resistant strains and extend the trial to the clinical stages of infection. After that, deworming should be done, and then the production parameters should be measured. Such an approach can effectively identify post-drench metabolic markers that distinguish animals experiencing reduced productivity despite a decrease in FEC in the post-drench phase. Extra nutritional supplements for such immunocompromised animals can increase their production.

Economic advantages of the metabolomics approach

Parasitic infections significantly impact sheep farming profitability; for example, the European sheep industry’s annual losses due to parasitic infection are around €1.8 billion92. At the individual-animal scale, lambs bearing > 1000 EPG lose ≈1 0.7 kg of saleable meat93. Current diagnostic tests have major limitations; for instance, the FEC analysis (costing $27.00 CAD/sample in British Columbia, Canada) fails to detect pre-patent stages of infection94, which leads to blind treatments that accelerate anthelmintic resistance. Anthelmintic resistance can cause several economic losses, as documented in several studies showing that losses of 9 kg in live weight gain, 4.7 kg in carcass weight, and 10–14% in carcass value95,96. Furthermore, the treatment costs (including labour 24%, veterinary services 2%, supplies 10% of production cost) exceed annual budget allocations of $7.0/head for veterinary care and $17.30/ewe for medications97,98.

Other existing diagnostic alternatives also underperform, especially for on-farm applications. The FAMACHA© system shows variable accuracy99,100, while PCR methods require sophisticated laboratory processing20,101, and current ELISA tests exhibit cross-reactivity issues102. Crucially, current methods lack the capability for rapid, field-applicable detection during the pre-patent stage or sub-clinical infections when intervention could be most beneficial. In contrast, our metabolite candidate biomarkers, after validation, could enable sub-clinical infection diagnosis approximately 14 days before traditional FEC detection, thereby allowing targeted selective treatment (TST) rather than blanket medication101,103,104. Specifically, candidate biomarkers such as IAA and trans-hydroxyproline can be detected using existing ELISA-based kits. Once modified and validated for sheep, these kits could be manufactured at scale for approximately $5–15 CAD per sample.

From a parasite-control standpoint, the primary value of an early-warning blood test lies in its ability to drive a truly TST programme instead of whole-flock treatment. By identifying infected lambs during pre-patent or early patent sub-clinical infection stages, producers can drench only those individuals that are genuinely required. Therefore, deliberately leaving the remainder of the healthy animals untreated to act as a refugia (unexposed to treatment) of drug-susceptible genotypes105. Fewer, earlier drenches are more effective during low infection intensity and high host immunity stages, which will be an effective means of controlling GIN. Computer-based modelling in Merino ewes showed that leaving just 10% of a flock untreated is sufficient to significantly delay the onset of drug resistance, albeit with only a minor loss of worm control106. In practical terms, this means more years of useful life for the available anthelmintic drugs and a marked reduction in the environmental footprint associated with anthelmintic residues. Therefore, we predict that such metabolomics-guided TST would reduce annual drench usage and slow the deterministic rise of drug-resistant alleles over time.

Metabolomics-based, cost-effective, farm-based predictive diagnostic tools could have significant economic implications for the worldwide sheep industry. For Alberta’s 100,000-head sheep industry, with annual parasite control costs of CAD $638,000 (Ivomec®: $4.80/animal; Safe-Guard®: $1.58/animal), implementing metabolomics-based diagnostics could reduce anthelmintic use by 50%. This estimate is based on the principle that early detection enables timely, targeted deworming of only affected animals, resulting in lower overall drug usage. Additionally, early control will reduce parasite transmission within the flock, minimizing the need for whole-flock treatments. This strategic approach would result in approximately $319,000 in annual savings. Moreover, this early intervention approach could shorten time to market by 17 days96 and potentially generate up to $2.1 million CAD in additional revenue.

Future directions

This study successfully identified three distinct serum candidate biomarker panels for the detection of pre-patent (7 dpi), transition to patency (21 dpi), and peak patent sub-clinical infection (28 dpi). Post-drench predictive biomarkers also effectively distinguished previously infected animals with parasites from healthy animals. While these initial results are promising, further validation is necessary before field-deployable point-of-care tests can be developed for detecting sub-clinical infections.

Candidate biomarkers validation should be performed across different sheep breeds, commercial production environments, and varying intensities of infection. It is also necessary to inoculate different H. contortus isolates that are both susceptible and resistant to the major classes of anthelmintics. This is particularly important because resistant parasites can induce different metabolic responses in the host. Future studies should also investigate correlations between certain metabolite levels and FEC intensity using an approach similar to that employed by Shaw et al.107, where sheep with high anti-CarLA IgA concentrations (> 1.0 U/ml) have 20–30% lower FEC. These kinds of correlations could enable metabolite-based selection of resilient animals, complementing genetic selection programs. If successfully validated and translated to farm settings, these metabolite-based diagnostic tools could significantly reduce anthelmintic dependence, slow the development of resistance, and improve global sheep health and productivity.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and animal management

All experimental procedures (Animal Use Protocol #064) were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee, Olds College of Agriculture and Technology, Alberta, Canada, and the animals were cared for in accordance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care108. Moreover, the study was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines for the reporting of animal experiments.

Sixty Rideau Arcott ewe lambs were sourced from a commercial sheep ranch near Olds, Alberta (T0C 2N0) and transported 140 km to Olds College of Agriculture & Technology (T4H 1E5) at 4 to 5 months of age. All animals were randomly selected from this single source to ensure genetic and management consistency across experimental groups. Following an acclimatization period, the current study commenced when animals reached 6 to 7 months old (44.6 ± 6.08 kg BW), and at the end of the study, all animals were confirmed to be in good health and free from drug residues. Prior to initiating the current 57-day trial, all animals were individually checked for their health status through analyses of the fecal and blood samples. Evaluating fecal samples for FEC showed no strongyle (H. contortus) eggs, and the complete blood count test results were within normal ranges. This further confirmed that all ewe lambs were parasite-free and healthy prior to the start of the trial.

On day − 1 (one day prior to inoculation procedure), animals were randomly assigned to two equal treatment groups, each with two subgroups, following a 2 × 2 factorial design: 1) Control, healthy ewe-lambs (CT, n = 30) that did not receive any H. contortus but were given a sham solution containing only distilled water and 2) Inoculated ewe-lambs (IN, n = 30) which received 5000 third-stage H. contortus larvae in a distilled water solution on day 0 (experiment initiation). Each treatment group was further divided into two subgroups, with 15 animals per subgroup: a) drenched ewe lambs (D), which received ivermectin and fenbendazole treatment on day 28, and b) non-drenched ewe lambs (ND), which received a sham solution. Therefore, the treatment groups were 1.a.) Control-Drenched (CT-D), 1.b.) Control-Non-Drenched (CT-ND), 2.a.) Inoculated-Drenched (IN-D), and 2.b.) Inoculated-Non-Drenched (IN-ND), 15 animals per treatment group. The detailed experimental timeline and sampling details are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

This study was conducted longitudinally over a 57-day period. Baseline sampling (blood, fecal and body weight [BW]) was performed on day − 1 (one day prior to experiment initiation), followed by parasite inoculation on day 0. Blood and fecal samples were collected at seven timepoints, including − 1, 7, 21, 28, 35, and 42, as well as on 56 (fecal samples) and 57 (blood sampling) days post-inoculation (dpi). At each timepoint, blood was drawn prior to fecal collection. Animal health status was monitored throughout the study, and blood samples were also analyzed for complete blood cell count to monitor hematological parameters as indicators of infection, while BW was recorded at each sampling timepoint to assess growth performance impacts.

On day 0, all animals were inoculated with their assigned treatment. The two infected groups (IN-D and IN-ND) were orally inoculated with 5,000 third-stage H. contortus larvae (L3), while the CT groups (CT-ND and CT-D) received an equivalent volume of sham. For the parasite inoculation, the larval solution was continuously stirred in a beaker on the sheep handling site. Using a 5-mL BD syringe, 2-mL of larvae solution (for IN groups) or sham (for CT groups) was administered orally, followed by 2-mL distilled water rinse to ensure complete ingestion. The anthelmintic-susceptible strain of H. contortus L3 was provided by Dr. Adriano F. Vatta’s laboratory (Louisiana State University, USA). This strain lacks drug resistance, ensuring effective impact of anthelmintic drugs with standard treatment and clear assessment of post-deworming metabolic changes.

No other procedure (except blood and fecal sample collections) was done on the animals until 28 dpi, when the drenched groups (CT-D and IN-D) received an oral inoculation of 0.8 mg/mL of ivermectin (Ivomec® Boehringer Ingelheim Animal Health Canada Inc., Ontario, Canada) at 2.5 mL/10 kg of BW and 100 mg/mL of fenbendazole (Safe-Guard®, Merck & Co., Inc., New Jersey, USA) at 2.3 mL/45 kg of BW. The dose rate was calculated based on the labelling of the drugs, veterinary approval, and average BW taken on 21 and 28 dpi. Similarly, the non-drenched groups (CT-ND and IN-ND) received an equivalent volume of sham (distilled water), calculated using the same formula as the drug dose calculation. On 35 dpi, all sixty animals received toltrazuril (Baycox®, Elanco Canada Limited, Ontario, Canada) at a dosage of 1 mL/2.5 kg of BW to address high coccidia counts detected during routine FEC analyses. This treatment specifically targeted coccidia and had no impact on H. contortus or the experimental outcomes, as toltrazuril is ineffective against GIN. All 60 animals were individually restrained while receiving their assigned treatments, with standardized handling procedures across all groups to minimize stress and variation.

To prevent cross-contamination, the four groups were housed in separate outdoor feedlot pens with no interaction between them throughout the trial. Each pen was equipped with wind protection, sun shelter, and wood shavings for bedding. All lambs had ad libitum access to water and feed. The diet consisted of a total mixed ration based on whole barley and a protein supplement (4:1 ratio), with wheat straw bales freely available in all pens. The mixed grain ration provided 16% crude protein and 4 Mcal/kg digestible energy (on a dry matter basis), with a total digestible nutrients value of 82%, supplemented with wheat straw to ensure adequate fibre intake.

Sample collection and analytical methods

Blood sampling and processing

Blood samples were collected at the Olds College sheep handling area by jugular venipuncture using 10-mL vacutainer tubes without anticoagulant. Blood samples remained at room temperature on site for approximately 30 min before being processed in batches of 16 animals in the centrifuge (2000 RPM, 4 °C) to collect the supernatant serum in 2-mL Eppendorf tubes. Eppendorf tubes were immediately placed in an insulated box with dry ice and then transferred to a − 20 °C freezer. Upon trial completion, 420 serum samples (60 animals, 7 samples each) were packaged in an insulated container with sufficient dry ice and transported to The Metabolomics Innovation Centre (TMIC) at the University of Alberta (approximately a two-hour drive), where they were received intact and stored at -80 °C until further analysis.

Fecal sampling and parasitological analysis

Immediately after blood collection, each time fecal samples (~ 10 g) were directly taken from the rectum of each sheep using disposable gloves, and the gloves were transferred to labelled Ziplock bags. Samples were stored in a cooler with ice packs and transported to a refrigerator (4 °C) on the same day of data collection. Within 7 days after collection, FEC analysis was performed using the modified McMaster technique109, mixing 2 g feces with 28-mL in-house prepared saturated magnesium sulphate solution. Haemonchus contortus eggs were identified by their morphological characteristics, such as shape and size. Total egg counts from both chambers of the McMaster slide were multiplied by 50 to determine eggs per gram (EPG) of feces109. The EPG value is a quantitative estimated measure of parasite burden and is a key parameter for assessing infection severity and treatment efficacy. Overall, the FEC method is considered the gold standard for detecting clinical parasitic infection in commercial farming and is also recognized for its cost-effectiveness.

Parasite species identification was performed through nemabiome sequencing by Dr. John Gilleard’s laboratory (University of Calgary). Strategically, we selected samples from 42 and 56 dpi from the IN-ND group (n = 15) as these represented optimal timepoints with established chronic infection and sufficient egg counts for comprehensive analysis. All 15 samples were homogenized for both timepoints, and two representative samples were taken. These two pooled samples were shipped in air-evacuated Ziplock bags at room temperature. As per Avramenko et al.110 protocols, larvae were cultured, harvested, and fixed in 90% ethanol, and deep sequencing analysis was performed110.

Metabolomics analysis

The serum samples were analyzed using DI/LC–MS/MS custom assay to identify and quantify up to 110 endogenous metabolites, including amino acids, acylcarnitines, biogenic amines, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and sugars111. In short, the analysis involved sample preparation, derivatization, extraction, and selective mass-spectrometric detection using multiple reaction monitoring pairs. Isotope-labeled internal standards were used for metabolite quantification. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using an ABSciex 4000 Qtrap® tandem mass spectrometry instrument (Applied Biosystems/MDS Analytical Technologies, Foster City, CA) equipped with an Agilent 1260 series Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA). The raw data generated by the mass spectrometer, coupled with the UHPLC, were processed and analyzed using Analyst 1.6.2 software. The processed data, which contained the identified metabolites and their corresponding concentrations, were used for further statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Analysis strategy

A total of 87 statistical comparisons across the metabolomics dataset were conducted. The analysis strategy was structured into three main categories. First, blood metabolome kinetics were examined by conducting 36 within-group comparisons to track longitudinal changes across CT-ND, CT-D, IN-ND, IN-D, CT, and IN. These included biologically relevant pairings in pre-drench phase such as dpi (− 1 vs. 7), (− 1 vs. 21), (− 1 vs. 28), (7 vs. 21), (7 vs. 28), (21 vs. 28), and post-drench phase intervals (35 vs. 42), (35 vs. 57), (42 vs. 57). Second, 30 between-group comparisons were performed at six timepoints (days 7 to 57) to evaluate inoculation with H. contortus and anthelmintic drenching effects. These included comparisons such as CT versus IN, CT-ND versus IN-ND, IN-D versus IN-ND, CT-D versus CT-ND, and CT-D versus IN-D. The third focus was candidate biomarker discovery, for which a total of 21 biomarker analyses were conducted to assess the diagnostic performance of selected metabolites across key treatment group comparisons and timepoints. These included CT versus IN and CT-ND versus IN-ND at days 7 to 57 to track infection-related metabolic changes over time. Post-drench candidate biomarkers evaluation included IN-D versus IN-ND, CT-D versus CT-ND, and CT-D versus IN-D at dpi 35, 42, and 57.

To develop mathematical predictive models, CT versus IN comparisons at 7, 21, and 28 dpi were prioritized, as these timepoints revealed the highest number of significantly altered metabolites and the strongest diagnostic potential. For CT-D versus IN-D, predictive model development was prioritized at 35, 42, and 57 dpi to distinguish previously infected but now parasite-free animals from healthy controls.

Analyzing the parasitology data

Statistical analysis was conducted on the FEC data using R version 4.3.3 (R Core Team, 2024). Due to the non-normal distribution of FEC data112, non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to assess differences between experimental groups, with statistical significance set as p < 0.05. The main goal of FEC analysis was to validate the experimental design by confirming both the establishment of infection in the IN-D and IN-ND groups and the validation of parasite-free status in the CT-D and CT-ND groups. In the post-drench phase, the FEC analysis was meant to monitor FEC reduction in the IN-D group (drenching efficacy) and the continuing presence of eggs in the IN-ND group.

Analyzing the metabolomic data

Metabolomic datasets were pre-processed and normalized using standard methods available via MetaboAnalyst 6.0113,114. From the 110 identified metabolites, 40 metabolites with > 20% missing values were excluded from metabolomics analysis. Of the 70 retained metabolites, 14 had partial missingness (0% < missing ≤ 20%) while 56 had complete data. For the partially missing metabolites, values were imputed using the default approach (limit of detection). Across the 87 statistical comparisons (comprising within-group kinetics, between-group effects, and candidate biomarker analyses), the number of imputed values ranged from 1 to 12 per comparison (1.67%-20% of the sample size). Univariate and multivariate statistical analyses, including fold change, Student’s t-test, volcano plot analysis, and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were conducted on processed data. The PLS-DA significance was verified using permutation testing (n = 1000, significance at p < 0.05). The PLS-DA analyses that were significant (p < 0.05) were also evaluated for the top 15 metabolites based on their Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores. Metabolites with VIP > 1 had the most significant contribution to separating the comparison groups.

For candidate biomarkers, significant (p < 0.05) metabolites identified through univariate and multivariate analyses, along with those reported in the literature as having potential roles in infections, were manually selected and evaluated using the biomarker module of MetaboAnalyst 6. Logistic regression with tenfold cross-validation was applied to various subsets of normalized and scaled metabolite concentration values to develop predictive mathematical equations. The biomarker analysis generated Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated Area Under the Curve (AUC) values with 95% confidence intervals and computed confidence bands. Predictive accuracy was assessed as the primary performance metric using permutation testing (n = 1000, p < 0.05). Finally, individual or combined metabolite predictive equations demonstrating high AUC values (≥ 0.70), and significant PLS-DA–based predictive accuracy were identified as candidate biomarkers. This candidate biomarker selection approach aligns with the recommended biomarker discovery framework described by Xia et al.115, and with recent methodologies employed in sheep metabolomics biomarker studies116,117.

Pathway and enrichment analyses were performed for significant and candidate metabolites to identify their significant impact on metabolic pathways. The threshold for statistical significance reported in this study is a p < 0.05 and a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (or Q) < 0.05 unless otherwise mentioned. Also, a 0.05 ≤ p < 0.10 is referred to as a tendency, while differences with a p > 0.10 are not significant.
