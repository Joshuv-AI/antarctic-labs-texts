Contrast-Enhanced Mammography: Advances, Challenges, and Case-Based Insights
Authors: Noam Nissan, Janice S Sung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Contrast-enhanced mammography (CEM) has increasingly been established as a valuable tool in breast imaging that enhances lesion detection and characterization by combining functional and anatomical information. This review highlights the recent key advances in CEM technology, explores its expanding clinical applications, and discusses the common interpretation pitfalls and current limitations. Instead of offering a comprehensive overview, this review focuses on providing a case-based perspective on emerging applications and how CEM can be efficiently incorporated into clinical practice. Through illustrative case examples, we offer practical insights into optimizing breast imaging strategies and demonstrate how CEM can effectively complement other imaging modalities in both routine practice and complex diagnostic scenarios.

INTRODUCTION

Digital mammography (DM) remains the foundation of breast imaging and plays a critical role in breast cancer screening and diagnostic evaluations. However, its limited sensitivity in women with dense breast tissue highlights the need for supplemental screening modalities [1]. The integration of tomosynthesis, adjunct ultrasound (US), and especially magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been shown to increase cancer detection rate (CDR) [234]. Recent studies further support the added value of supplemental screening MRI, particularly for women with extremely dense breasts, in which breast cancer is frequently DM-occult [5], and to a lesser extent for those with heterogeneously dense fibroglandular tissue [6], highlighting the need for accessible and cost-effective contrast-enhanced imaging options for breast cancer screening [7].

Over the past decade, contrast-enhanced mammography (CEM) has emerged as a promising alternative to breast MRI [8], offering comparable performance [9], lower cost [10], and potentially greater availability [11]. CEM employs a dual-energy technique that exploits the differential attenuation properties of breast tissue and iodine, producing low-energy images that are comparable to DM for anatomic assessment and recombined images that reveal areas of contrast enhancement [12]. From a technical standpoint, CEM is performed on standard DM units equipped with contrast injection capabilities and dedicated image recombination software. For each breast, both low-energy and high-energy images are acquired almost simultaneously, typically beginning approximately two minutes after contrast injection. Imaging is performed in conventional craniocaudal (CC) and mediolateral oblique (MLO) views, with the option of obtaining an additional delayed view, often a lateral projection, if needed for further evaluation [13]. The main limitations of CEM include the risk of allergic reactions to iodinated contrast agents and the increased radiation dose [14].

Instead of offering a comprehensive overview, this review focuses on providing a practical case-based perspective on emerging applications and how CEM can be efficiently incorporated into clinical practice. This review explores the current landscape of CEM and highlights the recent advancements and ongoing limitations in clinical practice. It also offers case-based insights supporting the integration and effective use of CEM in routine clinical settings.

Advances

Improved Cancer Detection in Screening of Women With Dense Breasts

The primary advantage of CEM over DM lies in its higher CDR, particularly in the setting of screening women with heterogeneously or extremely dense breasts, resulting in incremental CDR ranging between 6.5 to 23.9 more cancers per 1,000 cases [1516171819]. This benefit has also been demonstrated even when DM is combined with adjunct US [20] relative to MRI [21]. Furthermore, MRI and CEM are successful in detecting three times as many invasive cancers as US, even when cancers are half the size [22]. Improving early diagnosis in this population remains a critical goal, as dense breast tissue not only reduces the sensitivity of conventional mammography but also increases the risk of developing breast cancer [23]. Lesions detected by CEM exclusively on recombined images are typically invasive carcinomas at the T1 stage with negative lymph nodes, representing clinically significant cancers identified at an early and potentially more treatable stage. Robust Negative Predictive Value of Mammographic Findings

CEM studies with low levels of background parenchymal enhancement (BPE) offer distinct diagnostic advantages by providing a high negative predictive value for ruling out non-enhancing asymmetries seen on low-energy images, as illustrated in Figure 2. This is especially valuable in older patients without a prior available mammogram, in which the rate of detected asymmetries can reach up to 10% [24]. Furthermore, the absence of enhancement in architectural distortion on CEM is highly indicative of the absence of an underlying malignancy [25]. However, it should be stressed that low-energy images should still be read separately [26], since the lack of enhancement does not preclude malignancy especially when presenting as microcalcifications, or rarely as non-enhancing masses (See Non or Low-Enhancing Cancers).

Alternative to Breast MRI When Contra-Indicated

CEM has become a valuable alternative to breast MRI in clinical situations where MRI is typically indicated, such as high-risk screening or preoperative staging, but cannot be performed because of contraindications such as severe claustrophobia, non-MRI-compatible implants, or allergy to gadolinium-based contrast agents [27]. CEM Guided Biopsy

In the first few years following the introduction of CEM, enhanced findings with no correlation between targeted US and low-energy images were further evaluated using MRI and MRI-guided biopsies. CEM-guided biopsy capability is now available and showing promising results [282930313233] thereby enabling direct sampling of findings on the modality in which they were originally detected.

Characterization With an Additional Delayed Phase

In addition to the contrast enhancement capabilities that CEM and MRI share, breast MRI has the advantage of multiple pulse sequences, such as T2 weighted images [34] and diffusion-weighted imaging [35], both valuable in lesion characterization, and particularly in differentiating benign from malignant lesions. In addition, dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) breast MRI allows for further characterization of wash-in and washout dynamics, providing lesion characterization, as benign breast lesions usually exhibit persistent enhancement while malignant lesions tend to exhibit washout [36]. Likewise, CEM can also be acquired in a dynamic fashion, with the addition of a delayed image [37] together with the early images, enabling the assessment of wash-in and washout patterns [38] and potentially providing lesion characterization, as demonstrated in Figure 4. Nevertheless, this remains to be further studied.

Challenges

Background Parenchymal Enhancement

BPE denotes the normal signal uptake of fibroglandular tissue in contrast-enhanced breast imaging modalities such as MRI and CEM [39]. On MRI, BPE is known to create a diagnostic challenge in the differential diagnosis between non-mass enhancement (NME) and BPE, often leading to excessive diagnostic workup [4041]. However, BPE usually differs from pathological enhancement in its kinetics and dynamics, typically characterized by symmetric enhancement [42], a slower slope in the early phase, as demonstrated on ultrafast sequences [43], and persistent enhancement [4445]. In both modalities, BPE is known to be hormonally regulated [4647] and increases in premenopausal women [48], during lactation [49], with hormonal replacement therapy [50], and intrauterine devices [51]. However, several technical aspects differ in the appearance of BPE on CEM owing to differences in technique. In CEM, despite the injection being performed two minutes prior to imaging and compression, BPE levels have been linked to the compression force [52]. In addition, CEM images in the two conventional views are acquired for each breast separately, unlike in MRI, where both breasts are imaged simultaneously. CEM also lacks the multiparametric characterization of MRI, especially in the ultrafast and DCE parametric maps, which could potentially create a diagnostic challenge between BPE and NME [5354] (Fig. 5) [55], especially in low-conspicuity lesions such as low-grade tumors and invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) [56]. CEM also does not utilize the early ultrafast stages to increase tumor conspicuity compared to high levels of BPE [5758] owing to differences in kinetics between BPE and breast neoplasms. Altogether, recent evidence suggests the possibility of diminished diagnostic accuracy for CEM in high (moderate and marked) BPE cases [59], as well as difficulty in determining the extent of ductal carcinoma in situ presenting as NME when surrounded by high BPE [60]. Fortunately, these account for a minority of cases, as high BPE accounted for only 16% of cases [59] and low BPE levels accounted for up to 76% of both CEM and MRI [61].

False-Positive Enhancement of Benign Pathologies

In addition to malignant processes and physiological BPE, various benign and borderline breast lesions also exhibit enhanced CEM [62]. Simple breast cysts typically show negative enhancement on recombined images with thin rim enhancement, termed the “eclipse” or “crescent” sign [63], but inflammatory cysts may exhibit thicker rim enhancement [64], mimicking suspicious rim-enhancing malignant masses. Fibrocystic changes can appear as regions of NME, sometimes prompting biopsy when they are asymmetric. Fibroadenomas, often seen as circumscribed masses, may show variable enhancement on CEM depending on their internal composition. Intramammary lymph nodes are usually identifiable on low-energy images and show variable enhancement. US usually confirms their benign nature. Solitary intraductal papillomas can have nonspecific imaging features and often require biopsy for a definitive diagnosis [65]. Pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia may appear as parenchymal asymmetry or as a mass with variable enhancement. Although rare, phyllodes tumors can resemble fibroadenomas and generally appear as enhanced circumscribed masses requiring further evaluation via biopsy [66]. These examples highlight that CEM alone may not be able to differentiate benign lesions from malignant or high-risk lesions, resulting in false-positive biopsy recommendations.

Common Artifacts

Artifacts are frequently encountered in CEM, but rarely hinder image interpretation [67]. The commonly encountered artifacts in CEM include rim (also known as halo) artifacts [68], patient motion artifacts (ripples), axillary line (pectoral) artifacts, breast implant artifacts, corrugation artifacts, cloudy fat artifacts, hair artifacts, misregistration artifacts, contrast artifacts (retention and contamination), skin artifacts (skin-line enhancement), and skin lesions [69]. The rim artifact was reported to be almost universally present, appearing in 97%–99% of all projections, followed by the ripple artifact, which tended to be more prevalent in oblique views (80%–82%), and correlated with increased breast thickness. The axillary line artifact (exclusively on oblique projections, 63%) and skin-line enhancement were observed in 19%–46% of projections [67]. The new dual-energy subtraction algorithm appears to be effective for solving most CEM-related artifacts without compromising lesion conspicuity [71].

Non or Low-Enhancing Cancers

Despite the high negative predictive value of CEM when recombined images are negative [72], low-energy images should still be assessed independently [26] as non-enhancing breast cancers can occur, similar to false-negative cases on breast MRI [737475]. This most commonly involves cancers presenting as calcifications that may not have a corresponding enhancement. Certain malignancies and ILC remain a diagnostic challenge because of their faint enhancement compared with invasive ductal carcinoma [5676], as illustrated in Figure 7. More rarely, other breast cancer subtypes, including papillary [63] and mucinous [77] carcinomas, may also be undetected on reconstructed images owing to minimal vascularity.

Imaging of Silicone Implants

Silicone implants are used for cosmetic augmentation in healthy individuals and for reconstruction following mastectomy. Imaging of patients with silicone implants typically aims to assess both the breast tissue and implant integrity. Although the use of CEM in patients undergoing silicone augmentation has recently been shown to be feasible [78], several limitations remain. First, standard imaging protocols for silicone implants include additional “implant-displaced” views alongside conventional mammographic projections. In this context, CEM protocols have focused on acquiring dual-energy images only in pushback views, resulting in limited contrast-enhanced coverage of the peripheral breast tissue. Additionally, CEM involves an increased radiation dose, particularly when combined with tomosynthesis [7980]. Given that CEM alone contributes to an estimated 30% increase in radiation exposure compared to standard mammography [14], this is a noteworthy concern, especially considering the cumulative effects of ionizing radiation. Moreover, unlike MRI, CEM cannot be used for surveillance or diagnostic evaluation in patients with unilateral or bilateral mastectomies when insufficient residual tissue is available for implant displacement [8182]. Finally, MRI remains the most sensitive modality for assessing implant integrity, a feature not provided by CEM.

Summary

By combining standard mammographic imaging with iodinated contrast administration, CEM improves the visualization of tumor vascularity and enhances lesion detection, particularly in dense breast tissue. Its dual capability enables confident exclusion of non-enhancing findings observed on conventional mammography, with growing evidence supporting its role in lesion characterization, direct sampling, and as an alternative to MRI when the latter is contraindicated. Multiple studies have shown that CEM offers sensitivity comparable to that of breast MRI for cancer detection while providing shorter examination times, lower costs, and greater accessibility [11], thus suggesting the potential for expanded use of this evolving technique. However, several challenges remain to be resolved. Physiological or benign enhancement, CEM-specific artifacts, and the rare occurrence of non-enhancing malignancies can complicate the interpretation. Despite its promise, several practical considerations must be addressed before CEM can be fully integrated into routine practice. These include a radiation dose approximately 30% higher than that of DM [14], risk of adverse reactions to iodinated contrast (reported at a pooled rate of 0.82%) [83], and current reimbursement limitations [84].
