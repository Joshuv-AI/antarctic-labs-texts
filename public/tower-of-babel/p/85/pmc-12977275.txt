Engineering glyphosate tolerance in sugarcane through overexpression of a mutated version of its native EPSPS gene
Authors: Pâmela Ingrid Alves, Talita Heleodoro, Bruno Spinassé Floreste, Daniel Moreira Neris, João Pedro Silvério Pena Bento, Bradley J Smith, Fernanda Mendes de Rezende, Vitor Pinoti, Paulo Cézar de Lucca, Jovanderson Jackson Barbosa da Silva, Marcelo Falsarella Carazzolle, Gonçalo Amarante Guimarães Pereira, Paulo Mazzafera, Juliana Lischka Sampaio Mayer, Marcos Silveira Buckeridge, Marcelo Menossi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSugarcane is a globally important crop, widely used in both the food industry and biofuel production. Weed infestations can significantly reduce its productivity, and to address this challenge, herbicides such as glyphosate are commonly applied. Glyphosate acts as a structural analog of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), inhibiting the enzyme 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS), thereby disrupting the shikimate pathway and impairing the biosynthesis of essential aromatic amino acids required for plant growth and development. However, the limited tolerance of sugarcane to glyphosate restricts herbicide application to doses that are often insufficient for effective weed control. Previous studies have shown that amino acid substitutions in the EPSPS enzyme, particularly the T102I/P106A (TIPA) mutations, can confer glyphosate resistance in plants. In this study, we evaluated whether a specific mutation in the endogenous ScEPSPS gene could enhance glyphosate tolerance in sugarcane without compromising morphological, physiological, and biometric parameters evaluated under greenhouse conditions. The mutated gene was constitutively overexpressed in transgenic sugarcane plants, which survived glyphosate concentrations as high as 5% (v/v of the commercial formulation, 106.5 mM of glyphosate acid equivalent). Molecular analyses revealed approximately 25- to 80-fold overexpression of the transgene in resistant lines compared with non-transgenic controls. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) quantification showed elevated levels of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan 14 days after herbicide application in plants overexpressing the mutated ScEPSPS. Although the mutation affected certain histological and physiological parameters, biometric assessments indicated that the overall phenotype remained largely unchanged. The successful overexpression of the mutated endogenous ScEPSPS gene represents a promising biotechnological strategy to enhance glyphosate tolerance in sugarcane without compromising plant growth and development under greenhouse conditions.KEYWORDS: Herbicide tolerance, Saccharum, site-directed mutation

Introduction

Sugarcane is used both in the food industry and in biofuel production.1 However, factors such as weed infestations are well documented and widely recognized. Weeds can lead to a significant decline in sugarcane productivity, ranging from 20% to 30%,2 and in some scenarios yield losses are estimated to reach up to 82%, depending on the weed species involved.3 To address this issue, herbicides are employed for control, and glyphosate (N-(phosphonomethyl)glycine) is among the most used, due to its properties of a nonselective, systemic, and cost-effective herbicide.4,5 Glyphosate acts as a structural analog of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) that inhibits the activity of 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS). EPSPS first binds shikimate-3-phosphate (S3P), after which glyphosate competes with PEP for the binding site. This leads to the formation of a stable ternary complex (EPSPS–S3P – glyphosate), which blocks the synthesis of aromatic amino acids such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan, ultimately causing plant death.6,7 However, despite its high efficacy, the use of herbicides in agriculture can interfere with essential physiological processes in cultivated plants, resulting in reduced crop yields.5 Therefore, alternative strategies to enhance crop productivity and ensure food security include developing plants that are resistant to herbicides.8

It is well established that substitution of specific amino acids within the EPSPS gene can reduce plant sensitivity to glyphosate.4,9 In this context, double mutations at amino acids T102I/P106S (TIPS) and T102I/P106A (TIPA) have conferred resistance to glyphosate in various plant species, including Oryza sativa (rice),10 Linum usitatissimum (flax),11 and Arabidopsis thaliana.12 Although the TIPS mutation has been used to produce commercial transgenic maize,13 it often compromises the catalytic efficiency of the EPSPS enzyme, leading to a reduction in overall plant fitness.9,14–16 In contrast, the TIPA mutation demonstrates superior performance.16 Kinetic analysis of the maize EPSPS enzyme revealed that the TIPA variant provides exceptional resistance to glyphosate, with an inhibition constant (Ki) approximately 297-fold higher than the wild-type (WT) enzyme and 2.6-fold higher than the well-known TIPS mutant. Remarkably, this resistance was achieved without the typical fitness cost associated with such mutations. In fact, the TIPA variant exhibited an enhanced affinity for its native substrate, phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), with a Michaelis–Menten constant (Km) 2.7 times lower than that of the WT enzyme. This combination of high-level herbicide resistance and preserved catalytic efficiency makes the TIPA mutation an effective target for developing glyphosate-tolerant crops. Supporting this, Hummel et al.4 used gene editing and observed that plants harboring the TIPA substitutions exhibited vigorous growth without discernible fitness penalties. Consequently, exploring the potential of the TIPA mutation to induce glyphosate resistance in sugarcane represents a promising avenue for commercial application.

The use of transgenic techniques has been a consistent strategy to leverage plant breeding, aiming to develop cultivars better suited to human needs, with emphasis on increasing yield and supporting the profitability of the sugar-energy industry.17,18 Currently, the first glyphosate-resistant sugarcane variety is in the final stages of regulatory approval in Brazil. However, this cultivar is generated using a gene of exogenous origin, Agrobacterium sp., which confers glyphosate resistance, alongside two bioinsecticidal proteins derived from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt).19 It is well known that plants transformed with genes from the same species (either cisgenics or intragenics), have wider consumer acceptance.20,21 Furthermore, regulatory bodies like the European Union have acknowledged that intragenic plants are regarded as safe as plants produced by conventional breeding.22 Therefore, developing sugarcane with resistance to glyphosate via mutations in the endogenous genes holds clear economic potential.

As a first step toward producing cisgenic or intragenic glyphosate-resistant sugarcane, this study evaluated the impact of the TIPA mutations within the native sugarcane EPSPS gene. Tolerance assays demonstrated that overexpression of the mutated EPSPS, under the control of the maize ubiquitin promoter, allowed plants to withstand glyphosate concentrations up to 5% (v/v of a commercial formulation). These results underscore the substantial potential of this specific mutation. Furthermore, our findings confirmed that although the mutation affected certain histological and physiological parameters, biometric analyses indicated that the overall phenotype remained largely unchanged. This highlights both the viability of the approach and the need for long-term field studies. Thus, the strategy presented here offers a promising path for generating glyphosate-resistant sugarcane plants without incorporating exogenous DNA and may also facilitate future large-scale field evaluations.

Materials and Methods

In silico Analysis

The ScEPSPS protein structure was generated using the SWISS-MODEL homology modeling platform. The wild-type maize EPSPS (PDB ID: 8umj) served as the reference for both the wild-type ScEPSPS and the mutated ScEPSPSm. Protein – substrate docking was performed with SWISS-DOCK using the AutoDock Vina method, employing the two modeled ScEPSPS structures with glyphosate and PEP. In UCSF ChimeraX (version 1.9), we conducted cavity detection analyses (pyKVFinder) on the wild-type and mutant EPSPS proteins, along with docking visualization images. Protein alignment and visualization were carried out in Jalview (version 2.11.4.1) utilizing the integrated ClustalO tool, based on the homology models generated by SWISS-MODEL.

Production of Transgenic Sugarcane Plants Overexpressing a Mutated ScEPSPS

The complete sequence of the ScEPSPS sugarcane gene was found in the Saccharum database (http://bioinfo03.ibi.unicamp.br/spontaneum/.), which contains unigene data23 Nascimento et al. 2019]. Based on the wild type coding region of the ScEPSPS gene (NCBI accession PX102043), a mutated containing the TIPA mutations (NCBI accession PX283572) was chemically synthesized (Genone, Brazil) and cloned in the pGVG vector,24 producing the pGVG-EPSPS(TIPA) construct. This vector used the pUbi-1 promoter and the NOS terminator to direct transgene expression.

Agrobacterium-transformed cultures were sent to PangeiaBiotech (Campinas, Brazil) to produce transgenic sugarcane plants (cultivar SP80-3280), as described in Guidelli et al.24 The transgenic events were maintained on hormone-free medium (4.33 g/L MS salts, 1 mL/L MS vitamins, 25 g/L sucrose, 7 g/L agar, 200 mg/mL timentin, and 40 mg/L geneticin) to induce growth and rooting. The transgenic events were acclimatized in a greenhouse. As a control, three WT seedlings were used, obtained from calli that underwent the entire in vitro regeneration process like the transgenic events, except for the absence of the antibiotic.

Evaluation of Plant Tolerance to Glyphosate

Following the acclimation period, the plantlets were maintained in trays measuring 55.9 cm in length, 28.5 cm in width, and 8 cm in height, with a maximum capacity of 50 seedlings per tray. The plants were challenged with different glyphosate concentrations (0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2%, or 2.13 to 42.6 mM of glyphosate acid equivalent). These concentrations were established in an increasing order, starting from the minimum dose expected to cause mortality in the control plants. Negative control events were treated with tap water. A total of 100 mL of solution was sprayed per tray. Foliar application was performed uniformly until runoff was observed on the leaves. Twenty days after application, the plants were evaluated based on their phenotypic characteristics, following a classification system outlined in Table 1.

The event exhibiting the highest tolerance to glyphosate was replanted and cultivated in a 20 L pot. Additionally, three events classified as susceptible that stood out the most were selected for cultivation. All of these were used for subsequent analyses. To better assess the tolerance of the most resistant plant, five clones were produced using the micropropagation technique (pre-budded cuttings). After acclimatization in 3 L pots, these plants were treated with glyphosate concentrations of 2%, 3%, and 5% (42.6, 60, and 106.5 mM of glyphosate acid equivalent). Applications were performed uniformly, with a total volume of 25 mL per plant.26 Phenotypic evaluations were conducted 20 days post-application and documented through photography.

RNA Extraction

Total RNA was extracted from the leaves of five selected transgenic events exhibiting the highest tolerance to glyphosate. RNA isolation was performed using the phenol-chloroform extraction method.27 To remove contaminating genomic DNA, the RNA samples were treated with DNase I, Amplification Grade (D5307), according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Sigma-Aldrich). First-strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently carried out using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Quantitative Realtime PCR (qRTPCR)

The expression of the ScEPSPS gene was evaluated through RT-qPCR using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Reactions were performed following the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers forward (5’ TACAAGTCCCCCGGAAATGC 3’) and reverse (5’ TCATCTCCAGTACCTCCGCA 3’) were used to amplify both wild-type and transgenic plants. The polyubiquitin gene (NCBI accession CA179923.1)28 served as the normalization control for the reactions. Reactions were conducted on an Applied Biosystems 7500 real-time PCR thermocycler under the following conditions: 50°C for 2 minutes, 95°C for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 1 minute. Expression data were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method29 with three technical replicates and three biological replicates for the wild-type plants. Five independent events were evaluated for ScEPSPS gene expression.

High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) Analysis

Samples of event 26 and wild plants were collected prior to herbicide application. Subsequently, a 2% glyphosate solution was applied to the leaves of the third branch of each plant, which were then marked for identification. Fourteen days after application, the plant material was harvested, dried in a rotary evaporator for 36 hours, and then prepared for analysis. The extracts were prepared following de Souza et al.30 and diluted in 200 µL of ultrapure water.

Then, samples were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 minutes at 4°C to pellet particulate matter. Subsequently, 45 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a new tube containing 5 µL of 1% formic acid, resulting in a final formic acid concentration of 0.1%. Calibration curves for tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine were constructed over a concentration range of 500 to 50,000 ng/mL using amino acid standards (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Mass spectrometric transitions and voltages were selected based on the protocol established by Anesi et al..31 For each amino acid, one quantitative and one qualitative transition were monitored.31 Aliquots of 1 µL of each extract were injected onto a NanoEase 5 µm XBridge BEH130 C18 column (300 µm × 50 mm; Waters Corporation, USA) using a nano ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters Corporation, USA), with online elution directly coupled to a triple quadrupole TQD mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, USA) operating in positive electrospray ionization mode (ESI+). The capillary and cone voltages were set at 3.2 kV and 40 V, respectively. The chromatographic program consisted of an isocratic hold at 85% solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) for 15 minutes, followed by a column wash with 80% solvent B (acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid) for 10 minutes, and re-equilibration with 85% solvent A for 10 minutes prior to the next injection. Retention time windows were established based on standards and validated with sample extracts. The total run time per injection was 30 minutes. Total ion chromatogram (TIC) peak areas were quantified using MassLynx software (Waters Corporation, USA), employing automatic peak integration and baseline noise correction.

Evaluation of Agronomic Characteristics of the Resistant Plant

To assess whether mutations introduced into the ScEPSPS gene induce physiological, morphological, and biometric changes, 10 clones of the classified resistant event and 10 wild-type plants were cultivated. All plants were maintained in a greenhouse under identical environmental conditions for 7 months. Following this growth period, the analyses described below were performed.

Physiological Assessments

Gas exchange measurements and chlorophyll fluorescence analyses were conducted in 7-months-old plants not treated with glyphosate, including net photosynthetic rate (A), stomatal conductance (gs), intracellular CO2 concentration (Ci), transpiration rate (E), maximum quantum efficiency of PSII in light-adapted state (Fv’/Fm’), PSII quantum yield, and electron transport rate (ETR). These measurements were obtained using a portable gas exchange system (Li-6400 XT, Li-Cor, USA) following the best practices advised by.32 Measurements were taken from the central part of the leaf (+1 position) between 10:00 am and 1:00 pm. During measurements, saturating light intensity was maintained at 2000 μmol·m−2 ·s−1, with temperature set to 28°C, and CO2 concentration held constant at 400 μmol.mols−1.

Quantification of Sucrose Content in Sugarcane Juice

The brix degree was quantified from 1-year-old plants grown in greenhouse. The plants were cut and the juice extracted tightening the mature culm with pliers direct to refractometer (HI 96,801, Hanna Instruments Ltd) sensor. The data are shown in brix degree, each degree corresponds approximately 1% of sucrose in a solution (w/w).

Biometric Evaluation

The plant phenotype was assessed based on the following parameters: plant height (PH), measured in centimeters using a measuring tape from the base of the apex of the stem; stem diameter (SD) and stem length (SL), both measured in centimeters with a digital caliper. Fresh (SBF) and dry biomass (SBD) of the stem and leaf (LBF and LBD), respectively) were recorded in grams.

Histological Analyses

Samples from the middle region of the third stem internode were fixed in FAA 50 solution for 48 hours, subjected to vacuum infiltration to remove air from the tissues, and subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series until 70% ethanol for storage. For optical microscopy analysis, each sample was hand-sectioned using a steel blade. The sections were treated with Mäule reagent a histochemical stain for lignin detection. The sections were mounted on slides with coverslips for imaging using an Olympus B×51 microscope equipped with an Olympus DP71 digital camera.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Biometric and physiological analyses were performed using an unpaired t-test, while HPLC results were analyzed by two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. All statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

In silico Analysis of the Wild-Type and Mutated EPSPS Proteins

The alignment of EPSPS sequences from rice and wheat, and several sugarcane isoforms revealed that the residues Thr (169) and Pro (173) are highly conserved across different species (Figure 1(a)). The in silico docking analysis between the native ScEPSPS and the ligand PEP revealed that they interact through 33 hydrogen bonds, whereas glyphosate interaction involves 28 hydrogen bonds. In the mutant TIPA protein, PEP interacts with the same number of amino acids as in the WT protein, including Ile 169. In contrast, glyphosate interaction with the TIPA mutant involved a different set of residues, forming 30 hydrogen bonds, but notably, it still interacts with the mutated Ile 169 residue (see Supplementary Table S1). The docking models confirmed that PEP and glyphosate were positioned within the ScEPSPS cavity corresponding to the catalytic site. Interestingly, in both the WT and mutant proteins, PEP and glyphosate form hydrogen bonds with the amino acid at position 169 (Thr/Ile), without any interaction with the amino acid at position 173. The altered binding configuration of glyphosate with the mutated ScEPSPS is highlighted in (Figure 1(b, f)), showing a distinct spatial orientation compared to its binding in the WT protein.

Transgenic Sugarcane Plants Overexpressing the Mutant ScEPSPS Variant

The coding region with the mutated ScEPSPS gene was cloned into the pGVG expression vector, under the control of the maize Ubi-1 promoter and the CaMV 35S terminator (Figure 2(a)). A total of 124 putative transgenic events were obtained using calli from the sugarcane cultivar SP80-3280, following the protocol previously described.24 To identify glyphosate-tolerant lines, these events were subjected to a sequential screening assay with increasing concentrations of the herbicide (Table 2). Initially, 54 of the putative transgenic events were sprayed with a 0.1% glyphosate solution. The surviving plants from this initial screen were then subjected to a 0.5% glyphosate application, from which 48 events survived and were classified as tolerant. Subsequently, these 48 surviving lines, along with 70 additional putative transgenic events, were tested against a 1% glyphosate concentration. From this pool of 118 plants, 87 events survived and were then advanced to the final and most stringent screening with 2% glyphosate. At this concentration, a clear distinction in tolerance was observed: 69% of the plants (60 events) were classified as susceptible (showing significant damage but surviving), while 30% (26 events) were nonresistant (died after treatment). Notably, a single event was classified as highly resistant, as it displayed no visible phenotypic damage after exposure to this high dose of glyphosate.

As shown in Figure 2, most transgenic events were highly resistant to the commercial herbicide dosage (1%). Unlike the control plants, they showed no visible symptoms, retaining their green leaves and intact meristems.

To better assess the resistance of event 26, multiple clones were generated by vegetative reproduction from internode cuttings and exposed to a range of glyphosate concentrations. The resulting clones tolerated glyphosate up to a 5% concentration, remaining green and symptom-free (Figure 3(a)). This outcome contrasts sharply with the control plants (WT), which began showing signs of mortality by day 4, even at the lowest dose of 2%. These phenotypic evaluations confirm that the TIPA mutation confers high-level glyphosate resistance in sugarcane.

Interestingly, event 26, which exhibited the strongest resistance, had a lower transcript level than event 60, although it was still a robust 55-fold higher than the control.

Aromatic Amino Acid Levels are Upregulated in Event 26 After Glyphosate Treatment

Since the EPSPS enzyme catalyzes the synthesis of essential aromatic amino acids, we quantified the levels of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan in transgenic plants, before and after a 2% glyphosate application. Prior to the herbicide treatment, amino acid levels in transgenic plants were comparable to the WT plants. No changes in the amino acid profiles were observed in plants exposed to glyphosate for 1 and 3 days (data not shown). However, 14 days after application, event 26 exhibited a significant accumulation of all three amino acids relative to the WT (Figure 4). Interestingly, the WT plants in our experiment exhibited only a minor reduction in amino acids. This finding is in contrast to the sharper decreases (24–45% at 14 DPA) reported in sugarcane by6 One possible explanation for this difference is the variation in application protocols, as the spray volume used in the previous study may have delivered a greater quantity of the active ingredient, as well as the developmental stage of the plants.

The most dramatic increase was observed in phenylalanine, which was approximately 10-fold higher than in the WT and 7-fold higher than its own baseline at day 0 (Figure 4(a)). Tryptophan and tyrosine levels in event 26 also increased significantly, rising by 1.7-fold and 1.6-fold, respectively, compared to their pre-treatment levels (Figure 4(b,c)). These findings suggest that in event 26, glyphosate stress triggers a compensatory upregulation of the shikimate pathway, leading to an overproduction of these amino acids.

Physiological and Photosynthetic Performance

To evaluate the physiological effects of the overexpression of the mutant ScEPSPS, gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence analyses were performed in plants grown under greenhouse conditions. Under these controlled conditions, most physiological parameters did not show significant differences between transgenic and wild-type plants (Figure 5). However, a slight reduction was observed in the net photosynthetic rate, the effective quantum yield of photosystem II, and the electron transport rate in transgenic plants. These findings were corroborated by chlorophyll fluorescence analyses, which also indicated a modest decrease in the quantum efficiency of photosystem II and in the electron transport rate.

Histological Analysis and Lignin Composition

Histological analysis in 7-month-old plants confirmed the presence lignified cells in various tissues (epidermis, parenchyma, pericyclic fibers, and vessel elements of the vascular bundles) both WT (Figure 7(a,c)) and transgenic plants (Figure 7(d,f)). However, a primary difference was observed in the lignin composition. In WT plants, the lignified secondary cell walls stained yellow-orange, suggesting a predominance of syringyl (S). In contrast, transgenic plants exhibited intense red staining in both peripheral and central regions of the parenchyma cells, as well as the pericyclic fibers of the vascular bundles adjacent to the epidermis, which is indicative of higher concentration of guaiacyl (G) lignin.

Discussion

One of the primary challenges for agricultural producers is managing the weeds competition, which hinders crop growth and reduces productivity. To address this, transgenic technology has enabled significant advances in crop improvement.33 Site-directed mutagenesis is one of the main approaches used to introduce structural modifications in the EPSPS enzyme, conferring glyphosate resistance to plants.34

The use of a mutated endogenous gene represents a cisgenic strategy that may offer biological and regulatory advantages over conventional transgenic approaches, as it relies on native genetic elements and precise sequence modifications, potentially reducing unintended effects and regulatory constraints.35 However, such target-site modifications can alter the binding affinity for the substrate PEP due to conformational changes, diminishing the enzyme’s catalytic efficiency and consequently compromising plant fitness.14

Funke et al.36 and Yu et al.9 reported that rice plants engineered with the TIPS mutation, as well as goosegrass (Eleusine indica) with spontaneous TIPS mutations, exhibited reduced fitness in the absence of herbicide. This effect was likely attributable to the decreased catalytic efficiency of the mutated enzyme without compensatory upregulation of its expression. Similar observations were made in Arabidopsis thaliana harboring the TIPA mutation, which displayed a fitness penalty associated with a reduced Vmax, and consequently lower enzyme activity per mole.12

Nonetheless, the TIPA mutation confers more effective glyphosate resistance than the TIPS mutation. Although both variants maintain a similar affinity for the substrate (comparable Km values), the TIPA enzyme shows reduced sensitivity to the inhibitor, as indicated by its higher Ki value.16 Furthermore, Hummel et al.4 observed that cassava plants containing double amino acid substitutions analogous to the TIPA mutation sustained vigorous growth without apparent fitness costs. This finding raises questions about whether the TIPA mutations are inherently detrimental to plant fitness.

Docking analyses revealed that the catalytic site involving amino acid 169 (Thr169) engages with both the substrate PEP and the glyphosate molecule in wild-type and mutant EPSPS proteins. In contrast, proline173 (pro173), although proximal to the active site, does not appear to interact within these ligands.

According to Sammons et al.12 the T169I mutation reduces the enzyme’s affinity for glyphosate, thereby contributing to increased resistance. In that study, comparison of the catalytic activity of the wild-type (WT) protein and the glyphosate-resistant variant (TIPA) confirmed that Arabidopsis plants carrying the TIPA mutation are approximately 100-fold less sensitive to glyphosate than non-modified plants. However, this mutation imposes a significant functional cost, as it compromises the enzyme’s catalytic efficiency in PEP recognition, as widely reported in the literature.36–38 Sammons et al.12 further emphasized that the T102I mutation alone is catalytically detrimental, causing a strong reduction in selectivity for PEP (Km = 233 μM) and, simultaneously, an even greater decrease in affinity for glyphosate (Ki = 148.6 μM).

Based on our results, we propose that, analogous to the TIPS mutation, the two mutations present in the TIPA variant may act in a combinatorial manner, partially restoring selectivity for PEP while maintaining reduced affinity for glyphosate. However, this synergistic interaction could not be confirmed with the available data12,39

The mutations introduced into the ScEPSPS gene altered the three-dimensional structure of the enzyme, particularly in the herbicide binding region. These structural changes probably reduce glyphosate´s affinity for the enzyme, hindering its inhibitory action. Such modifications may result from altered hydrogen bonding or electrostatic charges, ultimately impairing the herbicide’s ability to inhibit the enzyme. Consequently, the plant can continue synthesizing essential amino acids even in the presence of glyphosate, leading to resistance. This mechanism aligns with findings from Achary et al.,14 who reported that mutations at the TIPS of amino acids caused protrusion of the methyl group A at the glyphosate´s binding site, preventing the herbicide from docking effectively and thus conferring protection to the plant.37

Furthermore, our HPLC data show that the event 26 lineage exhibited increased production of phenylalanine (Phe), tryptophan (Trp) and tyrosine (Tyr) in the presence of glyphosate. This metabolic response is consistent with observations in other glyphosate-resistant species. For instance, Fernández-Escalada et al.40 demonstrated that treatment with glyphosate of Amaranthus palmeri plants overexpressing the EPSPS gene resulted in a dose-dependent accumulation of transcripts for nearly all enzymes of the shikimate pathway. Also, rice seeds overexpressing a rice EPSPS gene containing the TIPS mutations had higher levels of Phe and Trp.14 This findings align with our data, which show a significant increase in aromatic amino acids following glyphosate application.

We observed that the quantification of amino acids before and after glyphosate application in wild-type plants did not show significant changes. This result is expected, since glyphosate inhibits the EPSPS enzyme, blocking the synthesis of new amino acids, but does not degrade those that were already produced prior to application.41 Consequently, the pool of free amino acids remains relatively stable during the early stages following treatment. These findings reinforce that plant death does not occur exclusively as a result of reduced amino acid production.42

Tolerance assays demonstrated that the transgenic plants exhibited resistance to glyphosate concentrations up to 2%. Notably, the event classified as resistant (ev.26) tolerated concentrations as high as 5%, which is four times the commercial dosage. This finding is consistent with previous studies. For example, Sammons et al.12 reported that Arabidopsis thaliana seeds overexpressing a single copy of the EPSPS-TIPA gene exhibited glyphosate resistance approximately 300-fold greater than wild-type plants. Additionally, Hummel et al.4 evaluated various combinations of point mutations in the EPSPS gene of cassava plants and confirmed that the TIPA mutation was the most effective, conferring glyphosate resistance without compromising plant fitness. In this context, the TIPA mutation emerges as a promising candidate for conferring resistance in glyphosate-sensitive crops like sugarcane.

To investigate the molecular alterations underlying this resistance, we conducted gene overexpression analyses. These analyses revealed that all transgenic plants exhibited higher expression levels that wild-type plants. Remarkably, the resistant plant (ev.26), along with one from the moderately resistant group (ev.60), displayed the highest expression levels. Increased transgene copy number is a well-established mechanism for herbicide resistance.43 For instance, in Poa annua plants a 7-fold increase in EPSPS gene copy number and a L106P mutation were associated to a marked increase in glyphosate tolerance43 In our study, the resistant plant exhibited expression levels 55-fold higher than the control, which likely contributes to its elevated herbicide tolerance. However, the relative contribution of each resistance mechanism, target-site mutation versus gene overexpression, to the final phenotype remains to be fully elucidated, although a synergistic effect is probable.43

Interestingly, event 60 exhibited EPSPS gene expression levels approximately 25-fold higher than those of event 26, which, in turn, showed greater resistance to glyphosate. It is plausible that this marked increase in EPSPS copy number raises the plant’s energetic cost and nitrogen demand, potentially triggering physiological stress. According to Yan et al.7 elevated EPSPS copy numbers in cotton plants promote intensification of the flavonoid pathway, increasing metabolic expenditure and impairing photosynthetic performance. This combination of effects may limit the plant’s capacity to tolerate the herbicide, even in the presence of higher enzyme production, thereby reducing its efficiency in coping with the additional stress imposed by glyphosate application.

Additionally, the data indicate that high EPSPS gene expression in event 26, in the absence of glyphosate, does not necessarily result in increased basal levels of aromatic amino acids. This finding is consistent with the results of Fernández-Escalada et al.40 who demonstrated that EPSPS gene overexpression did not significantly affect the overall aromatic amino acid biosynthetic pathway in Amaranthus palmeri.

However, we observed that a pronounced increase in amino acid accumulation in transgenic plants becomes evident only after exposure to glyphosate. Under these conditions, a fraction of the EPSPS protein retains its normal catalytic activity, whereas excess EPSPS can bind glyphosate, thereby reducing its inhibitory effect; in contrast, wild-type EPSPS is strongly inhibited upon herbicide exposure.44 This mechanism is consistent with the fact that flux through the shikimate pathway is tightly regulated by metabolic control mechanisms and substrate availability.45

It is well established that any modification overlapping the substrate binding site of a enzyme tends to compromise plant functionality, as amino acid substitutions are difficult to introduce without negatively impacting the enzyme’s catalytic efficiency.12 In our case, docking analysis revealed that the T-I amino acid substitution overlaps with the PEP recognition site, and previous studies with plants overexpressing the EPSPS-TIPA gene have indicated a reduction in catalytic efficiency. Therefore, it is plausible that such alterations could impair plant performance. In this context, our study compared the physiological, morphological, and biometric effects of transgenic sugarcane overexpressing the EPSPS-TIPA gene with those of wild-type plants to identify possible deleterious effects.

Physiological analyses showed that most of the evaluated parameters did not differ statistically from the control. However, net photosynthetic rate, photosystem II (PSII) efficiency, and electron transport rate were reduced in the transgenic plants. These changes may be associated with a possible reduction in catalytic efficiency resulting from the mutation in the EPSPS44 gene, which may trigger compensatory biochemical adjustments, including increased metabolic flux through the shikimate pathway to ensure adequate production of aromatic amino acids.44 This additional metabolic demand may lead to an imbalance in carbon and nitrogen metabolism, which is reflected in a reduction in the photosynthetic rate.

However, phenotypic assessments indicate that the transgenic plants remain very similar to the control, without visible losses. Although some parameters showed statistical differences, the mean values were quite close, suggesting that these changes would likely not compromise plant performance under field conditions. In agreement with this observation, sucrose content analyses showed that wild-type (WT) plants did not differ from the transgenic plants, indicating that the final product of photosynthesis was not altered. These results corroborate that the introduction of the mutation did not compromise the yield of the transgenic plants.46 Parameters related to stomatal conductance, intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), effective quantum yield of PSII (Fv’/Fm’), and transpiration showed no significant differences compared to the control.

Histological analysis revealed the presence of guaiacyl (G) lignin deposition in transgenic plants. Although this modification may enhance resistance to pathogen attack and mechanical stress, a more rigid cell wall could potentially restrict plant growth by limiting cell expansion. Despite this, biometric analyses indicated that height, stem length, and fresh biomass were comparable to the control, while a slight decrease in culm diameter and dry leaf biomass of the transgenic plants. This suggests that the overall phenotype of the transgenic plants was not significantly altered, although their performance under field conditions remains to be evaluated.

Interestingly, the transgenic plants did not exhibit reduced lignin production, in contrast to findings by Yanniccari et al.47), who reported that Kochia scoparia and Lolium perenne plants overexpressing the EPSPS gene were smaller than varieties with fewer gene copies. Overexpression of endogenous EPSPS has also been shown to cause dwarfism in cotton, associated with reduced lignin metabolites and xylem alterations.7 Similarly, loss-of-function mutations in lignin biosynthesis genes such as 4CL (encoding 4-coumarate:CoA ligase) may result in reduced lignin and dwarf phenotypes.48 However, our study confirms that the TIPA mutation did not induce such effects. In line with our findings, Fang et al.49 demonstrated that Arabidopsis plants overexpressing WT EPSPS exhibited increased leaf area, height, and silique number compared to wild-type plants. Taken together, these contrasting outcomes indicate that the consequences of EPSPS overexpression on plant fitness vary among species and are likely influenced by differences in metabolic context and regulatory networks.

The use of a constitutive promoter raises the possibility that increased EPSPS abundance could contribute to glyphosate tolerance. However, evidence from multiple plant systems indicates that overexpression of the wild-type EPSPS alone is generally insufficient to confer high levels of resistance. Overexpression of a wild-type EPSPS under the CaMV 35S promoter did not result in glyphosate resistance,50 and only limited tolerance to 1% glyphosate was reported in Arabidopsis lines overexpressing a wild-type EPSPS, with plants still displaying severe toxicity symptoms51). Similarly, overexpression of a rice wild-type EPSPS failed to confer resistance, as no plants survived a 2 mM glyphosate spray,49 despite the high sequence identity (92.72%) and full conservation of the region encompassing the TIPA site between rice and sugarcane EPSPS (data not shown). In contrast, sugarcane plants overexpressing the mutated EPSPS (TIPA) described in this study showed no visible toxicity symptoms even under 5% glyphosate (equivalent to 106.5 mM glyphosate acid equivalent). Taken together, these comparisons support the conclusion that the tolerance phenotype observed in transgenic sugarcane is not explained by enzyme overproduction alone but rather results from the combined effect of the TIPA substitution and high-level expression of the mutated EPSPS in a polyploid crop context.

It should be noted that all experiments in this study were conducted under controlled greenhouse conditions, which enabled the standardization of environmental variables such as temperature, light intensity, and water availability, thereby minimizing external interference and allowing a more precise evaluation of the effects of the TIPA mutation. Nevertheless, considering that physiological and agronomic responses may be influenced by more complex environmental conditions, future field trials will be necessary to confirm the stability of glyphosate tolerance and the performance of the plants under real cultivation conditions.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that overexpression of the sugarcane EPSPS gene carrying the TIPA mutation confers strong glyphosate tolerance without significant penalties to growth or development under controlled conditions. These results indicate that the TIPA variant provides an effective balance between resistance and plant fitness, and open perspectives for the development of cisgenic sugarcane using its own gene with precise sequence modifications, thereby offering a potentially more acceptable strategy for crop improvement under current regulatory frameworks.
