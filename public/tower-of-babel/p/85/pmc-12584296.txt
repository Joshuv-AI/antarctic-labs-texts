Global, regional, and national burden of maternal hemorrhage: a systematic analysis from the global burden of disease 2021 and projections to 2030
Authors: Jianbo Wei, Yang Yi, Xiaofei Li, Yuan Xu, Liang-sheng Fan, Huayu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal hemorrhage remains the leading cause of maternal mortality worldwide, hindering progress toward Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1, which aims to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR) to less than 70 deaths per 100,000 livebirths by 2030. Comprehensive estimates of its global burden and inequalities are limited.MethodsBased on data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, we assessed maternal hemorrhage burden across 204 countries from 1990 to 2021. Indicators included incidence, maternal mortality ratio (MMR), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Temporal trends were analyzed using estimated/average annual percentage changes (EAPC/AAPC), and health inequality was quantified using the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Concentration Index (CI). The relationship between burden and Socio-demographic Index (SDI) was examined via LOESS and frontier analysis. Projections to 2030 were conducted using a Bayesian age-period-cohort model.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, the global age-standardized incidence rate decreased by 27.7% (from 495.93 to 358.57 per 100,000 population), and MMR declined by 58.2% (from 86.63 to 36.23 per 100,000 livebirths). South Asia and East Asia were the fastest decrease in ASIR (EAPC = -2.71 [-2.86 to -2.55]) and MMR (EAPC = -7.76 [-8.47 to -7.05]), respectively. In 2021, low-SDI regions bore the highest burden, with maternal mortality up to 93 times higher than in high-SDI regions. Inequality analysis showed narrowing absolute disparities (SII: -980.27 to -288.12) but widening relative inequalities (CI: -0.57 to -0.66). Adolescents (10–14 years) and women aged ≥ 35 years had elevated MMRs. Frontier analysis revealed inefficient outcomes in some high-SDI countries. By 2030, maternal hemorrhage is projected to cause approximately 13 million incident cases and 33,186 deaths.ConclusionsDespite global improvements, maternal hemorrhage persists as a major public health challenge with significant regional inequalities. Tailored strategies are urgently needed to reduce the burden in low-resource settings and accelerate progress toward SDG 3.1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08353-x.Keywords: Maternal hemorrhage, Global burden of disease, Maternal mortality, Postpartum hemorrhage, Incidence, Disability-adjusted life years

Introduction

Maternal hemorrhage, which includes antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), is the leading cause of maternal mortality worldwide [1]. As a critical and persistent global health issue, the epidemiological trends of maternal hemorrhage have undergone significant transformation over the past century. In the early 20th century, high incidence and mortality rates were attributable to restricted medical technology, the absence of antibiotics, and rudimentary obstetric care [2]. The advent of antibiotics, advancements in transfusion techniques, and the standardization of obstetric practices significantly reduced maternal hemorrhage-related mortality worldwide in the following decades [3]. The use of oxytocin analogues and prostaglandins has revolutionized the management of PPH by improving uterine tone [4, 5]. Concurrently, innovations such as tranexamic acid have offered novel options for stabilizing coagulation during acute hemorrhage [6]. Although the global burden has decreased, maternal hemorrhage is still the greatest threat to maternal mortality, accounting for 27% of maternal deaths globally [1, 7].

Disparities in maternal hemorrhage outcomes persist alarmingly. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where prenatal care, emergency services, and healthcare infrastructure are often inadequate, the maternal mortality rate (MMR) due to hemorrhage far surpasses that in high-income countries [8, 9]. Furthermore, even within high-income countries, intra-national disparities persist, as marginalized communities encounter barriers to timely care owing to poverty, racism, and geographic isolation. In the United States, for instance, black women experience significantly higher rates of severe maternal morbidity compared to white women (28.6% vs. 19.8%) [10], and they receive fewer high-levels of antihemorrhagic intervention than white women (adjusted odds ratio = 0.55[0.30–0.98]) [11]. Paradoxically, recent decades have revealed a counterintuitive trend, with high-income countries, such as the United States [12], Canada [13], and the Netherlands [14], reporting increasing incidence rates of maternal hemorrhage. This co-existence of progress and disparity underscores the critical need for comprehensive research on the current global status of maternal hemorrhage.

In 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) established Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) Target 3.1, which aims to reduce the global MMR to less than 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 livebirths by 2030 [15]. As the primary cause of maternal mortality, investigating epidemiologic trends of maternal hemorrhage is critical for quantifying the current state of the global burden and regional disparities, so as to inform strategies to mitigate maternal mortality risk. Despite this, global epidemiological systematic studies specifically addressing maternal hemorrhage are notably scarce.

Beyond traditional approaches limited by fragmented data sources and heterogeneous metrics [16–18], this study provides the first comprehensive quantification of the global burden of maternal hemorrhage through the unified analytical framework leveraging the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database. Methodologically, we implemented multidimensional statistical models recommended by the WHO and the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, such as frontier analysis, Joinpoint analysis, and health inequality analysis, to describe the trends and health inequalities in maternal hemorrhage across 204 countries and territories. Through a Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model, this study predicted the disease burden to 2030, providing guidance for the realization of SDG3.1 goals.

Methods

Data sources

The GBD 2021, serving as the primary data source for this research, is a collaborative effort involving the WHO and numerous international partners. It provides comprehensive epidemiological estimates for 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories, encompassing indicators such as incidence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and MMR [19]. Detailed descriptions of data collection, processing, and modeling methodologies are available in prior publications [19].

Specifically, the data sources for maternal hemorrhage in GBD 2021 included literature review, demographic and health surveys, hospital discharge records, and insurance claims data. The DisMod-MR 2.1 modeling tool was utilized by GBD 2021 to process raw data and estimate the incidence of specific pregnancy complications. A method termed “crosswalk” and multiple adjustment factors were developed to mitigate systematic errors in data collection and processing. The GBD cause category for maternal hemorrhage is defined by the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), encompassing codes for postpartum hemorrhage (O72), other obstetric hemorrhage (O67, O62.2), placenta previa and abruption (O44-O46), other placental disorders (O43.2), and hemorrhage in early pregnancy (O20). GBD 2021 employed Wilson’s score interval approximation to compute uncertainty intervals, addressing potential heterogeneity arising from data collection discrepancies, non-reference data, and model uncertainty.

Data extraction

To ensure accuracy, two authors independently extracted data on maternal hemorrhage from the publicly accessible GBD 2021 Results Tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). Any discrepancies between the two extractors were resolved through discussion and consensus with a third author. Annual estimates for incidence, MMR, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021 for females aged 10–54 years, stratified into 5-year age intervals, were collected. Data extraction adhered to the standard operating procedures established for GBD studies to ensure consistency and accuracy of the data.

Definition of maternal hemorrhage

Within the GBD 2021 framework, maternal hemorrhage is defined as postpartum hemorrhage (blood loss exceeding >500 mL following vaginal delivery or >1,000 mL after cesarean delivery), antepartum hemorrhage (vaginal bleeding after 20 weeks of gestation but before delivery), or hemorrhagic placental disorders, regardless of blood volume lost or the timing of the hemorrhagic event [20]. Disease classification adhered to the ICD-10, encompassing codes O72, O67, O62.2, O44-O46, O43.2 and O20. A detailed mapping of disease subcategories is presented in Table S1.

The WHO defines maternal mortality as the death of a woman during pregnancy or within one year after the termination of pregnancy, regardless of the duration and site of the pregnancy, where the cause of death is directly related to or exacerbated by the pregnancy or its management, excluding deaths from accidental or incidental causes [21]. In this study, death counts refer to the number of deaths attributed to maternal hemorrhage occurring in a population during a certain period, whereas MMR is specifically defined by WHO as the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 livebirths [22].

In this study, the SDI was selected as a covariate due to its robust ability to quantify regional socio-economic disparities. Countries and regions were stratified into five quintiles (high, high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low) for comparative analysis of the disease burden trends. The SDI is a composite indicator developed by the GBD Study to quantify a region’s socio-economic development level on a scale between 0 (lowest level) and 1 (highest level) [23]. SDI values were obtained from the GBD 2021 dataset, and standardized using z-scores to ensure comparability across regions.

To investigate nonlinear relationships between SDI and ASRs, a local regression smoothing model (LOESS) was employed to model the trend. The bandwidth of the local polynomial regression was optimized by setting the span parameter to 0.5, which ensured the smoothness of the curve while effectively controlling the risk of overfitting. In the visualizations, the LOESS fitted curves were supplemented by 95% CI bands, and model adequacy was assessed through residual distribution analysis.

Joinpoint regression modelling

To analyze temporal trends in maternal hemorrhage-related incidence, DALYs, and MMR from 1990 to 2021, we utilized the Joinpoint Regression Program. This approach was prioritized over conventional EAPC modeling because it detects discrete transition points where trend magnitudes change significantly—critical for identifying acceleration/declination phases triggered by health system reforms or surveillance enhancements. The resulting average annual percentage change (AAPC) provides a weighted average of segmented trends, offering enhanced sensitivity to nonlinear policy impacts across heterogeneous periods. This method employs a segmented linear regression approach to identify temporal shifts in trends, determining the optimal number of joinpoints, which are points where the trend slope changes. For each outcome metric - incidence, DALYs, and MMR - we calculated the annual percent change (APC) with 95% CIs to quantify the magnitude and direction of trends within distinct time segments. Furthermore, the AAPC was calculated to summarize the overall trend throughout the study period. The statistical significance of trends was assessed using the 95% CIs for both APC and AAPC estimates.

Frontier analysis

We performed a frontier analysis to evaluate the ASR of maternal hemorrhage at national and regional levels across varying sociodemographic contexts from 1990 to 2021. This analysis establishes the “optimal” performance frontier, which represents the lowest achievable disease burden for a given level of socio-economic development, as measured by SDI. We employed the free disposal hull (FDH) algorithm to construct the frontier. This algorithm identifies the lowest observed values of maternal hemorrhage metrics for each SDI level and connects these points through stepwise horizontal and vertical segments, thereby defining an efficiency boundary that encompasses all data points. For each of the 100 bootstrap iterations, the entire dataset was resampled with replacement. Country-year observations were then sorted in ascending order of SDI and descending order of value, and the lowest observed burden values were sequentially identified.

This method, while not strictly formal FDH in a linear programming sense, mirrors the FDH principle by establishing a boundary below which no feasible observations lie, thus delineating the threshold of “optimal” performance. Deviations of country-specific observed age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR), or MMR from the estimated frontier were interpreted as inefficiencies, indicating the disparity between actual outcomes and the theoretically achievable minimum under similar developmental conditions. For each data point, the efficiency difference was calculated as , which quantifies the deviation from this frontier, interpreted as the unrealized potential for outcome improvement under similar developmental constraints.

The distribution of data points for both 1990 and 2021 was visualized in relation to the frontier, with LOWESS smoothing applied to depict the overall shape of the frontier. Countries exhibiting the largest and smallest efficiency gaps in 2021 were emphasized to highlight global disparities in progress. This methodology enables the identification of regions with suboptimal performance and the analysis of temporal trends in the efficiency of burden reduction.

Health inequality analysis

To assess global inequality in the burden of maternal hemorrhage, we conducted a comprehensive health inequality analysis using two widely accepted indices: the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the Concentration Index (CI).

For SII estimation, age-standardized DALY rates among females were regressed against countries’ relative socioeconomic positions, ranked by the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) and weighted by national population size. Relative rank was calculated as the cumulative midpoint of each country’s population across 204 countries, scaled to the global population in the given year (1990 or 2021). SII was derived as the absolute difference in predicted DALY rates between the highest and lowest socioeconomic positions based on robust linear modeling. A positive SII indicates a disproportionate burden among lower-SDI countries.

For CI calculation, we first estimated country-level DALYs by multiplying crude DALY rates with national population size. Countries were then ordered by ascending SDI, and cumulative DALY and population shares were computed to construct concentration curves. The CI was calculated as twice the area between the concentration curve and the line of equality (the 45° diagonal), ranging from -1 (full concentration among the least advantaged countries) to 1 (among the most advantaged).

To quantify uncertainty in the confidence intervals, we applied nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 replicates) to generate bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals. Visualizations include population-weighted scatter plots with robust regression overlays for SII and smoothed concentration curves for confidence intervals.

Result

Global burden of maternal hemorrhage and trends from 1990 to 2021

In 2021, the global incidence and DALYs counts of maternal hemorrhage cases reached 13,960,250 (10,898,097 to 17,749,443) and 2,955,463 (2,492,505 to 3,536,900), respectively (Table 1). Compared with 1990, the global burden declined significantly. EAPC analyses demonstrated significant reductions in ASIR (EAPC = −0.83 [−0.89 to −0.77]), ASDR (EAPC = −3.97 [−4.16 to −3.79]), and MMR (EAPC = −3.17 [−3.42 to −2.91]). Specifically, the global ASIR decreased from 495.93 (383.08 to 650.37) per 100,000 population in 1990 to 358.57 (280.34 to 457.97) per 100,000 population in 2021; the ASDR declined from 253.52 (225.92 to 283.13) per 100,000 population to 75.99 (64.06 to 90.89) per 100,000 population, and deaths counts due to maternal hemorrhage fell from 114,113 (101,092 to 127,762) to 46,874 (39,076 to 56,596) over the same period. However, the MMR attributable to maternal hemorrhage remained at 36.23 (30.14 to 43.61) per 100,000 livebirths in 2021 (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Regional disparities in the burden of maternal hemorrhage

The burden of maternal hemorrhage exhibited significant variation across SDI and GBD regions. In 2021, low SDI regions experienced the highest ASIR (740.0 [577.8 to 952.1] per 100,000 population) and ASDR (298.2 [241.1 to 362.2] per 100,000 population), whereas high SDI regions had the lowest burden (Table 1, Figure S1). Regional disparities were particularly pronounced in maternal mortality, with the MMR in 2021 being 74.13(59.24 to 92.21) per 100,000 livebirths in low SDI regions, but 0.82 (0.67 to 1.04) per 100,000 livebirths in high SDI regions (Table 1).

From 1990 to 2021, the most rapid declines in MMR (EAPC = −6.64 [−7.12 to −6.15]) and DALYs (EAPC = −6.21 [−6.36 to −6.06]) occurred in high-middle SDI regions, and the greatest reduction in ASIR was observed in low-middle SDI regions (EAPC = −2.07 [−2.15 to −1.99]). Conversely, low SDI regions exhibited the slowest decline (EAPC = −2.28 [−2.42 to −2.14]) in MMR. Notably, all regions showed significant decreases in ASIR, except high-middle SDI regions (EAPC = −0.31 [−0.64 to 0.02]) (Table S2).

Among the 21 GBD regions, Sub-Saharan Africa had the highest ASIR in 2021, led by Western Sub-Saharan Africa (897.57 [693.87 to 1,151.96] per 100,000 population), Central Sub-Saharan Africa (887.59 [681.87 to 1,145.97] per 100,000 population), and Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (767.38 [593.97 to 1,003.64] per 100,000 population) (Table 1; Fig. 2A). MMR and ASDR were also highest in Western Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Sub-Saharan Africa, and Oceania. In contrast, Australasia was the region with the lowest MMR (0.19 [0.14 to 0.25] per 100,000 livebirths), while the lowest ASIR (78.03 [56.17 to 103.89] per 100,000 population) and ASDR (1.14 [0.90 to 1.47] per 100,000 population) were found in High-income Asia Pacific (Table 1; Fig. 2B-C).

National disparities in the burden of maternal hemorrhage

National-level analysis similarly revealed substantial disparities in the burden of maternal hemorrhage. In 2021, Niger recorded the highest ASIR (1534.39 [1187.46 to 1944.33] per 100,000 population), followed by Chad and Somalia. Conversely, the Republic of Korea (43.64 [29.98 to 61.45] per 100,000 population), Singapore (70.28 [64.31 to 81.13] per 100,000 population), and Greece (82.58 [67.37 to 98.63] per 100,000) reported the lowest ASIRs (Table S3, Fig. 3A). Regarding ASDR, Sierra Leone (1081.27 [641.64–1635.87.64.87] per 100,000 population), Chad (967.81 [619.84–1406.86.84.86] per 100,000 population), and Niger (648.31 [397.89–939.60] per 100,000 population) reported the heaviest burden (Table S3, Fig. 3B).

For maternal mortality, the countries with the lowest MMR attributable to maternal hemorrhage were Iceland (0.02 [0.01 to 0.02] per 100,000 livebirths) and Slovenia (0.03[0.02 to 0.04] per 100,000 livebirths), while Sierra Leone (274.68[162.22 to 422.85] per 100,000 livebirths) and Chad (140.38[90.17 to 203.67] per 100,000 livebirths) had the highest (Table S3, Fig. 3C).

However, seven countries, including Dominica and Zimbabwe, experienced an increase in MMR compared to 1990 (Table S3, Fig. 3D-F).

Cross-national health inequality analysis based on dalys

Maternal hemorrhage had negative SII values, indicating that the burden of disease was concentrated in countries with lower SDI. From 1990 to 2021, the SII for maternal hemorrhage DALY rates shifted from − 980.27 (95%CI: −1078.68 to −881.86) to −288.12 (95%CI: −336.14 to −240.09), indicating a reduction in the absolute disparity between the hypothetically lowest- and highest-SDI nations (Fig. 4A). Conversely, the CI intensified from − 0.57 (95% CI: −0.69 to −0.35) to −0.66 (95% CI: −0.77 to −0.50), signifying progressive concentration of disease burden toward lower-SDI countries. This was visually corroborated by concentration curves (Fig. 4B).

Joinpoint regression analysis

We further analyzed the temporal trends of disease burden over the period 1990–2021 and identified key time points by Joinpoint regression analysis. From 1990 to 2021, the global ASIR, MMR, and ASDR for maternal hemorrhage all exhibited a downward trend. The ASIR declined relatively minimally overall (AAPC = −1.04 [−1.07 to −1.00], p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A, Table S4). Notably, the middle SDI region showed a significant increase in ASIR from 2007 to 2015 (APC = 2.19 [2.08 to 2.30], p < 0.001) (Table S5). The MMR (AAPC = −3.83[−3.96 to −3.70], p < 0.001) and ASDR (AAPC = −2.78[−2.92 to −2.65]) declined more rapidly (Fig. 5B-C, Table S4). The periods of most rapid decline were 2012–2016 for MMR (APC = −5.30, 95% CI: −5.99 to −4.61) and 2012–2015 for ASDR (APC = −5.45, 95% CI: −6.65 to −4.23) (Table S6-S7).

Analysis of maternal hemorrhage burden by age group: the U-shaped MMR pattern

Age-stratified analyses indicated that the ASIR of maternal hemorrhage in 2021 increased with maternal age until reaching the highest in the 25–29 years age group, and then gradually declined (Fig. 6A). ASDR followed a similar pattern, with the highest burden observed in the 20–24 years age group (Fig. 6B). In contrast, MMR exhibited a U-shaped pattern, with higher risks among adolescents aged 10–14 years, decreasing to a minimum in the 25–29 years age group (MMR = 24.47 [20.13 to 29.33] per 100,000 livebirths), and increasing again in women aged 35 years and older (Fig. 6C). From 1990 to 2021, all age groups demonstrated declining ASIR, ASDR, and MMR, with the most significant reductions observed in MMR among women aged 35 years and older (Fig. 6D-F).

Discussion

Global progress and persistent disparities

Our analysis confirms substantial global progress in reducing the burden of maternal hemorrhage over the past three decades, reflecting widespread improvements in obstetric practices and access to care [24]. However, the burden of maternal hemorrhage varied significantly across regions, with incidence in low SDI regions 3.8 times higher than in high SDI regions, and MMR up to 93 times higher, highlighting the global health inequalities and the persistent barriers to improving maternal health in resource-constrained settings. Our analyses further demonstrated a strong inverse correlation between socioeconomic development and the burden of maternal hemorrhage. Despite projections indicating continued reductions in global burden by 2030, maternal hemorrhage, as the greatest contributor to maternal mortality, will remain a significant barrier to achieving SDG 3.1.

Regional and national inequalities

The regional disparities in maternal hemorrhage highlighted gaps in maternal health infrastructure, policy implementation, and clinical management. High SDI regions had benefited from standardized protocols and advanced prenatal monitoring. Developed countries such as the United States, Australia, Canada, and the United Kingdom had issued national guidelines for hemorrhage prevention and management to reduce the risk of maternal hemorrhage and subsequent severe complications [25, 26]. Additionally, some developing countries had implemented positive actions. For example, China had achieved significant improvements in reducing the maternal mortality by strengthening high-risk pregnancy screening, securing blood supply and increasing primary-level emergency response capacity, particularly in rural areas [27]. Our results confirmed the effectiveness of these measures. In contrast, many LMICs continued to suffer from high incidence and mortality rates due to shortages of essential emergency medications, inadequate blood transfusion facilities, and outdated maternal health technologies. For example, the utilization of institutional delivery services in Ethiopia was only 24% [28], and traditional delivery made it challenging for untrained traditional birth attendants (TBAs) to manage maternal hemorrhage, particularly conditions such as placental retention or uterine atony [29]. Furthermore, harmful cultural stigma in these areas, such as gender inequality, female genital mutilation, and adolescent pregnancies, exacerbates these risks [30, 31].

Previous studies had found that maternal hemorrhage imposes a disproportionately heavy burden in low-resource regions, for instance, in Sub-Saharan Africa, maternal hemorrhage is attributable to over 28% of maternal mortality, whereas less than 15% in high SDI regions [1]. Our findings further demonstrate a strong negative correlation between SDI and MMR, underscoring the critical importance of socioeconomic development in reducing hemorrhage-related maternal deaths. Moreover, targeted government investment and robust healthcare infrastructure also profoundly impact maternal health within countries. For instance, persistent disparities in MMR persist between China’s western provinces and the more affluent eastern coastal regions [32]. Sub-national studies in Kenya and Nigeria also reveal considerable variation in PPH management and outcomes across different health facilities [33, 34]. This underscores that reducing the global burden of maternal hemorrhage is not only a clinical challenge but fundamentally a matter of political will and strategic resource allocation to build resilient and equitable maternal health systems from the sub-national level upward.

The cross-national inequality analysis reveals a complex duality in burden distribution. While absolute disparities between high- and low-SDI nations narrowed substantially, relative inequality intensified significantly, with the burden becoming increasingly concentrated among lower-SDI countries. Consequently, despite collective progress, some extremely low-income countries may have experienced stagnation or even worsening trends in maternal outcomes, potentially reversing earlier gains. This growing inequality poses a major threat to the quality foundation of SDG3.1, which calls for targeted redistribution of resources to high-population-density areas while providing remote regions with technology tailored to local conditions.

Age-specific risk patterns

Age-specific analyses revealed that incidence and DALYs were highest among women aged 20–29 years, likely reflecting higher fertility rates in this group. Interestingly, advanced maternal age (AMA) was not associated with an increased incidence of maternal hemorrhage. Previous clinical studies had shown that although AMA was more commonly associated with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, placenta accreta spectrum, and other adverse pregnancy outcomes, it was not associated with PPH [35]. Another retrospective study found that after adjusting for confounding factors, AMA was associated with a lower risk of PPH [36]. Actually, research has shown that AMA women exhibit no significant impairment in uterine contractility [37], but have a decrease in uterine blood perfusion with age [38], which may contribute to the lower incidence of maternal hemorrhage observed in older women. Regarding maternal deaths, as expected, our age-stratified analysis revealed a U-shaped pattern. These results likely reflected increased physiological vulnerability and a higher prevalence of comorbidities in AMA women, emphasizing the need for age-specific risk stratification and targeted perinatal care.

Implications for policy and future interventions

Transformative advances in medical technology, public health policies, and healthcare access have profoundly influenced the epidemiology of maternal hemorrhage [39]. Advances in various areas, including the widespread use of antibiotics and oxytocin, as well as improvements in surgical techniques, have led to significant reductions in MMR due to infection and hemorrhage in recent decades [40]. Furthermore, the application of the Internet of Things (IoT) concept in clinical practice represents one of the most revolutionary breakthroughs [41]. In the field of postpartum hemorrhage prevention, IoT-based systems enable real-time monitoring of physiological parameters such as blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and heart rate through wearable devices or sensor arrays [42]. These systems can transmit data to clinicians in real time in a cost-effective manner, offering the potential to reduce maternal hemorrhage-related mortality. Nevertheless, our analyses suggested a reversal of trends in some regions, such as rising incidence in Tropical Latin America and persistently high maternal mortality in the Caribbean. These trends may be associated with excessive cesarean section rates [43], inadequate management of the second and third stages of labor [44], and blood supply shortages in these regions [45]. Even in some developed countries, such as the United States, the rates of PPH rose from 2.7% in 2000 to 4.3% in 2019 [12]. These trends highlight the need for tailored interventions that address resource limitations and systemic barriers in healthcare delivery.

Since most cases of maternal hemorrhage and related deaths are preventable, addressing this issue is crucial for reducing the overall MMR [46]. Our projections also underscore the necessity of sustained efforts to further reduce mortality and achieve SDG 3.1. To mitigate the threat of maternal hemorrhage, particularly in high-risk populations and regions, a multifaceted strategy that integrates evidence-based clinical protocols, technological innovations, and policy reforms is essential. The WHO recommended active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL), including the promotion of uterotonic agents and uterine fundal massage, especially in low-resource areas [47, 48]. Early identification of high-risk individuals, such as multiple births, advanced maternal age, and anemia, is essential. These vulnerable groups require prioritized attention through targeted healthcare programs, including specialized antenatal care and intrapartum monitoring. Additionally, establishing multidisciplinary teams can help mitigate adverse outcomes in high-risk pregnancies [49]. In LMICs, where 99% of maternal deaths occur, TBAs play a critical role, and providing them with standardized training can improve pregnancy outcomes [50, 51]. As a cost-effective strategy, resource-limited areas should prioritize investment in emergency obstetric care (EmOC) [52, 53]. Moreover, interventions such as antenatal iron supplementation, nutritional support, enhanced community education, and improved transport systems have been shown to improve maternal health in resource-limited settings [8, 54].

Limitations and data challenges

This study offers the first up-to-date global estimates of maternal hemorrhage burden across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021, based on GBD 2021 data. However, it is subject to several limitations that warrant consideration. First, since the data were sourced from the GBD 2021 database, there may be unavoidable data collection mistakes, especially in the low SDI regions where data collection systems are often inadequate. This could lead to an underestimation of the true disease burden. Second, despite the fact that the GBD database has made multiple efforts to adjust, changes in diagnostic criteria and reporting practices over time may still affect the comparability of data across different regions and years. Third, the analysis may not fully account for confounding variables, such as differences in healthcare quality within countries or sub-national socioeconomic inequalities. To address these gaps, future research should prioritize improving data collection and standardization in low-income settings, refining the classification of maternal hemorrhage subtypes (e.g., antepartum vs. postpartum), and investigating the complex interplay between socioeconomic determinants and maternal health outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates both remarkable global progress and persistent inequalities in the burden of maternal hemorrhage. Key findings—such as 93-fold difference in maternal mortality between low- and high-SDI regions, the paradoxical trends of narrowing absolute but widening relative inequalities, and the U-shaped age pattern collectively underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions. These results highlight that maternal hemorrhage is not only a clinical challenge but also a matter of health equity, infrastructure, and political will. Strengthening emergency obstetric care, ensuring equitable access to blood transfusion and uterotonic agents, expanding the use of innovative technologies such as IoT-based monitoring, and prioritizing investment in maternal health systems are essential strategies. Without urgent and coordinated action, maternal hemorrhage will remain a major barrier to achieving SDG 3.1. Our results provide timely evidence to guide policymakers and health authorities in accelerating efforts to reduce preventable maternal deaths and to close the gaps between and within countries.
