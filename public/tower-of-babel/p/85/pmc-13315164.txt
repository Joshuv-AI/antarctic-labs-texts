Insomnia With Objective Short Sleep Duration Is Associated With Incident Cardiovascular and Cerebrovascular Disease: Evidence from the UK Biobank Data
Authors: Slobodanka Pejovic, Yimeng Shang, Lan Kong, Fan He, Julio Fernandez‐Mendoza, Kristina Lenker, Nikolaos Athanasiou, Yun Li, Alexandros N Vgontzas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study is to assess the longitudinal association of the insomnia with short sleep duration phenotype with cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cerebrovascular disease (CBVD) in the UK biobank using self‐reported insomnia and accelerometer‐measured habitual sleep duration.MethodsThe sample sizes for incident CVD and CBVD were 6959 and 7690 participants, respectively, with an average follow‐up time of 3.6 years for CVD and 3.4 years for CBVD. Short sleep duration was defined as a nightly sleep duration below the sample median (<7.3 hours) and insomnia as a self‐report of difficulty falling or staying asleep “Usually” versus Never/Rarely/Sometimes. Participants were classified into 4 mutually exclusive sleep groups based on the presence or absence of short sleep duration (Yes/No) and insomnia symptoms (Yes/No). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used adjusting for major confounders.ResultsCompared with normal sleepers with normal sleep duration, participants with insomnia with short sleep duration (hazard ratio [HR]=1.39, 95% CI=1.11–1.74), but not those with insomnia with normal sleep duration (HR=1.13, 95% CI=0.89–1.43) or normal sleepers with short sleep duration (HR=1.03, 95% CI=0.86–1.23) were significantly associated with incident CVD. Similarly, participants with insomnia with short sleep duration (HR=1.76, 95% CI=1.29–2.39), but not those with insomnia with normal sleep duration (HR=1.04, 95% CI=0.72–1.49) or short sleep duration (HR=1.13, 95% CI=0.87–1.48) were significantly associated with incident CBVD.ConclusionsThese results from the UK biobank database using accelerometer to assess habitual objective sleep duration, extend previous findings that the insomnia with short sleep duration phenotype is associated with significantly increased risk of incident CVD and CBVD.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, insomnia short sleep duration phenotype, UK BiobankSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

This is the first prospective study outside of the United States, in a large UK Biobank cohort, that demonstrated a significant association between the insomnia with short sleep duration phenotype, as measured objectively via accelerometry, and increased risk of incident cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, providing important cross‐cultural validation of this previously reported relation.

This is the first prospective study outside of the United States, in a large UK Biobank cohort, that demonstrated a significant association between the insomnia with short sleep duration phenotype, as measured objectively via accelerometry, and increased risk of incident cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, providing important cross‐cultural validation of this previously reported relation.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Accelerometry, despite its methodological differences from polysomnography, can effectively identify this high‐risk insomnia phenotype offering a practical clinical tool for risk stratification.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cerebrovascular disease (CBVD) remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for 18.6 million deaths annually. 1 The relationship between sleep disturbance and cardiometabolic health has gained increasing attention, with emerging evidence suggesting bidirectional association. 2 , 3 , 4 While insomnia independently is linked to cardiovascular risk, objectively measured sleep duration appears to significantly enhance this association. 3 , 5 Evidence from both population and clinical samples based on polysomnography indicates that insomnia with objective short sleep duration (ISSD) represents a biologically severe phenotype of the disorder, characterized by significant cardiometabolic morbidity, including hypertension and diabetes. 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 This phenotypic distinction carries important clinical implications, as it identifies a subgroup of insomnia patients at markedly high risk for adverse health outcomes.

In Europe, CVD and CBVD are responsible of 45% of all deaths, that is, 3.9 million people per year. 9 In the United Kingdom (UK), approximately 7.6 million people live with heart and circulatory diseases, causing over 160 000 deaths annually and resulting in an estimate of £19 billion in economic toll whereas in the United States, these diseases account for nearly 28% of all deaths despite extensive prevention efforts. 10 , 11 Insomnia, characterized by presence of difficulty initiating and/or maintain sleep accompanied by daytime consequences, affects approximately 10% to 15% of adult population, with higher rates in women, elderly and individuals with physical and mental health conditions. 12 The high prevalence of both CVD/CBVD and insomnia, along with their substantial socioeconomic burden, highlights the importance of understanding further their relationship, particularly as growing evidence suggest that sleep disturbance may represent a modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity. Recent longitudinal studies from the SHHS (Sleep Heart Health Study) and the Penn State Adult Cohort which assessed the association of ISSD phenotype and incident CVD and CBVD have reinforced this connection. 13 , 14 The SHHS demonstrated that participants with insomnia and polysomnography‐defined short sleep duration (<6 hours) had a 29% higher risk of incident cardiovascular disease and stroke compared with those without insomnia who slept ≥6 hours. 13 Similarly, based on data from Penn State Adult Cohort, ISSD was associated with a 2.5‐fold increased risk of incident heart disease and stroke. 14 Notably, to date, no study has examined the association between ISSD and heart disease and CBVD including stroke in a general population sample outside of the United States, limiting our understanding of this relationship across different geographical and health care contexts. 15

A recent analysis of accelerometer‐measured sleep in more than 100 000 UK Biobank participants has uncovered a comprehensive landscape of human sleep patterns, revealing 16 distinct sleep phenotypes that include multiple variants of the ISSD phenotype. 16 This large‐scale characterization of sleep profiles demonstrated how ISSD exists along the continuum of severity, with various manifestation of sleep fragmentation and shorten sleep duration. 16 Despite these important findings, longitudinal studies examining association of ISSD and heart disease and/or CBVD including stroke remain limited. Moreover, existing studies have primarily relied on in‐laboratory polysomnography, which, while considered the gold standard for sleep assessment, has significant limitations for large‐scale epidemiological research attributable to its cost and potential for first‐night effect that may not present typical sleep pattern. 6 , 8 No longitudinal study to date has examined the association of ISSD and heart disease and CBVD using ecologically friendly, cost‐effective, home‐based sleep monitoring devices, which could potentially replace polysomnography in large general population samples. Wrist‐worn accelerometry offers a promising alternative for objective sleep assessment in participants' natural environment over extended periods, possibly providing more representative data on habitual sleep patterns. 17 , 18

The aim of this study is to assess the association of ISSD and incident CVD and CBVD including stroke in the large UK Biobank cohort based on self‐reported insomnia symptoms and objectively measured habitual sleep duration by accelerometer. The UK Biobank represents an ideal cohort for this investigation because of its large sample size and longitudinal design with comprehensive follow‐up data on disease outcomes. 19 Thus, this study has a potential to validate a more accessible approach in identifying high‐risk insomnia phenotypes and contribute to improved cardiovascular risk stratification and prevention strategies.

METHODS

The authors will make the data, methods used in the analysis, and materials used to conduct the research available to any researcher for purposes of reproducing the results or replicating the procedure.

For the purpose of our study we used data from the UK Biobank, data version v18.1 (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/), which is a nationwide, population‐based prospective cohort study that has recruited more than 500 000 participants. 20

Data Collection and Cleaning

Based on the objectives of our study, we first sampled those subjects who had objective measures of sleep. From a total of 500 000 participants of the UK Biobank we identified 96 219 subjects who had 1‐week objective sleep measures using accelerometer during 2013 to 2016. The raw sleep data are stored in common weakness enumaration format (Data‐Field 90 001). Sleep duration defined from the accelerometer data (Category 727) with variable name ‘spt_duration_ad_t5a5_mn’ was used in the analysis. We applied R package GGIR version 3.0 to process and analyze data collected with wearable raw acceleration sensors. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Sleep periods and episodes were identified based on 5‐second epoch time‐series accelerometer signals, and determined as the longest block of combined inactivity bouts lasting ≥30 minutes. 25 After excluding participants withdrawn from UK Biobank cohort and those subjects with extreme objective sleep duration (≤3 hours/night or ≥11 hours of sleep/night), we ended with 95 598 subjects. From this sample we selected those subjects with valid data on the self‐reported insomnia symptoms item (see below) within±2 years from the time that objective sleep measures were obtained (n=8842). Finally, after removing subjects with ‘preferred not to answer’ for baseline variables or covariates such as insomnia, smoking and alcohol, the sample size was 8762. All participants were age ≥18. The UK Biobank received approval by the National Health Service (NHS) North‐West Multi‐Centre Research Ethics Committee. All participants provided written informed consent. The present study was completed under the UK Biobank application reference number 62277.

The data analyzed in this study are available through the UK Biobank website, accessible with approved application to the UK Biobank. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Exposure

Objective short sleep duration and self‐reported insomnia were the 2 exposure variables of interest. We used the median of the accelerometer obtained total sleep time of the total sample to define objective short sleep duration <7.3 hours/night. Participants who reported “Usually” having insomnia symptoms were categorized as having insomnia, whereas those who reported Never/Rarely/Sometimes were categorized as normal sleepers, based on prior studies in the UK Biobank. 26 , 27 Based on prior rigorous studies, 6 , 7 , 8 , 16 , 28 , 29 , 30 we created 4 mutually exclusive sleep phenotypes based on the presence of short sleep duration (Yes/No) and/or insomnia symptoms (Yes/No): participants who reported “insomnia symptoms” and slept objectively <7.3 hours/night (ie, insomnia with short sleep duration [ISSD] group); participants who reported “insomnia symptoms” and slept objectively ≥7.3 hours/night (ie, insomnia with normal sleep duration [INSD] group); participants who did not report insomnia symptoms and slept objectively <7.3 hours/night (ie, short sleep duration [SSD] group); and participants who did not report insomnia symptoms and slept objectively ≥7.3 hours/night (ie, normal sleep duration [NSD] group). The onset date of exposure was assumed to be 2 years before accelerometer measurements since the sleep patterns have been shown to be stable over a 2‐year period. 31

Outcomes

For the purpose of this study, the presence of CVD at baseline and at follow‐up was defined using (1) International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (I20–I25, I30–I52, 410–414, 420–429) in the health records and in primary care data using Read codes (Version 2 or Version 3) or (2) UK Biobank variables: history of heart attack, angina, and suffering from heart issues. The presence of CBVD at baseline and at follow up was defined using (1) ICD codes (I60–I69, 430–438) in the health records and in primary care data using Read codes (Version 2 or Version 3) or (2) UK Biobank variable: stroke.

Statistical Analysis

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are evaluated and presented as mean (SD) and proportions for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. To compare these characteristics according to incidence of CVD or CBVD, analysis of variance or chi‐square tests were used, as appropriate.

Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to illustrate the time to CVD in Figure 1 and CBVD in Figure 2, respectively. To evaluate the associations of the 4 sleep phenotypes with the risk of incident CVD and CBVD, respectively, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used. Residual analyses were performed to assess proportional hazards assumption and identify potential outliers, influential observations and nonlinearity. Specifically, the proportional hazards assumption was assessed by examining scaled Schoenfeld residuals plotted against transformed time. Influential observations were evaluated using deviance residual plots. Linearity of the log hazard with respect to continuous covariates was assessed using Martingale residual plots. Major confounding factors, including age, sex, body mass index, smoking, alcohol intake, diagnosis of sleep apnea, comorbidities (CVD/CBVD, depression, cognitive disorder, diabetes) were extracted from the study visit closest to the baseline and controlled for. We conducted step‐by‐step analysis with different sets of covariates in separate multivariable models. The baseline (time zero) was set to be the onset date of exposure (ie, 2 years before the objective sleep measure) and participants were censored at the time of diagnosis or last follow‐up of UK Biobank study visit, whichever occurs first. We also performed sensitivity analyses by defining baseline at the time of exposure measurement (objective sleep duration assessment via accelerometry) We excluded those with specific adverse event at or before time zero and those without any records at or before time zero. The sample sizes for analysis on CVD and CBVD were 6959 and 7650 respectively.

RESULTS

The overall incidence rates of CVD and CBVD were 10% and 4.4%, respectively. Among 8762 participants, 6959 did not have CVD at baseline and had complete data. Of these 6959 participants, a total of 697 developed incident CVD, whereas 6262 did not have CVD at follow‐up. Patients were followed up until the development of event of interest or last visit. Among 8762 participants, 7690 did not have CBVD at baseline and had complete data. Of these 7690 participants, a total of 337 developed incident CBVD, whereas 7353 did not have CBVD at follow‐up. Patients were followed up until the development of event of interest or last visit. Average follow‐up time for CVD was 3.57±2.21 years, and for CBVD was 3.42±2.11 years.

When considering baseline (time zero) as the time when objective sleep duration assessment was made, the results remained the same. Specifically, compared with normal sleepers with normal sleep duration, participants with ISSD, but not with INSD or SSD, had significantly increased risk of incident CVD (HR =1.37, 95% CI=1.07–1.76, P=0.0132). Participants with ISSD compared with normal sleepers with normal sleep duration had increased risk of incident CBVD, but the difference was no longer significant (HR=1.29, 95% CI=0.88–1.90, P=1.846). This loss of significance is related to the fact that a large proportion of CBVD events, about 40%, included in our original analysis, occurred before the sleep exposure.

As a sensitivity analysis, we run the Fine‐Gray models to account for competing events attributable to death before the development of CVD or CBVD. The estimation results for sleep phenotypes are similar to those based on the Cox models since the mortality rate was low, at around 3% in our study cohort. The assessment of Cox model assumptions indicated that age did not meet the proportional hazards assumption for the CBVD models. Hence, the interaction of age with time was added to the Cox models in the analysis of CBVD risk (Table 3). In addition, there were no apparent influential observations, outliers or nonlinearity based on the residual analysis. When adjusting for these additional covariates, the association between sleep phenotypes and CVD/CBVD risk remained similar to those presented in Table 3.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study outside of the United States, in a large UK Biobank cohort, that demonstrated a significant association between the ISSD phenotype, as measured objectively through accelerometry, and increased risk of incident CVD and CBVD, providing important cross‐cultural validation of this relationship. Specifically, the ISSD phenotype was associated with a 1.4‐fold significantly increased risk of incident CVD compared with normal sleepers after 3.6 years of follow‐up. Similarly, compared with normal sleepers, only the ISSD phenotype was associated with a 1.8‐fold significantly increased risk of incident CBVD after 3.4 years of follow‐up.

The robust association between ISSD and risk factors for adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular health outcomes, such as hypertension, has been consistently demonstrated across multiple populations and clinical studies. 6 , 8 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 The present accelerometry‐based findings of increased risk of incident CVD and CBVD are consistent with 2 polysomnography‐based studies in the SHHS and the Penn State Adult Cohort. 13 , 14 This consistency is particularly noteworthy given the methodological differences in objective sleep assessment (polysomnography versus accelerometry). Bertisch et al.'s analysis of the SHHS demonstrated that individuals with ISSD, but not those with INSD, had 1.3‐fold increased risk of incident CVD and/or CBVD over 11.4 years of follow‐up. 13 Similarly, a study of the Penn State Adult Cohort data found a 2.5‐fold increased odds of incident CVD and/or CBVD in individuals with ISSD after 9.2 years of follow‐up. 14 Also, our study results are in line with 2 recent studies from the MESA (Multiethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) cohort demonstrating that insomnia with objective short sleep duration is associated with increased levels of cardiac troponin T, a marker of subclinical myocardial injury and coronary artery calcification in women. 36 , 37 Our findings in the UK Biobank cohort provide further evidence of this relationship in a larger population. Also, the consistency of this study findings with previous research conducted in the United States suggest that the pathophysiological mechanism linking ISSD to CVD and CBVD risk likely transcend geographic and health care differences.

Additionally, our study is the first one to use sleep data obtained via accelerometry and our findings are consistent with previous studies that have applied polysomnography sleep recordings in identifying the ISSD phenotype. While polysomnography remains the gold standard, it requires specialized equipment and trained technicians, making it impractical for widespread population screening and current clinical guidelines do not recommend it for routine insomnia evaluation unless there is a suspicion of another primary sleep disorder such as sleep apnea. 38 In contrast, accelerometry represents a much more accessible and less expensive approach for sleep assessment, can be used in the patient's home environment and allow for multiple night recordings providing a more representative depiction of habitual sleep patterns. Furthermore, its use is suggested as a conditional recommendation for estimating sleep parameters in sleep disorders. 39 An important consideration in interpreting accelerometer‐based findings is the optimal threshold for defining “short sleep”. While polysomnography studies have typically used <6 hours, the median total sleep time in the general population, as the threshold for identifying ISSD in adults, 6 , 7 , 8 , 13 , 14 , 29 , 30 , 32 , 33 a higher threshold (eg, <7 hours) can be expected when using accelerometry, as this method typically overestimates total sleep time compared with polysomnography. 40 Overall, our findings suggest that accelerometry‐based sleep measures can effectively identify the high risk ISSD phenotype, offering a practical pathway for translating this research findings into clinical practice and population health strategies.

Furthermore, beyond cardiovascular disease, the ISSD phenotype has been linked to broader metabolic dysfunction, including increased odds or relative risk of prevalent type 2 diabetes (ORs=1.81–2.95, risk ratio=1.63, respectively) 7 , 41 , 42 and increased insulin sensitivity 28 , 43 , 44 These metabolic disturbances may represent additional pathways through which ISSD increases cardiovascular risk. In that regard, this study results suggest that objectively measured short sleep duration may serve as a useful biomarker for detecting insomnia patients at heightened cardiometabolic risk who might benefit from more specific prevention interventions.

This study findings on the association between ISSD phenotype and increased cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risks complement the work by Katori et al. who used the same UK Biobank accelerometry data set to identify distinct sleep phenotypes through unsupervised clustering methods. 16 While Katori and colleagues employed sophisticated machine learning approaches to detect 16 sleep phenotypes, including seven different insomnia‐like clusters, their research focused primarily on sleep phenotypes classification and not on clinical outcomes. 16 However, our study extends beyond phenotypic classification to demonstrate clinical significance of the objectively measured sleep patterns by establishing a clear link between ISSD and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcome. Importantly, this study findings validate that the ISSD phenotype identified through accelerometry, similar to one of Katori et al.'s clusters, represent not merely a sleep variant but a clinically significant risk factor for CVD and CBVD, reinforcing the utility of objective sleep measures in both research and clinical settings. 16

Clinical and Public Health Policy Implications

Until now, diagnosis and treatment of insomnia is based on clinical evaluation and subjective measures. Our finding suggests that objective measure of total sleep time is a clinically useful and valid assay to phenotype insomnia. Furthermore, the decision on how to treat insomnia relies on availability, patient's preference and clinician's training and background. Also, according to the recommendations of American College of Physicians, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is “first line treatment” for all patients with insomnia. 45 Our findings may lead to specific treatment related to each insomnia phenotype which is the goal of precision medicine. In that way, phenotyping of insomnia will maximize cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia treatment resources and minimize unnecessary exposure to adverse medication effects.

Until now only sleep apnea is acknowledged by public health organizations and scientific associations, that is, American Heart Association as a modifiable risk factor for hypertension and CVD/CBVD. Our study suggests that insomnia, the most common sleep disorder, should be acknowledged as a significant risk factor for hypertension and CVD/CBVD adverse events.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, which include a prospective longitudinal design allowing for temporal assessment of incident heart disease and CBVD including stroke in a large sample from the UK Biobank population, which enabled cross‐cultural validation of the findings previously limited to the US population. Also, objective sleep assessment using wrist accelerometry, rather than polysomnography, as well as multiple nights of sleep recordings provided more representative data of habitual sleep patterns.

However, there are several limitations that should be noted. First, assessment of insomnia was based on a single question regarding insomnia symptoms, not on a clinician's diagnosis of insomnia disorder according to current diagnostic critersia. However, definition of insomnia disorder would have included the more severe type of insomnia and would be expected to be associated with higher risk of CVD/CBVD. Indeed, the prevalence of insomnia symptoms in the UK Biobank sample is about 30% whereas the prevalence of chronic insomnia disorder in general population samples is about 8% to 10%. 6 , 46 Second, there was a time interval between measurements, as the objective sleep assessment via accelerometry occurred within a 2‐year window of self‐reported insomnia symptoms, rather than concurrently. It has been reported that objective sleep measures in normal sleepers and patients suffering from insomnia are stable across several consecutive nights and after 2 and half years. 31 To address potential reverse causation, we performed sensitivity analyses excluding participants that developed CVD/CBVD 2 years before objective sleep assessment via accelerometry and the results were in the same direction for both CVD and CBVD. The results for CBVD lost statistical significance most likely since 40% of CBVD events included in the primary analysis occurred before the sleep exposure. This suggests that future studies should not include CBVD events that predated the sleep exposure given that even silent strokes and white matter lesions may alter sleep architecture. Third, while we controlled for several potential confounding factors including sleep apnea, residual confounders could not be ruled out entirely, such as comprehensive assessment of other sleep disorders and use of sleep medications. Finally, our results based on a subsample of the total UK Biobank sample with accelerometer data cannot be generalizable because of much smaller sample size than that of the UK Biobank accelerometer study cohort. However, we should note that several characteristics such as body mass index, sex, and age are similar between our analysis cohort and the original accelerometer study cohort.

Future Directions

Future studies should validate simple wearable methods vis‐a‐vis polysomnography which is the gold standard to assess objective sleep duration. Also, large multi‐center, randomized clinical trials are needed to assess how insomnia phenotyping modifies treatment response.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence that ISSD significantly increases cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk in the UK population. This cross‐cultural validation strengthens the previous evidence of ISSD, primarily in US‐based samples, as a distinct phenotype with unique pathophysiological implications. Importantly, this study suggests that accelerometry, despite its methodological differences from PSG, may identify this high‐risk insomnia phenotype offering a practical clinical tool for risk stratification. Finally, these findings underscore the clinical importance of objective sleep assessment in patients with insomnia which may lead to more personalized approaches to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk reductions.
