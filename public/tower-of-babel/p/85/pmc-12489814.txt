A Comparison of Artificial Intelligence Platforms in the Utility of Answering Frequently Asked Questions About Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Calista Stevens, Mehreen Pasha, Dashun Liu, Andrew Block, Anthony Parrino, Craig Rodner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe rise of artificial intelligence (AI) in health care comes with increasing concerns about the use and integrity of the information it generates. Chat Generative Pre-Trained Transformer (ChatGPT) 3.5, Google Gemini, and Bing Copilot are free AI chatbot platforms that may be used for answering medical questions and disseminating medical information. Given that carpal tunnel syndrome accounts for 90% of all neuropathies, it is important to understand the accuracy of the information patients may be receiving. The purpose of this study is to determine the use and accuracy of responses generated by ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Bing Copilot in answering frequently asked questions about carpal tunnel syndrome.MethodsTwo independent authors scored responses using the DISCERN tool. DISCERN consists of 15 questions assessing health information on a five-point scale, with total scores ranging from 15 to 75 points. Then, a two-factor analysis of variance was conducted, with scorer and chatbot type as the factors.ResultsOne-way analysis of variance revealed no significant difference in DISCERN scores among the three chatbots. The chatbots each scored in the “fair” range, with means of 45 for ChatGPT, 48 for Bing Copilot, and 46 for Google Gemini. The rise of artificial intelligence (AI) use in health care comes with increasing concerns about the use and integrity of information being produced and disseminated.1, 2, 3, 4 For the general public, AI models are increasingly used across health care settings by patients for quick and understandable generation of information in a conversational tone and format.1 Chat Generative Pre-Trained Transformer (ChatGPT) 3.5,5 Google Gemini,6 and Bing Copilot7 are all free and popular AI chatbot platforms that are easily accessible to patients and can be used for answering medical questions and for dissemination of medical information.8 Many of these chatbots have been praised for their performance in medical education and research, with certain models even demonstrating abilities to reach passing thresholds on US Medical Licensing Examinations.9 Despite performing exceptionally on standardized testing, the clinical decision-making abilities of AI have been found to be unreliable when compared with clinical guidelines and standard of care.10 The use and validity of information that is produced by AI chatbots for consumers has been called into question repeatedly by the literature.3,11, 12, 13, 14 These popular AI models function by learning from previously published information and generating new responses based on these prior inputs to interpret information for the user.15 Unfortunately, studies have shown that AI models are susceptible to biased information, as they are unable to discern real from fabricated information that has been learned by the platform.1, 2, 15 Although there is some literature with varying results regarding the accuracy of the use of AI in the field of orthopedics, little is known about its use in hand surgery.16

One of the most common conditions seen in an orthopedic hand and upper-extremity practice is carpal tunnel syndrome, with carpal tunnel release surgeries performed >600,000 times per year.17 However, carpal tunnel syndrome is sometimes a nonsurgical clinical diagnosis that may also require further neurologic testing in certain situations. As there is no one-size-fits-all diagnosis and treatment plan, it is important for both providers and patients to fully understand the accuracy of the information that patients may be receiving on this pathology from easily accessible AI platforms and chatbots.18,19 In this study, we sought to investigate the use and accuracy of three major AI chatbots (ChatGPT 3.5, Google Gemini, and Bing Copilot) in answering commonly asked questions about carpal tunnel syndrome. We hypothesized that all three AI platforms would present relatively accurate information regarding carpal tunnel syndrome, but that ChatGPT would underperform compared with its peers due to a lack of citations at the time of our query.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study used a cross-sectional, rater-based design in which three distinct chatbots, ChatGPT 3.5, Google Gemini, and Bing Copilot, generated health-related responses that were subsequently evaluated by a panel of surgeons (CR, AP, and AB). This cross-sectional study followed Strengthening of Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology as closely as possible.

Sample

Academic orthopedic and hand-surgery websites were browsed to compile 10 frequently asked questions regarding carpal tunnel surgery.20, 21, 22 The 10 questions generated by the junior authors (CS and MP) were subsequently approved by one senior author (CR), who deemed the questions relevant and commonly asked in their practice. The questions all pertained to carpal tunnel syndrome or carpal tunnel release surgery and included any questions regarding information on anatomy, causes, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment options, recovery, complications, and prevention. When rating responses from the chatbots, sources with potential conflicts of interest, such as studies funded by companies with a vested interest in a particular treatment for carpal tunnel syndrome without proper disclosure, nonpeer-reviewed sources, personal blogs, opinion pieces, and websites without proper medical accreditation, were excluded.

Procedure

Because of the constantly evolving nature of AI platforms, all chatbots were probed with the same 10 questions on a single date, June 25, 2024. Brand new accounts were created for each chatbot with no prior questions asked in each account. The chatbots were each prompted with the questions included in the Table. Variable and measurements

The answers produced by the three AI platforms were independently scored by three senior authors (A.P., C.R., and A.B.) using DISCERN, a validated tool for judging the quality of consumer health information, and Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria.23,24 Scoring was determined by authors comparing information to clinical practice guidelines and consensus statements from orthopedic associations, peer-reviewed medical journals, reputable medical websites, and official health organizations. The survey was a 15-question assessment of the information produced on a five-point scale. Higher DISCERN scores suggested that the material was more trustworthy, comprehensive, and well referenced. DISCERN scores of 64–80 were considered “excellent,” 52–63 as “good,” 41–51 as “fair,” 30–40 as “poor,” and 16–29 as “very poor.”25 As a secondary measure, each response was also assessed using the JAMA rating, rooted in criteria from JAMA. Journal of the American Medical Association’s scoring system measures reliability by assigning one point for authorship, attribution, disclosure, and currency on a scale of zero–four, with four achieving one point for each criterion.

Any scoring criterion from either survey that was not applicable to the question being scored was left blank, rather than assigning a zero, to avoid unnecessary reduction in overall score. However, this should be considered in final analysis as DISCERN is typically scored out of 75 total earnable points, and with some DISCERN criterion left blank, this lowered the overall possible points for certain questions.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed by author D.L. who was blinded as to which response was associated with each reviewer and chatbot. A two-way analysis of variance model was fitted to assess two factors: the differences between chatbots (“main effect” of chatbot), the differences between individual surgeons (“main effect” of surgeon ID), and their “interaction,” specifically if the chatbot's effect on DISCERN scores depended on which surgeon was conducting the rating. Because each surgeon could have a unique baseline scoring tendency, a linear mixed-effects model was used to more accurately account for interrater variability. Under this framework, chatbot was included as a fixed effect, and surgeon ID was modeled as a random effect. By allowing each surgeon to have their own intercept, the model adjusts for personal biases or tendencies in scoring, thus providing a clearer picture of how each chatbot performs differently on average. To examine if DISCERN scores and JAMA ratings aligned in their evaluations of chatbot-generated content, a Spearman correlation was computed between these two measures.

Results

Outcome data

All three chatbots scored fair on both their DISCERN and JAMA scoring system. Of note, questions 9–15 of DISCERN were not always applicable to the chatbot’s responses, in which case the question was left blank, resulting in lower overall scores.

For ChatGPT 3.5, DISCERN scores ranged from 30 to 60 when analyzing all surgeons and all responses. For questions in which all DISCERN questions were applicable, the scores ranged from 50 to 60, falling within the “fair” to “good” range. The mean score was 45. For Bing Copilot, DISCERN scores ranged from 29 to 71 (30–71 if not accounting for ones with DISCERN criteria that were not applicable) with a mean score of 48. Google Gemini’s DISCERN scores ranged from 30 to 64 with a mean of 46. Thus, average DISCERN scores were considered “fair” for all chatbots.

Main results

One-way analysis of variance revealed no statistically significant difference in DISCERN scores among the three chatbots (P = .43). However, when surgeons were incorporated into the model using the two-way analysis of variance model, statistically significant differences were observed in the scoring among the three surgeons (P < .001) but not among the scores given to individual chatbots (P = .23). Additionally, when assessing the “interaction term” which investigated whether the effect of different chatbots on DISCERN scores changed depending on the surgeon, differences between chatbot and surgeon were not statistically significant (P = .65). This suggested that the variation in DISCERN scores across different chatbots was consistent regardless of the surgeon grading.

The average JAMA score for ChatGPT and Google Gemini surpassed that of Bing Copilot, with averages of 2.3, 2.3, and 1.8 respectively. Notably, Bing Copilot was the only chatbot to provide sources along with the information it generated; however, the quality and validity of the sources was not investigated as part of this study.

Discussion

The use of AI is exploding across health care fields, and with this comes the potential for misinformation to be received by patients.1,2 It is essential to understand the accuracy of the information that patients may be absorbing, especially as AI-generated responses are now readily available at the top of every Google search. In this study, our results show that each chatbot performed equally well in overall accuracy and digestibility, with some notable shortcomings including a lack of cited sources. The bots all scored within the “fair” to “good” range of DISCERN scoring, emphasizing the overall accuracy of information produced by the AI platforms.

Artificial intelligence chatbots have repeatedly demonstrated proficiency in medical literature understanding and interpretation but may fall short. Mavrych et al26 explored the use of AI chatbots in answering multiple-choice questions in the medical curriculum and found that one of the bots outperformed students by as much as 8%. They suggest that the bots performed higher on questions based on recall rather than questions that required critical thinking skills.26 This study highlights the proficiency of certain bots, including ChatGPT platforms, in answering multiple-choice questions, even outperforming the average medical student.26 Unfortunately, patients typically do not ask multiple-choice questions, and this study highlighted that the question remains as to whether these tools can reliably provide “new” information to medical questions asked by patients.26 When looking at orthopedic literature specifically, Schwartzman et al27 found that ChatGPT in particular is an unreliable source of peer-reviewed information for Common Total Knee and Hip Arthroplasty patient questions. Their results show that ChatGPT fabricated responses and generated sources were unable to be traced to an existing publication.27 They postulate that ChatGPT was either trained with accurate sources and improperly cited them, created fake sources or fake information, or a combination of these.27

This study similarly revealed that although ChatGPT 3.5 provided mostly reliable responses (DISCERN score of 45), the completeness of the answers was overall disappointing because of citations of inaccurate references.27 The present study expands upon the data in Schwartzman et al by comparing ChatGPT to two other chatbots, in addition to evaluation with the DISCERN criteria, which is commonly used to evaluate the quality of disseminated consumer health information.

The prevalence of AI in the world of medicine and accessibility to patients is undeniable, especially with Google Gemini now populating the top of every Google search. With the inevitable rise in patients probing AI for medical information, it is essential to understand the quality of information that they are digesting. Ultimately, patients should always consult a physician about their unique situation and treatment plan, but our results suggest that patients may cautiously indulge in the information provided by AI regarding carpal tunnel syndrome to increase their knowledge and health literacy.

Although many chatbots have been evaluated for accuracy in responding to probed questions related to orthopedics and across the field of medicine, there is a paucity of information related specifically to answering frequently asked questions about carpal tunnel syndrome, the most common neuropathy across medicine.16, 17, 18,27,28 In the present study, all chatbots provided fairly accurate and detailed responses related to carpal tunnel syndrome, with no chatbot significantly outperforming the others.

This study is not without limitations. First, there are inherent limitations with the use of AI given the evolving nature of the technology. Even over the course of a day of answer collection, there could be evolving information that influenced the results generated by each chatbot and could be influenced by the way a question was asked. Question wording was directly sourced from academy and society websites, using language that patients may frequently use. A single chat was used for all 10 questions in each chatbot, which could potentially confound the results by improving the answers throughout the chat. Additionally, there is always a limitation of interreviewer bias, which was attempted to be controlled for through advanced statistical analysis, including the use of a linear mixed-effects model, intended to control for individual grader biases. Moreover, several scoring questions were left blank when calculating DISCERN scores if certain criteria were not applicable to the response at hand, skewing the results downwards and perhaps deflating their overall accuracy. For example, DISCERN criteria such as “Does it describe the benefit of each treatment?” did not apply to all prompted questions. Finally, generalizability to other pathologies in orthopedics and beyond cannot be specifically determined through this study; therefore, future studies are warranted to determine the applicability of AI platforms as a patient resource recommendation in clinical practice.

In summary, ChatGPT 3.5, Google Gemini, and Bing Copilot platforms generated fairly reliable answers for patient questions about carpal tunnel syndrome. However, users should continue to be aware of the shortcomings of the information provided given the lack of citations and potential for misconstrued information.
