Aortic Regurgitation and Progressive Left Ventricular Dilation in a Professional Hockey Player
Authors: Jakob N Zoellner, Martin Halle, Martin Kullik, Bradley Lander, Cyril Besson, Chantal Daucourt, Aaron L Baggish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistinguishing physiologic left ventricular (LV) remodeling from pathological LV dilation is a challenge among athletes with aortic regurgitation (AR).Case SummaryA 30-year-old professional hockey player with longstanding moderate AR and progressive LV dilation was restricted from sport, and surgical valve replacement was recommended. Clinical Trajectory of a Male Professional Ice Hockey Player With Bicuspid Aortic Valve, Moderate AR, and LV Dilation

AR = aortic regurgitation; LV = left ventricular; LVIDd = left ventricular internal dimension at end-diastole; PPCS = preparticipation cardiovascular screening; SDM = shared decision-making; TTE = transthoracic echocardiography.

History of Presentation

An asymptomatic 30-year-old professional male hockey player was found to have interval left ventricular (LV) dilation in the setting of a bicuspid aortic valve and moderate aortic regurgitation (AR) during routine preseason screening. He had experienced no subjective decline in exercise capacity. He presented to our institution for a second opinion after being restricted from sport and referred for surgery. Physical examination was notable for normal bilateral upper extremity blood pressures, a grade 2/4 decrescendo diastolic murmur, and no signs of severe AR.

Past Medical History

The patient had been diagnosed with a bicuspid aortic valve, moderate left ventricular (LV) dilation (left ventricular internal dimension at end-diastole [LVIDd]: 62-64 mm), and moderate-severity AR on screening transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) 5 years prior. A repeat TTE 3 years prior to his current evaluation demonstrated stable parameters. His family history, which included first-degree relative screening for congenital aortic disease, was unremarkable for bicuspid aortic valves, connective tissue disease, aortopathy, sudden cardiac death, or pacemaker/defibrillator implantation. He took no prescription medications, had no known allergies, and denied performance-enhancing drug use.

Six months prior to our evaluation, the patient had signed a contract with a new team. At the direction of his new strength and conditioning coaches, his longstanding preseason exercise conditioning regimen, which was previously limited to weight training and routine hockey activity, was modified. He was instructed to add long-duration steady-state cycling (60-min sessions in a heart rate zone of 140-145 beats/min), extended-duration high-intensity cycling intervals (6-8 intervals of 4-5 min duration in a heart rate zone of 160-170 beats/min), and to perform either a 90-minute bike ride or an 8- to 10-km run on the weekends. After 5 months of this training regimen, he underwent preseason medical evaluation inclusive of TTE. Imaging revealed an LVIDd and left ventricular end-systolic dimension (LVIDs) of 67 mm and 48 mm, respectively. His LV ejection fraction was preserved (58%), and moderate AR was again noted. Similar parameters were confirmed on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. He was restricted from further training and competition. Surgical evaluation was pursued and urgent valve replacement, based on interval LV dilation, was recommended.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis was LV cardiomyopathy secondary to moderate AR versus progressive LV dilation attributable to exercise-induced cardiac remodeling (EICR).

Investigations

The previous clinical trajectory and the results of the diagnostic testing performed at the second-opinion evaluation are summarized as follows. A 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus bradycardia, a mild intraventricular conduction delay (QRS interval: 119 ms), a short PR interval (113 ms) without pre-excitation, anterior lead early repolarization, and precordial lead voltage criteria for LV hypertrophy (Figure 1). TTE, relying on multiple measurements, demonstrated LVIDd and LVIDs measurements of 66 to 68 mm and 43 to 45 mm (indexed end-systolic LV dimension: 21 mm/m2), respectively (Figure 2). LV systolic function (biplane LV ejection fraction: 62%) and diastolic function (lateral E′: 15 cm/s) were within normal limits. Concomitant right ventricular (RV) dilation and biatrial enlargement were observed. There was a bicuspid aortic valve with calcified fusion of the right and noncoronary cusps, no significant stenosis, and moderate-severity AR (pressure half-time ranging from 382 to 399 ms on multiple views). The diameter and morphology of the aortic root and ascending aorta were normal. A review of imaging data from his recent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was notable for 4-chamber cardiac dilation (LVIDd: 66 mm, LV end-diastolic volume: 146 mL/m2, RV end-diastolic volume: 111 mL/m2), normal biventricular systolic function (LV ejection fraction: 59%, RV ejection fraction: 49%), an aortic valve regurgitant fraction of 27%, and no late gadolinium enhancement (Figure 3).

Management

The recent history of markedly increased endurance exercise training load (ie, volume and intensity) paralleled the interval increase in LV size, and there were no imaging or exercise testing findings to suggest progressive pathologic remodeling. The patient's clinical status and diagnostic findings met no guideline-based criteria for surgical aortic valve replacement. Accordingly, surgery was deferred, and the diagnostic findings along with the potential risks (ie, accelerated LV dilation with eventual deterioration of LV function) and benefits (many) of continued professional hockey participation were discussed with the patient using a shared decision-making (SDM) process.

Outcome

After inclusion of relevant stakeholders identified by the patient (ie, family, peers, and team medical staff) in the SDM process, he confirmed a desire to continue his career. The high likelihood of meeting surgical indications for aortic valve replacement in the future, irrespective of hockey participation, was emphasized. The patient's decision to resume his professional career was supported by the medical team contingent on his willingness to comply with longitudinal clinical surveillance inclusive of imaging and exercise testing on a 6-month basis. This was mutually agreed upon, and the patient was ultimately able to resume all hockey activities. He was encouraged to enroll in an ongoing study aimed at defining long-term outcomes in athletes with cardiovascular disease.1 At present, he remains active as a professional athlete without interval change in his clinical status.

Discussion

This case involves a 30-year-old professional hockey player with a bicuspid aortic valve, stable longstanding moderate AR, and progressive LV dilation who was seen for a second opinion regarding sports eligibility and surgical intervention. Although progressive LV chamber dilation is a well-recognized complication of chronic AR, LV remodeling is often multifactorial in athletes. The cardiac volume challenge inherent in endurance exercise training is a potent stimulus for EICR of the LV, which is characterized by preserved systolic function, preserved or enhanced diastolic function, and concomitant RV and biatrial dilation.2 In the current case, there was definitive documentation of stable moderate AR, reduction of AR severity during exercise, no subjective decline in exercise tolerance or other cardiac symptoms, objectively preserved exercise capacity, and an exercise training history suggesting EICR as the leading cause of interval LV dilation.

Distinguishing EICR from pathologic LV dilation related to regurgitant valvular heart disease can be challenging, and these entities may synergistically contribute to LV morphology.3 At present, there are no definitive diagnostic tools to untangle the relative contributions of these etiologies, and clinical management thus relies on integrating “the company that the dilated LV keeps.”4 In this clinical case, some element of the observed LV dilation was likely driven by chronic moderate AR. However, the interval change that prompted sport restriction and consideration of surgery was not accompanied by concomitant valvular deterioration or additional markers of pathology, but rather by a marked increase in endurance exercise training volume and intensity. Accordingly, surgery was deferred and future sports participation was considered.

This case demonstrates the application of current consensus recommendations for management of regurgitant valvular heart disease in competitive athletes.5 This report indicates that athletes with moderate or severe AR can participate in all competitive sports if they demonstrate: 1) normal LV systolic function; 2) cardiac dimensions consistent with EICR; and 3) normal exercise tolerance, all of which were fulfilled by this athlete. In addition, recommendations for surgical intervention in athletes should be dictated by current American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines, which indicate aortic valve replacement for: 1) symptomatic severe AR; 2) LV systolic dysfunction (LV ejection fraction <56%); or 3) severe LV dilation based on an end-systolic dimension of >50 mm or 25 mm/m2.6 None of these criteria were met by this patient.

Finally, this case demonstrates the importance of using an SDM process to manage clinical scenarios with uncertain future risk.7 While this athlete met all sports cardiology criteria for unrestricted participation and fulfilled no indications for surgical valve replacement, we could not exclude the possibility that continued professional hockey participation would accelerate valve degeneration and precipitate LV deterioration. Thus, this theoretical risk was emphasized during the SDM process. Support from the medical team to return to play was predicated on the athlete's compliance with careful long-term surveillance and the presence of an emergency action plan during all training and competition.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the challenge of differentiating EICR from pathologic LV dilation in athletes with AR. Reconciling this clinical dilemma requires acquisition of a detailed exercise training history and the integration of this history with imaging and exercise testing data.
