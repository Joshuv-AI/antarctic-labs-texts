Masculine Norm Adherence Among Australian Men: Latent Structure of the CMNI‐22 and Its Links With Mental Health Outcomes
Authors: Vincent O Mancini, Jack Brett, Jasleen Chhabra, James Clarke, Thomas P Nevill, David A Preece, Zac Seidler, Bradley Roberts, Sean Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIssue AddressedMasculine norms influence men's health and wellbeing. In Australia, interest in the role of these masculine norms resulted in an assessment of masculine norm adherence being included in the Ten to Men Study, measured using the 22‐item version of the Conformity to Masculine Norms Inventory (CMNI‐22). The CMNI‐22 has been previously treated as a unidimensional (i.e., single overall score) and multidimensional (i.e., 11‐factors) assessment. However, there remains limited evidence regarding the optimal way to use the CMNI‐22 data provided by more than 14 000 Australian men. Accordingly, this study aimed to (a) investigate the latent factor structure of the CMNI‐22, and (b) to compare the utility of unidimensional and multidimensional approaches to predict men's wellbeing over time.Methods and ResultsFactor analyses revealed that the CMNI‐22 is best understood as a multidimensional measure containing 11 factors. These dimensions often report better internal reliability than the total score. The multidimensional conceptualisation also explains larger proportions of variance in a range of mental health outcomes (e.g., depression, anxiety, psychological wellbeing) compared to the unidimensional one, highlighting different associations between specific dimensions and mental health outcomes.ConclusionsScholars and policymakers utilising the Ten to Men dataset (or the CMNI‐22) to understand links between masculinity and men's health will benefit from using this multidimensional conceptualisation instead of the unidimensional alternative. So What? When more comprehensive screening assessments are not possible, the CMNI‐22 appears to provide a brief, but reliable, multidimensional assessment of masculine norms. It is not advisable to use a single total score.Keywords: cohort study, factor analysis, masculine norms, masculinity, mental health

Background

Mental health outcomes in boys and men are influenced by beliefs and behaviours related to masculinity [1, 2]. In developed Western nations, dominant masculine norms are often typified by toughness, strength, control, hostility, the prioritisation of one's career, and the absence of vulnerability, sadness, and fear [3, 4]. Strong conformity to these attitudes and behaviours is associated with poorer mental health and social outcomes [1, 5], with men displaying these prototypically ‘masculine’ behaviours being less likely to seek emotional support, less willing to engage in mental health services, and more likely to develop suicidal thoughts and ideation [6, 7, 8].

The gender role norms model proposed by Mahalik, Locke [9] highlights how, for men, the pressure to conform to certain masculine norms leads to unhelpful or maladaptive behaviours (e.g., reduced help‐seeking, emotional restraint, propensity to take unnecessary risks, or the use of violence). Accordingly, these maladaptive beliefs are often targets of prevention and health promotion interventions aimed at boys and men [3, 10]. In their theoretical review, Thompson Jr. and Bennett [11] describe how much of the current knowledge about the association between masculine norms and men's health and wellbeing remains guided by ‘first‐generation’ conceptualisations of masculinity ideology derived from a traditional, North American, mostly‐white, and hegemonic construction. Whilst the emergence of more localised and context‐specific ‘second‐generation’ constructions have embraced the importance of different masculine beliefs and standards among individuals and various subgroups, these historical and dominant masculine norms nevertheless shape the experiences of those nested within this larger cultural setting [9]. Accordingly, this traditional hegemonic construction of masculinity, continues to shape the experiences of today's boys and men and thus persists as a worthwhile investigation.

In this study, we examine how one of these ‘first‐generation’ measures of masculine norm conformity is associated with the mental health and wellbeing of Australian men, drawing on data from a large, longitudinal cohort study. Currently, scholars have scored this same measure in different ways, without clear guidance on the potential advantages and limitations of either approach and, crucially, without knowing how this methodological choice may shape our understanding and response to the role of masculine norms in men's health and wellbeing. Thus, we seek to more closely interrogate the measurement of masculine norms as it has currently been applied to Australia's largest cohort study of male health.

The Conformity to Masculine Norms Inventory (CMNI)

The Conformity to Masculine Norms Inventory (CMNI) developed by Mahalik, Locke [9] is one of the most frequently used measures of masculinity‐related constructs [12, 13, 14]. In our opinion, there appear to be six primary reasons underpinning the popularity of this measure: (a) an impressive body of literature documenting thorough psychometric validation and validity studies, (b) a more granular assessment of masculinity across 11 dimensions (Winning, Emotional control, Risk‐taking, Violence, Power over women, Dominance, Playboy, Self‐reliance, Primacy of work, Heterosexual self‐presentation, and Pursuit of status), (c) the inclusion of items that assess both behaviours and feelings about masculinity norms, (d) the availability of brief versions of the original 94‐item instrument (e.g., a 55‐item, 46‐item, 30‐item, 29‐item and 22‐item version), and (e) translation into multiple languages [12, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Like most instruments, the CMNI does have limitations. The items assess beliefs and attitudes more than behaviours, do not consider the context in which these beliefs may vary, and have a deficit‐focused view of masculinity, with items derived from the perspective of college‐aged, white, North American adult males from the early 2000s. Moreover, the dimensions assessed do not embrace more recent strengths‐based developments within masculinity research that highlight certain masculine qualities that may be personally or socially beneficial, such as courage or a willingness to protect the vulnerable [20]. Therefore, despite its multidimensional construction, it would be remiss to refer to the CMNI‐22 (or any other available scale) as a comprehensive assessment of masculine norm conformity. Despite these limitations, an impressive and robust body of literature has seen the CMNI persist as the most common assessment of masculine norm conformity across Western nations.

Using the CMNI‐22 to Assess Masculine Norms of Australian Men

Domestically, the most extensive cohort study on male health is the Australian Longitudinal Study on Male Health, also known as the Ten to Men Study. Here, the CMNI‐22 was completed by ~14 000 Australian men at Wave 1 (in 2013–2014). Although not administered in later waves, this data remains the largest assessment of Australian men's masculine norms to date and has been treated as an early predictor of more recent mental health and wellbeing.

The significance of masculine norm conformity to the health and wellbeing of Australian men is not only recognised empirically but also reflected in governing policies such as the National Men's Health Strategy [10]. Indeed, achieving the vision of the Ten to Men study (to fill gaps in knowledge about why males continue to report poorer health outcomes) would be incomplete without a measure of masculinity. However, the depth to which masculine norms can be interrogated in this cohort study (and others like it) needs to be balanced against the demands of also needing to assess many other domains of functioning; the Ten to Men study assesses six broad areas of men's health and functioning, comprising hundreds of items that condensed into a ~40‐min survey. Accordingly, brief assessments of various constructs are often all that can be accommodated in this large, longitudinal, and population‐representative cohort study [21].

The CMNI‐22 (the briefest validated version of the measure at the time) was selected based on a balance of promising evidence base and brevity. Vitally, the recently validated 30‐item version of the scale by Levant, McDermott [18] was not yet developed when these Australian men completed the CMNI‐22. Whilst longer instruments may be the natural preference for scholars concerned with precision in measurement [22], we concede that this is not always compatible with policymakers' or participants' preferences. For example, even eight additional items (the difference in items between the 22‐item and 30‐item versions) would still require significant justification, especially as other measures may need to be removed to accommodate. Thus, the CMNI‐22 was arguably the most pragmatic measure of masculine norm conformity available to Australian researchers at the time, with this data persisting as the only population‐representative assessment of Australian men's masculine norm conformity. Researchers have continued to leverage the CMNI‐22 to understand how masculine norms intersect with depression, suicidality, help‐seeking behaviour, father‐son relationships, experiences of interpersonal violence, among a constellation of other policy‐relevant and health‐related outcomes [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28].

In the absence of clear, population‐relevant evidence on how the CMNI‐22 should be scored—either as a total score or its dimensions—scholars have used both approaches without clear guidance on the potential advantages (or consequences) of using one over the other. This is a key gap we seek to address.

Australian studies that have used the total score run into an important conceptual issue. Knowing that the CMNI‐22 captures 11 qualitatively (and statistically) distinct masculine dimensions suggests that two individuals can have identical total scores derived from entirely different dimensions, which likely produce differential impacts on health and wellbeing [1]. Australian studies that have used CMNI‐22 subscale scores may circumvent this conceptual issue but introduce a statistical issue. Though the 22 items comprising the CMNI‐22 were retained from the original version because they were statistically most strongly aligned with the underlying dimension, social scientists are generally encouraged to avoid two‐item scales of latent constructs in favour of longer versions to reduce measurement error [29]. Two‐indicator factors in models are also often under‐identified in factor analyses [30]. However, this issue may be mitigated when latent factors are correlated, enabling meaningful comparisons between models in which factors comprise two indicators each. In other fields, the use of two‐item scales of similarly complex latent constructs is a common practice, with the Ten‐Item Personality Inventory by Ehrhart, Ehrhart [31] being a popular example.

We also note that, at the time of this study, the Ten to Men dataset provides only the total score for the CMNI‐22, not subscale scores, which may shape expectations about how the scale should be used, especially among scholars from other fields who are interested in the intersection of masculine norms with men's health.

Rationale for the Present Study

Researchers interested in understanding the experience of masculine norms among Australian men, including what this means for later mental health and wellbeing, raise critical questions about how best to utilise responses to the CMNI‐22 collected from the Ten to Men Study cohort. Notwithstanding the availability of newer, similarly sized iterations of the CMNI [18], our focus remains on the 22‐item version for the reasons outlined earlier. Rather than contend with the question of which masculinity measure to use, our study explores how best to use the rich data that has already drawn significant investment, and that scholars and policymakers continue to draw upon as they consider the role of masculine norms in shaping the health and wellbeing of Australian men.

Study Aims

The overarching aim of this study is to explore how conformity to masculine norms, as measured by the CMNI‐22, is associated with the wellbeing of Australian men, both proximally and in the future. Our capacity to achieve this aim is undoubtedly influenced by how masculine norms are scored. Given the ambiguities regarding whether a total score on the CMNI‐22 is sufficient, or whether two‐item measures of 11 different masculine dimensions yield reliable and valid estimates, we first explored the latent structure of the CMNI‐22 in a large sample of Australian men. From there, we then explored the predictive utility (proximal and distal) of total and subscale‐level CMNI scores in predicting both negative (e.g., depression, anxiety, psychological distress, problem alcohol use abuse, loneliness) and positive mental health outcomes (e.g., subjective wellbeing, resilience).

Method

Participants

Study data are drawn from the Ten to Men Study. The cohort is described in detail by Pirkis, Currier [32]. The study sample comprised 14 695 members of the cohort who had completed the CMNI‐22 at Wave 1 (2013–2014). Participants were aged between 15 and 57 years (M = 36.66 years, SD = 11.68 years). Most participants were born in Australia (76.9%), the United Kingdom (4.4%), or Southern Asia (4.0%). All participants were fluent in the English language. The sample was generally well‐educated, with 54.8% of the sample having graduated high school (or equivalent), 11.5% having completed year 11 (or equivalent), and 19.2% having completed year 10 (or equivalent). These participants were included in the analysis used to investigate the first study aim.

The second aim used all available data for participants who had completed the corresponding mental health measures for each subsequent Wave of the Study. Participant attrition resulted in fewer available data points for each subsequent Wave. Wave 2 was administered during 2015–2016, Wave 3 during 2020–2021, and Wave 4 in 2022. The sample size for each analysis is reported as part of the results for each analysis.

Measures

The Conformity to Masculine Norms Inventory (CMNI‐22) by Mahalik, Locke [9] is a 22‐item self‐report abbreviated version of the original 94‐item CMNI measure designed to assess cognitive, behavioural, and affective conformity to dominant masculine ideology. The CMNI‐22 uses two items from the 11 subscales of the original measure: winning (e.g., winning isn't everything, it's the only thing), emotional control (e.g., I like to talk about my feelings [reverse scored]), risk‐taking (e.g., I enjoy taking risks), pursuit of status (e.g., I would hate to be important [reverse scored]), primacy of work (e.g., my work is the most important part of my life), violence (e.g., sometimes violent action is necessary), power over women (e.g., I love when men are in charge of women), dominance (e.g., I should be in charge), playboy (e.g., I would feel good if I had many sexual partners), self‐reliance (e.g., it bothers me when I have to ask for help), and heterosexual self‐presentation (e.g., it would be awful if someone thought I was gay). The items are rated on a four‐point scale ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 3 (strongly agree). To obtain an overall score, responses on all 11 subscales are summed, with the overall score ranging between 0 and 66, and higher scores indicating stronger conformity to masculine norms. As a total score, the CMNI‐22 has previously demonstrated internal reliability that falls slightly below the conventionally recommended cut‐off of 0.70 (α = 0.67) [19].

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9) by Kroenke, Spitzer [33] is a nine‐item self‐report measure used to assess depression severity. Items (e.g., Over the last two weeks, I have been feeling down, depressed, or hopeless) are rated on a four‐point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). To obtain a total score, all items are summed, with scores ranging from 0 to 27, and higher scores indicating higher depression severity. The PHQ‐9 has demonstrated excellent internal reliability with a Cronbach's α = 0.89 [33].

The Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD‐7) by Spitzer, Kroenke [34] is a seven‐item self‐report measure used to assess the frequency of symptoms associated with anxiety over the past 2 weeks. Items (e.g., Over the last two weeks, how often have you been worrying too much about different things?) are rated on a four‐point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). An overall score ranging between 0 and 21 is obtained after summing all items, with higher scores indicating a higher level of anxiety. The GAD‐7 has demonstrated excellent internal reliability in past research (α = 0.92) [34].

The Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (MOS‐SSS) by Sherbourne and Stewart [35], specifically, the emotional/informational support subscale of the measure was used for this study. The subscale is an eight‐item self‐reported measure used to capture emotional/informational support available to participants if required. Items (e.g., How often is someone available to give you good advice about a crisis?) are rated on a five‐point scale ranging from 1 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time). Scoring is done by averaging the scores for each item, and higher scores indicate higher availability of emotional/informational support. The MOS‐SSS has demonstrated excellent reliability in past research (α = 0.95) [36].

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) by Babor, Higgins‐Biddle [37] is a 10‐item self‐report screening tool to assess alcohol consumption, drinking behaviours, and alcohol‐related problems. Items (e.g., How often during the last year have you needed a first drink in the morning to get yourself going after a heavy drinking session?) are rated on a five‐point scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (daily or almost daily). An overall score between 0 and 40 is obtained after scores on all items are summed, with greater scores indicating more hazardous alcohol use. The AUDIT has demonstrated good internal reliability in previous studies (α = 0.81) [38].

The Personal Wellbeing Index (PWI) by the International Wellbeing Group [39] is a seven‐item self‐report measure used to assess satisfaction in seven domains: standard of living, health, achieving in life, relationships, safety, community‐connectedness, and future security. Items (e.g., How satisfied are you with your health?) are rated on an 11‐point scale ranging from 0 (no satisfaction at all) to 10 (completely satisfied), with a total score (0–70) obtained by averaging the scores on the seven domains, and higher scores indicating higher satisfaction across the domains. Within an Australian sample, the PWI has previously demonstrated good reliability (α = 0.81) [40].

The Self‐Determination Scale (SDS) by Ryan and Deci [41] is an eight‐item self‐report measure used to assess one's satisfaction across three subscales: autonomy (e.g., I feel like I am free to decide for myself how to live my life), competence (e.g., people I know tell me I am good at what I do), and relatedness (e.g., I get along with people I come into contact with). Items are rated on a seven‐point scale ranging from 1 (not true at all) to 7 (very true), with a total score obtained by averaging the items on the relevant subscale, and higher scores indicating higher individual satisfaction. Previously, the SDS has demonstrated good reliability (α = 0.85) [42].

The Connor‐Davidson Resilience Scale (CD‐RISC‐10) by Campbell‐Sills and Stein [43] is a 10‐item self‐report measure used to assess the psychological trait of resilience. Items (e.g., Coping with stress can strengthen me) are rated on a five‐point scale ranging from 0 (not true at all) to 4 (true nearly all of the time). A total score is calculated by summing all 10 items, with scores ranging from 0 to 40, and higher scores indicating higher resilience. The CD‐RISC has demonstrated good internal reliability in past work (α = 0.85) [43].

Procedure

The recruitment and sampling design of Ten to Men has been described in detail elsewhere [44]. Briefly, recruitment of the baseline sample occurred between October 2013 to September 2014, using a stratified, multi‐stage, cluster random sampling design [44]. Eligible participants for the full study were male, aged between 10 and 55 years, Australian permanent residents or citizens, and had sufficient English language skills to complete the survey. In total, fieldworkers approached 104 884 households in each of the selected Statistical Areas (including 33 724 dwellings housing 45 510 eligible men). Overall, 16 021 men were recruited into Ten to Men, for an in‐scope response rate of 35.2%.

Ten to Men is maintained by the Australian Institute of Family Studies (AIFS) who also maintain oversight of ethics, governance, and access requirements. The data is not public but can be accessed once approval is granted by AIFS. Our study follows the same procedure as all projects that wish to access this data. We first submitted a project application to AIFS outlining project aims and scope, before access to the data was granted, meaning that all hypotheses were a priori. Approval for the current project was granted in July 2024. Following approval, institutional confidentiality agreements were drafted and signed, ensuring that this anonymised data can only be accessed by those listed on the application, and that it is stored and handled in a manner that complies with institutional requirements.

Analytic Plan

Factor Structure of the CMNI‐22

To establish the factor structure of the CMNI‐22, we tested four theoretically‐informed models, comprising (a) a unidimensional model comprising a single latent factor with the 22‐items, (b) an 11‐factor correlated model (i.e., oblique) with factors corresponding to the 11 subscales of the CMNI‐22, (c) a hierarchical (or ‘higher order’) model with a general masculinity factor explaining the variance between the 11‐dimensions, and (d) a bifactor model with a general masculinity factor explaining additional item variance, above the 11 specific factors (visualised in Figure 1).

Visual representation of models tested as part of a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on data from the 22‐item Conformity to Masculine Norms Inventory using data obtained from Wave 1 of the Ten to Men Study.

The CFAs included all available CMNI‐22 data from the Ten to Men Study. To accommodate the non‐normal distribution pattern of responses for some items, the robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimation was performed. The ‘best’ fitting model was determined using a range of model fit statistics: (a) the chi‐square value adjusted for degrees of freedom, (b) comparative fit index (CFI), (c) Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI), (d) root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), (e) standardised root mean square residual (SRMR), (f) Akaike information criterion (AIC), and (g) Bayesian information criterion (adjusted for sample size; aBIC). The recommended cut‐off scores for each value are reported as part of table 1 and are derived from past recommendations [45]. Additionally, log‐likelihood tests were performed to examine whether parsimonious models provided significantly worse model fit. When parsimonious models do not reduce model fit, these models are preferred.

Interpretability of a Total Score and Internal Reliability

Model‐based indices of the bifactor model were calculated to examine whether a total conforming to masculinity score is tenable or whether the CMNI‐22's subscales should be favoured. Model‐based indices included explained common variance (ECV), Omega (ω), Omega Hierarchical (ω H), and Subscale Omega Hierarchical (ω HS). The interpretation of these indices was based on guidelines from the literature. The correlations between dimensions were also examined to establish whether a total masculinity norm adherence score is homogeneous. To visualise the heterogeneity of CMNI‐22 total scores, graphs were created to illustrate that individuals with the same total CMNI‐22 score exhibit vastly different levels of specific masculine norm adherence. Lastly, Spearman‐Brown coefficients were calculated to examine the internal consistency reliability of the two‐item subscales [22].

Predicting Mental Health‐Related Outcomes Using the CMNI‐22

A series of regression‐based analyses were performed to examine the utility of both total and dimension‐level scores on the CMNI‐22 (administered at Wave 1 [October 2013—July 2014]) in predicting a series of mental health‐related outcomes measured at Wave 2 (November 2015—May 2016), Wave 3 (July 2020—February 2021), and Wave 4 (August 2022—December 2022). All outcomes were treated as continuous data, representing either total or mean scores to multi‐item instruments assessing each construct. Negative mental health‐related outcomes were depression, anxiety, loneliness, and problem alcohol use. Positive mental health‐related outcomes were personal wellbeing, self‐determination and resilience. This study included all currently available observation periods for these mental health‐related outcomes, ranging between one observation (e.g., resilience at Wave 2 of the Ten to Men Study) to four observations (e.g., depression at all four waves of the study). As part of this process, correlations and descriptive statistics for all variables were generated.

All analyses in this study used a complete‐case analysis to address the study aims. Accordingly, missing values were not replaced or imputed using an estimation procedure, particularly given the large, sufficiently powered sample.

Results

Factor Structure of the CMNI‐22

The 11‐factor correlated model provided a significantly better fit, according to log‐likelihood tests and model fit indices than the unidimensional model (see Table 1). When benchmarked against the rule‐of‐thumb model fit statistics, the 11‐factor correlated model fit indices reported adequate RMSEA and SRMR values, whereas the CFI and TLI were close to acceptable fit. Each pair of items loaded meaningfully (i.e., standardised loading > 0.32) [46] onto their respected masculinity‐related dimension (Table 2). Extending on the first‐order model, the bifactor variant, including a general factor, provided greater model fit indices. Assessing the Interpretability of Total CMNI Scores

The general factor only explained 11% of the common variance of the CMNI‐22 items (ECV = 0.11). Contrastingly, all subscales explained over 80% of the common variance of the CMNI, except for Dominance, which explained 52%. While the total score provided acceptable reliability (ω = 0.70), this reliability was not driven by a general masculinity factor (ω H = 0.17). Indeed, the shared variance between the items was reflected in the subscales, rather than in the total score. Once accounting for the general factor, all subscales provided acceptable reliability based on Dueber and Toland's (2023) cut‐off for an interpretable subscale (i.e., ω HS ≥ 0.25; see Table 2). Many subscales, excluding Primacy of work, Dominance, Power over women, and Pursuit of status, provided acceptable reliability based on more conservative recommendations (i.e., ω HS ≥ 0.50) [47, 48]. Overall, while the bifactor model provided the best model fit, it appears this was due to the flexibility often afforded by bifactor models and not due to a true general factor [49].

In addition to the bifactor model indicating a lack of evidence for a true general factor, the 11‐factor CFA model suggested that the inter‐correlations of the CMNI subscales are markedly varied (Table 3). The variability in the associations between these factors suggests that the total CMNI‐22 score is not homogeneous. In other words, people with an identical overall score may have achieved this via endorsing very different dimensions of masculine norms. To visualise the heterogeneity caused by the different specific masculine norm subscales, we created plots indicating the subscale scores of participants with the same CMNI total score (i.e., 28; the median of our sample). Internal Reliability

The Spearman‐Brown coefficient (ρ) was calculated to examine the subscale's internal reliability. This coefficient offers an alternative approach that is perhaps better suited to two‐item scales (with a ρ above 0.50 suggestive of good internal reliability). Similar to ω HS, many subscales provide acceptable reliability based on ρ (see Table 2). However, the Primacy of work, Power over women, and Pursuit of status subscales fell below the conventional cut‐off. Based on model‐fit indices and ρ, the CMNI‐22 should not be used as unidimensional and is best represented as a multidimensional scale consisting of 11 two‐item scales demonstrating largely acceptable reliability. However, several scales demonstrated sub‐par reliability.

Discussion

This study compared two different methods of scoring the CMNI‐22 in Australian men, drawing on data from the nation's most extensive cohort study of men's health and wellbeing—the Ten to Men Study. We aimed to generate the prerequisite data to help researchers and policymakers decide whether to use a single total score derived from all 22 items or 11 separate scores derived from each pair of corresponding items [19]. Currently, there is an opportunity cost to either scoring approach. The use of a single total score helps to overcome methodological concerns with two‐item instruments and provides a simple way to describe masculinity at the expense of overlooking the highly important multidimensionality of masculine norm adherence. Deriving 11 scores from item pairs presents the inverse problem. Our results help resolve this ambiguity, producing three key findings that suggest the CMNI‐22 should be treated as a multidimensional instrument.

First, the 11‐factor model produced better fit than a single‐factor model, and that while a bifactor model provided greater model fit, model‐based indices indicated that the general factor was not tenable (e.g., poor reliability). Further, visualisations of total scores made up from the dimensions further highlighted the significant heterogeneity of masculine norm adherence not addressed by a total score. Second, the reliability of the total score fell below conventional limits; whilst most of the reliability estimates for the 11 dimensions were appropriate for screening purposes. Last, and perhaps most importantly, the multidimensional approach explained considerably larger degrees of variance in the mental health and wellbeing outcomes of Australian men. This was upheld longitudinally across all time points. Insights from the current study may help guide further use of the CMNI‐22 with the Ten to Men study as further data are collected, with some important considerations.

The CMNI‐22 Should Be Used as a Multidimensional Screener of Masculinity Norm Adherence, but With Some Considerations

Our findings are the first to provide comprehensive support for the use of the CMNI‐22 that assesses the multiple dimensions of masculine norm adherence using two items per dimension. We found that most of the CMNI‐22 dimensions showed adequate internal reliability when using suitable statistics for two‐item scales (i.e., Spearman‐Brown coefficient), which provides researchers with some level of reassurance—especially as each pair of items was taken from a larger pool of indicators of that dimension from the original measure [9, 19]. However, the Primacy of Work, Power over Women, and Pursuit of Status dimensions fell just short of conventionally acceptable limits for ρ. Lower levels of internal reliability also introduce higher rates of measurement error. It is, therefore, important to consider the potential attenuation of relationships between these dimensions and mental health outcomes, meaning that these relationships may be stronger than those we observed. Certain methodologies (e.g., structural equation modelling) enable controlling for measurement error, though it is seldom applied in earlier work enlisting the CMNI‐22 in the Ten to Men Study [23]. Nonetheless, the current study's regression analyses show that the dimensions provide considerably greater predictive validity than the total score.

We acknowledge that the more recently developed 30‐item version of the CMNI‐30 by Levant, McDermott [18] likely supersedes the CMNI‐22 (offering three items for each of the 10 dimensions assessed), especially for researchers who are yet to collect their data. However, this does not discount the importance of the current work, or other studies concerned with the CMNI‐22. The administration of this scale as part of the Ten to Men study remains the only large and population‐representative assessment of masculine norms among Australian men who have also been observed over multiple timepoints and thus retains its own unique value within the domestic men's health landscape.

The Dimensional Approach to Masculine Norms Offers Greater Predictive Validity for Mental Health‐Related Outcomes in Australian Men

The dimensional approach to the CMNI‐22 accounted for a substantially larger proportion of variance across all the mental health‐related outcomes assessed in this study and across all time points than the total score for the CMNI‐22. These findings, in conjunction with our exploration of the latent factor structure of the CMNI‐22, suggest that although CMNI‐22 total scores were often associated with mental health‐related outcomes for Australian men, the use of dimensional scores enabled greater predictive validity, even after considering the potential limitations of those dimensions which fell short of conventional cut‐offs for internal reliability.

A practical recommendation is, therefore, to encourage researchers to consider using dimension scores as an alternative. This could also involve ensuring that the data that is provided to approved users of the Ten to Men study also include subscale scores (and not only the CMNI total score), ideally supplemented with information about its scoring. This recommendation also stands to benefit policy and intervention recommendations. Identifying the specific dimensions that increase risk (or resilience) for poor mental health helps to develop targeted policy and intervention strategies. In contrast, links between ‘overall’ masculine norm adherence might not afford these types of specific opportunities (i.e., which aspect of masculine norm adherence might be responsible for increasing risk or resilience to a particular outcome). To aggregate these dimensions into a single total score is to assume that these dimensions report similarly correlated with specific mental health outcomes—which was not the case in our results, nor what has been established in prior meta‐analyses by Wong, Ho [1].

In line with this thinking about the need to disaggregate overall ‘masculinity’ into its subordinate dimensions, it is critical that we also recognise that there remain evolving and important developments concerning what exactly masculine dimensions are (and are not). The popularity of the CMNI should not be conflated with this being a gold standard, or absolute, measure of masculine norms. Instead, we recognise that this is a measure that assesses one's beliefs related to traditional and western dimensions of masculinity, designed with the original ambition to predict associations with psychopathology [9].

Since its inception, newer scales that have different theoretical and/or ideological underpinnings have compelled masculinities researchers to move away from deficit‐based constructions of masculine norms and to embrace ‘positive’ or ‘healthy’ masculinities frameworks. For example, qualities such as a willingness to stand up for those who are vulnerable represents a prototypically masculine quality that may have individual and social benefit [50]. Such qualities are gaining recognition as crucial components of men's masculine experiences that have not been given sufficient attention in studies that continue to prioritise longstanding, more deficit‐based, constructions. Our findings do not resolve this directly, though do highlight that there is more room to improve the measurement of masculinity to better understand how it impacts men's health in meaningful ways. We also postulate that the inclusion of these other dimensions of masculinity would help to considerably improve the predictive utility of masculine norm conformity beyond only those dimensions captured by the CMNI‐22, which is easily testable in future empirical studies.

Associations Between Specific Masculine Norms and Different Mental Health Outcomes Vary Considerably

Our findings are congruent with prior work that has revealed that certain masculine norms report stronger associations with certain mental health outcomes. For example, higher Risk‐taking scores were associated with lower rates of anxiety. This may manifest due to risk‐taking, potentially giving rise to lower rates of avoidance‐type behaviours that maintain anxiety. Yet at the same time, higher Risk‐taking was associated with increased alcohol use, consistent with past research [51]. This result reinforces the importance of an ‘outcome‐centered perspective’ to understanding masculinity and its intersection with health and wellbeing [1]; adherence to certain masculine norms, in certain contexts, may be adaptive and/or protective against some outcomes and a risk factor for others. This further promotes the need to consider masculine norm adherence as a multidimensional construct. Moreover, how masculine norm adherence shapes the relationship between these mental health outcomes, and for whom, is also a critical step for future development. For example, past research linking alcohol use and internalising psychopathology highlights the potential for masculine norms to be indirectly associated with certain outcomes via their effect on others [52].

Health Promotion Initiatives Will Benefit From the Dimensional Perspective

The complex concept of masculinity is well‐understood by scholars and policymakers familiar with it. However, in other jurisdictions, where masculinity is not the focus, this knowledge may be lacking. We acknowledge the importance of experts in masculinity working with those in other disciplines concerned with men's health, especially when crafting policy and initiatives. To highlight one example of this partnership in action within the contemporary Australian context, the Government of Western Australia's Sexual Violence Prevention and Response Strategy (2025–2035) has moved beyond overly simplistic attributions of traditional masculinity as a driver of sexual violence and instead singles out those traditional norms characterised by dominance, control, and aggression as specific drivers. We see this as a beneficial development for two key reasons. First, it provides clear and specific targets for intervention. Second, it can also challenge unhelpful and outdated perceptions that overall masculinity is inherently and automatically destructive to individual and community health and wellbeing, which contradict more recent developments that highlight the benefits of positive or strengths‐based constructions of masculinity [53, 54].

Previous research shows that boys and men often resist gender equality efforts when they see a threat to their masculine identity [55, 56]. The dimensional approach to masculinity, especially one that incorporates a strengths‐based focus, could reduce this threat by highlighting specific masculine traits that affect wellbeing without threatening their identity. Such approaches could be conceived as a ‘fine‐tuning’ rather than a ‘complete overhaul’ of one's masculine identity. That is to say that for masculinities‐enriching health promotion efforts to be effective, and to reach those men who need it most, it may be crucial that these efforts disentangle those dimensions that are adaptive (and when) from those dimensions that may be harmful in both the short and long‐term.

Within our study, the dimensions of Violence and Self‐reliance were often significant predictors of increased risk across different mental health‐related outcomes and across timepoints, further highlighting how we could maximise the potency of health promotion initiatives by targeting these dimensions that may play a central role in networks connecting masculine norms to psychosocial functioning. These findings, particularly on the relevance of Self‐reliance as a predictor of poorer outcomes, are broadly consistent with other Australian studies that have looked at different subgroups within the Ten to Men cohort [23, 25], and international studies [1], which remain a key priority target for policies and initiatives that can concurrently help to shift men's perceptions that they should navigate their trials and tribulations independently, or optimise the health care system so that gender‐responsive approaches can be taken to addressing persistent and complex health challenges [56].

Limitations and Directions for Future Research

One limitation of this study is that only the CMNI‐22 was administered at one time point, limiting our ability to understand the temporal stability of masculine norm conformity, and what experiences may cause these to shift. Research that contrasts CMNI‐22 scores against longer‐form versions within the same sample may help to further validate the scale, as would multiple assessments to identify potentially important variations in masculine norm conformity over time, and what this means for mental health and social functioning. Our work focused on the broad assessment of masculine norm conformity and mental health in a large sample of Australian men taken from a population‐representative cohort study. Future studies may wish to explore how the construction and adherence to masculine norms vary within subgroups within this population. These distinctions could be generational, cultural, or social. For example, research using this data has compared CMNI‐22 scores between Australian men with and without disabilities [57]. However, there exist many other boundaries where differences in conformity to these traditional western dimensions of masculinity could be postulated. This might include the experiences of boys and men who grow up in non‐western cultures (including First Nations groups), men who are not heterosexual or gender diverse (e.g., trans‐men), or men who experience certain life events that may ostensibly deviate from what is culturally or developmentally anticipated to shape one's masculine identity (e.g., growing up without a father‐figure). How these groups construct their own masculine identities within a broader cultural context that continues to value older and traditional western masculine values, is an area ripe for further development.

Where a strength of our study resides in its large‐scale and quantitative design, we also concede that an opportunity cost of such an endeavour is a loss of nuance at the level of subgroups and, ultimately, individuals boys and men navigating their masculine identities. As we visualise in Figure 2, conformity to masculine norms can vary greatly among individuals. This highlights the need to complement such quantitative work with qualitative, and ideally, consumer‐informed initiatives that will answer important and different questions that will advance the field in a way that can benefit boys, men, and their communities, by understanding how masculine norms shape their health and social functioning.

Reliance on self‐report may also introduce some potential biases, as people may conceal their actual attitudes with those they perceive as socially desirable. Relatedly, the CMNI does not readily capture the often‐dynamic experience of masculine norm adherence in daily life, nor does it capture behaviours. Understanding how men may moderate their expressions of masculinity to fulfil social demands (e.g., their behaviour in front of male versus female peers, or at work versus at home) will also require further methodological innovation. Last, as men will often contend with a range of masculine beliefs and ideals, person‐centered methodologies, such as latent class analyses, may also help to reveal whether distinct ‘profiles’ of masculine beliefs exist and how these profiles may differ in the experience of mental health and wellbeing, with one recently published study highlighting the advantages of this approach [58].

Conclusion

Our study found that the CMNI‐22 demonstrated adequate psychometric properties that were robustly associated with a range of mental health outcomes across four Waves of data. Compared to a single overall score, which exhibited limited validity and reliability, the multidimensional assessment of masculine norms demonstrated acceptable (or close to acceptable) reliability, explained considerably larger variability in men's mental health outcomes, and is more closely aligned with theoretical development that recognises the complexity of masculinity. When it is not possible to administer longer‐form assessments of the CMNI, the 22‐item version could be used as a multidimensional instrument. Calculating a single total score is not advised.
