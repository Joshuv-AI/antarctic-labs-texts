High-Flux Halloysite Nanotube-Reinforced PVDF Membranes with Zwitterionic Coating for One-Step Separation of Complex Oil–Water Emulsions
Authors: Mingyang Lin, Dongming Xia, Xinghe Li, Junming He, Zheng Chen, Hongyan Pan, Mingrui Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Industrial oily wastewater containing heavy metal ions threatens ecosystems and human health, and developing high-flux multifunctional membranes for their simultaneous removal is a critical challenge. Herein, a high-flux multifunctional polyvinylidene fluoride composite membrane, HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA, was fabricated by blending halloysite nanotubes (HNTs) into the PVDF matrix via nonsolvent-induced phase separation (NIPS), followed by surface modification with a polydopamine (PDA)/poly­(sulfobetaine methacrylate) (PSBMA) zwitterionic coating. HNTs introduced one-dimensional water channels via inner/outer surface hydroxyl groups to boost water flux and provide active sites for heavy metal adsorption, while the PDA/PSBMA coating formed a robust hydration layer through electrostatic interactions with water molecules, endowing excellent antifouling and underwater oleophobicity as well as additional heavy metal adsorption capacity. Under optimized conditions (1.0 wt % HNTs, 2.0 mg/mL PSBMA), the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane exhibited a drastically enhanced water flux of 528.6 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1 for oil-in-water emulsions, representing a 455.2% increase compared with the pristine PVDF membrane (95.2 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1), and achieved an oil–water separation efficiency of 97.8%. For gravity-driven one-step treatment of oil-in-water emulsions containing 10 ppm heavy metal ions, the membrane maintained an oil–water separation efficiency of 97.5% and achieved a Cu2+ removal efficiency of 83.3%, with superior chemical stability, cyclic reusability, and mechanical reinforcement. This work establishes a scalable strategy for high-flux multifunctional membrane fabrication to enable efficient complex oily wastewater treatment.

Introduction

Rapid industrialization and urbanization have led to frequent marine oil spills and the continuous generation of oily wastewater from human activities, posing serious threats to ecosystems and human health. The composition of such wastewater is typically highly complex and frequently contains various pollutants, including heavy metal ions, dyes, microorganisms, and organophosphorus compounds. − These complex wastewaters are characterized by diverse sources, high treatment difficulties, and slow degradation rates. Conventional oil–water separation techniques, such as gravity separation, combustion, adsorption, dissolved air flotation, and chemical coagulation, are hindered by high energy consumption, low separation efficiency, complex operational procedures, elevated maintenance costs, and the risk of secondary pollution when applied to such complex wastewater. − Therefore, the development of novel and high-efficiency oil–water separation technologies is an urgent imperative, and membrane separation technology has emerged as a highly promising strategy for this application, featuring prominent advantages such as high separation efficiency, facile operation, and low energy consumption. −

Membranes for oil–water separation are generally categorized into hydrophobic-oleophilic and hydrophilic-oleophobic types based on surface wettability. − Hydrophobic-oleophilic membranes allow oil to permeate while retaining water; for instance, incorporating carbon nanotubes, hydrophobically modified kaolin, or modified graphene oxide into electrospun nanofiber membranes has been shown to enhance oil permeation flux and separation efficiency. Such membranes also maintain stable performance under harsh conditions, although prolonged operation often leads to oil fouling and pore blockage, reducing reusability. , To address this, hydrophilic-oleophobic membranes inspired by fish-scale mucus have been developed via surface modification strategies. − Among various methods, surface coating stands out for its simplicity and low cost, enabling the deposition of hydrophilic agents such as hydrogels, zwitterions, tannic acid, and metal oxides. , For example, codeposition of dopamine with functional nanoparticles or grafting of zwitterionic polymers , has produced membranes with excellent antifouling, self-cleaning, and stability properties. Despite these advances, real-world oily wastewater (e.g., from petroleum, electroplating, and metallurgy) often contains heavy metal ions, , which cannot be removed by the above membranes, necessitating additional treatments. − Moreover, membrane fouling remains a challenge, increasing operational costs. Therefore, the development of multifunctional membranes that integrate high flux, antifouling capability, and simultaneous oil–water separation with heavy-metal-ion removal is of significant scientific and industrial importance.

Doping hydrophobic or hydrophilic materials into the membrane matrix is a common approach to preparing membranes with specific wetting properties. According to literature reports, the introduction of hydrophobic polyurethane (PU) microspheres into the membrane matrix imparts superoleophilicity to the membrane surface, with an oil contact angle of approximately 0°. Similarly, the incorporation of hollow porous hydroxyapatite (HAP) into the membrane matrix creates rapid transport channels for water molecules, thereby significantly enhancing the membrane permeation flux. Halloysite nanotubes (HNTs) are one-dimensional nanoscale clay minerals composed of aluminosilicate, featuring an inner octahedral alumina layer and an outer tetrahedral silica layer. − Compared with synthetic nanomaterials that often require complex and costly preparation, HNTs are derived from natural clay deposits, offering low cost, good compatibility with polymer matrices, and easy availability. They possess abundant hydroxyl groups that readily associate with water molecules, facilitating the formation of one-dimensional water channels. Furthermore, HNTs have a large specific surface area and a porous microstructure, which renders them highly efficient adsorbents for heavy metal ions in aqueous systems. Moreover, the excellent mechanical properties of HNTs enable their incorporation into membrane matrices to enhance the mechanical strength of the resultant composite membranes. These versatile properties make HNTs a broadly applicable multifunctional additive for the fabrication of high-performance membranes. The prominent heavy metal adsorption performance of HNTs has been well validated in numerous previous investigations. For instance, previous research has demonstrated that HNTs serve as efficient adsorbents for heavy metal ions in aqueous systems, exhibiting a lead ion adsorption capacity of over 24.3 mg/g. Zhang et al. incorporated HNTs into cement binders for the immobilization of lead ions (Pb2+) and observed a significant reduction in Pb2+ leaching from the HNT-containing specimens. This immobilization enhancement was ascribed to the one-dimensional nanochannels of HNTs, which facilitated the formation of hydrogel phases and a denser matrix structure. Additionally, the intrinsic Pb2+ adsorption capability of HNTs further reinforced the immobilization efficiency. Collectively, these investigations demonstrate that HNTs possess excellent hydrophilicity and heavy metal ion adsorption capacity. The incorporation of HNTs into polymeric membrane matrices can endow the membranes with heavy metal removal functionality, while their one-dimensional nanochannels facilitate rapid water molecule transport, thereby effectively enhancing membrane water permeability.

Zwitterionic polymers possess equivalent cationic and anionic groups within their molecular backbones, endowing them with capabilities that enable robust hydration with water molecules to form a dense hydration-layer barrier. This hydration barrier effectively repels the adsorption of oil, protein, and bacteria, thus conferring exceptional antifouling performance to modified materials. − Our research group previously developed a one-step polymerization and codeposition strategy, via which polydopamine (PDA) and poly­(sulfobetaine methacrylate) (PSBMA) were successfully immobilized on PVDF substrate membranes. This modification notably enhanced the antifouling properties and the hydrophilicity of the PVDF membrane. Moreover, the charged groups of PSBMA promoted the adsorption of heavy metal ions by the modified membrane, thereby enabling the one-step separation of oil-in-water emulsions containing metal ions. Razavi et al. incorporated zwitterionic PSBMA-modified GO nanosheets into a polyamide (PA) separation layer to prepare forward osmosis GO-g-PSBMA-modified TFN membranes, which exhibited superior hydrophilicity and antifouling properties. Cheng et al. fabricated oleophobic membranes via the zwitterionic PSBMA modification of membrane fibers, and the resultant membranes displayed a significantly enhanced underwater-oil contact angle, verifying their excellent oil resistance in both air and aqueous environments. Beyond separation applications, polymer-based hybrid membranes can be used to extend electrode lifetime and stability in electrochemical energy storage and conversion devices such as batteries and supercapacitors, as well as in emerging energy-related electrocatalytic systems, fully demonstrating their multifunctionality and great application potential. −

In this work, HNTs were blended into a PVDF casting solution to fabricate an HNTs/PVDF composite substrate membrane via NIPS, followed by codeposition of a PDA/PSBMA zwitterionic coating to obtain the multifunctional HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane. The incorporation of HNTs constructs one-dimensional water transport channels, which significantly boost the membrane flux and effectively compensate for the flux loss typically caused by surface coating modification. Meanwhile, the abundant hydroxyl groups on the inner and outer surfaces of HNTs provide abundant active sites for efficient heavy metal ion adsorption. After PDA/PSBMA coating, the charged groups of PSBMA form a dense hydration layer on the membrane surface that repels protein and oil droplet adhesion, thus endowing the membrane with excellent antifouling performance and high-efficiency oil–water separation. Furthermore, the negatively charged groups in the zwitterionic coating enable additional electrostatic interactions with heavy metal ions. The synergy between the HNTs-derived water channels and the PSBMA hydration layer not only preserves a high permeation flux but also ensures rapid and efficient gravity-driven one-step separation of oil-in-water emulsions containing heavy metal ions. As a result, the modified HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane achieves a high water flux of 528.6 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1 for oil-in-water emulsions (a 455.2% increase over pristine PVDF), an oil–water separation efficiency of 97.8%, and a Cu2+ removal efficiency of 83.3% under gravity-driven conditions. This study provides a facile and scalable fabrication strategy for advanced multifunctional separation membranes, holding great practical potential for the treatment of complex oily wastewater containing heavy metal contaminants.

Materials and Experimental Methods

Materials

Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) powder and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) were purchased from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Halloysite nanotubes (HNTs) were purchased from Guangdong Jinna New Materials Technology Co., Ltd., and N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc) was purchased from Chengdu Jinshan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Copper sulfate pentahydrate, tris­(hydroxymethyl)­aminomethane (Tris), hydrochloric acid, dopamine (DA), petroleum ether, and anhydrous ethanol were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sulfobetaine methacrylate (SBMA), lead nitrate, kerosene, n-hexadecane, and epoxidized soybean oil were purchased from Shanghai McLean Biochemical Co., Ltd.

Preparation of HNTs/PVDF Composite Membrane

HNTs/PVDF composite membranes were prepared by the phase inversion method as a substrate for subsequent modification. First, HNTs were dispersed in N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc) via ultrasonic treatment to ensure homogeneous dispersion. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and PVDF powders were then added to the HNTs/DMAc dispersion under stirring to prepare the casting dope, with the detailed formulation listed in Table S1. The resultant casting dope was heated to 70 °C and magnetically stirred for 8 h to achieve complete dissolution and uniform mixing, followed by vacuum degassing to eliminate entrapped air bubbles. The degassed casting dope was cast onto a clean glass plate using a film applicator, and a uniform liquid film with a thickness of 400 μm was formed via a doctor blade. The glass plate with the liquid film was immediately immersed in a deionized water coagulation bath to induce nonsolvent-induced phase separation. After phase separation, the as-formed HNTs/PVDF composite membrane was peeled off from the glass plate and soaked in deionized water for 24 h to remove residual organic solvent.

Preparation of HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA Membrane

For the surface zwitterionic coating, 3 g of Tris was dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water. While stirring, concentrated HCl was slowly added to adjust the pH to 8.5 by using a pH meter. After the pH stabilized, stirring was continued for 10–15 min to ensure uniformity. Then, 200 mg of dopamine (DA) was dissolved in the above Tris buffer to obtain a dopamine concentration of 2 mg/mL, and the preprepared HNTs/PVDF composite membrane was immersed in the DA solution and shaken for 2 h at room temperature. The membrane was then taken out and thoroughly rinsed with deionized water three times to remove unreacted DA. Finally, the DA-modified membrane was immersed in a 2 mg/mL poly­(sulfobetaine methacrylate) (PSBMA) aqueous solution and shaken for another 2 h to obtain the target HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane. The surface-modified membranes were denoted as Mn–HX-Z, while the unmodified HNTs/PVDF membranes and the pristine PVDF membrane were denoted as Mn–HX and M0, respectively (where n represents the membrane number, and X indicates the mass fraction of HNTs).

Membrane Characterization

The oil content was determined by an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (UV-5120), and the concentration of metal ions was determined by flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (AA-6880F).

Mechanical Properties Testing

A universal testing machine (MTS E45.105) was used to evaluate the mechanical properties of the modified membrane, especially its tensile strength, which revealed the ability of the membrane to resist deformation under tensile force.

Scanning Electron Microscope Testing

The PVDF membranes, before and after modification, were dried in a vacuum oven, then brittle fractured using liquid nitrogen, and sprayed with gold before testing. The surface of the membrane was characterized and observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU8010), and the surface elements were analyzed by combining EDS spectroscopy.

Atomic Force Microscope Testing

The surfaces of the membrane, before and after modification, were scanned by atomic force microscopy in tapping mode to evaluate the surface roughness of the membrane. The membranes were dried before being tested.

Infrared Spectroscopy Testing

The surface chemical composition of the membranes was analyzed using a Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (FTIR, WQF-520) with a scanning wavelength range of 4000–500 cm–1 and a scanning accuracy and number of scans of 4 cm–1 and 64 times, respectively. The membranes were dried before testing.

Contact Angle Testing

The surface contact angle of the membrane before and after modification was measured by a contact angle analyzer (DSA25S), and the change in hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity was characterized. The same sample was measured three times to obtain the average value. The membranes were dried before being tested.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy Testing

The surface chemical composition of the membrane before and after modification was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ULVAC-PHI). The membranes were dried before being tested.

Calculation of Porosity and Pore Size

The porosity of the membrane was measured using the dry-wet weight method. First, the membrane surface was thoroughly wiped with a tissue to remove surface water and then weighed as w 1. The membrane was then dried to remove water. After drying, the membrane was weighed as w 2. The porosity ε was calculated using the following formula: ε(%)=w1−w2A×Δd×ρw where A (cm2) is the membrane area, Δd (μm) is the membrane thickness, and ρ w (0.997 g/cm3) is the density of water at room temperature. rm=(2.9−1.75ε)×8μlJε×TMP where μ is the viscosity of water (8.9 × 10–4 Pa·s), l is the membrane thickness (400 μm), J is the water flux (L·m–2·h–1), and TMP is the applied pressure.

Water Permeability Test

In this work, a vacuum filtration device was used to measure the water permeability before and after membrane modification. The membrane is fixed in the center of the glass instrument, the circulating water pump is opened to discharge the air from the suction filter bottle, and when the vacuum value of the instrument is stable at 0.085 MPa, 50 mL of deionized water is poured into the instrument to record the time. To obtain reliable results, all error bars were derived from three parallel experiments. The water permeability of the membrane is calculated according to the following formula, , J=VAΔtp where J is the permeance flux (L·m–2·h–1·bar–1), V represents the volume of deionized water passing through the membrane, A corresponds to the effective area of the membrane (m2), Δt is the time taken for deionized water to pass through the membrane (h), and p is the penetration pressure (bar).

Antifouling Tests

For evaluating the fouling-resistance ability of the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane, bovine serum albumin was selected as a contaminant, and the flux recovery ratio (FRR) was calculated. The specific operation steps were as follows: the membrane was fixed in the filter device, the circulating vacuum pump was opened, and the air inside the bottle was extracted. After the vacuum value stabilized, 30 mL of deionized water was poured into the bottle, the time taken for the deionized water passing through the membrane was recorded, and the flux J 1 was calculated. After that, 30 mL of BSA solution containing 1 g/L was poured onto the membrane. After the BSA solution had completely passed the membrane was removed and washed with deionized water. Then, 30 mL of deionized water was filtered again, the time was recorded, and the flux J 2 was calculated. To ensure the reliability of the results, all error bars are derived from three parallel experiments. The formula for calculating the flux recovery ratio FRR was as follows. FRR(%)=J2J1×100% where J 1 (L·m–2·h–1·bar–1) and J 2 (L·m–2·h–1·bar–1) refer to the pure water permeability before and after the filtration of the feed BSA solution, respectively.

Preparation and Separation of Oil–Water Mixture and Oil-In-Water Emulsion

First, various oils (Petroleum ether, n-Hexane, Kerosene, and n-Hexadecane) were stained with Sudan III, and 10 mL of stained oil and 50 mL of deionized water were mixed to prepare an oil–water mixture. The membrane was fixed in the filtration device, and when the instrument pressure was stable at 0.085 MPa, the oil–water mixture was poured to measure its permeability and separation efficiency. In order to increase the authenticity and repeatability of experimental results, the fixed membrane in the filtration device was precompressed with deionized water for 30 min before oil–water separation. Oil (Petroleum ether, Epoxy soybean oil, Kerosene, and n-Hexadecane) was stained in advance with Sudan III. The dyed oil and water (1:50 volume ratio) were mixed, and 0.1 g of surfactant Tween-80 was added. Then, the oil-in-water emulsion was vigorously stirred for 6 h to obtain an oil-in-water emulsion. To ensure the reliability of the results, all error bars are derived from three parallel experiments. The prepared membrane was fixed on the dead-end filtration device, and the oil–water separation was carried out under gravity drive. The oil content of the filtrate at 507 nm was quantitatively monitored by ultraviolet spectrophotometer, and the oil–water separation efficiency was calculated according to the formula, R(%)=1 −C1C0×100% where C 0 is the absorbance of the stock solution, and C 1 is the absorbance of the filtrate.

Separation of Oil–Water Emulsion Containing Heavy Metal Ions

CuSO4·5H2O, Pb­(NO3)2 and FeCl3 were dissolved to 10 ppm with deionized water, respectively, to prepare heavy ion solutions. Different oils (petroleum ether, epoxy soybean oil, kerosene, and n-hexadecane) were stained in advance with Sudan III. The dyed oil was mixed with the heavy ion solution (1:50, volume ratio), and 0.1 g of surfactant Tween-80 was added and vigorously stirred for 6 h to obtain an oil–water emulsion containing heavy metal ions. To assess the adsorption capacity of the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane, 30 mL of heavy metal ion solution was poured into the separation device, and the filtrate was collected. To obtain reliable results, all error bars are derived from three parallel experiments. An ultraviolet spectrophotometer and flame atomic absorption spectroscopy were used to test the oil–water separation efficiency and adsorption rate of the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane.

Oil Resistance of Membrane

First, kerosene was dyed red with Sudan III for visualization. The pristine PVDF membrane and the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane were prewetted with deionized water, then immersed in the dyed kerosene for 5 min. Subsequently, the membranes were transferred into deionized water and soaked for another 1 min. After removal, the membrane surfaces were observed for oil adhesion.

Stability of the Membrane

After the vacuum pressure of the device was stabilized at 0.085 MPa, an oil–water mixture of petroleum ether and water was introduced into the filtration system. The permeate flux and separation efficiency were measured in real time. Upon completion of each separation cycle, the membrane was retrieved, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water, and then subjected to the next cycle. This procedure was repeated for ten consecutive cycles to evaluate the durability and reusability of the membrane. The entire cyclic process was performed in three independent parallel replicates to ensure the reliability of the membrane stability results.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA Membrane

The microstructural characterization results of HNTs are presented in Figure S1, which exhibit one-dimensional nanotubular morphologies with distinct hollow cavity structures that can provide one-dimensional nanochannels for water molecule transport. HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membranes were fabricated via a two-step process. First, HNTs were dispersed in DMAc via ultrasonic treatment to ensure homogeneous dispersion, followed by the addition of PVP and PVDF powders to obtain a casting solution. The microscopic morphologies of PVDF nanocomposite membranes were characterized by SEM and are shown in Figure a–c and Figure S2a–c. As clearly observed in Figure a and Figure S2a, the pristine PVDF membrane surface featured a hill-like undulating structure, with the membrane pore structure being poorly discernible. The cross-sectional morphologies of the four distinct membrane types were characterized by SEM, as shown in Figure d–f and Figure S2d–f. The pristine PVDF membrane exhibited an asymmetric cross-sectional structure with a sponge-like porous interior (Figure d and Figure S2d). In comparison, the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane displayed a more abundant and interconnected sponge-like porous structure, as presented in Figure e–f and Figure S2e–f. In addition, this enhanced porosity was attributed to the increased content of halloysite nanotubes within the membrane matrix. The EDS mapping results (Figure g) revealed a uniform distribution of Al and Si elements, confirming that HNTs were successfully incorporated into the membrane matrix and were evenly dispersed. This homogeneous dispersion provided one-dimensional water channels that facilitated the transport of water molecules. Additionally, the uniform distribution of C, F, N, O, and S elements indicated that PDA/PSBMA had been successfully coated on the surface of the HNTs/PVDF membrane.

The surface roughness of the membrane before and after modification was characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM), with the results displayed in Figure . The surface roughness of M3-H1.0-Z was 36.9 nm, which was significantly lower than that of M0 (47.9 nm). This shows that the addition of halloysite nanotubes and the coating of PSBMA on the membrane surface can improve the surface roughness of the membrane, which is consistent with the SEM characterization analysis. This reduction in the surface roughness can be attributed to two synergistic factors. First, the incorporation of HNTs filled the surface micropores and defects inherent in the pristine PVDF membrane, thereby reducing the initial topographical irregularities. Second, the subsequent PDA/PSBMA coating formed a uniform thin layer that further smoothed the residual surface features, resulting in an overall decrease in the surface roughness.

Additionally, the peak observed at 1700 cm–1 was attributed to the CO stretching vibration of ester groups, indicating that PSBMA was successfully coated onto the surface of the HNTs/PVDF membrane. The porosity and average pore size of a series of PVDF membranes were calculated using the relevant formulas, with the results presented in Table S3. Compared with the pristine PVDF membrane, the HNTs/PVDF composite membranes exhibited increased porosity and pore size upon HNTs incorporation. Furthermore, the porosity of the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membranes gradually increased with increasing HNTs content up to 1.0 wt %. This enhancement can be attributed to the abundant hydroxyl groups (−OH) on the HNTs surface, which accelerated the phase-inversion process during membrane formation, thereby promoting the formation of a more porous structure. However, when the HNTs content exceeded 1.0 wt %, the porosity showed a slight decrease. This decline is likely due to the increased solid content and the corresponding increase in casting solution viscosity, which retarded the phase-inversion kinetics and suppressed further pore development.

The Wettability of HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA Membranes

The surface hydrophilicity of a series of PVDF membranes was evaluated by water contact angle measurements, with the results presented in Figure a andb. The pristine PVDF membrane exhibited a contact angle of 83.3°, indicating its hydrophobic nature. Upon HNTs incorporation, the contact angle of the HNTs/PVDF membrane decreased significantly, reflecting enhanced hydrophilicity. This improvement is primarily attributed to the abundant hydroxyl groups (−OH) on the HNTs surface, which effectively reduced the surface energy of the membrane and promoted water spreading. Following PDA/PSBMA coating, the contact angle decreased further compared with the M2-H0.5-Z and M1-H0.5 membranes, indicating additional enhancement in surface hydrophilicity. Under optimized conditions (1.0 wt % HNTs and 2 mg/mL PSBMA), the M3-H1.0-Z achieved the lowest contact angle of 49.4°, demonstrating optimal hydrophilicity. From the SEM images of the fracture surface morphology (Figure S4), it can be observed that when the HNTs content exceeds 1.0 wt %, pronounced agglomeration occurs in the membrane. Trace amounts of Al and Si are detected in the EDS spectra, confirming the presence of HNTs in the membrane cross-section. This agglomeration phenomenon is attributed to the aggregation of the HNTs themselves, leading to an increased contact angle and reduced hydrophilicity. This is consistent with the data in Table S3, where the membrane reaches the highest porosity at 1.0 wt % HNTs; above this loading, the porosity decreases due to HNTs agglomeration. A decrease in porosity implies an increase in the proportion of closed pores or poorly interconnected pores, which increases pore tortuosity and reduces flux.

Subsequent surface grafting of PSBMA further strengthened the hydration layer via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, which facilitated water molecule transport and continuously enhanced the water flux. ,

Under the optimized conditions (M3-H1.0-Z), the water flux reached a maximum of 1580.4 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1. In contrast, excessive HNTs content led to agglomeration, which simultaneously reduced porosity and hydrophilicity, thereby decreasing the water flux. The decrease in water contact angle indicates a significant enhancement in surface hydrophilicity, which is expected to promote water permeation and reduce oil droplet adhesion. The abundant hydrophilic groups on the membrane surface, such as −OH, −NH–, and −SO3H, readily associate with water molecules via hydrogen bonding, forming a stable hydration layer. This hydration layer enables rapid water wetting and passage through the membrane pores, thereby improving the oil–water separation efficiency.

Oil–Water Separation Properties and Stability

The oil–water separation performance of the membranes before and after modification was evaluated using an oil-in-water emulsion, with the results presented in Figure a. The pristine PVDF membrane (M0) exhibited an oil–water emulsion permeation flux of 95.2 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1. All modified membranes showed higher fluxes than the pristine PVDF membrane, which can be attributed to the enhanced porosity and hydrophilicity resulting from HNTs incorporation. Notably, this increase in flux was achieved without compromising the oil–water separation efficiency. Furthermore, upon the introduction of the zwitterionic polymer PSBMA, both the permeation flux and oil–water separation efficiency were further improved. The M3-H1.0-Z membrane exhibited the best overall performance, achieving a permeation flux of 528.6 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1 and a separation efficiency of 97.8% for the oil-in-water emulsion. To further evaluate its separation capability, four different oil-in-water emulsions were tested, with the results presented in Figure b. The variation in permeation flux across different emulsions was attributed to the differences in oil droplet sizes. Despite these variations, the membrane maintained a separation efficiency exceeding 95% for all tested emulsions, with the highest separation efficiency of 97.8% achieved for the kerosene-in-water emulsion. This excellent performance can be explained by two synergistic factors. First, the hydrophilic groups on PSBMA readily associated with water molecules via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, accelerating water molecule transport through the membrane. Second, the hydration layer formed on the membrane surface served as a protective barrier against oil droplet adhesion, thereby enhancing the oil–water separation efficiency. To visually demonstrate the separation effectiveness of the membrane for oil-in-water emulsions, the microscopic morphology of a kerosene-in-water emulsion was characterized before and after separation, as shown in Figure e. The feed emulsion appeared as a turbid brown liquid with an average droplet size of approximately 11.95 μm. After separation, the collected filtrate was transparent and colorless. Notably, no emulsion droplets were observed in the filtrate, confirming the excellent oil–water separation performance of the M3-H1.0-Z membrane.

In addition, the service lifetime and reusability of the membrane were evaluated using oil–water mixtures with different volume ratios. The experimental results are shown in Figure c and Figure S5. After ten consecutive oil–water separation cycles, with the membrane being simply rinsed with deionized water after each cycle, the membrane maintained an oil–water separation efficiency exceeding 97% under oil-to-water volume ratios of 1:25, 1:50, and 1:100. These results demonstrate the excellent reusability and operational stability of the membrane. To evaluate its chemical corrosion resistance, the M3-H1.0-Z membrane was immersed in high-concentration acid (HCl, 0.1 M), alkali (NaOH, 0.1 M), and salt (NaCl, 0.1 M) solutions for 12 h at 24 °C. The changes in water flux and oil–water separation efficiency were subsequently investigated. As shown in Figure d, the membrane exhibited no significant attenuation in either flux or separation efficiency even after exposure to these harsh environments. Specifically, after soaking in HCl, NaOH, and NaCl solutions, the M3-H1.0-Z membrane maintained water permeation fluxes of 500 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1, 460 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1, and 430 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1, with corresponding oil–water separation efficiencies of 97.5%, 95.23%, and 95.74%, respectively. In addition, different concentrations of NaCl were added to the oil–water mixture to simulate seawater conditions. The experimental results are shown in Figure S6. When the NaCl concentrations were 15, 35, and 40 wt %, the oil–water separation efficiency of the membrane remained above 95%, while the water flux gradually decreased with increasing NaCl concentration. This decline is attributed to the increase in osmotic pressure caused by the added salt, as well as the enhanced concentration polarization effect, both of which reduce the effective driving force of the filtration process. These results demonstrate the excellent antifouling property and operational stability of the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane. Furthermore, the oil-fouling resistance of the membrane was evaluated by visual inspection, with the results presented in Figure S7. The pristine PVDF membrane and the M3-H1.0-Z membrane were immersed in dyed kerosene and subsequently rinsed with deionized water. As shown in Figure S7a, the pristine PVDF membrane retained a distinct red coloration after rinsing, indicating poor oil repellency due to its hydrophobic nature. In contrast, the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membrane returned to its original black color after simple deionized water rinsing (Figure S7b), demonstrating excellent oil-fouling resistance. The superior antifouling property conferred by the PDA/PSBMA coating is expected to prolong the membrane’s service life and reduce maintenance costs, highlighting its great potential for the separation and purification of complex oily wastewater systems. In summary, the HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA composite membrane exhibits significant promise for the treatment of complex oily wastewater.

One-Step Separation of Oil–Water Emulsion Containing Heavy Metal Ions

To evaluate the one-step separation capability of the M3-H1.0-Z membrane for complex oily wastewater, three types of oil-in-water emulsions containing different heavy metal ions (Pb2+, Cu2+, and Fe3+) at an initial concentration of 10 ppm were prepared. The separation experiments were conducted under gravity-driven conditions without any external force, with the results presented in Figure a. The presence of heavy metal ions did not compromise the oil–water separation efficiency of the modified membrane. For all three heavy-metal-ion-containing emulsions, the membrane maintained a separation efficiency exceeding 97.0%, demonstrating excellent oil–water separation performance and stability. Notably, the M3-H1.0-Z membrane also exhibited efficient adsorption of heavy metal ions during the one-step separation process. The adsorption efficiencies for Pb2+, Cu2+, and Fe3+ were 82.1%, 83.3%, and 68.6%, respectively. In addition, Cu2+ solutions at concentrations of 30 and 50 ppm, as well as a mixed heavy metal solution containing Pb2+, Cu2+, and Fe3+ each at 10 ppm, were prepared to evaluate the one-step separation performance of the membrane for complex oily wastewater under varying heavy metal ion concentrations and compositions. The results are shown in Figures S8 and S9, indicating that changes in heavy metal ion concentration and composition did not affect the one-step separation ability of the M3-H1.0-Z membrane. The membrane maintained an oil–water separation efficiency of 97%, and the average adsorption efficiency for the three heavy-metal ions in the mixed solution was approximately 70%. This dual functionality can be attributed to two synergistic mechanisms. First, the abundant hydroxyl groups on the halloysite nanotubes (HNTs) chelate with heavy metal ions (e.g., Pb2+, Cu2+, and Fe3+) present in the emulsion, providing effective adsorption sites. Second, the charged groups (−NH– and −SO3H) on the PDA/PSBMA zwitterionic coating adsorb additional heavy metal ions through electrostatic interactions. The XPS spectra of the membrane after adsorption (Figure S10) reveal characteristic peaks of Pb 4f, Cu 2p, and Fe 2p, further confirming the effective adsorption of heavy metal ions from the oil–water emulsion. The combined effect of these two mechanisms enables the simultaneous removal of heavy metal ions during oil–water separation. Meanwhile, the oil–water separation itself is governed by size exclusion and the hydrophilic/underwater oleophobic surface properties of the membrane, which allow for rapid water permeation while effectively repelling oil droplets. Consequently, the membrane acts as a filtration-adsorption hybrid system where physical separation and chemical adsorption occur in one step without requiring a separate adsorption unit. To compare the individual contributions of HNTs and PDA/PSBMA to heavy metal removal, an HNTs-only membrane (M1-H1.0) and a PDA/PSBMA-only membrane (M0-Z) were prepared. Their Cu2+ removal efficiencies were measured under identical conditions and compared with those of pristine PVDF (M0) and the full composite membrane (M1-H1.0-Z). As shown in Figure S11, the Cu2+ adsorption efficiency of the M1-H1.0 membrane was only 10.2%, while that of M0-Z was 62.3%, and that of M1-H1.0-Z was 83.3%. These results indicate that metal-ion adsorption relies predominantly on the PDA/PSBMA coating. Nevertheless, the removal efficiency of the full membrane exceeded the simple sum of the individual contributions, confirming a synergistic effect between the two components.

The Mechanical Strength, Antifouling, and Stability of Membranes

The mechanical properties of nanocomposite membranes are critical factors that influence their service life and resistance to harsh environments. In this study, the maximum tensile stress of the pristine PVDF membrane and a series of HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA membranes was evaluated using a universal testing machine, with the results presented in Figure a. All measurements were performed in three parallel replicates to ensure reliability. The tensile stress increased progressively with increasing HNTs content. When the HNTs content reached 1.0 wt %, the membrane exhibited a maximum tensile stress of approximately 1.8 MPa, which was significantly higher than that of the pristine PVDF membrane, indicating that the incorporation of HNTs enhanced the mechanical strength of the PVDF membrane. This trend is consistent with observations reported for carbon nanotube (CNT)-reinforced polymeric membranes, , where mechanical reinforcement is achieved through uniform nanofiller dispersion. The improvement can be attributed to the morphological similarity between HNTs and CNTs as one-dimensional tubular nanofillers. When uniformly dispersed within the polymer matrix, both types of nanotubes effectively guide stress distribution, prevent localized stress concentration, and dissipate impact energy, thereby inhibiting membrane rupture. However, when the HNTs content exceeded 1.0 wt %, agglomeration occurred within the membrane matrix, which weakened the stress dispersion capability and led to a decrease in the maximum tensile stress.

The antifouling property of the M3-H1.0-Z membrane was evaluated by using bovine serum albumin (BSA, 1 g/L) as a model protein foulant. The flux recovery ratios (FRRs) of the membrane over multiple filtration cycles are presented in Figure b. After the first cycle of BSA solution filtration followed by water washing, the pristine PVDF membrane exhibited an FRR of 31.6%, whereas the M3-H1.0-Z membrane achieved a significantly higher FRR of 85.4%. This improvement demonstrates that the incorporation of HNTs into the PVDF matrix and the subsequent PSBMA coating effectively enhance the protein fouling resistance of the membrane. The enhanced antifouling capability can be attributed to two synergistic factors. First, the abundant hydroxyl groups on HNTs readily bind to water molecules, forming a hydrophilic layer on the membrane surface that prevents BSA adsorption. Second, PSBMA combines with water molecules through electrostatic interactions to form a robust hydration barrier on the membrane surface, effectively blocking protein adsorption on both the membrane surface and within its pores. , In addition to the aforementioned passive antifouling strategies, PVDF possesses piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties due to its highly developed electrostatic charge. − Under low-pressure filtration conditions, the M3-H1.0-Z membrane undergoes dynamic deformation, which induces spontaneous dipole polarization and generates an internal electric field, creating a potential difference at the solution-membrane interface. This potential difference increases the negative charge density on the membrane surface and further stabilizes the hydration layer through hydrogen bonding with water molecules, thereby enhancing the hydration barrier and effectively improving the antifouling performance.

Conclusion

In summary, a multifunctional HNTs/PVDF@PDA/PSBMA composite membrane was successfully fabricated via a two-step strategy involving HNTs blending into the PVDF matrix by nonsolvent-induced phase separation and subsequent PDA/PSBMA zwitterionic coating modification. Under optimized conditions of 1.0 wt % HNTs and 2.0 mg/mL PSBMA, the M3-H1.0-Z membrane achieved a water contact angle of 49.4°, with significantly enhanced hydrophilicity, exhibiting a high flux of 528.6 L·m–2·h–1·bar–1 (455.2% higher than pristine PVDF) and an oil–water separation efficiency of 97.8%. Notably, the M3-H1.0-Z membrane realized gravity-driven one-step simultaneous separation of oil and heavy metal ions from complex emulsions with a 10 ppm concentration, maintaining 97.5% oil–water separation efficiency and achieving 83.3% Cu2+, 82.1% Pb2+, and 68.6% Fe3+ removal efficiencies, along with superior chemical stability, cyclic reusability, mechanical reinforcement, and antifouling performance. The outstanding comprehensive performance stems from the synergistic effects of HNTs and the PDA/PSBMA coating, and this facile and scalable fabrication strategy offers a promising paradigm for high-performance multifunctional membranes for industrial oily wastewater containing heavy metal contaminants.
