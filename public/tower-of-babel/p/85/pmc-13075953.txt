Mapping adaptive immune responses toward fungal antigens in inflammatory bowel disease using T cell repertoire sequencing and phage-immunoprecipitation sequencing
Authors: Aya K H Mahdy, Hesham ElAbd, Melanie Prinzensteiner, Hannah Jebens, Kostas Sivickis, Petra Bacher, Thomas Vogl, Mathilde Poyet, Andre Franke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsImmune reactivity to the gut mycobiome has been implicated in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), particularly Crohn’s disease (CD), but the systemic adaptive immune repertoire targeting the gut mycobiome remains incompletely defined. We aimed to map systemic T cell and antibody responses to fungal antigens in IBD.MethodsWe quantified expansion of fungal-specific T cell receptor β (TRB) clonotypes in the SPARC IBD cohort (CD, n = 1890; ulcerative colitis [UC], n = 914). In participants with paired data (n = 637), we analyzed the gut mycobiome by ITS2 sequencing and linked prevalent amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) to TRB clonotypes using a co-occurrence framework. For humoral immune profiling, we built a Saccharomyces cerevisiae-focused PhIP-Seq library and screened sera from CD (n = 100) and healthy controls (n = 60), including anti-S. cerevisiae antibody+ (ASCA+) and ASCA− individuals.ResultsFungal-specific TRB clonotypes showed greater expansion in CD than UC, including increased responses toward S. cerevisiae and enrichment in more aggressive CD phenotypes. Co-occurrence analysis identified a limited number of TRB–mycobiota associations, consistent with the largely private nature of the gut mycobiome at the ASV level. Antibody reactivity to S. cerevisiae peptides was low relative to common viral and bacterial antigen classes, and no single S. cerevisiae peptide was associated with anti-S. cerevisiae antibody serostatus.ConclusionsCD is associated with expansion of fungal-specific T cell clonotypes, but S. cerevisiae peptide-based PhIP-Seq reveals limited shared IgG reactivity. Broader antigen libraries and complementary assays capturing glycosylated and virulence-associated fungal epitopes will be needed to comprehensively define antifungal humoral immunity in IBD.Keywords: TCR-Seq, PhIP-Seq, ITS-2, host–mycobiome interaction, S. cerevisiae, T cell responses against teast, B cell responses against yeast, antibody response against yeast

1. Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is an immune-mediated inflammatory disease of the gut with multiple genetic and ­environmental risk factors.1–6 Although the exact etiology remains unknown, several aberrant immune responses have been identified in individuals with IBD, such as dysregulated responses to the gut microbiota7 and increased T helper 1 (Th1) responses to prevalent members of the gut mycobiome, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Candida albicans.8 Individuals with Crohn’s disease (CD), a subtype of IBD, have higher titers of anti-S. cerevisiae antibodies (ASCA)9 targeting mannoproteins expressed in the cell wall of many fungi.10 Several studies have reported alterations in the composition of the gut mycobiome in individuals with IBD10–12; however, the immunological consequences of these alterations remain poorly understood.

The last decade has seen the maturation of multiple high-throughput profiling assays including T cell repertoire sequencing (TCR-Seq)13 and phage-immunoprecipitation sequencing (PhIP-Seq).14 TCR-Seq enables sequencing of T cell receptor chains present in a sample, for example the α (TRA) or the β (TRB) repertoire. TCR-Seq does not reveal the antigenic specificity of the identified TRA or TRB chains, that is, clonotypes. However, large-scale association studies across hundreds of individuals have identified hundreds of clonotypes that are associated with different antigenic exposures, for example, cytomegalovirus (CMV),15 Lyme disease,16 and SARS-CoV-2.17 Beyond infectious diseases, large-scale TCR-Seq studies have identified clonotypes involved in different immune-mediated inflammatory diseases,18 such as primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC),19 IBD,20–22 and type 1 diabetes.23

In contrast, PhIP-Seq can identify the antigens bound by serum antibodies in thousands of individuals but does not identify the DNA sequences encoding these antibodies. Conceptually, PhIP-Seq consists of several steps.24 First, a library of peptide antigens is designed, and the peptide sequences are reverse-translated in silico into DNA sequences that can be synthesized at high throughput. These sequences are then cloned in-frame into a suitable bacteriophage, for example, the T7 or the M13 bacteriophage, and the resulting phage library is incubated with sera from study participants. Bacteriophages displaying antigens recognized by antibodies in a given serum sample are subsequently enriched by immunoprecipitation ­targeting a specific antibody isotype (eg, IgG), while unbound bacteriophages are washed away. Finally, the inserted DNA sequences from antibody-bound bacteriophages are PCR-amplified and sequenced to identify the antigens recognized by antibodies in each sample.

PhIP-Seq has been used to infer antigenic-exposure histories at the population level,14,25 to map immune responses to infections,26 and to identify antigens involved in the pathogenesis of different diseases.19,27,28 Using both technologies, that is, TCR-Seq and PhIP-Seq, we aim to map adaptive immune responses to the gut mycobiome antigens, particularly in individuals with IBD. Specifically, we (1) examined the expansion of a set of fungal-specific TCRs previously identified, in a large US-based cohort of 2804 individuals with IBD; (2) analyzed the gut mycobiome (profiled by ITS2 sequencing) and the T cell repertoire (profiled by TCR-Seq) from the same individuals (n = 637) and integrated these data layers using a statistical co-occurrence framework; and (3) generated a PhIP-Seq antigen library comprising hundreds of S. cerevisiae proteins to quantify humoral responses to fungal antigens in individuals with and without IBD and to identify fungal linear epitopes driving prevalent immune responses.

2. Methods

2.1. Cohort description and ethical approval

Multiple cohorts were utilized in the current study. For analyses of anti-fungal TRB clonotypes previously identified by Martini et al.,8 we used data from the SPARC IBD cohort,29 a multi-centered US-based cohort maintained by the IBD Plexus research program of the Crohn’s & Colitis Foundation. Detailed cohort demographics and clinical characteristics have been reported previously.22,29 For ITS2 measurements, processed datasets were obtained from the Crohn’s & Colitis Foundation. For humoral immune-response analyses, we included a Crohn’s disease (CD) cohort (n = 100; comprising ASCA+ and ASCA− individuals) and a population control cohort (n = 60). The CD and control cohorts were frequency-matched for age and sex. All study procedures were approved by the Ethics committee Kiel (ethical votes number: 427/19 and D441/16). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, and data were analyzed in a de-identified manner.

2.2. Profiling and processing the TRB repertoire

The TRB repertoire of the SPARC IBD cohort was profiled from peripheral blood DNA (up to 18 μg per sample) using the immunoSEQ assay (Adaptive Biotechnologies).17,30 Following clonotype calling, non-productive rearrangements (ie, sequences containing stop codons or frameshifts that cannot encode a functional TRB chain) were removed. TRB sequences were then collapsed by identical V and J gene calls and identical complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) amino acid sequences, and their abundances were summed.

2.3. HLA restriction inference for fungal-specific TRB clonotypes

We inferred HLA restriction of fungal-specific TRB clonotypes using our previously published HLA-TRB association map,31 which links public TRB clonotypes to HLA alleles based on large-scale population data with paired HLA genotypes and bulk TRB repertoires. This resource was assembled from 5554 individuals and included cohorts from Germany and the USA. To discover TRB clonotypes associated with common HLA alleles (minor allele frequency [MAF] > 0.05), we used a statistical co-occurrence framework. Briefly, for each TRB clonotype, we tested whether clonotype carriage was enriched among carriers of a given HLA allele compared with non-carriers using Fisher’s exact test (FET). A clonotype was considered HLA-restricted if its FET P value was <1 × 10−4. Clonotypes were classified as “single-allele” restricted when they were significantly associated with only one allele, and as multi-allele associated when they were significantly associated with two or more alleles.

Although we attempted to account for linkage disequilibrium (LD) using L1-regularized logistic regression (L1LR),31,32 for some TRB clonotypes it was not possible to resolve haplotype-level associations to a single allele. When overlapping fungal-specific TRB clonotypes with the HLA-TRB association map,31 matches were defined by identical V and J gene calls and exact CDR3 amino acid sequence identity between the query TRB clonotype, that is, the fungal-specific TRB clonotypes, and the reference dataset.

2.4. Profiling the gut mycobiome using ITS2

All methods related to ITS2 sequencing and primary processing were obtained from DiversigenSM. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAgen MagAttract PowerSoil kit (cat. no. 27000-4-EP) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ITS2 region was amplified using the Invitrogen AccuPrime High Fidelity kit (cat. no. 12346094). Each PCR contained 10.05 μL nuclease-free water, 2 μL 10× reaction buffer, 0.15 μL AccuPrime Taq, 0.8 μL BSA, 5 μL template DNA, and 2 μL primer mix (4 μm). The target-specific primer sequences were 5′-GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC-3′ (ITS3F) and 5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′ (ITS4). Primers also contained adapter sequences and single-end barcodes (proprietary to DiversigenSM). PCR was performed with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 56°C for 45 s, and 72°C for 90 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (cat. no. 28104), and DNA yield was quantified using the Invitrogen Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA assay kit (cat. no. P7589). Amplicons were pooled and cleaned using the Invitrogen ChargeSwitch kit. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq instrument using a 2× 300 bp paired-end protocol.

Read pairs were demultiplexed based on their unique molecular barcodes, denoised, and merged using DADA2,33 and chimera removal was performed using VSEARCH.34 ITS2 sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity. Taxonomic assignment was performed using a scikit-learn classifier and an optimized, variable-region-specific version of the UNITE database.35 Following taxonomic assignment, an OTU table was generated and rarefied for downstream analyses.

2.5. Discovering mycobiota-associated TRB clonotypes

We focused this analysis on individuals from the SPARC IBD cohort with both TRB repertoire sequencing and ITS2 measurements (n = 637). Across these individuals, a total of 769 samples were available. Because SPARC IBD is a prospective study and individuals may contribute repeated samples, we aggregated measurements at the individual level for co-occurrence analysis: for individuals with multiple samples, mycobiome carrier status was defined using a union-rule (ie, an ASV was considered present if observed in any sample from that individual). We used presence/absence rather than abundance because the analysis relied on co-occurrence of carriership. We first identified prevalent members of the gut mycobiome, defined as ASVs with prevalence between 5% and 95% of the cohort. Only 35 ASVs met this criterion, reflecting the private and dynamic nature of the gut mycobiome. For each ASV, we split the cohort into carriers and non-carriers and applied a one-sided FET to identify TRB clonotypes enriched among carriers.15 We controlled for multiple testing using a Bonferroni correction across the 35 ASVs; thus, a clonotype was considered associated with a given ASV if P <2.8 × 10−6 (ie, 1 × 10−4 divided by 35).

2.6. Designing a novel library covering the proteome of S. cerevisiae

To construct a library of candidate S. cerevisiae antigens, we downloaded the S. cerevisiae pan-proteome from UniProt36 and selected proteins annotated as secreted or surface membrane-associated. Protein sequences were then in silico digested into overlapping peptides using a sliding-window approach (64 amino acids, 20-amino-acid overlap). We additionally included positive-control antigens from common viral pathogens (mumps, measles, rubella, Epstein–Barr virus [EBV], and CMV), as well as negative controls (including human proteins, non-human pathogens, and previously used negative controls14). All positive- and negative-control proteomes were combined with the S. cerevisiae protein set and digested using the same sliding-window procedure. To reduce redundancy, peptide sequences were clustered using CD-HIT,37 and representative peptides were reverse-translated in silico into DNA. During reverse translation, codon usage was optimized for expression in Escherichia coli and EcoRI and HindIII restriction sites were removed. Adapter sequences were then appended to each oligonucleotide (prefix: GATGCGCCGTGGGAATTCT; suffix: TGAAAGCTTGCCACCCGAC), yielding 230-nt inserts. The final set of ∼15 000 oligonucleotides was synthesized commercially using Agilent SureDesign.

2.7. Cloning the S. cerevisiae-focused library and PhIP-Seq

After synthesis, the oligonucleotide pool was PCR-amplified according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, 1 μg of the amplified library was digested with EcoRI and HindIII (FastDigest; Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The digestion product was purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter) to remove fragments <150 bp, salts, and residual enzymes. The purified insert (202 bp, with cohesive ends) was cloned into the T7 Select 10-3b vector using the T7Select®10-3 Cloning Kit (Merck Millipore; product no. 70550). Following 16 h of ligation, recombinant phage particles were assembled by in vitro packaging using the T7Select® Packaging Kit (Merck Millipore; product no. 70014). Packaged T7 phages were amplified in liquid culture and titrated to estimate library yield, followed by plate-based amplification to generate a high-titer working stock. The resulting yeast library was then combined with previously described libraries14,26,38 to generate the final input phage library, and PhIP-Seq was performed on the combined library as described previously.14

2.8. Identifying antibody-bound antigens in PhIP-Seq

After sequencing and demultiplexing, reads were down-sampled to 1 250 000 per sample using seqtk. Reads were aligned to the reference oligonucleotide library using Bowtie (v.1.0).39 To generate an oligo-by-sample count matrix, we retained only paired-end reads that aligned perfectly (no mismatches) to a single oligo.

To identify antibody-enriched oligos, we modeled oligo read counts using a generalized Poisson distribution parameterized by a rate (λ) and dispersion (θ):

Model parameters were estimated using library “input” counts and mock immunoprecipitations performed on the same input library without serum (ie, no antibody enrichment).14 This approach accounts for oligo representation in each phage production batch and provides an expected background (null) distribution for each oligo in the absence of antibody binding. For each oligo in each sample, we computed a one-sided P-value representing the probability of observing a count at least as large as that observed under the null model; smaller P-values indicate greater evidence of antibody-mediated enrichment. We called an oligo a positive antibody “hit” in a sample if it passed a Bonferroni-corrected threshold controlling the family-wise error rate at α = 0.05 across 352 858 peptides (P < 1.4 × 10−7). We defined antibody responses as private if a hit was observed in only one individual and public if the same hit was observed in two or more individuals.

2.9. Differential enrichment analysis of PhIP-Seq counts using a DESeq2-like model

We performed differential enrichment testing directly on the antigen-level PhIP-Seq count matrix using PyDESeq2 (a DESeq2-like negative binomial framework).40,41 Antigen-level read counts were generated as described above. Prior to modeling, peptides derived from SARS-CoV-2 were excluded due to differences in cohort collection. Low-signal peptides were removed by filtering on total abundance across the dataset (sum of counts across all samples ≥10). The remaining counts were modeled using a negative binomial generalized linear model with diagnosis (CD vs healthy control) as the design factor. PyDESeq241 was used to estimate size factors, dispersions, and log2 fold changes and to test for differential enrichment between groups using Wald statistics; P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Peptides were considered differentially enriched if they satisfied Padj < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| > 1.

3. Results

3.1. Individuals with CD have elevated T cell responses against dominant gut mycobiota

Expanded anti-fungal CD4+ T cells in individuals with CD, particularly cross-reactive clonotypes recognizing C. albicans, C. tropicalis, and S. cerevisiae, were previously described by Martini et al.8 In that study, T cells from individuals with and without CD were stimulated with fungal lysates, activated CD4+ T cells were sorted, and single-cell TCR sequencing together with single-cell RNA-Seq was performed to obtain paired TCR sequences and transcriptional phenotypes. Martini et al.8 identified 18 303 unique anti-fungal TRB clonotypes from six donors (three with CD and three controls). Because these clonotypes were derived from a small number of individuals, we next asked whether their expansion could be detected at the population level. To this end, we quantified the expansion of these fungal-specific clonotypes in the SPARC IBD cohort (“A Study of a Prospective Adult Research Cohort with Inflammatory Bowel Disease”), maintained by the Crohn’s & Colitis Foundation.29 This cohort includes bulk TRB repertoire sequencing from 1890 individuals with CD and 914 individuals with UC, together with detailed clinical metadata (Figure 1A).22,29

Of the 18 303 clonotypes reported by Martini et al.,8 5335 were detected in the SPARC IBD cohort. Of these, 3839 were detected in both CD and UC, whereas 1144 were detected only in CD and 348 only in UC. Across clonotypes, the median number of carriers was nine (mean, 42.3), and the most frequent carrier count was one. Notably, 937 clonotypes were observed in only a single individual in the SPARC IBD cohort, whereas 387 clonotypes were detected in >5% of the cohort (ie, ≥141 of 2804 individuals). The highest carriership frequency for any clonotype was 60% (1713 participants). Consistent with an elevated anti-fungal response in CD, the expansion of fungal-specific TRB clonotypes was higher in individuals with CD than in those with UC (Figure 1B). This pattern was observed for clonotypes specific to each of the three fungal species previously investigated, C. albicans, C. tropicalis, and S. cerevisiae (Figure 1C). Expansion was not significantly associated with age (Figure S1) or years since diagnosis (Figure S2).

Next, we focused on Th1 cytotoxic responses, defined as clonotypes from Martini et al.8 annotated with a Th1 phenotype, because this subset was previously shown to be expanded in individuals with CD.8 Quantifying the expansion of these clonotypes in the SPARC IBD cohort revealed increased responses to S. cerevisiae in individuals with CD compared with those with UC (Figure 1D). Within the S. cerevisiae-specific Th1 subset, expansion was not significantly associated with disease location (Figure 1E) but was associated with disease behavior (Figure 1F): individuals with penetrating disease exhibited higher expansion of anti-S. cerevisiae responses. Expansion was also associated with perianal disease (Figure 1G), with individuals with perianal disease showing higher anti-S. cerevisiae Th1 responses. Together, these results indicate elevated T cell responses to fungi in individuals with CD relative to those with UC, with the greatest expansion observed in individuals with more aggressive disease phenotypes, such as penetrating behavior. However, because the SPARC IBD cohort lacks healthy controls, these analyses cannot determine whether clonotypes are disease-specific; rather, they quantify differential expansion within IBD. Lastly, a subset of these fungal-specific clonotypes overlapped with CD-associated clonotypes previously described by Pesesky et al.20 and ElAbd et al.,22 suggesting that while most IBD-associated clonotypes are not necessarily fungus-directed, a fraction may reflect anti-fungal reactivity.

3.2. Differential presentation by fungal antigens in individuals with CD relative to healthy controls

To gain insight into the HLA restriction of these clonotypes, we leveraged our previously published dataset linking HLA alleles to restricted TRB clonotypes.30 Of the 18 303 unique TRB clonotypes reported by Martini et al.8 (derived from three individuals with CD and three healthy controls), we were able to resolve the HLA restriction for 807 clonotypes. Among these, 584 (72.3%) were associated with a single HLA allele, whereas 223 (27.7%) were associated with multiple alleles, most of which occur on the same haplotype, consistent with LD among HLA loci.

Across clonotypes from both CD and control donors, the most frequent restriction was to HLA-DRB1*04:01 (Figure S3A). When stratified by donor group, clonotypes derived from individuals with CD were most frequently restricted to HLA-DQA1*01:01-DQB1*05:01 (Figure S3B), whereas clonotypes derived from healthy controls were most frequently restricted to HLA-DRB1*04:01 (Figure S3C). Additional clonotypes were associated with other alleles, including the CD-enriched risk allele HLA-DRB1*07:01,4 which is common in individuals of European ancestry. Some associations also involved alleles that frequently co-occur with risk alleles on the same haplotype, such as clonotypes associated with HLA-DRB1*03:01 and HLA-B*08:01, which are part of the ancestral 8.1 haplotype. Together, these observations highlight a key limitation of in silico HLA-restriction deconvolution in the presence of strong LD, where signals may reflect haplotype-level rather than allele-specific associations.

Although these results suggest differential antigen presentation between individuals with CD and healthy controls, they should be interpreted cautiously. HLA restriction was inferred in silico from population-level HLA-TRB associations and does not constitute experimental validation of peptide–HLA binding or TCR specificity. Moreover, because the fungal-specific clonotypes were derived from only six donors in the Martini et al.8 dataset (three CD and three controls), group comparisons are underpowered and may be confounded by ancestry and broader HLA genetic background. We therefore view these observations as hypothesis-generating, and larger cohorts containing fungal-specific clonotypes will be required to more definitively assess differences in fungal antigen presentation.

3.3. Mapping the interaction between the gut mycobiome and the TRB repertoire using statistical co-occurrence analysis

Motivated by these findings, we sought to map interactions between the gut mycobiome and the host TRB repertoire by testing for statistical co-occurrence. We analyzed 637 individuals in the SPARC IBD cohort29 with both ITS2 sequencing-based mycobiome profiles and bulk TRB repertoires (Methods). Because SPARC IBD is an ongoing prospective study, ITS2 and TRB samples were not necessarily collected at the same time point, and some individuals contributed repeated samples (769 total samples). Most individuals contributed a single sample (543/637), whereas 94/637 contributed two or more samples; among those, the median (and mode) number of samples was two, only 24 individuals contributed three or more samples, and the maximum was seven (one individual). To enable an individual-level co-occurrence analysis, we aggregated repeated measurements per individual using a union-based presence/absence profile (Methods) and then applied a variant of the framework developed by Emerson et al.15 to identify TRB clonotypes associated with prevalent gut mycobiota (Figure 2A).

Most mycobiome members were private, with only 35 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) out of 3036 detected in ≥5% of samples (Figure 2B). Using a stringent Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (P <2.8 × 10−6), we identified 31 TRB clonotypes significantly associated with these 35 ASVs. Taxonomic assignment of the associated ASVs indicated correspondence to diverse taxa, including members of the kingdom Plantae, C. albicans, Cladosporium, and Saccharomyces (Figure 2C). Because these associations are based on statistical co-occurrence, they should be interpreted as correlation-based links between clonotype and ASV carriership and do not demonstrate that the implicated TRB clonotypes recognize fungal antigens. Together, these results suggest that the gut mycobiome yields relatively few robust, population-level TRB co-occurrence associations compared with strongly immunogenic bacterial or viral exposures (eg, CMV,15 SARS-CoV-2,17 and Borrelia burgdorferi16).

3.4. Mapping antibody responses toward S. cerevisiae in healthy controls and individuals with CD using PhIP-Seq

Whereas we detected increased anti-fungal clonotypes in the T cell repertoire of individuals with CD, prior PhIP-Seq studies and serological studies have primarily reported elevated responses to herpesviruses43 and bacterial flagellins in CD.28 This discrepancy may partly reflect limited representation of fungal antigens in existing PhIP-Seq libraries. To address this, we designed a phage display library comprising >12 000 peptides derived from 774 S. cerevisiae proteins (Methods). Proteins were enriched for those annotated as secreted or membrane-associated, as these are more likely to be accessible targets of antibody binding. Using this library, we aimed to map the dominant S. cerevisiae peptide antigens recognized in CD. We also included the full proteomes of common viral pathogens, including EBV and CMV, as well as vaccine antigens (eg, poliovirus vaccine) to contextualize the overall immunogenicity of S. cerevisiae relative to well-characterized antigenic exposures (Figure S4). The S. cerevisiae library was combined with our previously described libraries14,26,38 and used to screen sera from 100 individuals with CD and 60 healthy controls (Figure 3A).

We observed no significant difference in the total number of antibody-bound antigens between individuals with CD and healthy controls after inclusion of the S. cerevisiae library (Figure 3B, C). Focusing specifically on S. cerevisiae antigens, most responses were private, with limited sharing across individuals in either group (Figure 3D, E). When considering the full library, antibody responses to flagellin antigens were significantly elevated in individuals with CD relative to healthy controls (Figure 3F). In contrast, restricting the analysis to S. cerevisiae antigens revealed no significant group-level differences (Figure 3G). Together, these results suggest that antibody responses to S. cerevisiae peptides constitute a smaller component of the altered humoral landscape in CD compared with the prominent anti-flagellin signal.

As a complementary analysis, we performed differential enrichment testing directly on the antigen-level count matrix using a DESeq2-like40 negative binomial model implemented in PyDESeq241 (Methods). Consistent with the prevalence-based analyses, this count-based approach identified a limited set of differentially enriched peptides between CD and healthy controls (Padj < .05 and |log2FoldChange| > 1), with the strongest signals driven by viral antigens (CMV and EBV) and only a small number of S. cerevisiae proteins (n = 10) (Figure S5).

To examine this further, we focused on the yeast library and compared the prevalence of antibody responses to S. cerevisiae peptides with responses to other antigen classes in the broader library, including common viral pathogens, bacterial virulence factors, and vaccine antigens (Figure 4A). Compared with prevalent viral infections, the prevalence of anti-S. cerevisiae responses was markedly lower than that of EBV (P = 3.3 × 10−20) and CMV (P = 1.2 × 10−11). Anti-S. cerevisiae responses were also less prevalent than responses to bacterial virulence factor antigens from common pathogens, including Klebsiella pneumoniae and Vibrio cholerae (P = 7.6 × 10−5), and less prevalent than responses to vaccine antigens such as poliovirus (P = 6.8 × 10−4). Notably, the prevalence of anti-S. cerevisiae responses was comparable to that observed for negative-control antigens, including cyanophage proteins and random peptide sequences.

Among the 1956 S. cerevisiae peptides for which antibody binding was detected in at least one participant, only 83 peptides, derived from 68 proteins, were reactive in >5% of the study cohort (ie, ≥8 of 160 participants). The most prevalent S. cerevisiae peptide mapped to PSH1 protein,44 generating the most prevalent response in ∼19% of individuals, followed by an antigenic peptide from the PAN1 protein45 at ∼18% and lastly from the LSB6 protein46 at ∼15%. These results demonstrate that most S. cerevisiae proteins do not elicit broadly shared antibody responses across individuals, in contrast to many viral and pathogenic bacterial antigens.

We next compared the prevalence of responses to S. cerevisiae antigens between individuals with CD and healthy controls. To enable a balanced comparison, we down-sampled the CD group to match the number of healthy controls (n = 60 per group) and compared response prevalence between groups (Figure 4B). Across antigen classes included in the library, prevalence patterns were largely comparable between CD and healthy controls, suggesting that differences in immunogenicity of the antigenic source dominate over case-control differences in this dataset. Consistently, the overall burden of responses was similar between groups for S. cerevisiae (Figure 4C), pathogenic bacterial antigens (Figure 4D), and herpesvirus antigens including CMV (Figure 4E) with a slight reduction in anti-EBV responses in individuals with CD (Figure 4F).

3.5. The repertoire of ASCA+ individuals harbors multiple alterations relative to that of ASCA− individuals

We next investigated the association between ASCA seropositivity and antibody repertoire features. Our dataset included 80 ASCA+ individuals (50 with CD and 30 healthy controls) and 80 ASCA− individuals (50 with CD and 30 healthy controls). Using the S. cerevisiae-focused PhIP-Seq library (which also includes non-fungal control antigens), we first compared antibody reactivity to the different organisms included in the library. Among individuals with CD, overall response levels across organisms were comparable between ASCA+ and ASCA− participants (Figure S6A). We then restricted the analysis to S. cerevisiae peptides and compared response frequencies between ASCA+ and ASCA− individuals within CD (Figure S6B), within healthy controls (Figure S6C), and in the combined cohort regardless of disease status (Figure S6D). Across these analyses, we did not identify any S. cerevisiae peptide that was significantly associated with ASCA seropositivity.

As noted above, the S. cerevisiae-focused library was combined with previously published PhIP-Seq libraries, including Vogl et al.14 (244 000 peptides), Leviatan et al.38 (100 000 peptides), and Klompus et al.26 (13 192 peptides) (Figure 5A). The S. cerevisiae-focused component comprised ∼15 000 peptides, representing ∼4% of the total peptide content used to screen sera from the 160 study participants. Among antibody-enriched peptides detected across samples, the majority originated from the Vogl et al.14 and Leviatan et al.38 libraries (Figure 5B). We therefore compared antibody repertoire features between ASCA+ and ASCA− individuals primarily using antigens defined in these two libraries.

Comparing antibody repertoires of ASCA+ and ASCA− individuals with CD, we identified 44 peptides that were significantly more prevalent in ASCA+ than in ASCA− individuals, and 17 peptides that were significantly more prevalent in ASCA− than in ASCA+ individuals (Figure 5C). In contrast, when comparing ASCA+ and ASCA− healthy controls, we did not detect any peptide significantly associated with ASCA status, potentially due to the smaller control sample size (Figure 5D). Finally, in the combined cohort (all ASCA+ vs all ASCA− individuals), we identified 64 peptides enriched in ASCA+ individuals and 30 peptides enriched in ASCA− individuals (Figure 5E).

To characterize antigens associated with ASCA seropositivity, we analyzed the union of peptides that were significantly enriched in ASCA+ individuals across the comparisons above (Figure 5C–E). A dominant signal corresponded to bacterial flagellins, primarily from members of the order Clostridiales and, in particular, the family Lachnospiraceae, including Roseburia inulinivorans and Roseburia faecis. These anti-flagellin responses mapped to two major sequence clusters (Figure 5F, G), indicating that flagellar proteins from these taxa represent prominent antigenic targets in ASCA+ compared with ASCA− individuals. In addition to the elevated anti-flagellin signal, we observed increased reactivity to several host proteins in ASCA+ individuals, including ryanodine receptor 3, thyroglobulin, and large aggregating proteoglycan core protein. We also detected increased antibody responses against several bacteriophages, including Enterobacter, Lactobacillus, and Klebsiella phages. In contrast, fewer antigens were enriched in ASCA− individuals; these were predominantly derived from bacterial virulence ­factors, including antigens from Streptococcus pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, and pathogenic E. coli strains.

4. Discussion

Here, we sought to characterize systemic adaptive immune responses to the gut mycobiome in the context of IBD using peripheral blood-based TCR profiling and serum antibody profiling. On the T cell side, we quantified the population-level expansion of fungal-specific CD4+ T cell clonotypes and further used a statistical co-occurrence framework to identify TRB clonotypes associated with prevalent members of the gut mycobiome. On the humoral side, we designed a dedicated PhIP-Seq library to profile antibody reactivity against S. cerevisiae peptides. This focused library was motivated by the clinical relevance of ASCA but does not represent the full diversity of the gut mycobiome.

We began by quantifying the population-level expansion of fungal-specific TRB clonotypes previously identified by Martini et al.8 within the SPARC IBD cohort.29 Using this approach, we observed higher expansion of fungal-specific clonotypes in individuals with CD than in UC, including increased responses toward S. cerevisiae and enrichment in individuals with more aggressive disease phenotypes. Notably, a subset of these clonotypes was highly public and detectable in hundreds of individuals, enabling population-scale analyses despite their discovery in a small set of donors. However, an important limitation of this approach is that the fungal-specific clonotype reference set was derived from only six individuals (three with CD and three healthy controls).8 As a result, the clonotypes available for analysis are constrained by the HLA backgrounds present in those donors. Because conventional CD4+ T cell responses are HLA-restricted,31 individuals carrying different HLA alleles may mount anti-fungal responses mediated by distinct clonotypes that would not be captured by this reference set. Consistent with this, our in silico HLA-restriction analysis suggested differences in inferred HLA presentation patterns between clonotypes derived from CD donors and healthy controls; however, given the small number of donors in the source dataset of Martini et al.8 and the strong LD across HLA loci, these observations should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. Future studies should therefore identify fungal-specific clonotypes in larger and more genetically diverse cohorts, which would expand coverage to other HLA alleles and enable more comprehensive population-level analyses of anti-fungal T cell expansion across IBD.

We next used a statistical co-occurrence framework to link the blood TRB repertoire with the presence of gut mycobiota profiled by ITS2 sequencing. A key limitation was the highly private nature of the gut mycobiome at the ASV level: most ASVs were detected in only a small number of individuals, leaving limited statistical power to identify reproducible TRB–mycobiota associations across the cohort. In addition, because paired ITS2 and TRB measurements were not always obtained at the same time point in this longitudinal cohort, temporal variability in the mycobiome probably further attenuates detectable co-occurrence signals. Importantly, clonotypes identified by this approach should not be interpreted as demonstrably fungus-recognizing; rather, they are clonotypes whose presence is statistically associated with carriage of a given ASV and may reflect shared exposures, correlated immune states, or indirect ecological relationships. Co-occurrence mapping has proven powerful for identifying public TCR signatures of antigenic exposures, including CMV15 and in Lyme disease16 as well as immune-mediated diseases.19,20,23 In our dataset, the limited number of robust TRB–ASV associations suggests that common gut mycobiota leave a comparatively weak signature in the circulating TRB repertoire. This may reflect predominantly private anti-fungal responses that are not shared across individuals, compartmentalization of anti-fungal T cells to the intestinal mucosa rather than blood, or both, highlighting the need for complementary approaches such as antigen-stimulation assays47 and tissue-based repertoire profiling48,49 to more comprehensively resolve anti-fungal T cell immunity in IBD.

Following the T cell analyses, we profiled systemic humoral responses using PhIP-Seq by constructing a dedicated S. cerevisiae peptide library. This design was motivated by the long-standing clinical relevance of ASCA in CD, but it has important scope constraints. First, S. cerevisiae exhibits substantial strain- and isolate-level diversity,50,51 and our library represents a pan-proteome-based peptide set rather than the full breadth of circulating or dietary strains. Second, the library focuses on S. cerevisiae and therefore does not capture antibody responses to other mycobiome members implicated in IBD (eg, Candida spp. or Malassezia), highlighting the need for expanded libraries in future studies. Third, our PhIP-Seq screen was performed on a moderate number of samples, which limits power to detect small effect sizes and modest group differences in antibody reactivity. Within these constraints, our results indicate that antibody reactivity to S. cerevisiae peptides is relatively low compared with responses to common viral exposures, pathogenic bacterial antigens, and vaccine antigens. Several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms could explain this pattern: (1) antifungal responses may be largely private at the peptide level, such that different individuals recognize distinct S. cerevisiae targets, limiting the emergence of broadly shared “public” IgG specificities; (2) antifungal humoral immunity may be compartmentalized to the gut and dominated by ­mucosal IgA rather than systemic IgG; and (3) fungal antigens are frequently glycosylated and contain Post-translational modifications (PTMs)- and conformation-dependent epitopes that are not well captured by peptide-display PhIP-Seq. In addition, immunodominant anti-fungal targets may be enriched among virulence- or pathogenicity-associated proteins, as suggested by prior large-scale PhIP-Seq efforts and toxin/virulence-focused libraries,14,52 whereas S. cerevisiae is not typically pathogenic and our library emphasized predominantly secreted and membrane-associated proteins rather than curated virulence factors. Together, these considerations motivate future PhIP-Seq designs that expand fungal taxonomic breadth and prioritize virulence-associated antigen classes to more comprehensively map antifungal humoral immunity in IBD.

Across our PhIP-Seq analyses, ASCA serostatus was not associated with reactivity to any single S. cerevisiae peptide antigen. One probable explanation is technical: ASCA primarily recognizes carbohydrate-rich fungal cell wall epitopes (eg, mannans/mannoproteins), which are not represented in peptide-display PhIP-Seq and would also be missed due to the absence of glycosylation and other PTMs.53 Beyond assay constraints, ASCA-positivity is clinically informative and has been linked to earlier disease onset, ileal involvement, and more complicated/aggressive disease behavior in CD,53 and elevated ASCA levels have also been reported in other inflammatory disorders affecting the small intestine such as celiac disease.54,55 Together, these observations suggest that ASCA is unlikely to reflect a single dominant S. cerevisiae protein target and may instead represent a systemic marker of altered mucosal immune exposure, potentially in the context of small-intestinal inflammation, rather than ongoing mucosal colonization by S. cerevisiae.

Consistent with prior serological studies, ASCA positivity co-occurred,56 with reactivity to multiple bacterial flagellins (including CBir1,57 Fla2, and FlaX58), and in our data the most prominent ASCA-associated signal comprised anti-flagellin responses, particularly against Clostridiales/Lachnospiraceae-derived flagellins. This corroborated previous findings from Bourgonje et al.28 and aligns with recent PhIP-Seq results from Angkeow et al.,52 who also reported elevated flagellin-directed antibody reactivity in CD. Differences in the specific bacterial sources implicated across studies probably reflect differences in library composition and depth: the ToxScan library is enriched for environmental toxins and virulence factors, whereas our composite library spans a broader set of gut microbiome, environmental, and dietary antigens, with flagellins represented across many taxa.14,26,38 More broadly, these surface antigens (flagellins and fungal cell wall components) may become immunologically visible when barrier integrity is compromised, allowing microbial products to access submucosal tissues and elicit systemic antibody responses even if the source organisms are not directly driving pathology. Accordingly, elevated anti-fungal/ASCA-associated responses may serve as markers of an aggressive, small-intestine-linked disease phenotype rather than evidence of a causal role for specific fungal exposures.

Although our analyses focused primarily on CD, they also inform UC. Across both the circulating TRB repertoire and serum PhIP-Seq profiles, UC exhibited weaker systemic immune signatures than CD, consistent with prior work reporting more pronounced systemic adaptive immune perturbations in CD.20,22,28,59 This may reflect fundamental disease biology; CD more often involves deeper, transmural inflammation and ileal disease, which may promote systemic immune activation, whereas UC inflammation is typically mucosa-confined and may be more compartmentalized to the colonic mucosa. Future studies will benefit from larger UC cohorts and integrated profiling of local mucosal immune repertoires (eg, tissue-resident T cells and mucosal antibody responses), alongside expanded fungal antigen libraries and complementary platforms that capture glycan/PTM-dependent epitopes, to more comprehensively define mycobiome-directed immunity across IBD.
