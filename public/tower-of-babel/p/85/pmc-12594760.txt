Aortic calcification is associated with decreased abdominal aortic aneurysm growth
Authors: Sydney L Olson, Valentyna Kostiuk, Tanner I Kim, John A Curci, Timothy Baxter, Jon S Matsumura, William C Blackwelder, Michael L Terrin, Raul J Guzman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) rupture remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, but predictors of continued growth and rupture risk remain limited. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between abdominal aortic calcification and AAA growth via a secondary cohort analysis of the Non-Invasive Treatment of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Clinical Trial (N-TA3CT), a prospective multicenter randomized study. Arterial calcification Agatston scores and maximum transverse diameter were measured in non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scans in patients enrolled in N-TA3CT. Uni- and multi-variable linear regression were used to assess the association of anatomic calcium burden and comorbid conditions with rate of aneurysm growth. Of the 261 randomized patients in the trial, 136 patients met inclusion criteria for analysis. On univariable analysis, baseline calcium score at all assessed anatomic locations- the superior mesenteric artery (spearman correlation coefficient (SCC) -0.20, p = 0.0176), renal artery (-0.22, p = 0.0120, infrarenal aorta (-0.26, p = 0.0020), common iliac artery (-0.19, p = 0.024), external iliac artery (-0.26, p = 0.003), and sum of all measured sites (-0.28, p = 0.001)- was significantly associated with lower AAA diameter growth rates. Of individually measured sites, baseline infrarenal aortic calcification had the strongest negative association with aneurysm growth. Interestingly, infrarenal calcium score was not significantly associated with baseline aneurysm diameter (R2 0.0001, spearman correlation p = 0.94), or diabetes status (p = 0.59). In a multivariable regression model, factors significantly associated with faster diameter growth included baseline volume and current tobacco use. Factors associated with reduced growth rate included diabetes and baseline infrarenal aorta calcium score thereby establishing aneurysmal calcification as a marker for slower aneurysm growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26407-0.Keywords: Abdominal aortic aneurysm, AAA, Calcium score, Agatston score, Arterial calcification, Aneurysm growthSubject terms: Predictive markers, Aneurysm, Aortic diseases, Calcification

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAA) are newly diagnosed in 200,000 people in the United States annually and account for up to 1% of all deaths1. AAAs are increasingly identified at smaller diameters as cross-sectional body imaging becomes more widely used, and as population screening guidelines expand2–4. When the AAA diameter is below accepted sizes for repair, patients enter into surveillance protocols until their aneurysm exceeds specific thresholds2. For aneurysm below these size criteria, there are no approved medications or interventions that have been shown to slow or stabilize AAA growth, and patients often report anxiety based on perceived unpredictability of their course5. Studies have shown that aortic aneurysms display a range of growth rates6, and specific factors that affect an individual’s aneurysm growth rate have not been fully defined. Current clinical practice guidelines on abdominal aortic aneurysm management highlight the need for additional study into biomarkers or imaging-derived determinants of aneurysm risks2.

Arterial calcification is one image-derived metric often seen in studies of patients with AAA7. However, the relationship between aortic calcification and aneurysm growth has not been clearly defined with some studies reporting no significant association between infra-renal aortic calcification volume and growth of small AAAs (measuring < 5.5 cm)8. In contrast, a recent study by Klopf et al. suggested an inverse relationship between aortic calcification and aneurysm growth, however, the aortic calcification score failed to hold independent prognostic significance for AAA growth after adjusting for comorbidities, morphological, and blood parameters9.

We thus sought to investigate the effects of aortic calcification on the growth rate of abdominal aortic aneurysms. We used CT scans from the Non-Invasive Treatment of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Clinical Trial (N-TA3CT) to quantify baseline calcification and its progression, as well as to assess aneurysm growth rates. This was an exploratory, retrospective, sub-study analysis looking at systematically collected data from one of the largest serial CT imaging studies of small AAAs.

Methods

Study design

The data included in this study were collected in the Non-Invasive Treatment of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Clinical Trial (N-TA3CT, NCT01756833, 20/12/20212, n = 261); the protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at all clinical sites, core laboratories, and coordinating centers and all randomized patients gave written informed consent; the trial followed practices in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. The present supplemental protocol was approved by the Yale University Institutional Review Board (IRB#2000026166) and was deemed exempt from patient consent due to retrospective nature. The purpose of N-TA3CT was to test whether doxycycline treatment could reduce the growth of small AAAs (3.5 to 5.0 cm for male, 3.5 to 4.5 cm for female participants) over 2 years. The study design, methods, and findings of N-TA3CT have been previously published10, and the primary outcomes demonstrated that doxycycline did not significantly affect aneurysm growth11. Based on this finding, and analysis showing calcium progression to be similar between treatments, the present analysis pooled both treatment arms12. All clinical comorbidity factors collected by the original N-TA3CT trial are included in demographic results; additional clinical factors were not able to be collected due to the post-hoc nature of this sub-study. Notably, of the 261 randomized patients in NTA3CT over the 2 years of the study, no AAA ruptures occurred, 22 patients underwent AAA repair due to size exceeding clinically recommended intervention thresholds, and 7 patients died; these outcomes were not different between treatment groups.

Patients were included for the present analysis if they had non-contrast CT imaging at baseline and at least one follow-up time point; scans were collected at regular 6 months intervals in N-TA 3 CT. Patients who underwent only contrast CT imaging were excluded due to the inability to reliably compare calcification scores between contrast and non-contrast scans. Non-contrast imaging allows for clearer definition between calcium deposits and vessels and is required for the calcium quantification protocol described below. Patients with incomplete imaging of the abdominal vessels, those who underwent operative intervention including open or endovascular repair prior to completion of two non-contrast CT scans were excluded due to the inability to complete necessary calcium and aneurysm growth analysis.

CT acquisition protocol and analysis

Abdominal and pelvic non-gated CT scans were obtained at baseline, and at six-month intervals thereafter for 18 to 30 months based on initial randomization date and trial completion date. Patients (n = 204, 80%) completed imaging through the 24-month window. The acquisition protocol was standardized across clinical sites and required slice thickness of ≤ 2.5 mm. CT images were transferred from clinical sites to the AortaCore Imaging Core Laboratory at the University of Wisconsin, Madison, for diameter analysis on post-processing workstations (Aquarius Intuition version 4.4.12, TeraRecon, Foster City, CA) with a standardized and validated protocol11.

Aneurysm measurements

Aneurysm maximum transverse diameter (MTD) was measured via the double oblique method by selecting the largest section of the AAA in the multiplanar reconstruction image in a plane perpendicular to the aneurysm walls. Aneurysm volume was defined as the region from the lowest significant renal artery (LRA) to the aortic bifurcation. All diameter and volume measurements were completed by a single reader, masked to treatment allocation, with previously validated high intra- and inter-reader reliability10.

Calcium measurements

Agatston calcium score was measured throughout the abdominal aortoiliac vessels including: superior mesenteric artery, renal arteries, infrarenal aorta, common iliac arteries, and iliofemoral arteries. Calcium scoring was performed using semi-automated calcium scoring software (TeraRecon, Durham, NC) as previously described13. In brief, using cross sectional imaging, areas of calcification along the aorta and associated vessels with a cross-sectional area greater than 1 mm2 and with a density of > 130 Hounsfield units (HU) were identified automatically. Regions of interest along the aorta were additionally manually selected and labeled. Calcification in the renal arteries was measured from their origin to the renal hilum. SMA calcium was assessed from the origin to the first branch. Infrarenal aortic calcium was measured in the aorta at the level of the lowest regnal artery distally to the aortic bifurcation. Common iliac calcification was measured from their origins to the iliac bifurcation. External iliac calcification was measured from the common iliac bifurcation to the femoral artery bifurcation. This scoring method has previously been demonstrated to have excellent interobserver reproducibility within this research group with Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.98 and a P < 0.00113. Agatston volume and mass scoring were performed as originally described by Agatston et al.14.The Agatston score reflects the total area of calcium deposits and the density of the calcium; this method was selected due to widespread use and comparable transition from coronary calcium scoring. Total calcium score was calculated by summing all individually measured segments. A score of zero signifies no calcium.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was aneurysm growth as measured by change in MTD estimated by simple linear regression of diameter data over time using all available CT scans up to 30 months after the baseline measurement for each individual participant. The progression rate (slope of linear regression) was expressed in units of centimeters per year (calculated by exact days between scans); previously reported data identified linear diameter growth in 70% of patients (compared to exponential 4%, staccato 3%, non-classifiable 23%) and therefore selected as a best approach to model growth over time6. Calcification progression was similarly estimated by simple linear regression of calcium data over time using all available CT scans up to 30 months after the baseline measurement. Progression rate (slope of linear regression) was expressed as change in calcium score units per year.

Biomarker analysis

Assessment of serum biomarkers was conducted to derive plasma matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) 3 and 9 levels. This was measured by an ELISA, 2-site sandwich method (R&D Systems, Quantikine, DMP900) in a core lab. Exploratory associations of biomarker concentration and relation to calcium burden were conducted with linear regressions.

Statistical analysis

Shapiro-Wilk testing was used to assess for normal distribution of continuous data. Pearson’s correlation coefficients (PCC) were used to test strength of correlation between baseline calcium scores and other continuous variables; Spearman’s correlation coefficients (SCC) were added for correlation testing for data sets that did not follow gaussian distributions. Established PCC/SCC correlation coefficient thresholds of 0.00–0.10.00.10 negligible, 0.10–0.39 weak, 0.40–0.69 moderate, 0.70–0.89 strong, and 0.90–1.00.90.00 very strong were followed to define strength of correlation15. Univariable linear regressions were used to estimate the association between calcium burden and aneurysm baseline size and growth. Univariable linear (continuous) or logistic (categorical) regression was used to assess the effect of demographics, comorbid conditions, medications, and baseline aneurysm characteristics on calcium burden as well as on aneurysm growth rates.

Kruskal-Wallis and Mann Whitney tests were used to compare calcium burden distributions between multiple and two groups, respectively. Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparison testing was used to compare differences in calcium burden across previously established aneurysm growth categories (high, low, minimal to no significant growth)6.

Multiple linear regression was selected to build a final model to assess role of key factors on aneurysm diameter growth. Clinical factors with prior documented and well accepted association with AAA growth were included in the model: age16–18, sex19,20, tobacco use16,21,22,diabetes22–24, baseline diameter25,26, and baseline volume27. Infrarenal calcium score was added to the model as it was identified as the strongest single site associated with aneurysm growth on univariable testing. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS Studio (Cary, North Carolina, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 from GraphPad Software, LLC. Results with a P value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant, and all P values were 2-sided.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 261 patients included in the N-TA3CT, 136 had non-contrast imaging at baseline and at least 1 follow up time point enabling their inclusion in this analysis. Reasons for exclusion included no baseline non-contrast CT (n = 116), no follow up non-contract CT (n = 3), incomplete scan range (n = 5), iliac stenting (n = 1). The mean age at enrollment was 71 years, 14% of subjects were female. (Table 1). History of and present tobacco use is prevalent (93%), as is hypercholesteremia (75%), CAD (35%), and diabetes (24%). Common medications included statins (74%), β-blockers (47%), and aspirin (66%). Baseline infrarenal abdominal aortic maximum transverse diameters (MTD) ranged from 3.5 to 5.0 cm for male and 3.5–4.5 cm for female patients.

Higher baseline calcium scores at all measured sites- SMA, renal arteries, infrarenal aorta, common iliac arteries, and iliofemoral arteries were significantly associated with reduced AAA diameter growth rate (Table 2). Of baseline sites, infrarenal calcification and total calcification had the strongest correlations with slower diameter growth (SCC − 0.263 p = 0.002, and SCC − 0.279, p = 0.0013 respectively). Relationship of infrarenal calcium and aneurysm progression

Of individually measured sites, baseline infrarenal aortic calcium had the strongest negative association with aneurysm growth (Fig. 2). Using previously established rates of diameter growth categories6, high growth aneurysms (> 0.25 cm/year) had significantly lower baseline infrarenal calcium compared to both low growth aneurysms (0.05–0.25 cm/year) and minimal to no significant growth aneurysms (< 0.05 cm/year) (high vs. no growth Tukey’s multiple comparison p = 0.04, high v low Tukey’s multiple comparison test p = 0.03, ANOVA p = 0.01) (Supplemental Figure 3). A similar pattern of AAA volume growth but not baseline volume being significantly associated with infrarenal calcium burden was also observed (Supplemental Fig. 2B).

Multivariable aneurysm progression modeling

Univariable analysis for demographics, comorbid conditions, medications, baseline aneurysm characteristics was used to characterize effects on MTD growth rate in this patient population (Supplemental Table I). Multivariable analysis of establish growth rate factors and the addition of baseline infrarenal aorta calcium score found infrarenal aortic calcium to be independently associated with reduced aneurysm growth (Table 3). Additional factors significantly associated with MTD growth on multivariable analysis included: current tobacco use, baseline aneurysm volume, history of diabetes. Factors not significantly associated with AAA MTD growth in this multivariable model included: sex, age, and baseline AAA diameter. Covariation between model variables were assessed with Pearson’s r matrix and were overall negligible to weak with the exception of aneurysm diameter with volume and age with intercept which were both moderate (Supplemental Fig. 4).

Mechanistic exploration of calcium deposition in AAA

Factors significantly associated with increased baseline infrarenal calcium burden included age, CAD, and aspirin use (Table 4). Current smokers had significantly lower infrarenal calcium burdens compared to former and compared to combined former and never smokers (Table 4, Supplemental Fig. 5). Serum CRP and MMP-9 were collected to address the N-TA3CT studies primary endpoint11 and were included in this analysis as exploratory mechanisms. We found that increased baseline infrarenal calcium burden was associated with lower baseline serum CRP on linear regression testing, though this was not significant on non-parametric testing (Table 4, Supplemental Fig. 6). There was no significant relationship between baseline infrarenal calcium score and serum MMP-9 (Table 4, Supplemental Fig. 7). In contrast to the association with aneurysm growth, no difference in baseline infrarenal calcium score was observed between patients with and without diabetes. (Supplemental Fig. 8).

Discussion

In this study involving 136 patients with small aortic aneurysms, we found that the extent of calcification in the abdominal aorta and its major branches was associated with slower aneurysm growth over two-years. The infrarenal aortic calcium score was independently associated with reduced AAA growth rates when controlling for previously identified factors associated with aneurysm growth15 including age16, sex16–19, tobacco status21–24, diabetes25,26, baseline diameter6,27, and baseline volume15.

The role of calcification in AAA growth and rupture has been examined previously. Klopf et al. compared calcification scores in fast (> 2 mm/6 month) versus slow (< 2 mm/6 month) growing AAAs (n = 102), and showed that those with slow growth were characterized by higher median calcification volume of the infrarenal and total aorta, as well as higher total customized Agatston calcium (cAC) scores9. However, after adjusting for morphological and blood parameters as well as comorbidities and medications, the cAC score did not hold independent prognostic information for AAA growth. In contrast, we found that aortic calcification had a persistent effect in multivariable modeling that included factors associated with changes in AAA expansion rates. Possible explanations include different scoring techniques and the larger sample size in the present study. We confirmed findings from recent CT based analyses that baseline aneurysm diameter was minimally associated with future growth6,15. We found that baseline aneurysm diameter also was not associated with baseline infrarenal aortic calcium score or aneurysm growth rates.

Several studies failed to identify the association between AAA growth and calcification. For example, Hendy and colleagues examined AAAs measuring < 5.5 cm and found that infra-renal aortic calcification volume did not predict the AAA growth7. In contrast to the current analysis, the authors defined AAA growth as the volume change per year, while the baseline infra-renal aortic calcification volumes were categorized into two groups based on the median calcification volume: one group with volumes less than 1600 mm³ and another group with volumes greater than 1600 mm³. Therefore, the result differences could be attributed to the fact that the study included larger AAAs measuring up to 55 mm, the use of AAA volume rather than diameter as an outcome measure, and that calcification was treated as a categorical variable instead of a continuous one, with the data divided into two categories. A different study of AAA growth patterns in patients with aneurysm measuring 4.0–4.9 cm previously suggested that calcification assessed using a semiquantitative scale (less than 10% of the diameter, 10–50%, and over 50%) at three locations (aortic neck, aorta at level of maximal diameter, and at the aortic bifurcation) was not associated with aneurysm expansion and did not have a relationship with whether growth was continuous (linear, exponential) or discontinuous (one or more no growth interval, staccato)28,29. We did not examine the relationship between AAA growth and calcification patterns as this was not within the goals of the present study. However, variable results reported from previous analyses could be explained by differences in calcium evaluation methods that are dependent on the available imaging acquisition and scoring techniques. Finally, our analysis included aneurysms measuring 3.5–5.0 cm in comparison to a narrower range of diameters (4.0–4.9 cm AAAs).

Prior studies also have shown inconsistent results on the effects of diabetes on AAA growth rates. For example, Vega de Ceniga with others was unable to find an association between diabetes and AAA growth rates28, while Golledge et al. reported significantly slower AAA expansion in patients with diabetes and proposed a mechanism involving reduced matrix degrading enzyme secretion and inflammatory markers shown by monocytes exposed to glycated type I collagen lattices30, and Kristensen et al. found an inverse association between HbA1c levels and aneurysm progression26. Our multivariable modeling shows that diabetes and infrarenal aortic calcium are independently associated with slower AAA growth rates suggesting that they contribute independently to this pathological process. Metformin use and glycemic control data were not available for this cohort which limits further conclusions on the mechanism between diabetes and aneurysm growth. Interestingly, infrarenal calcium burden was not different between patients with and without diabetes further highlighting the multifactorial process of aneurysm growth and development.

A few studies have explored the relationship between AAA and peripheral artery disease (PAD), but the evidence is inconsistent. One study investigating AAA growth found that a low ankle-brachial index (ABI) was associated with slower AAA growth25. Another study focused on AAAs measuring 3–5.4 cm and examined clinical risk factors for AAA expansion26. While the authors confirmed a positive correlation between smoking and AAA expansion, as well as a negative correlation between diabetes and AAA growth, the association between claudication and AAA expansion was not statistically significant. Furthermore, a systematic review of 15 studies involving 4873 patients found no consistent association between PAD and reduced AAA growth31. In contrast, a meta-analysis identified a negative correlation between PAD and AAA growth, despite a positive correlation between PAD and AAA presence32. This meta-analysis focused only on studies that reported a link between PAD and AAA, screening 612 publications and excluding 532 based on titles and abstracts. As a result, 80 articles were included, from which 11 studies involving 4573 patients were selected to examine the relationship between PAD and AAA growth. However, it is unclear whether some of the excluded studies failed to show any association between PAD and AAA, raising the possibility of selection bias. Additionally, the definition of PAD varied widely across studies, with some authors using criteria such as claudication, ABI values, chronic lower limb ischemia, physical exam findings combined with clinical interview, or CTA evidence of occlusive or stenotic PAD. Given this broad range of PAD definitions, discrepancies in study results are inevitable. Future research on this topic, using more consistent and standardized definitions of PAD and inclusion criteria, would be valuable in better understanding the true nature of these associations.

The mechanism of aneurysm wall calcification resulting in slower aortic enlargement is currently under investigation. One hypothesis is that calcium may stabilize the weakened aneurysmal tissue by affecting atherosclerotic plaque composition. Theoretical modeling supports this theory showing that aortic stiffness increases with greater burdens of calcium33. Another study evaluated whether the presence of vessel wall calcification could alter stress levels in the adjacent aortic tissue34. The authors conclude that calcification can influence AAA shape based on computational modeling and simulations. The simulation results showed that the presence of calcifications had significant load-bearing effects and reduced stress in the adjacent aortic wall.

This present study is limited to the cohort included in the N-TA3CT study of patients with small abdominal aortic aneurysms, and it is unclear how the relationships we identified may change at larger aneurysm diameters. The N-TA3CT study tested the effect of doxycycline on aneurysm growth, and groups were combined here for analysis based on no difference in baseline calcium rates between randomized groups12 and no difference in growth rate over 2 years11, but minor effects could persist. Follow up was limited to two years, and it is possible that aortic growth patterns and calcification trends may change beyond this period. Additionally, not all patients who were enrolled in the trial were included for analysis, as patients without at least 2 non-contrast CT scans over time were excluded potentially introducing unknown bias, however, our final cohort included patients represented across clinical sites and various comorbid conditions. The study is strengthened by the protocolized regular interval collection of CT scans that allowed for detailed growth and calcium assessment that would be unfeasible to obtain outside of a clinical trial. Current clinical standards employ ultrasound to surveille AAA growth; while the ability to apply CT based calcium assessments to routine surveillance is limited this study identifies an important area of future mechanistic study.

Conclusions

The extent of infrarenal aortic calcification is associated with slower AAA growth rates, independent of age, sex, diabetes, tobacco use, and aneurysm size. The presence of diabetes was also independently associated with slower growth, while current tobacco use and aneurysm volume were associated with increased growth rates. One unexpected finding of the study was that infrarenal calcification did not correlate with diabetes suggesting the influence of other important and, as yet, unidentified factors. Additional studies are needed to develop an understanding of the mechanistic role of aortic calcification in stabilizing aneurysms, and further, to assess its role in predicting rupture risk
