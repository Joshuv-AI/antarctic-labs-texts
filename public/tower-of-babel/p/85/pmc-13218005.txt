Relevant insights from personalized inhaled carbon monoxide dosing in a safety study in pneumonia and ARDS
Authors: Rebecca M. Baron, Antonio Arciniegas, Susan Sullivan, Michelle Courchaine, B. Taylor Thompson, Alex Stenzler, Mary E. Choi, Augustine M.K. Choi, Mark A. Perrella, Tilo Winkler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow-dose inhaled carbon monoxide (iCO) has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in preclinical studies, and its safety has been established in human studies. However, the safety of delivering personalized iCO concentrations of 200 to 500 ppm in ventilated patients with pneumonia and ARDS, targeting 7% HbCO at 90 min of exposure, remains unknown.MethodsPersonalized iCO concentrations needed to reach a plasma HbCO of 7% at 90 min were determined using a Coburn-Forster-Kane (CFK) calculator, utilizing baseline and 20-minute (20-min) arterial HbCO measurements at an iCO test concentration of 200 ppm. The personalized iCO concentration was administered for the remainder of the 90 min with HbCO measurements performed at 60-, 75-, and 90-minutes.ResultsThere were no safety incidents observed during the study, and all exposed subjects were alive and well at 6-month follow-up. Surprisingly, we found significant deviations between measured and predicted HbCO levels at 90 min for individualized exposures with iCO of 220 to 425 ppm (p < 0.001), which were more discrepant at higher iCO doses. However, fitting the CFK model to the 20- to 90-minute HbCO measurements confirmed the validity of the CFK model and revealed significant deviations for baseline HbCO (p < 0.001), suggesting pulmonary vascular responses to iCO during the 20-min test exposure, confirmed by observed changes in DLCO.ConclusionsPulmonary vasodilation in response to iCO can affect early HbCO kinetics, which is relevant to refining protocols for reliable, personalized iCO administration that optimize pulmonary iCO concentrations, thereby increasing the benefits of therapeutic iCO administration in lung diseases.

Introduction

Pre-clinical studies show potential for the therapeutic use of low-dose inhaled carbon monoxide (iCO) in critical illness [1]. The safety of low-dose iCO in ARDS (100–200 ppm) has been studied, including accurate predictions of carboxyhemoglobin (HbCO) kinetics after iCO exposure based on the Coburn-Forster-Kane (CFK) model [2]. Fixed low-dose iCO (100–200 ppm) has also been investigated in a Phase 2a randomized, controlled clinical trial in Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis, demonstrating safety; although no clinical differences, nor differences in a target biomarker were observed. However, HbCO plasma levels were low [3]. We and others proposed that higher iCO concentrations delivered to the lung may benefit pulmonary disease with an acceptable benefit-risk profile. We hypothesized that a bedside CFK calculator incorporating individual lung function parameters to predict HbCO kinetics [2] could allow us to safely deliver personalized iCO concentrations (up to 500 ppm) targeting 7% plasma HbCO at 90 min [4]. Using this approach, we conducted a first-in-human study of personalized iCO dosing in ventilated pneumonia and sepsis-induced ARDS subjects, including placebo (air) administrations in a control group (NCT04870125). The trial was stopped prematurely due to low enrollment, but we identified important insights in HbCO responses during personalized iCO administrations of up to 500 ppm in four enrolled subjects.

Methods

Between December 2023 and May 2024, eligible subjects with sepsis-induced ARDS or pneumonia requiring mechanical ventilation were enrolled and randomized to receive iCO vs. Placebo daily for up to 3 days (barring safety exclusion criteria for daily treatment) (Table 1). Five subjects were enrolled, but one subject withdrew from the study prior to any study procedures being performed, so data was collected on four subjects, one of whom was unable to receive exposure to study drug due to elevated lactate levels prohibiting daily treatment (Table 2).

Personalized iCO concentrations needed to reach a plasma HbCO of 7% at 90 min were determined using a CFK calculator [2, 7, 8] in subjects randomized to iCO exposure, utilizing baseline and 20-minute (20-min) arterial HbCO measurements (HbCOm) at an iCO test concentration of 200 ppm, measured body weight, inspired O2 fraction (FiO2), minute ventilation (MV), dead space ratio (Vd/Vt), and plasma hemoglobin (Hgb). After the 20-min test, the personalized iCO concentration was administered for the remainder of the 90 min, with HbCO measurements performed at 60, 75, and 90 min. Note that the personalized iCO concentration is specific to the patient’s condition at the time of exposure and, thus, can vary among exposure days.

Lung carbon monoxide diffusing capacity (DLCO) for different time segments of the iCO exposure is based on HbCO measurements at the beginning and end of each time segment [7]. The 95% confidence interval of the CFK model-based predictions of HbCO kinetics (HbCOp) was determined based on the iCO = 200 ppm exposure data of a previously published study [2] using two times the standard deviation (SD) as a reference. The 95% confidence interval of fluctuations in measured HbCO, a reference for fitting 20–90 min HbCO kinetics (HbCOf), is based on the placebo exposure data of a previously published study [2] using two times the SD. The p-values were calculated as probabilities of the null hypotheses of the different findings, and p < 0.05 was considered significant. Blood for plasma mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) level was collected pre- and post-iCO or placebo administration daily as described previously, and data shown are for those subjects who had mtDNA levels measured pre-day 1 and post-day 3 exposure [2].

Results

Enrolled subjects, their characteristics, and physiological parameters are summarized in Table 2. There were no safety incidents observed during the study, and all exposed subjects were alive and well at 6-month follow up. We found unexpectedly that HbCOp at 90-minutes for exposures with iCO > 200 ppm (220–425) (subjects (s) s1, s2), based on 20-min tests with 200 ppm iCO, fell outside of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for continuous iCO = 200 ppm exposure for 0–90 min from our previous study [2] (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1B). The deviation of HbCOm from HbCOp was more discrepant at higher iCO doses (note the iCO of 425 ppm in subject 1 on day 1 (s11) (Fig. 1A)). Surprisingly, for each of the daily exposures for this subject (s1, days 1–3 (i.e., s11-3)) at the higher personalized iCO doses predicted by the CFK calculator (390–425), the deviations fell significantly below the lower boundary of the 95% CI (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1B), raising concerns that the CFK model does not accurately predict HbCO kinetics for iCO > 200 ppm. However, 20 to 90-minute CFK fittings for the personalized iCO dose demonstrated that the HbCOf values were within the 95% CI of placebo exposures of our previous study [2] (Fig. 1C). Thus, the excellent agreement between the HbCOf kinetics and the measured 20 to 90-minute HbCOm at a constant personalized medicine iCO dose demonstrates the intrinsic validity of the CFK model for personalized iCO concentrations > 200 ppm. However, back-extrapolating the 20 to 90-minute HbCOf to the baseline measurement at iCO = 0 ppm revealed significant residuals outside of the 95% CI (p < 0.001), with the three exposures of s1 at higher iCO doses falling consistently below the lower boundary (p < 0.001). Given the known vasodilating effects of CO [9], we speculated that iCO exposures led to changes in regional pulmonary perfusion and ventilation-perfusion ratios (V/Q), thus affecting the DLCO (Fig. 1C). We confirmed such changes in DLCO during iCO exposure (Fig. 1D). Plasma mitochondrial DNA levels (post-day 3 exposure compared with pre-day1 exposure) decreased more after iCO vs. Placebo exposure, similar to our prior report [2] (Table 2).

Discussion

In this first-in-human trial of personalized iCO administration in ventilated sepsis-induced ARDS and pneumonia subjects, we observed no significant treatment-related adverse events, including for iCO concentrations of up to 425 ppm for 90 min in critically ill patients. In contrast to our prior studies of fixed low-dose iCO (100–200 ppm) in ARDS (NCT03799874) [2], we were surprised to find that measured plasma HbCO levels deviated substantially from those predicted by the CFK model, particularly with higher iCO concentrations.

Given the potential vasodilatory effects of iCO [10], we hypothesized that iCO administration might alter lung ventilation-perfusion matching, and our observed changes in DLCO (Fig. 1D) support this hypothesis. Additionally, a retrospective review of a previous study using iCO = 200 ppm [2] showed similar behaviors in several subjects without exceeding the 95% CI, so that the behavior had previously not been identified. The iCO > 200 ppm exposures that we report here increased the magnitude of the deviations between the predicted and measured HbCO, resulting in clearly recognizable and significant findings at the higher iCO exposures. It is important to note the remarkable similarity in the HbCO kinetics of subject s1 on three consecutive days of exposure, which shows that the behavior is systematic within a subject and a highly relevant insight for personalized iCO treatments.

Confidence intervals are an important statistical reference for predictions and curve fitting. The 95% confidence interval of deviations between HbCOm and HbCOp (Fig. 1B) shows the range within which 95% of the measurements are expected to be according to the results of a previous study using iCO = 200 ppm [2]. We note that HbCOp kinetics are predictions based on 20-min tests using iCO = 200 ppm and that the CFK model accounts for the expected change in HbCOp kinetics (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the 95% confidence interval of the 20–90 min HbCO fitting is a reference for expected fluctuations in HbCO measurements that was derived from placebo exposure data of a previously published study [2], since it is commonly assumed that such variations are superimposed on the fitted kinetics (Fig. 1C). We also note that the very good agreement of HbCOm with HbCOf during the 20–90 min interval suggests that there were, in contrast to baseline, no significant changes in the lungs affecting HbCO kinetics.

Our findings show that using a 20-min test of 200 ppm iCO to determine personalized iCO concentrations can be inaccurate if iCO alters the patient’s DLCO, especially at higher iCO concentrations, where we observed substantial deviations from the predicted HbCO kinetics. Optimizations of iCO delivery protocols based on the CFK model should be considered for future studies using personalized medicine approaches. Based on our knowledge of the CFK model and experience with iCO delivery, we recommend longer test periods with higher iCO concentrations, which lower the relative effect of changes in DLCO on the predicted HbCO kinetics and, thus, are expected to decrease the deviations between HbCOm and HbCOp, increasing the accuracy of personalized iCO concentrations targeting 7% HbCO at 90 min. Alternatively, changing the time points for HbCO measurements, e.g., taking HbCO measurements at 20 and 40 min, can mitigate the effects of DLCO changes on personalized iCO concentrations. Similarly, dosing of other drugs that affect pulmonary vascular tone and thus independently alter ventilation-perfusion relationships [11, 12] may need to be avoided shortly before and during 90-minute iCO treatments.

There are a number of limitations of our study. First, we were only able to analyze four iCO exposures, three of which were in one subject (s1) using higher doses than had been previously tested on this protocol [2]. However, the reproducibility of these three exposures in s1 on three consecutive days highlights the power of this personalized medicine approach in tailoring the iCO exposure to an individual’s rapidly evolving physiologic parameters. Furthermore, the data observed in s2 exposed to 220 ppm mirrors our findings in eight subjects exposed to a fixed dose of 200 ppm in our prior Phase 1a study [2], affirming the validity of the personalized medicine approach to iCO dosing. In contrast to group differences in large cohort studies, our findings demonstrate that small cohorts can yield significant insights in the context of personalized medicine. Second, three of the four evaluated subjects had sepsis/ARDS, while one subject had sepsis/pneumonia. Our initial eligibility criteria included only sepsis/ARDS subjects, but due to low initial enrollment during the peri-COVID pandemic, we expanded eligibility criteria to include ventilated patients with pneumonia. It is increasingly appreciated that ventilated pneumonia patients represent a spectrum of illness related to ARDS with similar biologic and clinical pro-inflammatory pathways, likely to be modulated by iCO. Similarly, the LUNG-SAFE study [13], including a large cohort of ventilated ICU patients, found similar outcomes for pneumonia (bilateral or unilateral infiltrates) and ARDS patients. Additionally, our pre-clinical FDA IND-enabling studies examining non-human primates [14, 15] demonstrated a benefit for iCO in pneumococcal pneumonia (unilateral infiltrate) as well as activation of similar pathways observed in our prior Phase 1a study of fixed-dose inhaled CO in ARDS [2]. Third, the limited number of biospecimens available precludes large-scale analyses, but the relative changes within an individual over time remain relevant and mirror our findings in a prior larger-scale analysis [2].

In conclusion, incorporating the vasodilatory effects of iCO on early HbCO kinetics can help refine protocols for reliable, personalized iCO administration that optimize pulmonary iCO concentrations and potential beneficial effects in the lung without exceeding the selected HbCO safety threshold [12]. Our findings in this small-cohort phase 1 trial provide valuable information for future personalized iCO delivery studies in critical illness.
