Unsupervised clustering of clinical and laboratory features of systemic lupus erythematosus: insights from a multicentre cohort
Authors: Yuke Hou, Yan Wang, Bo Wei, Yinghua Chen, Chenghua Weng, Shiyu Wu, Jingyuan Liang, Yujia Zhai, Ping Wang, Yadan Zou, Guanmin Gao, Shanzhao Jin, Jiachen Li, Sitian Zang, Xinxin Li, Jianhui Cheng, Haihong Yao, Zhichun Liu, Guochun Wang, Zhi-Hong Liu, Zhanguo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study explored the relationship between clinical phenotypes and immuno-molecular features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) using unsupervised machine learning and multi-omics integration.MethodsA multicentre cohort of 1065 SLE patients from five teaching hospitals was studied. Unsupervised clustering was performed using integrated clinical and laboratory data to identify distinct phenotypic clusters. Multi-omics profiling, including proteomics and transcriptomics, was conducted for each cluster, comparing with healthy controls. In addition, 899 patients were prospectively followed for over 1 year to assess therapeutic responses and remission outcomes.ResultsUnsupervised clustering identified five distinct clusters of SLE based on clinical phenotypes and immunological signatures, and an intrinsic association was found between each cluster and immune-inflammatory features. Cluster 1 (renal; 14.27%) had predominant renal involvement (87.5%) and high disease activity. Cluster 2 (mucocutaneous; 39.53%) exhibited mainly cutaneous manifestations (72.45%) with mild visceral involvement and the highest rates of achieving low disease activity state (46.42%). Cluster 3 (neuropsychiatric; 14.74%) had a high frequency of neuropsychiatric manifestations (96.18%) and anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies positivity (42.04%). Cluster 4 (haematologic; 22.63%) was characterised by haematologic involvement, predominantly cytopenia (95.85%), with frequent antiphospholipid antibody positivity and direct Coombs positivity (87.55%). Cluster 5 (cardiopulmonary; 8.83%) exhibited prominent cardiopulmonary involvement (97.87%) and serositis, with enrichment of inflammatory CD161+ regulatory T cells. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses confirmed distinct molecular signatures and cluster-specific enrichment of biological pathways. Key molecule apolipoprotein A4 was validated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Regarding therapeutic outcomes at 1 year follow-up, Cluster 2 had the highest proportions achieving lupus low disease activity state (46.42%) and tapering glucocorticoids. Cyclophosphamide use was associated with greater clinical improvement (r=0.284; p=0.023) in Cluster 3, while in Cluster 5, rituximab use was associated with favourable responses (r=0.286; p=0.019).ConclusionsWe identified five SLE clusters with distinct clinical, immunological features and treatment outcomes, demonstrating an intrinsic link between clinical involvement and immune-inflammatory alterations.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Autoimmune Diseases, Glucocorticoids, Lupus Nephritis

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a paradigmatic chronic autoimmune disease characterised by multisystem involvement and marked clinical heterogeneity. The disease spectrum ranges from mild mucocutaneous manifestations to life-threatening renal, neurological, cardiopulmonary complications and various vasculitis presentations.1 The marked clinical heterogeneity of SLE poses significant diagnostic challenges in early-stage identification and differential diagnosis, with patients often experiencing diagnostic delays of up to 67.2 months from symptom onset to confirmed diagnosis.2 3 This widespread therapeutic delay contributes to persistently high rates of multisystem involvement. Studies demonstrate that SLE patients with renal or neurological involvement exhibit 10-year survival rates of only 83% and 67%, respectively,4 underscoring the critical association between organ damage and mortality risk.

Machine learning approaches, particularly unsupervised clustering, have provided unprecedented opportunities for unravelling the heterogeneity of autoimmune diseases, with existing research demonstrating their application in subtyping SLE, Sjögren’s syndrome and antiphospholipid syndrome.5,9 Unlike supervised methods constrained by existing disease labels, unsupervised algorithms can reveal natural patient clusters based on the intrinsic structure of integrated clinical-laboratory datasets.10

By applying unsupervised machine learning approaches to a prospectively collected cohort of 1065 SLE patients, we aim to: (1) identify clinically meaningful clusters based on integrated clinical-laboratory profiles and (2) validate the prognostic relevance of these clusters through longitudinal outcome tracking. Moreover, we performed serum proteomic profiling to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) across clusters, complemented by peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to investigate the underlying gene expression signatures. This integrated approach provided proteomic and transcriptomic evidence supporting the molecular heterogeneity of SLE. This investigation will facilitate early cluster recognition and precision interventions to mitigate organ damage, ultimately advancing diagnostic accuracy and prognostic outcomes in SLE.

Methods

Study population

The dataset analysed in this study was derived from the Cross-Country Lupus cohort and included 1065 patients with SLE who were hospitalised between January 2012 and August 2024 at five tertiary centres in China: Peking University People’s Hospital (n=1005), Peking University International Hospital (n=15), Jinling Hospital of the Medical School of Nanjing University (n=15), China–Japan Friendship Hospital (n=15) and the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (n=15). Inclusion criteria: (1) age 18–70 years and (2) diagnosis of SLE meeting both the 1997 American College of Rheumatology classification criteria and the 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics classification criteria.2 11 Exclusion criteria: incomplete core medical records precluding reliable clinical assessment. In addition, 899 patients were followed prospectively for 1 year to evaluate treatment and remission outcomes across clusters. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (2019PHB007-01), and all patients provided informed consent.

Data collection

Clinical and laboratory data were comprehensively collected at baseline and at 1-year follow-up, including demographic characteristics (age, sex, disease duration), multisystem involvement and standardised disease activity indices. Cutaneous involvement—including rash, photosensitivity, mucosal ulcers, alopecia, Raynaud’s phenomenon and cutaneous vasculitis—was documented. Neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE) manifestations, including epileptic seizures, psychiatric symptoms, organic brain syndrome, cranial neuropathy, lupus headache, cerebrovascular accidents (CVA), myelopathy and peripheral neuropathy, were also recorded. Cardiac involvement included pericarditis, myocarditis, endocarditis, coronary artery disease and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). Pulmonary complications, including diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, interstitial lung disease and pleuritis, were systematically evaluated. Systemic vasculitis, comprising retinal and mesenteric vasculitis, as well as lupus nephritis, was also documented.

Disease activity was assessed using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K). Laboratory assessments included complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, renal indices (urinalysis, 24-hour urinary protein, serum creatinine), hepatic transaminases, complement components (C3 and C4), immunoglobulin isotypes (immunoglobulin G (IgG), IgA and IgM), autoantibodies and antiphospholipid antibodies. Continuous variables comprised age, disease duration, laboratory measurements and SLEDAI-2K. Childhood-onset (onset before 18 years), clinical manifestations, autoantibody status and hypocomplementaemia were analysed as binary variables.

Antinuclear antibodies were detected by indirect immunofluorescence and defined as positive at a titre ≥1:40. Extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) antibodies measured by chemiluminescence immunoassays included anti-Smith antibodies (anti-Sm), anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1RNP), anti-Sjögren’s syndrome-related antigen A antibodies and anti-ribosomal RNP, classified as positive using assay-specific cut-offs (anti-Sm >40 RU/mL; other ENA antibodies >20 RU/mL). Anti-nucleosome antibodies (AnuA) were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and defined as positive at >20 RU/mL. Anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies (anti-dsDNA) were measured by chemiluminescence immunoassay and defined as positive at >30 IU/mL. Anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL; > 10 IU/mL) and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies (aβ2-GPI; > 20 IU/mL) were measured by ELISA, and lupus anticoagulant (LAC) was assessed using dilute Russell’s viper venom time (dRVVT ratio >1.2). All positivity cut-off values were defined according to the manufacturers’ recommendations for the respective assays. All autoantibodies were coded as binary variables (positive vs negative) for downstream statistical analyses. Direct Coombs test results and therapeutic regimens were recorded.

Clustering analysis

Unsupervised clustering was performed on integrated clinical and laboratory data from 1065 patients with SLE. Forty-six variables were selected based on clinical relevance (asterisked in figure 1C). Continuous variables were z-score normalised, and categorical variables were encoded as ordinal or one-hot variables. Variables with >20% missingness were excluded, and remaining missing values were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (five iterations, predictive mean matching), with convergence assessed by diagnostic trace plots (online supplemental figure 1).

Dimensionality reduction was conducted using factor analysis of mixed data (FAMD; FactoMineR package, R). Based on cumulative explained variance and scree plot inspection, the first five components (65.7% of total variance) were retained and used for hierarchical clustering with Ward’s linkage and Euclidean distance, selected a priori for clinical phenotyping.

The optimal number of clusters was determined to be five based on average silhouette width analysis (k=2–10) and dendrogram inspection, with the average and individual silhouette plots provided in the online supplemental figure 2. Cluster stability was assessed with the clusterboot function (fpc package, R; 1000 bootstrap iterations), with mean Jaccard similarity coefficients >0.75 indicating robust clusters. Sensitivity analyses using the adjusted Rand index (mean ARI=0.82), together with individual silhouette plots and consistent dimensionality-reduction visualisations, further supported clustering robustness.

Flow cytometry analysis

PBMCs used for baseline immunophenotyping were isolated from ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated whole blood of 188 patients with SLE (Cluster 1, n=34; Cluster 2, n=64; Cluster 3, n=23; Cluster 4, n=43; Cluster 5, n=24) using Ficoll-Paque PLUS density gradient centrifugation (Cytiva). All samples were processed within 2 hours of venipuncture using a standardised protocol applied uniformly across patients. Immunophenotyping was performed on CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter) and FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences) instruments calibrated daily using CST beads (BD Biosciences). Lymphocyte subsets were quantified using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against: T lymphocytes (CD3+), B lymphocytes (CD19+), helper T cells (CD3+CD4+), cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+) and natural killer (NK) cells (CD3⁻CD16+ CD56+).

Regulatory T cell (Tregs) subsets were defined as follows: Total Tregs (CD4+CD25hiCD127low), cutaneous lymphocyte antigen (CLA)+ Tregs (CD4+CD25hiCD127lowCLA+), CD161+ Tregs (CD4+CD25hiCD127lowCD161+) and Foxp3+ Tregs (CD4+CD25hiFoxp3+; intracellular staining with Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher)). Additional subsets included naïve T cells (CD4+CD45RA+), effector T cells (Teff; CD4+CD25⁻Foxp3⁻) and peripheral T follicular helper cells (pTfh; CD4+CD45RA⁻CXCR5+PD-1+CCR7low).

For helper T (Th) cell polarisation analysis, PBMCs were isolated from heparin-anticoagulated whole blood. PBMCs were stimulated for 5 hours with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail (BD Biosciences) containing phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate, ionomycin and brefeldin A. Following surface staining for CD3 and CD4, cells were fixed and permeabilised (Cytofix/Cytoperm, BD Biosciences) and then intracellularly stained to identify T helper subsets: Th1 cells (interleukin-2 (IL-2), tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interferon gamma (IFN-γ)), Th2 cells (IL-4) and Th17 cells (IL-17).

Absolute counts were calculated from complete blood counts and subset frequencies. Data analysis used FlowJo v10.8 (BD Life Sciences) with gating strategies validated by isotype controls and fluorescence-minus-one controls.

RNA-seq approaches

RNA-seq was performed on PBMCs from 52 patients with SLE with active disease (SLEDAI-2K ≥6; Cluster 1, n=10; Clusters 2–4, n=11 each; Cluster 5, n=9) and 10 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. PBMCs were isolated from EDTA-anticoagulated blood and processed within 2 hours of venipuncture. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a false discovery rate (FDR)<0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥1. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package, with Gene Ontology (GO) terms considered significant at adjusted p<0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Detailed experimental and bioinformatic procedures are provided in the online supplemental methods.

Proteomic approaches

Serum proteomic analysis was conducted in 100 patients with SLE (n=20 per cluster, active disease phase) and 12 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Peripheral blood for serum preparation was collected in tubes containing clot activator and separation gel, and serum was obtained from the supernatant after centrifugation according to standardised procedures. DEPs were identified using an FDR <0.05 and |log2 fold change|≥0.58. Functional annotation and GO enrichment analysis were performed using the clusterProfiler package with multiple-testing correction, and enriched terms were defined at adjusted p<0.05. Full details of sample preparation, mass spectrometry acquisition and data processing are described in the online supplemental methods.

ELISA

Serum apolipoprotein A4 (APOA4) concentrations were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.27.0 (IBM Corp.), R V.4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and GraphPad Prism V.10.0 (GraphPad Software). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean±SD and non-normally distributed variables as median with IQR. Group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) when both normality (p≥0.05) and homogeneity of variance (p ≥0.05) assumptions were met; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. For multiple comparisons, Scheffé’s method was applied for normally distributed variables, while the Nemenyi test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction following ANOVA, or Dunn’s test after Kruskal–Wallis. Categorical variables were summarised as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were assessed using Pearson’s χ² test when expected cell frequencies were ≥5; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation (10 000 replicates) was applied. Associations between therapeutic clusters and clinical improvement were evaluated using partial Spearman correlation, adjusting for baseline SLEDAI-2K scores. Multiple testing was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <0.05 or FDR<0.05, where applicable.

Results

Unsupervised machine learning identifies five distinct SLE clusters

Among the 1065 patients with SLE included in the analysis, the median age was 34 years (IQR 26–48), and 89.7% were female (n=955). The median disease duration was 4.00 years (IQR 0.50–10.00). Baseline data revealed significant inter-individual variability, with diverse organ involvement patterns (table 1).

Unsupervised clustering identified five distinct and clinically interpretable SLE phenotypes (figure 1A,B). Cluster robustness was supported by high Jaccard similarity coefficients (0.76 to 0.88 across clusters; online supplemental figure 3) and consistent results in sensitivity analyses. Each cluster demonstrated characteristic patterns of system involvement, disease activity, laboratory parameters and immune cell subset distribution. The clinical and laboratory features of each cluster are summarised in figure 1C, with key immune cell differences presented in the heatmap (figure 1D) and detailed in online supplemental table 1. Disease duration differed across clusters, with Clusters 1 and 2 characterised by longer disease duration and Clusters 4 and 5 by shorter disease duration; in addition, the proportion of childhood-onset SLE (disease onset before 18 years of age) differed significantly across clusters, with higher proportions observed in Clusters 1 and 3 and lower proportions in Clusters 4 and 5 (figure 1C). To assess whether disease duration was a primary driver of the clustering structure, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding disease duration from the clustering input. The overall clustering structure remained highly similar, as illustrated by comparable dimensionality-reduction patterns (online supplemental figure 4), with high concordance of cluster assignments between analyses (ARI=0.79). Below, we describe the defining features of each cluster in turn. The recruitment centre did not appear to substantially influence the clustering structure, as indicated by the even distribution of centres across clusters in dimensionality-reduction analyses (online supplemental figure 5 and online supplemental table 2).

Cluster 1 (renal; 14.27%) comprised patients with severe renal involvement, including a high prevalence of lupus nephritis (87.5%, p<0.001), the highest 24-hour urinary protein levels (1.82 g/L, p<0.001), and elevated disease activity (median SLEDAI-2K, 14, p<0.001). Immunophenotyping showed an increased proportion of B cells (16.40%, p=0.014) and comparatively reduced NK cells (4.80%, p=0.004) and Th1–TNF-α CD4+ cells (31.90%, p=0.005) relative to other clusters.

Cluster 2 (mucocutaneous; 39.53%) was characterised by predominant mucocutaneous manifestations with mild visceral involvement. Rash (57.24%, p<0.001) and photosensitivity (28.03%, p<0.001) were more frequent than other systemic features. This group had the lowest disease activity (median SLEDAI-2K, 6, p<0.001) and the lowest rates of autoantibody positivity and B cells (8.50%, p=0.014) among all clusters.

In Cluster 3 (neuropsychiatric; 14.74%), neuropsychiatric lupus (NPSLE) was the predominant feature, manifested by lupus headache (42.68%, p<0.001), organic brain syndrome (15.92%, p<0.001), CVA (14.65%, p<0.001) and epileptic seizure (13.38%, p<0.001). Anti-U1RNP (42.04%, p=0.036) was prevalent, while immunophenotyping showed a relative reduction in Foxp3+ Tregs (8.33%, p=0.034).

Cluster 4 (haematologic; 22.63%) showed marked haematological involvement, with cytopenia in 95.85% of patients, Coombs positivity in 87.55% (p<0.001) and hypergammaglobulinaemia (18.80 g/L, p<0.001). Antiphospholipid antibodies were common, including aCL (41.91%, p<0.001), aβ2-GPI (31.54%, p<0.001) and LAC (29.05%, p<0.001). Lymphopenia was observed, with a relative reduction in Tregs (8.90%, p=0.042).

Cluster 5 (cardiopulmonary; 8.83%) was characterised by prominent cardiopulmonary involvement (97.87%, p<0.001) and serositis: PAH (26.60%, p<0.001), pleural effusion (80.85%, p<0.001) and pericarditis (36.17%, p<0.001). A distinctive immune profile was observed in this cluster, with increased proportions of CD161+ Tregs (16.40%, p=0.006) and NK cells (8.99%, p<0.004), along with a reduced proportion of CLA+ Tregs (12.00%, p=0.025).

Transcriptomic profiling across SLE clusters

To investigate the molecular pathways underlying the clinical heterogeneity of SLE, we performed bulk RNA-seq of PBMCs from representative patients in each cluster. Compared with healthy controls, patients with SLE showed increased expression of type I interferon–stimulated genes, including interferon-induced transmembrane protein 1 (IFITM1), interferon-induced protein 44 (IFI44), IFI44-like (IFI44L) and toll-like receptor 9, alongside upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL6 and TNF (online supplemental figure 6).

DEGs were identified for each of the five molecular clusters and analysed by GO pathway enrichment and protein–protein interaction network analysis to identify functionally relevant genes (online supplemental figure 7). As shown in figure 2, each cluster was associated with a distinct transcriptional profile. Cluster 1 was characterised by increased IL6 expression together with reduced IL10 and showed enrichment of inflammatory and cytokine-mediated signalling pathways. Cluster 2 exhibited upregulation of chemokine genes, including C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CXCL5), with enrichment in leucocyte migration and chemokine signalling pathways. In Cluster 3, TNF and CCL20 were prominently upregulated, accompanied by enrichment of pathways related to immune activation and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction. Cluster 4 was distinguished by upregulation of CCL3 and CCL4, with enrichment of pathways associated with myeloid cell activation and inflammatory responses. Cluster 5 showed increased expression of cell cycle–related genes, including cyclin-dependent kinase 1 and cyclin B1, with enrichment of pathways involved in cell cycle regulation and proliferation.

Proteomic analysis for SLE patients from clusters

We further performed serum proteomic analysis in 100 patients with SLE (n=20 per cluster from the original cohort) and age- and sex-matched healthy controls to investigate potential immunopathological mechanisms underlying the identified clusters.

Proteomic profiling revealed distinct molecular alterations between SLE patients and healthy controls (online supplemental figure 8A). Principal component analysis demonstrated clear group separation, reflecting widespread proteomic changes in SLE (online supplemental figure 8B). DEPs were primarily associated with complement regulation, ECM remodelling and thrombo-inflammatory pathways in SLE (online supplemental figure 8C–E).

As shown in figure 3, each cluster displayed a distinct serum proteomic signature. Cluster 1 was characterised by increased expression of APOA4, prostaglandin-H2 D-isomerase (PTGDS) and insulin-like growth factor–binding protein 2 (IGFBP2), with Gene Ontology enrichment highlighting extracellular matrix (ECM)-related and endoplasmic reticulum lumen–associated pathways. To further validate the proteomic findings, serum APOA4 levels were quantified by ELISA, demonstrating significantly higher levels in SLE patients with renal involvement compared with SLE patients without renal involvement, disease controls and healthy controls (figure 3P). Cluster 2 showed upregulation of secreted protein acidic and cysteine-rich (SPARC), CCL18 and pro-platelet basic protein, with enriched terms related to platelet alpha granule lumen, immune effector processes and antimicrobial humoral responses. In Cluster 3, glycoprotein V, annexin A1 and integrin beta-3 were significantly upregulated, accompanied by enrichment of pathways involved in intermediate filament organisation, cell junction assembly and cytoskeletal structure. Cluster 4 exhibited marked upregulation of immunoglobulin heavy constant gamma chains (IGHG1–3), with enrichment of immunoglobulin complex, IgM immunoglobulin complex and lymphocyte differentiation pathways, consistent with enhanced humoral immune activity. Cluster 5 was characterised by increased levels of thrombospondin-1 (THBS1) and P-selectin (SELP), with functional enrichment implicating cell adhesion, platelet activation and integrin-mediated signalling pathways.

Clinical improvement was defined as either a reduction in SLEDAI-2K by ≥4 points from baseline to 1 year, or achievement of a SLEDAI-2K <4 at 1 year. Differential treatment outcomes

At the 1-year follow-up of 899 patients, there were significant differences in treatment response across SLE clusters. The reduction in disease activity, assessed by the change in SLEDAI-2K score (ΔSLEDAI-2K), varied markedly between clusters (p<0.001). Cluster 1 exhibited the greatest improvement, with a median ΔSLEDAI-2K of 10.0 (IQR 4.0–15.0), whereas Cluster 2 showed the smallest reduction, with a median of 3.0 (IQR 0.0–6.0).

Treatment target attainment differed significantly between clusters. The lupus low disease activity state (LLDAS) was achieved most frequently in Cluster 2, with 46.42% of patients meeting this target, and least frequently in Cluster 1, with 27.27% achieving LLDAS (p=0.005). Prednisone tapering to ≤7.5 mg/day was more frequent in Cluster 2 than Cluster 1 (53.01% vs 35.54%, p=0.015) (table 3). Among patients with active disease at baseline (SLEDAI-2K>4; n=668), the proportion achieving post-treatment SLEDAI-2K<4 was highest in Cluster 4 (84.07%) and lowest in Cluster 1 (65.14%; p=0.005) (online supplemental table 3). Given the differences in disease duration across clusters, we additionally performed logistic regression analyses with LLDAS as the outcome and included log-transformed disease duration as a covariate. After adjustment, cluster membership remained significantly associated with achieving LLDAS at the overall level (online supplemental table 4).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive molecular stratification of SLE based on a large and multicentre cohort. Unsupervised clustering revealed five distinct clusters, each characterised by differing system involvement, including renal, cutaneous, neuropsychiatric, haematological and cardiopulmonary manifestations. These clusters were also associated with distinct immunological and molecular features.

SLE exhibits substantial clinical and molecular heterogeneity, reflected in variable phenotypes, complex pathogenic pathways and differing therapeutic responses.1 This complexity continues to drive efforts to define clinically meaningful subtypes and to link phenotypic diversity with underlying biological mechanisms. Previous studies were often limited by small sample sizes or lacked validation against immunological and molecular features.59 12,15 In contrast, our approach integrated standard clinical parameters with multi-omics profiling to delineate molecularly coherent disease subsets. Longitudinal follow-up further allowed evaluation of treatment response across clusters, enhancing the clinical relevance of this stratification framework. Disease duration differed across clusters and may partly reflect disease stage and cumulative treatment exposure. Although sensitivity analyses indicated that the clustering structure and key outcome associations were not primarily driven by disease duration, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes complete disentanglement of stage-related effects from intrinsic biological heterogeneity.

Common immunopathogenic pathways were observed across clusters, particularly interferon-inducible gene activation (eg, IFI44, IFITM1) and cytokine dysregulation.16 In particular, transcriptomic analyses identified IL-6 as a significantly upregulated gene within the nephritis-predominant cluster. IL-6 has long been implicated in lupus pathogenesis by driving B-cell differentiation and T-cell activation; experimental studies support its role in promoting autoantibody production and renal injury, while clinical trials of IL-6 receptor blockade in lupus nephritis have yielded inconclusive results to date.17,21 Our findings reinforce the relevance of IL-6 dysregulation specifically within renal lupus and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target warranting further investigation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that chemokines play a role in the pathogenesis of SLE by recruiting immune cells into affected tissues.22 Beyond the shared immunoinflammatory pathways, transcriptomic results revealed distinct chemokine signatures across the molecularly defined SLE clusters. In the cutaneous-predominant cluster, upregulation of CCL2 and CXCL5 may promote monocyte and neutrophil infiltration, contributing to skin inflammation. Notably, CCL20 was markedly elevated in the neuropsychiatric-predominant cluster. Although previously reported in autoimmune encephalitis and multiple sclerosis, its role in NPSLE remains poorly defined. Our findings suggest that CCL20 may contribute to the pathogenesis of neuropsychiatric involvement in SLE, warranting further investigation.23 24 In the haematological-dominant cluster, we observed upregulation of CCL3 (macrophage inflammatory protein-1α, MIP-1α) and CCL4 (MIP-1β), both of which are key chemokines involved in monocyte and T cell recruitment, and have been linked to heightened inflammatory activity in SLE.22 Whether these chemokines contribute specifically to the development of cytopenias in SLE remains to be elucidated.

Proteomic analyses further delineated the molecular heterogeneity underpinning the five data-driven SLE clusters. In the nephritis-predominant cluster, differential expression of APOA4 and PTGDS suggested dysregulated ECM remodelling and altered prostaglandin metabolism, both of which are implicated in tubulointerstitial injury and glomerular fibrosis.25,27 Importantly, the association of APOA4 with renal involvement was further supported by independent ELISA validation, which demonstrated significantly elevated serum APOA4 levels in patients from the renal-predominant cluster compared with other SLE clusters, disease controls and healthy controls. In parallel, the haematological-dominant cluster was characterised by overexpression of IGHG1–3 and JCHAIN, reflecting enhanced immunoglobulin complex assembly and sustained humoral activation, a hallmark of immune cytopenias and hypergammaglobulinaemia in SLE.28 This molecular profile also resembles the lymphoid cluster previously described in integrative molecular stratification studies of systemic autoimmune diseases, further supporting the biological coherence of this subgroup.29 Notably, the cardiopulmonary cluster exhibited upregulation of THBS1 and SELP, molecules associated with endothelial activation, platelet adhesion and leucocyte recruitment. These alterations point toward ongoing vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, processes that may underlie pulmonary hypertension, serositis and accelerated atherosclerosis in lupus.30,32

Importantly, the clinically defined clusters identified in our study exhibit clear conceptual correspondence with molecular endotypes reported in recent multi-omics-based stratification studies of SLE and related systemic autoimmune diseases.29 33 34 Although these studies primarily derive clusters from transcriptomic or proteomic data rather than clinical variables, overlapping biological themes emerge when the results are viewed side by side. For instance, the haematological-dominant cluster in our cohort, characterised by cytopenia, hypergammaglobulinaemia and enrichment of immunoglobulin-related proteomic signatures, parallels the lymphoid and humoral immune-dominant molecular endotypes described in integrative multi-omics analyses.29 Likewise, the cardiopulmonary cluster, marked by endothelial activation and platelet-associated pathways, is consistent with vascular-inflammatory signatures identified in recent multi-omics studies of SLE, particularly those focusing on vascular and endothelial dysfunction.34 Taken together, the recurrence of shared biological features across distinct analytical starting points suggests that clinically driven and molecularly driven clustering approaches capture complementary aspects of SLE heterogeneity, thereby supporting the biological relevance and robustness of the identified subgroups.

In summary, our study highlights the value of unsupervised machine learning applied to clinical data in uncovering biologically and therapeutically meaningful clusters of SLE. These clusters reflect distinct immunopathogenic mechanisms and differential treatment responsiveness. Importantly, integrative proteomic analyses identified cluster-specific molecular signatures, including enrichment of APOA4 in the renal-predominant cluster, which was further supported by independent ELISA validation. Together, these findings strengthen the biological relevance of the clinically defined subgroups and underscore the potential of combining clinical phenotyping with molecular profiling to inform precision medicine in SLE.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the 1-year follow-up period restricts evaluation of long-term clinical outcomes and disease trajectories. Second, although multi-omics analyses identified differential gene and protein signatures across clusters, including APOA4 enrichment in the renal-predominant subgroup, the mechanistic roles of these molecules in disease pathogenesis were not directly investigated. Functional studies in experimental models will be required to elucidate their causal involvement. Future studies incorporating extended longitudinal follow-up and experimental validation of candidate biomarkers are warranted.

Conclusion

In a large Chinese SLE cohort, we applied unsupervised machine learning integrated with multi-omics data to identify clinically and immunologically distinct disease subgroups. Each subgroup exhibited unique phenotypic patterns and immune molecular signatures. Our findings reveal intrinsic links between clinical manifestations and underlying molecular alterations and provide a biologically informed framework for patient stratification, with potential implications for future precision medicine in SLE.
