Refining EI-MS library search results through atomic-level insights
Authors: Islambek Ashyrmamatov, Umit V Ucak, Juyong Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe inherent complexity of mass spectra and the lack of direct correlation between spectral and structural similarities retards structure elucidation and accurate peak annotation. Our methodology employs modified atomic environments from topological radii zero to represent collections of annotated spectral peaks. Rather than aiming for de novo structure prediction from spectra, our objective is to refine and re-rank candidate structures retrieved by existing library search methods using predicted atom environments. We conducted a multi-step complexity reduction to mass-to-fragment mappings and trained the Transformer model to predict the atomic environments of compounds directly from mass and intensity data, achieving a peak precision of 86.1% and a recall rate of 78.4% on the test set. This novel framework not only aids in interpreting EI-MS data by providing insights into structural contents but also refines cosine similarity rankings by suggesting the inclusion or exclusion of certain atomic environments. Our findings over the NIST database suggest that our approach complements conventional methods by improving spectra matching through an in-depth atomic-level analysis.Subject terms: Cheminformatics, Mass spectrometry

Introduction

Chemical analysis fundamentally hinges on structural elucidation by mass spectrometry (MS). Despite a plethora of literature, identifying small molecules from their mass spectra remains an unsolved problem due to its complicated nature1. Electron ionization mass spectrometry (EI-MS) is particularly notable for small-molecule investigation, often used in tandem with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) setups. This technique ionizes molecular samples at a standardized ionization energy, typically set at 70 eV, resulting in a mass spectrum that represents a frequency distribution of ions based on their mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio. While EI–MS can generate fragment-rich mass spectra, the method is limited by the low abundance or absence of the molecular ion, which is essential for accurate calculation of elemental compositions2.

With the advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), various computational approaches have emerged specifically for EI-MS analysis. These can be broadly categorized into three main directions: spectrum prediction, structure elucidation, and structural feature detection. For spectrum prediction from molecular structures, NEIMS3 pioneered neural network-based prediction, achieving 91.8% recall@10 accuracy with millisecond inference times, enabling rapid in silico library augmentation. Subsequent improvements include graph neural networks that better capture molecular topology4,5, RASSP, which combines physically plausible substructure enumeration with deep learning for 92.9% weighted dot product accuracy6, and FastEI, which achieves 592-fold speedup through spectral embeddings, successfully building million-scale in-silico libraries7.

Complementing these machine learning approaches, data-driven fragmentation models like CFM-EI provide peak annotations crucial for downstream learning8, while physics-based simulators compute EI spectra from first principles or semiempirical methods9–13, offering physically grounded predictions but at significantly higher computational cost. For the inverse problem of structure elucidation from EI-MS spectra, DeepEI predicts molecular fingerprints using convolutional neural networks14, while recent transformer architectures like SpecTUS achieve 43% exact structure reconstruction without requiring precursor mass information15. Additional approaches include functional group detection using array-based ML16, deep classification models for direct compound identification17, and two-stage pre-retrieval methods that reduce computational requirements18. Despite these advances, spectra-to-structure prediction faces challenges due to the limited size of available training sets, as only thousands of small molecule MS data are publicly available19, and these end-to-end approaches currently have low accuracy and are difficult for practitioners to incorporate into their existing workflows.

While significant progress has been made in EI-MS, the majority of in silico structure prediction research has focused on tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS). One line predicts molecular fingerprints from spectra and ranks candidates accordingly (e.g., early ML fingerprinting, SIRIUS/CSI:FingerID, and subsequent neural variants)20–22. Another line predicts or scores MS/MS spectra for candidate structures (e.g., CFM-ID 4.0)23. A third line performs de novo or generative translation from MS/MS to structures24–27, with recent models exploring graph transformers, prefix-tree decoding, and GNNs for high-resolution spectra5,28–30. These MS/MS-focused methods aim for full-structure recovery or candidate ranking under tandem fragmentation, whereas here we work with EI-MS and output atomic-environment constraints to refine EI library hits.

Although spectra searching is the preferred approach for EI-MS interpretation, it suffers from a coverage problem: If the query spectrum is outside of the library domain, the correct identification can be overly challenging. This is an issue in practice, since existing mass spectral reference libraries, such as the NIST/NIH/EPA MS database31, Wiley Registry of Mass Spectral Data32, and MassBank33, only contain hundreds of thousands of reference spectra. One strategy to alleviate the coverage problem is augmenting existing libraries with model-generated synthetic spectra; however, the high computational cost of current prediction methods has limited their use in practical applications. While some machine-learning-based methods, such as NEIMS3, enable rapid predictions, other approaches—particularly those relying on quantum chemical simulations—remain computationally expensive, limiting their practical use. Recording additional spectra for more molecules can help mitigate this issue; for instance, NIST updates its library every 3 years, adding ~20K new spectra each time. The inclusion of new molecules in these libraries is often restricted to those of widespread interest, leaving out many newly synthesized compounds.

The accuracy of library matching largely depends on how well the metric reflects the true similarity between the query and reference spectra, assuming reasonable measurement noise (0.4–0.005 Da for low to high-resolution measurements) in obtaining the query spectrum34. Various search algorithms and similarity metrics have been developed over the years to improve this process. Initially, algorithms like the dot-product35 and probability-based matching36 were introduced. Further, metrics such as normalized and absolute Euclidean distance37,38, Hertz similarity index39 representing the weighted average ratio, and neutral-loss matching40 were also developed. Other significant advancements feature Fourier-wavelet-transform-based and partial correlation-based measures, introduced by Koo et al.41 and Kim et al.42, respectively. Among all, the weighted cosine similarity (defined by the standard dot-product), along with its variants such as the simple match factor and identity match factor, is widely adopted in mass spectrometry for molecular identification43.

High-quality spectrum libraries, effective matching algorithms, and accurate peak assignments (annotations) are prerequisites for achieving reliable results in structural elucidation. With the high complexity of mass spectra in mind, there are instances where spectra matching may not yield insightful results, as spectral similarity does not necessarily imply structural similarity. We propose a framework for this problem by using reduced atomic environments from topological radii zero (rAEs) to represent collections of annotated spectral peaks. Rather than aiming for de novo structure prediction from spectra, our objective is to refine and re-rank candidate structures retrieved by existing library search methods using predicted atom environments. Our approach posits that while individual mass spectral fragments are highly diverse and do not directly reconstruct the molecule, the set of molecular building blocks obtained by breaking down fragments into their fundamental rAEs provides both a firm and sufficient basis for predicting molecular content. By employing a multi-step one-to-many complexity reduction strategy for mass-to-fragment mapping, we train a transformer model to predict the structural content of compounds from these rAEs, rather than just fragments. This approach not only facilitates the interpretation of EI-MS data by offering insights into atom-types but also enhances library search results by suggesting corrections (inclusions and/or exclusions) based on the predicted molecular structures, particularly focusing on fragments in the lower mass range.

Results and discussions

Spectral library search stands as the most widely employed technique for structural elucidation. When the exact spectrum of the analyte is absent from the reference library, analysts typically rely on spectra with high similarity scores to infer structural features of the unknown compound. Library searches typically link spectral to structural similarity-a trend supported by Demuth et al.44. We systematically examined the relationship between spectral similarity (modified Match factor3)and structural similarity for 10,000 query spectra randomly selected from the NIST main library. To simulate a realistic scenario, query spectra were removed from the reference library prior to matching. Each query was compared against all remaining spectra, ranked by spectral similarity, and the top-1 candidate was selected.

Overall, correlations with spectral similarity remained weak (Pearson r = 0.508–0.592; Spearman ρ = 0.530–0.634), with many instances exhibiting high spectral similarity but low structural similarity. These patterns confirm that, in the absence of the exact match, high spectral similarity does not necessarily equate to structural similarity.

Mass-to-fragment complexity reduction

Accurate annotation of mass spectra is crucial for deducing key aspects of a compound, such as its molecular formula, structural features, and overall structure47,48. Incorrect annotations can amplify structural dissimilarities, necessitating reevaluation and correction. For instance, identifying the base peak, which represents the most stable fragment, is essential for determining a compound’s molecular formula. However, relying solely on mass for formula determination is challenging due to the possibility of multiple molecular formulae within a 2 millidalton window, a range within the instrument’s measurement error49. Therefore, additional data, such as fragmentation patterns, must be integrated.

To address this, we used CFM-EI8 in-silico fragmentation to explore all candidate ions to populate m/z versus fragments one-to-many mappings. It was selected for its automation capability. Unlike NIST MS-Interpreter, it allows systematic extraction of mean absolute errors (MAEs) and makes it more suitable for high-throughput analysis. Figure 2a outlines the workflow for fragment ion collection. Our analysis focused on experimental EI-MS data from the NIST mass spectral library (version 20), encompassing 350,643 spectra for 306,869 compounds.

The rAEs were extracted from the complete initial dataset of 192,479 NIST compounds with molecular weight ≤ 400 Da prior to any train/test splitting, ensuring comprehensive coverage of chemical space and preventing issues with unseen atom-types during evaluation. Quality filters were applied to remove compounds exceeding the MW threshold: 27,031 compounds, 9823 with fewer than two annotated peaks, and 11,547 containing rare atom environments outside our elemental coverage, resulting in 144,078 remaining compounds. Duplicate source-target pairs were eliminated, and additional sequence length constraints (source > 2 tokens, target ≤ 100 tokens) were applied, yielding 135,891 unique spectra-to-structure datapoints. These were randomly split into a training set of 130,455 samples (95%) and a test set of 5436 samples (5%). The choice of the 400 Da cutoff is not arbitrary but follows prior work and practical considerations in small-molecule mass spectrometry. Our aim is to focus on structurally compact organic molecules, commonly referred to as “small molecules,” which are often defined as having a molecular weight below 500 Da50,51. In particular, we followed the convention used by MolDiscovery19, where molecules with MW≤400Da are treated as super small molecules.

CFM-EI is tailored to predict and annotate EI-MS spectra. It estimates the likelihood of any given fragmentation event occurring, thereby predicting those peaks that are most likely to be observed. At its core, it utilizes a probabilistic generative model to simulate the fragmentation process in the mass spectrometer, scrutinizing and breaking down each bond in a molecule in a breadth-first manner to explore all possible fragment states. It assigns a ’break tendency’ value to the transition from one fragment state to another, calculated based on the chemical characteristics of the bond undergoing fragmentation. The algorithm then focuses on fragments with a high likelihood, allowing it to generate further derivatives and, ultimately, the predicted spectrum. It is important to note that CFM-EI is not an actual simulation of the fragmentation process but rather an annotated interpretation of the spectrum, where each peak is labeled with corresponding molecular fragments. Considering there are more than a hundred peaks on average in experimental EI mass spectra, annotated fragmentation spectra represent the same data with 25 in-silico peaks on average. The CFM-EI model generated a staggering 4,899,239 fragment SMILES across 192,000 input spectra, resulting in 338,076 unique fragments based on canonical SMILES deduplicated using RDKit. For instance, at an m/z value of 150, our dictionary contained 4505 potential ion fragments expressed as SMILES. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1, experimental NIST spectra contain an average of 106.37 ± 63.30 peaks per spectrum, while the corresponding in-silico spectra are notably sparser, averaging 25.45 ± 26.31 peaks.

To address the amplified complexity, we first applied a structural pairwise similarity cutoff to reduce redundancy among ion fragments. We utilized the Tanimoto metric with the Morgan fingerprint, configured with a radius of 1 and a bit vector length of 1024. Formally, letting I be the collection of ions, where each ion i∈I is associated with a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) that belongs to the integer domain, we define a function Tc(i, j) to compute the Tanimoto coefficient between any two ions i,j∈I. Then, for all pairs (i, j) where i,j∈I and i ≠ j, if sim(i, j) ≥ 0.80, we removed either i or j from I. For m/z value of 150, from nearly 10 million pair comparisons, we identified 83 pairs above the threshold of 0.8 and eliminated 78 of them. Our observation reveals that highly similar ions were not as common as anticipated, particularly at lower mass-to-charge ratios. We were able to discard only two ions out of 94 fragments for m/z at 70.

Closer inspection of ion structures showed the presence of atom-type level intricacies in the fragment dataset. We filtered specific atom-types and corresponding ions, guided by their frequency of occurrence. This elimination was absolutely necessary and, concomitant with the inspection, allowed us to scrutinize for further refinement possibilities. As depicted in generalized SMARTS notations52 in Fig. 2b, our attention was particularly on singletons, doubletons, and those with less common features such as heavy metals, aromatic phosphorus and selenium. Consequently, from the initial dataset of 338,076 ions, we purged 115 atom-types that were present in 15,495 ions.

We have represented fragment ions by their constituent atomic environments (AE), a method whose representational effectiveness and applicability in AI models were demonstrated in our previous studies53,54. Our dataset curation adhered to specific rules aimed at maintaining informational balance in AE representation, including avoiding heavy atom connections, preserving ring structures and aromaticity, and neutralizing positive charges (e.g., the presence of sulfur in a fragment implies its presence in the parent molecule, while charge states are disregarded when preserving MAEs) This curation is depicted in the spider graph (see Fig. 2b) for the most common elements in the consolidated 200 mappings derived from an initial broader set (see Supplementary Tables S1–S8 for the complete list of mappings). For instance, 19 nitrogen-centric AEs were collapsed into 9 rAEs through this protocol. The chemical elements covered by our fragment dataset are limited to C, N, O, S, P, Si, B and halogens, but enough to cover more than 94% of druglike molecules based on the ChEMBL database55,56.

To clarify our approach, we emphasize the distinction between combining fragments and combining rAEs. rAEs serve as fundamental units that are more descriptive than individual atoms, providing a richer context for understanding molecular structure. While each peak in the mass spectrum corresponds to a unique fragment, these fragments are generally subgraphs of the original molecular structure. However, in cases of rearrangements or secondary processes during fragmentation, the resulting fragments might not exhibit straightforward subgraph isomorphism with the original structure, complicating the task of molecular inference. From a computational standpoint, simply aggregating these fragments does not allow for accurate molecular structure prediction because the fragments do not wholly support each other’s existence in a way that reveals the overall molecular content. In contrast, our approach treats rAEs as the core building blocks of both fragments and entire molecules. With only a few hundred unique rAEs compared to tens of thousands of distinct fragments, focusing on these rAEs enables a more effective deduction of molecular content.

Lastly, our isotopic abundance evaluation, especially for elements like chlorine, bromine, and sulfur, necessitated an adjustment in peak cutoff values regarding the peak density. We implemented an intensity cutoff of 0.1 if they were present in the mass spectra, compared to a standard cutoff of 0.4. While these elements’ isotopic patterns are not always observable, when available, they offer substantial insight. In Fig. 2b, we summarize the abovementioned multi-step complexity reduction process. After the whole reduction procedure, we quantified the so-called complexity (average number of fragments per m/z) reduction as 36.5% (see Fig. 3). In the subfigure of Fig. 3, the initial count of 4505 fragments at m/z value of 150 was reduced by about 45.1%.

Model performance

We assessed the model’s performance in Fig. 4c by calculating set-based accuracy, recall, and precision metrics, which were adapted from traditional classification metrics to evaluate our predictions. In our target data setup, we can evaluate the model outputs in two ways: by considering all rAEs from both fragments and the molecule, or by focusing exclusively on the molecular rAEs predicted after the special <mol> token. In Fig. 4c, accuracy is defined as the fraction of total correct predictions, where P represents the set of predicted atom-types and A denotes the set of actual atom-types, and is calculated as ∣P ∩ A∣/∣P ∪ A∣. This yielded an accuracy of 56.32% for all rAEs and 68.19% for molecular rAEs. Recall, ∣P ∩ A∣/∣A∣, represents the proportion of actual atom-types that were correctly predicted, with values of 60.39% for all rAEs and 78.39% for molecular rAEs. Precision, (∣P ∩ A∣/∣P∣), measures the relevancy of predicted atom-types, and was recorded at 86.10% for all rAEs and 78.18% for molecular rAEs.

Transformer model for predicting reduced atomic environments (rAEs) from EI-MS.

a Peak intensities are encoded as logRanks and combined with m/z values as inputs to the transformer encoder. b The decoder then predicts structural content of fragment ions and molecular content as reduced atomic environments (rAEs). c The bar graph displays the model’s accuracy, recall, and precision metrics. d Exemplifies the input-output representation as source–target pairs.

These metrics describe the performance of our model in predicting rAEs from spectral data, where the set-based accuracy corresponds to the Tanimoto coefficient, and the complement of recall relates to Hamming Loss, indicating how effectively the model identifies relevant atom-types. These results provided a basis for refining spectral similarity rankings through in-depth analysis at the atomic level. To assess the stability of our model with respect to random initialization, we conducted five independent training runs using different random seeds: 42, 101, 314, 777, and 2023. We evaluated performance using set-based accuracy. The resulting accuracies for both “all rAEs” and “molecular rAEs” settings are reported in Supplementary Table S9. Standard deviation is a common indicator of model robustness, with higher values suggesting greater dependence on random seed selection57. Across these runs, we observed low standard deviations (≤0.9%), indicating minimal sensitivity to initialization.

Figure 5 illustrates the framework as it collects information from each peak and collectively elucidates the structural content. Since atom types are conserved across fragments (environments may change due to bond breaking), the model assesses the frequency of each rAE within the spectrum. For example, in Fig. 5, predicted atom-types that are recurrent across multiple fragments for the given in-silico spectrum with 17 peaks are shown. Each column in the main matrix represents a peak in the spectrum, and each row represents an rAE. The integer values in the matrix indicate the occurrence of each rAE in that peak’s predicted fragment(s). Therefore, the sum column at the right reflects the total number of supporting peaks for each rAE. A higher sum suggests greater support for the presence of that atom-type in the molecule, as multiple fragment ions corroborate its presence. In other words, an atom type supported by a majority of fragments would be deemed highly probable to be part of the true structure. Conversely, an atom-type supported by only a few fragments would raise doubts about its presence, potentially pointing to an incorrect annotation. By exploiting the interconnectedness and richness of fragment ions, the output of the model can be seen as a probabilistic representation of the molecular content, where the likelihood of each rAE’s presence is quantified. The results allow us to fine-tune reference library hits, suggesting targeted inclusions and/or exclusions of atom-types for improved accuracy.

In this setting, true positives (TP), identified by the presence of any atom-types correctly predicted by the model and confirmed by the ground truth but absent in the reference molecules, are recorded for each case. Similarly, the presence of any atom types present in both the ground truth and the reference molecules, but missed by the model, is recorded for false negatives (FN). The multi-set molecular content comparisons in Fig. 6 reveal consistent trends across all three search methods. There is a noticeable increase in TP and TN rates as the top-k parameter increases, while FN rates display a downward trend. When using all rAEs, TP rates range from 47% to 66%, and TNs vary between 32% and 49%. The molecular rAE counts follow a similar trend but are switched in order, leading to a higher TN rate of 63% at the top-10 for mMF. The rate of FNs drops by 5–6% in both scenarios. All three trends act together in the direction of improving the quality of suggestions made to the library hits.

When comparing the different methods, we find that the performance of library searches is similar whenever the query molecule or its analog is not present in the library. The results in Fig. 6 pin down a way to interpret the quality of the library search results. For instance, the Top-1 results for all rAEs in the NIST hybrid search59,60 show that 50% of the test cases miss at least one rAE, while 35% include an rAE not present in the query molecule. The rationale behind the proposed approach is further validated by performance metrics that are independent of the specific library search strategy employed. Note that TP, TN, FP, and FN are defined per predicted rAE within each query molecule, rather than per molecule. Consequently, a single molecule may simultaneously contribute to multiple categories. For instance, the model may correctly predict one rAE (TP), fail to recover another (FN), and correctly exclude a third (TN). Therefore, the percentage values in Fig. 6 reflect the proportion of molecules exhibiting each classification outcome, not mutually exclusive events.

False positive are not equally important as other metrics due to the nature of fragmentation. The model is very inclusive in its structure and design; hence, it tends to predict potential positives that arise from bond breaking. Particularly, this occurs when a ring structure is broken, resulting in the loss of ring-specific information. Although the specific tokens change, the atom-types themselves remain unchanged. False positives are also evident in the hydrogen counts on carbon and nitrogen atoms. Human experts can readily recognize the relation in bonding types, such as between CH3, CH2, CH, and C or between NH2, NH, and N, and interpret those variations as specific indicators of elemental presence.

Case studies

Through the review of the results, three possible cases were identified: inclusion, exclusion, and a combination of both, as illustrated in Fig. 7. In the first case, the query molecule N#CCN=C=S was matched with a top-1 hit that exhibited a spectral similarity score of 0.831. The identified rAE content of the top-1 hit, comprising '[CH3]', '[S]', '[CH2]', '[C]', '[CH]', did not include nitrogen. This was in contrast to our model’s prediction, which included nitrogen, corroborated by multiple peaks and specifically indicated by '[N]': 4. In the second case, the library search for the molecule CCCCCCCCC(C)NCCCC yielded a top-1 candidate with a spectral similarity score of 0.869. The rAE content of the top-1 hit encompassed a variety of atom-types, including '[CH3]', '[C;R]', '[CH2]', '[C]', '[O;R]', '[N]', '[CH2;R]', and '[CH;R]', indicating the presence of an oxygen atom within a ring structure '[O;R]'. Our model, however, did not predict oxygen and instead assigned scores to other atom-types: '[CH3]': 21, '[CH2]': 24, '[NH2]': 6, '[C]': 2, '[CH]': 12, '[NH]': 1. The absence of '[O;R]' in our prediction suggested that oxygen, especially within a ring, may not be a part of the molecule’s true structure.

The third case examined was an organosulfur compound CN(C)C(=S)NC(=S)N(C)C. The top-1 hit included silicon and oxygen within the following content '[CH3]', '[Si]', '[O]', '[N]'. In contrast, our model featured sulfur at five peaks ('[S]': 5) and omitted silicon, suggesting that sulfur is likely to be a part of the actual structure, while silicon is a result of an erroneous annotation. These examples highlighted the potential of our method to refine structural hypotheses by enabling the inclusion of atom-types that are supported by the model and the exclusion of those that are not. The content generated by our model introduces an additional layer to refine spectral similarity results to further increase the identification rates.

The inclusion or exclusion—more precisely, the presence or absence—of substructures in NIST software fundamentally differs from the approach we proposed herein. NIST’s substructure analysis estimates the likelihood of specific substructures in an unknown compound by examining user-generated search results. This involves comparing the substructures of compounds in the hit list to those of the unknown compound, using predefined parameters to predict the probability of certain substructures. As such, NIST’s substructure analysis requires the presence of a hit list as a prerequisite.

As a representative biological case study, we selected a small-molecule chitinase inhibitor (C10H6F3N3OS; MW = 273.24 Da; preclinical stage) with favorable drug-like properties (QED = 0.91, no Lipinski violations). This compound was chosen because the comparison among ground truth, predicted, and reference EI-MS spectra covers all disjoint rAE regions in our Venn analysis, making it ideal for illustrating fragment-level interpretation. Chitinase inhibitors61 hold biomedical potential for treating respiratory diseases such as asthma and allergic airway inflammation, as well as for combating chitin-containing pathogens. The compound was developed to inhibit human acidic mammalian chitinase, an enzyme linked to airway inflammation, asthma, and certain parasitic and fungal infections62.

Refining library search rankings

To evaluate ranking improvements, we employed three complementary metrics that capture different aspects of ranking quality. Kendall’s tau coefficient63 quantifies the ordinal association between two rankings by counting concordant and discordant pairs, ranging from −1 (perfect disagreement) to +1 (perfect agreement). The average swap distance, derived from Kendall’s tau as n(n−1)4(1−τ), represents the minimum number of adjacent transpositions required to transform one ranking into another, where lower values indicate closer alignment between rankings. Normalized discounted cumulative gain64 (NDCG@k) measures the quality of ranking by comparing predicted rankings against ideal rankings based on structural similarity, with logarithmic discounting that emphasizes top positions. NDCG values range from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect ranking.

Table 2 presents the ranking evaluation across 5436 query molecules. The baseline spectral ranking achieved NDCG@10 of 0.909 ± 0.083, while molecular rAEs maintained competitive performance at 0.902 ± 0.093. More notably, molecular rAEs improved Kendall’s tau to 0.337 ± 0.351 from the baseline 0.240 ± 0.300, representing a 40% increase in ranking correlation with ground truth. This improvement translates to a reduction in average swap distance from 34.2 ± 13.5 to 29.8 ± 15.8 swaps, indicating that molecular rAEs produce rankings requiring 13% fewer pairwise corrections to reach the ideal ordering.

Limitations

At present, we recognize that no tool, including CFM-EI or the recent version NIST MS-Interpreter65, is without limitations in peak assignment accuracy. The limitation we refer to is tied to the quality of the generated training data. In this context, our statement was not an indictment of CFM-EI per se, but rather a reflection of the general limitations inherent in using predicted structures corresponding to MS peaks. Also, while the model exhibits robust performance within its specified domain, its applicability to more complex scenarios like MS/MS tandem mass spectra is currently limited. Furthermore, our fragment dataset, confined to elements C, N, O, S, P, Si, B, and halogens, provides sufficient coverage only for drug-like small organic molecules.

Our model was trained on spectra of super-small molecules; thus, relaxing the molecular weight cutoff beyond 400 Da increases the complexity of mass-to-fragment mapping, thereby tightening the trade-off. In inference mode, particularly for larger spectra, the model relies more heavily on the lower end of the spectrum for structural content prediction. Despite these limitations, the model finds strength in the sufficient number of peaks present in idealized in-silico spectra and fragmentation patterns. All rAE fragments were constructed from low-resolution EI spectra in the NIST 2017 library. While this ensures consistency with prior benchmarks, high-resolution EI-MS data such as that acquired via Orbitrap or TOF could improve fragment-level interpretation. However, such high-res spectra remain limited in open databases like MassBank and GNPS, and show systematic differences from low-res spectra, making integration non-trivial6,13,66,67.

Conclusion

Mass spectral reference libraries provide a means of identification for compounds. In EI-MS data, where the molecular ion peak is typically missing, conducting a thorough library search becomes even more important. In addition to the absence of a direct correlation between spectral similarity and structural similarity, the inherent complexity of mass spectra introduces a significant challenge for structure elucidation, specifically for accurate peak annotation. In this work, we have introduced a follow-up analysis to library search, employing atomic environments, the CFM-EI fragmentation tool, neural machine translation, and the structural similarity concept. This approach aims to refine library search results of unknown EI-MS data, offering a novel solution to the challenges of structure elucidation. To achieve this, we utilized reduced atomic environments (unconnected, stand-alone substructures) to represent fragment ions as well as an approach that models how a molecule might fragment within the collision cell of the mass spectrometer.

In many instances of EI-MS data, peaks are not uniquely identifiable, leading to ambiguity in structural elucidation. We developed a multi-step plan to reduce the one-to-many (mass-to-fragment) mapping complexity. Subsequently, we trained a transformer model to predict the structural content of unknown peaks using their mass and intensity information. Each peak, through its assigned rAE blocks, contributes to a unified understanding of the compound’s structure at the atomic level. The interconnectedness of fragment ions is crucial because it implies that the peaks corroborate each other’s content. The outcomes of the model undergo post-processing to yield scores for each atom-type, thereby facilitating targeted corrections to the results of the library search. In other words, if the candidate structure retrieved at higher ranks and structural content predicted by our model have differences in atom-types, our model suggests a need to adjust (inclusions and/or exclusions) certain annotations yielding the differences. Tests on EI-MS data from the NIST database have demonstrated that predicting the structural content in conjunction with spectral hits helps to reduce this uncertainty, narrowing down the range of potential candidates for consideration.

Methods

Our model operates on the principle of interpreting each peak not as a unique fragment, but as an assembly of rAEs. This allows for a granular breakdown of each fragment into its constituent atom-types. Figure 4b demonstrates our method of translating spectral data into structural information using a transformer model68,69. Peaks are assigned a logRank, a logarithmic intensity measure ranging from 1 to 7 (see Fig. 4a). This system, adapted from the work of Cao and Guler19, is designed to minimize parameter counts and prevent overfitting, offering a more refined approach than traditional intensity rankings. Inputs to the transformer model consist of m/z values, intensity indicators (’i’), and their corresponding logRanks.

The heart of the model is the transformer decoder, which takes the encoded context and predicts output probabilities for each input token, essentially ’decoding’ the tokens that carry both mass and intensity information into the target data. The target data in our model is structured to capture the relationship between fragment ions and molecular content. Specifically, it is represented by sequences of blocks of rAEs–groupings of atom-types that constitute the observed fragment ions–followed by a special token <mol>, which marks the transition to representing the overall molecular structure. Each block of rAEs on the left side of <mol> depicts atom-types from individual fragments, treated as separate tokens, while the rAEs to the right of <mol> indicate the atom-types comprising the molecule itself, also treated as individual tokens. Since rAEs represent disconnected substructures, permuted variants (e.g., [O].[N] vs. [N].[O]) correspond to the same underlying chemical structure. To ensure consistency and reproducibility, all rAEs-groupings are sorted alphabetically before being passed to the model. Although the self-attention mechanism in transformers is inherently permutation invariant, we still apply positional encodings, as prior studies have shown that incorporating positional information–as a training-time convention–improves learning stability and model performance, even for order-invariant inputs70–72.

In the absence of a molecular ion peak in the training data, the structural content of the molecular ion can still be accurately represented by deriving its rAEs directly from the molecular structure. These rAEs are equivalent, in terms of atom-type content, to those that would be obtained from the molecular ion fragment if it had been observed or correctly predicted. Our transformer model does not depend on the presence of a molecular ion peak. Instead, it learns peak-to-fragment relationships via contextual embeddings over the entire spectrum. This means the model does not require a strict one-to-one correspondence between individual peaks and their source fragments. Rather, it integrates contextual evidence from all observed peaks to infer the most probable molecular atom-types.

Such structured tokenization aligns with our hypothesis that the molecular content can be deduced from the rAEs-groupings generated from spectral peaks. As a final step, the generated rAE blocks undergo post-processing-aggregated, decomposed, and organized into a set to construct atom-type content of a given mass spectrum. The PyTorch machine learning library was used for constructing and training the transformer model73. Model training setup and hyperparameters are summarized in the Supplementary Methods. As a side note, while methods such as MolForge53 exist for reconstructing molecules from atomic environments, direct reassembly from predicted rAEs remains infeasible in our setting. This is because EI-induced bond dissociations generate fragment-derived rAEs not present in the intact molecule, inflating the prediction space with spurious atom environments and hindering accurate reconstruction.
