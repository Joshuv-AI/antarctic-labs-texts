Relationship quality among people living with HIV/AIDS: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Marcin Rzeszutek, Ewa Gruszczyńska, Magdalena Grabowska, Paula Malinowska, Barbara Sosińska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to summarize the existing literature on the factors associated with relationship quality among people living with HIV (PLWH).MethodA structured literature search was performed on APA PsycArticles, ProQuest and PubMed for quantitative, peer-reviewed studies published in English between 1994 and March 2025. Fifteen studies (N = 2579; 78% men, predominantly in serodiscordant couples) met the inclusion criteria and contributed 76 effect sizes, which were categorized as either protective or risk factors for relationship quality among PLWH. A three-level meta-analytic model was employed to address the nested data structure.ResultsThe pooled effect sizes were comparable for protective factors (r = 0.26, 95% CI [0.08, 0.43]) and risk factors (r = 0.24, 95% CI [0.16, 0.31]), reflecting small-to-moderate effects. Significant heterogeneity was observed at the between-study level, whereas within-study variability was negligible. The only significant moderator was relationship length: the association between protective factors and relationship quality was stronger in longer-term PLWH relationships.ConclusionThis review and meta-analysis suggest that, despite the unique context of HIV/AIDS, several correlates of relationship quality may reflect broader patterns observed across populations. The findings improve our understanding of factors associated with psychological well-being among PLWH, particularly given the importance of satisfying romantic relationships for quality of life in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02555-3.

Introduction

Intimate relationships are a key element of overall quality of life [1]. Numerous studies have demonstrated a strong association between relationship status and various quality of life indices, particularly with regard to emotional and physical health outcomes (see, for example, the following reviews and meta-analyses [2–4]. However, psychological well-being depends not only on having a relationship, but also on its quality [5]. Both classic and contemporary research indicates that individuals in distressed or ‘unhappy’ marriages often report a lower quality of life, for example poorer physical health and psychological functioning, than their single counterparts [2].

Relationship quality is a subjective, multidimensional evaluation of one’s romantic or intimate partnership, typically encompassing satisfaction, emotional support, intimacy, commitment and conflict [6, 7]. In other words, it reflects the extent to which partners’ perceived emotional, relational and instrumental needs are met [8, 9]. It is often assessed using self-report instruments; the most widely used are the Dyadic Adjustment Scale [10], the Couple Satisfaction Index [7] and the Quality of Relationship Inventory [11]. While these measures differ in their theoretical foundations and the specific dimensions assessed, relationship quality has consistently been linked to a wide range of outcomes. A growing body of research has demonstrated that high-quality relationships support psychological functioning, self-regulation, and adaptive coping throughout an individual’s lifespan, as well as physical health and longevity [12, 13]. Individuals in high-quality, satisfying relationships experience lower levels of stress, depression and anxiety, and exhibit better immune and cardiovascular functioning, greater resilience and stronger goal pursuit and self-efficacy. They are also more likely to engage in health-promoting and prosocial behaviours [4, 9, 14, 15]. By contrast, low-quality or distressed relationships have been linked to emotional dysregulation, heightened physiological reactivity, impaired sleep and an increased risk of mental and physical health issues [16].

The benefits of an intimate relationship are also evident among couples coping with chronic illnesses such as HIV/AIDS [17, 18]. Advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have transformed HIV/AIDS from a terminal illness into a manageable chronic condition [19]. However, the aforementioned great progress in biomedical care of people living with HIV (PLWH) has not directly translated into an improvement in their psychological well-being. In particular, PLWH have consistently reported lower levels of well-being and higher emotional distress compared to the general population [20], as well as poorer health-related quality of life (HRQoL) compared to those with other chronic diseases [21]. Therefore, further examination of the factors responsible for enhancing HRQoL in this population is necessary. According to an increasing number of authors, one of the most relevant factors is having a satisfying intimate relationship [22, 23].

Advances in HIV/AIDS treatment have changed the way couples living with HIV function. In the early stages of the epidemic, affected couples were concerned about losing a partner, transmitting HIV within the relationship, uncertainty regarding disease progression and pervasive social stigma [24]. However, with life expectancy for PLWH now approaching that of the general population, AIDS-related mortality is no longer the primary source of distress for these couples [25]. The advent of next-generation ART has substantially shifted the definition and perception of HIV-related risk, particularly among men who have sex with men (MSM) [26]. In such relationships, partners often no longer perceive HIV as a significant threat compared to other health or social concerns. Instead, they tend to prioritise relationship quality over issues related to sexual safety or HIV transmission [27]. However, HIV/AIDS-related stigma persists as a primary source of distress and diminished quality of life, including health-related quality of life, among PLWH at both the individual and dyadic levels [21, 28–30]. A recent meta-analysis found that, despite changes in the explicit manifestations of stigmatisation, the overall perceived level of stigma among PLWH has remained largely unchanged since the onset of the epidemic [20].

Most research on HIV-related stigma in couples has focused on serodiscordant couples (i.e. couples where one partner is HIV-positive and the other is HIV-negative). However, few studies have considered seroconcordant couples (i.e. couples in which both partners are HIV positive) despite evidence suggesting that stigma may be experienced differently depending on the composition of the couple [31]. For example, Siegel et al. [32] found that HIV-negative partners in serodiscordant couples may experience ‘courtesy stigma’, such as social avoidance, because they are perceived as being the same as their HIV-positive partner. Conversely, seroconcordant couples may face distinct challenges linked to their shared HIV-positive status [33].

Aim of the review and metanalysis

A systematic review of the literature on how PLWH function in romantic relationships could inform couples-based intervention programmes [21]. In particular, the factors associated with relationship quality in these couples are not well understood [22]. To address this, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, aiming to synthesise evidence on the associations between relationship quality (the outcome construct) and positive (protective) and negative (risk) correlates, including various psychological and physical health aspects, in these couples. We included original quantitative, empirical, full-text publications in English that were published in a peer-reviewed journal, and that (1) had adult participants with HIV who were currently in a romantic relationship with an HIV-positive partner (seroconcordant couples), or an HIV-negative partner (serodiscordant couples); (2) measured the relationship quality of at least one HIV-positive partner using psychometrically sound measures; and (3) examined the association between relationship quality and the aforementioned correlates. We excluded studies focusing on caregivers, children, or other family members of PLWH, as well as systematic reviews, scoping reviews, meta-analyses, qualitative reports and interventional studies.

Methods

Study selection

The literature review and study selection were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [34]. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO on 24 March 2025 (record number CRD420250654079). The APA PsycArticles, ProQuest and PubMed databases were searched between 1 and 14 March 2025 using the following Boolean query: (HIV OR AIDS OR acquired human immunodeficiency syndrome OR human immunodeficiency virus) AND (couples OR marriage OR married OR relationships OR marital OR dyad) AND (relationship quality OR quality of relationship). Specifically, we aimed to capture studies that explicitly conceptualize the focal construct at a global, relational level rather than studies examining isolated components (e.g., trust, intimacy, or sexual satisfaction) without framing them as indicators of overall relationship functioning. This approach was designed to improve construct coherence and comparability across studies. Following the initial screening, the reference lists of the full-text papers included were manually checked for any significant studies that had been missed.

All studies retrieved from the database were uploaded to Nested Knowledge (https://about.nested-knowledge.com/) and deduplicated using the automatic duplicate detector. Five authors (MR, MG, BS, PM and EG) screened all records independently based on their titles and abstracts. Next, the full text of the selected articles was retrieved and reviewed by all five authors to make the final selection. The reporting completeness of the included observational studies was assessed by three reviewers (PM, MG and BS) using the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist [35]. This tool evaluates the completeness and transparency of reporting across key methodological domains, including study design, participant selection, variable measurement, and statistical methods. A study was only included in the analysis when unanimous agreement was reached.

Moreover, two reviewers (MG and BS) also extracted descriptive data, which was checked by a third reviewer (PM). Apart from data on sample description, author names and year of publication, the reviewers extracted detailed data on relationship quality measures and correlates. In cases where there were multiple relationship quality measures or correlates, all of these were included in the data extraction, resulting in a hierarchical structure for our meta-analysis database. The correlates were classified by content-based domain (e.g. mental health: psychological distress, depressive symptoms; physical health: adherence, self-efficacy, substance use; sexual risk behaviours: condom use) and by the direction of their association with relationship quality (protective factors: common dyadic coping; risk factors: depressive symptoms). This approach reflects whether a given variable is associated with more favorable or less favorable relationship outcomes. It should be noted that we refrained from inferring causality or temporal precedence, as this was beyond the scope of the present meta-analysis, particularly given that only two of the included studies employed a longitudinal design.

Statistical analysis

Traditional meta-analytic approaches assume statistical independence among effect sizes. However, to address the issue of multiple effect sizes derived from the same study sample, we conducted a multilevel meta-analysis (MLMA [36, 37]. MLMA extends the classical random-effects model by incorporating three levels of variability: (1) sampling variance within effect sizes; (2) within-study variance (i.e. between effect sizes from the same study); and (3) between-study variance [38]. From a practical standpoint, distinguishing between these levels allows us to identify sources of heterogeneity in the findings. Specifically, sampling variance, or Level 1 variance, reflects the precision of each individual effect-size estimate and is largely determined by study-specific sampling error. Within-study variance, or Level 2 variance, indicates whether different instruments, subscales, or outcomes used within a single study yielded consistent results. Between-study variance, or Level 3 variance, reflects the extent to which effect sizes varied across study populations and contexts. By partitioning variance in this way, this approach enables more precise estimation of the average effect size and heterogeneity across studies while accounting for statistical nonindependence [39].

Thus, a three-level MLMA was performed using the ‘metafor’ package [40] in R v4.4.2. Effect sizes were operationalised as Pearson’s r, computed via Fisher’s z transformation. When necessary, unstandardised and standardised regression coefficients reported in primary studies were converted to Pearson correlation coefficients, following the procedures outlined by Lipsey and Wilson [41] and using the esc package. Only the absolute values were used.

To assess heterogeneity among effect sizes, we used Cochran’s Q statistic, which determines whether the observed dispersion is greater than would be expected from sampling error alone [42]. In the context of three-level models, Q provides an omnibus test of heterogeneity; however, it does not differentiate between variance components at the within- and between-study levels [36]. The variance components at Levels 2 and 3 (denoted as τ2) represent true heterogeneity beyond sampling error, reflecting the extent to which effect sizes vary due to methodological or contextual differences within and between studies [36, 37]. A larger τ2 at Level 3 suggests substantial variability attributable to study-level characteristics, whereas a near-zero τ2 at Level 2 indicates consistency of effects reported within individual studies.

Analyses were conducted separately for protective and risk factors because combining these categories would have made the pooled effect difficult to interpret. In the primary studies, protective factors were expected to be positively associated with relationship quality, whereas risk factors were expected to be negatively associated with relationship quality. Accordingly, combining positive and negative coefficients in a single analysis could have attenuated the pooled estimates because of opposing effect directions.

To facilitate comparisons of effect-size magnitudes across categories, absolute values of the coefficients were used in both analyses. Thus, for risk factors, a larger pooled effect indicates a stronger adverse association with relationship quality. Similarly, for protective factors, a larger pooled effect indicates a stronger favorable association with relationship quality.

Potential moderators were examined using meta-regression. Moderators included correlate category (mental health, physical health, or sexual risk behavior), couple type represented by individuals in the study sample (serodiscordant only, seroconcordant only, or mixed), participants’ age, relationship length in years, and whether participants’ age, education, or relationship length had been statistically controlled in the primary studies. Covariate adjustment was included as a moderator because, when primary studies reported regression coefficients rather than Pearson’s r, these coefficients were transformed to Pearson’s r and used as the effect-size metric. However, regression coefficients may vary depending on whether covariates, such as participants’ age, education, or relationship length, were included in the original models. Therefore, we coded whether these covariates had been controlled and included this information as a moderator to examine whether covariate adjustment was associated with variation in pooled effect-size estimates. Sexual orientation and gender were not included in the moderator analyses because these variables were not reported consistently across studies.

Potential publication bias was assessed using contour-enhanced funnel plots. Funnel plot asymmetry may indicate selective reporting of statistically significant findings or an underrepresentation of small-sample studies with nonsignificant effects. However, asymmetry may also reflect sources of heterogeneity other than publication bias alone [43, 44].

The pooled cohort was predominantly male (88.6%). Participants’ mean age ranged from 30.08 years [23] to 49.6 years [17]. Study samples demonstrated variability in terms of couple serostatus and sexual orientation. Nine of the studies involved only serodiscordant couples, four involved couples of varying serostatus (i.e. either HIV-positive or HIV-negative), and two [48, 49] did not report information about partner serostatus. Relationship duration also differed, with average relationship length ranging from 3.5 years [23] to 16.5 years [17]. Geographically, seven studies were conducted in the United States, four in Asia, three in Africa and one in Europe.

Relationship quality was operationalised using various instruments. Specifically, five studies employed the Dyadic Adjustment Scale [10]; four, the Couple Satisfaction Index [7]; three, the Triangular Love Scale [51]; and two, measures adapted from Kurdek’s [52, 53] work on relationship quality.

Discussion

Our study aimed to critically review and investigate the existing literature on the associations between the quality of intimate relationships formed by PLWH and their positive (protective) and negative (risk) correlates. A systematic review of the 15 eligible articles yielded a few general observations. Firstly, all of these studies comprised highly diverse samples in terms of couple serostatus, sexual orientation and relationship length. Nevertheless, there was a tendency for same-sex male couples to be in serodiscordant relationships. Therefore, more research is needed on seroconcordant couples living with HIV, particularly given the differences in experiences of HIV/AIDS stigma depending on couple composition [31].

Secondly, the majority of the reviewed studies were from the USA, which again illustrates the dominance of USA research on various psychological issues among PLWH, ranging from well-being to stigma (e.g. [20, 54]). The latter fact highlights the need for more studies on relationship quality, particularly in Europe, from which only one eligible study originated.

Thirdly, we found significant heterogeneity in the measurement of relationship quality and psychological covariates across the reviewed studies. While this heterogeneity in assessment is notable, it is not uncommon in reviews and meta-analyses addressing complex psychological constructs. A broad operationalisation is often necessary to capture the multifaceted nature of relational dynamics and enhance ecological validity. For example, [55] took a similar inclusive approach to defining relationship quality in their meta-analysis of romantic relationship quality among individuals with chronic illnesses. Consequently, while the use of diverse measures introduces variability, it also reflects the conceptual richness and multidimensionality of relationships [56, 57]. Such a multidimensional approach to operationalizing relationship quality should also be applied to couples living with HIV.

In the meta-analytical part, we examined the relationship quality reported by PLWH who were in romantic relationships in which one or both partners were living with HIV, focusing on its potentially protective and detrimental factors. Our meta-analysis revealed comparable absolute pooled effect sizes for these two categories, with moderate associations with relationship quality: r = 0.26 (95% CI [.08, .43]) for protective factors and r = 0.24 (95% CI [.16, .31]) for risk factors. These correspond to Cohen’s d of 0.49 and 0.54, respectively [58]. While no previous meta-analysis has specifically examined relationship quality among PLWH, it is informative to consider whether these results are consistent with those from other clinical or general populations. Prior meta-analyses on relationship or marital satisfaction, both in general populations and among individuals coping with chronic illness, have highlighted similar associations in terms of direction and strength. For instance, health status, psychological well-being and anxiety have been consistently associated with relationship quality [3, 4, 59, 60] with most effects ranging from small to moderate in size. These findings suggest that, while the context of HIV introduces unique relational stressors (e.g. stigma), the general covariates of relationship quality may be broadly comparable across populations.

Additionally, while 11 studies contributed more than one effect size, greater variability was observed between studies than within them. Notably, the potential codependence among protective factor indicators was negligible, suggesting that methodological and contextual differences between studies have a stronger influence on variability in effect sizes than sampling or measurement factors within studies. It is notable that protective factors constituted only 24% of the extracted effects, indicating a substantial research gap. Further empirical research into relational strengths and resilience processes in couples affected by HIV is needed, particularly to inform the development of interventions at individual and dyadic levels aimed at promoting relationship functioning and broader psychosocial well-being in this population.

Moderators

The observed between-study heterogeneity highlights the importance of examining moderators at the study level. However, within the current set of studies, only one moderator emerged as significant: protective factors. Specifically, the association with relationship quality was stronger in studies involving couples in longer-term relationships. This pattern has been identified previously in studies of relationship quality in general, and it may reflect the cumulative effect of adaptive relational processes, such as communication, commitment and emotion regulation, which become more important as relationships mature [61, 62]. Long-term couples may also benefit from greater interdependence and stability, enabling protective factors to exert more consistent and enduring effects [63]. These findings are consistent with theoretical models that emphasise the importance of dyadic resilience and partner responsiveness in maintaining relationship quality over time [13, 64]. This suggests that these processes may be universal, operating regardless of couple composition (e.g. same-sex versus different-sex couples) or the health status of the partners.

However, given the limitations of the available data, particularly the small number of longitudinal studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, although the analyses in the present study did not yield significant moderating effects for sociodemographic characteristics, such as participant age or couple serostatus, these null findings should not be overinterpreted as evidence that moderation was absent. Several moderator categories, most notably sexual orientation and couple type, were likely underpowered because of the small number of studies representing these groups.

Also, relationship length did not moderate the impact of risk factors. This is inconsistent with prior research reporting declines in relationship quality over time, particularly among long-term couples [61, 65]. Nevertheless, our findings might indicate an asymmetry in the moderating role of relationship length depending on the nature of the covariate. Specifically, the association between protective factors and relationship quality was stronger in longer-term relationships, whereas no such effect was observed for risk factors. This pattern suggests that the moderating role of relationship length may depend on whether the covariates reflect relational resources or deficits. This highlights the potential interplay between relationship duration and the characteristics of individual-level variables that enhance or undermine relationship quality.

Limitations and future directions

This systematic review and meta-analysis had several limitations. First, the overall sample was predominantly male and in serodiscordant relationships, which limits the generalisability of the results. Second, the measurement of relationship quality and its covariates varied considerably across studies, which may have introduced bias related to differences in the psychometric properties of the instruments used. In addition, the number of effect sizes extracted from individual studies ranged from 1 to 24. To account for this heterogeneity in the data structure, we used multilevel meta-analysis. Third, as most studies used a cross-sectional design, it was not possible to determine causal relationships. Only two studies employed the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model [50], so a dyadic meta-analysis was not possible (e.g. [66]). We accounted for differences in study design by including it as a potential moderator at the between-study level, which did not yield a significant effect. Fourth, the observed asymmetry in the funnel plot suggests the possibility of publication bias, indicating that studies with smaller samples and weaker effects may be underrepresented in the published literature. However, because funnel plot asymmetry can also result from methodological heterogeneity or chance, particularly when the number of studies is small, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of publication bias.

Finally, although the eligibility criteria and search strategy were theoretically justified, they may have led to the exclusion of some relevant studies because of the databases selected and the terminology used. In particular, gray literature was not covered by the search strategy, which was finalized at preregistration in March 2025 to minimize post hoc bias in study selection.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis is the first to provide a quantitative synthesis of correlates associated with relationship quality—a crucial aspect of quality of life—among PLWH. The findings revealed comparable, moderate associations of relationship quality with both protective and risk factors, consistent with patterns observed in other populations and clinical contexts. These associations were not significantly moderated by sociodemographic variables or by the different domains of correlates. The only significant moderator was relationship length, and this effect emerged only for protective factors.

Overall, the findings suggest that, despite the unique context of HIV/AIDS, several correlates of relationship quality among PLWH may reflect broader, cross-population patterns. These findings contribute to a better understanding of factors associated with psychological well-being among PLWH, particularly given the importance of satisfying romantic relationships for quality of life in this population.

However, the findings also highlight the small number of longitudinal studies and the predominant focus on risk factors in the existing literature. Future studies should consider adopting an ecological framework to categorize correlates of relationship quality into individual, dyadic, and social-contextual levels.
