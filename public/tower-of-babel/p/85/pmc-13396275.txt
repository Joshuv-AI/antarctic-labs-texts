Sexual Satisfaction in Psycho-Oncology Patients: Characteristics and Correlates
Authors: C Hyung Keun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective Quality sexual relationships predict higher marital satisfaction and lower emotional distress in cancer patients. However, no studies have investigated the sexual satisfaction of patients visiting a psycho-oncology clinic. In this study, I examined characteristics of non-respondents to a sex-related question and identified characteristics and correlates of sexual satisfaction in patients visiting a psychooncology outpatient clinic in a tertiary hospital in a sex-stratified format. Methods For 417 female and 169 male, those who answered the single sexual satisfaction item of the Functional Assessment of the Cancer Therapy-General (FACT-G) were categorized as “respondents,” and others as “non-respondents” by sex. After adjusting for age, cancer diagnosis, and time after diagnosis, sex-stratified linear regression models were established using the FACT-G except the sexual item and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale. Results Female were 1.466 times more likely to decline to answer about sexual satisfaction. Older patients, and female with active diseases or recent chemotherapy, also tended to avoid answering. Among female and male, 24.5% and 26.0%, respectively, reported no sexual satisfaction, with no between-sex difference. Lower age, a longer time since diagnosis, greater social/family well-being, and lower depressive symptoms for female and greater physical well-being for male contributed to sexual satisfaction. Conclusion Psycho-oncologists should sensitively address sexual issues among older female in poor health. Paying equal attention to sexual issues for both sexes is necessary. For recently diagnosed older female with lower social/family well-being and higher depressive symptoms and male experiencing lower physical well-being, sexual issues should be thoroughly assessed.Keywords: Sexual behavior, Neoplasms, Psycho-oncology, Depression, Quality of life

INTRODUCTION

With the cancer survival rate steadily increasing [1], there is a growing interest in not only the development of state-of-theart diagnostics and treatment modalities but also improving and maintaining their health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and psychological well-being. The HRQOL addresses multidimensional aspects of physical, social, emotional, and cognitive functioning, which are lowered in cancer patients [2].

Quality sexual relationships predict higher marital satisfaction and lower emotional distress in cancer patients [3]. Many patients with life-limiting illness value communicating about sexuality with a health professional with whom they are already well-acquainted, instead of being referred to another expert [4]. In this regard, sexual concerns of psycho-oncology patients deserve clinical attention, and a psycho-oncology clinic should be where cancer patients can freely communicate about their sexual life. Therefore, it is of great importance for psychooncologists to have a good understanding of the characteristics and correlates of sexual satisfaction among their patients, defined as “an affective response arising from one’s subjective evaluation of the positive and negative dimensions associated with one’s sexual relationship [5].” Various factors impact sexual satisfaction, and cancer treatment may be one such factor having direct consequences for it. For example, damage to the pelvic autonomic nerves and the pelvic floor during rectal cancer surgery may cause sexual problems [6]. Non-biological factors can also be related to one’s sexual satisfaction: physical and mental health status, intimacy, social and familial support, and cultural or religious beliefs [7]. Thus far, no studies have investi-gated the sexual satisfaction of patients visiting a psycho-oncology clinic.

Moreover, evaluating sexual satisfaction may be challenging because some might consider such a question sensitive and even offensive. For example, Mediterraneans are more reluctant to respond to questions about sexual life than Northern Europeans [8]. In a South African study on cancer patients, respondents felt uncomfortable when asked to answer sexual questions [9]. Hence, it would also be helpful to know who would be more reluctant to communicate concerns regarding their sexual life in order to ensure that psycho-oncologists employ a more sensitive approach.

The aim of this cross-sectional chart review study was to examine characteristics of non-respondents to a sex-related question and to identify characteristics and correlates of sexual satisfaction in patients visiting a psycho-oncology outpatient clinic in a tertiary hospital in a sex-stratified format. I hypothesized that higher HRQOL in certain domains and lower degrees of psychiatric symptoms would be associated with sexual satisfaction.

METHODS

Patients

The present study was performed in the psycho-oncology clinic of the Asan Medical Center, a tertiary hospital located in Seoul, Republic of Korea. In the clinic, patients diagnosed with cancer and over 17 years of age are treated for psychiatric symptoms by psycho-oncologists. Before consulting doctors, only those under 81 years of age fill out a routine symptom checklist to evaluate the severity of various psychological and physical symptoms. As this is not mandatory, those who refused to fill out the questionnaire, were unable to understand Korean, or were not in good medical condition were excluded. The checklist was self-administered, and patients were free to skip individual items, including the sexual satisfaction item. Electronic medical charts of the first visits, defined as index visits, of all the patients visiting the clinic within the study period (May 21, 2019 to May 20, 2020) was reviewed by the author. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (2023-1573) and conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived because the study was designed as a retrospective chart review.

Measures

Demographic and psychiatric-related variables

Demographic variables included age and sex. Psychiatric diagnosis was determined according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition [10], during the index visits.

Cancer-related variables

Cancer diagnoses were categorized according to the World Health Organization Classification of Tumours [11], and the date of diagnoses was determined based on the hierarchy from the European Network of Cancer Registries [12]. For those who had multiple primary cancers, the one that dominated the treatment— index cancer—was adopted for the analysis. Cancer status was grouped as disease-free or active disease based on the criteria which had been used in a previous study with minimal modification [13]: radiofrequency ablation was added as an alternative treatment modality to a surgical operation for one of the disease-free conditions: “metastatic cancer that had been surgically removed, and no documentation of recurrence.” Information on cancer stage was not available and therefore could not be included in the analyses. Cancer treatment within 8 weeks was categorized under surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or other therapy including hormone therapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy.

Functional Assessment of the Cancer Therapy-General

The Functional Assessment of the Cancer Therapy-General (FACT-G) is a 27-item self-report scale evaluating various aspects of HRQOL for the past week [14]. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4 (where 0=“not at all,” 1=“a little bit,” 2=“somewhat,” 3=“quite a bit,” and 4=“very much”). The scale consists of 4 subscales: 7 items for “physical well-being (PWB),” 7 items for “social/family well-being (SWB),” 6 items for “emotional well-being,” and 7 items for “functional well-being (FWB).” A higher total score on the scale indicates a greater level of HRQOL. Item 7 of the SWB, “I am satisfied with my sex life,” which evaluates sexual satisfaction, is included in different versions of the FACT (e.g., Functional Assessment of the Cancer Therapy-Prostate), including the FACT-G, and was administered as part of the FACT-G in the routine symptom checklist. This single item has been adopted in several studies on the sexual well-being of cancer patients and survivors [15,16]. The present study also used this single item to evaluate sexual satisfaction. For the analyses, a modified SWB score (0–24), excluding the score for the aforementioned item, was used to avoid multi-collinearity.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is a 14-item self-report scale comprising 2 subscales: 7 items for “depression” and 7 items for “anxiety,” for the assessment of the depressive and anxiety symptoms for the past week [17]. Each subtotal score ranges from 0 to 21, with a greater score indi cating higher severity of depression or anxiety.

Distress thermometer

The distress thermometer (DT) is a 1-item self-report scale for the detection of psychological distress over the past week [18]. The score ranges from 0 to 10, with a higher score corresponding to more severe distress.

Statistical analyses

According to sex, those who responded to item 7 of the SWB were categorized as “respondents,” and those who did not answer it were categorized as “non-respondents.” Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, whereby cases with missing values on variables included in the analyses were excluded. The characteristics between the two groups were compared for the purpose of evaluating the representativeness of the respondents, and characteristics of the non-respondents were evaluated with Student’s t-test and Pearson’s chi-squared test for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Following this, sexual satisfaction between sexes was compared with Student’s t-test. Finally, sex-stratified linear regression was conducted to identify the relationship between potential correlates and sexual satisfaction. After adjusting for age, cancer diagnoses, and time passed since index cancer diagnosis, sex-stratified multivariable linear regression models were established using all potential independent variables with p value <0.10 from univariable models. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows version 21.0 for PC (IBM Corp.). A two-tailed p value less than 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study sample and the non-respondents

In total, 417 female and 169 male patients responded or expressed their refusal to respond to item 7 of the SWB (Tables 1 and 2). There were 247 (59.2%) female and 115 (68.0%) male respondents. Female were more likely than male to refuse to report their sexual satisfaction (χ2=3.957, p=0.047, odds ratio=1.466). Among female, the difficulty in the assessment of older patients, patients with active disease, patients diagnosed with digestive system and thorax cancer, patients who had received chemotherapy within the last 2 months, and patients with lower FWB was reflected in the significant differences between the non-respondents and the respondents. In male, the only difficulty occurred in evaluating older patients. Further information on the proportion of patients who completed the checklist was not available.

Characteristics of sexual satisfaction

Of the patients, 142 (24.5%) female patients and 44 (26.0%) male patients reported no sexual satisfaction during the past week; that is, they were not satisfied at all with their sex life. The mean score of item 7 of the SWB was 1.22 (±1.20) for female patients and 1.25 (±1.21) for male patients, corresponding to being minimally satisfied as a group. No significant difference in sexual satisfaction was found between males and females (t=-0.217, p=0.828).

Correlates of sexual satisfaction

The results of univariable and multivariable linear regression are presented in Table 3 for female and male. Although item 7 of the SWB was measured on a bounded 0–4 scale, its distribution showed no extreme skewness or kurtosis (female: 0.552 and -0.673; male: 0.500 and -0.728). Linear regression assumptions, including homoscedasticity and normality of residuals, were evaluated through visual inspection of residual plots, and no substantial violations were observed. Item 7 of the SWB was analyzed as a continuous outcome using linear regression because its distribution was approximately symmetric, and previous studies [19] suggest that linear regression provides reliable estimates for ordinal outcomes with five or more categories. After adjusting for age, cancer diagnoses, and time elapsed since index cancer diagnosis, the multivariable models included 367 female and 169 male patients. The decreasing age (p=0.003), a longer time since cancer diagnosis (p=0.005), a higher level of SWB (p<0.001), and a lower degree of depressive symptoms (p=0.007) for female patients and a higher degree of PWB (p=0.004) for male patients turned out to be significantly associated with sexual satisfaction.

DISCUSSION

The current study determined characteristics and correlates of sexual satisfaction among psycho-oncology patients according to sex. Female patients were about 1.5 times more likely than male patients to decline to disclose the degree of sexual satisfaction. Approximately, one-fourth of the patients of both sexes reported no sexual satisfaction during the past week. After controlling for age, cancer diagnoses, and time elapsed since index cancer diagnosis, lower age, greater SWB, and lower depressive symptoms for female and greater PWB for male were exploratorily associated with sexual satisfaction.

There were 38.2% non-respondents in my sample (41.8% of female and 32.0% of male). The statistic is similar to the results—26% to 45%—of the multi-ethnic registry study on cancer survivors that used the same questionnaire [15]. The nonresponse rate in this study was very similar to that of the Chinese respondents (37%) of the study in particular; this is interesting as Koreans share many East Asian cultural elements with the Chinese [15]. Another study illustrated a non-response rate of 44.2% for the Chinese cancer patients, which was similar to my result [20]. Although non-response rates are different depending on ethnic groups, the non-response rates, including those in my study, are notably higher than that in the United States sample (7.1%) [21]. Depending on the culture, discussing sexual life openly may be considered inappropriate or offensive; for example, addressing sexual dysfunction has generally been considered taboo in Asia [22]. Besides, it is likely that, in my study, the prevalence of no sexual satisfaction was underestimated, and the level of sexual satisfaction was overestimated since non-respondents of the FACT-G sex question have greater unmet needs for sex than the respondents [15]. As a question about sexual well-being is more likely to be skipped if it is optional [15], removing the directive can be more helpful in assessing the sexual aspect of HRQOL, particularly in routine screening settings for Asian populations, including Koreans.

The findings on the patients’ characteristics associated with non-response to the question about sexual satisfaction are in alignment with those of previous studies showing that female [15,20] and older patients [15] are less likely to respond to a question about sexual interest. Generally, most female feel uncomfortable talking about sex and are afraid of being judged when they express sexual desire or talk about sexual behavior [23]. Older people commonly deal with their sexual health concerns internally instead of seeking help from healthcare professionals [24]. Due to perplexity, dissatisfaction with treatment, and negative attitudes and perceived disinterest from the professionals, they are reluctant to openly talk about sexual issues [25]. Finally, chemotherapy within 2 months and active disease status results in a poorer health condition; it was reported that those with such a health condition are more likely to decline answering the question about sexual satisfaction [20]. Psycho-oncologists should employ a more sensitive approach when addressing sexual issues of female and other clinically vulnerable subgroups, including older adults and those with poorer health conditions.

For both sexes, approximately 25% of patients reported no sexual satisfaction. Given the relatively high and sex-differential non-response rate, these prevalence estimates are based on respondents only and may not fully represent the overall clinic population; thus, they should be interpreted with caution. Notably, another Chinese study on cancer survivors using the same item as that used in my study found higher proportions of no sexual satisfaction (42.7% in male and 42.1% in female). In my study, 37.6% of the patients had already been being treated in the psycho-oncologic clinic. It is well-known that psychiatric disorders contribute to sexual problems; patients with mood, anxiety, and substance use disorders have lower degrees of sexual satisfaction [26]. Thus, while psycho-oncologic intervention may be associated with improved sexual satisfaction, given the cross-sectional design, this interpretation is speculative and should be regarded as exploratory.

Previous research on the sex difference in sexual satisfaction in cancer patients and survivors has demonstrated mixed results. In a study on patients with rectal cancer, after surgery, male reported higher impairment of sexual life [6], whereas another study on colorectal cancer patients showed that female patients had lower sexual satisfaction [27]. According to a study on young adult cancer survivors, while male experienced greater levels of sexual satisfaction, female had higher levels of sexuality needs [28]. My result of no significant difference in sexual satisfaction between the sexes is consistent with the findings of a previous study on cancer patients with mixed diagnoses in the United States [29]. Differences in sample characteristics— cancer types, cancer stage, treatment status, etc.—and instruments for the evaluation of sexual satisfaction may account for the inconsistent results. Nevertheless, the present findings have an important clinical implication in that psycho-oncologists should pay equal, careful attention to the sexual concerns of their patients.

I revealed that different factors by sex contribute to sexual satisfaction among psycho-oncology patients. In female, older age was associated with decreased sexual satisfaction. A previous study on the general population revealed that those in intimate relationships have more sexual intercourse [30], and having a functional sexual relationship results in greater levels of sexual satisfaction [31]. As sociodemographic data on marital or cohabitant status were unavailable, I may only speculate with caution that older female patients are likely to be widowed or divorced, and thus they might not be satisfied with their sexual life. However, this association was not observed in male. A study on those aged 57–85 years showed that male are more likely than female to have a partner with whom they are in a sexually active relationship and maintain more positive and permissive conceptions toward sexual activity [32]; this is likely to contribute to the maintenance of sexual satisfaction among older male. In addition, a large-scale survey on the general population revealed that, only in female, satisfaction with sexual intercourse is associated with younger age [33].

I found greater SWB and lower depressive symptoms to be a significant contributor of sexual satisfaction in female. The items in the SWB domain of the FACT-G primarily address the quality of relationships with friends, family, and one’s partner. The quality of the partner relationship was found to be an important predictor of sexual satisfaction among the survivors with cervical cancer [34]. Similarly, a positive correlation between marital and sexual satisfaction in patients with ovarian cancer was reported [35]. According to a systematic review on couples facing breast cancer, relationship with one’s partner influences degrees of depression [36]. The patients with breast cancer who have better dyadic coping exhibit lower levels of depression37; those who experience relational difficulties with their partners such as negative social communications and lower quality of relationship are more depressed [37]. Depression is associated with lowered sexual arousal [38] and difficulties in communication with one’s partner [39], which could lead to higher dissatisfaction within a sexual relationship.

Previous studies identifying predictors of male’s sexual satisfaction have primarily dealt with patients with cancer types that occur only in male [16,40]. My result that greater PWB is a correlate of higher sexual satisfaction is in close agreement with those of past studies. A recent review reported physical health for patients and their partners as one of the most important contributors of sexual satisfaction after prostatectomy [40]. Another study on male with prostate cancer revealed that erectile dysfunction, presented as a more specific, physical determinant, is a significant predictor of sexual satisfaction [16]. The items evaluating the PWB in the FACT-G are mainly related to somatic distress (lethargy, nausea, pain, side effects of treatment, etc.). For male psycho-oncology patients, psychooncologists can help reduce somatic symptoms with bupropion for fatigue, tricyclic antidepressants for pain, and mirtazapine for side effects of chemotherapy such as chemotherapyrelated nausea [41], probably resulting in the improvement of sexual satisfaction.

The current study has several limitations. First, sexual satisfaction was evaluated only after cancer diagnosis; the scores do not necessarily reflect aggravation due to cancer diagnosis and therapy. Second, because of the cross-sectional study design, reverse causality cannot be excluded. Third, the generalizability of the results is limited because the study was conducted on a sample from a single institution and a single country. Fourth, as only a general question on the degree of subjective sexual satisfaction was used, the multidimensional components of sexual life—such as sexual desire, arousal, pain, and relational/partner satisfaction—were not captured, which limits interpretability of the findings. Fifth, psychiatric diagnoses were made by clinical interview only; with a structured interview, diagnostic accuracy for psychiatric diagnoses would have been higher. Sixth, because information on key psychosocial (e.g. partner/relationship status, sexual activity, and menopausal status) and treatment-related factors (e.g. stage and cancer treatment) was either unavailable or not adjusted for, the direction of residual confounding cannot be determined. Consequently, it was not possible to evaluate their potential influence on sexual satisfaction, psychological distress, or response patterns. Despite the limitations, this study also demonstrates certain strengths. First, this is the first study to evaluate characteristics and correlates of sexual satisfaction among patients visiting a psycho-oncology clinic. Second, I provide findings reflecting a real-world clinical practice setting by using consecutively sampled patients.

In conclusion, the present findings suggest that psychooncologists may benefit from employing a more sensitive approach when addressing the sexual issues of female, older adults, and those suffering from poor health conditions. Approximately one-fourth of the patients of both sexes did not experience sexual satisfaction at all during the past week with no differences in sex, implying the importance of equal, careful attention to the sexual concerns of both sexes. In cases of older female patients who have been diagnosed with cancer more recently and exhibit lower SWB and higher depressive symptoms and male patients who report lower PWB, a thorough assessment of sexual issues may be warranted. Future studies addressing various aspects of sexual satisfaction will help identify which interventions should specifically be used to enhance sexual satisfaction.
