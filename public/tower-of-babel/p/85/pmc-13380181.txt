Evaluation of Stiffness in the Tensor Fasciae Latae and Iliotibial Band After Direct Anterior Approach Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Haruki Osanami, Yuki Kitta, Akira Inoue, Takatoshi Kaneko, Yuki Hirai, Koji Ugai, Eiki Tsushima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate postoperative changes in the stiffness of the tensor fasciae latae (TFL) and iliotibial band (ITB) after total hip arthroplasty via the direct anterior approach. Associations with hip alignment, pain, and hip range of motion (ROM) were also investigated.MethodsForty-eight patients (64 hips) undergoing primary total hip arthroplasty from April to November 2023 were included. Shear wave elastography (SWE), an ultrasound-based technique that quantifies tissue stiffness, was used to measure TFL and ITB stiffness preoperatively and daily during the first postoperative week. Changes over time and correlations between SWE values and femoral offset (FO), leg length, pain during gait, and hip ROM were assessed.ResultsSWE values of the TFL and ITB (higher values indicate greater stiffness) were higher than preoperative values on postoperative days 1–5 (TFL) and 1–4 (ITB) (P < .01) and were no longer significantly different by postoperative day 6 and postoperative day 5, respectively. Greater FO increase was associated with higher SWE values of the TFL and ITB on postoperative day 1 (P < .01). Higher SWE values were associated with greater pain during gait on postoperative days 2–7 (P ≤ .01) and with reduced hip adduction and extension ROM (P < .05).ConclusionsEarly postoperative TFL and ITB stiffness were higher than preoperative values. On postoperative day 1, greater FO increase was associated with higher TFL and ITB stiffness. Postoperative stiffness was also associated with greater pain during gait and reduced hip adduction and extension ROM early postoperatively.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is widely performed for hip osteoarthritis and has been shown to be a cost-effective intervention in a health economic analysis [1]. Systematic reviews have reported that the enhanced recovery after surgery program reduces the length of stay for THA, as well as pain reduction and improved gait [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Thus, although the importance of treatment for pain and gait in the early postoperative period of THA has been reported, reports focusing on the relationship with the muscles surrounding the hip joint have not been adequately reviewed.

Factors that influence patient dissatisfaction after THA include early postoperative pain, limited range of motion (ROM), leg length and offset discrepancies, and excessive postoperative stiffness of the muscles surrounding the hip joint [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. With these considerations in mind, THA adjusts leg length and offset to optimize soft tissue tension. Decreased soft tissue tension after THA is associated with an increased risk of dislocation [10]. In contrast, excessive leg length and offset have been shown to increase soft tissue tension [11] and cause decreased hip ROM [12]. Biomechanical studies have reported that excessive stiffness of the tensor fasciae latae (TFL) and iliotibial band (ITB) is associated with pain in the iliotibial ligaments and lateral thigh [13]. Furthermore, Bhave et al. [14] reported that among patients with contractures resistant to physical therapy after THA, 57% of them had TFL contractures. Therefore, evaluation of TFL and ITB stiffness in the postoperative period may be important for postoperative rehabilitation.

In recent years, shear wave elastography (SWE) has attracted attention as a method for objectively evaluating muscle stiffness. SWE is an ultrasound imaging technique that can assess tissue stiffness by measuring shear modulus and has recently been widely used in the field of orthopaedics [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Furthermore, a previous study that investigated early postoperative muscle stiffness using SWE reported changes over time in the shear modulus of the supraspinatus muscle in the early postoperative period after rotator cuff repair [20]. Based on these findings, a quantitative assessment method using SWE can be used to measure changes in muscle stiffness over time in the early postoperative period of THA and can serve as an indicator for evaluation and treatment.

This study aimed to evaluate changes over time in stiffness of the TFL and ITB after THA using SWE and the relationship between early postoperative pain and ROM of the hip joint. Additionally, we examined the relationship between changes in leg length and femoral offset (FO), which may affect TFL and ITB stiffness. We hypothesized that TFL and ITB stiffness would increase early postoperative after THA and would be associated with pain and hip ROM.

Material and methods

Participants

Forty-eight patients with 64 hips who underwent primary THA via the direct anterior approach between April 2023 and November 2023 were included in this study (men: 16, women: 32, mean age: 68.05 ± 10.03 years; height: 156.97 ± 8.74 cm; weight: 59.16 ± 10.38 kg; and body mass index: 23.93 ± 3.24 kg/m2) (Table 1). Exclusion criteria were previous hip surgery, rheumatoid arthritis [21], a history of neurologic disease affecting lower-extremity function (eg, stroke with residual motor deficits), hemoglobin A1c > 7.5% [22], and trochanteric bursitis or ITB friction syndrome diagnosed preoperatively. According to the Tönnis classification system [23], 10 hips were grade 2 and 54 were grade 3. This study was a prospective correlational study conducted at a single institution, with surgery and rehabilitation performed by one orthopaedic surgeon and one physical therapist, respectively. Postoperative protocol consisted of gait and ROM training using a walker on the first postoperative day, which continued until 2 weeks postoperatively. Common interventions such as ice application were consistently applied to all patients. Postoperative pain management consisted of intravenous patient-controlled analgesics on postoperative day one only, followed by oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and acetaminophen. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the institutional review board (approval number: 3518). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants received a thorough explanation of the study and provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. A direct anterior approach was employed in all cases to access the intermuscular plane between the TFL and sartorius muscles. A standard operating table was used; no specialized traction table was utilized. The conjoined external rotator tendon was generally preserved. Surgical time averaged 29 ± 5 minutes for unilateral THA and 69 ± 13 minutes for bilateral THAs.

Measurements

Muscle stiffness measurements were performed by a single experienced musculoskeletal sonographer using an ultrasound (LOGIQ S8, GE Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 9L-D linear transducer [24]. To ensure proper contact, ample ultrasound gel was used between the probe and skin surface [9]. The measurer was careful when applying constant pressure at the lowest level at which ultrasound could be visualized [25]. In previous studies, the SWE test measured SWE values (kPa) of the TFL and ITB [26,27]. Since shear wave velocity correlates with the tissue’s elastic modulus, the system automatically calculates Young’s modulus from the wave speed [28,29]. A higher modulus value indicates increased tissue stiffness [30,31]. The measurement site of the TFL was set at the midpoint between the anterior superior iliac spine and greater trochanter of the femur [26], and the measurement site for ITB was 2 cm proximal to the lateral femoral epicondyle (Figure 1, Figure 2) [27]. The region of interest was positioned centrally within the muscle belly of the TFL and across the thickness of the ITB. Two SWE measurements were obtained at each test site (TFL and ITB), and the mean value was used for analysis. SWE measurements were conducted in 2 different hip limb positions: one with the subject lying supine and the hip in a neutral alignment, and the other in maximum abduction. Differences in stiffness were calculated by subtracting the SWE values measured during maximum hip abduction from that measured in the neutral hip position. To prevent changes in muscle stiffness due to measurement time, each measurement was obtained within 15 seconds [32]. Alignment, leg length, and FO were assessed using preoperative, immediate postoperative, and postoperative day 7 radiographs. All the patients underwent pain assessment and hip ROM measurements. To assess pain, the numeric rating scale was used to rate anterolateral thigh pain during gait on a scale of 0 to 10 [35]. Pain was assessed one side at a time to account for contralateral hip effects. To assess ROM, the Todai goniometer (TTM-KO, Sakai Medical, Japan) was used to measure the ROM of hip adduction and extension. Pain and ROM were evaluated at the same time points as the SWE examinations (preoperatively and daily on postoperative days 1–7).

Data analyses

Statistical analyses consisted of separate, single-factor (time) repeated-measures analysis of variance for SWE values of the TFL and ITB over time. For multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the Holm’s step-down procedure. Pearson correlation analyses were performed to investigate the association between SWE values of the TFL and ITB, pain, and ROM preoperatively and on postoperative days 1–7. Finally, Pearson correlation analyses were performed to investigate the association between changes in TFL and ITB SWE values and changes in leg length and FO.

To assess intraobserver reliability of SWE measurements for the TFL and ITB, the examiner repeated the measurements twice at each site while blinded to the previous results. Intraobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [1,1]. The Landis scoring system was used to interpret ICC values (almost perfect: >0.81, substantial: 0.61–0.80, moderate: 0.41–0.60, fair: 0.21–0.40, and slight: 0.00–0.20) [36]. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.0.2), and statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Intraobserver reliability

The ICC for intraobserver reproducibility of the SWE values of the TFL was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.84–0.94), which corresponds to almost perfect. The ICC for intraobserver reproducibility of the SWE values in the ITB was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.78–0.92), which corresponds to almost perfect.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate changes in SWE values of the TFL and ITB over time after THA, and the relationship between early postoperative pain and ROM of the hip joint. Additionally, we examined the relationship between SWE values of the TFL and ITB and amount of changes in leg length and FO. To our knowledge, this is the first study to focus on muscle stiffness in the early postoperative period of THA using SWE. In this study, SWE values of the TFL and ITB increased in the early postoperative period, and there was a significant correlation between SWE values of the TFL and ITB and pain during gait and ROM of the hip joint. Additionally, we found a positive correlation between SWE values of the TFL and ITB and amount of change in FO in the immediate postoperative period.

It has been reported that pain and limited ROM in the early postoperative period after THA are directly related to patient dissatisfaction [[6], [7], [8],33], and early physical therapy has been shown to be effective in reducing pain and improving ROM [5]. In addition to surgical intervention, changes in muscle and ligament stiffness due to changes in leg length and FO are also important factors that affect pain and ROM limitation. However, reports investigating changes in muscle stiffness of muscles and ligament after THA have not been thoroughly reviewed. Previous studies on soft tissue muscle stiffness in artificial joints revealed scattered reports evaluating the deltoid muscle after reverse shoulder arthroplasty surgery using SWE [[37], [38], [39]]. Reverse shoulder arthroplasty results in distalization and medialization of the center of rotation, lengthening of the deltoid by 10–20%, and an increased deltoid wrapping angle [40]. In contrast, increased deltoid muscle stiffness has been reported to be significantly correlated with increased pain levels [37]. Therefore, it is important to evaluate deltoid muscle stiffness using SWE after reverse shoulder arthroplasty. Takao et al [11] reported that an increase in postoperative offset affects increase in soft tissue tension after THA. Additionally, it has been reported that advanced preoperative hip deformity increases postoperative soft tissue stiffness around the hip joint [41]. Furthermore, there was a positive correlation between the amount of change in the TFL and ITB stiffness and amount of change in FO on the postoperative day, suggesting an association between intraoperative alignment changes and increased muscle stiffness on postoperative day 1. No correlation was observed between changes in TFL and ITB stiffness and changes in FO on postoperative day 7, which may reflect resolution of the transient early postoperative increase in TFL and ITB stiffness.

The present study showed that TFL stiffness increased during postoperative days 1–5 and was no longer significantly different from preoperative values by postoperative day 6. ITB stiffness increased during postoperative days 1–4 and was no longer significantly different from preoperative values by postoperative day 5, with no significant difference thereafter through postoperative day 7. Although a greater increase in FO was positively correlated with higher TFL and ITB SWE values on postoperative day 1, no such correlation was observed on postoperative day 7. Previous studies have reported that increased muscle stiffness as indicated by SWE values may be associated with tissue edema [42]. A review of studies examining SWE values and pain reported that muscle stiffness can be higher in symptomatic individuals [43]. In addition, prior studies have reported that SWE values reflect muscle contraction [44]. Therefore, the early postoperative increase in SWE values in our cohort may be attributable to postoperative swelling and associated involuntary muscle activity, and the subsequent decrease after postoperative day 6 may reflect improvement in swelling and pain. Clinically, because greater stiffness was associated with greater pain during gait and reduced hip adduction and extension ROM, these findings highlight the importance of standard early postoperative measures to mitigate swelling and pain (eg, cryotherapy, appropriate use of anti-inflammatory/analgesic medications, and gradual progression of ambulation and ROM exercises), particularly through postoperative day 5. Based on these findings, we do not recommend a change in surgical technique at this time; rather, surgeons and rehabilitation providers should be aware that transient TFL/ITB stiffness may occur in the first postoperative week and should counsel patients and optimize early postoperative symptom management accordingly.

In this study, there was a negative correlation between SWE values of the TFL and ITB and ROM of hip adduction and extension, indicating that the higher the muscle stiffness, the more restricted the ROM. This can be understood from the similarity with the Ober’s test position (hip extension and adduction) used to measure the stiffness of the TFL and ITB [45]. In the relationship between SWE values of the TFL and ITB and walking pain, this study showed a significant positive correlation on postoperative days 2–7, with higher muscle stiffness being associated with greater pain during gait. It is important to ensure ROM of hip adduction and extension during gait, and in healthy adults, the hip adduction angle during gait is reported to be 0 ± 6° [46] and ROM of extension is 13.5 ± 3.2° [47]. In the present results, we observed cases in which the ROM of the hip joint did not reach the ROM that would allow normal gait, especially on postoperative days 2–7, when the pain during gait was particularly severe. These findings are consistent with the possibility that increased TFL and ITB stiffness is associated with greater stretching during gait and greater pain. No significant correlation was observed between TFL and ITB stiffness and pain or hip ROM on postoperative day 1. One possible explanation is that intravenous patient-controlled analgesia used only on postoperative day 1 may have attenuated the observed associations. A previous systematic review reported decreased hip adduction and extension angles during gait after THA compared with those of healthy individuals [48]. These findings support careful early postoperative rehabilitation and symptom management, particularly for pain and ROM limitations potentially associated with TFL and ITB stiffness.

This study has several limitations. First, this study was a prospective correlational study conducted at a single institution and may not be generalizable to other surgeons or institutions. Multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm the generalizability of our results. Second, this study focused on changes in SWE values on postoperative days 1–7 after THA and did not provide data beyond 1 week. In the future, long-term clinical data should be provided. Third, soft tissue stiffness other than the TFL and ITB was not evaluated. Other soft tissues may have different results with increased leg length and FO. Willett et al reported [49] that ITB stiffness may also affect structures proximal to the hip joint, such as the gluteus medius and minimus muscles and the hip joint capsule, and it is necessary to capture the entire proximal hip structure in correlation with ROM. Other soft tissues may have influenced ROM of hip adduction and extension. In the future, other soft tissues should also be measured. Fourth, the causal relationship with pain and ROM is unknown because only correlations were investigated in this study. There are multiple potential confounders that could affect postoperative pain that were not considered, such as preoperative analgesic use and pain catastrophizing. In addition, postoperative edema was not quantified. In the future, factors affecting pain and ROM should be investigated.

Conclusions

TFL and ITB stiffness increased in the early postoperative period after THA and, on postoperative day 1, were positively correlated with FO change. Additionally, TFL and ITB stiffness were associated with pain during gait and reduced hip adduction and extension ROM. These findings support careful early postoperative rehabilitation and symptom management, particularly for pain and ROM limitations potentially associated with TFL and ITB stiffness.
