Ensemble deep learning model for accurate assessment of renal fibrosis in chronic kidney disease using two-dimensional shear wave elastography images
Authors: Dalin Ye, Zhaoxing Ou, Feile Ye, Shuqing Wang, Tong Li, Shushan Zhang, Jiaxin Chen, Yongquan Huang, Zhongzhen Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe accuracy of shear wave elastography for non-invasive assessment of renal fibrosis (RF) in chronic kidney disease (CKD) needs further improvement. We developed a tool using an ensemble deep learning model (EDLM) that can accurately assess RF in CKD patients based solely on two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) images.MethodsRetrospective data were collected from CKD patients between April 2019 and October 2024, along with renal 2D-SWE images obtained before biopsy. Pathological evaluation was the reference standard of RF. All patients were randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:1:2 ratio. An EDLM integrating three convolutional neural networks (ResNet18, DenseNet121, and EfficientNet-b7) through a voting strategy at the output level was developed and validated using 2D-SWE images. The diagnostic performance of the EDLM was compared with that of radiologists.ResultsA total of 286 CKD patients (mean age ± standard deviation: 41.86 ± 14.94, males: 162) and 858 2D-SWE images (mild RF: 405, moderate-severe RF: 453) were included. In the test set, EDLM achieved an accuracy of 93.0% (95% CI: 88.1, 95.9), negative predictive value of 89.6% (95% CI: 81.5, 94.5), positive predictive value of 96.4% (95% CI: 90.0, 98.8), specificity of 96.3% (95% CI: 89.7, 98.7), and sensitivity of 90.0% (95% CI: 82.1, 94.7). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curves of the EDLM was 0.989, surpassing experienced radiologist by 0.186 (P < 0.001) and less experienced radiologist by 0.279 (P < 0.001).ConclusionEDLM based on 2D-SWE images significantly improved the diagnostic performance of RF in CKD. The EDLM was expected to be a potential tool for accurately non-invasive assessment of RF in CKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-01964-y.Keywords: Ensemble deep learning model, Two-dimensional shear wave elastography, Renal fibrosis, Chronic kidney disease

Background

Renal fibrosis (RF) is a significant risk factor for poor prognosis in chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1, 2]. Accurate assessment of RF is crucial for the diagnosis and management of CKD. Renal biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing RF, but it is an invasive procedure associated with risks such as infection, bleeding, and even mortality, making repeated biopsies impractical [3].

Two-dimensional Shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) is a non-invasive, radiation-free, cost-effective, and repeatable ultrasonography [4]. Previous studies have demonstrated its clinical utility in the non-invasive assessment of RF, with an accuracy ranging from 78% to 87% [5–7]. One meta-analysis found that SWE had an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.87 for mild, 0.78 for moderate, and 0.86 for severe RF [6]. Chen et al. developed a nomogram based on the maximum Young’s modulus and clinical indicators for assessment of RF, and had an AUC of 0.84 in validation set [8]. Ge et al. developed a nomogram using both multimodal radiomics and clinical features, and had an AUC of 0.83 in validation cohorts [9]. However, current RF models relied on multiple elasticity parameters and clinical indicators, including maximum elasticity, minimum elasticity, mean elasticity, age, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, serum creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. A retrospective study demonstrated that mean elasticity value decreased with the degree of renal tubulointersitial fibrosis [10]. Zahrani et al. found that high BMI was associated with an increased risk of renal tubulointersitial fibrosis [11]. We constructed a summary table to show the relevance of SWE parameters and clinical measures with RF (Supplemental Table 1). The diagnostic performance might be hindered by complex operational requirements and inevitable measurement errors. In high BMI patients, the acoustic radiation force in SWE is often subject to attenuation and distortion, resulting in reduced shear wave displacement and compromised signal quality [12]. Moreover, SWE examination required patients to maintain breath-holding for 5 s to acquire stable images, which presented challenges for individuals with cardiorespiratory comorbidities. Additionally, in less developed regions, the lack of specialized SWE expertise in nephrology posed a challenge for the widespread adoption of non-invasive RF assessment.

Deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), excel at extracting image features and have demonstrated superior performance over radiologists in various medical imaging classification tasks [13]. Furthermore, ensemble deep learning model (EDLM), which integrated multiple CNNs, exhibited enhanced performance and generalization capabilities [14]. For instance, Zhou et al. developed an EDLM based on ultrasound gallbladder images for diagnosing biliary atresia, achieving an accuracy of 95%, surpassing human experts [15]. Similarly, Tang et al. constructed an EDLM based on X-ray images for COVID-19 diagnosis, demonstrating higher accuracy than individual deep learning models [16].

Therefore, this study aimed to develop an EDLM based on SWE images to enable convenient and accurate non-invasive evaluation of RF in CKD.

Methods

The study was performed in agreement with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was exempted from patient informed consent and was approved by the ethics committee. The data were collected from CKD patients who underwent right renal 2D-SWE examination and right renal biopsy at our center between April 2019 and October 2024. A total of 312 CKD patients were initially enrolled. After excluding 22 cases with unclear SWE images and 4 cases without renal pathology data, 286 patients were ultimately included. Data collection

The 2D-SWE examination was performed using the AixPlorer ultrasound machine (SuperSonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France), with an XC6-1 convex array probe operating at a frequency range of 1–6 MHz. The scale of 2D-SWE measurement range was 0–80 kPa. All 2D-SWE images were obtained within 24 h before the renal biopsy. The acquisition process is as follows: patients were placed in a prone position, instructed to hold their breath for 3–5 s [17]. The elastography frame was placed in the renal parenchyma area of the mid-region of the right kidney, and sTable 2D-SWE images were frozen and captured [17]. The procedure was repeated three times to obtain three high quality 2D-SWE images. High quality was defined as 2D-SWE images with no artifact interference, complete color filling within the region of interest, and continuous stability [18]. We created a dedicated acquisition protocol table for 2D-SWE technical reproducibility (Supplemental Table 2). After obtaining sTable 2D-SWE images, the Young’s modulus of the renal cortex was measured, including the maximum, minimum, and mean values. Patient demographic information, including gender, age, BMI, CKD stage was recorded.

The lower pole of the kidney was percutaneously biopsied under real-time ultrasound guidance. The selection of renal 2D-SWE measurement sites and kidney biopsy locations was based on the following considerations: (1) previous clinical study demonstrated that the number of glomeruli, the degree of interstitial fibrosis, and the proportion of global and segmental scarred glomeruli were not influenced by biopsy location [19]; (2) the lower pole renal biopsy was recommended because it minimized the risk of injury to the main renal arteries and veins, thereby reducing the potential for hemorrhagic complications [20]; (3) A methodological study suggested that 2D-SWE measurements taken from the mid region of the kidney exhibited a higher correlation with pathological scores compared to those obtained from the lower pole [17]. Two specialized pathologists independently evaluated the pathological specimens, and both were blind to the characteristics of CKD patients and 2D-SWE examination. For cases with inconsistent evaluation, the final outcome was determined by joint discussion between the two pathologists. The pathological features of RF included glomerulosclerosis, renal interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, vascular sclerosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration [2]. We used a semi-quantitative pathological scoring system proposed by Katafuchi et al. to assess RF [21]. The detailed pathological scoring criteria are shown in Supplementary Table 3. Pathological scores were classified as mild impairment (≤ 9 points), moderate impairment (10–18 points), and severe impairment (≥ 19 points) [22, 23]. Due to the small number of severely impaired cases in the data (26 of 286 cases, accounting for 9%), these cases were combined with moderately impaired cases into one category (moderate-severe impairment) to reduce the noise feature overlearning of unbalanced samples by CNNs.

Inclusion criteria were as follow:1) patients diagnosed with CKD. 2) patients who underwent both renal SWE examination and renal biopsy.

Exclusion Criteria were as follow: (1) 2D-SWE images that were unclear or had incomplete color filling. (2) 2D-SWE images that were compromised by artifacts, preventing the acquisition of true images. (3) Patients who did not have sufficient tissue samples for RF pathological scoring.

Image preprocessing

To minimize the influence of non-elastic information in the ultrasound images, the grayscale portion of the images was removed. To reduce the influence of elastic information from structures outside the renal parenchyma, we used a square box to perform necessary cropping on the 2D-SWE images, retaining those where the renal parenchyma constituted over 90% of the image. Structures included the renal cortex, renal medulla, renal capsule and a small portion of perirenal fat. Finally, all SWE images, sized at 256 × 256, were normalized and input into the CNNs.

Model construction

Computer codes are available online (https://github.com/SYSU2024DL/EDLMinRF).

All patients were randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:1:2 ratio. We used three single CNNs (ResNet18, DenseNet121, and EfficientNet-b7) and one ensemble CNNs to construct the model [24–26]. The CNNs model backbone of ResNet18, DenseNet121, and EfficientNet-b7 were loaded from the “Torchvision” package. The EDLM was based on the three CNNs combined by a voting method based on the unweighted average of the three outputs. All CNNs utilized pre-trained model and weights (ImageNet). Pre-trained model can effectively address the issue of insufficient data and help accomplish classification tasks on small datasets. The use of pretrained weights facilitates faster convergence of the model, enabling it to achieve optimal performance more quickly. The fully connected layers of all CNNs were modified for binary classification, with the softmax activation function applied to map outputs into class probability distributions. Final predictions were determined by selecting the class with the highest probability value. The cross-entropy loss function and “Adam” optimizer were employed for training. Early stopping strategy and learning rate decay were used to prevent overfitting. Other hyperparameters were configured as follows:

Batch sizes: ResNet18: 32, DenseNet121: 32, EfficientNet-b7: 16, EDLM: 16. Learning rates: ResNet18: 2e-6, DenseNet121: 5e-6, EfficientNet-b7: 5e-6, EDLM: 5e-6. Training epochs: ResNet18: 15, DenseNet121: 15, EfficientNet-b7: 20, EDLM: 15.

Model comparison with radiologists

Deep learning models, experienced radiologists (with 6 years of experience), and less experienced radiologists (with 3 years of experience) independently performed RF classification on the test set. In addition to 2D-SWE images, the corresponding maximum, minimum, and mean Young’s modulus values of the renal cortex were provided to the radiologists for assisting in their interpretations. These elasticity values are quantitative parameters directly measured by 2D-SWE technology. 2D-SWE images are generated based on these values through color-coding techniques. To better minimize subjective bias and suit clinical applications, we provided radiologists with the above elasticity parameters. In contrast, the deep learning model solely assessed RF based on the 2D-SWE images. Both the deep learning model and the radiologists were blinded to the pathological results.

Model explanation and deployment

Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-GAM) was used to create a heatmap to visualize area of the image which were important in predicting its class. Grad-GAM could visualize model decisions and enhance clinical explanation. The “Streamlit” package was used to assemble the developed and evaluated EDLM into a web application. This application allowed users to upload 2D-SWE images and predict RF in CKD patients.

Data analysis

The computations were performed using Python (version 3.12). Accuracy, negative predictive value, positive predictive value, specificity, sensitivity, and the AUC were calculated to compare the performance of CNNs model with that of the radiologists. Delong test was used to analyzed the statistically significant of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The Kappa test was used to analyze the consistency between the models and radiologists. P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The highest proportion of patients were in CKD stage I (45.8%). Males represented a higher proportion of patients than females by 13.3% (male vs. female, 56.6% vs. 43.4%). The average age was predominantly middle-aged (41.86 ± 14.94 years).

Comparison with radiologists

Table 3 also presents the comparison of performance between EDLM and radiologists. Compared with less experienced radiologist (with 3 years of experience), EDLM improved accuracy by 32.8%, negative predictive value by 32.1%, positive predictive value by 33.3%, sensitivity by 31.1%, and specificity by 34.6%. Compared with experienced radiologist (with 6 years of experience), EDLM also improved accuracy by 23.4%, negative predictive value by 23.7%, positive predictive value by 22.6%, sensitivity by 24.4%, and specificity by 22.2%.

Figure 2 shows the ROC curves of all models and radiologists in the test set. EDLM presented the highest AUC value (0.989, 95%CI = [0.980, 0.998]). The AUC value of EDLM was significantly increased by 0.279 compared with less experienced radiologist (P value of Delong test < 0.001) and by 0.186 compared with experienced radiologist (P value of Delong test < 0.001).

In the test set, consistency analysis showed that the Kappa value between EDLM and the experienced doctor was 0.53 (P < 0.001), the Kappa value between EDLM and the less experienced doctor was 0.32 (P < 0.001), and the Kappa value between the experienced doctor and the less experienced doctor was 0.77 (P < 0.001).

Using Grad-GAM, we found that area of higher elasticity values on 2D-SWE images was important in predicting its classification. Considering the convenience of model use, we deployed the EDLM onto a web page (http://123.57.207.222:8501). Discussion

SWE is an effective non-invasive method for assessing RF [4]. Chen et al. developed a nomogram based on the Young’s modulus and clinical indicators for assessment of RF [8]. Similarly, Ge et al. developed a nomogram using both multimodal radiomics and clinical features [9]. However, accurate evaluation requires measuring multiple parameters, which can be cumbersome, and the diagnostic performance is limited. 2D-SWE images provide more comprehensive imaging information [7]. We developed and validated an EDLM that integrates three convolutional neural networks (ResNet18, DenseNet121, and EfficientNet-b7) through a voting strategy at the output level, utilizing SWE images. The EDLM preformed higher accuracy than single model (ensemble model: 93.0%; single model: 91.8%, 91.8%, and 92.4%). The diagnostic performance of EDLM was significantly higher than that of experienced radiologist and less experienced radiologist.

The EDLM based on 2D-SWE images enhanced the diagnostic performance for RF. Chen et al.‘s studies showed that the diagnostic accuracy of a RF prediction model based on SWE multi-parameters and clinical indicators ranged from 85% to 88% [8]. A meta-analysis also indicated that the diagnostic accuracy of SWE for RF ranged between 78% and 87% [6]. In contrast, our EDLM showed an improvement of 15% to 5% in accuracy. Our model outperformed previous models in terms of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. We observed that while the diagnostic differences between different experienced radiologists were significant, experienced doctors were more likely to achieve good diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, the areas of focus for EDLM might be more comprehensive than those of radiologists. Grad-CAM heatmaps revealed that EDLM focused on regions with higher elasticity values in the images, not limited to the renal parenchyma. In contrast, radiologists primarily concentrated on the elasticity values within the renal parenchymal areas. We thought this might be one potential reason why EDLM demonstrated significantly improved diagnostic performance compared to radiologists. Therefore, we demonstrated that 2D-SWE images, which contained more complete characteristic information, could be analyzed and predicted more accurately through CNNs for better prediction.

The performance of EDLM was superior to individual models. Zhou et al. developed an EDLM based on ultrasound gallbladder images with an accuracy of 95% [15]. Tang et al. developed an EDLM based on X-ray images for COVID-19 diagnosis, which showed higher accuracy than individual CNNs models [16]. In this study, EDLM incorporated three complete, high-performance CNNs, and the final result was obtained through ensemble output. In the test set, the diagnostic performance and generalization ability of the EDLM were higher than those of individual CNNs. However, the small increase in performance might not reach statistical significance. We thought that the possible reason was that individual CNNs models in this study already had very high diagnostic performance, leaving limited area for improvement.

This study has some limitations. First, the lack of an external validation set compromised the generalizability of our results and introduced a potential overestimation of the diagnostic performance. Future research should consider multi-center data to validate and optimize the model. Therefore, we made the EDLM fully open-source, allowing more centers to test it. Second, we lacked multiple SWE devices to acquire images, which could limit the ability of the EDLM to predict RF based on SWE images from other manufacturers. Previous studies reported significant differences in the SWE images and parameters obtained from different manufacturers systems [27]. Therefore, integrating SWE images from different systems should be considered in further research. Third, an end-to-end model was not developed, as the model requires cropping of the SWE images. Further research would focus on developing an end-to-end model for faster predictions. Fourth, the small sample size of severe RF may compromise the statistical power and precision of the findings. Fifth, we lack long-term follow-up data. Future research should collect long-term follow-up data to develop more precise prognostic or diagnostic models.

Conclusions

The EDLM model based on 2D-SWE images significantly improved the diagnostic performance of non-invasive assessment of RF in CKD patients. CNNs model was expected to be a potential tool for accurately non-invasive assessment of RF in CKD patients.
