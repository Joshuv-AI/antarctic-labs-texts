The renaissance of research on psychedelics in child and adolescent psychopharmacology
Authors: Marc-Antoine Crocq, Philippe Auby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe recent regulatory approval of esketamine in adults heralded the renaissance of research into psychedelic compounds. However, the relevance of this resurgence for children and adolescents remains unclear. This review examines the rationale for investigating psychedelics in pediatric psychopharmacology.MethodsWe reviewed recent literature, regulatory documents, and clinical trial registries addressing the psychiatric use of classic serotonergic psychedelics (e.g., psilocybin, LSD), the entactogen MDMA, and dissociative compounds such as ketamine and esketamine in adolescent and young populations. Although ketamine is not a classic serotonergic psychedelic, it represents a paradigm for innovative pharmacological approaches.ResultsOngoing and planned trials primarily involve adolescents aged 16 years and older. Interest in these compounds is driven by significant unmet needs in child and adolescent psychiatry, where few medications are approved, and unsatisfactory therapeutic response is common. Potential targets include anorexia nervosa, core symptoms of autism spectrum disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, resistant depression, and severe PTSD. The fact that psychedelics may have long-lasting effects after only a few sessions raises the question of their effects on neuroplasticity.ConclusionsAlthough psychedelic research has entered a new phase in adult psychiatry, translation to pediatric populations requires caution due to developmental vulnerabilities and limited long-term safety data. Rigorous clinical trials, ethical safeguards, and regulatory oversight are essential before broader application in children and adolescents can be considered.Keywords: Psychedelics, Pediatric, Children, Adolescents, LSD, MDMA, Psilocybin, Ketamine, Esketamine, Psychopharmacology

Various names for psychedelics

The Oxford English Dictionary (OED) [1] defines psychedelics as drugs that produce an apparent expansion of consciousness. This term was coined from the Ancient Greek ψγχή [psyche] and δηλοῦν [to make manifest], and it literally translates as “reveal the psyche.” This word was first attested in 1956 in a letter by Humphry Osmond to Aldous Huxley. Osmond, a British psychiatrist, experimented with mescaline at George’s Hospital in London after World War 2, and in 1951 joined the Wayburn Mental Hospital in Saskatchewan, Canada, where he developed research about LSD. Osmond coined this word because he was not satisfied with the terms psychotomimetic and hallucinogenic. Osmond is mainly remembered for introducing the writer Aldous Huxley to mescaline—800 mg of Mescaline on May 5, 1953, in California—and LSD—on Christmas Eve in 1955 [2]. Huxley had heard about the research being conducted at Wayburn Hospital and had asked Osmond to supply the drugs and supervise the sessions. Huxley’s books on his mystical experience with mescaline and LSD reached a large audience.

The mystical experiences are reflected in the term entheogen, from the ancient Greek ἔνθεος [possessed or inspired by a god]. According to the OED, it was coined as an alternative to hallucinogen and psychedelic, which were seen as having negative connotations. The word entheogen has been criticized for its religious connotation. Other words have been coined to designate the clinical effects of psychedelics. Entactogen, literally “touching within,” was coined by D.E. Nichols [3]. He noted that, probably due to N-methylation, MDMA was less hallucinogenic than the related compound methylenedioxyamphetamine (MDA). MDMA was characterized by promoting affiliative social behavior and introspection. A further distinction suggested that entactogenic refers to being deeply connected to oneself, whereas empathogenic means being deeply connected to others. Based on a putative mechanism of action, it has been suggested that psychedelics belong to a broader class of compounds called psychoplastogens that robustly promote structural and functional neural plasticity in key circuits relevant to brain health. This may explain why psychedelics produce long-lasting therapeutic effects across a wide range of brain disorders [4].

In DSM‑5‑TR [5], psychedelics-related disorders are classified in the section on “Hallucinogen-related disorders,” which comprises two subsections: (i) phencyclidine and (ii) “other hallucinogens.” Ketamine is included in the first subsection because it is considered pharmacologically similar to phencyclidine but less potent. Both are dissociative anesthetics that act primarily as non-competitive antagonists at the NMDA glutamate receptor. In the text of DSM-5-TR, the second subsection includes examples of “other hallucinogens” such as mescaline, DOM (2,5-dimethoxy-4-methylamphetamine), MDMA (3,4‑methylenedioxymethamphetamine), LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), LSA (lysergic acid diethylamide [morning glory seeds]), salvia divinorum, and jimsonweed. In addition to hallucinations, psychedelics alter perception, emotions, consciousness, and social interactions.

From a contemporary clinical and research perspective, no single universally accepted technical definition of “psychedelic” exists. Regulatory agencies have adopted pragmatic working definitions. In its 2023 draft guidance on psychedelic drug development, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) use the term “psychedelic” as “shorthand to include classic serotonergic psychedelics, typically understood as 5-HT2A receptor agonists such as psilocybin and lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), as well as entactogens or empathogens such as methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA).” In research contexts, however, the term “classic psychedelics” is often reserved for compounds whose principal mechanism involves 5-HT2A receptor agonism and which reliably induce alterations in perception, cognition, and sense of self. Other compounds frequently grouped under the psychedelic umbrella, such as MDMA or ketamine, have distinct pharmacological profiles and subjective effects. For the purposes of this article, the term “psychedelics” is used in this broader regulatory sense unless otherwise specified.

Classifications of psychedelics

Kelmendi et al. [6] classify psychedelics into four chemical classes: tryptamines, ergolines, phenethylamines, and dissociatives and “deliriants” (Table 1). Many classic psychedelics exert their effect by being agonists at the 5-HT2A serotonin receptors, but this is not a universal feature of all compounds grouped under the broader psychedelic umbrella, as exemplified by the fact that MDMA has a weak binding to serotonin receptors. Ergolines are the least selective class of psychedelics with high affinities for serotonin, dopamine, histamine, adrenergic, and sigma receptors. In general, tryptamines have high affinity for most serotonin receptors, while phenethylamines tend to be more selective for the 5-HT2 family.

Beyond their chemical classification, psychedelics show considerable variations in their effects. Each substance has a unique profile (Table 2). The effects vary from subject to subject and are highly dependent on the environment and setting [7]. Physiological toxicity is remarkably low for 5-HT2A psychedelics in humans at recreational doses. In contrast, MDMA, ketamine, and especially ibogaine carry significant physical risks. MDMA and ketamine are pharmacologically distinct but often grouped in discussions about psychedelic-assisted therapy. In terms of pharmacological mechanism (see Table 2), ketamine, a NMDA agonist with only low-affinity binding at the 5-HT2A receptor, is not a “true” psychedelic.

Psychedelics as a subject of medical research

Psychedelics gained a bad reputation after their ban from medical research in the 1970s. Yet, this ban was brief compared to the long human relationship with psychoactive drugs, which dates back to hunter-gatherer times. Historical records and archaeology show that humans have used these compounds for religious, medicinal, and recreational purposes for millennia [8]. Advocates often use this ancient use to support renewed research into psychedelics.

In Eurasian cultures, the traditional symbol of psychedelics is Amanita muscaria, also known as the fly agaric. This mushroom, depicted in fairy tales with a bright red cap covered with distinctive white warts, was used in shamanistic rituals by peoples of northeast Siberia and Kamchatka, such as the Koryaks [9]. In ancient Greece, groups of initiates underwent mystic experiences in the Eleusinian mysteries, held in the city of Eleusis, now called Elefsina. It is supposed that the subjects were initiated into the cult of the goddess Demeter with psychedelic compounds in a potion called Kykeon (Ancient Greek: κυκεών, kykeȏn; from κυκάω, kykáō; “to stir, to mix”). According to G. Samorini [10], fragments of Claviceps purpurea, an ergot fungus, were found at the Mas Castellar site (Girona, Spain) in a vase and in the dental calculus of a 25-year-old man, providing evidence of ergot consumption. Ergot contains ergotamine, not lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD). This supports the hypothesis that LSD-like psychedelic alkaloids may have been an ingredient of the Eleusinian kykeon.

Miller et al. [11] reported that the analysis of organic residues from a shamanic paraphernalia recovered from archaeological excavations in Bolivia, dated to ca. 1,000 C.E., identified traces of bufotenine, dimethyltryptamine, harmine (a MAO-A inhibitor, occurring int the plant banisteriopsis, that is essential for enabling the oral activity of DMT in Ayahuasca), and cocaine, including its degradation product benzoylecgonine. In addition, the article by Miller et al. reports findings from other studies on several pre-Columbian mummies from South America that identified cocaine and/or its metabolite BZE, in young infants who likely received the chemical through their mother’s breastmilk, and traces of harmine in the hair of an infant, likely from banisteriopsis consumption for medicinal reasons. This suggests that young infants were exposed to potentially psychedelic compounds directly or through maternal breastmilk.

After decades of ill repute, psychedelics became respectable therapeutic drugs, with dozens of pharma companies and biotech organizations currently developing psychedelic-based therapies. In 2019, the US Food and Drug Administration authorized an esketamine nasal spray (Spravato®) in combination with an oral antidepressant as a therapy for treatment-resistant depression. In January 2025, the FDA expanded this drug’s approval to include stand-alone use [12, 13]. This drug is currently approved in adults aged 18 or older. Both the US and EU regulations mandated pediatric development for Spravato®, including deferred clinical trials to evaluate its safety and efficacy in adolescents. Moreover, anecdotal reports indicate that some clinicians might consider prescribing it off-label for patients aged 16 and older. Australia’s Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) [14] legalized the prescription by psychiatrists of MDMA for PTSD and psilocybin for depression. From July 1, 2023, Australian psychiatrists can use MDMA to treat post-traumatic stress disorder and psilocybin to treat depression if the patient has not responded to other treatment. In Switzerland, the Bundesamt für Gesundheit (BAG) operates a compassionate use program that allows psychiatrists and other qualified physicians to apply for exceptional authorizations on a case-by-case basis (Article 8, paragraph 5, of the Federal Act on Narcotics and Psychotropic Substances [BetmG; SR 812.121]). As of 2024, the program had expanded significantly, with around 100 authorized physicians and hundreds of patient treatments annually (primarily psilocybin for depression, MDMA for PTSD, and LSD for various indications).

In the 1960s, psychedelics were primarily used as facilitators of psychotherapy, rather than stand-alone pharmacological treatments. Their pharmacological effects were considered to enhance insight and emotional processing within an ongoing psychotherapeutic framework. Contemporary research encompasses different approaches. Some protocols continue to embed psychedelic administration within structured standardized psychotherapy, whereas others emphasize the pharmacological and neuroplastic effects of the compounds themselves, with more limited psychotherapeutic support. The respective contributions of drug effects and psychotherapeutic context remain subjects of ongoing scientific investigation and regulatory discussion.

Does this Renaissance apply to children and adolescents? Can we envisage a therapeutic use of psychedelics in persons under 18?

The rationale for psychedelic research in pediatric psychopharmacology

What looks like a psychedelic trip in children’s literature is Lewis Carroll’s Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland. After drinking from a bottle, Alice, a seven-year-old girl, experiences body distortion: she is “shutting up like a telescope” and shrinks to a height of only ten inches. Next, she meets a blue caterpillar quietly smoking a long hookah, sitting on a mushroom. The caterpillar tells her she will grow smaller or taller by eating from one side or the other of the mushroom. When Alice nibbles from different sides of the mushroom, she feels scary changes in her height.

The question of whether psychedelics have a place in pediatric psychopharmacology should not be determined solely by adult efficacy data but should instead be based on three interrelated pillars: a plausible neurobiological mechanism relevant to developmental psychopathology, the presence of severe and persistent unmet clinical needs, and a regulatory and ethical framework capable of ensuring rigorous safeguards.

A significant hurdle in the study of psychedelics in children and adolescents is the lack of long-term safety data regarding their effects on the developing brain. In terms of brain development, there are no precise age boundaries separating childhood, adolescence, and adulthood. The 18-year limit for distinguishing the minority from the majority is merely a legal notion. The brains of children, adolescents, and young adults demonstrate considerable plasticity from birth to 25 years of age. Research mapping the anatomical progression of human cortical gray matter development from ages 4 to 21 years [15] indicates that higher-order association cortices mature after lower-order somatosensory and visual cortices, which they integrate, and that phylogenetically older brain regions mature earlier than more recently evolved areas. There is a paucity of research involving psychedelics in individuals under 18 years of age. The medical principle of “primum non nocere” is particularly emphasized in pediatric populations, where the highest standards for drug safety and tolerability are required. The challenges associated with drug development in children contribute to the limited availability of pharmacological treatments for pediatric and adolescent populations compared to adults. Psychedelics have been suspected of inducing prolonged psychotic symptoms and, in some cases, psychotic disorders. Ismi et al. [16], reported that acute subjective effects varied by age group; adolescents experienced significantly more challenging experiences and ego-dissolution. Among adolescents, visual symptoms associated with “hallucinogen persisting perceptual disorder” (HPPD) were observed at a higher prevalence than in adults (73.5% vs. 34.2%, p < .001), although only one adolescent participant reported these symptoms as distressing.

Classical psychedelics exert their effects primarily through agonism at the 5-HT2A receptor, leading to acute alterations in large-scale network synchrony and subsequent increases in markers of neuroplasticity. These actions may transiently destabilize entrenched network patterns and open a window for psychological and behavioral change, as demonstrated in preclinical and adult human studies.

The proposed neurobiological mechanisms are theoretically relevant to adolescence, a developmental period characterized by heightened neuroplasticity and ongoing maturation of frontolimbic circuits involved in emotion regulation, cognitive flexibility, and social cognition [17]. Many psychiatric disorders emerging during adolescence are associated with maladaptive network rigidity, impaired fear extinction, and dysfunctional large-scale connectivity. Psychedelics, conceptualized as psychoplastogens, have been shown in adult and preclinical models to increase expression of plasticity-related genes and proteins (e.g., BDNF), promote synaptogenesis, and modulate network dynamics. Although direct pediatric data remain limited, this mechanistic framework aligns with current models of adolescent psychopathology and supports cautious, hypothesis-driven investigation. Such mechanistic plausibility does not in itself establish clinical efficacy or safety in minors, but it provides a theoretical foundation beyond simple empirical extrapolation from adult studies.

In addition to this mechanistic rationale, ethical considerations support cautious investigation of psychedelics in children and adolescents, especially given the significant unmet needs in pediatric psychopharmacology.

Cortese S, Purper-Ouakil et al. [18] published a position paper highlighting these unmet needs, emphasizing that few medications are approved for pediatric use, particularly in Europe compared with the USA. Several approved drugs, such as the widely prescribed cyamemazine in France, are older antipsychotics that have not been evaluated according to contemporary research standards. The scarcity of approved medications has led to widespread off-label prescribing in this population. For all pediatric disorders, current pharmacological or psychological therapies demonstrate only modest efficacy (except for ADHD). According to a comprehensive survey among members of the European College of Neuropsychopharmacology–Child and Adolescent Network, priority areas for further pharmacological development include autism spectrum disorder (core symptoms), emotional dysregulation and irritability, anorexia nervosa and depressive disorders (with equal priority), suicidal behaviors (including self-harm and suicidal ideation or attempts), conduct disorder and aggressiveness, substance use disorders, borderline personality disorder, eating disorders other than anorexia nervosa, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and body dysmorphic disorder.

Persistent unmet needs in pediatric psychopharmacology underscore the necessity for novel therapeutic approaches. Jeffrey et al. [19] argue that the benefit-to-risk ratio justifies clinical trials involving classic serotonergic psychedelics, such as psilocybin, and compounds with empathogenic and entactogenic effects, such as 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA), in combination with psychotherapy for select adolescents aged 16 to 17 years. The authors specifically recommend evaluating these interventions in adolescents within this age group who exhibit treatment-resistant psychiatric disorders, defined as multiple unsuccessful trials of current evidence-based therapies, or who present with psychiatric conditions that align with the adult evidence base, as indicated, for example, by the US Food and Drug Administration’s breakthrough designation for specific psychedelic medicines (e.g., psilocybin for major depressive disorder, MDMA for posttraumatic stress disorder).

Data from existing studies so far show that classic psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, DMT, 5‑MeO‑DMT) are generally well tolerated in clinical or research settings. Hinkle et al. [20] reviewed 214 unique studies, of which 114 (53.3%) reported analyzable adverse event (AE) data for 3504 participants. Serious adverse events (SAEs) were not observed among healthy participants and occurred in approximately 4% of participants with preexisting neuropsychiatric disorders; among these SAEs were worsening depression, suicidal behavior, psychosis, and convulsive episodes. In contemporary research settings, there were no reports of deaths by suicide, persistent psychotic disorders, or hallucinogen persisting perception disorders following administration of high-dose classic psychedelics. certain catastrophic events reported in recreational or nonclinical contexts have not yet been reported among contemporary trial participants.

Studies of psychedelics in children and adolescents

In a 1973 review of the clinical and experimental literature on “childhood autism,” Ornitz [21] reported that the psychotomimetic drug LSD-25 had been used to treat autistic children. Two reports suggested that the drug increased social responsiveness. One study emphasized the rapid mood swings from elation to depression, the anxiety, the flattening of affect, and the auditory and visual hallucinations and decreased alertness, which were induced in most of the children. A study indicated that while positive changes in affect occur, they are highly variable and transient and are accompanied by diminished responsiveness. According to Ornitz, the evidence to date threw doubt on the usefulness of this “psychotomimetic” agent as a therapeutic adjunct in the treatment of childhood autism.

A scoping review by Rajwani et al. [22] on 21st-century psychedelic research found only three trial registrations and one trial plan in the period between January 2000 and April 2025 involving participants younger than 18 years. These studies proposed to investigate MDMA- or psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy in adolescents with PTSD, autism with social anxiety, or self-harm. One of these proposals on MDMA-assisted psychotherapy in adolescents with PTSD was withdrawn. The authors stressed that “this lack of research indicates a critical evidence gap that will impede informed clinical decision-making.” However, it seems that psychedelic pediatric studies are becoming more frequent. A large, randomized study was published very recently investigating esketamine in adolescents with major depressive disorder at imminent suicide risk [23]. It is remarkable that this study recruited adolescents with a moderate to extreme suicide risk, a population that is typically excluded from antidepressant trials. This study included a total of 146 adolescents (60 aged 12 to 14, and 86 aged 15 to 17), which probably makes it the largest psychedelic study conducted so far in this age group. Esketamine-treated study participants experienced a rapid reduction of depressive symptoms. Results on the primary efficacy endpoint revealed statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in depressive symptoms at 24 h post-first dose with the pooled esketamine 56 mg and 84 mg doses compared with psychoactive placebo (midazolam), each in conjunction with a comprehensive standard of care.

Much modern clinical research is focused on depression and PTSD. However, psychedelics might also prove useful in disorders that are currently notoriously difficult to treat, such as anorexia nervosa (AN), obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Anorexia nervosa is a very lethal disease with very few effective treatments. According to Kaye et al. [25], the benefits/risks ratio of psilocybin treatment of AN is satisfactory in adolescents, when considering the mortality and morbidity of untreated AN (death, failure to achieve normal body height). The potential interest of psychedelics in OCD was reviewed by Collins [26]. Thirty to sixty% of patients with OCD do not respond to SSRI treatment and are considered treatment-resistant. Although cognitive behavioral therapy and exposure and response prevention therapy are psychological treatments for OCD, their effectiveness depends on access to highly trained specialists and regular sessions, which limits their availability. Given these challenges, it is important to investigate alternative biological mechanisms. For example, the 5-HT2A receptor may be involved in the emergence of OCD symptoms, and psychedelics act, at least acutely, as agonists at these receptors. Based on these observations, hypotheses have emerged regarding enhanced neuroplasticity and modulation of the default mode network (DMN).

Adolescents with autism are sometimes stuck in the avoidance of situations with a rigid refusal to envisage other solutions. These studies are conducted with psilocybin or MDMA. Most studies concern adults, but the results can be generalized to subjects aged ≥ 16 years. The results of the first controlled study of MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for social anxiety in autistic adults (NCT02008396) were published by Danforth et al. [27]. The MDMA group had a greater mean reduction of social anxiety, as measured with the Leibowicz Social Anxiety Scale, than the placebo group after 1 month. This improvement persisted at the 6-month follow-up. The participants were 31.3 years old on average, but the results could probably be extrapolated to younger persons with ASD. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that a combination of MDMA and psychotherapy can diminish anxiety and improve social functioning in persons with ADHD.

Neuroplasticity and long-term considerations

Agnorelli et al. [28] posit that psychedelics might distinguish themselves from traditional medications by demonstrating rapid and enduring therapeutic effects after a single or a few administrations, which might stem from their neuroplasticity-enhancing properties. Clinical trials have shown that with 2–3 active MDMA-assisted sessions, the therapeutic effects were still present 1–2 months after the final session. Classic psychedelics and related compounds produce rapid and sustained reductions in symptoms of mood disorders. Holze et al. [29] reported that after 2 sessions, LSD produced long-lasting and notable reductions in anxiety and comorbid depression symptoms up to 16 weeks. While the acute effects of these drugs on perception have long been appreciated, their sustained effects are now being investigated to explain how they can produce long-lasting therapeutic responses after only a single or a few administrations [30].

Such durability of psychedelic effects, while therapeutically promising, also raises important questions in the context of the developing brain. Often attributed to neuroplasticity-enhancing properties, these enduring effects have particular long-term implications in pediatric populations. Ongoing neurodevelopment may confer vulnerability to persistent neurobiological changes, including potential behavioral sensitization. While plasticity-related mechanisms may underpin sustained therapeutic benefit, they also warrant careful evaluation of unintended developmental consequences.

Evidence from studies of adolescent recreational substance use, particularly early and heavy cannabis use, demonstrates an association with increased risk of psychotic disorders, highlighting the developmental vulnerability of the adolescent brain to exogenous psychoactive exposures.

In contrast, epidemiological data on classic psychedelics do not consistently show an increased incidence of persistent psychotic disorders in the general population. Large genetically informative studies in adolescents suggest that, after adjusting for other substance use, psychedelic exposure is not associated with increased psychotic symptoms and may even correlate with fewer such symptoms; however, psychedelic use has been associated with increased manic symptoms in individuals with higher genetic vulnerability to schizophrenia or bipolar I disorder [31]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses further indicate that, in contemporary research settings employing rigorous screening procedures, the incidence of persistent psychotic disorders following classic psychedelic exposure is very low.

These findings underscore the importance of thorough psychiatric screening, careful exclusion of individuals with personal or familial vulnerability to psychotic or bipolar disorders, and long-term safety monitoring in pediatric research protocols.

Addressing these concerns requires a stepwise approach combining juvenile animal studies to assess neurotoxicity and sensitization phenomena, carefully designed adolescent trials incorporating extended follow-up and neurocognitive monitoring, and strict adherence to regulatory safeguards governing pediatric drug development.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued draft guidance in 2023 on psychedelic drug development, emphasizing rigorous safety evaluation, long-term follow-up, and careful risk–benefit assessment [32]. Although such guidance does not specifically mandate juvenile animal toxicology studies for psychedelics, broader pediatric regulatory frameworks require that pediatric development be formally considered, including age-specific risk-benefit evaluation, consideration of juvenile animal toxicology studies, appropriate developmental safety assessments, and independent safety oversight mechanisms such as data monitoring committees. Furthermore, post-marketing surveillance, including structured pharmacovigilance reporting, is essential to detect any adverse outcomes. Together, these scientific and regulatory measures provide a structured framework for evaluating long-term implications in younger populations.

Scope and limitations

This review focuses primarily on clinical, developmental, and regulatory considerations related to psychedelic research in pediatric psychopharmacology. Given the limited availability of pediatric-specific data, much of the mechanistic and therapeutic rationale discussed is derived from adult clinical studies and preclinical research. Particular emphasis has been placed on clarifying terminology and pharmacological classifications in order to reduce conceptual ambiguity for this renaissance of psychedelic research. Broader societal, ethical, and policy implications, including issues related to medicalization and public perception, are of paramount importance but extend beyond the scope of this article and warrant dedicated interdisciplinary analysis.

Conclusion

The renaissance of psychedelic research in psychopharmacology, exemplified by regulatory approvals for esketamine in adult populations, holds promise for addressing unmet needs in child and adolescent psychiatry. While classical and atypical psychedelics such as esketamine, psilocybin, MDMA, and LSD offer innovative mechanisms—potentially enhancing neuroplasticity and yielding long-lasting effects after limited sessions—their application in pediatric populations demands rigorous caution due to the vulnerabilities of the developing brain, and limited long-term safety data. Emerging clinical trials, predominantly in adolescents aged 16 and older, demonstrate preliminary efficacy in conditions like treatment-resistant depression, PTSD, anorexia nervosa, OCD, and core symptoms of autism spectrum disorder, with favorable tolerability in controlled settings. However, the near-absence of research in younger children leaves a crucial evidence gap, particularly for urgent scenarios such as imminent suicide risk or palliative care. As psychedelic-assisted therapies evolve, prioritizing ethical frameworks, robust trial designs, and multidisciplinary oversight will be essential to balance potential benefits against risks, ultimately fostering safer, more effective interventions for youth facing severe psychiatric challenges.
