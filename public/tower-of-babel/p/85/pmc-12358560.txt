The association between fronto-insular interaction and executive function during childhood and adolescence in a resting state fMRI study
Authors: Jiyea Kim, Narae Kim, Yeeun Kim, Bumhee Park, Min Hyeon Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Insula is an area of the brain that plays a vital role in various functions, mainly several executive functions (EFs) during neurological development. Functional connectivity (FC) between the Frontal lobes and the Insula, the regions most commonly associated with EFs, is claimed to play an essential role in EFs. However, there is insufficient data on the relationship between the FC between the two brain regions and the EFs in childhood and adolescence. We investigate the relationship between the degree of functional connection between the Insula and the Frontal lobe and EFs in a sample of Korean children’s and adolescent communities. Total 95 participants between the ages of 6 and 17 were recruited. An fMRI seed-based connectivity analysis was conducted with the Insula and all the Frontal lobe areas of interest. A partial correlation analysis of the Stroop and Children’s Color Trails Test (CCTT) were performed to measure the overall EFs. One hundred eleven children and adolescents (average age 12.23) participated in the resting functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) study. The Stroop and CCTT tests found a strong positive correlation with the functional connection between the Insula and the Frontal lobes. In particular, this study confirmed that the solid functional connection between the Inferior front gyrus and the Insula is related to good linguistic ability and attention. The functional solid connection between the Prefrontal gyrus and the Insula is related to developed inhibition and cognitive flexibility.

Introduction

The Insula is a triangular-shaped portion of the cerebral cortex. This hidden cortex cannot be seen from the surface1 and is surrounded by the anterior, superior, and medial limiting sulcus and separates the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes2. The development of the Insula starts relatively early in the fetus’s life, but maturation progress is protracted throughout childhood and adolescence as the cortex continues to thin3,4. Cortical thickness of the Insula decreases from 10 to 22 years of age, and changes in structural connectivity of the Insula occur primarily from age 12 to 30 with decreases in fiber density for connections to or from the Insula3,5. For example, between the ages of 6 and 11, the Insular cortex and the Middle Frontal gyrus in the Frontal lobe gradually thin bilaterally with age4. This may help explain how the Insula appears to be associated with different functions in various thinking and behavior6 and why the Insula is essential to understanding children’s and adolescents’ development.

Along with the Frontal lobe, numerous researchers suggested the Insula as a critical region for executive functions (EFs)7–9, including the associated functions such as impulsiveness, working memory, and attention. EFs refer to high cognitive functions with six subcomponents: focused attention, inhibition, sustained attention, processing speed, shift attention, and working memory. It is well known for its development over the human life span critically develops during childhood and adolescence10. During these periods, brain regions are functionally specialized for specific cognitive operations through functional connectivity, and these activities affect the degree of particular cognitive processing abilities11.

Such research has suggested the Insula as a critical region for EFs. For example, according to Rossi et al.7, smaller gray matter (GM) volume was observed in those who performed better in executive tasks, and these smaller GM volumes were linked in the left Superior Frontal gyrus, the Anterior Insular cortex, and extended to the Inferior Frontal gyrus. These were consistent with their previous meta-analysis that the Insular cortex extended from the bilateral Prefrontal area, associated with cognitive control in children and adolescents with reliable activity across functional neuroimaging studies about inhibition, switching, and working memory tasks5,8. Previous neuroimage studies have shown that the Insula is complex with extensive networks of cortical and subcortical brain regions as an anatomical integration hub capable of higher-level functions such as serving sensory, emotional, motivational and cognitive functions3,12–14. Recent neuroscience studies have evaluated the relations between the Frontal lobe and the Insula and their association with EFs within the triple network models, including default-mode network (DMN), central-executive network (CEN) and salience network (SN), which is based on a central hypothesis that SN particular fronto-insular cortex (FIC) acts as a hub to mediate the interaction between DMN and CEN15–17. For instance, it was discovered that the right front-insular cortex was activated significantly in switching between CEN and DMN during attention and working memory tasks from resting state fMRI data18,19.

Even though many studies have evaluated FIC and its effect on EFs8, there is still not enough evidence to identify the association between Frontal-Insula functional connection with EFs, particularly regarding natural brain development during childhood and adolescence. Many previous neuroimaging studies have focused on structural changes in age-related development and EFs with the association between the Insula and the Frontal lobe during childhood and adolescence3–5,7,9,20. Other studies on the functional connectivity (FC) between the Insula and the frontal lobe have been investigated mainly in adult or clinical populations (e.g., Addiction, Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder (OCD), Depression, Alzheimer’s disease, and epilepsy)21–29. Many neuroimaging studies have been based on the population with clinical diagnosis because neuroimage studies suggested assessing various psychiatric research studies to give objectivity to a diagnosis and complement the limitation of diagnosis the psychiatric disorders regarding subjective self-reporting to understand specific brain functional dimensions30. For instance, the different dynamic functional connectivity patterns from rfMRI are presented between children with and without ADHD31. While many recent MRI studies have been extended to explore child and adolescent data from adult data, there are still difficult points to detect due to the fact that developmental brains, such as children and adolescents, may be more sensitive than in adults and they can be more atypical for brain maturation32. This is why we are examining the children and adolescents’ fMRI with clinical evaluation to explore the pattern of brain connection dimension for cognitive function and how it helps clarify the brain biomarker to understand neutral human brain development.

To consider the above concerns, this research was prompted to characterize specific age-related changes to functional connectivity significantly between the Insula and the Frontal lobes that might be related during the developmental period. It was based on the idea that the FC strengths between the Insula and multiple regions at the Frontal lobe will be well-associated with the EFs for children and adolescents. For this aim, we hypothesized that stronger FC between the Insula and Frontal lobe would be presented with better EFs performances in developing populations. The present study used fMRI seed-based connectivity to evaluate the degree of FC between the Insula and all Frontal subregions. Partial correlation analysis was then used to examine the relationship between FC and EFs according to the general development from the community sample of children and adolescents. We set the bilateral Insula and all Frontal subregions as our regions of interest (ROIs). We used the Stroop and Children’s Color Trails Test (CCTT) to examine overall EFs.

Methods and materials

Participants

For the present study, we recruited children and adolescents aged between 6 and 17 via flyers posted in schools and libraries in Seoul and Gyeonggi-do Province. We endeavored to enroll the participants who met the following criteria: a participant (a) should be capable of understanding and following the instructions and descriptions fully about the present study; (b) was taking no medications that could significantly influence their waking and sleep conditions; and (c) had no previous problems in neuroimaging and psychological tests. Participants with any clear neuropsychiatric history of psychiatric disorders (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or pediatric psychosis), developmental disorders (autism or intellectual disabilities), learning disabilities, language impairments, uncorrected sensory impairments, neurological disorders (explosive disorder), or acquired brain injury (cerebral palsy) were excluded from the analyses of this study. Of the 129 recruited participants of this study, 18 subjects were excluded during the data collection due to their rejecting or failing brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning or incomplete responses in EF test questions, including Stroop, CCTT, and Full-Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ). Also, during the processing the data, 16 participants are excluded due to the MRI head motion correction. Thus, the data from 95 participants (including 61 males and 50 females, 12.23 ± 2.99) were finally analyzed. This research does not obtain any other personal data except those mentioned above.

The Stroop test

The Stroop test consists of three components: word test, color test, and color word test, and it has been widely used to measure various EFs33. The score for each task is estimated based on the number of completed items on each card34. The word task required participants to read words with black ink-colored names on cards to measure basic reading skills and speed. The color task, which assesses color blindness and motor skills, asked participants to read the color name on a card in the same color ink33–35. Participants were asked to name ink colors instead of printed words for a color word task designed to measure inhibition and cognitive flexibility control. For example, if the card“yellow”is written in red ink, the answer is red36. For each task, participants were given 45 s to answer 100 questions. Each score was corrected T-score for age without other factors, such as the grade or diagnosis of participants, and it was based on the Korean version of age-specific standards. A high T-score indicated better cognitive ability37. The interference score is obtained by subtracting the word score from the color-word score. The smaller the interference score, the higher the cognitive flexibility and ability to respond to task needs37,38. Previous studies have shown that the Stroop test involves various executive functions, including inhibition efficiency, attention, processing speed, cognitive flexibility39, and working memory40. Although many studies have included the Stroop test to measure inhibitory control function, we focused on the EFs for the Stroop effect. This effect can be explained by the discovery of ‘semantic interference’ in previous studies on Stroop, which means that the response time of color reading under inconsistent conditions (W–C) is slower than other conditions (W or C). This might be because there is a conflict between what is done automatically or consciously, making the process more time-consuming. This phenomenon has been described as inhibiting interference relating to the attention that subjects selectively maintain attention on target stimuli and is likely associated with various executive functions, including subjects’ cognitive flexibility and processing speed. It has also been reported that working memory function is closely related to this selective attention41. Considering this, this study focused on the EFs for the Stroop effect and thought it was necessary to comprehensively evaluate all elements of the Stroop performance (word reading, color naming, named color-word) conditions.

Children’s color trails test (CCTT)

Children’s Color Trails Test (CCTT) is a neuropsychological test created to evaluate the Frontal lobe functions in children and adolescents, such as sustained attention, sequencing, and mental processing speed41. In detail, CCTT is one of the quick administering measurements to examine attention, psychomotor speed, perceptual sequencing and tracking (CCTT 1), flexibility, inhibition, and alternating attention (CCTT 2). CCTT has been used to measure children’s executive functions under various conditions, such as the level of executive functioning in children with academic performance for whom without any neurological disease42, impaired executive function of children after physical surgery43, assessing executive function for preschool-aged children44 and executive function of autistic children45. Moreover, it has been used increasingly in evaluating children in cross-cultural environments for neurological and psychiatric disorders because it reduces reliance on language and diminishes the effects of cultural bias and parental verbal reports46,47. CCTT consists of two sub-tests; CCTT-1 is a test for connecting circles in order upon written request, and CCTT-2 is a test that alternately connects colors (yellow and pink) inside a circle while connecting circles with numbers in order. Each test’s completion time, completion time percentile, and interference index A (CCTT2-CCTT1/CCTT1) and B (CCTT2-CCTT1) were measured41. The raw scores of the completion time and the interference index A and B were converted to T scores with an average of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 based on the age standard48.

Korean educational developmental Institute-Wechsler intelligence scale for children

The KEDI-WISC is modified as a Korean version of the original WISC test for Korean populations (WISC-R) and is standardized to measure the intelligence of Korean children49–51. The test comprises Full-Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ) as a total score of the subtests for verbal IQ and performance IQ, including 12 subtests; five verbal scores consisting of vocabulary, information, comprehension, arithmetic, and similarities; five performance scores consisting of picture arrangement, block design, picture completion, digit symbol, object assembly; and two additional subtests of digit span and maze52. We used FSIQ for our 12-year-old or younger participants in this study.

Korean-Wechsler adult intelligence scale III

The K-WAIS III is a Korean version of the revised Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-R)53. It has been widely used to assess the intelligence of Korean adolescents and adults because of its strong validity and reliability54. It contains six verbal subtests, including information, vocabulary, arithmetic, digit span, similarity, and comprehension, and five performance tests, including picture completion, picture arrangement, digit symbol, block design, and object assembly55. In this study, FSIQ was used for our participants who were 13 years or older. Both KEDI-WISC and K-WAIS III are the measures.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data acquisition

We collected data from structural and resting state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) using a 3.0-Tesla scanner (Siemens, Magnetom Tim-Trio, Erlangen, Germany). High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were obtained from every participant using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) pulse sequence (176 slices; TR, 1900 ms; TE, 3.13 ms; flip angle, 9°; voxel size, 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 mm; FOV, 230 × 230 mm). Rs-fMRI data were acquired with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 3000 ms; echo time (TE) = 30 ms; flip angle (FA) = 90°; field-of-view (FOV) = 230 × 230 mm2; matrix size = 64 × 64; voxel size = 3.59 × 3.59 × 4.5 mm3; volumes = 190. Before scanning, participants were instructed not to focus on their specific thoughts nor sleep during the rs-fMRI scanning. While scanning subjects, foam pads were used to reduce the impact of head motion-induced artifacts.

Data preprocessing

Rs-fMRI data was canonically preprocessed with Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/, Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK) toolbox56. After discarding the initial three volumes for addressing MRI signal saturation issues, there remained 187 rs-fMRI volumes. These were preprocessed as follows; (A) slice timing for adjusting the different acquisition time of each slice, (B) realignment for removing any potential head movement, (C) co-registration to T1-weighted images, (D) spatial normalization into a standard stereotactic Montreal Neurological Institutes (MNI) space, and (E) spatial smoothing with 6 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) gaussian kernel.

Defining functional connectivity (FC)

Thirty-two regions of interest (ROIs) containing the bilateral insular and 15 frontal areas that were defined using the automated anatomical labeling (AAL) atlas57 were selected for the present study. Regionally-averaging fMRI time series for each ROI has been further processed to minimize potential effects of nuisance factors, including head motion, cardiac signals and other confounders from non-target tissues through 1) linear detrending; 2) regressing out effects of six rigid motions and their derivatives, and five principal components of the white matter and the cerebrospinal fluid mask segmented using SPM12; 3) spike detection and despiking based on four times of the median absolute deviation; 4) band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz)55–58. Then, the functional connectivity between the regions was defined as Pearson correlation coefficients among their time series and applied Fisher’s r-to-z transformation to improve normality in further correlation studies with Stroop and CCTT test scores.

Correlation analysis between insular-frontal FC strengths and Stroop and CCTT test scores

We investigated the relationship between each insular-frontal FC strength and Stroop/CCTT test scores through partial correlation analyses controlling for age, sex, and FSIQ as covariates with all participants’ data. Also, we analyzed two subgroups by age: Children under 12 and adolescents over 13 (covariates sex and FSIQ). Moreover, each of the subgroups is analyzed by two sex groups, male and female. We considered such ties to be statistically significant if the False Discovery Rate (FDR) is less than 0.1 (i.e., q < 0.1) in our results. Considering our modest sample size, a loose threshold of p-value < 0.01 (uncorrected) was also supposed to show potentially underlying information. MATLAB 2023a was used for all neuroimaging data analyses (MathWorks, Sherborn, MA, USA) (“MATLAB—MathWorks—MATLAB &Simulink,” n.d.) for the present study and SPSS 20.0 for MAC OS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Interestingly, we found a strong association between FC of the bilateral Insula with several frontal subregions and Left Anterior Cingulate cortex (ACC) and Stroop word, color scores, and color-word score. Table 2 shows that Stroop word test scores, color-word scores, and CCTT Index B scores positively correlated with FC strengths between the bilateral insular and various frontal subregions (Covariates: Age, sex, FSIQ). Significantly, Stroop word scores correlated with the bilateral insula pairs with left OFGinf and OFCmid. Discussion

Childhood and adolescence are pivotal periods during which brain regions undergo functional specialization through functional connectivity, leading to the development of specific cognitive processing abilities11. Building upon this neurodevelopmental understanding, this study aimed to investigate the range of functional connectivity (FC) between the bilateral Insula and Frontal lobe subregions, which are recognized as significant areas for Executive Functions (EFs) and to evaluate the hypothesis that stronger FC between these regions is associated with better EFs in generally developing children and adolescents from community samples. To achieve this, we defined the bilateral Insula and Frontal subregions as regions of interest (ROIs) and a resting-state fMRI seed-based connectivity analysis, with EFs assessed using the Stroop and Children’s Color Trails Tests (CCTT). A significant finding of this study was the positive correlations observed between the Insula and Frontal lobe’s FC and their association with EFs across the general age-related development of children and adolescents.

The results collectively suggest that stronger FC between the Insula and the Frontal subregions may be associated with better EFs and performances, consistent with the general developmental trajectory of children and adolescents. Our main findings support the notion that the Insula and the Frontal lobe are essential brain regions for EFs, aligning with numerous previous neuroimaging and cognitive developmental studies16,59–62. For example, the right Dorsolateral Prefrontal cortex, right Middle Frontal gyrus, and bilateral Anterior Insula have been found to be significantly activated in inhibition, switching, and updating tasks during middle childhood63.

The current findings underscore the significant role of fronto-insular functional connectivity (FC) in the development of Executive Functions (EFs) during childhood and adolescence. This observed strengthening of FC between the Insula and various Frontal lobe subregions not only reflects the maturation of localized executive control abilities but also provides crucial insight into the broader maturation of large-scale brain networks, such as the salience and frontoparietal systems. Recent neuroscience studies emphasize the"triple network model,"which includes the default-mode network (DMN), central-executive network (CEN), and salience network (SN)15–19. Within this model, the salience network, particularly the fronto-insular cortex (FIC), is hypothesized to act as a crucial hub to mediate interactions between the DMN and the CEN64–66. The CEN is strongly associated with executive functions, while the DMN is typically active during introspective and internally focused thought processes67. The efficient switching and coordination between these large-scale networks are fundamental for adaptive cognitive control and flexible behaviour15–17.

Our findings, demonstrating robust positive correlations between Insula-Frontal FC and various EF domains, directly support the idea that the maturation of executive control networks, represented by this fronto-insular interaction, is integral to the development of these larger systems. For instance, the significant activation of the right fronto-insular cortex during switching between CEN and DMN in attention and working memory tasks68, as noted in previous resting-state fMRI data, highlights the FIC’s pivotal role in dynamic network reconfiguration18,19. Furthermore, the involvement of frontal regions, which are key components of the frontoparietal control system (often overlapping with the CEN), in our observed correlations reinforces this interpretation. The frontoparietal system is known for its role in goal-directed behavior, working memory, and cognitive flexibility – all core EFs60. Thus, the strengthening of FC between the Insula and frontal areas like the orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus (OFGinf), orbital part of the middle frontal gyrus (OFCmid), dorsal part of the superior frontal gyrus (SFGdor), medial part of the superior frontal gyrus (SFGmed), and precentral gyrus (PrCG) directly contributes to the overall maturation and efficiency of these large-scale frontoparietal executive control systems. This maturation enables children and adolescents to more effectively allocate attentional resources, inhibit irrelevant information, and flexibly adapt to changing cognitive demands, reflecting a more finely tuned interplay within the brain’s major functional networks69–72.

In detail, we found many Frontal regions associated with the Insula functionality in the Stroop word test, which measures verbal ability, motor speech, attention, and cognitive flexibility. The relationship between the Insular and Frontal lobe as critical for verbal skills has been consistently evaluated in some studies. Augustine1 presented Insula-Frontal lobe connections associated with speech and motor skills. His research suggested that the Insular cortex might be a specialized region for language processing, and its connectivity with the Inferior Frontal gyrus showed an association for this function. Moreover, Tonkonogy and Goodglass73 indicated that the FC between the Inferior Frontal gyrus and the Insula was significantly associated with speech and motor functions in aphasia cases, also including the Rolandic operculum area linked to word-finding difficulties. Our results further showed that the left Orbital part of the Inferior Frontal gyrus and the bilateral Insula have significantly positive correlations with Stroop word scores. This suggests that strong FC between the Orbital part of the Inferior Frontal gyrus and the Insula is likely associated with better verbal ability and motor speech function. Additionally, we observed that FC between the Orbital part of the Middle Frontal gyrus and the Insula was associated with Stroop test scores, which may indicate a strong correlation between these regions’ FC and attention and cognitive flexibility. This result is consistent with previous findings that showed a decrease in FC between the Insula and the Middle Frontal cortex during attentional distraction, leading to lower performance in Stroop tasks74. We also found a positive correlation between the FC of the bilateral Insula with the right Precentral gyrus and EF domains, primarily suggesting resilience to cognitive interference and improved attentional change and sequential processing ability, based on our CCTT interference scores48. This is a noteworthy novel finding in our sample of children and adolescents, paralleling previous research indicating a correlation between the Insula and Precentral gyrus in vulnerable cognitive ability functioning. We hypothesize that the FC between the Precentral gyrus, implicated in various EFs such as controlling voluntary motor movement and attention75,76, and the insula’s inhibition control power collaboratively contribute to successful inhibitory control against cognitive interference77. This interaction may also facilitate the adjustment of sequential processing with greater flexibility and attention. This understanding is supported not only by research on structural developmental changes in children and adolescents7,8 or adults with ADHD but also by our findings in typically developing children and adolescents78.

To accurately capture the development of executive function in our sample of children and adolescents, this study employed the Stroop Test and the Children’s Color Trails Test (CCTT). Both instruments are well-established neurocognitive measures widely used in developmental research and clinical practice due to their age-appropriateness and psychometric validation for assessing EFs across various developmental stages33,41. The Stroop Test is a widely recognized measure for various EFs, including inhibition efficiency, attention, processing speed, cognitive flexibility, and working memory35,39. Its utility in children and adolescents is supported by its design, which involves tasks like word reading, color naming, and color-word interference35. The“Stroop effect”itself, characterized by slower response times under inconsistent conditions due to conflict between automatic and conscious processes, is directly linked to inhibiting interference and selective attention, which are central to EFs40. Importantly, the scores in this study were corrected by age-specific T-scores based on a Korean version of age-specific standards, indicating its adaptability and standardization for the developmental range studied. A high T-score indicates better cognitive ability and a smaller interference score reflects higher cognitive flexibility and task responsiveness. The Children’s Color Trails Test (CCTT) is another crucial neuropsychological tool employed, specifically created to evaluate frontal lobe functions in children and adolescents, such as sustained attention, sequencing, and mental processing speed42. In detail, CCTT is one of the quick administering measurements to examine attention, psychomotor speed, perceptual sequencing and tracking (CCTT 1), flexibility, inhibition, and alternating attention (CCTT 2)46. The CCTT’s strength lies in its quick administration and reduced reliance on language, which minimizes cultural bias and parental verbal reports, making it particularly valuable for cross-cultural evaluations in neurological and psychiatric disorders in children46,47. Its effectiveness has been demonstrated in diverse child populations, including those with academic performance concerns without neurological disease, children after physical surgery, preschool-aged children, and autistic children. Similar to the Stroop, CCTT raw scores were converted to age-standardized T-scores, ensuring the developmental appropriateness of the measurements43–45. Together, the selection and application of the Stroop and CCTT tests in this study are psychometrically sound, providing a comprehensive assessment of various EF domains. Their established validity and age-appropriateness, particularly through the use of age-corrected standardized scores, enable reliable capture of the nuances of EF development across childhood and adolescence, thereby strengthening the interpretation of the observed brain-behavior relationships74.

Limitation

Despite the novelty of our findings, our study is subject to several limitations that merit attention. First, the study’s cross-sectional design limits causal inferences between fronto-insular functional connectivity and executive functions, while its modest sample size of 95 participants restricts generalizability and increases susceptibility to outliers or developmental population heterogeneity. Second, although the Stroop and CCTT tests are well-established measures to cover a wide range of EFs, they may be limited to measuring only some aspects of EFs. Investigating this area using multiple measures of EFs that cover a wide variety of domains of EF may be essential, as a wide range of mental health problems, including cognitive issues (e.g., ADHD), often first occur during childhood or adolescence75. This may also provide further insight into a reference for the clinical diagnosis of psychopathology across development76. Also, other critical components of executive functions, such as planning, problem-solving, and emotional regulation, were not evaluated. Future research would benefit from employing a more comprehensive battery of neuropsychological tests to capture the multifaceted nature of executive functions and their neural correlates. Finally, while we controlled for age, sex, and FSIQ, other unmeasured variables could have influenced the observed associations. Factors such as socioeconomic status, educational environment, and lifestyle habits can also impact brain development and cognitive performance. Future investigations should consider these potential confounding variables as multivariate methods, such as the controllability model, to provide a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between fronto-insular connectivity and executive functions77.
