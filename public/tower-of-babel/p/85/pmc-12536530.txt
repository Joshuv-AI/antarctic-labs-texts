Universal health coverage and factors associated with enrolment in the town of Souza-Cameroon
Authors: Mohamed Mounchili, Alice Ketchaji, Kelly Cynthia Fodom Tchogang, Protais Cédric Mouenbori Sawi, Verance Carline Kegha Ngodem, Joseph Raphael Moyo Tachoum, Wilfriede La Grâce Djopwouo Fosto, Divine Guimazeu Tsoplefack, Godfroy Rostant Pokam Djoko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUniversal Health Coverage (UHC) aims to guarantee access to health services without financial risk for the entire population. Despite the efforts made in Cameroon, particularly in Souza, the enrolment rate remains low. This study aims to identify the socio-demographic factors, knowledge, and perceptions that influence the enrolment of residents in the UHC, in order to adapt health policies and strengthen equitable access to care.MethodologyThe study was carried out in the city of Souza over a six-month period, from March to August 2024, with data collected between May and July. A sample of 400 participants was selected, including residents aged 21 and over. A structured questionnaire, pre-tested for clarity, was administered during face-to-face interviews. The data were analysed using R Studio, conducting descriptive, bivariate, and multivariate analyses to identify factors associated with enrolment in the UHC.ResultsThe results show that out of 400 inhabitants, only 7% (28 inhabitants) were enrolled at the UHC. The most common gender was male (51.25%), 34.5% were aged between 21 and 30, and 55.25% had a secondary education. The results of the multivariate analyses showed that having a university education [AOR = 2.01; 95% CI: 1.12–4.91; p = 0.04], having heard of universal health coverage [AOR = 18.99; 95% CI: 4.25–84.88; p = 0.04], and perceiving the cost of enrolment as low [AOR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.45–6.19; p = 0.03] significantly increased the probability of enrolment in universal health coverage.ConclusionsThis study highlights significant barriers to UHC enrolment, including a lack of awareness and negative perceptions of costs. The results suggest that targeted communication efforts and awareness-raising initiatives are essential to improve enrolment. Strengthening education and information about the UHC could contribute to better access to healthcare and increased participation by residents.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13493-z.Keywords: Associated factors, Enrolment, Universal health coverage, Souza-Cameroon

Introduction

Universal health coverage (UHC) is designed to ensure that the entire population has access to the preventive, curative, palliative, rehabilitative, and health-promoting services they need throughout their lives, without the risk of financial hardship [1]. It is based on strong, person-centred primary healthcare, which is of sufficient quality to be effective [1]. In many countries, growing social pressures and the ever-increasing costs of healthcare services have led governments to introduce models of protection against the risk of illness [2, 3]. Although the approaches vary according to local contexts, they all share the same objective: to guarantee equal access to care [4], to ensure equitable treatment of illnesses, and to protect people against the financial risks associated with healthcare expenditure.

Globally, progress towards achieving universal health coverage, set as a target in the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3.8), is slow [1]. Since 2015, health service coverage has stagnated, while the proportion of the population exposed to catastrophic out-of-pocket health expenditure has risen steadily since 2000 [1]. This trend is observed in all regions and in the majority of countries. The service coverage index has risen from 45 in 2000 to 68 in 2021, but recent progress has been weaker: between 2015 and 2021, only three additional points were gained, and there has been no improvement since 2019 [5]. In Africa, the situation regarding universal health coverage (UHC) remains worrying, with major disparities between countries. However, the region has made significant progress: the average service coverage index has risen from 23 in 2000 to 44 in 2021 [5], showing a significant improvement but still insufficient to achieve the targets set. This progress is accompanied by increased efforts, notably the increase in health spending as a percentage of GDP in 23 African countries by 2024, bringing them closer to the 5% target set by the African Union [6]. However, this figure still falls well short of the targets set in the 2001 Abuja Declaration, which called on African Union member states to allocate at least 15% of their national budgets to the health sector. In Cameroon, the service coverage index has also evolved, rising from 22 in 2000 to 44 in 2021, reflecting a similar dynamic to that of the region [5].

Despite these advances, coverage remains limited in Cameroon: in 2021, only 6.46% of the population benefited from social health protection, with a very low mutualisation rate of around 2% [7]. Challenges also persist in terms of access and quality of care, exacerbated by insufficient human resources and fragile healthcare systems [8]. The absence of universal healthcare coverage has serious consequences for public health and the economy. Individuals who are not covered are often forced to face unexpected health expenses, plunging them further into poverty [9]. This lack of access to healthcare leads to an increase in untreated illnesses, lower productivity, and increased pressure on already fragile healthcare systems [10]. What’s more, the absence of UHC exacerbates social inequalities, making the most vulnerable populations even more exposed to health risks [11].

Although many African countries have integrated Universal Health Coverage (UHC) into their national health strategies, progress remains slow [12]. As part of its strategic vision to guarantee equitable access to quality healthcare for Cameroonians, the Cameroonian government has initiated a process aimed at eventually providing the country with universal health coverage [13]. Despite efforts to improve people’s health, the country has a service coverage index of only 44, with fragmented funding between the state, community mutual insurance schemes (1.3% coverage), social security, and private insurance (0.2%) [14]. The financing of the Cameroonian healthcare system is essentially based on State budget al.locations, coordinated by the Ministry of Public Health, and on direct payments from households, which account for 70% of total healthcare expenditure [14].

In Cameroon, implementation of the Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is still in its infancy and has yet to achieve optimum efficiency on a national scale. As part of the pilot phase, the East region has been chosen as a pioneering site to test the scheme, with the official launch of the first phase on April 12, 2023, in Bertoua, under the chairmanship of the Minister of Public Health [15]. This mechanism aims to facilitate access for all citizens to a range of quality healthcare services, without money being an obstacle. Phase I of the UHC covers promotional and preventive care, such as vaccination and screening, as well as some curative care, illustrating the government’s commitment to equitable access to health services. This phase also includes services that are heavily subsidized by the state, such as hemodialysis, while mechanisms for public contribution, such as health vouchers, are mobilized to facilitate access to care. However, by 2023, public allocation to healthcare has fallen from 5.9% in 2019 to 3.3%, leaving households to finance around 72% of healthcare expenditure [16, 17]. The aim of this study is to identify the socio-demographic factors, knowledge, and perceptions that influence the enrolment of Souza residents in the UHC.

By analyzing these elements, we hope to formulate practical recommendations for improving health policies and reinforcing equitable access to care. This research also aims to fill a gap in the literature on UHC in Central Africa, a field that has yet to be fully explored, by shedding light on the equity gaps and financial barriers that hinder universal access to care in low-income contexts [18]. In Cameroon, UHC is still at an early stage of implementation, with a pilot phase covering mainly preventive and promotional care, as well as some basic curative care. The care basket includes, among other things, vaccination, screening, and a range of subsidized services such as hemodialysis, gynecological and neonatal care via the health voucher, as well as free treatment of HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis and malaria for children under five. However, actual coverage of the population remains limited. Financing mechanisms include health vouchers and direct contributions from beneficiaries, who are still required to cover a significant proportion of costs. Governance of the scheme is based on coordination between the Ministry of Public Health, healthcare providers, local authorities, technical and financial partners, and the beneficiaries themselves. Highlighting these aspects will provide a better understanding of the structural and financial challenges facing UHC in Cameroon, and thus strengthen the relevance and scope of the recommendations arising from this work.

Methodology

Study framework and period

The study took place in the town of Souza (Fig. 1) over a six-month period from March 2024 to August 2024. The town of Souza is located in the Abo Health District, in the eastern region of Cameroon, and is bordered to the north by the district of Mbanga; to the north-east by a large forest and wildlife reserve separated by the Dibombé River from Mpobo and Mamba; to the south and east by the district of Dibombari; and to the west (and north) by the Moungo River. It enjoys a four-season equatorial climate, with an average annual temperature of around 23 °C. It has a population of 24,006, representing 77.63% of the District’s total population. It is divided into 58 villages, managed by 40 chiefs in a patriarchal manner. The relatively low standard of living has an impact on access to health and education services, which limits the population’s awareness of the UHC and, consequently, its involvement in this initiative.

Map of the city of Souza (OpenStreetMap)

The town of Souza was chosen for this study because of its relatively low standard of living, which impacts access to healthcare. With a population of 24,006 spread over 58 villages, Souza reflects the challenges faced by many rural populations in Cameroon. The region’s cultural diversity and geographical inequalities make it a relevant setting for examining the factors influencing enrolment in Universal Health Coverage.

Study design and study population

This was a cross-sectional quantitative study with an analytical focus, aimed at analyzing the factors associated with enrolment in universal health coverage by the inhabitants of the town of Souza.

The target population was residents of Souza aged 21 and over who had given informed consent to participate in the study. Non-inclusion criteria included residents under the age of 21 and those who refused to participate in the study, while exclusion criteria included residents who wished to withdraw from the study at any time and those who did not speak the national languages (French or English).

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was determined using the Lorenz formula: n = Z²x P x Q / d² where Z represents the operational error (1.96 for a 95% confidence interval), d the random error (5%), P the proportion of individuals not known, which is why we used 0.5, and Q = 1 - P = 0.5%. Applying these values, we obtained a minimum sample size of 384 individuals. To account for non-response, this size was increased by 16, bringing the final number to 400 individuals, all sexes combined. Households were the primary sampling unit, but within each selected household, all individuals meeting the eligibility criteria were included in the study.

The households to be surveyed for this study were selected using a non-probabilistic method based on convenience. The starting point was determined by throwing a pen on the ground from the centre of the target area; the direction indicated by the tip of the pen defined the direction of the survey’s progress. From the first household containing a target person willing to provide informed consent, the interviewers proceeded in a straight line, selecting the next household located at a distance of approximately 100 m from the previous one.

Data collection techniques and tools

Data collection was carried out by means of a household survey, using a questionnaire structured into 03 main sections, namely: demographic characteristics of participants, knowledge of universal health coverage, and factors associated with enrolment (Questionnaire).

A pre-test of the questionnaire was carried out with 15 participants in another town in the same district in order to assess the clarity of the questions and the effectiveness of the collection method. This pre-test enabled the necessary adjustments to be made to the questionnaire before it was deployed in the town of Souza, thereby ensuring the quality and relevance of the data collected.

Definition and operationalization of variables

In this study, the dependent variable was enrolment in universal health coverage. The independent variables were: participants ‘socio-demographic characteristics (sex, age, marital status, educational level, and religion); participants’ knowledge and perception of the UHC.

Data collection procedure

After obtaining ethical and administrative approval from the regional delegation of public health Delegation régionale de la santé publique du Littoral, we sought the agreement of the head of the Abo health district. In the field, we met with the neighbourhood chiefs to explain the objectives of the study and present the official documents.

Data collection was carried out by trained interviewers, using a structured questionnaire administered individually during face-to-face interviews, in an environment that guaranteed the confidentiality of the participants. Before the conclusion of each interview, the questionnaire was systematically checked to identify and complete any missing data. In addition, a daily debriefing was set up to assess the quality of the data collected and to discuss any difficulties encountered during the survey day.

Data processing and analysis

The data collected was cleaned and coded using Excel 2021, which was also used to generate the tables. Data analysis was carried out using R Studio version 4.2.1. Descriptive analysis of the variables was presented in the form of frequencies and percentages. A bivariate analysis using logistic regression was performed to highlight statistically significant associations between dependent and independent variables at the UHC. All variables with a P value < 0.05 in the bivariate analysis were considered candidates for multivariate analysis in order to control for confounding factors and to identify independent predictors of UHC coiling. The various indicators were estimated with a significance level alpha (α) = 0.05, and only variables with a risk estimate (OR) associated with a P value of less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that only 7% of participants in the city of Souza benefited from Couverture Sanitaire Universelle (CSU). This result concurs with that of Deville et al., who reported a subscription rate of 7% among households in Mali [19], and Christian & Eric, who documented a rate of 9% in Cameroon [20]. Although these studies share methodological similarities with our own, differences in population size, geographical characteristics, and overall education levels must be taken into account when interpreting the results. A significant barrier to underwriting appears to be the lack of awareness of CSU, as many people are unaware of the resources and benefits associated with this coverage. Participants reported several negative perceptions contributing to their reluctance to enroll, including concerns about costs, skepticism about the effectiveness of the coverage, and the perceived complexity of the enrollment process. In addition, participants identified several administrative hurdles, such as high documentation requirements, long processing times, and insufficient guidance, which further complicate the enrolment process. These findings highlight the multifaceted challenges that impede participation in the CSU, underscoring the need to improve awareness and streamline processes to facilitate enrolment.

Moreover, in comparison with the results of studies such as the one carried out in Mali, which reported a coverage rate of 14% [21], and those in Senegal [22] and Ghana [23], which observed enrolment rates of over 50%, there is a significant difference. These disparities can be attributed to more effective public health policies, better infrastructure, and stronger political and community commitment. In line with the WHO recommendation to design a CMU implementation framework adapted to each country’s situation, we propose in this work a reflection adapted to the Cameroonian context, integrating local socio-economic, institutional, and health realities.

However, this study found that enrolment in the UHC was significantly associated with a higher level of education. This finding corroborates the results of another study in Ghana [24], as well as those of Van der W [25]. and a meta-analysis of 23 sub-Saharan African countries [26]. This trend can be explained by several factors. A higher level of education enables individuals to better understand their health rights and the benefits of the UHC, which encourages them to enrol. Moreover, educated people generally have better access to information about health services, making it easier for them to register. In addition, education influences people’s economic situation, making them better prepared for unforeseen health expenses and enrollment in coverage programmes.

The study also found that having heard of universal health coverage was significantly associated with enrolment in the scheme. This result corroborates the findings of a study carried out in Mexico [27], as well as those of Fadlallah et al. [28], Mali [21], and Ethiopia [29]. This association can be explained by the fact that awareness and knowledge of health programmes are essential to encourage people to get involved. Indeed, when people are informed about the UHC, they are more inclined to take steps to enrol. Effective dissemination of information about the UHC, through awareness-raising campaigns, community training, and local initiatives, can play a crucial role in increasing citizen engagement, thereby reducing fears and uncertainties about access to healthcare.

Finally, this study showed that people who perceived the cost of enrolment in the UHC as being low were more inclined to enrol in this system. This finding is in line with studies carried out in Senegal [30], by Fadlallah et al. [28], and by Mussa et al. in Ethiopia [29], which have also shown that favorable perceptions of enrolment costs encourage individuals to enroll. A perception of affordability can significantly reduce the fears and reluctance often associated with enrolment. When individuals feel that enrolment costs are reasonable, they are more likely to consider the CSU a realistic and beneficial option for their health. It’s important to note that the costs associated with the CSU are not fixed; they can vary according to the services provided, further influencing individuals [31]. This dynamic transforms an abstract idea into a concrete possibility and strengthens people’s confidence in the healthcare system. In this way, costs that are deemed affordable help to create an atmosphere in which access to healthcare is perceived not only as a right, but also as an accessible reality.

Limits of the study

This study has several limitations that must be taken into account when interpreting the results. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the factors identified and Universal Health Coverage (UHC) enrolment. Secondly, the sample size, although sufficient for statistical analysis, may not fully reflect the diversity of the Souza population. In addition, participants’ responses may be influenced by social desirability biases, where respondents might underestimate or exaggerate certain responses to conform to social expectations. Finally, the data collection was limited to a single district and does not take into account potential variations in other regions of Cameroon, which could restrict the generalisability of the results.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the enrolment of Souza residents in Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is severely hampered by a number of obstacles, including a lack of awareness and negative perceptions about the costs of enrolment. With only 7% of participants enrolled, it is clear that significant efforts are needed to improve the situation. The results underline the importance of education and information about the UHC, as higher levels of education and knowledge of the benefits of coverage are key factors in encouraging enrolment. To increase the enrolment rate, it is imperative to set up awareness campaigns tailored to the needs of the population. These initiatives should aim to inform citizens about the benefits of the UHC and dispel fears about the associated costs. By facilitating access to information and reinforcing education, decision-makers can promote greater adherence to UHC, thereby contributing to a more inclusive and equitable healthcare system in Cameroon. The success of these efforts will be key to achieving public health goals and ensuring that all Souza residents benefit from quality healthcare without financial risk.
