Late-onset Spinal Infection Caused by Parvimonas micra Following Spinal Instrumentation
Authors: Rohun Gupta, Atharva M. Bhagwat, Sonia S. Patel, Matthew Lindsey, Ali Aral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:The aim of this study is to report on a unique case of a patient with a history of spinal instrumentation who developed an epidural abscess from Parvimonas micra without any overt risk factors. The incidence of spine-related procedures has increased in the last 15 years. However, several studies have found the rate of complications to be up to 26%, including delayed healing and cerebrospinal fluid leak. Other complications may include deep surgical site infections, which are typically caused by gram-positive cocci. We report on a case of a 77-year-old woman with a known history of spinal instrumentation who developed a delayed surgical site infection by P. micra, an anaerobic bacteria. The patient was admitted and underwent debridements along with removal of her hardware. Infectious disease was consulted and the patient was placed on an antibiotic regimen for 6 weeks. The patient required flap-based closure by plastic surgery and was eventually discharged to rehabilitation in good condition. We highlight the importance of managing patients with spinal infections with underlying hardware with broad-spectrum antibiotics along with prompt surgical intervention. Risk factors for P. micra infection may still be unknown.

In the last 15 years there has been a significant increase in the number of spine procedures performed, along with age of patients and the number of procedures performed for degenerative disc disease.1 In the United States alone, up to 900,000 patients undergo spine-related procedures annually.2 Complication rates following spinal surgery are as high as 26%, with typical complications including delayed wound healing, hematoma, and cerebrospinal fluid leakage.3 Freire-Archer et al., found that the rate of deep surgical site infection was 1.5% with recurrence rates and serial debridement rates ranging from 12% to 25%.4 Typical pathogens include gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, with risk factors such as spondylotic disease and procedures performed in the thoracolumbar region.5

Parvimonas micra is an anaerobic, gram-positive cocci, found in the oral and gastrointestinal flora of humans, which has typically been implicated in infections through hematogenous seeding.6 In addition, typical risk factors include periodontal disease or recent dental procedures.7 Furthermore, we present a case report of P. micra infection in a patient without any apparent predisposing risk factors, highlighting the challenges that may occur in a patient’s hospital course.

CASE

A 77-year-old woman with history of hypertension, hypothyroidism, and degenerative scoliosis underwent T10 to pelvis posterior instrumented spinal fusion with laminectomies at L1–L5 with L5–S1 interbody in 2014. The patient noted significant improvements in neurogenic claudication following the procedure and healed well. The patient’s course was complicated by a motor vehicle accident in 2020, resulting in hardware failure requiring rod replacement. The patient had no acute neurologic or spinal complaints following her procedure.

Five years later, the patient presented to our institution with a 1-month history of progressive drainage for which she had previously started oral antibiotics and local wound care. Imaging was conducted, which found hardware in an appropriate position along with a sinus tract communicating from the surgical site. Initial laboratory evaluation resulted with a peripheral leukocyte count (white blood cell) of 9000 cells/mm3 (normal range, 4000 to 10,700 cells/mm3), an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 49 mm/h (normal level < 30 mm/h), and a C-reactive protein of 52 mg/dL (normal range, 0 to 0.5 mg/dL). Blood cultures were obtained and were negative.

The patient underwent an initial irrigation and debridement with findings of multifocal pockets of purulence, which were cultured and ultimately positive for P. micra (Fig. 1). Additionally, there was noted to be erosion of hardware through fascia bilaterally with bridging bone through the region of hardware. A thorough intraoral examination was performed by the hospitalist and infectious disease (no dental consultant available), and there was no concern for an intraoral infection. The patient did not have a prior history of spine-related infection or recent dental work. A preliminary malignancy screening was negative with plans for the patient to complete a colonoscopy on an outpatient basis. Given the work-up, infectious disease recommended 6 weeks of antibiotic therapy with vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and metronidazole. Targeted therapy was not conducted given the recent increase in resistance of the microbe to antibiotics and a lack of culture sensitivities.

The patient underwent repeat debridement with removal of her spinal hardware by orthopedic surgery. Repeat intraoperative cultures were found to be negative. Also, a postoperative stability assessment was conducted with imaging and a detailed physical and occupational therapy assessment, which demonstrated no acute concerns for insufficient biomechanical integrity. Given overall improvements in the patient’s clinical condition and wound status, plastic surgery was needed due to the extent of the devitalized tissue and underlying fascia.

A thorough work-up was conducted by our service. A prior magnetic resonance imaging of the spine had been conducted which was reviewed and noted to have perforators to the paraspinous musculature, although significantly limited given the extent of motion and hardware related artifact. The patient underwent repeat debridement by orthopedic surgery and bilateral paraspinous flaps for closure with the plastic surgery team. Dissection was initially conducted in a suprafascial plane bilaterally to elevate the skin and subcutaneous tissue. This was undertaken until the lateral aspect of the paraspinous muscle was encountered, followed by a longitudinal incision at the lateral aspect allowing for midline transposition. Following adequate mobilization of the muscle and spinal coverage, the patient’s wounds were closed (Fig. 2). Following surgical intervention, the patient did well and was discharged to rehabilitation in good condition. She was evaluated outpatient and was noted to have healed well at her 3 month follow up.

DISCUSSION

Our case highlights a unique scenario for a patient with spinal hardware that required hardware explantation along with plastic reconstructive surgery closure due to infection from P. micra. A literature search was conducted at the time of the conception of this article with a total of 4 databases (PubMed, COCHRANE, EMBASE, and CINAHL) in December of 2025 with the following search terms: Parvimonas micra, spine/spinal infection, and hardware. Although there was 1 report in the literature of a patient with spinal instrumentation to have an epidural abscess from the same pathogen, to the best of our knowledge this is the first report of a patient with spinal hardware to have a delayed onset (> 1 y) and hardware-associated infection from this pathogen without any typical predisposing risk factors.8

Several studies and reviews have determined that relative risk factors for P. micra colonization include malignancy, diabetes, postarthroplasty, and recent dental work. There have been fewer than 35 cases of P. micra bacteremia reported in the literature, with spinal and peri-prosthetic infections being the most common.9 Although no clear source of bacterial infection was found in our patient, it is possible there may have been translocation from the gastrointestinal tract, dental carries, or even an undiagnosed malignancy. Additional causes may be attributable to the biofilm-forming capacity of P. micra which can modulate molecular expression on the biofilm matrix. Finally, given the bridging bone formation around the hardware, it is possible there may have been a chronic indolent infection.

Furthermore, our case is unique in that it shows that individuals with minimal risk factors for certain bacterial infections may still develop bacterial infection and joint seeding. In this patient, there was no prior periodontal history or immediate concerns for malignancy, although it was recommended that a more thorough work-up be conducted on an outpatient setting, something that did not occur secondary to patient compliance. Several studies have demonstrated that oral cavity infections are some of the most common causes of pyogenic spondylodiscitis, which once again raises the questionability of dental prophylaxis before spinal procedures.10 Also, based on literature review, it appears the majority of spinal-related cases of P. micra infections involve the native spine and disc, unlike this patient. All in all, this case demonstrates the importance of further research and team-based and patient-centric care.

In patients with spinal instrumentation who present with a chronic infection, it is critical to involve our infectious disease colleagues to appropriately determine the source of infection. In this case, this patient was diagnosed with infection from P. micra, which is typically found following dental procedures without having any prior risk factors. The extent of her infection required prompt surgical drainage and eventual reconstruction with plastic surgery. Reconstruction for this patient was centered on providing durable and well-vascularized tissue over her spine to promote appropriate healing.
