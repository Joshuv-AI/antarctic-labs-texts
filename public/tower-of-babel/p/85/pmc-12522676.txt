Prepubertal testicular neuroendocrine tumor in postpubertal males: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Zhang Zhiqi, Nie Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn 2016, the World Health Organization classification system of testicular tumors included the new entity prepubertal-type testicular neuroendocrine tumor based on its morphological and molecular profile, and the realization that these tumors may occur in postpubertal men. For treatment and prognostic purposes, it is important to distinguish prepubertal-type testicular neuroendocrine tumor from the usual postpubertal-type testicular neuroendocrine tumor, because the former is benign unlike the latter. The distinction may be challenging. Therefore, we hope that ultrasound examination can play an important role in the differential diagnosis of testicular tumors.Case presentationsThese two cases involved postpubertal males, one is 41-years-old and the other is 62-years-old, they both are Chinese and of the Han ethnicity. Both ultimately underwent surgical treatment without subsequent chemotherapy or other interventions. Up to now, during follow—up, the patients have exhibited no other symptoms.ConclusionPrepubertal-type testicular neuroendocrine tumor is a rare neuroendocrine tumor, and accounts for about 0.23% of testicular tumors. Clinical manifestations and images of it are not specific, routine ultrasound has significant advantages for early detection of lesions and short-term follow-up, and when combined with relevant laboratory tests, it may aid in the diagnosis of the disease, and the treatment is mainly radical surgery, and the prognosis is good.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-025-05579-3.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are a rare type of tumor originating from peptidergic neurons and neuroendocrine cells, characterized by neuroendocrine differentiation and the expression of neuroendocrine markers. They can occur throughout the body, with the most common sites being the lungs and the gastroenteropancreatic system (gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasm, GEP-NEN) [1]. Pathologically, on the basis of the degree of differentiation, NENs are classified into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs). Testicular neuroendocrine tumors (prepubertal-type), previously known as carcinoids, were first reported in 1907 by the German pathologist Oberndorfer. These tumors are extremely rare, accounting for only 0.23% of all testicular tumors [2]. In the 2022 fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Urinary System and Male Genital Organs, the term “carcinoid” for testicular tumors was renamed “prepubertal-type testicular neuroendocrine tumor” (postpubertal NETs are rare) [3].

Case report

Case 1: A 41 year-old Han Chinese male patient was admitted to the hospital due to “discovery of right scrotal swelling for more than 6 months.” Specialized physical examination revealed normal scrotal skin, mild enlargement of the right testicle with a hard texture and no tenderness, and no abnormalities detected in the left testicle. Laboratory tests showed: AFP: 1.98 ng/ml (reference range 0.00–7.00), CA72-4: 13.90 U/ml (reference range 0.00–6.90), NSE 30.60 ng/ml (reference range 0.00–20.46). The patient was healthy and had no family history of the disease. Ultrasound examination revealed a isoechoic lesion approximately 3.2 cm × 2.5 cm in size within the right testicle (Fig. 1), with clear boundaries, regular shape, and heterogeneous. Case 2: A 62 year-old Han Chinese male patient was admitted to the hospital due to “progressive enlargement of a left testicular mass over 3 months.” Specialized physical examination revealed normal scrotal skin, significant enlargement of the left testicle, which was hard and nontender, with no abnormalities detected in the right testicle. Laboratory tests showed: HCG < 1.0 mIU/ml (reference range 0.00–10.00). The patient was healthy and had no family history of the disease. Ultrasound examination revealed a isoechoic lesion measuring approximately 3.0 cm × 2.3 cm within the left testicle (Fig. 5), the boundary is not clear, irregular shape, and heterogeneous with calcification. A hypoechoic halo was observed around the lesion, and Color Doppler Flow Imaging (CDFI) detected color flow signals within and around the lesion (Fig. 6). After being discharged following surgery, the two patients did not follow medical advice for subsequent medication or follow-up.

Discussion

These two cases of neuroendocrine tumors (prepubertal type) occurring in the testis present with clear boundaries, regular morphology, and heterogeneous internal echoes similar to those of the testis. Color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) shows a small amount of blood flow signal within the lesion. Differential diagnoses include: (1) Seminoma: a well-defined hypoechoic solid mass with smooth margins and homogeneous echogenicity in the early stages; in advanced stages, the echogenicity becomes heterogeneous, the morphology irregular, and necrotic cystic echoes may be observed. CDFI shows abundant blood flow signals, appearing as short linear or branching patterns, with a low resistance index (RI < 0.6) [4]. (2) Embryonal carcinoma (yolk sac tumor/endodermal sinus tumor): typically found in infants, children, or young adults, with elevated serum AFP and HCG levels. The tumor presents as a mixed echogenicity mass with clear boundaries but irregular morphology. CDFI reveals rich blood supply within the tumor, with irregular linear blood flow signals [5]. (3) Testicular teratoma: benign teratomas appear as well-defined multilocular cystic nodules (less than 20 mm), with calcifications and cystic anechoic areas. Blood flow signals are observed around the tumor margin and as punctate signals within the tumor. Malignant teratomas present as mixed echogenicity masses with unclear boundaries and irregular morphology, showing coarse calcifications and larger cystic areas, with detectable blood flow signals. They may be accompanied by inguinal lymph node enlargement [6]. (4) Leydig cell tumor: a solitary, round, small nodule with clear boundaries and regular morphology, presenting as a solid hypoechoic mass. CDFI shows rich blood flow signals surrounding the lesion, with a low resistance index (RI < 0.5) [7]. (5) Testicular malignant lymphoma: the affected testis is significantly enlarged, presenting as a homogeneous hypoechoic mass, with some areas showing a reticular or patchy hypoechoic pattern. CDFI shows abundant blood supply within the lesion, with a low resistance index. The distribution of the testicular artery remains normal (testicular lymphoma typically proliferates and infiltrates within the testicular septa, generally not destroying the testicular structure) [8].

Conclusion

Testicular neuroendocrine tumors (prepubertal type) are extremely rare. Initially, they were thought to have a favorable biological behavior, but subsequent studies revealed they might also exhibit poor biological behavior [9] and are often linked to an unfavorable prognosis. Diagnosis and treatment require a multidisciplinary approach, primarily involving tumor resection, with chemotherapy or radiotherapy added if needed. Prognosis depends on tumor differentiation, stage, and metastasis status. Well-differentiated NETs usually have a better prognosis, whereas poorly differentiated NECs have a poorer one. The typical presentation is a testicular mass, possibly with pain or discomfort. Diagnosis is based on clinical history, pathology, and imaging, with an emphasis on excluding metastasis. Radical surgery offers a promising prognosis, and postoperative follow-up is essential [10, 11]. Routine ultrasound is advantageous for early lesion detection and short-term monitoring. When combined with relevant lab tests, it may assist in diagnosis. However, a definitive diagnosis still requires pathological and immunohistochemical results.
