Exploring patient centered communication on health outcomes: Role of trust and online information seeking in Turkish cancer patients receiving chemotherapy
Authors: Ersin Eskiler, Cemil Bilir, Remzi Altunisik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient-centered communication (PCC) is an important concept at the core of patient-centered care. PCC can help physicians understand patients’ needs, provide adequate information to patients, and establish trust between patients and physicians, thus promoting satisfaction with information received and perceived quality of life. This study aimed to examine the mediating role of trust in physicians in the relationship between satisfaction with information received through PCC and perceived quality of life in patients receiving chemotherapy, and the moderating role of online cancer information seeking behavior in the relationship between these variables.MethodsThis study, designed as a cross-sectional design from quantitative research methods, included 467 participants who had received at least four chemotherapy treatments at the Department of Oncology, Faculty of Medicine, Sakarya University. Convenience sampling method was used. Data were collected using a face-to-face survey technique in the chemotherapy healthcare waiting area under the supervision and permission of a physician. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and structural equation modeling.ResultsThe results showed that the relationship between PCC and perceived quality of life was insignificant, and satisfaction with information received through PCC was low in the cancer information seeking group and moderately correlated in the non-seeking group. Additionally, trust in physician mediated the relationship between PCC and quality of life in both groups. Trust in physician mediated the relationship between PCC and satisfaction with information received in the cancer information seeking group, but this effect was found to be insignificant in the other group. Moreover, the proposed model explained 5% of the variance in quality of life and 43% of the variance in satisfaction with information received in the cancer information seeking group. In the other group, the proposed model explained 7% of the variance in quality of life and 26% of the variance in satisfaction with information received.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that effective patient-centered care initiatives in cancer care settings yield beneficial outcomes for patients and online information-seeking behavior contributes to these positive results. Therefore, improving the applicability and reliability of online health information and developing patient-doctor interaction and patient-doctor trust could beneficial for practitioners and researchers in the field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13506-x.Keywords: Cancer patients, Chemotherapy, Information seeking, Life quality, Patient-centered communication, Satisfaction, Trust

Background

As one of the increasingly common and leading causes of death worldwide, cancer is the second most significant cause of death among adults in Turkiye [1]. According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 report, ∼ 18.7 million new cancer cases were diagnosed worldwide, and ∼ 9.7 million cancer deaths (51.6% of incidence) are detected worldwide. In Turkiye, 240,013 new cancer diagnoses and 129,672 cancer related deaths (54% of incidence) were reported in 2022 [2].

Typically, after experiencing cancer symptoms, stages such as consulting a doctor, comprehensive examinations, getting a cancer diagnosis and the disease treatment process (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, etc.) span a long time. Chemotherapy is widely used (65% of patients) in the management of cancer treatment and chosen as the first-line treatment for 25% of patients. However, due to its serious side effects and long-term nature, it places a significant personal, mental, and emotional burden on patients, reducing their quality of life. During this stressful time, acquiring appropriate information is associated with an overall improved quality of life for individuals receiving chemotherapy [3, 4]. During this period, cancer patients who received chemotherapy are frequently in contact with healthcare personnel. This situation increases the burden on healthcare professionals and leads to very few individuals being satisfied with healthcare services [5], and it is frequently emphasized that the quality of services provided to patients needs to be improved [6]. In recent years, patient-centeredness has been one of the promoted approaches to improving healthcare delivery [7, 8].

Patient-centeredness advocates the idea that healthcare providers should provide care that is sensitive to patient’s needs, values, and preferences, and it is stated that the most important mean of patient-centeredness is patient-centered communication (PCC) [5, 7–9]. PCC dates back to 1969 and is based on the belief that each patient should be “understood as a unique person,” encourages communication and shared decision-making between patients and physicians [10]. Nevertheless, the concept has not had sufficient attention and acceptance over the past fifty years [11]. Data from numerous studies since the early 2000s have demonstrated that PCC improves patient health (e.g. by lowering physiological arousal, pain, fear, and anxiety while promoting more positive emotions like hope and optimism) and healthcare outcomes (e.g. increased trust in the healthcare provider, and positive evaluation of the health service quality) [5, 8, 12–15]. These results demonstrate the importance of PCC in enhancing patients’ entire healthcare experience as well as their mental wellbeing. On the other hand, limited number of studies examine hypotheses regarding the association of PCC with trust in physicians, satisfaction with information received, and overall quality of life. Furthermore, for patients undergoing chemotherapy, the relationships among these variables are not well defined, despite their frequent interactions with healthcare providers. While healthcare professionals are the most trusted and widely used source of information for cancer patients, practices for providing information to cancer patients are largely physician-centered and based on standard clinical protocols [16]. In recent years, online channels have been acknowledged for their substantial potential in acquiring health and disease information, including disease nature, course, prognosis, and treatment side effects. Online channels also facilitate supportive communication, enhancing decision-making processes and promoting positive interactions with healthcare professionals. These underscore the increasing significance of online resources in patient engagement and care [13, 17, 18].

Cancer care and PCC

Communication is essential in cancer care, influencing both the procedure and the outcomes. Effective communication has been found to improve health outcomes, especially between patients and healthcare practitioners. While communication is essential throughout the cancer illness experience, it plays a more vital role during the treatment stage. This stage frequently entails complex decision-making, controlling side effects, and meeting patients’ emotional and informational requirements, all of which benefit from clear, empathetic, and effective communication [4]. PCC represents a transition from a doctor-centered model, characterized by the physician setting the agenda and directing patient interactions, to a more patient-centered framework in healthcare service provision. PCC is a process that includes the content and results of interactions between healthcare providers and patients. This approach emphasizes active patient involvement in care, taking into account their preferences, values, and needs, thereby promoting a collaborative and empowering relationship between patients and healthcare providers. This transition seeks to enhance the patient experience and healthcare outcomes [12, 13]. PCC includes exchanging information in decision-making, addressing emotions, managing uncertainty, facilitating patient self-management, and promoting healing relationships [12]. Although there are different models for defining PCC, Saha and Beach emphasize two universal elements. The first is the concept of “mutuality,” which involves power sharing and collaboration between healthcare providers and patients. This is a meaningful way to positively influence patients’ trust in their physicians. The second is the “whole person” orientation of healthcare providers to patient care, where patients’ bio-logical needs are considered, as well as the psychological, social, and behavioral dimensions of health and/or illness [8]. In the healthcare context the objective of PCC is to establish a relationship among patients, physicians, and other healthcare professionals, promoting information exchange and ensuring active patient involvement in healthcare management and decision-making processes [8, 9, 19].

According to research, PCC has a favorable effect on patients in various circumstances. Despite the potential benefits, achieving the appropriate level of PCC is not always possible because of the inadequate communication skills of healthcare workers [10] and time limits during medical consultations [19]. These constraints can impede the effective implementation of PCC, making it difficult for healthcare providers to fully incorporate patient-centered practices into their interactions with patients [13]. Furthermore, the relationship between healthcare providers (especially physicians) and patients is considered one of the most complex interpersonal relationships [20, 21]. Several factors influence the complexity of the relationship between healthcare providers and patients: (i) the interaction between the parties in unequal positions, (ii) the asymmetry of the relationship, (iii) the often involuntary nature of the interactions, (iv) the involvement of life-critical issues, (v) the emotional burden associated with health care decisions, and (vi) the necessity for close collaboration [20]. In particular, getting a cancer diagnosis, researching treatment options, participating in decisions, undergoing treatment, and worrying about recurrence can cause severe emotional distress in cancer patients [10]. The relationship between healthcare providers and patients becomes more complex due to the seriousness of the disease, the contentious nature of providing information to patients in cancer care, the involvement of multiple healthcare professionals (such as different doctors, nurses, etc.), multiple toxic treatment methods (such as oral and intravenous medications, radiation, etc.), long-term outcome observation, uncertainty, frequent visits, and the changing healthcare providers over time [9, 12, 22–24]. These unique elements highlighted by researchers make PCC research important and imperative in cancer care.

Research correlating PCC with patient health status has shown that PCC results in improvements in adherence to treatment recommendations [14, 15, 25], management of chronic diseases [26], disease-related outcomes [27] and overall quality of life [9]. PCC has also been shown to positively affect trust in healthcare providers, quality of patient care, and patient satisfaction [13, 28, 29].

PCC on health outcomes

Epstein and Street introduced a conceptual model that thoroughly assesses the ways in which patients’ overall health is influenced both directly and indirectly by PCC [12]. This model holds significant importance in PCC research, as it conceptually uncovers the fundamental mechanisms by which PCC influences health outcomes both directly and indirectly [13–15, 19, 24]. The model indicates that PCC can have a direct positive impact on health outcomes, including survival, disease-free survival, health-related quality of life, and satisfaction with the information received. Proximal and intermediate results are significant milestones in achieving health outcomes from PCC. The proximal and intermediate outcomes may enhance the role of PCC on health outcomes by acting as mediating and moderating variables [12]. However, the proposed conceptual models and/or pathways regarding the direct and indirect effects of PCC on different positive health outcomes have been empirically tested in a limited number of studies [5, 8, 13–15, 29]. Additionally, the researchers emphasize the importance of further research to empirically test the pathways between PCC and its outcomes [12, 14]. Based on the model proposed by Epstein and Street, the current study focused on the individual’s perceived quality of life and received information satisfaction as a health outcome (outcome variables), and trust in the physician (mediating variable) as a proximate PCC outcome.

The moderating role of online cancer information seeking

On the other hand, due to the elements that make cancer care unique, cancer patients have high information needs (e.g. progress and prognosis of the disease, side effects of treatment, social life, and self-care problems), and it is stated that these needs, which change throughout the treatment, are often not met [17, 30–32]. Cancer patients whose information needs are not met or who are less satisfied with the information received reported relatively higher levels of anxiety, depression, and lower quality of life [30, 31]. Providing adequate, patient-friendly information, PCC determines the type, quality and quantity of information patients can obtain about their disease and treatment [12]. Although research shows that patients generally prefer and use the information they receive from their doctors, the sources used to seek information about the disease are shifting from healthcare professionals to online channels [12, 17, 33]. Patients who actively collect information about their disease and are more communicative tend to receive better quality information from their doctors by asking more relevant and critical questions during the appointment/interview [33]. However, even while internet channels are a good way for patients to get the information they require, they could become frustrated since there isn’t enough information available to them. Confusion can also result from contradicting information from dubious sources and the lack of precise standards for judging the accuracy of information [12, 33]. Therefore, cancer patients require the guidance and support of healthcare professionals [34], which enhances the importance of PCC in cancer care. In other words, patients’ online cancer information-seeking behavior may influence the strength of the relationships among the previously discussed research variables. On the other hand, in the conceptual model proposed by Epstein and Street [12], how information obtained from different sources such as the Internet will affect PCC has been ignored. Specifically, it is anticipated that the mediating effect of trust in the physician between PCC, satisfaction with the information received, and perceived quality of life will be stronger in the group of patients engaging in online information-seeking behavior.

This study aimed to investigate the following among chemotherapy patients: (1) the association of PCC with satisfaction with the information received and perceived quality of life, (2) the mediating role of trust in the physician in the relationship between PCC and both satisfaction with the information received and perceived quality of life, and (3) the influence of online cancer information-seeking behavior on the relationships among these variables. In the following sections, the main variables of the study were defined and the relationships between these variables were explained.

In this context, the proposed hypotheses are as follows:

H1a

In the group that do have online cancer information-seeking, there is a relationship between PCC and life quality.

H1b

In the group that do not have online cancer information-seeking, there is a relationship between PCC and life quality.

H2a

In the group that do have online cancer information-seeking, there is a relationship between PCC and received information satisfaction.

H2b

In the group that do not have online cancer information-seeking, there is a relationship between PCC and received information satisfaction.

H3a

In the group that do have online cancer information-seeking, there is a relationship between PCC and trust in their physician.

H3b

In the group that do not have online cancer information-seeking, there is a relationship between PCC and trust in their physician.

H4a

In the group that do online cancer information-seeking trust in their physician is a mediating effect on the relationship between PCC and life quality.

H4b

In the group that do not have online cancer information-seeking, trust in their physician is a mediating effect on the relationship between PCC and life quality.

H5a

In the group that do online cancer information-seeking trust in their physician is a mediating effect on the relationship between PCC and received information satisfaction.

H5b

In the group that do not have online cancer information-seeking, trust in their physician is a mediating effect on the relationship between PCC and received information satisfaction.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

This study, designed as a cross-sectional design from quantitative research methods. Patients treated at the Department of Oncology, Faculty of Medicine, Sakarya University were included. All oncology patients who received chemotherapy treatment at the Department of Oncology, Faculty of Medicine, Sakarya University between April and November 2021 were determined as the study population. Data were collected from patients who were sampled in the chemotherapy healthcare waiting area using the convenience sampling method, under the supervision and permission of a physician, using a face-to-face survey technique. After introducing themselves, the researchers gave a brief explanation of the research purpose. Informed consent letters were given before the surveys, and participants could withdraw if they felt uncomfortable. Data were collected from patients who received at least four consecutive chemotherapy treatments and gave informed consent to participate in the study. Patients who received three or fewer chemotherapy treatments and did not want to participate in the study were excluded.

In this study, 467 individuals who received at least four occasion chemotherapy treatments at the Department of Oncology, Faculty of Medicine, Sakarya University volunteered to participate. The participants included 48.6% (n = 227) were male and 51.4% (n = 240) were female. The participants were between the ages of 18–85 ( = 56.29 ± 12.56). Participants were asked to focus on their communication practices during their experiences with healthcare professionals. To ensure data quality in the study, participants were asked to confirm their demographic information and clinical status previously collected by the panel (Demographic: Gender, Age, Cancer type, clinical trial). The survey, administered in Turkish, took 8–10 min to complete, and all participants completed the informed consent process. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Institutional Review Board (or Ethics Committee) of Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (protocol code E.6607 and date of approval 22/07/2020) for studies involving humans.

Measurements

Considering the characteristics of the study group, a comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify the most suitable measurement tools to enhance acceptability/participation and ensure the reliable collection of data. Following expert consultations, a questionnaire was developed consisting of two sections and a total of 25 statements, with the selected items listed as Supplementary File 1. In this context, the patients of PCC perceptions were measured with 7 items taken from Epstein et al. (2005) [19] and Xiang et al. (2017) [35] studies, trust in physician was measured with 5 items taken from Anderson & Dedrick (1990) [36] and Peng et al. (2020) [37] studies, satisfaction with the information received was measured with 6 items taken from Thomas et al. (2004) [38] studies, and quality of life was measured with 2 items taken from Ong et al. (2000) [9] study.

Patient-centered communication (PCC)

Participants rated their communication with healthcare professionals (doctors, nurses, and other healthcare providers) encountered during their chemotherapy process using seven statements on a 5-point Likert type scale (5 = Always, …, 1 = Never). This scale included the core dimensions of PCC, such as empathy, responding to patients’ emotions, information exchange, and shared decision-making, as suggested by previous studies [12, 19, 35]. The fulfillment of information needs was measured with two items, emotional needs with two items, and the inclusion of sufficient bidirectional interaction and shared decision-making processes with three items. According to the results of the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), the fit index values were found to be χ2/sd = 2.06, RMSEA = 0.05, GFI = 0.98, AGFI = 0.96, NFI = 0.98, and CFI = 0.99 (factor loadings = 0.80 − 0.65). The scale’s internal consistency coefficient was found to be Cronbach’s alpha = 0.86, composite reliability (CR) = 0.87, and average variance extracted (AVE) = 0.51. The mean of the responses to the seven items was calculated (=3.97±0.50); the high scores obtained by participants from the scale indicate a greater level of PCC rather than provider-centered care.

Trust in their physician

To assess the trust of chemotherapy patients in their physicians, the studies by Anderson & Dedrick (1990) and Peng et al. (2020) were utilized. The scale, consisting of one dimension and five items, employs a 5-point Likert format (5 = Strongly agree, …, 1 = Strongly disagree). According to the results of the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), the fit index values were found to be χ2/sd = 1.89, RMSEA = 0.04, GFI = 0.99, AGFI = 0.97, NFI = 0.99, and CFI = 0.99 (factor loadings = 0.89 − 0.67). The scale’s internal consistency coefficient was found to be Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89, composite reliability (CR) = 0.89, and average variance extracted (AVE) = 0.64. The mean of the responses to the five items was calculated (=4.13±0.47); the high scores obtained by participants from the scale indicated a greater level of trust in their physicians.

Received information satisfaction

The Information Satisfaction Questionnaire (ISQ), created by Thomas et al. (2004) to meet the needs of cancer patients and integrate information into standard practices, was employed. The third section of the ISQ assesses patient satisfaction regarding the information provided in six key categories outlined in the “Bedford Hospital NHS Trust Blue Sky Patient Information Project.” Responses in each category were evaluated using a uniform 5-point scale (0 = Very unsatisfied, …, 4 = Very satisfied). An elevated average score suggests patient satisfaction with the information provided, as indicated by the ISQ overall score recommendations: “0–4 = Very poor, 5–9 = Poor, 10–14 = Fair, 15–19 = Good, 20–24 = Excellent”. The study revealed an average response of =17.57 ± 2.64. This value indicated that patients are generally satisfied with the information provided, suggesting that minor adjustments were necessary (Good – Minor fine-tuning required). The scale’s internal consistency coefficient was found to be Cronbach’s alpha=0.80.

Quality of life

Patients undergoing chemotherapy assessed their quality of life using two questions on a 7-point scale (1 = Very poor, …, 7 = Excellent). The question “How would you rate your overall physical condition during the past week?” has been suggested in previous studies. The question statement, “How would you rate your overall quality of life during the past week?” was utilized [9, 39]. The mean of the responses was calculated as =4.50 ± 1.49. A high average score indicated an elevated perception of quality of life among patients undergoing chemotherapy.

Online cancer information seeking

Online cancer information-seeking behavior was measured with the question “Have you ever searched for health or medical information online regarding your current illness?” measured dichotomously as (1) yes and (0) no. This item was used to divide participants into two groups based on their engagement/non-engagement in online cancer information-seeking behavior and served as a moderator variable in the model. To this question, 49% (n = 229) of the participants answered yes, and 51% (n = 238) answered no.

Statistical methods

The research hypotheses were tested using structural equation modeling (SEM) in AMOS v. 24. The descriptive statistics were evaluated using the SPSS v. 24 software. Before the analyses, whether the data met the normality assumption was examined by taking into account the criterion that the skewness and kurtosis values were within ± 2 as suggested by George and Mallery (2016). In addition, multivariate normality, was assessed using kurtosis and critical ratio. The Kurtosis value of less than five, a critical ratio between − 1.96 and + 1.96 was declared to test the presence of multivariate normality. The assumption of multivariate normality was not fulfilled. Therefore, the bootstrapping technique was to manage multivariate non-normality. Therefore, Maximum likelihood estimation with bootstrapping was used in this study an increasingly popular and promising approach in several contexts, and this resampling method can be used to correct fit and standard errors for non-normality in SEM.

The reliability and validity of the multi-item scales for PCC and trust in their physician were evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The maximum likelihood (ML) estimation method was used to estimate the measurement model. The study employed structural equation modeling (SEM) for hypothesis testing. In the model, linear relationships between endogenous and exogenous variables were assumed and the linear regression model was used. In evaluating model fit; chi-square to the degrees of freedom ratio (χ2/sd ≤ 3), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMESA ≤ 0.05), Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI ≥ 0.95), Normed Fit Index (NFI ≥ 0.95), Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.95), Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index (AGFI ≥ 0.90) values were used [40–42]. After CFA was completed, Cronbach’s alpha (≥ 0.70), average variance extracted (AVE ≥ 0.50), and composite reliability (CR ≥ 0.70) values were examined to test the reliability of each construct [41]. To test a structural model, squared multiple correlations (R2), and path coefficient were estimated to measure the relationship between variables, as well as a 95% confidence interval, and a p-value < 0.05 was applied to determine statistical significance. Finally, multi-group analysis was performed through critical ratios [43].

Characteristics of patients

The predictive power of the tested model was evaluated by squared multiple correlations (R2). As shown in Table 3; Fig. 1, the proposed model explained 5% (R2 = 0.054) of the variance in quality of life and 43% (R2 = 0.425) of the variance in satisfaction with the information received in the cancer information-seeking group. In the other group, the proposed model explained 7% (R2 = 0.074) of the variance in quality of life and 26% (R2 = 0.260) of the variance in satisfaction with the information received.

The proposed hypotheses of the relationship of both, PCC and trust in their physician and PCC and satisfaction with information received were supported by the collected data in both groups. However, the relationship between PCC and quality of life in both groups did not support the hypothesis. On the other hand, trust in their physician mediated the relationship between PCC and quality of life in both groups. It was found that trust in their physician mediated the relationship between PCC and satisfaction with information received in the cancer information-seeking group. However, the mediation effect of trust in their physician was not significant in the other group (Table 4).

There was no directly significant relationship (B = 0.409, β = 0.124; B = 0.228, β = 0.085; p > .05) between PCC and quality of life and therefore our findings did not support H1a and H1b (Tables 3 and 4; Fig. 1). As predicted in H2a and H2b, the relationship between PCC and satisfaction with the information received was found to be statistically significant in both groups (B = 0.223, β = 0.218, p < .05; B = 0.351, β = 0.466; p < .01) (Tables 3 and 4; Fig. 1). Similarly, the relationship between PCC and trust in the physician was also statistically significant in both groups (B = 0.861, β = 0.720; B = 0.374; β = 0.562; p < .001). These results supported the proposed hypotheses H3a and H3b (Tables 3 and 4; Fig. 1).

Analysis of the mediating effect of trust in the physician revealed that the indirect effect of PCC on quality of life was statistically significant in both groups (B = 0.726, β = 0.220, p < .01; B = 0.326, β = 0.121, p < .05), suggesting that trust in the physician serves as a mediator in the relationship between these variables. The findings supported the proposed hypotheses H4a and H4b (Tables 3 and 4; Fig. 1). The analysis of the group seeking online cancer health information revealed a statistically significant indirect effect of PCC on satisfaction with the information received (B = 0.351, β = 0.343, p < .001), indicating that trust in the physician mediated the relationship between these variables. Conversely, in the alternative group, the indirect effect of PCC on satisfaction with the information received was determined to be statistically non-significant (B = 0.031, β = 0.041, p > .05). Consequently, the proposed hypothesis H5a was accepted, whereas hypothesis H5b was rejected (Tables 3 and 4; Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study focused on cancer patients receiving chemotherapy offers empirical support for and elaborates on the conceptual model put forth by Epstein and Street (2007). Our results demonstrated a statistically significant positive correlation between PCC and patients’ trust in their physician, satisfaction with the information received, and quality of life. The findings aligned with results from prior studies in the healthcare sector [9, 13, 27, 28]. The role of online information-seeking behavior was evaluated for cancer patients receiving chemotherapy. Furthermore, online information-seeking behavior indicated a moderating effect between PCC and trust in physicians and trust in physicians and receiving information satisfaction of patients.

This study was the inaugural empirical examination of the moderating influence of online cancer information-seeking behavior on the relationship between PCC and outcomes. The direct effects of PCC on quality of life, satisfaction with the information received, and trust in the physician were analyzed individually for each group. Initially, it was noted that no significant relationship existed between PCC and quality of life in the cohort that participated in online cancer information-seeking behavior. A significant positive relationship between PCC and quality of life was observed in the group that did not participate in online cancer information-seeking behavior. Our finding were partially consistent with prior studies that demonstrate a significant association between PCC and quality of life [44, 45]. However, when the moderating variable (trust in the physician) was added to the model, there was no significant relationship in both groups between PCC and quality of life.

Furthermore, a positive correlation was identified between PCC and satisfaction with the information received in both groups, and we accepted hypotheses H2a and H2b. Yancey (2013) found that patient satisfaction scores are significantly higher in hospitals offering PCC services compared to those lacking such services. Epstein and Street (2007) state that effective patient-centered care practices could have a positive impact on patient satisfaction. The present study corroborates pertinent research findings and author perspectives. Thirdly, the examination of the relationship between PCC and trust in physicians revealed a positive correlation between the variables in both groups, and we accepted hypotheses H3a and H3b. These results were consistent with the results of previous experimental [29] and cross-sectional [13, 37] studies.

According to the results, trust in the physician mediated the relationship between PCC and quality of life, and the indirect effect of PCC on quality of life was statistically significant in both groups. Hypotheses H4a and H4b were accepted. The findings provide empirical evidence for the role of trust in healthcare providers as a mediator in the relationship between PCC and quality of life as a health-related outcome variable, as theoretically proposed by researchers [12, 14]. It was consistent with the research reports of Hong and Oh (2020), which found a mediating effect of trust in the physician in the relationship between PCC and patients’ evaluations of the quality of healthcare. Additionally, the mediation effect was notably significant among individuals who participated in online cancer information search behavior, indicating that these participants reported a higher quality of life. This finding corroborated previous studies indicating that cancer patients with unmet information needs experience a diminished quality of life [30, 31]. The study found that trust in the physician mediated the relationship between PCC and satisfaction with the information received among individuals engaging in online cancer information search behavior. The indirect effect of PCC on satisfaction with the information received was statistically significant, supporting hypothesis H5a. Conversely, it was found that the indirect effect of PCC on satisfaction with the information received was statistically non-significant in the group that did not engage in online cancer information-seeking behavior (hypothesis H5b was rejected). In other words, participants who engaged in online cancer information-seeking behavior were more likely to trust their physician and be satisfied with the information received.

Online information-seeking behavior improves the level of trust in the physician and strengthens the role of trust on health outcomes (perceived health-related quality of life and satisfaction with the information received). This is logical because patients tend to turn to the internet to meet their information needs before and during cancer consultations [17, 33]. The results obtained also expand the theory of value co-creation. In the theory of value co-creation, value is the realization of common interests by the actors in the service process (health care providers and patients) carrying out the resource integration process together [46].

Patient value co-creation behavior encompasses the processes of online information acquisition, personal interactions with offline service providers, information sharing, and collaboration [37, 46]. This study presents significant evidence regarding the enhancement of medical knowledge about diseases through online information seeking, the recognition of value co-creation with offline service providers in relation to the acquired information, and its impact on health outcomes. For instance, alignment between online information and that provided by offline healthcare professionals, or the alleviation of concerns through PCC-based communication addressing distressing online information, may lead patients to exhibit increased trust. In fact, PCC is seen as a moral philosophy that meets patients’ needs, perspectives, and expectations, helping them feel understood and allowing them to understand their illness and/or participate in decisions affecting their health [19]. In this context, it is expected that the feeling of trust resulting from the information obtained from the internet and an effective PCC will positively affect health outcomes [14, 27]. Healthcare providers can assist patients in understanding, interpreting, and evaluating information acquired from various sources [12, 47]. For this reason, improving and developing patient-doctor interaction and patient-doctor trust is important. In particular, strong healthcare provider-patient relationships based on trust can allow patients to be directed to accurate and reliable online information sources. Furthermore, it is important to improve the applicability and reliability of online health information, combined with the physician’s guidance and correction of information from other sources, to enable patients to use online health information effectively [37].

Implications and limitations

The current study’s findings provided empirical evidence that effective PCC in cancer care settings yields positive outcomes for patients, and that online information-seeking behavior increases these effects. This study, while contributing theoretically and practically, had certain limitations. The current study included patients receiving chemotherapy at the Department of Oncology at Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine. To reach a larger number of patients, comprehensive studies involving patients treated in different oncology departments could be conducted. The characteristics of patients aligned with the cancer incidence rates and demographic features observed in the region and Turkey. Future research must consider participant diversity and various treatment modalities (e.g., oral treatment, radiotherapy) as repeated experiences of PCC may enhance trust in healthcare providers over time [13]. Conversely, while the most suitable data collection tools were identified according to the sample characteristics (such as intravenous therapy), limitations exist in the conceptualization and functionalization of the measurement items. Single items were employed to evaluate quality of life and online information-seeking behavior. Single-item measurements are deemed valid and reliable for general constructs that lack ambiguity, such as quality of life and online information-seeking behavior [48]. Therefore, the findings of the current study should be interpreted with this consideration, and future research should incorporate multi-item measurement tools. The validity of the model proposed in the study was tested on the general sample; however, alternative model scenarios and different subgroup comparisons did not perform. This situation limits the interpretation of whether the model shows the same validity in different contexts or sample subgroups. Future studies are encouraged to examine alternative model structures and conduct subgroup analyses (e.g., by gender, age, or CA type) to test the robustness and generalizability of the proposed model across diverse contexts.

This study evaluated the impact of PCC on various outcome variables within the framework of online information-seeking behavior. However, additional factors related to the conceptual model proposed by Epstein and Street (2007) warrant empirical testing. The findings of the current study, characterized by their cross-sectional nature, may be validated by experimental studies. The factors influencing patients’ willingness or reluctance to seek health information online could be further investigated. Understanding the utilization of online resources and the rationale behind the selection of particular activities can aid healthcare providers in enhancing the management and optimization of patient care and satisfaction. This information is essential for formulating effective strategies in healthcare management. Future studies should explore additional factors, alternative theoretical frameworks, and varied research methodologies to enhance the current study model [47].

Conclusions

This study emphasizes the importance of patient-doctor interaction and the enhancement of patient-doctor trust. The interaction of these variables with online information seeking was assessed for cancer patients receiving chemotherapy. Our findings suggest that online information-seeking behavior provides effective PCC in cancer care settings and positive outcomes for patients. By supporting patients’ access to health information online, healthcare providers can encourage patients to engage actively in communication with their physicians, thereby enhancing the trust between healthcare providers and patients. This increase in trust is likely to positively impact patients’ health-related quality of life and their satisfaction with the information received. In strengthening the PCC, possible positive and negative results of adopting online communication tools for both healthcare providers and cancer patients receiving chemotherapy should be identified in future studies.
