Rapid CO2 effects on protein‐related metabolism during photosynthetic gas exchange
Authors: Corentin Dourmap, Cyril Abadie, Marlène Davanture, Willy Bienvenut, Julie Lalande, Guillaume Tcherkez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYIt is common practice to vary atmospheric CO2 mole fraction (C a) during gas exchange experiments to manipulate photorespiration and explore photosynthetic properties such as maximum carboxylation velocity. In doing so, it is assumed that changing C a essentially alters the carboxylation‐to‐oxygenation ratio, with negligible effects on other metabolic pathways. There is now substantial evidence that this assumption is incorrect, with strong modifications in day respiratory metabolism, N and S assimilation and cytosolic translational activity. In principle, these modifications should reconfigure protein content and composition which might in turn affect photosynthetic activity. To address this point, we carried out gas exchange experiments on sunflower leaves under varying CO2, either using a portable system or a special leaf chamber designed for 13C‐labelling. Non‐targeted analysis of metabolites, proteins and isotopic features was performed to look at metabolites, protein synthesis rate and proteins affected by CO2 mole fraction within 1 to 3 h. Proteogenic amino acids and amino acid derivatives were amongst the most affected metabolites. Protein synthesis rate increased with C a, except at very high CO2. Accordingly, proteins affected by C a included proteins involved in translation (ribosomal proteins, initiation factors) and proteases, showing a change in protein turn‐over. Also, C a impacted some proteins of the photosynthetic machinery. Taken as a whole, our results demonstrate that CO2 mole fraction has broad short‐term effects on leaf protein‐related metabolism. In principle, this effect should have consequences on several aspects of photosynthesis, such as day respiration or photosynthetic capacity itself.

INTRODUCTION

Leaf net photosynthesis is the result of CO2 fixation by carboxylation and CO2 production by photorespiration and day respiration (i.e. non‐photorespiratory CO2 production in the light). Photosynthetic response curves to CO2 mole fraction are carried out routinely to get estimates of important photosynthetic parameters, such as maximum carboxylation velocity (V cmax) (von Caemmerer, 2013). It is usually assumed that when CO2 mole fraction is varied in A/c i or A/c c curves, oxygenation and carboxylation rates change (along with stomatal conductance and possibly internal conductance for CO2) while day respiration does not vary. This assumption implies that non‐photosynthetic (and non‐photorespiratory) metabolism is impacted minimally by CO2 in the short term, so that CO2 liberation does not change significantly during gas exchange experiments like an A/c i curve.

There is now substantial evidence that this assumption is problematic (Tcherkez & Limami, 2019). In effect, it has been shown that nitrogen and sulphur assimilation increases rapidly (with 1–2 h) when photorespiration increases (at low CO2), along with changes in various pathways including amino acid production, and this in turn can impact CO2 generation (Abadie et al., 2017; Abadie et al., 2018; Abadie & Tcherkez, 2019b; Bloom et al., 2010; Bloom et al., 2014; Rachmilevitch et al., 2004). Metabolomics kinetics experiments have also shown that within 30 min, many metabolic pathways are affected by CO2 (and O2) mole fraction, in particular amino acid synthesis (Abadie, Boex‐Fontvieille, et al., 2016).

As a result, an important process that may be affected by photosynthesis is protein production. This can have important implications since protein synthesis is associated with an ATP cost that should, in principle, drive respiratory CO2 production (Amthor, 2000; Bathe et al., 2023). Furthermore, amino acid biosynthesis per se may be associated with CO2 production (for example branched‐chain and aromatic amino acid biosynthetic pathways liberate CO2). At the leaf scale, protein turn‐over is believed to represent an energy consumption between 16 and 61% of total respiration (Bouma, 2005). It has also been proposed previously that protein synthesis in illuminated leaves represents an ATP budget of about 10% of that generated by day respiration (Tcherkez et al., 2020). Polysome formation has been shown to be activated by light (compared to darkness) in bean leaves, independently of transcriptional activity (assessed using the inhibitor cordycepin) (Smith, 1976; Smith et al., 1977). Also, protein translation activity and polysome abundance in Arabidopsis rosettes has been shown to be higher in the light compared with darkness (Ishihara et al., 2015; Seaton et al., 2018). Using different gas exchange conditions in the light (with varied CO2 and O2 mole fraction), it has been shown that the phosphorylation of selected translation initiation factors changed, with an increase in ribosomal protein S6 (RPS6) under conditions that favoured photosynthesis, as opposed to photorespiration (Boex‐Fontvieille, Daventure, et al., 2013; Boex‐Fontvieille, Gauthier, et al., 2013). In addition, 13CO2 labelling, polysome quantitation and phosphoproteomics have shown that protein translation initiation was stimulated when photosynthesis was increased using low O2 or high CO2 conditions, as reflected by higher RPS6 (A and B), eIF4B2 and eIF3d phosphorylation and lower eIF4G phosphorylation (Tcherkez et al., 2020). Collectively, these results suggest that protein synthesis is controlled by photosynthetic activity and thus represents a variable fraction of carbon allocation within the leaf, which can affect CO2 production by day respiration in the short term.

Nevertheless, it should be recognised that assessing protein synthesis, including using isotopic labelling, is not easy (for a specific discussion of this issue, see (Davies, 1981; Davies, 1982)). In fact, the 13C content found in leaf proteins after carbon‐13 labelling (i) depends on the isotope enrichment in source amino acids, (ii) is the net result of protein production and protein degradation (turn‐over) and (iii) is an average—while all proteins are not turned over at the same rate. 15N labelling and isotope‐assisted proteomics have demonstrated a broad range of protein turn‐over times, from hours to days (Li et al., 2017).

Limitation (i) is potentially important to take into account since the turn‐over of amino acids varies greatly and depends on photosynthetic conditions. Using a double isotopic (3H, 14C) technique, important differences amongst amino acids have been found in recycling and incorporation into proteins in Lemna fronds (Davies & Humphrey, 1978). It has been demonstrated that alanine and aspartate can be labelled easily with 13CO2 in the light (due to the action of glycolysis and phoshoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) activity, respectively), while other amino acids are less labelled due to the remobilisation of reserves (e.g. glutamate) or the contribution of several precursors with contrasted 13C‐enrichment (e.g. phenylalanine) (Abadie et al., 2017; Abadie et al., 2018). Also, the isotope enrichment strongly depends on the carboxylation‐to‐oxygenation ratio (photorespiration activity) and thus on CO2 mole fraction. For example, methionine is more actively synthesised at low CO2 due to the higher C1 metabolism activity driven by photorespiration, leading to changes in both whole‐molecular average and positional 13C contents (Abadie & Tcherkez, 2019b).

Limitation (ii) is related to the control of protein degradation when photosynthesis varies. Up to now, very little is known about short‐term changes in leaf protein degradation driven by CO2 mole fraction. Our previous work has shown an increase in aspartic protease abundance in Arabidopsis rosettes subjected to low CO2 for 4 h, as opposed to the increase in ribosomal proteins RPS8a and RPL15a at high CO2 (Tcherkez et al., 2020). In soybean, long‐term exposure to CO2 leads to a concerted increase in four proteases (aspartic protease family, ubiquitin‐specific protein 16, subtilisin‐like protein C1 and proteasome subunit α type 3) and ribosomal proteins (Ainsworth et al., 2006). Several proteases have been shown to be important for photosynthesis (either maintenance of the photosynthetic machinery or homeostasis of PSI and PSII activity) under high light, high temperature or drought, such as the FtsH metalloprotease, Deg and Clp proteases, and leucine aminopeptidase (Butenko et al., 2018; Ford et al., 2011; Kato & Sakamoto, 2018; Nishimura et al., 2016; Sharma & Gayen, 2021; Sjögren et al., 2006). Also, a papain‐like cysteine protease has been shown to be required to control chloroplastic gene expression (such as RbcS, which encodes for the small subunit of ribulose 1,5‐bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) and thus control photosynthesis (Alomrani et al., 2021). However, how these proteases vary with short term CO2 change is unknown.

Limitation (iii) can be addressed via the specific analysis of proteins taken separately. Whenever isotopic analysis of peptides is possible along with a kinetic (pulse chase) experiment, this allows one to determine the specific turn‐over of individual proteins. A recent analysis has been conducted to examine the effects of high light in Arabidopsis, using 13CO2 labelling followed by the analysis of 13C‐enrichment in specific peptides, demonstrating a significant increase in protein turn‐over rate for enzymes of the Calvin cycle, nitrite reductase, some enzymes of glycolysis and redox metabolism (Li et al., 2022). But to our knowledge, this has never been done under varying photosynthetic conditions (varying CO2 mole fraction). Shotgun proteomics carried out under various CO2/O2 conditions in Arabidopsis rosettes have shown some short‐term changes in protein abundance, such as histidinol dehydrogenase or tryptophan synthase that increased at low CO2 (Abadie, Mainguet, et al., 2016). Interestingly, proteins involved in photosynthesis itself increased at high CO2, such as photosystem II subunit s (NPQ4), light harvesting complex subunit LHCB4.2, glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase or ADP‐glucose pyrophosphorylase while proteins involved in photorespiration appeared to be more abundant at low CO2 such as catalase 3 or amino transferases (Abadie, Mainguet, et al., 2016). This suggests that adjustments in protein pools via the control of protein synthesis could play a role in short‐term acclimation of photosynthetic metabolism to CO2 condition.

As an aid in overcoming these three limitations and providing a broader view of short‐term CO2 effects, we carried out experiments with sunflower leaves to monitor changes in protein and amino acid metabolism under varying CO2 mole fraction, for very low CO2 (5 μmol mol−1) to very high CO2 (1200 μmol mol−1). Sunflower was used as a model species here because it has been used previously to characterise 13C allocation within leaves (Abadie et al., 2018), and leaf protein synthesis and remobilisation is critical for yield and resilience to environmental cues (Agüera & de la Haba, 2021). We used two experiments (illustrated in Figure S1): in the first experiment (referred to as ‘response curve’), we used a gas exchange system with its own leaf cuvette (6 cm2) and assessed metabolic changes using untargeted metabolomics. In the second experiment (referred to as ‘labelling’), we coupled the gas exchange system to a big external chamber (100 cm2) used for 13CO2 labelling, allowing multiple analyses (13C‐NMR, non‐targeted metabolomics and proteomics). We quantified both protein and amino acid enrichment after isotopic labelling to calculate the net protein synthesis rate. Untargeted metabolomics and shotgun proteomics were used to assess changes in specific metabolites (in particular of amino acid synthesis and degradation pathways) and proteins involved in protein synthesis and turn‐over, including ribosomal proteins and proteases.

RESULTS

Photosynthesis and protein synthesis

As expected, there was an increase in net photosynthesis as CO2 mole fraction increased, covering both Rubisco‐limited and ribulose 1,5‐phosphate (RuBP) regeneration‐limited domains (Figure 1). The two series of experiments differed in maximum carboxylation velocity and apparent carboxylation efficiency (slope A/c i at low c i ) showing different leaf properties, likely including lower internal (mesophyll) conductance in the ‘response curve’ experiment. The difference in the photosynthesis response to CO2 was also probably due to environmental conditions in the leaf cuvette, in particular higher relative humidity in the ‘labelling’ experiment (near 90%, while it was about 70% in the ‘response curve’).

The isotope composition of total leaf proteins was measured after labelling and shown in Figure 2a as a function of %N (which is a relative index of protein content in leaf organic matter). There was an increase in δ13C value with CO2 mole fraction, except at very high CO2, where the δ13C value was similar to that at 400 μmol mol−1 CO2. There was a progressive increase in 13C content in amino acid as reflected by higher 13C‐NMR signal (Figure S2a) and also an increase in 13C‐13C interactions, generating NMR multiplets (Figure S2b). Targeted NMR signals were then used to compute 13C mole fraction (%13C) in sugars and amino acids. There was a progressive increase in %13C with photosynthesis, in particular in soluble sugars (sucrose, glucose, fructose) where it reached 93% 13C under 1200 μmol mol−1 CO2 (Figure S2c). Similar maximal %13C was observed in several amino acids, with the exception of glutamate, which had maximal %13C near 60%. When all NMR‐resolved amino acids were averaged, the isotopic enrichment in amino acids reached about 85% at high CO2.

The value of average amino acid isotopic enrichment (source carbon for protein synthesis) could then be used to calculate the proportion of ‘new’ proteins and thus protein synthesis rate (Figure 2bc). To do so, it was necessary to have an estimate of the absolute content of leaf proteins. Two estimates were used here: Protein content estimated via leaf %N, or via tryptophan fluorescence assay (WF). There was no significant change in total protein content with CO2 mole fraction (Figure 2c). Regardless of the method used for protein content estimation, there was an increase in protein net synthesis rate with CO2 mole fraction, except at very high CO2 (Figure 2b), with an average rate of about 0.3 μmol C m−2 s−1, meaning 7.8 μg m−2 s−1. Protein synthesis rate was about 15 times higher at high CO2 (800 μmol mol−1) compared with low CO2 (40 μmol mol−1).

13C‐NMR features

NMR spectra were analysed using a non‐targeted method via the decomposition into 200 features (buckets, i.e. chemical shift windows). The dataset was used to compute the %13C in each bucket (ratio of intensity after 13C labelling to that without labelling) and pool size (intensity without labelling thus reflecting only 13C natural abundance). Multivariate analysis was combined to univariate analysis in a volcano plot, where the significance limit shown was the Benjamini–Hochberg corrected threshold of P = 0.05 (Figure 3). Most significant features (%13C) corresponded to various C‐atom positions, including from sugars (red dots) and amino acids (green dots) (Figure 3a), which increased with CO2, in agreement with Figure S2c. Interestingly, one of the most significant feature was associated with the C‐2 atom of serine, which was higher at high CO2, unlike the C‐1 atom which formed a common signal with the C‐1 atom of glycine. This discrepancy simply comes from the fact that serine is not only a photorespiratory intermediate but also a product of the cytosolic glycerate pathway. Pool sizes that increased with CO2 were mostly associated with sugars, but also glycerate, also suggesting the involvement of the glycerate pathway for serine synthesis when photosynthesis increased (Figure 3b). This effect is consistent with the duality of serine production in leaves, by both photorespiration at low CO2 and alternative pathways (glycerate pathways, phosphoserine), mostly at high CO2 (Gashu et al., 2025; Jardine et al., 2024).

Non‐targeted GC–MS features

Exact mass GC–MS analysis generated thousands of metabolic features (exact mass, retention time), which are presented in Figure 4 as a volcano plot combining multivariate and univariate statistics. To minimise the false discovery rate, significance limit in the statistical analysis was set at the Bonferroni threshold. For the ‘labelling’ experiment, a relatively small number of features were significant, simply because of the interaction effect with labelling (i.e. some metabolites were labelled with 13CO2 and thus their monoisotopic (non‐labelled) form disappeared) (Figure 4a). Amongst these was sucrose, threonate, glycerate and cycloserine (common derivative of serine in GC–MS analysis) thus confirming 13C‐NMR analysis (Figure 3). As expected, for the ‘response curve’ experiment, much more significant metabolites were found, because there was no interaction with labelling (Figure 4b). Metabolites more abundant when CO2 mole fraction increased included sugars and derivatives of the pentose phosphates pathway (red frames), many amino acids (including tryptophan degradation products: hydroxytryptophan, kynureamine, aspartyl‐decarboxytryptophan, cyanoindole; metabolites of the lysine pathway: oxoadipate, lysine and pipecolate; serine) and their associated derivatives (green frame), some lipids, phenylpropanoids and organic acids. Photorespiratory intermediates (including the methylated derivative of glycine) were more abundant at low CO2 mole fraction, along with some lipids, organic acids (including two oxo‐acids), tryptophan and ornithine. Taken as a whole, there was a broad metabolic reconfiguration, in particular for amino acids biosynthesis and degradation, such that lysine and serine were more accumulated when CO2 increased while tryptophan accumulated at low CO2.

As expected, 13C‐labelled isotopic species of major products (sucrose, fructose, serine, etc.) exhibited an interaction (labelling × CO2) effect, with an increasing abundance as 13CO2 mole fraction increased and a very low content under CO2 at natural abundance (Figure S3a). However, Figure 4 and Figure S3a show metabolites associated with a quantitative statistical analysis, that is with CO2 mole fraction as a quantitative response variable in the multiple linear regression (Figure 4a, Figure 3a) or the simple linear regression (Figure 4b). Therefore, metabolites that did not respond monotonously (either increasing or decreasing) with CO2 did not appear as being significant. We therefore conducted an analysis of variance (one‐ or two‐way ANOVA) to look at metabolites affected by a specific CO2 condition (i.e. using CO2 as a qualitative response variable; Figures S3 and S4).

In the ‘labelling’ experiment, metabolites associated with CO2‐specific variations including amino acids (such as threonine and leucine) and amino acid‐related metabolites (deaminohistidinol, urocanate, methyl‐valerate, amino‐oxobutanoate, homoserine lactone) were significant (Figure S3b), suggesting a specific regulation of amino acid synthesis and degradation when photosynthesis increased with CO2 mole fraction. Interestingly, when the four significant metabolites of the branched‐chain amino acid pathway were plotted together (Figure S3c), there was a strong positive relationship between leucine and methyl‐valerate while pantothenate (derivative of the valine biosynthetic pathway) and threonine (isoleucine precursor) fell abruptly when the relative leucine content was above 1‰, suggesting a balance between branched‐chain amino acids, promoting leucine when CO2 increased. Also, urocanate (histidine degradation product) fell abruptly when diamino‐histidinol (histidine precursor derivative) was above a certain threshold (2‰ relative content).

In the ‘response curve’ experiment, many metabolites were associated with a significant CO2 effect using the ANOVA (Figure S4), forming three clusters in the hierarchical clustering: sugar and several amino acid derivatives (dopamine, pipecolate, etc.) that increased with CO2 mole fraction (cluster 1), various compounds (including nitrogenous compounds and shikimate, the precursor of aromatic amino acids) with a maximum abundance at moderate CO2 (cluster 2), and photorespiratory intermediates (glyoxylate, glycine), malate, tryptophan and compounds of the raffinose family that were more abundant at low or very low CO2 (cluster 3).

In summary, there was a complex regulation of amino acid synthesis and degradation pathways when photosynthesis varied with CO2 mole fraction, some amino acids being more abundant at low CO2 while others are more abundant at high CO2 (Figure 4; Figures S3 and S4). Importantly, the CO2 dependence did not only concern photorespiratory‐related amino acids (like glycine) since lysine, leucine, tryptophan, threonine, histidine or their derivatives were amongst significant features. Furthermore, serine was unrelated to glycine, suggesting the onset of the cytosolic glycerate pathway at high photosynthesis (Figure 4).

Proteomics

Proteomics analyses were carried out with samples associated with the ‘labelling’ experiment (sample size was insufficient for proteomics in the ‘response curve’ experiment). Unfortunately, despite the 13C labelling found in total proteins (Figure 1), it was not possible to resolve the 13C enrichment in individual proteins using the isotopic pattern of peptides in the proteomics analysis (as exemplified by Figure 3 in (Li et al., 2022)). In fact, there was a broad isotopologue species distribution (from the 13C1 species to 13Cn, n >> 1) with most signals in the background noise (data not shown). Therefore, the results described here are limited to the shotgun analysis of non‐labelled samples (experiments with CO2 at natural abundance) (Figure 5a). The dataset comprised 1668 proteins that were analysed with univariate and multivariate statistics (as in Figure 4), yielding 217 proteins significantly affected by CO2 (accounting for the Benjamini–‐Hochberg correction for the false discovery rate) (listed in the File S1). CO2 mole fraction had an impact on various proteins. Several photosynthesis‐related proteins were affected by CO2. In particular, ferredoxin NADP oxidoreductase (FNR2) was more abundant at high CO2 while carbonic anhydrase was more abundant at low CO2 (green dots, Figure 5a). Interestingly, a high proportion (45%) of proteins affected by CO2 mole fraction were associated with protein turn‐over: Many ribosomal proteins were more abundant at high CO2 (red dots) while several proteases, chaperones and proteins of maturation and folding tended to be more abundant at low CO2 (blue and purple dots). Differentially abundant proteases included two aspartyl proteases (including aspartic protease 1) and three proteasome subunits.

Translation‐related proteins were further examined using a heatmap representation with hierarchical clustering (Figure 5b). 19 ribosomal proteins were more abundant as CO2 increased—but not at very high CO2 (cluster 3). Also, a translation elongation factor, eEF1α, was more abundant as CO2 increased. At very high CO2 (1200 μmol mol−1), there was the specific accumulation of two ribosomal proteins (RP30S and RPS2) and the translation initiation factor eIF2β (cluster 2). At low CO2, three ribosomal proteins were more abundant, along with eIF3 subunits (cluster 1).

Like for metabolites (see above), we conducted an analysis of variance (ANOVA) to look at proteins affected by a specific CO2 condition (i.e. using CO2 as a qualitative response variable; Figures S5 and S6) rather than a quantitative response to CO2. 14 proteins passed the Bonferroni threshold (Figure S5), including 5 ribosomal proteins, a protein involved in the photosynthetic electron transfer chain (ferredoxin) and in photorespiration (phospho‐glycolate phosphatase). These five ribosomal proteins were more abundant when photosynthesis increased, except at very high CO2. Using a less stringent false discovery rate correction (Benjamini–Hochberg), many proteins were significant (Figure S6), and they formed three clusters: Proteins generally more abundant at low CO2 with some variations at moderate and high CO2 (including subunits of eIF3E; clusters 1 and 3), and proteins that were more abundant at moderate and high CO2 (cluster 2, including many ribosomal proteins).

Relationship between protein synthesis and ribosomal protein abundance

When the protein synthesis rate (Figure 2) was plotted against relative ribosomal protein abundance (average across ribosomal proteins positively related to CO2; cluster 3 of Figure 5), there was a linear relationship (Figure 6). It suggests that in addition to phosphorylation events that are well‐known to control translation, the content in ribosomal protein might have played a role to sustain protein synthesis when photosynthesis increased. It is worth noting that at very high CO2 (1200 μmol mol−1), there was a decline in both protein synthesis and in ribosomal protein abundance (Figures 2 and 5), but still, datapoints associated with this condition fell on the same relationship as other CO2 conditions (dark red and dark grey dots).

DISCUSSION

Protein turn‐over is controlled by CO2 in illuminated leaves

Our results show that in the short term (within 3 h), CO2 mole fraction influenced protein synthesis and therefore the abundance of several proteins (Figures 2b and 5a). Unsurprisingly, since the CO2 treatment was deliberately short (we examined short‐term effects here), total leaf protein content was not significantly modified (Figure 2c) and only specific proteins were increased or decreased. Amongst the most impacted proteins were ribosomal proteins, the content of significantly increased ribosomal proteins showing a ≈9‐fold increase (Figures 5 and 6). The results obtained here on sunflower mature leaves are consistent with previous observations in Arabidopsis rosettes kept for 4 h under given CO2 conditions, where 13C utilisation to synthesise proteins increased by about 50% at 1000 μmol mol−1 CO2 compared with 100 μmol mol−1 CO2 (Tcherkez et al., 2020). Here, protein synthesis was nearly doubled at 800 μmol mol−1 CO2 compared with 120 μmol mol−1. Our present estimates are numerically different since we measured the isotope enrichment in amino acids, allowing the calculation of the true net protein synthesis (Figure S2). It is worth noting that the carbon allocation to protein synthesis (in % of net assimilation) increased from 1.1 under ambient to 1.8% under elevated (800 μmol mol−1) CO2 and thus remained within the same order of magnitude (compare Figures 1 and 2b), in line with previous values in Arabidopsis (Tcherkez et al., 2020).

Our observations show a relationship between photosynthesis, net protein synthesis rate monitored by 13C labelling and ribosome abundance (Figures 2 and 6). At first glance, this suggests a simple carbon supply effect on protein production. However, we found here a strong depressing effect of very high CO2 (1200 μmol mol−1) such that protein synthesis had a rate like that under ambient CO2. It suggests that CO2 had also a signalling effect at high concentration that inhibited the translation machinery, as reflected by the ribosomal protein content (Figure 6). The examination of the effect of growth under elevated CO2 on the transcriptome and translatome in Solanum species has also suggested that high CO2 mole fraction does not only act via the carbon supply (increased carbon source) but also involve signalling, causing a decrease in the abundance of translation machinery in S. lycopersicum (Gray et al., 2020). However, as stated recently, the specific mechanisms of high CO2 signalling responsible for the decline in protein synthesis are not known and by analogy with stomatal and defence mechanisms, might involve reactive oxygen species (via RBOH activity), β carbonic anhydrase activity and/or SnRK2‐mediated signalling (Foyer et al., 2025). Interestingly, one of the most impacted proteins was Exordium (Figure 5a). Exordium protein expression has been shown to be affected by carbon supply and hypoxia (Schröder et al., 2012), perhaps via a change in cellular pH (Jain & Schmidt, 2024) which could indeed be impacted by very high CO2 via the equilibration with bicarbonate catalysed by carbonic anhydrase.

Potential actors of protein turn‐over regulation

Collectively, our results, past and present, suggest a stimulation of protein synthesis by increasing CO2 mole fraction (except at very high CO2). Since the protein content is the net result of protein biosynthesis from amino acids and protein degradation, we can envisage several concurrent mechanisms to explain our observations: (i) the stimulation of cytoplasmic translation initiation and the formation of polysomes; (ii) the increase in ribosomal protein abundance (auto‐catalytic effect); (iii) the modulation of protease activity and/or protein maturation activity; and (iv) the provision of amino acids to sustain translation.

Mechanism (i) has already been discussed before and shown to involve the phosphorylation of key actors such as RPS6, eIF4B and eIF3d, and the dephosphorylation of RPP1/2, eIF4G and eIF5A (Boex‐Fontvieille, Daventure, et al., 2013; Boex‐Fontvieille, Gauthier, et al., 2013; Tcherkez et al., 2020). Molecular determinants of such phosphorylation modifications likely include mTOR and SnRK1‐mediated signalling via the RPS6 kinase (Baena‐González & Hanson, 2017), as well as casein kinase 2 (CK2) (Dennis & Browning, 2009) which may phosphorylate RPP1/2 (Hasler et al., 1991; Nusspaumer et al., 2000; Tchórzewski et al., 2000). Mechanism (ii) is demonstrated here, with a linear relationship between protein synthesis and the abundance of ribosomal proteins of cluster 3 (Figure 5). Interestingly, this correlated with the elongation factor eEF1α (allowing aminoacyl‐tRNA binding to the ribosome during elongation initiation) and anticorrelated with eIF3 proteins (eIF3C, F and E). The anticorrelation with eIF3 factors might appear surprising at first glance since the eIF3 complex is essential for binding the eIF4‐mRNA complex allowing scanning and AUG recognition (Browning & Bailey‐Serres, 2015). However, eIF3 proteins are also associated with the 26S proteasome and thus may be involved in protein turn‐over via the COP9 signalosome and the formation of the so‐called ‘PCI complex’ (Proteasome‐COP9‐eIF3) (Browning, 2014; Browning & Bailey‐Serres, 2015; Paz‐Aviram et al., 2008; Sha et al., 2009; Yahalom et al., 2008). The initiation factors eIF3E and eIF3C have been shown to bind to the CSN7 subunit of the COP9 signalosome in Arabidopsis (Paz‐Aviram et al., 2008; Yahalom et al., 2001). However, to date, the specific role of eIF3F in plants in protein degradation regulation is limited. Mutants characterisation or expression analysis suggest a role of eIF3F in various aspects of development, regardless of whether it may be linked to translation or protein degradation (Medina‐Hernández et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2010). The potential role of eIF3 in proteasome links to mechanism (iii), whereby the control of protein degradation participates in the observed increase in net protein synthesis. Here, we also found a change in the abundance of proteasome subunits, a subtilisin‐like serine protease, a leucine aminopeptidase and aspartic protease 1 (APP1), driven by CO2 mole fraction (File S1). Leaf aspartic proteases are represented by APP1, CND4 and NANA, which are involved in photosynthetic maintenance and adaptation to stress (Yu & Feng, 2025). A direct link has been made between APP1 and photosynthesis in wheat, whereby the suppression of APP1 via a STOP mutation in the encoding gene led to an increase in photosynthesis rate of 1 to 2 μmol m−2 s−1 (Niu et al., 2023). It is worth noting that here, APP1 abundance increased with photosynthesis (positive OPLS loading in multivariate analysis, Figure 5a and File S1), thus suggesting that increased protein synthesis was the result of a proper balance between translation stimulation and protease‐mediated degradation when CO2 increased (i.e. with more PCI complex‐mediated degradation at low CO2, and protease‐mediated degradation at high CO2).

Mechanism (iv) (provision of amino acids) would be consistent with the general increase in many amino acid contents when CO2 mole fraction increased (Figures 3b and 4). The higher availability in free amino acids also promotes translation initiation (mechanism (i) above) since the mTOR regulation pathway is directly influenced by the amino acid‐to‐sugar balance (Lutt & Brunkard, 2022). For example, it has been shown in Arabidopsis that glutamine and isoleucine stimulate the TOR kinase and RPS6 phosphorylation—and thus translation (O'Leary et al., 2020). The change in amino acid profile as CO2 mole fraction increased was also associated with a progressive modification in associated metabolic pathways as reflected by the increase in several amino acid derivatives (Figure 4b; Figures S3b and S4), and the 13C distribution upon 13CO2 labelling (Figure 3a). First, both serine content and 13C enrichment increased with CO2 mole fraction (along with that in glycerate and diethanolamine), suggesting the onset of the cytoplasmic glycerate pathway of serine biosynthesis as photosynthesis increased. Second, there was a general reconfiguration of aromatic amino acids and compounds synthesised therefrom, with a higher 13C content and/or pool size in phenylalanine‐derived compounds (phenylpropanoids: coumarate, dopamine, hydro‐caffeate, caffeate, chlorogenate) as photosynthesis increased, at the expense of tryptophan (while tryptophan degradation products tended to be more abundant at ambient or high CO2). Third, branched‐chain amino acid and lysine metabolism was stimulated by CO2 (Figures S3c and S4, cluster 1). Interestingly, mTOR/SnRK1 signalling has been proposed to be linked to branched‐chain and lysine degradation under circumstances of carbon supply shortage (Heinemann & Hildebrandt, 2021). Fourth, there was a shift in the balance between reserve remobilisation, anaplerotic PEPC activity and glycolysis to feed the tricarboxylic acid pathway (TCAP). In effect, the isotope enrichment in glutamate increased progressively with CO2 mole fraction (Figure S2), showing that it relied less on stored citrate remobilisation (Abadie et al., 2024). Also, the isotope enrichment in alanine and glycerate increased, suggesting a higher 13C commitment in glycolysis (or a higher 13C enrichment in glycolytic intermediates). Conversely, PEPC activity was more important at low CO2, as reflected by the considerable increase in malate (Figure S4, cluster 3) and the significant increase in fumarate (Figure 3). This observation agrees with previous findings on direct assessment of PEPC activity using malate isotopologues (Abadie & Tcherkez, 2019a). Interestingly, there was also an increase in cysteine and a decrease in homoserine (Figure S4) at low CO2, in line with the stimulation of S assimilation when photorespiration increased (Abadie & Tcherkez, 2019b).

Nevertheless, it should be recognised that the impact of CO2 mole fraction on amino acid metabolism was not progressive and linear (this is particularly visible in Figure S3, clusters 1 and 3, and Figure S4, cluster 2). For example, the maximum pool size of shikimate (aromatic amino acid precursor) and the tryptophan derivative kynureamine was observed under ambient CO2 conditions (400 μmol mol−1) (Figure S4) while threonine and leucine did not vary linearly with CO2, in an anticorrelated manner (Figure S3). A non‐linear effect of photosynthetic conditions on the content (and 13C enrichment) of valine and phenylalanine, for example, has already been observed before (Abadie et al., 2021). It is possible that this effect partly came from the fact that the demand in individual amino acids varied with CO2, simply because proteins that are synthesised (and thus their amino acid composition) changed. To illustrate this, we used the sequence of proteins that were most impacted by CO2 conditions (Figure S5) and calculated the amino acid consumption it would represent, using the protein synthesis rate obtained here (Figure S7). It appears that the consumption rate depends on amino acids (typically, tryptophan or histidine being associated with a low consumption rate due to their limited occurrence in protein sequences). Moreover, it was not linear at all when CO2 varied. In particular, there was an important difference between leucine and threonine, probably explaining why these two amino acids behaved so differently (and non‐monotonously) as CO2 mole fraction increased (consumption offset in purple, Figure S7). In other words, it is likely that amino acid pool sizes we observed at a given CO2 mole fraction were not only due to changes in their biosynthesis rate but also to their consumption to sustain the synthesis of specific proteins that accumulated in that CO2 condition.

Consequences of protein turn‐over modifications with CO2

Our results show that the content in several enzymatic proteins also changed with CO2 mole fraction (Figure 4; Figure S6). This effect included proteins involved in photosynthesis (chloroplastic electron chain) and photorespiration. Under the assumption that this has consequences on activity, it suggests that CO2 conditions led to an adjustment of enzymatic capacity in the short term. In other words, it probably contributed to the acclimation to CO2 mole fraction either to improve the recycling of photorespiratory intermediates, increase carboxylation capacity or facilitate RuBP regeneration.

To date, short‐term (5 days to 2 weeks) effects of CO2 mole fraction on photosynthesis have already been observed in various species, with either a stimulating effect of high CO2 pre‐conditioning on net assimilation rate, photosynthetic induction or Rubisco content (Campbell & Young, 1986; Cheng et al., 1998; Evans & Hendrey, 1992; Jones et al., 1985; Tomimatsu et al., 2019). Of course, the short‐term CO2 acclimation effect could affect the shape of A/c i curves and the numerical solving of V cmax and J max. To ascertain this, it would be necessary to monitor protein contents and carry out A/c i curves at different time points to assess whether there is a link between significantly accumulated proteins and pre‐conditioning at a given CO2 mole fraction. We nevertheless recognise that fast A/c i curve methods can be used, probably limiting the effect of short‐term CO2 acclimation (Lin et al., 2023; Saathoff & Welles, 2021; Stinziano, Adamson, & Hanson, 2019; Stinziano, McDermitt, et al., 2019; Tejera‐Nieves et al., 2024). Fast A/c i curve methods may be relevant to field experiments, where high‐throughput photosynthetic measurements are carried out, although they are probably not appropriate under all circumstances, for example when coupling gas exchange to chlorophyll fluorescence or investigating stomatal responses. We nevertheless suggest that verifying protein properties, for example using %N and 13C content (affected by inlet CO2 of the gas exchange system), could be a useful precaution during photosynthetic gas exchange experiments. That said, we recognise that our findings have the most relevance under controlled conditions during photosynthetic measurements like CO2‐response curves. Further work is needed to investigate the impact of other situations where the short‐term response to changing CO2 could be important outside of an experimental context, such as (i) canopies with non‐well‐mixed air and thus with CO2 drawdowns, (ii) conditions were stomatal conductance and thus CO2 (c i ) are impacted, such as water restriction or potassium deficiency.

An important consequence of our result is that the considerable change in protein synthesis rate with CO2 mole fraction should impact maintenance respiration in the light and thus the rate of CO2 efflux (day respiration). From low (40 μmol mol−1) to high (800 μmol mol−1) CO2, the increase in protein synthesis (0.04 to 0.6 μmol C m−2 s−1) represents a CO2 production of 0.008 to 0.12 μmol m−2 s−1 (i.e. 1–15% of the day respiration rate, which is about 0.8 μmol m−2 s−1 in our sunflower variety (Abadie et al., 2024)), assuming an average respiratory efficiency of 5 ATP required per amino acid and 5.25 ATP per CO2 produced (Amthor, 2000). In addition to protein biosynthesis per se, our results also suggest a reorchestration of amino acid metabolism, which can also produce CO2 (for example tryptophan biosynthesis from shikimate produces 2 mol CO2 mol−1 tryptophan; leucine biosynthesis from pyruvate produces 3 mol CO2 mol−1 leucine). Of course, a proper estimate of CO2 evolution (in μmol m−2 s−1) caused by amino acid metabolism (biosynthesis and degradation) would require a quantitative analysis (absolute quantitation in mol m−2) of intermediates, and it has to be addressed in the future. Collectively, our observations provide experimental support to the idea that day respiration is unlikely to be a constant parameter when CO2 mole fraction varies—for a specific discussion of this aspect and how it impacts on photosynthesis measurements, see Moreno‐Echeverry et al. (2026).

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Plant material and growth conditions

Sunflower seeds (Helianthus annuus, var. XRQ) were sown in potting mix (Substrat 5, greenfibre, Klassmann‐Deilmann). After 4 weeks, seedlings were transferred to 10‐L pots and grown in the greenhouse at 24/18°C, 60/55% relative humidity, 16/8 h photoperiod (day/night), with natural light supplemented by 400‐W sodium lamps. Plants were watered by drip irrigation with 1.5 g L−1 nutrient solution (PlantProd, Brampton, Canada) with a N/P2O5/K2O composition of 15/10/30 including a nitrogen balance of 11/4 for nitrate/ammonium. Plants were used for experiments prior to flowering during a 3‐week window from 6 to 7 weeks after sowing. Plants were used for experiments 60 days after sowing. We used leaves of rank 10–12, which are the mature source leaves with maximum photosynthetic capacity at this developmental stage.

Gas exchange and labelling

For the ‘labelling experiment’, leaves were subjected to different CO2 mole fractions as in (Tcherkez et al., 2025). We used an adapted (90 cm2) chamber coupled with the LI‐COR 6800 (LI‐COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Gas exchange conditions were (the gas exchange experimental design is shown in Figure S1): 800 μmol m−2 s−1 photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), gas flow 35 L h−1, 80–90% relative humidity and 21–23°C air temperature. The CO2 mole fraction was varied between 40 and 1200 μmol mol−1 (C a) in order to vary photosynthesis. Isotopic labelling was performed using 13CO2 (Sigma‐Aldrich [Saint Quentin Fallavier, France], 99% 13C). Note that experiments with unlabelled CO2 (natural abundance of carbon‐13) were carried out to have the background isotope signal (and thus subtract natural abundance and calculate the isotope excess). At the end of the experiment (3 h at the desired CO2 mole fraction), samples were instant‐frozen with liquid nitrogen spraying and stored at −80°C. For the ‘response curve’ experiment, we ran gas exchange with the 6‐cm2 LI‐COR 6800 chamber, under 70–80% relative humidity, and the same light intensity and temperature as in the ‘labelling experiment’, using CO2 mole fractions from 5 to 1600 μmol mol−1 (see Figure S1). After stabilisation at the desired CO2 (1 h), the leaf was rapidly removed from the chamber, the blade surface that was in the chamber was cut with an auger and plunged in liquid nitrogen. This procedure took less than 5 sec to avoid changes in metabolism that would compromise metabolomics analyses. A/c i curve modelling (fitting) was done using the plug‐in of (Sharkey et al., 2007) in Excel. To generate Figure 1, A/c i response curve was plotted in SigmaPlot 17® (San Jose, CA, USA). Note that A/c i fitting is of limited importance in this paper since it is not used for other calculations.

Elemental analysis and isotope ratio mass spectrometry (EA‐IRMS)

EA‐IRMS was performed on raw material (frozen‐dried total organic matter) and purified protein. 2.5 mg of sample was weighted in tin capsules. %C, N and S and delta values (δ13C, δ15N and δ34S) were measured using an elemental Analyser (EA Vario Pyro Cube, Elementar, Villeurbanne, France) coupled with an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (IRMS, Isoprime PrecisION, Elementar). Possible offsets of IRMS values were prevented using international IAEA (Vienna) and home‐made standards, sucrose‐methionine‐glycine mixture (Merck, Saint Quentin Fallavier, France) and whey protein (Maxi nutrition).

13C nuclear magnetic resonance and GC–MS analysis

Sampling associated with the ‘labelling experiment’ was analysed by quantitative 13C‐NMR (including using spin–spin interaction analysis and %13C in molecules) as described in (Abadie et al., 2018; Abadie et al., 2017). Non‐targeted NMR and GC–MS were carried out via bucketing and MZMine extraction, respectively, as in Abadie et al. (2021).

Shotgun proteomics

Proteomics analyses were carried out as in (Balliau et al., 2025) and are described in Supplementary Notes S1. Proteins were extracted using trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and acetone. Protein content in extracts was measured using the tryptophan fluorescence (WF) method (Wiśniewski & Gaugaz, 2015). After trypsin digestion, peptides were analysed by LC–MS/MS (nano‐HPLC coupled with mass spectrometry via an electrospray interface, Q‐Exactive +, Thermo Scientific, Courtaboeuf, France). Protein identification, filtering and grouping were carried out using the X!Tandem pipeline, after Langella et al. (2024).

Statistics and calculations

Multivariate analysis (Orthogonal Partial Least Square, OPLS) was realised using Simca® version 18 (Sartorius, Dourdan, France) with CO2 mole fraction as the response quantitative variable. Results are presented as a volcano plot whereby the loading of the OPLS (pq) is shown with the logarithm of the P‐value in the univariate analysis. Univariate analyses (ANOVA in Supplementary Figures or linear regression in main text figures) were performed using R‐Studio (version 2024.09.1) with R‐base and the packages dplyr, broom, tidyr, tidyverse. Depending on the number of features, figures show the Benjamini–Hochberg correction for the false discovery rate or simply the Bonferroni correction, as indicated in main text and legends. The rate of protein synthesis (r ps) was calculated by isotopic mass balance, accounting for the isotope enrichment (%13C) in free amino acids (p aa), as follows: rps=pprot−punp/paa−puna×U×k/100, where p prot and p unp, and p una are the %13C in proteins after labelling, without labelling and in amino acids without labelling (i.e. natural abundance, 1.079% 13C as determined by IRMS); U is the protein content in μmol m−2; and k is the %C in proteins (determined by EA). The protein content was determined by two methods: using WF or the %N in leaf organic matter, accounting for a coefficient of 17.2 between protein total mass and protein N mass (N‐ratio of 5.8). The rate of protein synthesis from ‘new’ amino acids (13C amino acids) was calculated by replacing p aa by 0.99 (99% 13C, which is the %13C in labelling CO2). The rate of protein synthesis from recycling was then calculated by difference.
