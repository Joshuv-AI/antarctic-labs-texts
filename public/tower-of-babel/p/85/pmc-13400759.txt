Delayed Candida keratitis associated with Descemet's stripping endothelial keratoplasty
Authors: Deena Shaath, George Harocopos, James Walsh, Adam Snowden, Augustine Hong, Praneetha Thulasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeOur purpose is to report a unique case of Candida interface keratitis that remained quiescent for 3 years after Descemet's stripping endothelial keratoplasty (DSEK), within the endokeratoplasty graft itself, in a patient with underlying uveitis and local immunosuppression from a corticosteroid implant.MethodsWe describe a 20-year-old woman with a history of congenital glaucoma and secondary corneal edema who exhibited delayed worsening of fungal keratitis requiring treatment 3 years after a DSEK and concurrent fluocinolone acetonide intravitreal implantation.ResultsThe patient was treated with topical, oral, and intrastromal antifungals without resolution of infection, necessitating a therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty. The corneal button showed Candida lusitaniae infection located between the posterior stroma and Descemet's membrane within the endokeratoplasty graft.ConclusionsThis report describes a case of late-developing fungal keratitis 3 years after DSEK that occurred in the graft itself, in the plane between the posterior stroma and Descemet's membrane, rather than between the donor and host cornea. This highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for fungal keratitis in patients with persistent or worsening opacities in endothelial keratoplasty grafts.Keywords: Interface keratitis, Fungal keratitis, Descemet's stripping endothelial keratoplasty (DSEK), Candida keratitis

Introduction

Fungal interface keratitis is a well-described complication after lamellar keratoplasty, especially endothelial keratoplasty (EK). The reason for the increased risk of fungal keratitis after EK compared to other keratoplasties is thought to be due to the increased warming time associated with tissue processing. Fungal keratitis typically presents within a few months after surgery, with no documented cases of delayed presentation beyond two years. Due to the routine use of corticosteroids following surgery, typical signs of infections may be masked. We report a case of fungal keratitis after Descemet's stripping endothelial keratoplasty (DSEK) with concurrent intravitreal injection of fluocinolone acetonide implantation that remained quiescent for three years, though the exact mechanism underlying this prolonged stability remains unclear. The infection was treated with topical, oral, and intrastromal antifungals but ultimately required a therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty for complete resolution.

Case

The patient was a 20-year-old female with a past ocular history of congenital glaucoma, bilateral intermediate uveitis with cystoid macular edema (CME), and secondary corneal edema of the left eye. Her most recent surgical history in the left eye included Ahmed (New World Medical, Rancho Cucamonga, California) and Molteno valves (Molteno Ophthalmic Ltd., Dunedin, New Zealand) (nine and five years before presentation, respectively). A DSEK with concurrent fluocinolone acetonide 0.18 mg intravitreal implant placement was performed uneventfully. Cornea donor rim cultures were not obtained, as our institution did not routinely perform them at the time of our patient's surgery. Two months after surgery, the patient was noted to have three deep corneal opacities without haze or overlying epithelial defects (Fig. 1). Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) at that time was stable at 20/50. These opacities remained stable over the next few months. After extensive discussion with the patient and family, the patient opted for observation of these opacities and to undergo further treatment only in the event of any worsening. The patient tapered off all topical steroids over 6 months. The opacities remained stable for three years, at which point she presented with a larger central stellate endothelial plaque with overlying haze and without any superficial infiltrate or epithelial defect (Fig. 2). Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed a deep stromal opacity appearing like an endothelial plaque (Fig. 3). Confocal microscopy was non-diagnostic. She was initially treated with topical moxifloxacin and oral levofloxacin without any improvement. The patient was taken to the operating room for an anterior chamber paracentesis, and intracameral vancomycin, ceftazidime, and voriconazole were administered. Intrastromal voriconazole was simultaneously delivered. There was no growth on culture from the aqueous samples, and next-generation sequencing for fungi was also negative. Despite negative sequencing, there was a high index of suspicion for a fungal infection, and she was started on topical voriconazole 1% and oral voriconazole 200mg twice a day for presumed fungal keratitis. Due to lack of improvement, three doses of intrastromal voriconazole (100mcg/0.1mL) and amphotericin (2mcg/0.1mL) were given at 1-week intervals without any change in the appearance of the infiltrate. At this point, we proceeded with a penetrating keratoplasty (PK), taking care to excise all visible opacities. The excised cornea was sent for pathology and culture. The culture grew Candida lusitaniae, and pathology showed yeast dissecting the posterior stroma and Descemet's membrane within the endokeratoplasty graft (Fig. 4). The patient continued topical voriconazole drops for 3 months after surgery without any evidence of recurrent infection. The new graft remained clear postoperatively, with a BCVA of 20/90 at 7 months (Fig. 5).

A clear graft was noted seven months after a therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty, without any evidence of recurrence of infection.

Discussion

We report a case of Candida lusitaniae infection occurring within the endokeratoplasty graft after DSEK surgery in a patient with underlying chronic uveitis and local immunosuppression from fluocinolone acetonide steroid implant. Our case highlights two important aspects: the location of the infection within the endokeratoplasty graft (between the posterior stroma and the Descemet's membrane), and the delayed nature of progression in the setting of local immunosuppression from intravitreal fluocinolone.

Fungal keratitis is a rare complication that may occur in all types of keratoplasty, reported in fewer than 1% of transplants.1,2 Risk factors include topical steroid use, exposed sutures, persistent epithelial defects, reduced corneal sensation, extended contact lens wear, and contaminated tissue during processing.2,3 Interface keratitis is a specific type of infection that occurs between the graft and the host and may develop after any lamellar keratoplasty. This complication is especially difficult to treat, with the deep location of infection in the graft-host interface making it difficult to obtain samples for culture and to reach sufficient depth with treatment.

There is a 1.5-3 times higher risk of fungal infections following an endothelial keratoplasty than a penetrating keratoplasty.4 The biggest risk factor is thought to be contaminated donor tissue. The increased warming time associated with processing the endothelial keratoplasty tissue is believed to promote the growth of infectious organisms. The lack of antifungal agents in the corneal storage medium may also prevent inhibition of fungal species.4 Indeed, hypothermic storage using Optisol-GS (which traditionally has only gentamicin without an antifungal agent) had a much higher positive rim culture rate when compared to organ culture medium (which contains an antifungal agent) to store and preserve donor corneas (9.8% vs 1.3%). Similarly, clinical cases of fungal keratitis had a higher incidence in tissues stored in hypothermic medium storage (0.5%) compared to organ culture storage medium (0.2%).5

There have been many cases reports of fungal keratitis following DSEK procedures 1,3,6, 7, 8, 9. In one review, 31 cases of interface infectious keratitis were reported. Of these, Candida species were the most common, reported in 75% of samples. The median time to development of these infections was 28 days (range of 1-120 days). Antifungal agents and penetrating keratoplasty were necessary to eradicate the infection in most of these cases.7 Another review identified 62 cases of infectious interface keratitis, 46 (75.41%) of which were fungal, with an average onset of 51.5 days after endothelial keratoplasty. Most of these cases (39, 62.9%) required penetrating keratoplasty after being unresponsive to medical treatment and/or repeat lamellar keratoplasty.10 Another case series reviewing 99 DSAEK procedures had 2 cases of Candida species interface keratitis. In one case, the infiltrate presented 20 days after surgery, and topical and intracameral amphotericin as well as oral fluconazole were started prior to a repeat DSEK. On post-operative day 3, the infiltrate returned, and repeat intracameral injections were performed. The patient ultimately underwent a PK, and her graft remained clear at 5 months. In the second case, the infiltrates were visible on day 9, and repeat DSEK was performed; however, a PK was required 4.5 months after the first surgery due to infection recurrence. The graft remained clear at 2.5 months.8 Another case series describes 24 cases of interface keratitis after EK, occurring an average of 49.2 days (1-120 days) after the procedure, and PK was performed in 79% of the cases to resolve the infection.11

Our patient's infection was located within the endokeratoplasty graft, between the posterior stroma and Descemet's membrane of the donor tissue. To the best of our knowledge, there is no case report describing a fungal infection in this location for DSEKs. There are reports of post-DMEK fungal keratitis in the plane between the posterior stroma and Descemet's, which is where the surgical interface is located.12,13 (It is worth noting, however, that those two reports did not show histopathology; rather, the diagnosis was established on the basis of clinical examination and culture of either the tissue transport medium or of the donor tissue.) While the classic interface infections between the donor and the host stroma are associated with donor tissue contamination while processing, a fungal nidus within the graft itself, as with our patient, is harder to explain by this mechanism. It may have been from donor endogenous origin or introduced during surgical manipulation. Unfortunately, at this time, we do not have definitive evidence of the source of infection.

Previous cases have reported correlations between fungal interface keratitis and positive donor rim cultures. One retrospective review reported positive cultures in 14 of 127 (11%) DSEKs, only 1 of which developed fungal keratitis, requiring PKP.14 A similar retrospective review of 3414 keratoplasty cases reported positive donor rim cultures in 71 (2.1%) of cases, of which 26 (36.6%) were DSEKs. Three of these patients developed keratitis.15

Although routinely sending donor rim cultures is not standard of care, a positive rim culture may allow for early and appropriate antifungal treatment for presenting infections.16 As mentioned previously, cornea donor rim cultures were not performed routinely at our institution at the time of our patient's surgery. If our patient's donor rim culture had been positive, there would have been a higher likelihood that the patient's corneal opacities at two months would have been immediately treated despite the lack of change in lesions. It also may have helped us identify the source of infection – a donor rim culture positive for the same fungal species would have supported the conclusion that the source of infection was related to either donor risk factors or tissue processing.

Steroids in the setting of fungal keratitis are generally thought to worsen the infection. There are previous studies that suggest that fungal replication is increased when corticosteroids are administered.17 There is also evidence that steroids can cause expansion of infection in the deeper layers of the cornea, which can make it more difficult for antifungals to reach the deeper layers.18 Steroids can also suppress inflammation, limiting the immune system's ability to fight infections.19 It is therefore surprising that our patient's infection worsened as the fluocinolone implant was losing its efficacy.20 This infection presented about 2 months after surgery but remained completely stable for about 3 years. What caused the initial opacity and the relative stability over 3 years is still unclear. The longest previously reported intervals between endothelial keratoplasty and fungal interface keratitis are approximately two years — Araki-Sasaki et al. reported a case following DSAEK in the setting of chronic topical steroid use and multiple prior surgeries, though that infection originated superficially before involving the interface,21 and Vaidya et al. described a case after Descemet's membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) with a two-year latency.22 Our case, at three years, exceeds both. The mechanism underlying the prolonged quiescence in our patient remains unclear. We hypothesize that the anatomical sequestration of the organism between the graft stroma and Descemet's membrane, combined with sustained local immunosuppression from the fluocinolone acetonide implant, may have limited both immune recognition and antifungal penetration. However, this remains speculative, and alternative explanations — including low-virulence colonization or gradual subclinical progression — cannot be excluded.

Diagnosis and treatment of interface keratitis after EK is challenging due to the deep location of the infection, making it difficult to culture and for the topical antimicrobial drops to penetrate the cornea. Since our patient did not respond to topical antifungals, oral voriconazole and intrastromal antifungal injections, which have better ocular penetration for deeper lesions, were initiated.23 While the infection stabilized after intrastromal injections, the opacities never resolved. While C. lusitaniae demonstrates variable susceptibility to amphotericin B and other first-line antifungals, the infection failed to resolve despite a multi-drug regimen. Although amphotericin resistance may have contributed to treatment failure, we suspect the deep location of the infection and its encasement between the donor Descemet's membrane and stroma played a central role in limiting drug penetration and efficacy. Most patients with this type of infection require a PK, similar to our patient.1,23 There are some reports of removing the donor lenticule if infection is noted shortly after surgery, but these reports do not mention whether the location of infection was within the interface or within the endokeratoplasty graft. Nevertheless, this is rarely performed and usually does not result in resolution of the infection.24 Given that our patient's infection was isolated to the endokeratoplasty graft only, had we recognized this earlier on, our patient might have benefited from early removal of the donor lenticule without necessitating a penetrating keratoplasty. At the time of presentation, although anterior segment OCT suggested the infection may have been confined to the DSEK graft, the clinical picture was insufficiently definitive to pursue isolated graft removal with confidence. A therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty was therefore chosen to ensure complete excision. In retrospect, earlier and more certain recognition of graft confinement might have supported a less invasive approach.

This report describes a unique case of interface keratitis that appeared 2 months post-surgery but remained quiescent until 3 years later, ultimately presenting in the donor graft between the posterior stroma and Descemet's membrane. Early use of anterior segment OCT might help us identify patients with infections that are isolated to the donor lenticule only and thus may be amenable to removal of the DSEK graft alone, without requiring a penetrating keratoplasty for resolution of infection. Our patient also remained stable while she was locally immunosuppressed, a finding that is difficult to reconcile with the known tendency of steroids to worsen fungal keratitis. More research is needed to better understand the role of chronically altered ocular immunity and the presentation and proliferation of deep fungal infections.
