Clinicopathological features and departmental distribution of sarcoma patients: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Mei Kang, Liyang Zhu, Li Niu, Li Zhang, Yichun Wang, Jun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcomas are heterogeneous malignancies with high misdiagnosis and referral challenges. Limited real-world data on sarcoma subtype distribution, age-specific patterns, and departmental referral characteristics in Chinese populations hinders the optimization of clinical management strategies. This study aimed to analyze the clinicopathological features and departmental distribution of 295 sarcoma patients, subtype patterns, to optimize diagnostic and referral strategies.Methods This retrospective study enrolled 295 inpatients with pathologically confirmed sarcomas who underwent surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between September 2020 and November 2023. Demographic, clinical, and pathological data were extracted from electronic medical records and pathology databases. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 20.0 and R 4.5.2, with Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical associations and one-way analysis of variance for age differences across subtypes.Results The cohort had a median age of 56 years, with middle-aged and older adults (41-80 years) accounting for 75.9% of cases, and a male-to-female ratio of 1.27:1. Patients were admitted across 17 departments, with Orthopedic Oncology (41.0%), Urology (12.9%), and Plastic Surgery (10.8%) as the top three. Extremities (35.6%) were the most common tumor location. The top five pathological subtypes were unspecified sarcoma (24.7%), liposarcoma (16.9%), chondrosarcoma (15.3%), osteosarcoma (10.5%), and dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP, 6.8%). Significant associations were identified between pathological subtypes and age (χ²=144.5, P<0.001), admitting department (χ²=370.6, P<0.001), tumor location (χ²=412.4, P<0.001), and gender (χ²=22.2, P=0.014). immunohistochemical findings included CD34 positivity in 82.4% of DFSP cases and S-100 positivity in 76.7% of liposarcoma cases.Conclusions Sarcoma subtypes show distinct clinical factor-specific patterns, informing optimized referrals and treatment.Tailored pathways and standardized pathological assessment can enhance diagnostic efficiency and treatment outcomes for Chinese sarcoma patients.Keywords: Sarcoma, Pathological subtype, Departmental distribution, Clinical characteristics

Introduction

As malignant neoplasms arising from soft tissues and bones, sarcomas span a broad patient demographic encompassing adults, adolescents, and children.Sarcomas account for approximately 1% of all adult malignancies and 15% of pediatric solid tumors globally [1]. According to the 2020 World Health Organization(WHO) Classification of Soft Tissue and Bone Tumors, sarcomas are stratified into more than 100 distinct subtypes, each characterized by unique biological behaviors, differential treatment responses, and disparate prognostic trajectories [2]. A defining feature of these tumours lies in their profound morphological heterogeneity notably, several subtypes lack specific histopathological signatures that could facilitate definitive diagnosis [3]. This profound molecular and clinical heterogeneity poses formidable challenges to clinical management: misdiagnosis rates of rare sarcoma subtypes may surpass 30%, and inappropriate interdepartmental referrals frequently result in delayed administration of standardized therapeutic regimens [4].

In China, large-scale retrospective studies that systematically characterize the subtype distribution and clinical management paradigms of sarcomas remain scarce. Most available datasets are either confined to individual subtypes or lack critical details regarding immunohistochemical profiles and interdepartmental referral pathways [5]. Against the backdrop of precision medicine advances, there is an urgent imperative to generate real-world evidence that can inform subtype-specific diagnostic strategies, specialized clinical triage, and prognostic stratification.

In light of the aforementioned challenges and research gaps, the present study aims to first systematically analyze the distribution of sarcomas across different departments in our hospital. By supplementing real-world clinical data on sarcomas, we intend to explore the subtype distribution across distinct age groups, and seek to investigate the association between clinical departments and sarcoma subtypes, thereby providing evidence for optimizing interdepartmental referral processes and facilitating centralized diagnosis and treatment. Additionally, we will evaluate the expression profiles of common immunohistochemical markers across various sarcoma subtypes to screen for potential diagnostic and prognostic indicators tailored to Chinese patients.

Methods

Patients selection

This retrospective observational study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility [6]. Data for this study were extracted from the electronic medical record system and pathological database of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Eligible subjects were sarcoma patients who were admitted and underwent surgical resection between September 2020 and November 2023.

Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) Pathological diagnosis conforming to the criteria outlined in the 2020 WHO Classification of Soft Tissue and Bone Tumors; (2) Availability of complete clinical data, including age, gender, tumor location, and referring department; (3) Confirmed primary sarcoma cases.

Exclusion criteria were established as follows: (1) Patients diagnosed with non-sarcomatous malignancies; (2) Patients with incomplete clinical data; (3) Outpatients, as only inpatients were enrolled to ensure data integrity; (4) Gastrointestinal stromal tumors were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were retrieved from the hospital’s Electronic Medical Records system, while pathological subtypes and immunohistochemical(IHC) markers were extracted from the digital database of the Department of Pathology.

Data extraction was independently performed by two trained researchers using a standardized data collection form. The specific indicators collected are detailed as follows: (1) Age: Calculated as the age at the time of pathological diagnosis, and stratified into five groups (0–20years, 21–40years, 41–60years, 61–80years, and 81–100years) for descriptive analysis; (2) Admitting department: Defined as the first department of admission, including 17 departments such as Orthopedic Oncology, Urology, Plastic Surgery, and Gynecology; (3) Tumor location: Categorized into 9 anatomical regions (extremities, trunk, visceral organs, body surface, pelvic cavity, retroperitoneum, head and neck, pelvis, and others) based on preoperative imaging data and surgical findings; (4) Pathological subtype: Classified in accordance with the 2020 WHO guidelines; rare subtypes with a sample size of less than 5 (n < 5) were grouped into the “Others” category to avoid statistical instability; (5) IHC markers: Extracted from pathology reports issued by the Department of Pathology, including CD34, S-100, CK, Ki-67, and other relevant markers.

Given the retrospective design of this research, which involved the analysis of existing clinical data and records without direct interaction with participants, and the assessment of minimal risk to individuals, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (Version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 adopted for all tests.

Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%). Missing data were reported explicitly. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro—Wilk test: normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median. Associations between categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Differences in age across different pathological subtypes were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance.

Age and Gender Distribution of 295 Sarcoma Patients

Demographic distribution of sarcoma patients

The study cohort comprised 295 patients aged 1 to 93 years, with a median age of 56 years and a mean age of 53.1 ± 19.1 years. Age distribution was marked by a distinct predominance of middle-aged and older adults: the most prevalent subgroup was 41–60 years, accounting for 128 patients (43.4%); the second largest subgroup was 61–80 years, comprising 96 patients (32.5%); pediatric and young adult patients (0–20 years and 21–40 years) accounted for smaller proportions, at 33 (11.2%) and 27 (9.2%) cases, respectively; and the least common subgroup was adults aged 81–100 years, with only 11 patients (3.7%). Consistent with this pattern, a histogram further confirmed a right-skewed age distribution, with the majority of patients concentrated in the 40–80 years range.

Of the 295 patients, 165 (55.9%) were male and 130 (44.1%) were female, corresponding to a male-to-female ratio of 1.27:1. Statistical analysis using an independent samples t-test revealed no significant difference in age between male and female patients (P = 0.236).

Admission department distribution

Patients were admitted from 17 different clinical departments, and this distribution underscoresa the multidisciplinary management paradigm for sarcomas. The top five admission departments in terms of case volume were as follows: Orthopedic Oncology (121 cases, 41.0%), which served as the primary referral department for bone and extremity sarcomas and accounted for 87.1% of all osteosarcoma cases in the cohort; Urology (38 cases, 12.9%), dedicated to the management of retroperitoneal and pelvic sarcomas with 68.2% of retroperitoneal liposarcomas treated in this department; Plastic Surgery (32 cases, 10.8%), specialized in superficial soft tissue sarcomas including 75.0% of dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) cases; Gynecology (21 cases, 7.1%), responsible for uterine and ovarian sarcomas with 100% of uterine leiomyosarcoma cases managed herein; and Hepatobiliary Pancreatic Surgery (14 cases, 4.7%), designated for the treatment of primary liver and biliary tract sarcomas.

The remaining 83 patients (28.2%) were distributed among 12 other departments, with thoracic surgery and gastrointestinal surgery each accounting for 14 cases.The specific distribution are presented in Table 2.

Tumor location distribution

A total of 295 sarcoma patients were enrolled, and the anatomical distribution of their primary tumor sites was systematically analyzed (Table 3) and visually presented using anatomical atlases (Fig. 2). The results revealed a distinct spatial clustering pattern of sarcomas: the extremities were the most prevalent primary site (35.6%, n = 105), with a significantly higher proportion than other locations; the trunk was the second most common (12.2%, n = 36), followed by the pelvic cavity (11.9%, n = 35) and visceral organs (11.2%, n = 33); moderate incidence was observed for the body surface (10.2%, n = 30) and retroperitoneum (7.5%, n = 22); head and neck sarcomas were relatively rare (5.4%, n = 16); and the remaining 18 cases (6.1%) were categorized as Others due to uncommon or poorly specified locations.

Anatomical Localization of Sarcoma Tumors in 295 Patients

Statistical analysis confirmed non-uniform anatomical distribution of sarcomas (χ²=128.36, P < 0.001). Post-hoc comparisons showed that extremity tumors were significantly more numerous than those in the head and neck (P < 0.001), retroperitoneum (P < 0.001), and Others (P < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were found between the trunk and pelvic cavity (P = 0.892), or between visceral organs and body surface (P = 0.765).

Pathological subtype distribution

There were seventeen distinct pathological subtypes were identified in the cohort, exhibiting marked histopathological heterogeneity. The five most prevalent subtypes were as follows: unspecified sarcoma (73 cases, 24.7%), which was primarily attributed to incomplete pathological classification of rare subtypes (e.g., undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma); liposarcoma (50 cases, 16.9%), consisting of well-differentiated (n = 28, 56.0%) and dedifferentiated (n = 22, 44.0%) variants; chondrosarcoma (45 cases, 15.3%), including conventional (n = 32, 71.1%) and clear cell (n = 13, 28.9%) subtypes; osteosarcoma (31 cases, 10.5%), with high-grade (n = 25, 80.6%) and parosteal (n = 6, 19.4%) subtypes; and DFSP(20 cases, 6.8%), all of which were CD34-positive on IHC. The remaining five subtypes (leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, fibrosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and others) collectively accounted for 24.2% of all cases (Table 4).

Associations between clinical factors and pathological subtypes

Association between age and pathological subtypes

Our study demonstrated a statistically significant association between age groups and pathological subtypes (χ² = 144.5, P < 0.001; Fig. 3), with distinct distribution patterns across the four age strata.

Distribution of Pathological Subtypes Across Different Age

In the pediatric group (≤ 18 years), osteosarcoma was the most prevalent subtype (60.7%), rhabdomyosarcoma was restricted to younger patients (14.3% in the pediatric group vs. 6.3% in the young adult group), and no cases of angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, or liposarcoma were detected; in the young adult group (19–40 years), chondrosarcoma ranked as the most common subtype (21.9%), followed by DFSP (15.6%), and the proportion of unspecified sarcomas (15.6%) was relatively low compared with other age groups.

In the middle-aged group (41–65 years), the most diverse subtype distribution was observed, with unspecified sarcoma (23.0%), liposarcoma (15.8%), and chondrosarcoma (17.8%) as the leading subtypes, and leiomyosarcoma was most frequently identified in this group (7.9%); in the elderly group (≥ 65 years), unspecified sarcoma was the dominant subtype (36.1%), followed by liposarcoma (27.7%), the proportion of chondrosarcoma (10.8%) was relatively lower than in younger age groups, and no rhabdomyosarcoma cases were documented.

Association between admission departments and pathological subtypes

Statistical analysis revealed a highly significant association between clinical departments and pathological subtypes (χ² =370.6, P < 0.001). This heatmap (Fig. 4) further illustrates the percentage distribution of major sarcoma subtypes across clinical departments, visually demonstrating the robust department-subtype correlations observed in this cohort.

Distribution of Pathological Subtypes Across Departments

Orthopedic Oncology, which specializes in the management of bone and soft tissue sarcomas, had osteosarcoma (24.0%) and chondrosarcoma (24.0%) as the most prevalent subtypes and managed the majority of osteosarcoma cases. Urology, primarily responsible for retroperitoneal and pelvic sarcomas, had liposarcoma (31.6%) and unspecified sarcoma (44.7%) as the dominant subtypes, accounting for 24.0% of all liposarcoma cases. Plastic Surgery exhibited a strong association with DFSP(95.0% of all DFSP cases), and also handled a substantial proportion of cutaneous and superficial soft tissue sarcomas. Gynecology, specialized in pelvic and gynecological sarcomas, had a high proportion of Other subtypes (47.6%) and leiomyosarcoma (28.6%). Gastrointestinal Surgery, focusing on abdominal and gastrointestinal sarcomas, had liposarcoma as the most common subtype (50.0%).

Association between tumor location and pathological subtypes

As showed in the Fig. 5, a statistically significant association was identified between tumor location and pathological subtypes (χ²=412.4, P < 0.001), with distinct location-specific distribution patterns.

Distribution of Pathological Subtypes Across Tumor Location

The extremities, the most common primary site for sarcomas, had a high prevalence of osteosarcoma (18.3%) and chondrosarcoma (16.7%), with DFSP predominantly localized to this region (85.0% of all DFSP cases); the retroperitoneum was dominated by liposarcoma (38.5%), consistent with the well-established predilection of this subtype for the retroperitoneal space, and unspecified sarcoma also showed a high prevalence (30.8%) at this location; the head and neck region displayed a diverse subtype distribution, with chondrosarcoma (22.2%) and unspecified sarcoma (33.3%) as the most common subtypes, and a relatively higher proportion of pediatric sarcomas was observed in this anatomical location; the trunk was characterized by unspecified sarcoma (31.6%) and liposarcoma (21.1%) as the leading subtypes, with leiomyosarcoma showing a relatively high prevalence (10.5%) compared with other anatomical sites.

Association between gender and pathological subtypes

A statistically significant association was identified between gender and pathological subtypes (χ²=22.2, P = 0.014). Gender-specific differences in subtype distribution were observed as follows: male patients had a higher prevalence of liposarcoma (22.4% vs. 10.0% in female), a slightly elevated proportion of unspecified sarcoma (26.7% vs. 22.3% in female), and a greater incidence of DFSP (9.1% vs. 3.9% in female). In contrast, female patients demonstrated a higher prevalence of leiomyosarcoma (8.5% vs. 2.4% in male) and chondrosarcoma (20.8% vs. 10.9% in male). No statistically significant gender-related differences were detected for osteosarcoma or synovial sarcoma.

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study systematically characterized the clinicopathological features, departmental distribution, and subtype-specific clinical correlations of 295 pathologically confirmed sarcoma patients, with the aim of refining diagnostic referral pathways and optimizing multidisciplinary management for this heterogeneous malignancy. The key findings revealed robust, statistically significant associations between sarcoma pathological subtypes and age, admitting department, tumor location, and gender. These results provide valuable insights into the optimization of sarcoma diagnosis, referral, and personalized management strategies, while offering real-world evidence specific to the Chinese patient population. Notably, these findings provide actionable clinical clues for differential diagnosis and targeted referral, even as they acknowledge the inherent limitations of such clinical factors in substituting for definitive imaging and pathological diagnosis [3].

The age-stratified analysis demonstrated that sarcoma subtypes exhibit distinct age-related distribution trends, with a clear predominance in middle-aged individuals (41–60 years; 43.4%), which is consistent with the core conclusions of existing epidemiological studies on sarcomas [7]. In the pediatric population (≤ 18 years), osteosarcoma was the most prevalent subtype (60.7%), followed by rhabdomyosarcoma (14.3%).This is consistent with previous research indicating that osteosarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma are typical pediatric sarcomas, with osteosarcoma exhibiting a peak incidence in adolescents and rhabdomyosarcoma being almost exclusive to children and young adults [8]. The absence of certain adult-predominant subtypes in children provides important clues for clinical differential diagnosis, helping to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary examinations. In middle-aged (41–65 years) and elderly (≥ 65 years) populations, unspecified sarcoma accounted for the highest proportion (23.0% and 36.1%, respectively), with the proportion increasing with age. This phenomenon may be attributed to the complex clinical manifestations of sarcomas in older patients, comorbidities as well as the limitations of pathological diagnosis imposed by tissue heterogeneity. Notably, liposarcoma showed a significant increasing trend with age, with the highest proportion in the elderly (27.7%), mainly including dedifferentiated or pleomorphic subtypes. This aligns with the current understanding that liposarcoma is more common in middle-aged and elderly individuals, and retroperitoneal liposarcoma is a common subtype with a poor prognosis [9]. For clinicians, patient age can serve as an initial diagnostic clue: extremity masses in young patients should raise suspicion for osteosarcoma, while retroperitoneal masses in elderly patients warrant comprehensive evaluation for liposarcoma.

Gender was identified as a significant modifier of sarcoma subtype distribution, with male patients exhibiting higher rates of liposarcoma (22.4% vs. 10.0% in females) and DFSP (9.1% vs. 3.8% in females), and female patients having a higher prevalence of leiomyosarcoma (8.5% vs. 2.4% in males) and chondrosarcoma (20.8% vs. 10.9% in males). The male predominance in liposarcoma and DFSP may be linked to gender-specific differences in adipose tissue metabolism and hormonal regulation of dermal fibroblast proliferation [8]; while the female bias in leiomyosarcoma is directly driven by uterine leiomyosarcoma, a subtype exclusive to the female reproductive system. These gender-specific patterns further refine clinical differential diagnosis, complementing age and location clues to narrow the scope of potential subtypes before definitive pathological confirmation.

Statistical analysis identified that tumor location exhibited striking anatomical specificity for sarcoma subtypes. Osteosarcomas showed an overwhelming predilection for the extremities (87.1%), predominantly the lower limbs, reflecting their origin in weight-bearing long bones [10], while DFSP was nearly exclusively confined to the body surface (85.0%), a pattern directly linked to its derivation from dermal fibroblasts [11]. Liposarcomas demonstrated a dual predilection for the retroperitoneum (42.0%) and extremities (36.0%), mirroring the widespread anatomical distribution of adipose tissue in these regions [12]. These subtype-location correlations are invaluable for preoperative imaging interpretation, enabling radiologists and clinicians to prioritize specific pathological subtypes based on anatomical site and guide targeted biopsy and diagnostic testing.

The analysis of sarcoma subtype distribution across clinical departments further revealed the close correlation between tumor anatomical location and clinical specialty division. The distribution of patients across 17 admitting departments, with Orthopedic Oncology serving as the central hub (41.0%).Orthopedic Oncology managed the vast majority of osteosarcoma cases (87.1%),while Plastic Surgery handled mostly DFSP cases. In Urology, unspecified sarcoma (44.7%) and liposarcoma (31.6%) were the main subtypes, which is consistent with the high incidence of retroperitoneal and pelvic sarcomas in this department [8]. Gynecology showed a disproportionately high share of other subtypes (47.6%) and leiomyosarcoma (28.6%), which is related to the fact that Gynecology is mainly responsible for the diagnosis and treatment of uterine and pelvic soft tissue malignancies [13]. Notably, while patients were admitted to a specific department based on initial presentation and tumor location, subsequent primary treatment responsibility may shift to other departments—such as the Department of Oncology—particularly in cases of metastatic disease or neoadjuvant chemotherapy, a critical consideration that underscores the need for interdisciplinary collaboration rather than siloed care [14]. These department-subtype clustering patterns provide a evidence-based foundation for targeted interdepartmental referral, with clear pathways for osteosarcomas to Orthopedic Oncology, DFSP to Plastic Surgery, and retroperitoneal liposarcomas to Urology or General Surgery.

A key limitation of this study is the high proportion of unspecified sarcoma (24.7%), the most prevalent subtype in the cohort, which may compromise the accuracy of subtype distribution analysis.This could potentially be attributed to two factors: (1) Only 57.6% of the patients had IHC available, rendering it impossible to accurately classify the pathological types; (2) The inadequate classification of rare subtypes, such as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, underscores the necessity for standardized pathological protocols, including Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS), to enhance subtype delineation [15]. The limited use of IHC is a major constraint, as additional diagnostic testing, in particular the NGS, as a critical tool for sarcoma subtyping, enabling the identification of highly recurrent type-specific molecular alterations that aid in definitive diagnosis and treatment decision-making [16]. Its absence in this study represents a missed opportunity for more precise subtype delineation.

This study has certain limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, this is a retrospective study based on existing clinical data, which may be subject to selection bias and incomplete data. Second, although including 295 patients, may still be relatively limited for rare sarcomas, which may affect the statistical power of some rare subtypes. Third, the study did not analysis of clinical outcomes was performed, as the primary study aim was to characterize clinicopathological and departmental distribution rather than prognostic factors.This omission limit the clinical impact of the findings.Finally, while age, gender, location, and department provide valuable clinical clues, they cannot replace definitive imaging and pathological diagnosis.This critical caveat must be emphasized to avoid over-reliance on these factors in clinical practice.

Despite these limitations, the findings of this study still have important clinical significance and practical value. The age-subtype and department-subtype distribution patterns identified in this study can help clinicians narrow down the differential diagnosis scope, and reduce unnecessary testing, and facilitate earlier referral to subspecialized teams.To translate these findings into clinical practice, the establishment of interdisciplinary sarcoma tumor boards and specialized and certified sarcoma centers are critical [17]. Those centers centralize care, standardize pathological assessment and facilitate seamless interdepartmental collaboration.

Future research directions should address the study’s limitations and enhance its clinical and scientific value.First, prospective multi-center studies with larger sample sizes are needed to verify the study, and improve pathological review and standardized IHC or NGS testing to reduce unspecified sarcoma rates and improve subtype accuracy [18]. Second, prognostic factors and treatment outcome data to explore the impact of subtype, age, location, and departmental management on patient prognosis, should be incorporated to enabling the identification of prognostic factors and the development of risk-stratified treatment strategies. Third, the development of a diagnostic nomogram or predictive model integrating clinical factors and imaging features could further enhance the practical value of these findings, providing clinicians with a quantitative tool to prioritize potential sarcoma subtypes and guide targeted diagnostic workup.

Conclusions

This study found distinct associations of sarcoma subtypes with age, location, and department, directly impacting diagnosis and treatment. Middle-aged patients predominate, with subtype-specific age and anatomical features requiring personalized care. The findings provide evidence for optimizing diagnosis, referrals, and multidisciplinary management. Despite limitations like retrospective design, small sample sizes, and lack of prognostic analysis, the study underscores the value of multidisciplinary teams and standardized pathology. Future research should focus on molecular features and prospective outcomes to improve sarcoma management.
