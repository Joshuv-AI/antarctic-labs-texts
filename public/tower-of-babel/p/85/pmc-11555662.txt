Effects of Approved Pharmacological Interventions for Insomnia on Mood Disorders: A Systematic Review
Authors: Laura Palagini, Mario Miniati, Donatella Marazziti, Dieter Riemann, Pierre A Geoffroy, Angelo Gemignani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveInsomnia can be a contributing factor, a comorbid disorder, or a transdiagnostic element to several mental disorders, including mood disorders (MDs). A recent meta-analysis has already shown the effectiveness of cognitive behavioral treatment (CBT) for insomnia that is comorbid with MDs. This work aimed to systematically review data on pharmacological insomnia treatment in the context of MDS. In agreement with the current guidelines, pharmacological interventions for insomnia include gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)A receptor agonists such as short-medium acting benzodiazepines and benzodiazepine receptor agonists – Z-drugs, melatonergic receptors agonists, specifically melatonin 2 mg Prolonged Release (PR) and ramelteon, and dual orexin receptors antagonists (DORA) such as daridorexant, lemborexant, and suvorexant.MethodA systematic search was carried out on PUBMED database, according to the PRISMA Guidelines.ResultsThirty-three papers, 15 on gabaergic receptor agonists, 14 on melatonergic receptor agonists and 4 on DORA, were selected.ConclusionsAvailable data suggests that the treatment of insomnia symptoms with specific pharmacological options can improve both insomnia and comorbid conditions. Specifically, eszopiclone and melatonin 2 mg PR have demonstrated promising outcomes. Moreover, daridorexant and suvorexant, both belonging to the DORA class, have demonstrated efficacy in treating insomnia and mood symptoms. To summarize, current literature would suggest that targeting insomnia could potentially regulate the sleep system and, as such, improve mood symptoms.Keywords: insomnia, mood disorders, hypnotics, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, melatonin 2 mg pr, daridorexant, suvorexant

1. Introduction

Mood disorders (MDs) refer to a spectrum of conditions including major depression (MDD) and bipolar disorders (BDs) that are amongst the most prevalent and severe diseases with a tendency to be recurrent, chronic and disabling (APA, 2022). The first condition is characterized by depressive episodes, while mood swings of opposite polarity from hypomanic to manic states are typical of BDs. Insomnia is considered an independent risk factor playing an important role in relapses and recurrences, associated with a more adverse course and lower response rates to antidepressant treatment (Seow et al., 2018). while being perhaps an early sign occurring before both depressive and manic episodes (Hertenstein et al., 2019; Palagini et al., 2022). Some authors hypothesize that insomnia might also act as a comorbid condition and a clinical feature that is highly represented across the entire course of MDs, with a percentage of 80%-100% during a depressive episode and 45%-55% during the bipolar inter-episodic period. Again, insomnia has been related to illness severity, cognitive dysfunctions, levels of hopelessness, low resilience, increased risk of substance use, aggressive and impulsive behaviours, emotional dysregulation, and increased suicidal risk (for an overview, see Palagini et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019; Geoffroy et al., 2021 Palagini et al., 2022).

If sleep is fundamental in maintaining brain homeostasis, enhancing brain plasticity, and promoting mental wellbeing (Aquino et al., 2024), insomnia may lead to a state of allostatic overload, thereby impairing brain plasticity, emotional, immune, and endocrine pathways, and potentially favoring MDs (Palagini et al., 2019; Hertenstein et al., 2019; Riemann et al., 2022; Palagini et al., 2022; Akkaoui et al., 2023; Aquino et al., 2023). It is noteworthy to underline that recent genetic data support the hypothesis that insomnia might even represent a causal factor in MDs (Palagini et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024).

Indeed, insomnia can be conceptualized as disorder of hyperarousal leading to central and peripheral hyperactivation of stress and the inflammatory system (Riemann et al., 2015; 2022) with consequent dysfunctions in brain neuroplasticity involved in MDs (Palagini et al 2019, 2021; Dressle et al 2022).

Recently, some lines of evidence have implicated the orexin system in the pathophysiology of insomnia (Palagini et al., 2023) and major depression as well (Fagan et al., 2022; Palagini et al., 2024), therefore, although data are limited, it is possible to hypothesize a link between dysregulation of the orexinergic pathway and pathophysiology of MDs (Carpi et al., 2024; Palagini et al., 2024).

Currently, insomnia is considered to be a transdiagnostic factor across mental disorders, and targeting insomnia may hold a preventive role in psychopathology including mood disorders. (Riemann et al., 2020; Riemann et al., 2022; Palagini et al., 2022; Palagini et al., 2024). A recent meta-analysis already showed that Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment for Insomnia (CBT-I), which is the first line treatment for chronic insomnia, is effective in treating insomnia and depressive symptoms when insomnia is comorbid with mood disorders (Riemann et al., 2023) (Hertenstein et al., 2022).

In this context, the aim of the present work was to systematically review data on pharmacological insomnia treatment in the context of MDs. According to the current guidelines, pharmacological treatments for insomnia include gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) A receptor agonists such as short-medium acting benzodiazepines (BZ) and benzodiazepine receptor agonists (BZRA) commonly referred to as Z-drugs, melatonergic receptors agonists such as melatonin 2 mg Prolonged Release (PR) and ramelteon, and dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORA) such as suvorexant lemborexant and daridorexant recently approved for insomnia treatment in Europe (Sateia et al., 2017; Riemann et al., 2023; Palagini et al., 2023).

On the contrary, sedating antidepressants such as amitriptyline, mirtazapine, trimipramine, trazodone, which are frequently employed in the clinical practice for insomnia symptoms in patients with mental disorders, are not currently recommended (Riemann et al., 2023) and will not be discussed in this work. Hence, our review exclusively encompassed approved pharmacological options for insomnia treatment and their impact on mood and insomnia symptoms within MDs.

2. Methods

We conducted a systematic search on PubMed and Scopus electronic databases for English literature published until March 2024 according to PRISMA Guidelines (Page et al., 2020). The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) scientific articles published in English only; (2) articles with cross-sectional and longitudinal approach; (3) studies examining insomnia pharmacological treatments in mood disorders measuring insomnia symptoms with validated rating scales or according to DSM criteria; (4) studies in adults. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Reviews; (2) non-original articles; and (3) studies examining other sleep disorders including sleep quality or sleep duration.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria were selected according to PICOS framework (Schardt et al., 2007). Inclusion criteria Population: All age groups of patients with insomnia and mood disorders; Intervention: A study investigating the use of pharmacological interventions for insomnia in individuals with MDs. Only the pharmacological options recommended for insomnia treatment guidelines were included; Comparison: patients in treatment for insomnia vs patients with no insomnia treatment; Outcomes: studies with primary outcomes exploring the effects of pharmacological insomnia treatment in mood disorders. Studies: Original articles on studies with a longitudinal design; prospective or retrospective, observational (analytical or descriptive), experimental or quasi-experimental, controlled or non-controlled studies; articles accepted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal, written in English. Exclusion criteria Studies involving subjects with other comorbid conditions including other sleep and mental disorders, Studies including reviews and non-original articles (i.e., case reports, editorials, letters to the editor and book chapters) and paper published not in English.

2.1 Information source and Search procedures.

PubMed database was searched, and the following queries in the ‘PubMed Advanced Search Builder’ were applied:

Search 1: ((insomnia treatment) AND (hypnotic benzodiazepines)) AND (mood disorders): 211 papers retrieved. Search 2: ((insomnia treatment) AND (z-drugs)) AND (mood disorders): Insomnia: 26 papers retrieved. Search 3: (((insomnia treatment) AND (melatonin Prolonged Release 2 mg)) AND (mood disorders) 11 papers retrieved. Search 4: (((insomnia treatment) AND (ramelteon)) AND (mood disorders)": 26 papers retrieved. Search 5: (((insomnia treatment) AND (DORA)) AND (mood disorders)": 24 papers retrieved. Forward citation searching was conducted using the “cited by” function in PubMed. Backward citation searching was performed by reviewing the reference list in identified studies. Two reviewers (LP, MM) independently screened the title, abstract, and keywords of each study identified by our search strategy and applied the inclusion criteria. Following this screen, the same procedure was applied to the full text of eligible studies. Authors were contacted to provide missing data. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers completed study selection and data extraction. The authors LP and MM manually extracted the following variables: (1) general information, (2) study methods, (3) participants, (4) insomnia and mood disorders assessment, (5) results and conclusion. Selected studies based on titles, abstracts, and full texts were retrieved and read in an independent manner.

3. Results

A total of 298 papers were retrieved, and 34 papers were selected according to inclusion and exclusion criteria (figure 1). Among these studies, 16 regarded hypnotic benzodiazepines or Z-drugs, 14 melatonin receptor agonists and 4 DORAs.

PRISMA Flow chart

Twenty studies involved patients with MDD, 16 regarded the use of hypnotic benzodiazepines and z-drugs, 2 the use of melatonin prolonged release and 2 the use of DORA suvorexant and daridorexant. One study regarded treatment resistant depression and one seasonal affective disorder (SAD). Both studies were conducted with the use of melatonin-prolonged release. Eleven studies have been carried out in BDs. One study involved manic-hypomanic patients treated with ramelteon and another with melatonin-prolonged release. Seven studies involved bipolar depression, five with melatonin prolonged release and two with DORA daridorexant. Two study using ramelteon were conducted in euthymic bipolar patients.

Three studies reported the use of hypnotic short-medium acting benzodiazepines (table 1). In one double-blind crossover-design study, 53 psychiatric outpatients were treated with triazolam and placebo in insomnia refractory to treatment with a tricyclic antidepressant (Cohn et al., 1983). Sleep measurements showed triazolam to be consistently more effective than placebo in promoting and maintaining sleep with some measures indicating improvement in anxiety and depression symptoms. In another randomized double-blind controlled trial, 81 patients with insomnia and MDD received treatment with fluoxetine 20 mg + placebo or fluoxetine 20 mg + clonazepam 0.5–1.0 mg (Londborg et al., 2000. Low-dose, short-term clonazepam added to fluoxetine was safe and effective in reducing anxiety and sleep disorder symptoms during the first three weeks of treatment, and accelerated response over 21 days of treatment. One later study from the same group confirmed the useful use of clonazepam when combined with fluoxetine (Smith et al., 2002).

In another placebo-controlled crossover trial, 38 patients with insomnia and MDD received lormetazepam 0.03 mg/kg and a placebo (Benedetti et al., 2004). Results demonstrated that active treatment significantly improved insomnia independently from the severity of depression which remained unchanged (table 1).

Thirteen publications involved Z-rugs with six on zolpidem (three publications have been conducted on the same sample), and seven on eszopiclone. Regarding zolpidem, a randomized, double-blind, parallel-group study involved men (N = 40) and women(N = 150) with MDD and persistent insomnia who received treatments with fluoxetine (< or =40 mg/day), sertraline (< or =100 mg/day), or paroxetine (< or =40 mg/day) with either placebo (N = 96) or zolpidem, 10 mg (N = 94) nightly for four weeks (Asnis et al., 1999). Zolpidem (10 mg) was effectively and safely co-administered with the SSRIs, while also resulting in improved self-rated sleep, daytime functioning, and wellbeing. After placebo substitution, the zolpidem group showed significant worsening in total sleep time and quality, as compared with both pre-treatment and first post-treatment night. In another multicenter trial), 385 patients with MDD and insomnia received open-label escitalopram 10 mg/ day, and were randomized to concomitant zolpidem extended-release 12.5 mg/night or placebo, for 24 weeks in a randomized, parallel-group (Fava et al., 2011). Zolpidem extended-release was well tolerated and improved insomnia and some next-day sleep-related symptoms and functioning, but did not significantly augment the antidepressant response of escitalopram. In a double-blind randomized controlled trial involving 103 patients with insomnia, MDD and suicidal ideation patients were randomized to either SSRI plus zolpidem extended-release (up to 12.5 mg), or SSRI plus placebo at bedtime, for 8 weeks (Mc Call et al., 2019; Rumble et al., 2020; Mc Call et al., 2023). Data showed a significant benefit of adding zolpidem-extended-release to an SSRI for both insomnia and depressive symptoms, as shown by the improvement of the Hamilton Rating Scale Depression (HRSD) suicide item (table 1).

Regarding eszopiclone, a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled parallel-group study involved 373 patients with insomnia and MDD who received morning fluoxetine and were randomized to nightly eszopiclone three mg or placebo for eight weeks (Fava et al., 2006). The eszopiclone/fluoxetine co-therapy was relatively well tolerated and associated with rapid, substantial, and sustained sleep improvement, a faster onset of antidepressant response and a greater magnitude of the antidepressant effect. These data were confirmed in four subsequent randomized trials conducted in populations with insomnia comorbid with MDD and involving more than 1600 patients taking eszopiclone and an SSRI, while concluding that this strategy was effective in improving both insomnia and depressive symptoms (Snedecor et al., 2010; Mc Call et al., 2010; Fava et al., 2011; Krystal et al., 2012). Other randomized controlled studies conducted on eszopiclone in insomnia and MDD showed that eszopiclone discontinuation did not result in significant withdrawal symptoms, rebound insomnia, or rebound depression, and improvements in sleep and depressive symptoms were maintained (Krystal et al., 2007). In another randomized, controlled trial, 18 patients with MDD with insomnia receiving benzodiazepine treatment for more than two weeks were randomized to four weeks of suvorexant (20 or 15 mg/day) or eszopiclone (3 or 2 mg/day). Patients taking suvorexant or eszopiclone improved insomnia symptoms (−4.3 for suvorexant and −4.1 for eszopiclone) at week 4). Both drugs also tended to improve depressive symptoms in the 2nd and 4th weeks after switching. Switching to suvorexant or eszopiclone was well tolerated and improved the severity of benzodiazepine-unresponsive insomnia in MDD patients (Shigetsura et al., 2022) (table 1).

Globally, these data suggest that targeting insomnia in MDD with benzodiazpeines and Z-drugs can lead to improvements in both insomnia and potentially even depressive symptoms. However, it is important to note that there are currently limited studies on hypnotic benzodiazepines, and the available data cannot be considered conclusive. On the other hand, more studies have been conducted on Z-drugs. Specifically, three studies have been carried out with zolpidem showing that its co-administration with an SSRI can improve insomnia symptoms. However, information on the effects of this co-administration on depressive symptoms remains elusive. Notably, one study found that the effects of zolpidem prolonged release, when co-administered with an SSRI, improved both insomnia and suicidal ideation in patients with insomnia, MDD and suicidal ideation (Mc Call et al., 2019; Rumble et al., 2020; Mc Call et al., 2023). Eszopiclone has the most evidence supporting its effectiveness in treating insomnia and depressive symptoms when combined with an SSRI in patients with MDD. Five randomized controlled trials, involving nearly 2000 patients, demonstrated that the co-administration with an SSRI led to a quicker onset of antidepressant response and a more significant antidepressant effect (Fava et al., 2006; Snedecor et al., 2010; Mc Call et al., 2010; Fava et al., 2011; Krystal et al., 2012). Interestingly, discontinuing eszopiclone did not result in notable withdrawal symptoms, and rebound insomnia or depression. Additionally, eszopiclone proved to be beneficial for treating insomnia and depressive symptoms when switching from BDs’ treatment (Kristal et al., 2007; Shigetsura et al., 2022). When compared with zolpidem or other sedative hypnotics, eszopiclone showed stronger evidence of efficacy in treating insomnia and mood symptoms in patients with insomnia and co-morbid depressive symptoms.

No studies have been conducted in BDs or other subtypes of depression involving sedative-hypnotics as a treatment for insomnia.

In unipolar depression, a randomized study was conducted assessing the effect of 5-10 mg of PR melatonin) combined with fluoxetine 20 mg which led to an improvement in insomnia symptoms (Dolberg et al., 1998). A small open-label trial involved nine patients with resistant depression at a tertiary care-outpatient depression clinic. Patients received PR melatonin 5 mg per day for the first 2 weeks and 10 mg per day for the final 2 weeks, in addition to their antidepressant medication. The findings a 20% mean decrease in HRSD scores after 4 weeks of treatment, and a 36% decrease in the HRSD-3-item related to insomnia. The greatest decrease in insomnia occurred during the last 2 weeks of the study, following the increase in dosage to 10 mg per day of PR-melatonin. Patients also reported significantly lower levels of fatigue post-treatment. Although PR-melatonin seems to be a useful adjunct for sleep, it does not substantially augment existing antidepressant efficacy in some patients with treatment- resistant depression (Dalton et al., 2000).

Leppämäki et al. (2003) conducted a randomized controlled trial in 58 patients with seasonal affective disorder (SAD) and/or the negative or positive type of weather-associated syndrome (WAS). The patients were randomised to either 2 mg of PR melatonin or placebo tablets 1-2 h before a desired bedtime for 3 weeks. The study revealed that melatonin 2 mg PR administration significantly improved the quality of sleep and vitality in the subjects with SAD. A subsequent (double-blind RCT) study tested 6 mg of PR melatonin as adjuvant to antidepressant treatments, in 31 patients with insomnia, and a decrease in depressive symptoms and in sleep disturbances as was shown (Serfaty et al., 2010). In a 6-week multi-centre double-blind randomized placebo-and comparator-controlled study, the combination of buspirone and melatonin 3 mg PR in subjects with acute MDD was investigated (Fava et al., 2012). The combination revealed a significant antidepressant response in subjects with MDD on several measures, as compared to either placebo or buspirone 15 mg monotherapy. In another randomized double-blind parallel-group, 3-week comparison of PR 2 mg melatonin (n = 21) vs. placebo (n = 20) was studied in patients with BD of type I or II. However, no significant difference was detected between melatonin and placebo on the primary outcome, namely manic symptom (Quested et al., 2020).

Five studies were carried out on the efficacy of PR melatonin 2 mg benzodiazepines and antipsychotics discontinuation, Romo-Nava (2014) conducted an 8-week double-blind randomized placebo-controlled parallel-group clinical trial, with 44 patients treated with second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) (20 with BDs, and 24 with schizophrenia) who randomly received placebo (n = 24) or melatonin 5 mg PR (n = 20). Results showed that melatonin was effective in attenuating adverse metabolic effects of SGAs, particularly in patients with BDs. Baandrup et al. (2016a; 2016b; 2016c; 2017) examined the efficacy of PR melatonin 2 mg on objective and subjective sleep quality, during BZ discontinuation, in subsamples of patients with schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or BD who were long-term users of benzodiazepines in combination with antipsychotics. PR melatonin 2 mg significantly improved self-reported sleep quality, and benzodiazepines’ discontinuation was not associated with rebound insomnia.

Currently, in our knowledge only three studies investigated ramelteon in MDs .McElroy et al. (2011) conducted a 8-week, double-blind randomized controlled trial in 21 outpatients with BDI, manic symptoms and sleep disturbance who were randomized to receive either ramelteon (N=10) or placebo (N=11) in fixed dose (8 mg/day). Ramelteon and placebo had similar rates of reduction in ratings of symptoms of insomnia, mania, and global severity of illness. In another study, Norris et al. (2013) evaluated ramelteon effect vs. placebo in 83 euthymic BD and sleep disturbances patients. Ramelteon was administered in addition to their regular psychiatric medications for up to 24 weeks or until they experienced a relapse. Participants who received ramelteon were less likely to relapse (odds ratio 0.48, p=.024). Another randomized trial (Mahableshwarkar et al., 2017) was conducted in BDI patients, randomized to once-daily ramelteon in different sublingual formulations, in addition to their existing treatment. No significant differences between any dose of ramelteon sublingual formulation and placebo were observed (table 2).

To summarize, melatonin 2-10 PR could potentially serve as an adjunctive therapy for insomnia symptoms in patients with MDs, specifically unipolar/bipolar depression. Combining it with antidepressants may also prove beneficial in alleviating depressive symptoms. However, there is currently limited data on its effects on manic symptoms. Notably, some studies explored the use of immediate release formulations of melatonin for insomnia treatment and observed antimanic effects (for a comprehensive review, see Palagini et al., 2021). Additionally, the administration of 2 mg PR melatonin may aid in discontinuing sedative-hypnotics in mood disorders, and enhance sleep quality during this process (refer to table 2). As for ramelteon, conflicting findings exist, necessitating further research with larger sample sizes.

3.3 Dual Orexin Receptor Antagonists (DORA)

Few studies examined the effects of DORAs on insomnia comorbid with MDs. Four studies have been selected according to inclusion exclusion/criteria (table 3) (Kishi et al., 2019; Shigetsura et al., 2022; Palagini et al., 2024a; 2024b). The efficacy of suvorexant has been studied in patients with MDD and insomnia, in a randomized controlled trial reporting an improvement in both insomnia and depressive symptoms during benzodiazpeines discontinuation (Shigetsura et al., 2022). In another study carried out in 57 psychiatric outpatients reporting insomnia symptoms a significant improvement was reported in self-reported sleep quality, duration, and continuity, after one week of treatment with suvorexant 15 mg or 20 mg/day. In a naturalistic study, conducted in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid unipolar and bipolar depression, daridorexant 50 mg significantly reduced insomnia symptoms. Moreover, improvement in depressive and bipolar mixed symptoms after 1 month and 3 months of treatment were observed. Interestingly, suicidal risk also improved in patients with insomnia comorbid with MDs (Palagini et al., 2024a). A similar study was conducted in a larger sample from the same group (Palagini et al., 2024b). Repeated ANOVA analyses showed that insomnia symptoms, dysfunctional beliefs about sleep and pre-sleep arousal improved across times (respectively F=68.818, p<0.001, F=47.561; p<0.001, F=28.142; p<0.001). Similarly, depressive/manic symptoms and suicidal risk significantly decreased over time. Multiple-regression models showed that clinical improvement of depressive symptoms was best predicted by improvement in dysfunctional beliefs about sleep after 1 and 3 months of treatment; manic symptoms were best predicted by the improvement of insomnia symptoms at T2.

The overall findings regarding the impact of DORAs on insomnia symptoms within the realm of MDs seem quite promising. Specifically, suvorexant and daridorexant have shown potential in enhancing mood symptoms, when utilized for insomnia management. In particular, daridorexant demonstrated effectiveness in improving both insomnia and mood symptoms in individuals with unipolar and bipolar depression, while also exhibiting a positive influence on manic symptoms. In addition, both suvorexant and daridorexant could prove beneficial during benzodiazepines switching or discontinuation in patients with insomnia and mood disorders.

4. Discussion

There are several possible mechanisms through which insomnia might increase the risk or perpetuation of MDs. Insomnia is characterized by hyperarousal, which involves the overactivation of stress and inflammatory systems, along with dysregulation in GABAergic, glutamatergic, orexinergic, and circadian systems (Palagini et al., 2022), all factors playing a role in the impairment of brain neuroplasticity. Addressing insomnia within the context of MDs might be of help to regulate multiple systems linked to MDs. Currently, pharmacological drugs approved for insomnia treatment are agonists of sleep promoting systems acting on GABAA receptors and melatonergic receptors or antagonizing arousal or promoting orexin system. Similarly to CBT-I (Hertenstein et al., 2022), data indicated that co-treatment of insomnia symptoms with these pharmacological options may improve both insomnia and mood symptoms.

In particular, among GABAA receptor modulators, eszopiclone was the most studied compound. It is emerging that the co-administration of eszopiclone with SSRIs received more evidence compared to zolpidem or other sedative hypnotics on effectiveness on insomnia and depressive symptoms. Totally five randomized controlled trials involving almost 2000 patients showed that the co-administration with SSRI was related to a faster onset of antidepressant response and a greater magnitude of the antidepressant effect (Fava et al., 2006; Snedecor et al., 2010; Mc Call et al., 2010; Fava et al., 2011; Krystal et al., 2012). Interestingly, eszopiclone discontinuation did not result in significant withdrawal symptoms, rebound insomnia, or rebound depression. Since chronic insomnia may be related to alterations in GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmission, GABAA receptor modulators may be able to target sleep architecture and related brain neuronal dysfunction (Palagini & Bianchini, 2023), that have been also implicated in MDs (Brambilla et al., 2003; Della Vecchia and Marazziti, 2022; Thompson, 2024) As such, it can be hypothesized that GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmission might be one of the potential neurobiological pathways linking insomnia to MDs (Palagini et al., 2019) and that GABAA receptor modulators might improve mood symptoms through sleep regulation. Among the GABAA receptor modulators, eszopiclone, which is the S-isomer of racemic zopiclone, exhibits a distinct binding profile. Eszopiclone modulates the receptor complex in a unique manner, with its affinity for the GABA receptor 1 subunit, as well as its binding to the 2 and 3 subunits. This suggests the possibility that eszopiclone has both hypnotic and anxiolytic effects (Greenblatt & Zammit, 2012). Animal studies suggest that, among Z-drugs, eszopiclone is the only one that does not impair sleep-dependent consolidation plasticity (Aton et al., 2009) and may enhance the expression of brain plasticity-related genes (Gerashchenko et al., 2017). Eszopiclone was also shown to enhance adult hippocampal neurogenesis, while suggesting that probably this effect is due to its activity as GABA receptor agonist, (Methippara et al., 2010). Animal studies suggest that eszopiclone may attenuate stress-induced anxiety-like behaviours in animals (for an overview, see Palagini & Bianchini, 2023).

Changes in melatonin secretion have been observed in major unipolar depression; depression includes lower nocturnal melatonin levels, phase advance of melatonin onset or peak, a delay in the peak, onset, or offset of melatonin secretion (Geoffroy & Palagini, 2021). A “low melatonin syndrome” was proposed for both depression and insomnia (Geoffroy & Palagini 2021; Palagini et al., 2021). In this case, the exogeneous administration of melatonin may help to stabilize mood, the sleep-wake cycle disorders, and to prevent psychiatric relapse and recurrence, with an ensuing activity on brain neuroprotection. (Geoffroy & Palagini, 2021; Palagini et al., 2021; Biggio et al., 2021). It is possible that higher doses of melatonin might be needed in patients with MDs compared to patients with insomnia disorder, depending on polymorphisms of circadian clock genes or mutations in melatonin pathway-related genes and receptors (Palagini et al., 2022). Indeed, when administered exogenously, melatonin exhibits the identical sleep-inducing and phase-shifting properties as melatonin. Melatonin has been demonstrated to induce sleep by modulating the GABAA-benzodiazepine receptor complex with a suppression of neuronal activity. Melatonin has a chronobiotic effect due to the presence of melatonin receptor agonists M1 and M2 in the brain Therefore, exogenous melatonin possesses numerous properties, including a sleep-promoting effect known as "soporific" and a synchronization effect known as "chronobiotic" (Geoffrey & Palagini, 2021; Palagini et al., 2022; Maruani et al., 2023). It is possible that melatonin 2-10 PR may regulate mood via soporific and “chronobiotic” effects on sleep.

As dysregulation in orexins has been linked to hyperarousal in insomnia and to alterations in the mesocorticolimbic dopamine circuit in depression, the DORAs suvorexant, lemborexant, and the new one daridorexant may possess promising potentialities (Palagini et al., 2023; Palagini et al., 2024). The efficacy of suvorexant, assessed in patients with unipolar major depression and that of daridorexant in unipolar and bipolar depression has demonstrated an improvement in insomnia symptoms, mood symptoms and suicidal risk as well (table 3). Both suvorexant and daridorexant were also useful for insomnia and depressive symptoms when switching from sedative-hypnotics, and the discontinuation was not associated with rebound insomnia or rebound depression. In particular, daridorexant has been used to treat insomnia in unipolar and bipolar depression and it may be useful for improving sleep and mood as well when co-administered with antidepressants and mood stabilizers.

These data support hypotheses about the potential role of sleep and orexin dysregulation in MDs and suicidal risk (Salvo et al., 2023; Palagini et al., 2023; Carpi et al., 2024; Palagini et al., 2024).

5. Conclusions

To summarize, insomnia is a risk factor for MDs with the potential to have a detrimental effect on their course, by increasing the likelihood of suicide and deteriorating the clinical presentation. Conversely, addressing insomnia can be a key strategy in managing mood disorders. While previous research demonstrated the effectiveness of CBT-I in treating insomnia and depressive symptoms in individuals with comorbid mood disorders (Hertenstein et al., 2022), this systematic review is the first to focus on pharmacological treatments for insomnia in the context of mood disorders. Similar to CBT-I (Hertenstein et al., 2022), overall data suggest that using certain pharmacological options approved for insomnia treatment alongside insomnia symptoms may lead to improvements in both insomnia and mood symptoms. Currently, pharmacological treatments approved for insomnia target sleep-promoting systems by acting on GABAA receptors and melatonergic receptors, or by inhibiting the arousal-promoting orexin system (figure 2). Notably, there are growing data in support the use of eszopiclone, melatonin 2 mg PR, daridorexant and suvorexant in treating insomnia in individuals with MDs potentially improving both sleep and mood.

While GABAA receptor modulators have been tested mostly in unipolar major forms of depression, melatonin 2 mg PR and daridorexant have also shown promise in managing bipolar depression, potentially improving both sleep and mood. By modulating these dysfunctional sleep neurotransmissions, it might be possible to enhance mood symptoms through the regulation of the sleep system. However, currently there are no sufficient data to suggest guidelines for treating insomnia during manic or hypomanic phases of mood disorders. This is a gap in the literature that requires to be deepened in future studies.
