Magnetic fluorine-functionalized covalent organic framework coupled with ambient mass spectrometry for rapid determination of benzoylurea insecticides in fruits and vegetables
Authors: Yaonan Liu, Yue Yang, Jing Zhang, Zhenxia Du, Yan Tang, Luhong Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstablishing a convenient and sensitive method for the enrichment and analysis of benzoylurea insecticides (BUs) in fruits and vegetables is essential for food safety monitoring. To this end, a novel fluorine-rich magnetic covalent organic framework (Fe3O4@COF-F) was designed and synthesized as an adsorbent in magnetic dispersion solid phase extraction (MDSPE), enabling efficient capture of BUs through synergistic interactions. This material exhibited rapid adsorption (4 min) and high adsorption capacities (150.1–163.1 mg g−1). By coupling Fe3O4@COF-F-based MDSPE with pulsed direct current electrospray ionization (PDESI), an ambient mass spectrometry (AMS), a rapid approach was established for the determination of BUs in cucumbers, cabbages, and apples. The proposed MDSPE-PDESI-MS approach demonstrated good linearity (r > 0.995), low limits of detection (LODs) of 0.16–0.30 ng g−1, and satisfactory spiked recoveries (80.56–93.98%). This method shows great potential for high-throughput and sensitive analysis of BUs in complex food matrices.Keywords: Benzoylurea insecticides, Magnetic dispersion solid phase extraction, Covalent organic framework, Ambient ionization mass spectrometry, Fruit and vegetable samples

Introduction

Benzoylurea insecticides (BUs), a class of insect chitin synthesis inhibitors, are highly effective for pest control due to their characteristics of high selectivity, broad spectrum and low environmental persistence (Mu et al., 2023). However, excessive application of BUs has led to their widespread presence in environmental media and agricultural products, thereby increasing the risk of human exposure through the food chain (Song et al., 2023). Consequently, international regulations have been established to restrict the improper use of BUs by establishing maximum residue limit (MRLs) for food commodities. As stipulated the National Food Safety Standard (Zang et al., 2022), the MRLs of BUs in fruits and vegetables range from 0.5 to 5 mg kg−1. Therefore, it is crucial to develop accurate and sensitive detection method for BUs in food samples.

Accurate detection for BUs primarily relies on high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled with various detectors, owing to its strong qualitative and quantitative capabilities as well as high sensitivity (Feng et al., 2022). However, conventional HPLC-based methods are associated with lengthy column separation and laborious sample pretreatment, resulting in significant time consumption. These limitations hinder their applicability for rapid, real-time detection. Ambient mass spectrometry (AMS) offers a promising alternative by enabling direct sample analysis in open-air environments, offering advantages such as in situ capability, minimal preparation requirements, and high analytical speed (Shen et al., 2026). Among the diverse AMS techniques, pulsed direct current electrospray ionization (PDESI) is an innovative form of ESI that uses a dc power supply to generate pulsed electrospray, thereby offering higher sample economy. This technique shows significant potential for trace-level sample analysis and is widely applied in food safety monitoring and biological sample analysis (Liu et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2015). Given the inherent complexity of food matrices and the typically low concentrations of BUs residues, appropriate sample pretreatment is essential prior to instrument analysis to achieve effective analytes enrichment and accurate detection.

Current sample preparation methods for the detection of BUs primarily include QuEChERS, solid-phase extraction (SPE), dispersive solid-phase extraction (dSPE), and magnetic dispersive solid-phase extraction (MDSPE) (Kim et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Sasano et al., 2024). Among these, MDSPE has attracted significant attention for the pretreatment of complex matrices owing to its operational simplicity, time efficiency, and high extraction performance (Okhravi et al., 2024). To further enhance extraction effectiveness, the development of advanced sorbent materials with high adsorption capacity has become a major research focus in MDSPE. Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are a class of crystalline porous polymers formed by covalent linking of lightweight elements. Owing to their highly tunable functionality, designable ordered structure, and excellent stability, COFs have emerged as highly promising adsorbent materials (Ahmed et al., 2024; Talekar et al., 2024). In particular, fluorinated COFs exhibit a strong affinity toward fluorous compounds through specific fluorine-based interactions. Given the abundance of fluorine atoms in BUs, developing fluorine-functionalized COFs offers a feasible strategy for the selective enrichment of BUs (Sun et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025). Although COFs modified with tetrafluorinated ligands have been reported to exhibit favorable selectivity toward BUs, their overall adsorption performance requires further improvement (Su et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2022). It is expected that increasing the fluorine content in COFs will significantly enhance adsorption capacity for BUs in complex food matrices. Additionally, the bottom-up approach for synthesizing fluorine-rich COFs allows for precise functionalization through rational monomer design, ensuring uniform distribution of functional groups and a simplified synthesis process, in contrast to common post-modification strategies. Therefore, this study aims to develop highly fluorinated COFs as an efficient adsorbent through a facile strategy.

In this work, a novel magnetic COFs (Fe3O4@COF-F) was synthesized through a facile approach using 2,2′-bis (trifluoromethyl)benzidine (TFMB) and 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxaldehyde (Tb) as building monomers. The obtained Fe3O4@COF-F was applied as an adsorbent for MDSPE to selectively enrich BUs in fruits and vegetables. Key parameters influencing the MDSPE efficiency were systematically optimized, and the adsorption behavior of BUs on Fe3O4@COF-F was comprehensively investigated. Furthermore, in combined with PDESI-MS, a rapid and sensitive analytical method based on Fe3O4@COF-F MDSPE was proposed for the analysis of four BUs in cucumbers, cabbages, and apples (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

Ferric trichloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), 2,2′-bis (trifluoromethyl)benzidine (TFMB), and 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxaldehyde (Tb) were all obtained from Macklin chemicals (Shanghai, China). Benzidine (BD), sodium acrylate, sodium acetate (NaOAc), ethylene glycol (EG) and acetic acid (HAc) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HPLC-grade methanol (MeOH) and acetonitrile (ACN) were purchased from TEDIA Company (Fairfield, OH, USA). Acetone (AC) and anhydrous ethanol (EtOH) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemicals used were of analytical grade unless otherwise mentioned. Benzoylurea insecticides standards including diflubenzuron (DFU), teflubenzuron (TFZ), hexaflumuron (HFU), and novaluron (NVU) were obtained from the Tan-Mo Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China).

Instruments

The mass spectrometry analysis of BUs was performed on a commercial linear ion trap mass spectrometer (Scientific Finnigan LTQ, Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) equipped with an ambient PDESI ion source (Hua Yi Ning Chuang, Ningbo, China). Collision-induced dissociation (CID) experiments were conducted to identify characteristic product ions for the quantification of each BUs. PDESI source was employed in negative ion mode with a spray voltage of 3.0 kV. The operating conditions of LTQ were set as follows: tubular lens voltage of 120 V, capillary temperature of 275 °C, multipole RF amplitude (Vp-p) of 400 V, and multiplier voltages 1 and 2 of −1200 V. The ion injection time and ionization time was adjusted to 100 ms and 0.3 min, respectively. The detailed parameters of BUs were summarized in Table S1 and Fig. S1.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted on a SU-70 instrument (Hitachi, Japan) and Tecnai F20 instrument (FEI, USA), respectively. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Cu Kα radiation) were obtained using a powder diffractometer (SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan). Fourier transform-infrared spectra (FT-IR) was examined using a Nicolet™ iS20 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) tests were conducted on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha equipment (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The specific surface area and pore size distribution were explored by an ASAP 2460 surface area analyzer (Micromeritics, USA) using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. Magnetic hysteresis loops were measured using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM, LakeShore 7404, USA). The hydrophobicity of the prepared materials was characterized by water contact angle measurements on a JY-82C instrument (China).

Synthesis experiments

Preparation of the Fe3O4 nanoparticles. Carboxylic-modified ferric oxide nanoparticles (Fe3O4-COOH) were prepared using a solvothermal method according to our previous work (Liu et al., 2025).

Preparation of Fe3O4@COF-F. The Fe3O4@COF-F was synthesized according to previously reported methods with modifications (Ma et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2021). Briefly, 60 mg of Fe3O4 was dispersed into 3 mL of ACN/EtOH (7,3, v/v) and sonicated for 15 min. Subsequently, TFMB (0.225 mmol, 72 mg) was added to the above uniform solution, followed by ultrasonication for an additional 20 min to ensure complete dissolution. The obtained mixture was stirred mechanically at 500 rpm for 2 h at room temperature to ensure successful pre-grafting of TFMB on Fe3O4. Then, Tb (0.15 mmol, 24.3 mg) was added to the above mixture, followed by the dropwise addition of 900 μL aqueous HAc solution (12 M). After stirring for 24 h, the powder was collected by magnetic separation, sequentially washed with MeOH and ACN until the supernatant was clear, and finally dried overnight at 60 °C. For comparison, Fe3O4@COF was synthesized following a similar procedure to that describe above, except that the monomer of TFMB was replaced with BD.

Sample preparation

A total of forty-five conventional fruit and vegetable samples, including cucumbers, cabbages, and apples, were purchased from local supermarkets and retail stores in Ningbo, China. The detailed information of all sample information is presented in Table S5. The samples were finely chopped and homogenized using a laboratory homogenizer. To optimally balance method sensitivity and matrix interference, 10.0 g of the homogenate was weighed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube. After centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was collected and diluted to a final volume of 10 mL with water for analysis.

MDSPE procedure

A 50 mL centrifuge tube containing Fe3O4@COF-F (2 mg) and the above sample solution (10 mL) was vortexed for 4 min. The Fe3O4@COF-F adsorbent was separated from the solution using an external magnet. 2 mL of ultrapure water was used to remove potential matrix components adsorbed on the adsorbent surface. Then, 2 mL of methanol was added to the centrifuge tube, and the target analytes were desorbed from Fe3O4@COF-F by ultrasonication for 10 min. The resulting desorption solvent was collected and dried under a nitrogen stream at 30 °C. Finally, the residue was redissolved in 300 μL of acetonitrile and filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE-Q membrane prior to subsequent analysis.

Method validation

Standard stock solutions (10 μg/mL) of BUs were prepared in acetonitrile and stored at 4 °C. The matrix matching calibration curve of the established MDSPE method was evaluated by analyzing BUs in spiked blank samples. The sensitivity of the method was evaluated by the limit of detection (LOD) and the limit of quantification (LOQ), which were calculated as 3*Stdblk/M and 10*Stdblk/M (Stdblk: the standard deviation of the signal of the blank sample, M: the slope of the calibration curves at low concentration), respectively. The intra-day and inter-day precisions were assessed by analyzing spiked samples in six replicates per day over six consecutive days. The matrix effect was evaluated by comparing the signals of the post-extraction spiked blank matrix (5 ng g−1) to that of a standard solution at the same concentration. Accuracy was assessed via recovery experiments using samples spiked at three concentration levels.

Results and discussion

Characterization of Fe3O4@COF-F

Fe3O4@COF-F with a core-shell structure were synthesized through Schiff base condensation reaction. The morphologies and structures of Fe3O4 and Fe3O4@COF-F were characterized using SEM and TEM. As shown in Fig. 2a, the Fe3O4 exhibited a relatively regular spherical morphology with a certain degree of aggregation. The SEM image of Fe3O4@COF-F (Fig. 2b) revealed a nearly spherical morphology with a coarse surface texture, indicating the successful formation of a COF layers (Ma et al., 2023). The TEM images of Fe3O4@COF-F clearly revealed the core-shell structure of the material. Meanwhile, EDS elemental mapping further confirmed that the successfully coating of the Fe3O4 core with a COF shell (Fig. 2c).

The crystal structures of Fe3O4 and Fe3O4@COF-F were characterized using XRD. As shown in Fig. S2, in the XRD pattern of Fe3O4@COF-F exhibited a distinct diffraction peak at 3.3°, consistent with the characteristic structure of COF-F as reported in the literature (Ma et al., 2023). The characteristic diffraction peaks at 30.1°, 35.5°, 43.1°, 53.4°, 57.0°, and 62.1° correspond to those of Fe3O4, indicating that the crystalline structure of Fe3O4 was well preserved after the COF coating (Zeng et al., 2024).

The chemical structure of Fe3O4@COF-F was characterized using FT-IR (Fig. 2d). The peak observed at 580 cm−1 was attributed to the Fe-O-Fe stretching vibration of Fe3O4, and the adsorption peak at 1410 cm−1 was assigned to the vibration of the C—F bond. These spectral features confirmed the successful formation of an imine bond through Schiff base condensation, demonstrating the successful synthesis of the material (Ma et al., 2023).

XPS analysis was conducted to investigate the surface chemical composition and element constituents of Fe3O4@COF-F. As shown in Fig. 2e, the survey spectrum confirmed the presence of C, N, O, and F elements. The C 1 s spectrum exhibited three peaks at 284.8 eV, 286.5 eV, and 288.5 eV, which were attributed to C-C/C=C, C-N/C=N, and C—F bond, respectively (Fig. S3a). The F 1 s spectrum displayed a characteristic peak at 686.8 eV, corresponding to the C—F bond (Fig. S3b) (Yu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). These characteristic peaks further confirm the successful synthesis of Fe3O4@COF-F.

N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm experiment was conducted to characterize the porous properties of Fe3O4@COF-F. As shown in Fig. S3c, the BET surface area was calculated to be 173.5 m2 g−1, with an average pore size of 3.8 nm. The saturation magnetization values of Fe3O4 were 58.28 emu g−1 and decreased after COF coating (Xu et al., 2021). Fe3O4@COF-F dispersed in the solution can be rapidly separated within 5 s under the aid of an external magnet, demonstrating its high magnetic performance (Fig. 2f). As shown in Fig. S3d, the water contact angles of Fe3O4 and Fe3O4@COF-F are 19.8° and 102.8°, respectively. The enhancement of the hydrophobicity could be attributed to the introduced COF layer, which facilitates the adsorption of BUs (He et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2020).

MDSPE procedure optimization

To achieve optimal MDSPE performance for the four BUs, key parameters were systematically optimized, including eluent solvent type and volume, solution pH, salt concentration, adsorbent dosage, adsorption and desorption time. The optimization was performed based on the extraction recovery rates of the BUs from samples at a spiked level of 50 ng/mL.

The selection of an optimal eluent solvent is critical for achieving efficient desorption of the analytes from the adsorbent. To evaluate the performance of different desorption solvent, several commonly used solvents were evaluated, including methanol (MeOH), acetonitrile (ACN), acetone (AC), methanol containing 1% formic acid (1% FA-MeOH), and mixed organic solvents (MeOH: ACN, 1:1). As shown in Fig. 3a, methanol exhibited the highest desorption efficiency for the target analytes. Therefore, methanol was chosen as the eluent solvent for subsequent experiments.

Optimization of sample pretreatment methods (n = 3): (a) eluent solvent; (b) pH; (c) salt dosage; (d) adsorbent dosage; (e) adsorption time; (f) desorption time; and (g) desorption solvent volume.

The sample pH influences extraction efficiency by altering both the chemical state of the analytes and the surface characteristics of the adsorbent. The effect of pH ranging from 3 to 11 on extraction recovery rates was investigated. As shown in Fig. 3b, the extraction recovery rates increased as the pH values rose from 3 to 7, but decreased under more alkaline conditions. This phenomenon may be attributed to the instability of BUs under alkaline conditions, resulting in their transformation into acid and amide (Wang et al., 2014). Given that the sample solution pH was approximately neutral, further experiments were conducted without pH adjustment.

Ionic strength can significantly affect extraction efficiency through both salting-out and competitive adsorption. The effect of ionic strength on the extraction efficiency was evaluated by adjusting the NaCl concentration (0–8 wt%). As shown in Fig. 3c, the extraction recovery rates decreased as the NaCl concentration increased, which may be attributed to competitive adsorption becoming the dominant mechanism over the salting-out effect (Tu et al., 2024). Thus, subsequent experiments were conducted without the addition of NaCl.

The amount of adsorbent is a critical factor influencing the MDSPE extraction efficiency. An optimal dosage is essential to achieve effective adsorption while minimizing material consumption (Guo et al., 2021). In this study, the effect of adsorbent dosage was evaluated at 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 mg. As shown in Fig. 3d, the extraction recovery rates increased as adsorbent dosage rose from 1 to 2 mg, suggesting a greater availability of adsorption sites. Further increasing the dosage to 8 mg caused no obvious change, indicating that the adsorption sites had become sufficient to capture the target analytes and equilibrium was reached. Therefore, 2 mg of the adsorbent was selected for subsequent experiments.

Adsorption and desorption time are also key parameters affecting MDSPE efficiency. To optimize the extraction process, the effect of extraction time (0–10 min) on the extraction efficiency was investigated. As shown in Fig. 3e, the recovery rate increased gradually with extended extraction time and reached the adsorption equilibrium at 4 min. Interestingly, a slight decline in adsorption efficiency was observed between 6 and 10 min, which may be attributed to the escape of target analytes from the adsorbent caused by temperature rise during prolonged vibration process (Yang et al., 2016). Therefore, 4 min was chosen as the optimal absorption time. The effect of different desorption time on recovery rates was also investigated. As shown in Fig. 3f, a 10-min desorption time was sufficient to achieve efficient elution of the target BUs.

The effect of elution volume was further investigated in the range of 1–5 mL. As shown in Fig. 3g, the recovery rates increased with the elution volume up to 2 mL. Further increases adversely affected the recovery rates, most likely due to analytes loss caused by the extended nitrogen blowing time. Therefore, 2 mL was selected as the optimal volume of desorption solvent.

In summary, the optimal conditions were determined as follows: 2 mg of adsorbent, 4 min extraction, 2 mL MeOH as eluent, 10 min desorption, and with no pH or NaCl adjustment.

Reusability and reproducibility of Fe3O4@COF-F

The reusability and reproducibility of an adsorbent are critical factors in assessing its practical application (Zhang et al., 2024). In this study, the Fe3O4@COF-F adsorbent was rinsed twice with methanol to remove residual analytes before the next cycle. As shown in Fig. S4, the extraction recovery rates remained consistent over six consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles, demonstrating excellent reusability and potential for practical applications. To evaluate the reproducibility of Fe3O4@COF-F preparation, six batches of Fe3O4@COF-F materials were synthesized and applied for the MDSPE of BUs. The resulting RSDs were below 5.08%, indicating the reliability and consistency of this preparation process (Table S2).

Adsorption mechanism

Adsorption isotherm

The adsorption performance of Fe3O4@COF-F for BUs was evaluated through static adsorption experiments at concentrations ranging from 1 to 70 mg/L. As shown in Fig. S5, the adsorption capacities of the four BUs increased significantly with initial concentration and reached equilibrium at 30 mg/L. The maximum capacities of DFU, TFZ, HFU, and NVU were 150.1, 161.0, 163.1 and 160.8 mg g−1, respectively, demonstrating the substantial adsorption capacity of Fe3O4@COF-F for these targets. To gain further insight into the adsorption process, the isothermal behavior between Fe3O4@COF-F and BUs was analyzed using both the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models (Wang & Liu, 2014). The corresponding parameters were summarized in Table S3. The Langmuir model (0.9625 < R2 < 0.9692) provided a better fit to the experimental data than the Freundlich model (0.8915 < R2 < 0.9011), suggesting monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface rather than multilayer adsorption.

Possible interactions

The possible adsorption mechanism of Fe3O4@COF-F for BUs was investigated through material characterizations and experimental analysis. FT-IR and XPS analyses were performed on Fe3O4@COF-F before and after BUs adsorption to elucidate the underlying interactions (Fig. 4). The FT-IR peak shift of the C—F stretching vibration from 1005 cm−1 to 1011 cm−1 indicated fluorine-related interactions (e.g., F—F, F—O, F-π interactions), arising from the structural features of the BUs containing fluorine, oxygen atoms, and benzene rings (Wang et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2022). XPS F 1 s spectra showed the binding energy of the C—F bond exhibited a significant shift from 686.8 eV to 687.6 eV after adsorption. Furthermore, a new peak emerged at 689.3 eV, which was assigned to F···F, suggesting the possible formation of fluorine‑fluorine interaction between the BUs and Fe3O4@COF-F (Ran et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). This was further supported by a corresponding shift of the C—F bond in the C 1 s spectrum to a binding energy of 288.7 eV. Such shifts also suggested hydrogen bonding interaction due to the strong electronegativity of fluorine atoms (Mao et al., 2019). In addition to these chemical interactions, structural and physicochemical properties also contributed significantly to the adsorption. The average pore size of Fe3O4@COF-F (3.8 nm) was larger than the molecular size of BUs (<1.92 nm), thus facilitating efficient mass transfer and improved extraction performance (Song et al., 2023). The hydrophobic nature of Fe3O4@COF-F, evidenced by a contact angle of 102.8°, along with the high log Kow values of the BUs (ranging from 3.59 to 5.85), suggested that hydrophobic interactions contribute to the effective extraction of the target BUs.

Furthermore, the selectivity of Fe3O4@COF-F was evaluated by examining its extraction efficiency toward various commonly detected pesticides, including neonicotinoids, carbamates, organophosphorus, and phenylpyrazole insecticides (Fig. S6). As shown in Fig. S7a, Fe3O4@COF-F maintained satisfactory recovery rates for BUs even in the presence of these coexisting interferents. The presence of interferents in the supernatant further confirmed its selectivity, as the low recovery rates were attributed to poor adsorption affinity rather than incomplete desorption.

The aforementioned experimental results demonstrated that Fe3O4@COF-F exhibited significantly enhanced adsorption performance toward fluorine-containing compounds (BUs and fipronil) compared to non-fluorinated compounds, which can be attributed to its selective fluorine affinity. To further assess the specific selectivity of Fe3O4@COF-F, recovery rates obtained with Fe3O4@COF-F were compared with those of Fe3O4@COF, a structurally similar material lacking fluorine functional groups. As shown in Fig. S7b, the fluorine-free Fe3O4@COF exhibited considerably lower adsorption efficiency for BUs than the fluorine-rich Fe3O4@COF-F, thereby confirming the critical role of fluorine–fluorine interactions in the adsorption process.

In summary, the excellent capturing ability and selectivity for BUs could be attributed to the synergistic interactions and the distinctive structural features of Fe3O4@COF-F (Fig. S8).

Method performance

Under optimized conditions, the performance of the established analytical method was evaluated, including linear range, correlation coefficient (r), limits of detection (LODs), limits of quantification (LOQs), precision (RSD), and matrix effect (MEs). The calibration curve for the MDSPE procedure was established by plotting the relationship between the concentration and the resulting signal intensity (Fig. S9), demonstrating satisfactory linearity with coefficient of determination (r) above 0.995 (Table 1). The LODs and LOQs were 0.16–0.30 ng g−1 and 0.55–1.00 ng g−1, respectively, which were below the maximum residue limits established by GB 2763–2021 (Table S4). The intra-day and inter-day RSDs (n = 6) were within 0.28–3.15% and 1.21–5.70%, respectively, indicating excellent repeatability. Matrix effect analysis showed acceptable interference in this study, with values ranging from 8.22% to 14.16%.

Real sample analysis

To evaluate the practicality of the developed MDSPE-PDESI-MS method, it was applied to the determination of BUs in fifteen cucumber samples, fifteen cabbage samples, and fifteen apple samples. As shown in Table S5, the target BUs were not detected in the majority of the samples. Trace levels of HFU were detected in six cucumber and three apple samples, all of which were below the LOQs. To further validate the accuracy of the method, recovery experiments were conducted using spiked samples at concentrations of 1, 5, and 20 ng g−1, with six replicates for each level. As shown in Table 2, the recoveries ranged from 80.56% to 93.98%, with RSDs below 11.66%. These results confirm that the proposed method is reliable for the analysis of trace BUs in the selected food samples.

Conclusions

In this study, a facile and efficient method based on MDSPE-PDESI-MS was developed for the analysis of four BUs in fruits and vegetables. The magnetic adsorbent Fe3O4@COF-F was successfully synthesized through a room-temperature strategy and employed for the efficient enrichment of BUs. The resulting material exhibited excellent adsorption performance for BUs, attributed to multiple interactions including fluorine affinity, hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions. By combining the Fe3O4@COF-F-based MDSPE with the ambient ionization capability of PDESI-MS, a sensitive and accurate analytical method was established for the detection of BUs in cucumbers, cabbages, and apples. The method displayed excellent linearity, low LODs (0.16–0.30 ng g−1), satisfactory recoveries, and good reproducibility. In summary, this study provides a practical and sensitive strategy for the rapid screening of benzoylurea insecticide residues, as well as demonstrating the potential of the Fe₃O₄@COF-F concept for analyzing other fluorinated contaminants.
