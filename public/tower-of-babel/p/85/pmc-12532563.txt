CAA-derived IL-6 promoted the PD-L1 expression of breast cancer via STAT3/miR-497a-5p signaling
Authors: Chongru Zhao, Xiaomei Zhou, Xing Li, Gezi Li, Qi Zhang, Jun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdipocytes are engaged in the development and progression of breast cancer (BC). Cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs) are specific adipocytes located at the invasive front of BC that modulate tumor behaviors. This study aimed to investigate the effect of CAA-derived IL-6 in regulating BC progression.MethodsHuman BC specimens and adipocytes co-cultured with BC cells were used to explore the characteristics of CAAs. Adipocytes and 4T1 cells co-implanted in mouse model and tail vein metastasis model were used to explore the effect of CAAs on malignant progression and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) of BC in vivo. The functional assays, flow cytometry, ELISA, miRNAs sequencing and dual-luciferase reporter assay were implemented to investigate the role of CAA-derived IL-6 in regulating programmed cell death protein ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression.ResultsCAAs were present at the invasive front of BC with a de-differentiated fibroblast phenotype. CAAs enhanced the malignant behaviors of 4T1 BC cells in vitro, and promoted 4T1 tumor growth and lung metastasis with decreased CD8+T cells and upregulated Tregs. The IHC results of both human BC specimens and xenografts showed that CAAs could upregulate PD-L1 expression in BC. Besides, CAAs could secrete abundant IL-6 and thus enhanced PD-L1 expression in 4T1 cells and tumors. More importantly, CAA-derived IL-6 could activate STAT3, while STAT3 blockade in CAA-cultured 4T1 cells upregulated miRNA-497a-5p expression and downregulated PD-L1 expression. Dual-luciferase reporter assay indicated that PD-L1 was a direct target of miRNA-497a-5p.ConclusionsOur study demonstrated that CAAs promoted the malignant behaviors of BC and enhanced immunosuppression in TME. Specifically, CAA-derived IL-6 promoted the PD-L1 expression of BC via STAT3/miR-497a-5p signaling, thereby contributing to BC progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-025-06324-5.Keywords: Breast cancer, Cancer-associated adipocytes, PD-L1, IL-6, STAT3, miR-497a-5p

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) ranks first in the incidence of female cancer worldwide and is the leading cause of cancer-related death due to the early recurrence and rapid distant metastasis (Bray et al. 2018; Li and Kang 2016). Despite the rapid advances in the treatment of BC, the mortality rate of BC remains high. Understanding the pathological alterations in BC is particularly important in improving the survival rate of BC patients. Growing evidence indicates that interactions between cancer cells and stromal tissue contribute to the malignant transformation of cancer (Laplane et al. 2018).

Adipocytes are important stromal cells adjacent to tumor cells in BC tumor microenvironment (TME) and are being gradually transformed from a silent bystander to an active participant in the BC progression (Choi et al. 2017). In invasive BC, tumor cells can break through the basement membrane and come into direct contact with adipocytes. These adipocytes, closely adjacent to BC tissue, are denoted as cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs) (Dirat et al. 2011). Compared to adipocytes in the non-tumoral part, CAAs exhibit a fibroblast phenotype with reduced size and dispersed lipid droplets, and present an activation state characterized by downregulation of C/EBP-α expression and upregulation of matrix metalloprotein (MMP)-11, interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-1β expression (Dirat et al. 2011; Fujisaki et al. 2015). Adipokines secreted by CAAs are key factors in remodeling BC progression by influencing multiple cellular activities, including cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Zhao et al. 2020).

In addition to these effects, CAAs are important contributors to regulating the immune-activated state in the BC entity. For instance, CAAs up-regulated the secretion of leptin and resulted in inhibited anti-tumor effects of CD8+T cells by suppressing glycolysis (Zhang et al. 2020a). The metabolic accumulation of lactate and adenosine of CAAs can suppress the function of CD8+T cells (Brand et al. 2016; Cekic and Linden 2014). Free fatty acids (FFAs) and lactic acid produced by the abnormal catabolism of CAAs also provide energy for the differentiation and function of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and facilitate immunosuppression of tumors (Lochner et al. 2015; Angelin et al. 2017). Hence, CAAs can influence the differentiation and function of innate and adaptive immune cells through aberrantly secreted adipokines and metabolites, further strengthening immunosuppressive TME and promoting tumor progression (Rybinska et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021).

Tumors can evade host anti-tumor immunity through a variety of immunosuppressive mechanisms (Sharma and Allison 2015). The immune checkpoint pathway is an essential immune escape mechanism common to many types of cancer, including BC (Munn and Bronte 2016). The binding of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand PD-L1 is critical in the immune escape of BC cells (Wu et al. 2018). Emerging evidence has proved that high PD-L1 expression in BC suppresses the function of CD8+T cells, targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 is an essential strategy for restoring the anti-tumor immune response (Sau et al. 2018). Exploring the regulatory mechanisms of PD-L1 expression can help identify patients sensitive to targeted therapy and improve the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.

IL-6 is an important cytokine with multiple functions and differential effects in various tumor settings. Especially, IL-6 expression is not only up-regulated in adipocytes co-cultured with BC cells but also shows high abundance levels in CAAs of human BC tissues (Lee et al. 2017). IL-6 is a critical active factor in the adipocyte secretion repertoire and plays an unparalleled function in adipose and tumor interactions (Gyamfi et al. 2019; He et al. 2018). Furthermore, there is a strong link between the expression and activity of IL-6 and immune status. IL-6 has been found to be a regulator of PD-L1 in multiple cell types (Zhang et al. 2020b; Xu et al. 2018). In thyroid cancer, IL-6 and PD-L1 expression levels are both upregulated and associated with tumor invasion, possibly through regulating the MAPK and JAK-STAT3 pathways (Zhang et al. 2021). More interestingly, Li et al. (2020a) have shown that adipocytes can up-regulate PD-L1 expression in melanoma and hepatocellular carcinoma cells by secreting TNF-α and IL-6, demonstrating the promotional effect of obesity on BC. However, whether CAA-secreted IL-6 is involved in the formation of the immunosuppressive TME and its relationship with PD-L1 expression in BC has not been investigated.

Based on the above background, we hypothesized that CAAs could promote BC immunosuppression by secreting IL-6 to upregulate PD-L1 in BC cells. Specifically, we primarily adopted clinical samples, in vitro experiments, and tumor mouse models to explore the phenotypic characteristics of CAAs, the behavioral effects of CAAs on 4T1 cells, and the suppressive effects of CAA-derived IL-6 on tumor immunity and the potential related mechanisms. The implementation of this study is conducive to gaining insight into complex regulatory networks, which is beneficial for exploring novel CAA-targeted strategies for BC therapy.

Materials and methods

Clinical BC specimens

The 11 human BC specimens were derived from patients who were diagnosed with invasive BC and underwent mastectomy at Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, for hematoxylin/eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining in this study. Adipocyte sizes in the H&E-stained section were measured with ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, MD, USA). The clinicopathological information of the 11 BC patients was shown in Table S1.

Cell culture and acquisition of mature adipocytes

Murine 4T1 cells and 293T cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Murine 3T3-L1 preadipocytes were purchased from Procell Life Science&Technology (Wuhan, China). All the cells were cultured in DMEM-H medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). Mature adipocytes were acquired by adipogenic differentiation of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes, as previously described (Wei et al. 2019). Differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes were identified with Oil Red O staining (Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

Cell co-culture and conditioned medium preparation

Co-culture assay with a transwell system (pore size 0.4 μm, Corning, USA) was conducted to obtain CAAs. 4T1 cells were seeded into the upper chamber with or without adipocytes in the lower chamber for 3 days. To generate the conditioned media (CM) of CAAs (CAA-CM) and adipocytes (AD-CM), cells were further cultured in an FBS-free medium for 24 h. CAA-CM and AD-CM were then collected and centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm. 1% FBS was added to the CM for subsequent experiments.

Proliferation and wound-healing assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a cell counting kit (CCK-8; Yeasen, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were inoculated in a 96-well plate at 5 × 103/well and cultured in CM. Then, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well at 0,12, 24 and 36 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was acquired by an enzyme-labeled instrument (BioTek, VT, USA).

A total of 1 × 106 4T1 cells were plated in a 24-well plate and grew to confluence. Then, the cells were scratched in a straight line with a 10 μL pipette and were incubated for 24 h with or without CM. The five different fields of healing wounds were selected and obtained by a microscope (SOPTOP CX40, Ningbo, China) and were analyzed with Image J software.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

For the transwell migration assay, 5 × 104 4T1 cells were cultured in the upper chamber of the transwell system (pore size 8 μm). 500 μL of CM was added into the lower chamber of the transwell in a 24-well plate. After incubating for 48 h, the migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet for 15 min. Photographs of the migrated cells were obtained under a microscope (SOPTOP CX40, Ningbo, China), and the stained cells were washed with 33% glacial acetic acid. Then, the OD values of each well were measured at 590 nm by the enzyme-labeled instrument.

For the transwell invasion assay, the transwell chambers were first coated with the Matrigel (R&D system, MN, USA) at a final concentration of 3 mg/mL. Subsequently, 2 × 105 4T1 cells were incubated in the upper chamber of the transwell system (pore size 8 μm). The following procedures were consistent with the transwell migration assay.

Quantitative real-time PCR and western blotting assay

Cultured cells were lysed by TRIzol (Takara, Shiga, Japan), and total RNAs were extracted for cDNA synthesis with the 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed with QuantStudio1 (ABI Q1, CA, USA) with SYBR GreenTM Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR were available in Table S2.

For the western blotting assay, cultured cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer with PMSF (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Then the lysates were quantified by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Total proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE to transfer onto a PVDF membrane (0.22 μm; Millipore, MA, USA). Subsequently, the membranes were blocked in 5% BSA and incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the protein bands were detected by ECL chemiluminescent reagents (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). All antibodies employed in the western blotting assay were presented in Table S3.

Animal models

BALB/C mice (6–8 w, female) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). All mice were bred at 21–25 °C and under 12/12 h light and dark alternating conditions. The animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. For the subcutaneous tumor-bearing mouse model, 30 mice were randomly divided into two groups. The mice of the CAAs group received a subcutaneous injection of 5 × 105 4T1 cells and 2.5 × 106 adipocytes. The mice in the Control group received an injection of 5 × 105 4T1 cells only. Mice were observed and weighed at 1-day intervals from day 1 post-inoculation. The tumor volume was measured every 3 days when small and hard nodules could be palpated. After 4 weeks post-inoculation, blood was taken from the eye and the mice were sacrificed. The blood was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant was collected and stored at − 80 °C. The tumor tissues were dissected and weighed. One portion of the tumor tissues was taken for tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) extraction, the other portion of the tumor tissues was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin, and the rest of the tissues were frozen at − 80 °C.

For the tail vein metastasis assay, 4T1 cells were cultured with the control medium or with CAA-CM for 48 h. Then, these cells were harvested and injected into the tail vein of each mouse. After 14 days, mice were executed to obtain the lung tissue, which was isolated and embedded in paraffin for H&E staining to evaluate the pulmonary metastases.

Flow cytometry (FCM)

TILs were extracted from murine mammary tumors by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation, previously described (Tan and Lei 1960). TILs were first cultured in a cell activation cocktail (Biolegend, CA, USA) for 4–6 h. Next, TILs were stained with antibodies APC-Cy FVS780, FITC anti-mouse CD3, PE anti-mouse CD8, PerCpCy5.5 anti-mouse CD4, and PECy7 anti-mouse PD-1 (Biolegend, CA, USA) for 15 min at room temperature in darkness. Cyto-Fast™Fix/Perm Buffer was used to detect the intracellular cytokines of TILs and True-Nuclear™ Fix/Perm Buffer was used to examine the intranuclear factor based on the manufacturer's introductions (Biolegend, CA, USA). Then, PerCpCy5.5 anti-mouse GzMB and APC anti-mouse IFN-γ PE, and anti-mouse FOXP3 (Biolegend, CA, USA) were added to the TILs for 20 min at room temperature in darkness. Labeled cells were detected using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA).

H&E, IHC and immunofluorescence (IF) assay

For the H&E assay, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Sections were stained with Hematoxylin and eosin. Representative H&E-stained photographs were taken with the microscope (SOPTOP CX40, Ningbo, China).

For IHC, the sample tissues were dissociated in xylene, rehydrated in ethanol solutions of varying concentrations, and heated in citrate buffer for antigen restoration. Subsequently, the paraffin sections were blocked with 5% BSA and immune-stained with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation in HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Then the sections were visualized using DAB and were counterstained with Hematoxylin for 2 min. Digital images of the sections were captured by the microscope (SOPTOP CX40, Ningbo, China).

For the IF assay, the procedures of the sections before incubating the secondary antibodies were the same as those for IHC. Next, the sections were incubated with the fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Then, nuclear 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used to restain the nuclei. Digital images of the sections were captured by the fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). All antibodies involved in the IHC and IF assay were presented in Table S3.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The secretion levels of IL-6 in the serum of mice in the CAAs group and CAA-CM were measured using mouse IL-6 ELISA kits (Invitrogen, CA, USA) based on the manufacturer’s protocols.

miRNAs sequencing

The RNA samples from 4T1 cells cultured in CAA-CM or control medium were submitted to miRNA sequencing with the Nextseq CN500 sequencer in BerryGenomics (Beijing, China). Differential miRNAs were identified based on the sequencing results.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

For the dual-luciferase reporter assay, GV272-CD274 3′-UTR vectors (Ribo Biotech, Guangzhou, China) containing the mutant and wild-type binding sites of miR-497a-5p were constructed. Then, these constructed reporter vectors were co-transfected with TK promoter-Renilla and miR-497a-5p inhibitors or miR-497a-5p NC to 293T cells or 4T1 cells using DNA transfection reagent (Neofect, Beijing, China). The relative luminescent signals were detected by the enzyme-labeled instrument according to the manufacturer's protocols (Yeasen, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analyses

Graphpad (version 9.0, CA, United States) was used for statistical analysis of the experimental data. All data were obtained from three replicate experiments and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (means ± SD). Differences between two independent samples were compared using Student’s-t test, and differences among multiple groups were analyzed using One Way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered a statistical significance.

Results

CAAs exhibited a de-differentiated phenotype

The morphological characteristics of CAAs were evaluated in 11 human BC specimens by H&E staining. CAAs were found to be located at the invasive front of the tumor, including peritumoral CAAs (Fig. 1A, black arrow) and intra-tumoral CAAs (Fig. 1B black arrow). CAAs exhibited significantly smaller size in diameter, compared to normal mammary adipocytes (NA) (Fig. 1C). To verify the de-differentiation characteristics of adipocytes, the mature adipocytes were acquired by adipogenic induction of 3T3-L1 cells. The mature adipocytes induced from 3T3-L1 cells exhibited large amounts of lipid droplets (Fig. 1D), which were presented as red by Oil red O staining (Fig. 1E). Next, the mature adipocytes were co-cultured with 4T1 cells to obtain CAAs by transwell assay. The co-culture assay (Fig. 1F) showed that adipocytes possessed decreased size, elongated shape similar to fibroblasts, as well as smaller and more dispersed lipid droplets, compared to mature adipocytes (Fig. 1G–I). The mRNA expression levels of C/EBP-α, PPAR-γ, and Adipoq were significantly lower, and preadipocyte markers HSL and α-SMA, and pro-inflammatory IL-6 were significantly higher in CAAs, in comparison to mature 3T3-L1 adipocytes (Fig. 1J). Therefore, CAAs possessed representative de-differentiation features and the mature adipocytes could be converted into CAAs upon contact with adjacent BC cells, which was consistent with previous studies (Lee et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2020; Takehara et al. 2020).

CAAs enhanced the malignant behaviors of 4T1 cells

Then, we explored the impact of CAAs on the proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT capability of 4T1 cells. CAAs and mature adipocytes were cultured with serum-free DMEM-H medium, and then CAA-CM and AD-CM were collected, respectively. CCK-8 assay validated that CAA-CM promoted the proliferation ability of 4T1 cells compared with AD-CM and control medium (Fig. 2A). The scratch assay and transwell migration assay showed that CAA-CM significantly improved the migration ability of 4T1 cells, compared with AD-CM, while the pro-migration ability of AD-CM was stronger than that of the control group (Fig. 2B, C). The invasion assay also indicated a similar trend that CAA-CM effectively promoted the invasion ability of 4T1 cells (Fig. 2D). The mesenchymal cell marker Vimentin protein expression was significantly upregulated compared to the control cells, while there was no statistical significance compared with AD-CM. In contrast, the epithelial cell marker E-cadherin protein expression was significantly decreased in 4T1 cells cultured with CAA-CM than in the control cells. Compared with AD-CM, the expression of E-cadherin decreased, but there was no significant statistical difference. (Fig. 2E, F). These results demonstrated that CAA-CM enhanced the malignant behavior of 4T1 cells.

CAAs promoted the growth and metastasis of 4T1 cells in vivo

In the in vivo assay, the subcutaneous tumor-bearing and lung metastasis mouse models were established to elucidate the effect of CAAs on 4T1 cells. Compared with the only 4T1 cells implanting (Control group), the mixed implanting of CAAs and 4T1 cells (CAAs group) markedly promoted the 4T1 xenograft volume and growth rate (Fig. 3A, B), as well as the tumor weight (Fig. 3C). Then, the IHC of tumor biopsies proved that cell proliferation marker Ki67 and endothelial cell marker CD31 were higher in the CAAs group compared to the Control group, which indicated a higher proliferation capacity and more vascular survival (Fig. 3D, E). The IHC results also indicated that the expression of E-cadherin was diminished in the CAAs group compared with the Control group, while the expression of Vimentin was enhanced (Fig. 3F). In the lung metastasis model, the 4T1 cells pre-treated with CAA-CM could accelerate the spread of BC cells to colonize and metastasize to the lung compared to 4T1 cells alone (Fig. 3G, H). The above results demonstrated that CAAs could promote the growth, EMT, and lung metastasis of BC tumors in vivo.

CAAs enhanced immunosuppression in BC TME in vivo

TILs were extracted from 4T1 tumors to further illustrate the effect of CAAs on the immune microenvironment of BC in vivo. The results of FCM analysis indicated that the proportion of CD8+T cells, IFN-γ+CD8+T cells and GzmB+CD8+T cells was significantly decreased in the TILs of the CAAs group compared with the Control group (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, the proportion of CD4+T cells and FOXP3+CD4+T cells in the CAAs group was significantly higher than that in the Control group (Fig. 4C, D). The IF results showed a decreased quantity in CD8+T cells and an increased quantity in PD1+CD8+T cells in the tumors of the CAAs group compared with the Control group, and an increased amount in CD4+T cells and FOXP3+CD4+T cells (Fig. 4E, F). The above results provided evidence that CAAs could reduce the proportion of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and increase the proportion of Tregs, thus creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment for BC in favor of BC progression and immune evasion.

CAAs up-regulated PD-L1 expression of 4T1 cells

To further confirm the immunosuppressive effect of CAAs on tumors in relation to PD-L1, we examined the PD-L1 expression in clinical specimens, xenografts and cell lines. In human BC specimens, it was apparent that there was a higher abundance of PD-L1 expression at the invasive front of BC compared to the central site (Fig. 5A). Besides, the adipocyte-derived IL-6 expression at the invasive front of BC was higher than that at normal breast adipose tissue (Fig. 5B). The western blotting assay further confirmed that, compared to the Control group, the expression of PD-L1, as well as IL-6, was significantly elevated in the xenograft of the CAAs group (Fig. 5C, D). It also presented that the 4T1 cells expressed more PD-L1 when treated with different ratios of CAA-CM, especially in 50% and 75% of CAA-CM (Fig. 5E, F). It was presumed that these invasive CAAs might be a trigger for PD-L1 upregulation and were probably related to IL-6 expression.

CAA-derived IL-6 promoted PD-L1 expression in 4T1 cells through activating STAT3 signaling

Next, we intend to further validate the role of CAA-derived IL-6 in PD-L1 expression. Firstly, the IL-6 concentrations were detected by ELISA, showing that serum IL-6 concentration in the tumor-bearing model was higher in the CAAs group (Fig. 6A). At the cellular level, there was also a higher content of IL-6 in CAA-CM compared to the AD-CM and control medium (Fig. 6B). The protein levels of PD-L1 and p-STAT3/STAT3 were significantly increased in 4T1 cells cultured in CAA-CM compared to the control medium (Fig. 6C–E). Meanwhile, neutralizing IL-6 with an anti-IL-6 blocking antibody (10 ng/mL) significantly inhibited the protein expression of PD-L1 and p-STAT3/STAT3 in 4T1 cells (Fig. 6C–E).

C–E PD-L1 and p-STAT3/STAT3 protein expression were detected by western blotting assay in CAA-CM-treated 4T1 cells with the addition of an anti-IL-6 blocking antibody. I–K PD-L1, p-STAT3, and STAT3 protein expression was detected by western blotting assay in CAA-CM treated 4T1 cells transfected with si-STAT3. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001

To further validate the role of STAT3 signaling in PD-L1 expression, 4T1 cells were treated with different concentrations of STAT3 inhibitor (stattic) in CAA-CM. Both 5 μM and 10 μM stattic could remarkably inhibit PD-L1 expression, and 10 μM stattic achieved the most significant inhibitory effect in 4T1 cells (Fig. 6F, G). For protein level validation, si-STAT3-3 exhibited the best knockdown efficiency and was selected for the next siRNA assay (Fig. 6H). This result showed that PD-L1 expression was significantly lower in the si-STAT3-transfected 4T1 cells than in the control 4T1 cells, and p-STAT3 protein expression was significantly decreased (Fig. 6I–K). The above results collectively confirmed that CAA-derived IL-6 promoted PD-L1 expression by activating STAT3 signaling in 4T1 cells.

CAAs down-regulated miR-497a-5p expression in 4T1 cells

To investigate the regulatory effect of CAAs on miRNAs in 4T1 cells, miRNA sequencing was adopted to identify the differentially expressed miRNAs in 4T1 cells cultured with CAA-CM and the control medium. A total of 16 miRNAs were significantly altered in CAA-CM-educated 4T1 cells compared to the control, of which 13 miRNAs were significantly down-regulated and 3 miRNAs were significantly up-regulated (Fig. 7A, B). We noted that low miR-497a-5p expression was associated with poor cancer prognosis (Sun et al. 2017; Tian et al. 2021; Qu et al. 2019). In renal cancer cells, miR-497a-5p was a direct target of PD-L1, and downregulation of miR-497a-5p promoted the expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells (Qu et al. 2019). In the present study, miR-497a-5p expression levels were significantly decreased in the 4T1 xenograft of the CAAs group compared to the Control group (Fig. 7C). Compared with the control group and the AD-CM cultured 4T1 cells, the expression levels of miR-497a-5p in CAA-CM cultured 4T1 cells in vitro were significantly decreased (Fig. 7D). Hence, it was assumed that CAAs could down-regulate the expression of miR-497a-5p in 4T1 cells.

CAAs up-regulated PD-L1 expression in 4T1 cells via IL-6/STAT3/miR-497a-5p signaling

At last, we explored the specific role of IL-6 in regulating the miR-497a-5p expression of CAAs in 4T1 cells. The qRT-PCR results showed that IL-6 blocking significantly weakened the inhibitory effect of CAAs on miR-497a-5p expression in 4T1 cells (Fig. 8A). More intriguing, miR-497a-5p expression was significantly higher in CAA-CM + siSTAT3 group than in CAA-CM + siNC group, thus reducing the effect of CAAs in suppressing miR-497a-5p expression (Fig. 8B). These results demonstrated that CAAs negatively regulated miR-497a-5p expression in 4T1 cells via the IL-6/STAT3 signaling. Western blotting analysis showed that si-STAT3 caused a significant reduction in PD-L1 expression in CAA-CM + si-STAT3 group relative to the CAA-CM group, while miR-497a-5p inhibitors significantly attenuated this inhibitory effect (Fig. 8C, D). To confirm whether PD-L1 was a direct target of miR-497a-5p, GV272-CD274-WT and GV272-CD274-MUT plasmids were constructed according to the binding site of miR-497a-5p to CD274 3′-UTR predicted by the online website TargetScan (Fig. 8E, F). When 293T and 4T1 cells were transfected with WT plasmids and miR-497a-5p inhibitors, luciferase activity was greatly increased. The effect of miR-497a-5p inhibitors was significantly reduced when the predicted 3’-UTR binding site was mutated (Fig. 8G, H). Thus, CAAs could up-regulate PD-L1 expression in 4T1 cells via IL-6/STAT3/miR-497a-5p signaling.

Discussion

The close interaction of adipose stromal cells and tumor cells is an important event in the BC process. It is well-documented that the adipocyte secretome is a reservoir that influences tumor cell behaviors via complicated mechanisms. In this study, we found that adipocytes at the invasive front of human BC became smaller, and adipocytes co-cultured with BC cells altered into a fibroblastic phenotype, suggesting that adipocytes underwent de-differentiated reprogramming into CAAs. In vitro experiments revealed that CAA-CM enhanced the proliferation, migration and invasion, and promoted EMT in 4T1 cells. We also indicated that CAAs could promote the growth and lung metastasis of 4T1 tumors in mice, accompanied by enhanced immunosuppression in BC TME. Moreover, we revealed a potential mechanism that CAA-derived IL-6 could facilitate the malignant PD-L1 expression of BC via STAT3/miR-497a-5p signaling.

Studies have confirmed that cancer cells derived from multiple tissues, including the breast, prostate, pancreas and ovary, can be co-cultured with adipocytes to obtain CAAs (Dirat et al. 2011; Sau et al. 2018; Takehara et al. 2020; John et al. 2019). In surgically excised BC specimens, we found that CAAs at the invasive front of BC were smaller and presented in two main distributions, the peri-tumoral CAAs and the intra-tumoral CAAs. In addition, in the transwell co-culture system of adipocytes and BC cells, adipocytes exhibited a characteristic phenotype: smaller and longer in shape, showing a phenotype similar to fibroblasts; reduced and dispersed lipid droplets; absence of adipocyte markers such as C/EBP-α, PPAR-γ and Adipoq; acquisition of pre-adipocyte markers such as HSL and α-SMA, and the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6. The in vivo and in vitro phenotypic changes of CAAs described above are consistent with the existing studies (Dirat et al. 2011; Fujisaki et al. 2015; Zhao et al. 2020; Al-Khalaf et al. 2019; Tan et al. 2011). To date, there is a paucity of research on the mechanisms and characteristics of CAA formation, and there are no clear CAA-specific markers. Further exploration of CAA’s biomarkers is needed to guide the diagnosis of BC and to predict treatment response and prognosis.

There is growing evidence that not only BC tumors can affect the de-differentiation status and function of CAAs, but CAAs can also influence the evil behaviors of BC through paracrine function. Here, CAA-CM significantly promoted the proliferation of 4T1 cells, which was consistent with the results of the previous study (Choi et al. 2017). The present study also indicated that CAA-CM could stimulate the migration and invasion of 4T1 cells. Dirat et al. (2011) demonstrated for the first time that CAAs could increase the invasiveness of cancer cells by using mouse adipocytes. Some studies have also reported that CAAs can promote the migration and invasion of a variety of cancer cells, including BC cells (Fujisaki et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2020; Takehara et al. 2020; John et al. 2019). Notably, we identified that CAA-CM induced the expression of EMT-related proteins in 4T1 cells, leading to EMT properties in BC cells. It was shown that adipocytes co-cultured with BC cells enhanced the invasive behavior of BC cells and induced the EMT phenotype, thereby promoting tumor growth, as well as lung and liver metastasis (Gyamfi et al. 2018; Wei et al. 2016; Song et al. 2018). Although current studies have identified CAAs in human BC clinical specimens and have confirmed their effects on cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion and EMT through in vitro experiments, there are few in vivo studies of CAAs in animals. Our mouse model of in vivo 4T1 xenograft and lung metastasis showed that co-injection of adipocytes significantly enhanced tumor growth rate, tumor volume, and lung metastatic nodules. Al-Khalaf et al. (2019) co-injected human mammary CAAs with MDA-MB-231 cells under the nipples of nude mice to establish a BC xenograft model, and discovered that CAAs promoted mammary tumor growth and angiogenesis. We also observed that the CD31 expression was upregulated in the tumors of the co-injected group, suggesting more angiogenesis. Moreover, Wei et al. (2019) found that subcutaneously co-transplanted MDA-MB-231 cells and human mammary adipose tissue could significantly promote BC metastasis in nude mice. Similarly, we discovered that CAAs could induce lung metastasis of BC in mice by a tail vein metastasis assay. Thus, the interaction between CAAs and BC cells ultimately led to BC proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion, and lung metastasis.

There is a direct and indirect impact of CAAs on adaptive anti-tumor immune processes in the adipose immune microenvironment. CAAs can release adipokines and immunomodulatory metabolites to mediate the differentiation and functional regulation of adaptive immune cells, creating an immunosuppressive TME for tumor progression (Brand et al. 2016; Cekic and Linden 2014; Lochner et al. 2015; Angelin et al. 2017; Fontenot et al. 2017). Consistent with the results of previous studies, the mammary tumors in the CAAs group were significantly larger than those in the Control group in the present study. We further extracted TILs from mouse mammary tumors and pointed out that the proportion of CD8+T cells was significantly decreased in the CAAs group compared with the Control group, as was the proportion of CD8+T cells expressing IFN-γ and GzmB. Furthermore, the proportion of CD4+FOXP3+Tregs was significantly increased. The above results implied that CAAs mediated the formation of immunosuppressive TME in mouse mammary tumors, thus promoting tumor growth. Wu et al. (2018) reported that PD-L1 expression was significantly up-regulated in mature adipocytes compared to pre-adipocytes. The upregulated PD-L1 in adipocytes further suppressed the anti-tumor function of CD8+T cells activated by the anti-PD-L1 antibody. PD-1 and PD-L1 are immune checkpoint proteins. Aberrant expression of PD-L1 is considered to be an oncogenic driver (Kurozumi et al. 2017). Direct binding of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibits cytokine production and T cell activation, resulting in the decrease and apoptosis in tumor-specific T cells, promoting immune escape of cancer cells (Setordzi et al. 2020). In the present study, we observed the enhanced PD-L1 expression at the invasive front of BC in human BC specimens and the CAAs group of the mouse tumor-bearing model. In vitro CAA-CM model, 50% of CAA-CM significantly increased PD-L1 expression in 4T1 cells. However, a higher percentage of CAA-CM had a diminished effect on PD-L1, probably due to the lack of serum and other nutrients in CM. CAAs might promote PD-L1 upregulation in cancer cells by secreting certain cytokines.

IL-6 is a multifunctional and potent cytokine and is closely related to the expression and function of PD-L1. Li et al. (2020b) found that adipocytes from obese patients could up-regulate PD-L1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma and melanoma by secreting TNF-α/IL-6. Similar to adipocytes from obese patients, upregulation of adipokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 was also observed in CAAs (Zhao et al. 2021). Adipocytes co-cultured with BC cells were found to up-regulate IL-6 expression and enhance proliferation, migration and invasion of BC cells through activation of STAT3 signaling (Gyamfi et al. 2018, 2019). STAT3 is the most common downstream factor of IL-6, and the IL-6/STAT3 axis appears to be critical in the malignant progression of CAA-educated BC cells. Adipocyte-derived IL-6 could activate STAT3 and promote PD-L1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Zhao et al. 2021). Therefore, our study focused on IL-6 and the further effect of CAA-derived IL-6 on PD-L1 expression in BC cells. We found that blocking IL-6 partially weakened the promoting effect of CAA-CM on PD-L1 in 4T1 BC cells, suggesting that other CAAs cytokines and IL-6 might also be synergistically involved in the promoting effect of PD-L1, which was required further study. Interestingly, when IL-6 in CAA-CM was neutralized, p-STAT3/STAT3 protein levels were also reduced. STAT3 inhibitors and siRNAs were employed to interfere with STAT3 expression, and the results indicated that PD-L1 expression was decreased. This suggested that CAAs could induce PD-L1 expression in BC cells through activation of IL-6/STAT3, which was consistent with the aforementioned studies that IL-6 up-regulated PD-L1 expression in different target cells in a STAT3-dependent pathway (Zhang et al. 2019, 2020b; Zhao et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2018; Pucci et al. 2021).

MiRNAs have been confirmed to be key regulators of cancer cell-immune cell interactions. miRNAs can hijack immune surveillance to promote cancer immune escape and progression (Gomarasca et al. 2020). miR-497-5p expression was reported to be significantly reduced in osteosarcoma and hepatocellular carcinoma tissues and was correlated with tumor malignant progression and poor prognosis (Sun et al. 2017; Tian et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2019). Luo et al. (2013) identified lower expression of miR-497 in BC specimens than in adjacent normal tissues. miR-497 functioned as a tumor suppressor gene in BC and overexpressed miRNA-497 inhibited BC cell growth and invasion by targeting Cyclin E1 (Luo et al. 2013). We found that CAAs could further reduce miR-497a-5p expression in 4T1 cells in vitro and in xenograft, and downregulation of miRNA-497a-5p was correlated with upregulation of PD-L1 in 4T1 cells. Our results firstly posed that PD-L1 was a direct target of miRNA-497a-5p in BC. Our findings are in accordance with the results of Qu et al.’s (2019) study, which emphasized that downregulation of miRNA-497-5p was associated with PD-L1 upregulation and shorter survival in human renal clear cell carcinoma. PD-L1 expression is intricately regulated at the transcriptional, translational and post-translational levels, and miR-497a-5p is only one of the many contributing factors.

As a transcriptional factor, STAT3 typically regulates miRNA expression by binding to their promoters to promote disease development, but recent studies highlight its capacity to suppress specific miRNAs (Liu et al. 2024). Rokavec et al. (2014) demonstrated that in colorectal cancer cells, IL-6-activated STAT3 inhibited miR-34a expression through a conserved binding site in the MIR34A gene’s first intron, establishing a STAT3/miR-34a/IL-6R feedback loop that could accelerate tumor cell invasion and migration. Similarly, Zhu et al. (2017) found that in epithelial ovarian cancer cells, STAT3 activation by IL-6 repressed miR-204 via a binding site near the TRPM3 promoter, forming a STAT3/miR-204/IL-6R loop that enhanced cisplatin resistance and contributed to treatment failure. In our study, we also confirmed the inhibitory effect of STAT3 on miRNA; that is, the activation of STAT3 could inhibit the expression of miR-497a-5p. These findings collectively indicate STAT3's dual role in both activating and inhibiting miRNA expression. However, the specific regulatory mechanism is not fully clear. Future studies are needed to identify the precise molecular intermediates involved in this regulatory pathway to fully elucidate the mechanism.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study proved that CAAs possessed a typical de-differentiated feature and might contribute to the immunosuppressive microenvironment for supporting BC progression. More importantly, CAA-derived IL-6 promoted the PD-L1 expression of BC via STAT3/miR-497a-5p signaling (Fig. 9). Finally, this work preliminarily provided a new clue into adipocyte-tumor cell interaction and the potential regulatory mechanism, posing novel therapeutic strategies for combating BC.
