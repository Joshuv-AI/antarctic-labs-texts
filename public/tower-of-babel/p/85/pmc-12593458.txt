Whom to include? A cross-sectional survey study investigating how culture impacts diagnostic decision making for paediatric cancer in Pakistan
Authors: Dylan Graetz, Joseph Wardell, Ambreen Hameed, Afia Tul Quanita, Atoofa Najmi, Safwan Ahmad, Muhammad Shafiq, Asma Naheed, Shabnam Munir, Gia Ferrara, Courtney Staples, Yichen Chen, Meenakshi Devidas, C Rodriguez-Galindo, Sima Jeha, Jennifer Mack, Syed Ahmer Hamid, Muhammad Rafie Raza, Alia Ahmad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study sought to explore decision making among caregivers of children with cancer in Pakistan, one of the largest lower middle-income countries in the world.DesignCross-sectional survey studySettingThis study was conducted in Pakistan at Indus Hospital and Health Network in Karachi and Children’s Hospital of Lahore. Children’s Hospital of Lahore is a public sector hospital, and Indus Hospital has a foundation-based funding structure. Both are larger tertiary care centers. Over 2,500 new patients are seen at these centers annually, this accounts for almost 50% of all children with cancer in PakistanParticipantsEligible participants included bedside caregivers, defined as a parent or family member involved in communication with the medical team, of children with cancer (<19 years of age) diagnosed within the past 8 weeks, with plans to receive chemotherapy, radiation or surgery.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcome measures included caregiver priorities and experiences related to communication including decision-making role, involvement of the paediatric patient and decisional regret.ResultsParticipants included 200 caregivers of children<1–16 years old with a range of cancer diagnoses; 83% were parents and 60.5% were female. Most caregivers shared (46.5%) or completely deferred (43.5%) cancer-related decision making to other family members. Of the 10% of respondents primarily responsible for decisions related to their child’s cancer, 70% were male. While 68% of caregivers preferred shared decision making with the child’s oncologist, only 65% experienced decision making in the role they preferred. Caregivers expressed a range of preferences regarding child involvement; however, 70% said nobody talked to their child about cancer. Few caregivers (17%) endorsed regret.ConclusionsFindings from this study highlight the importance of exploring preferences for decision making and empowering bedside caregivers while respecting cultural norms. In the Pakistani context, it may be specifically important to consider gender roles and the inclusion of extended family members. Future work should investigate paediatric patient involvement in diverse settings.Keywords: Health Surveys, Paediatric oncology, PUBLIC HEALTH, Quality of Life, Clinical Decision-Making

Introduction

Decision making in paediatric oncology is complex. The literature has historically focused on the ethical principles and clinical realities regarding if, and how, caregivers and clinicians might share responsibility for decisions.1 Shared decision making has been demonstrated to improve patient-reported outcomes in cancer care.2 Caregivers of children with cancer who have their preferred decision-making role report increased trust in their healthcare team3 and decreased regret.4 More recently, studies have highlighted the importance of patient involvement in decision making,5 particularly when the patient is an adolescent.6 7

However, most of these studies have been conducted in high-income Western settings.8 9 Meanwhile, most children with cancer live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).10 In LMICs, childhood cancer survival is estimated at 37.5%11 and rates of treatment abandonment,12 including upfront refusal, are as high as 75%.13 While there is a recognition that culture impacts decision making, including where and whether to seek care, paediatric cancer decision making in LMICs has been understudied.

Culture impacts risk tolerance,14 and for caregivers of childhood cancer patients, an individualist versus collectivist approach15 may drive who is responsible for decisions. Recognition of the cultural context is crucial to the relationship between the patient, family and their treating team,16 particularly at diagnosis. In collectivist contexts, extended family and community members may be included as part of a shared decision-making process.17 Furthermore, it is unknown how cultural norms might impact inclusion of paediatric patients in communication and decision making.

Pakistan is a lower middle-income country with a population of over 220 million where childhood cancer survival is <50% and abandonment rates have been estimated to be >20%.18 It is a predominantly Muslim country with a cultural orientation towards collectivism,19 77 established languages, >25 ethnic groups and a large refugee population. The purpose of this study was to expand the research on paediatric cancer decision making in LMICs through a cross-sectional survey investigating decision making among Urdu and Pashto-speaking caregivers of children recently diagnosed with cancer in Pakistan. Insights regarding how these caregivers with diverse backgrounds conceptualise and prioritise decision making may enable better care at treatment centres in Pakistan and improve our understanding regarding decision-making practices for paediatric cancer patients in other socioculturally diverse LMICs.

Methods

Settings and participants

This study was conducted at Indus Hospital and Health Network in Karachi and Children’s Hospital of Lahore. Children’s Hospital of Lahore is a public sector hospital, while Indus Hospital has a foundation-based funding structure. Each hospital cares for >1200 newly diagnosed children annually. Together, this accounts for almost 50% of all children with cancer in Pakistan.20 Additionally, these sites are culturally and clinically distinct and were specifically chosen for their differences and combined ability to represent the diversity of the Pakistani population. Karachi is Pakistan’s largest city and a major economic hub with a mix of Urdu-speaking Muhajirs, Sindhis, Punjabis, Pashtuns, Balochis and other groups. Lahore, the capital of Punjab province, is a historic and culturally significant city, with a primarily Punjabi population. Eligible participants included bedside caregivers, defined as a parent or family member present with the child and involved in communication with the medical team, of children with cancer (<19 years of age) diagnosed within the past 8 weeks, with plans to receive chemotherapy, radiation or surgery.

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor the public were involved in the design, conduct or reporting of our research; however, they will be involved in dissemination efforts.

Ethics statement

This study involves human participants and was approved by the Institutional Review Board at St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital (#00000029) as well as the relevant review boards and ethics committees at Indus Hospital and Health Network (IRD_IRB_2020_12_010) and Children’s Hospital of Lahore (2021-206-CHICH). All participants provided voluntary signed informed consent prior to participation in the study.

Survey development

The cross-sectional survey utilised in this study included items previously used in studies of paediatric cancer communication from high-income421,23 and middle-income countries.17 24 An English version of the survey was reviewed by local study teams with questions added or modified for the Pakistani context. For example, when asking about decision making within the family, an option was added for ‘another family member makes most of the decisions’. A question was also added to assess the role of extended family members in helping make cancer decisions. Other novel questions included demographic characteristics such as residence and number of children within the home, and questions regarding communication with the paediatric patient. The survey was translated into Urdu and Pashto and pilot tested via cognitive interviews with 35 caregivers at Indus and 46 at Children’s Hospital Lahore (over the course of five rounds) with iterative revision to establish face and content validity. Participants included in cognitive interviews were intentionally selected to reflect the range of participants eligible for the study, including low literacy and illiterate caregivers. Members of the study team conducting the cognitive interviews in Karachi included experienced psychologists who had worked in the paediatric psycho-oncology unit at Indus Hospital for years, lived in Karachi and whose primary language was Urdu. In Lahore, the cognitive interviews were conducted by two members of the study team originally from the Punjab province who spoke Urdu as their primary language and were also fluent in Punjabi and English. Both of these interviewers were oncologists at Children’s Hospital of Lahore, were familiar with the local cultures of Punjab and had experience interacting with families from rural areas. The final version of the Urdu and Pashto surveys was back translated into English to ensure the original intent of the questions was preserved. A visual aid24 accompanied the survey and was utilised with certain Likert scale questions as detailed below. The final version of the survey is included in online supplemental figure 1.

Data collection

The final survey was verbally administered to 100 caregivers at each site (total=200) from March to November 2023 by the same members of the study team engaged in cognitive interviews. This sample size was determined based on a power calculation to assess for marginal asymmetry between responses for different communication experiences. With a sample size of n=200 parents, there is 80% power (alpha 5%) to detect an effect size of 0.055. Eligible caregivers were approached sequentially at each site during scheduled visits to the centre. Willing participants provided consent, and the survey was either administered immediately or at a mutually agreed on later time in a private setting. Only one potential participant declined participation.

Sociodemographic information included relationship to the child, participant’s gender, languages spoken, ethnicity, religion, residence, education, household income, distance to the hospital and number of children. Child’s gender, age and diagnosis were obtained through caregiver report.

Decision making within the family was assessed by asking caregivers ‘who do you consider to be the person who makes most decisions in your house in general?’ and ‘…about your child’s cancer treatment?’. Response options for both questions included ‘I am the person most involved in making decisions’, ‘I share decision-making equally with my child’s other parent or another family member’, ‘another parent makes most of the decisions’ or ‘another family member (eg, grandparent, aunt/uncle) makes most of the decisions’. Caregivers were also asked ‘How do you prefer information about your child’s diagnosis and treatment to be presented to your family?’ with response options of ‘I want all information to be presented to all members of my family’, ‘I want some information to be presented to all members of my family, and other information to be presented only to the parent present’ or ‘I want all information presented only to the parent present’.

External involvement in decision making was assessed by asking caregivers ‘what is the role your community played in helping you make decisions?’ and the same question regarding extended family and religious/spiritual leaders. Response options to these questions included ‘I/We made the decisions about treatment without input from my [community; extended family; religious/spiritual leaders]’; ‘I/we made the decisions with help from my…’ and ‘My [community; extended family; religious/spiritual leaders] made the decision and told me what was best’.

Decision-making role with the oncologist was assessed by asking about preferred role in decision making through the question ‘What is the role you would prefer to play when decisions about treatment for your child’s cancer are made?’ with response options of ‘I prefer to make the decisions’, ‘I prefer that my child’s oncologist and I make the decisions together’, ‘I prefer that my child’s oncologist make most of the decisions’. Caregivers were then asked about decision-making experiences: ‘What is the role you actually played when making decisions about treatment for your child’s cancer?’ and responded, ‘I made the decisions about treatment’; ‘My child’s oncologist and I made the decisions together’ or ‘My child’s oncologist made the decision’.

The role of the child in conversations about treatment was assessed by asking caregivers, ‘What is the role you would prefer for your child when conversations about his/her cancer are had’ with response options of ‘I prefer that I talk to my child about his/her treatment’, ‘I prefer that I and my medical team both talk to my child about his/her treatment’, ‘I prefer that my child’s medical team talk to my child about his/her treatment’ or ‘I prefer that nobody talk to my child about his/her treatment’. A corresponding question asked caregivers ‘What is the role your child actually had during conversations about his/her cancer’ with response options of ‘I talked to my child about his/her treatment’, ‘I and my medical team both talked to my child about his/her treatment’, ‘my child’s medical team talked to my child about his/her treatment’ or ‘nobody talked to my child about his/her treatment’.

Decisional regret was assessed using an adapted version of the Decisional Regret Scale25 that asked caregivers ‘how much do you agree or disagree with … I have made the right decisions’, ‘… I regret the choices that were made’, ‘… I would make the same choices if I had to do it all over again’, ‘… my choices did my child harm’, ‘… the decisions I made were wise’. Caregivers answered using a three-point Likert scale of ‘completely agree’, ‘slightly agree’ or ‘disagree’ with the visual aid.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with R, V.4.4.1. Sociodemographic information and items related to decision making were analysed descriptively. Caregivers’ responses regarding preferred and actual decision-making role, as well as preferred and actual role for the child, were compared using McNemar-Bowker test. When expressed preferences matched experiences, this was considered ‘having their preferred role’. Preferred roles were evaluated across demographic groups with comparisons conducted using the χ2 Test of Independence or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Kruskal-Wallis tests were conducted to compare median age across the categories of preferred role and actual role for the children. Post-hoc Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed to identify specific pairwise differences in the actual role for the patient. Univariate logistic regression was used to assess the impact of sociodemographic characteristics on ‘preferred’ role in decision-making. Due to small counts and similar preferred rates, education level was collapsed for univariate analysis into four categories: no education/grades 1–5, grades 6–10, grades 11–12 and high school graduate. Previously reported methods were used to calculate a decisional regret score using a three-point Likert scale.17 Scores of 0 were categorised as no regret, 1–25 as mild regret and >25 as heightened regret, consistent with prior literature.25 Caregiver responses regarding regret were collapsed into ‘no regret’ or ‘some regret’. Decisional regret was evaluated across demographic groups with comparisons conducted using χ2 Test of Independence or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Results

Most participants (60.5%) were female, and 83% were parents; other relatives included aunts, uncles, cousins, grandparents and siblings. Of note, these demographics differed by site. At Children’s Hospital Lahore, 86% of the participants were female and 93% were parents. The increased numbers of women and mothers at this site are likely related to a paucity of individual rooms and policies that allow only females to stay at the bedside after hours, with males allowed to visit during restricted hours or for major procedures. Patients ranged in age from <1 to 16 years old (median 7 years); 67.5% had leukaemia, 13.5% had lymphoma, 17% had solid tumours and 1.5% had brain tumours. Decision making

Most of the bedside caregivers included in this survey shared responsibility for household decisions with another parent or family member (39%) or completely deferred these decisions to another parent (37.5%) or other family member (15.5%). Only 8% of caregivers surveyed reported that they made most decisions in their household; within this group, 81% were male. Regarding decisions about their child’s cancer, only 10% of caregivers said they were most involved in making decisions; 70% of these caregivers were male. Many caregivers (46.5%) shared cancer care decision-making with another parent or family member, while others reported that they were not involved in decisions regarding the child’s cancer, and decisions were made by another parent (38%) or family member (5.5%). Responses to these questions did not vary based on whether the respondent was a parent or another relative (p=0.29); however, responses did vary significantly by gender (p<0.0001). Most males either shared decision-making equally with another parent (41%) or made the decisions (16%). Most females completely deferred decision making either to another parent (49%) or family member (11%). Relatedly, many caregivers wanted some (35.5%) or all (21%) of the information presented not just to the family member at the bedside but to all family members. Table 2 depicts decision making roles by gender, including cancer related and non-cancer related decisions. Beyond the immediate family, many caregivers (64%) reported help from their extended family when making decisions related to their child’s cancer, and a smaller percentage reported involvement of spiritual or religious leaders (25%) or other members of the community (26.5%).

Decision-making roles by gender

When asked about preferences, 68% of caregivers wanted to make decisions with their child’s oncologist, while 32% preferred the oncologist make most of the decisions and no caregivers wanted sole responsibility for these decisions. However, when reporting experiences, 55% said that they were involved in making these decisions with their child’s oncologist, 43% said that their child’s oncologist made the decisions and 1.5% of caregivers reported that they were responsible for decisions about their child’s cancer. Around two-thirds (64.8%) of caregivers had their preferred role in decision making; 22.6% had a less active role than desired and 12.6% had a more active role than desired. Caregivers having their preferred role varied significantly only by caregiver education status (p=0.0068), with no differences observed across other demographic factors (table 3). Univariate logistic regression revealed that caregivers with no or little education (up to fifth grade) had 29% higher odds of having their preferred decision-making role compared with high school graduates (OR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.57, p=0.015), while those with grades 6–10 or grades 11–12 level education were not found to have significant differences in having their preferred decision-making role when compared with high school graduates. Education level was not associated with caregivers’ preferred or actual roles (p=0.58; p=0.92).

Decisional regret

Reflecting on decisions, most caregivers (83%) had no regret, 16% had mild regret and two caregivers (1%) experienced heightened regret. There were no significant associations between endorsing any regret and caregiver demographic characteristics (table 4), including gender (p=0.98), ethnicity (p=0.69), urban/rural residence (p=0.54), education (p=0.44) or household income (p=0.27). Caregiver regret was also not associated with patient age (p=0.44), gender (p=0.62) or diagnosis (p=0.27). No associations were found between caregiver regret and caregivers having their preferred role in decision making (p=0.26).

Child involvement

Caregivers differed in the extent to which they wanted their child involved in conversations about his or her cancer. Many caregivers (42%) preferred that nobody talk to their child about his or her treatment, 24% wanted to talk to their child about his or her treatment alone and 34% of caregivers wanted to talk to their child with the medical team. None of the caregivers preferred that the medical team talk to their child without the caregiver about his or her treatment. When reporting actual experiences, 72% of caregivers said nobody talked to their child about his or her treatment, 12% talked to their child about his or her treatment alone and 17% talked to their child about his or her treatment with the medical team. Of the caregivers who reported that nobody talked to their child about his or her treatment, almost half (48%) said that they had wanted to talk to their child about his or her treatment with (26%) or without (22%) the medical team. The McNemar-Bowker test revealed a significant discrepancy (χ² (3) = 42.00, p<0.001) between caregiver preferences and experiences for child involvement (online supplemental table 1). While caregiver preferences for child involvement varied, there was no statistically significant difference based on the patient’s age (p=0.14). However, caregivers’ responses on actual experiences regarding their child’s involvement varied by patient age. Caregiver response of ‘nobody talked to my child about his/her treatment’ was primarily among the youngest children and the median age was significantly lower than the other two categories of response (p<0.0001 (figure 1)).

Discussion

Clinicians in high-income, Western settings, often assume that the caregiver at the bedside is responsible for decision making. However, this may not be the case in all cultural contexts. In some settings, related to financial, cultural and familial constraints, the bedside caregiver may change frequently and may or may not be empowered to make crucial decisions. In this study, many bedside caregivers in Pakistan were not the primary decision makers in the household and were not responsible for decisions related to their child’s care. Some caregivers did engage in shared decision making with other members of their households, which may reflect shared decision-making as a coping strategy for dealing with the life-threatening illness of a child. However, >40% of surveyed caregivers were not at all involved in decision making and this was particularly true of mothers, who made up most of our sample. The exclusion of mothers from decision making may be rooted in societal gender roles in Pakistan, which has been specifically noted in relation to healthcare.26 In the Urdu and Pashto-speaking populations included in this study, for example, it is often typical for a male family member (either a father or elder brother) to be responsible for key decisions, while a female caregiver may have the primary role in caring for a child during illness. Additionally, many caregivers reported involvement from non-family members including spiritual or religious leaders and other members of the community, highlighting the importance of community to the study population and reflecting the often integral nature of religious support for devout communities.27 This study emphasises the importance of assessing decision-making roles and ensuring that decision makers are included in important conversations. At times, this may include addressing tensions between cultural or familial norms and the need to empower bedside caregivers to be involved in cancer-related decisions. These findings are relevant in all settings, but particularly for families in which norms may dictate a communal or hierarchical decision-making structure.

In addition to assessing who the decision makers are and supporting and empowering bedside caregivers, clinicians should be directly inquiring about decision-making preferences. Consistent with prior paediatric cancer literature,2 28 many caregivers in this study preferred shared decision making with their child’s oncologist. This differs from literature regarding adult cancer patients in Pakistan who often deferred to their treating physician for decision making.29 Only two-thirds of caregivers in our study had their preferred decision-making role; a figure that is strikingly like the number of caregivers with their preferred role in other settings.17 22 In Pakistan, as in the USA,22 most caregivers who did not have their preferred role had a less active role than desired. While most demographic factors were not associated with preferred decision-making role, educational status was, with the least educated caregivers more likely to have their preferred role. Prior work demonstrates that more educated people may prefer a more active role in decision making;30 however, this is not supported by our data and thus future work should further investigate this finding.

Paediatric patient involvement in decision making has been highlighted as a bioethical standard and an important element of patient and family focused care. However, literature regarding the roles of children and adolescents in shared-decision making for cancer care has been limited, particularly in LMICs.31 In this study, most caregivers reported that nobody talked to their child about their treatment, consistent with the preferences of many caregivers. In both Urdu- and Pashto-speaking communities in Pakistan, parents may want to avoid telling their child about a cancer diagnosis if they feel they are not old enough to understand their disease. However, almost half of caregivers who reported that nobody talked to their child preferred someone would have. This included 22% of caregivers who stated a preference for talking to the child themselves, but reported they did not do it, suggesting disempowerment or discomfort with these conversations. These findings further implicate the potential role of the clinician to empower the parent or other bedside caregiver, not only to make decisions, but to include the child as desired. Interestingly, there were no associations between caregiver preferences and child’s age. However, experiences did differ by age, with older children more likely to be included in communication, suggesting clinicians may be more likely to include adolescents regardless of caregiver preference. Future work is needed to investigate the paediatric patient’s perspective including their preferences and experiences.

Fortunately, most caregivers included in our study did not endorse regret. In fact, fewer caregivers in Pakistan endorsed regret at diagnosis than seen in studies conducted in the USA32 or Guatemala.17 This may be related to cultural values, as there is some evidence linking regret to the endorsement of individual or collectivist values,33 although this should be further investigated in future work. We did not note any association between regret and caregivers having their preferred role in decision making; however, correlative findings may have been limited by the small number of caregivers expressing regret.

Additional limitations of this study include that paediatric patients were not included as respondents. This is particularly relevant to findings related to their inclusion in communication. Furthermore, this study was conducted at only two of the centres providing childhood cancer care in Pakistan. While these are the two largest centres and serve a diverse population likely representative of the country, it is possible that caregiver priorities and experiences differ elsewhere. Additionally, this survey was conducted in Pashto and Urdu, thus results are limited to caregivers who spoke those languages. The survey utilised a three-point Likert scale which may have limited our ability to capture nuance or subtle variations. However, this scale has been used with caregivers of paediatric cancer patients in other LMICs, where the limited response options improved reliability.24 A strength of the study included the involvement of multidisciplinary clinicians on study teams across both sites who were members of the communities engaged in the research and were involved in survey development, and the consideration of caregiver perspectives in survey domain choice and wording.

Overall, this study demonstrates the importance of considering culture and caregiver preferences when approaching decisions for paediatric cancer care. Our findings advocate for a more inclusive model of shared decision making, one that respects family structure and cultural norms, while simultaneously engaging and empowering bedside caregivers. This model of deep and empowered communication may seem overwhelming and time intensive, particularly in resource-constrained settings where there is limited communication training and many competing priorities for clinician time. However, diagnosis is a particularly critical time for communication and structured time upfront may engender trust that not only saves the clinical team time later but encourages treatment adherence.34 35 Our findings differ from previous work conducted in other settings, highlighting cultural differences which, in Pakistan, may be related to collectivism, gender roles, deference to medical providers or other aspects of this unique and socioculturally diverse setting. These findings encourage a more tailored approach to diagnostic conversations in clinical settings, and we hope they will influence interventions that improve childhood cancer communication in Pakistan. Future work should continue to investigate paediatric cancer decision making in different LMICs and cultural contexts. Additional studies that explore the role of paediatric patients outside of high-income Western settings are needed and should include adolescent respondents.
