When context matters: social disparities in preventable hospitalizations among Medicare beneficiaries with dementia
Authors: Viktoryia A Kalesnikava, Brian Madden, Nela Vugdalic, Paul Lin, Elham Mahmoudi, Michelle Putnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesWe examined geographic variation in the risk of potentially preventable hospitalization (PPH) among individuals with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias.Research Design and MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we analyzed a 20% random sample of 2017–2018 traditional Medicare claims (n = 67,110), merged with the Area Health Resource File and National Neighborhood Data Archive. Multivariable generalized estimating equation models adjusted for age, sex, frailty index, rurality (metropolitan; micropolitan; rural), and county-level numbers of primary care physicians and hospitals. PPH risk was assessed for the overall cohort, residents of highly disadvantaged areas, and those in rural-or-micropolitan areas.ResultsOne-third of hospitalizations were PPHs. Urinary tract infection (33.8%), heart failure (25.7%), and pneumonia (18.6%) accounted for 78% of all PPHs. Unadjusted PPH rates were higher in highly disadvantaged areas (23.9%) compared to low-disadvantaged areas (20.6%). In the overall sample, Black and Hispanic individuals had higher odds of PPH than White individuals, as did those in highly disadvantaged areas compared to low-disadvantaged areas. Among highly disadvantaged urban and rural-or-micropolitan area residents, adjusted PPH rates were 23% and 27%, respectively. Odds of PPH were markedly higher in highly disadvantaged rural-or-micropolitan areas compared with low-disadvantaged areas. Racial/ethnic disparities diminished in these areas, while risk increased for female adults compared with male adults.Discussion and ImplicationsThe highest PPH risk occurred in highly disadvantaged rural-or-micropolitan areas. Although racial/ethnic disparities were attenuated in these populations, the overall PPH risk was elevated. Policy efforts should prioritize expanding access to ambulatory care in disadvantaged areas.Keywords: Race/ethnicity, Rurality, Disadvantaged communities, Preventable hospitalization, Female

This study is innovative in its integration of large-scale Medicare claims with granular sociodemographic and healthcare resource data to identify geographic and social disparities in potentially preventable hospitalizations (PPHs) among beneficiaries with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD). By focusing on how place-based disadvantages shape PPH risk, the study goes beyond individual-level explanations and offers insights that can inform upstream policy changes and public health interventions. The translational impact lies in guiding the targeted expansion of ambulatory care infrastructure, transportation, and community-based support for people with ADRD, thereby advancing health equity and reducing avoidable hospitalizations among vulnerable older adults.

In 2024, the estimated cost of healthcare for approximately seven million U.S. adults with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) was $360 billion, a number projected to surpass $1 trillion by 2050, together with the expected doubling of dementia prevalence. While Medicare and Medicaid cover about 68% of these costs, patients and their families must cover the rest (Alzheimer’s Association, 2024; Wong, 2020). People with ADRD are significantly more likely to be admitted or readmitted to the hospital compared to individuals without ADRD; thus, hospitalization accounts for the greatest proportion of their direct medical costs (Shepherd et al., 2019). Research shows that people with ADRD are also at a substantially greater risk of hospital-acquired complications, including delirium, infections, and falls, which, in turn, fuel a vicious cycle of future hospitalizations that compound both individual suffering and societal healthcare costs (Han et al., 2022).

Many hospitalizations are potentially preventable because they could have been avoided if patients had timely access to ambulatory care (Mahmoudi et al., 2020). People with chronic conditions and disabilities, such as those with ADRD, are especially at risk for potentially preventable hospitalizations (PPHs) due to their complex physical and mental health needs (Khan et al., 2022). Substantive research demonstrates that access to primary care services is a key preventive measure for PPH (Ridge et al., 2022). For example, among Medicare beneficiaries, higher use of primary care services and better continuity of care are likely to reduce PPH rates (Amjad et al., 2016).

PPHs represent non-value-added healthcare utilization, which is commonly measured via diagnosed chronic and acute conditions that can typically be managed through outpatient care. For example, diabetic complications such as foot ulcers, lower-limb amputations, and severe episodes of hypoglycemia can be prevented through regular blood glucose monitoring and annual foot exams (Smith et al., 2022). Similarly, hospitalizations for complicated urinary tract infections, a common and costly condition among older adults that can lead to serious health consequences, can be avoided through timely diagnosis, patient education, and early treatment (Lodise et al., 2021). At the system’s level, PPH is regarded as a proxy indicator of healthcare quality and access, because reliance on diagnostic codes makes this measure scalable and comparable across contexts (Sara et al., 2021). Yet, at the patient level, determining which hospitalizations are truly preventable is challenging, as these depend on the interaction between individual use behavior (i.e., seeking care, adherence, etc.) and contextual factors, including availability of primary and mental health clinics, insurance coverage options, and community support services (Johnston et al., 2020).

Furthermore, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis show that among people with ADRD, PPHs are associated with comorbidities and mobility limitations rather than dementia severity alone, emphasizing the importance of addressing primary medical and functional needs alongside dementia-specialized care (Shepherd et al., 2019). A recent national study of hospitalized people with ADRD found that over 40% of all admissions for acute or chronic conditions could have been potentially prevented (Anderson et al., 2020).

However, preventive and ambulatory care services are not distributed equitably across the United States (Anderson et al., 2020). Rural communities and racial and ethnic minority groups often reside in medically underserved and resource-poor areas, where primary care is limited, specialty clinics are not within a reasonable driving distance, and healthcare plans may be of lower quality (Planey et al., 2023). Growing research demonstrates disparities in PPH and emergency utilization between Black and Hispanic people with ADRD and White Medicare enrollees, where some of the observed gaps in healthcare use were attributed to the differences in socioeconomic environments (Gilmore‐Bykovskyi et al., 2023; Park et al., 2022).

Systematic evidence further shows an association between low socioeconomic status or living in poverty-segregated areas and increased rates of PPH (Wallar et al., 2020). Similar disparities have been documented for people with ADRD, with higher PPH rates found among older adults experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage (Cai et al., 2025). Finally, while female sex disparities in ADRD are well-documented, with higher prevalence among older women and those with greater socioeconomic disadvantage (Zubiagirre et al., 2024), studies show the opposite trend in such health outcomes as hospitalization and emergency use after ADRD diagnoses (Lusk et al., 2025). Understanding how PPH risk varies by place, and which contextual and individual factors drive these place-based disparities among people with ADRD, can provide policy-relevant insights into reducing PPH at scale.

In this study, we used 2017–2018 traditional Medicare (TM) claims data to examine PPH risk among people with ADRD in different residential settings: (1) the overall sample; (2) socioeconomically disadvantaged areas; and (3) rural-or-micropolitan areas. Consistent with prior research, we hypothesized that beneficiaries living in disadvantaged and rural-or-micropolitan areas will have the highest risk of PPH, compared to living in less disadvantaged areas. Following the affordances framework (LaVeist et al., 2011; Roux, 2016), we argue that these place-based disparities in PPH risk reflect not only individual factors but primarily multidimensional constraints in social and material resources that systematically limit the opportunities to meet basic health and care needs, making theoretically preventable hospitalizations practically unavoidable. The place-based affordances framework posits that socioeconomic dimensions (e.g., educational and employment opportunities, residential composition, and networks) and features of physical environment may shape individual behaviors, lifestyles, and aging trajectories (LaVeist et al., 2011; Roux, 2016). Consistent with this framing, we further hypothesized that racial and ethnic disparities in PPH risk within disadvantaged areas are probably narrower because structural limitations may converge risk across beneficiaries living in similar settings. However, following prior evidence, PPH risk among rural residents and male beneficiaries in more disadvantaged areas might be elevated (Lusk et al., 2025).

Methods

Data

We used a 20% random sample of 2017–2018 TM beneficiaries. We utilized the Master Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF) for enrollment information and demographic characteristics. We also used the Medicare Provider and Analysis Review (MedPAR), the Outpatient Claims and Outpatient Revenue, the Carrier (also referred to as “Part B”) Claims and Line Items, and the Durable Medical Equipment files [Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). FFS Data (2015–2023)]. To include information about rurality, number of hospitals, primary care physicians (PCPs) at the county level, and Medicare Advantage market share, we merged the claims with the 2019–2020 Area Health Resources File (AHRF), using publicly available annual cross-walk files between Zip Codes in claims data and Federal Information Processing Standards codes [Data Lumos. United States Department of Health and Human Services. Health Resources and Services Administration. Area Health Resource Files (V1)]. Socioeconomic status of the neighborhoods was obtained by merging our claims data with the 2016–2020 National Neighborhood Data Archive (NaNDA). NaNDA compiles data from the Census Bureau and American Community Survey.

Sample selection

To ensure a complete and accurate account of all healthcare utilization during the study period, all beneficiaries included in the cohort were required to be continuously enrolled in traditional Medicare for two continuous years (2017–2018). We included individuals aged 65 and older. We identified individuals with ADRD using the Bynum algorithm, which defines ADRD as a primary diagnosis from one inpatient stay or two or more outpatient visits at least 7 days apart (see Supplementary Table 1 for diagnosis codes for ADRD) (Grodstein et al., 2022). Since we were examining PPH risk, we included people with at least one hospitalization in 2018. According to AHRQ guidelines, any inpatient stays that were the result of a transfer from another facility (including a skilled nursing facility) were included in the count of all hospitalizations (denominator) but excluded from the count of PPH (numerator) (AHRQ. Prevention Quality Indicators in Inpatient Settings Measures. PQI Resources. 2024). Our final sample size was n = 67,110. The schematic flow of the sample size is presented in Supplementary Figure 1.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was the occurrence of PPH in 2018 (yes = 1; no = 0). Applying the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) definition, we utilized the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM), Current Procedural Terminology 4, and the Healthcare Procedural Coding System to define PPHs (AHRQ. Prevention Quality Indicators in Inpatient Settings Measures. PQI Resources. 2024). We applied the previously published algorithm defined by AHRQ to identify PPH (Mahmoudi et al., 2024).

Controls

Our analytic models controlled for age, sex, racial and ethnic status, frailty index, number of office visits during 2017, rurality, number of available PCPs, and residents per 1000, number of hospitals per 100,000, Medicare Advantage market share, and socioeconomic status of the area. Age (continuous variable), sex (binary), and race/ethnicity (categorical; Black, Hispanic, Asian, White [reference], and other) were obtained from the MBSF. Frailty index (continuous) was constructed using a previously published algorithm that relies on diagnosis and procedure codes (Cheng et al., 2021). Given the limited clinical detail available in claims data, the frailty index is commonly used as a proxy for dementia severity (Park et al., 2023). The higher number indicates a higher level of frailty. Since older adults’ frailty may change over time, we used data from the past 365 days before the first hospitalization in 2018 to estimate it. The number of office visits was counted using Carrier and outpatient files during 2017. Rurality (categorical: metropolitan, micropolitan, and rural), number of PCPs and residents per 1,000 (continuous), and number of hospitals per 100,000 (continuous) were extracted from the AHRF file. AHRF includes county-level geographic classifications derived from standard federal definitions. Metropolitan areas contain at least one urbanized area with ≥50,000 population, and micropolitan areas contain at least one urban cluster with 10,000–49,999 population. Counties not classified as metropolitan or micropolitan are considered rural (HRSA).

Disadvantaged area (ranging from 0 to 1) is an average of four census indicators obtained from NaNDA at the ZIP Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA) level (proportions of female-headed families with children, households with public assistance income or food stamps, families with income below the federal poverty level, and unemployment) (NaNDA, 2020). For ease of interpretation, we categorized the continuous variable representing the probability of living in a disadvantaged area based on its empirical distribution in our data. Specifically, individuals with scores at or above the 95th percentile were classified as highly disadvantaged, those between the 75th and 94th percentiles as moderately disadvantaged, and those below the 75th percentile as low disadvantaged (Lin et al., 2025; Ratakonda et al., 2023). We created a separate category for individuals with missing geographic information (n = 7,655, 11.41%) and included this category in all our regression models. Characteristics of these beneficiaries are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

We examined bivariate analyses of demographic and patient characteristics between those with at least one PPH and no PPH in 2018. For continuous variables, means and standard deviations were compared using the Student t-test. For categorical variables, column percentages were compared using the chi-squared test. The unadjusted rate of PPH was computed as the ratio of the total number of PPHs to the total number of hospitalizations in 2018. We used generalized linear models with a binary distribution and log link function to examine the risk of PPH (Mahmoudi et al., 2020). To address clustering of individuals at the ZCTA and county level, we used a generalized estimating equation (Hardin, 2005). Furthermore, we developed additional models to examine PPH risk in (1) highly disadvantaged areas and (2) rural-or-micropolitan areas.

We conducted two sensitivity analyses addressing the robustness of results against alternative categorizations of neighborhood disadvantage: (1) modeling neighborhood disadvantage as a continuous variable and (2) categorizing it into quintiles based on its empirical distribution. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis by including an interaction term between sex (female) and race/ethnicity in the model.

Statistical testing was two-tailed, with a significance level of alpha = 0.05. We estimated the least square means and odds ratios based on our adjusted regression models. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). Our Institutional Review Board approved this study.

Results

The average number of PCPs in disadvantaged (0.82 vs 0.84) and rural-or-micropolitan (0.58 vs 0.59) areas was substantially lower than the overall sample average (Supplementary Tables 3–4).

Finally, we found no differences in PPH by rurality within socioeconomically disadvantaged areas. However, within rural-or-micropolitan areas, we observed a proportional increase in the adjusted predicted probability of PPH with increasing levels of disadvantage. Specifically, compared to beneficiaries living in low-disadvantaged areas, relative odds of PPHs were 53% (OR = 1.53; 95% CI: 1.26, 1.85) and 22% (OR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.10, 1.35) higher among beneficiaries in highly and moderately disadvantaged areas, respectively. All regression results are presented in Supplementary Tables 5–7.

Our sensitivity analysis showed that modeling disadvantage as a continuous variable supports our main findings. A one-unit increase in area disadvantage (range 0–1) was associated with 2.35 times higher odds of PPH (95% CI: 1.86, 2.98). Quintile-based analyses were consistent, showing higher odds of PPH associated with living in extremely (OR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.17, 1.34) and highly disadvantaged areas (OR = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.06, 1.21).

Modeling the interaction between sex (female) and race/ethnicity showed that Black female beneficiaries had higher odds of PPH (OR = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.21), while Asian female adults had lower odds (OR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.72, 0.99) compared with their White counterparts. All sensitivity results are presented in Supplementary Tables 8–10.

Discussion

Using 2017–2018 TM claims data linked to administrative sources, this cross-sectional study examined the association of place-based structural differences and PPH risk among Medicare beneficiaries with ADRD in the overall sample, socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, and rural-or-micropolitan areas. Our findings are fourfold. (1) Across all models, beneficiaries with ADRD living in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas had higher odds of PPH compared to those living in low-disadvantaged areas. (2) In the full sample, Black and Hispanic people had higher odds of PPH compared with White beneficiaries. However, we did not observe these racial/ethnic disparities within subgroups living in socioeconomically disadvantaged or rural-or-micropolitan areas, suggesting that area-level factors may account for much of the observed racial/ethnic disparities. (3) Although rural residence was associated with lower adjusted odds of PPH in the full sample, within rural-or-micropolitan areas, higher levels of area disadvantage were associated with increased risk of PPH. (4) Finally, across all models, female adults had higher odds of PPH compared to male adults, with wider sex differences observed among beneficiaries living in disadvantaged and rural-or-micropolitan areas.

Consistent with our hypothesis, our overall findings demonstrated that Medicare beneficiaries with ADRD experience persistent place-based PPH disparities, with the highest cumulative prevalence among beneficiaries living in more disadvantaged areas. These findings align with the place-based affordances framework (Roux, 2016) and prior work showing that people with ADRD in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas commonly experience significant structural barriers that can inhibit timely access to ambulatory care, including limited healthcare infrastructure, a fragmented care continuum due to scarcity of medical workforce, and high rates of burnout among the providers (Henry, 2024). Indeed, in our study, the average number of PCPs in disadvantaged areas was substantially lower than the overall sample average.

Our findings also emphasize the role of area disadvantage as an important catalyst for rural disparities in PPH risk. Our overall model showed that rural residence (vs metropolitan) was associated with lower odds of PPH, illustrating averaged estimates across various levels of disadvantage. However, within rural-or-micropolitan areas, we observed a compounding effect of higher levels of disadvantage. This pattern supports our study hypothesis and corresponds with existing research identifying structural barriers linked to area disadvantage as fundamental drivers of rural health disparities (Arsenault-Lapierre et al., 2023). People with ADRD living in more rural areas tend to experience poorer health outcomes, including higher mortality and hospitalization rates, often associated with limited availability of primary and specialty care and lower use of home health services (Arsenault-Lapierre et al., 2023). In our sample, beneficiaries with ADRD living in rural-or-micropolitan areas who experienced any PPH (vs non-preventable hospitalizations) were more likely to be female, frail, and have lower healthcare access. People with ADRD in rural-or-micropolitan areas are more likely to live in poverty with lower educational attainment (Kang et al., 2025). Public transportation either does not exist or is poorly managed in rural areas, disproportionately affecting people with ADRD who, because of reliance on others for their transportation needs, may put off or forgo their non-urgent care (Gordon et al., 2025).

Furthermore, although in rural-or-micropolitan areas, access to ambulatory care services is limited for everyone, a shortage of healthcare providers and rural hospital closures may make it more challenging for female older adults to utilize fragmented transportation to get care (Gordon et al., 2025). Our results demonstrated that female beneficiaries with ADRD had significantly higher odds of PPH than male beneficiaries. Consistent with past research, women living in disadvantaged and rural-or-micropolitan areas are more likely to experience cost-related nonadherence, lower health literacy, and transportation-related delays in care (Lee et al., 2020). However, this finding contrasted with our hypothesis, informed by recent evidence showing higher all-cause hospitalizations and healthcare use among male Medicare beneficiaries with ADRD (Lusk et al., 2025); this discrepancy may in part reflect systematic bias due to differential measurement or selective survival by sex and comorbidity (Rotstein et al., 2025).

Our final findings supported our study hypothesis that plausibly area-level factors have a leveling influence on racial and ethnic disparities in PPH among Hispanic and Black beneficiaries with ADRD compared with their White counterparts. Although overall adjusted-PPH risk was higher among Hispanic and Black beneficiaries compared with White adults, when we limited our sample to those in highly disadvantaged or rural-or-micropolitan areas, we did not observe any Black-White or Hispanic-White disparities.

The attenuation of risk in highly disadvantaged areas may reflect floor or ceiling effects rather than the true estimate of risk. Empirical evidence shows that Black and Hispanic people disproportionally live in disadvantaged neighborhoods (Doshi et al., 2017), where the clustering of structural constraints (e.g., limited healthcare infrastructure or transportation barriers) may chronically elevate baseline risk. In this context, statistical adjustment alone may not explain persistent racial and ethnic disparities across pooled levels of disadvantage (Fiscella & Sanders, 2016). Yet, when the sample is restricted to the areas with high disadvantage, it is plausible that the estimated risk across racial and ethnic groups converges under similar constraints, even if the true nature of disadvantage experienced by Black or Hispanic groups may differ qualitatively from that of White groups (LaVeist et al., 2011).

This pattern may be further reflected in the mixed empirical evidence. Although literature shows persistence of racial and ethnic disparities in health outcomes, including among adults with ADRD (Hinton et al., 2024), findings are inconsistent when comparing individuals living in similar social contexts. For example, Wilson-Frederick et al. (2014) show that Black-White differences in physical inactivity substantially narrow for those who live in a similar environment. Similarly, Thorpe et al. (2013) show no Black-White disparity in receiving preventive care services between Black and White men living in the same healthcare marketplace. However, other studies show that racial disparities in such outcomes as 30-day readmissions (Gilmore‐Bykovskyi et al., 2023) or end-of-life hospitalizations (Temkin‐Greener et al., 2021) persist among people with ADRD even within similar social and geographic contexts. A recent study further found that although racial disparities in hospitalizations among people with ADRD remained across similar social contexts, their magnitude and direction varied (Poghosyan et al., 2025) emphasizing the moderating role of social environment.

In sum, reducing PPHs, without increasing risk of harm to medically or socially vulnerable populations, including those with ADRD, remains an economic and public health imperative (USDHHS). Study findings contribute to the evidence showing that prevention efforts to reduce PPH should focus on improving community-level resource availability among high-risk populations living in a disadvantaged context (Hogg-Graham et al., 2024). Policies aimed at lowering PPHs must address structural factors such as shortages of PCPs and unreliable or unaffordable transportation affecting residents of underserved areas, particularly older adults living with ADRD. Investment in improving access to healthcare or enabling older adults to better utilize telehealth might reduce the PPH risk, particularly those in socially disadvantaged areas (Chen et al., 2024).

Our study had a few noteworthy limitations. First, about half of Medicare beneficiaries are now enrolled in one of the Medicare Advantage plans. Using only traditional Medicare claims, our results are not representative of all Medicare beneficiaries. Second, traditional Medicare is also no longer a homogeneous plan. Many Accountable Care Organizations (ACOs) work like health maintenance organizations in Medicare Advantage and are incentivized to provide coordinated care to prevent expensive non-value-added services. It is plausible that PPH risk is lower among beneficiaries receiving their primary care services through ACOs in TM. We did not have access to files that identify ACOs in TM and could not account for ACOs in this study. Third, administrative claims data inherently lack non-medical information on caregiver availability, educational attainment, health literacy, and lifestyle factors, all of which are important predictors of PPH risk. However, claims data allow for the use of medical codes and detailed service-use information to objectively identify PPHs. Finally, although we followed the guidelines provided by AHRQ to identify PPHs, we acknowledge that not all PPHs are preventable. In many cases, a PPH is the only way a patient can get timely care.

In conclusion, our national study of Medicare beneficiaries with ADRD revealed persistent social disparities in PPH in later life. Area-level disadvantage was consistently associated with high PPH risk, including within rural and micropolitan areas, emphasizing the role of structural barriers in underserved communities. Female beneficiaries experienced a particularly high risk of PPH, especially in disadvantaged and rural areas, a finding that expands prior evidence, suggesting that PPH risk among high-risk female beneficiaries with ADRD warrants closer examination in future studies. The racial and ethnic risk gradient observed when examining the PPH risk across the full sample was substantially attenuated within disadvantaged and rural-or-micropolitan areas, possibly reflecting the high baseline risk in these communities. Our findings have implications for public health policies aiming to reduce PPH among older adults with ADRD, need to improve access to primary care, and strengthen local healthcare infrastructure, especially in socially vulnerable areas. To further inform policy decisions, future research should examine the downstream consequences of PPH for older adults living with dementia and how PPH may alter trajectories of health and independence, including decline in quality of life, transition to long-term care, caregiver burden, and early mortality.
