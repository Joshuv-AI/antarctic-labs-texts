Novel and High‐Throughput Method of Isolating Single Fetal Cells Using FACS for NIPT
Authors: Ripudaman Singh, Bolette Hestbek Nicolaisen, Mathias Kølvraa, Inga Baasch Christensen, Mads Tang Dalsgaard, Lars Henning Pedersen, Niels Uldbjerg, Ida Vogel, Lotte Hatt, Katarina Ravn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) as a method of single‐cell isolation of rare circulating fetal cells from maternal blood for use in cell‐based non‐invasive prenatal testing (cbNIPT).MethodBlood samples (30 mL) were collected from 75 ‘low‐risk’ pregnant women (gestational age 10–15 weeks). Fetal cells were enriched and stained using magnetic activated cell sorting. Following enrichment, single fetal cells were sorted in individual PCR tubes by FACS. After cell lysis, verification of fetal cell origin was performed using short tandem repeat (STR) analysis with the GlobalFiler PCR Amplification kit.ResultsAn average of 13.7 cells were sorted using FACS. STR analysis identified 8.2 fetal cells on average, representing 60.2% of the sorted cells. The four‐step single‐cell isolation procedure facilitated an overall enrichment of approximately 16‐million‐fold. One sample did not render any fetal cell, corresponding to 1.3% of the samples.ConclusionFACS, which is typically used for segregation of large populations of cells, can be used for single‐cell isolation of rare fetal cells in an automated setup. This not only helps in making cell isolation faster and high throughput but also provides fetal cells for a more comprehensive genetic analysis of the fetus.Keywords: aneuploidies, copy number variants, fetal cells in maternal blood, fluorescence activated cell sorting, noninvasive prenatal testing, prenatal diagnosis

Introduction

Fetal cells circulate in the maternal blood during pregnancy. Detection and isolation of these cells provide access to the fetal genome, which is applicable to the emerging technology called cell‐based non‐invasive prenatal testing (cbNIPT). At an early stage of gestation, cbNIPT has demonstrated detection of fetal aneuploidies as well as smaller copy number variants (CNVs) [1, 2]. cbNIPT offers a safe alternative to CVS and amniocentesis, which both require invasive tissue sampling, hence associated with a small risk of unintended pregnancy termination [3]. Furthermore, cbNIPT provides access to the intact fetal genome without a maternal DNA background and may therefore enable detection of fetal genomic abnormalities beyond those accessible through conventional NIPT, which is based on the analysis of cell‐free fetal DNA (cffDNA) in maternal blood [4]. Thus, cbNIPT has the potential to circumvent challenges with maternal malignancies and maternal obesity observed in cell‐free NIPT (cfNIPT) [5, 6].

The major hindrance for a widespread clinical implementation of cbNIPT has been the extreme rarity of circulating fetal cells, complicating their enrichment and single‐cell isolation. Enrichment approaches have exploited both physical and immunological affinity‐based properties [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, since contamination from maternal blood cells is inevitable following enrichment, single‐cell isolation is essential to obtain a pure fetal genome for genetic analysis. For clinical applicability, technology for single‐cell isolation must be time‐efficient, non‐subjective, scalable, and preserve cell integrity during cell capture.

Fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) has the potential to fulfill these requirements. Thousands of cells per second can be quantitatively analyzed across multiple parameters, and single cells can be rapidly and automatically sorted [11]. This technology has been used for the detection of rare cells such as circulating tumor cells and circulating endothelial cells [12]. However, FACS has not been widely reported as a successful strategy for rare fetal cell isolation. In 1979, Herzenberg et al. were the first to pursue FACS for enrichment and detection of circulating fetal cells [13]. Cells were sorted directly on glass slides, and fetal cells were identified using quinacrine staining to detect the Y‐chromosome. In the following decades, subsequent studies reported bulk sorting of circulating fetal nucleated red blood cells (nRBCs) and identifying them using Y‐fluorescence activated cell sorting, although with low yield and purity [14, 15]. Since then, FACS has generally been considered technically challenging for fetal cell isolation due to its extremely low frequency in maternal blood. It has been estimated that 2 to 6 fetal cells are present in 1 mL of maternal blood [16, 17]. This includes all varieties of circulating fetal cells, including nRBCs and fetal extravillous trophoblasts (fEVTs). When only targeting fEVTs, which have shown to be the most promising candidates for cbNIPT in the first trimester, the frequency is even lower. The aim of the study was to develop a method of single‐cell isolating fEVTs (from here on referred to as ‘fetal cells’) using FACS. We present results from 75 pregnant women where fetal cells were enriched and stained using magnetic activated cell sorting (MACS), followed by single‐cell isolation using FACS. Single cells were lysed and subjected to short tandem repeat (STR) analysis to confirm their fetal origin. Total as well as average fetal cell numbers from every sample were calculated.

Methods

Preparation of Blood and Fetal Cell Enrichment

The study was approved by The Central Denmark Committees on Health Research Ethics (1‐10‐72‐225‐19). All participants gave informed written consent prior to blood sampling. Pregnant women in gestational ages (GAs) of 10 + 0 to 14 + 6 weeks, with a combined first‐trimester screening risk < 1:300 (low‐risk), were recruited from Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark. Thirty milliliters of peripheral blood was drawn from the women in Cell‐Free DNA BCT Tubes (Streck, Omaha, USA). Within 48 h of collection. The blood was fixed in formaldehyde followed by red blood cell lysis as previously described [18]. After washing and pelleting in PBS, the cells were incubated with CD105 and CD141 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions (Patent: US 9.429,520 B2). The incubated cells were added to one Miltenyi column (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and washed with PBS. The positive cells retained in the column were eluted in a tube and added to another column where they were stained with a cocktail of cytokeratin antibodies, directly conjugated to an AF488 fluorophore. Furthermore, the cells were stained with antibodies targeting the leukocyte markers CD45 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, USA) and CD14 (Thermo Fischer Scientific, USA) conjugated to PE. Following staining, the cells were washed in PBS and stained with Hoechst 33,342 (Sigma Aldrich).

Single‐Cell Isolation Using FACS

The enriched and stained cells were sorted on a FACS Melody (BD Biosciences, USA) using 405 nm, 488 nm, and 561 nm lasers. Fluorescence signals were collected with the respective bandpass filters: 448/45 nm (Hoechst 33,342), 527/32 nm (AF488/FITC) and 585/40 nm (PE). A 100 μm nozzle was used for sorting. Hierarchical gating was applied by first excluding events with SSC‐A higher than 250,000 to avoid cell debris. Next, the remaining cells were gated for Hoechst+, which subsequently were gated for CK+CD45−CD14. This gate was pre‐set for all samples, but because the location of the main population differed between samples due to biological variation, the gate was adjusted if necessary. The cells were individually sorted into 200 μL PCR tubes containing 5 μL TE buffer and placed in a 96‐well PCR plate. The sorted cells were kept in a −80oC freezer until STR analysis. This technology used for single‐cell sorting of fetal cells is patented (US 11,573,229 B2). The workflow from blood processing to single cell isolation of fetal cells using FACS took approximately 5 and half hours.

STR Analysis

STR was performed on all single sorted cells to count the number of isolated fetal cells. Cells were lysed using PrepGem Universal (ZyGEM, New Zealand) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Maternal genomic DNA used in the STR profiling was isolated from maternal whole blood using the Maxwell RSC Whole Blood DNA Kit Maxwell (Promega, USA).

The STR kit GlobalFiler PCR amplification (Themo Fischer Scientific, USA) was applied which analyzes 24 STR loci in multiplexes, and the genotypic profile was generated with fragment size capillary electrophoresis using an ABI 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fischer Scientific, USA). Data analysis was performed using GeneMapper ID‐X fragment size analyzer software (Thermo Fischer Scientific, USA).

Results

Typically, following RBC lysis, 30 mL of whole blood sample is left with 1.35‐3.3 × 108 (average 2.3 × 108) of white blood cells. After enrichment and staining on MACS, the cells were sorted on a FACS Melody with hierarchical gating. Figure 1A‐B shows a typical scatter plot, Hoechst+ plot, and a CK‐AF488 versus CD45‐PE/CD14‐PE plot. The median number of Hoechst positive cells was 202,560 (IQR 139,051). On average 13.7 cells were single‐cell sorted from every sample. STR analysis confirmed an average fetal cell number of 8.2 cells per sample (95% CI: 6.92–9.53), with a median of 7 (IQR 6.5). No fetal cells were detected in one sample, corresponding to 1.3% of the total samples. The four‐step method from blood processing to single‐cell sorting reduced the total cell population from 230 million to an average of 14 cells, of which 8 were fetal cells, yielding a final purity of 57%. Distribution of fetal cells is shown in Figure 2. Overall, this corresponds to an enrichment of approximately 16‐million‐fold (Figure 3). In total, single cells were sorted into 1025 tubes, of which 49 tubes (4.7%) did not render results after STR, indicating that the tubes were empty. The performance of the STR analysis on the isolated fetal cells was assessed by examining the number of fully informative loci and the degree of allele dropout (ADO). A fully informative locus was defined by the presence of one maternal allele, and one allele not matching the maternal genotype, implying a paternally inherited allele. Seven tubes (0.68%) showed a mixed maternal and fetal STR profile, whereas 3 tubes (0.29%) showed inconclusive STR results due to ADO. Discussion

For long, the extreme rarity of circulating fetal cells has limited the development of clinically viable cbNIPT technology. Prior studies have reported between 2 and 6 fetal cells per mL of maternal blood, where the fetal cell numbers were deciphered by measuring the presence of Y‐chromosome signals in interphase cells in samples from women carrying male fetuses. Moreover, no selective enrichment of fetal cells was performed; hence, these numbers were unbiased by cell‐type (Krabchi et al. Hamada et al.). By using a more specific protocol to isolate fetal trophoblast cells from maternal blood, we previously showed that on average 12.8 fetal cells could be isolated from 30 mL of maternal blood sample. [19] This method was also based on enrichment and staining of cells by MACS, followed by smearing the cell pellet on microscope slides. The slides were scanned using a fluorescence microscope, and positive fetal cells were identified using their unique nuclear and cytoskeletal staining patterns relative to the background maternal white blood cells. However, this method was laborious and relied heavily on subjective criteria to distinguish the right candidates among the negative maternal leukocytes. Moreover, cells immobilized to the glass slides were not accessible for downstream molecular genetic analyses. Fluorescence microscopes coupled with cell micromanipulators, especially capillary based cell picking instruments, have also been widely used for isolating fetal cells [2, 19, 20]. These techniques, though useful in getting access to the rare cells and using them for genetic analyses, came with their own limitations and challenges. Apart from being time‐consuming and relying on subjective criteria to identify positive fetal cells, these methods were also prone to cell contamination from unwanted maternal cells surrounding the fetal cell candidate. Specialized non‐capillary base microfluidic devices have also been used for the isolation of rare fetal cells. But these instruments are also time consuming and rely on subjective evaluation of cell morphology and staining patterns [10]. On the other hand, FACS can overcome these challenges. However, switching from staining patterns and morphology to a rapid cell‐sorting technology based solely on fluorescence parameters is neither direct nor obvious. FACS has previously been used as an enrichment step where potential positive cells are ‘bulk sorted’, before they are smeared on microscope slides and picked using a cell micromanipulator.

This study demonstrates that FACS can be used in consecutive samples to sort rare fetal cells individually and confirm their identity using molecular analysis. In consecutive maternal blood samples from 75 pregnant women, fetal cells could be isolated in 98.6% of the samples. Single‐cell sorting from one sample required approximately 20 min, which was significantly faster than capillary based instruments which typically require around 2 h to pick cells from one sample.

The average fetal cell count in this study was 8.2 cells, which is lower than 12.8 cells per sample that was reported earlier by us using the same enrichment and staining protocols, but by counting the cells using a fluorescence microscope. The lower yield of final positive cells after FACS can be explained by the fact that incorporating another sorting step before final cell count may lead to some level of cell loss. However, this is weighed off by FACS’ ability to collect cells faster, without subjectivity, and with high purity.

In this study, STR analysis was performed on cell lysates without the DNA being subjected to whole genome amplification (WGA). However, when the cells are used for whole genome sequencing, their DNA must be amplified, and a portion of the amplicons are then used for STR analysis to confirm the cell's fetal origin. Because the scope of this study was to demonstrate the use of FACS single‐cell sorting for isolating rare fetal cells, whole genome sequencing was not performed on the sorted cells.

Additionally, WGA kits suitable for CNV analysis do not necessarily perform well for STR analysis as the WGA polymerase is not optimized for the repetitive sequences utilized in STR analysis [21]. We have also observed that the fetal cells which are only subjected to STR analysis have a higher number of informative loci and almost no ADO, as compared to cells which underwent WGA prior to STR analysis (data not shown). Even though STR interpretation becomes less clear after WGA, STR genotyping in cbNIPT is only used to reliably distinguish between fetal and maternal cells, meaning a partial STR profiling is still sufficient for a clear determination of the origin of the cell. Single‐cell isolation using FACS, followed by WGA has been used by us to perform cbNIPT for screening of aneuploidies and monogenic disorders [22, 23]. In a recent study on 130 samples, a FACS single‐cell sorting step was used for isolating fetal cells, followed by analysis of aneuploidies and CNVs by whole genome sequencing. Among the 130 samples, 67 were from normal pregnancies, 27 showed aneuploidies, 5 had sex chromosome anomalies, 4 showed mosaics, 5 had deletions larger than 5 Mb, 11 had deletions and duplications between 1 and 5 Mb, and 11 had deletions and duplications between 0.3 and 1 Mb. [24]. However, in those studies not all cells sorted by FACS were analyzed and therefore the total number of fetal cells per sample was not deciphered.

No fetal cells were detected in one of the 75 samples, corresponding to 1.3% of the total samples. However, since the cells were not analyzed for aneuploidies or copy number variants, this does not necessarily reflect the final no‐call rate for cbNIPT. There were two samples that yielded one fetal cell each. Although we and others have shown that one fetal cell can be sufficient for performing prenatal genetic analysis, it is not evident from the data in the current study that the two samples with one cell each would have produced successful cbNIPT results. This becomes particularly challenging when the analyzed cells are trophoblasts, as they may be affected by placental mosaicism. Analysing additional cells may improve the likelihood of detecting placental mosaicism; however, confirming or excluding fetal involvement may still require follow‐up testing by amniocentesis.

FACS‐based isolation of circulating fetal cells may represent an important step toward scalable cell‐based non‐invasive prenatal testing and making it commercially and clinically viable. This method of isolating cells not only improves the turnaround time of single‐cell isolation but also renders individual cells without subjectivity bias.
