Barriers and facilitators to the use of clinical practice guidelines in osteoarthritis care: a qualitative study among German physiotherapists
Authors: Carolin Bahns, Lisa Happe, Christian Kopkow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objective of this study was to explore barriers and facilitators to the use of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) in osteoarthritis (OA) care among German physiotherapists.DesignQualitative semistructured interviews were conducted with German physiotherapists. An interview topic guide based on the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) was developed to explore perceived barriers and facilitators to the use of CPGs in OA care. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. In a first step, the data were coded inductively using thematic analysis. Subsequently, the emerged themes were deductively reassigned to the relevant domains of the TDF and categorised as barriers and facilitators.SettingOutpatient physiotherapy practices in Germany.ParticipantsPhysiotherapists working in an outpatient setting in Germany and involved in the management of patients with hip and knee OA were eligible to participate.ResultsEleven physiotherapists (7 women, 4 men) were interviewed between February and September 2023. The mean age was 34.2 years (± 9.9, range 23–57). Most factors influencing guideline-recommended care were identified in the TDF domains ‘knowledge’, ‘beliefs about consequences’, ‘environmental context and resources’ and ‘social influences’. For example, physiotherapists perceived low awareness of existing CPGs, loss of individuality in treatment, time constraints and dissonant patient expectations as barriers to the use of CPGs. Discussion of guideline recommendations within the team and the expectation of improved patient outcomes and resource savings were reported as facilitators.ConclusionsMultiple factors were identified that were perceived to influence clinical practice in Germany. The results of this study can inform the development of theory-based interventions aimed at changing physiotherapists’ behaviour to support them in providing optimal OA care.Trial registrationGerman Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00030800). Registered 12 December 2022.Keywords: qualitative research, decision making, clinical decision-making

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common chronic joint disease and, with more than 300 million cases worldwide, mainly affects weight-bearing joints such as the hip and knee.1 2 In Germany, the prevalence of hip and knee OA in older patients is 21.8% in outpatient settings, with OA being more common in women and increasing in prevalence with age.3 OA is characterised by a combination of mechanical, inflammatory and metabolic factors that lead to changes in the whole joint.2 4 Structural destruction and failure of the synovial joint result in pain, stiffness, functional disability and reduced quality of life.5 As a result of expanding life expectancy, rising cases of obesity and decreasing levels of physical activity, the OA population is expected to increase substantially, placing an increasing economic burden on healthcare systems.1 2

Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) systematically summarise the best available evidence on various diagnostic and therapeutic options for specific clinical conditions. The recommendations are intended to guide healthcare professionals and patients in clinical evidence-based decision-making to optimise the quality of care.6 In the absence of cure, current management of OA is mainly symptom-based and aims to relieve pain and improve physical function in order to maintain quality of life and prevent or delay surgery. Higher-quality guidelines for hip and knee OA, including those from the National Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE), the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and the OsteoArthritis Research Society International (OARSI), highlight the importance of non-pharmacological conservative therapy and consistently recommend patient education, exercise therapy and weight management (if overweight or obese) as core management strategies.7

Adherence to CPGs can improve patient outcomes, reduce the use of ineffective treatments and thus lower costs to the healthcare system.8 9 However, Zadro et al10 identified that the median percentage of physiotherapists providing guideline-recommended treatments for musculoskeletal conditions was 54%, while 43% chose treatments that were not recommended and 81% chose treatments for which there was no recommendation due to conflicting or lack of evidence. Non-adherence to guidelines relates to all healthcare professionals, for example, physicians, nurses or occupational therapists.11,13 The reasons are complex and manifold and may be related to for example, personal, external or guideline-related factors. Some of the most common barriers are time constraints, poor applicability of guideline recommendations in real-world practice, lack of knowledge and skills and poor motivation.14 In contrast, technical support, timely education and training for both healthcare professionals and patients have been identified as factors that can improve guideline adherence.14

Theory-driven, tailored implementation interventions are needed to increase guideline use and adherence.15 16 Identifying barriers and facilitators to the use of CPGs in clinical practice is essential for initiating implementation efforts.17 The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) is one of the most commonly used theories in healthcare settings to systematically explore factors that influence clinical behaviour and guideline adherence.18 Developed and validated by Michie and colleagues,19 the TDF synthesises 84 theoretical constructs across 33 psychological theories of behaviour and behaviour change into 14 (originally 12) domains20: knowledge; skills; social/professional role and identity; beliefs about capabilities; optimism; beliefs about consequences; reinforcement; intentions; goals; memory, attention and decision processes; environmental context and resources; social influences; emotion; and behavioural regulation. The TDF provides a robust theoretical basis for understanding, informing and facilitating effective behaviour change and, in conjunction with other tools (eg, the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW)21), a methodology for moving from investigation to intervention.22

In a recent online survey of German physiotherapists, only 17.2% of participants were considered fully adherent with CPGs for the management of hip OA and 8.6% for knee OA.23 Approximately half of the participants reported that they were not aware of any CPG for the management of OA. Even among those who were aware of a guideline, 38.9% reported not using the recommendations in clinical practice. Factors influencing the use of CPGs by healthcare professionals have been investigated in many studies, and a systematic review by Egerton et al24 focused specifically on barriers and facilitators in OA care. However, the perspective of physiotherapists was poorly represented due to a lack of data and little evidence from Germany was included. It can also be assumed that the transferability of the results to the national physiotherapy context is limited due to country-specific factors. In contrast to other countries, physiotherapy training in Germany is predominantly located at vocational schools, with only 3% of therapists graduating from university.25 In addition, direct access has not been implemented in Germany, thus physiotherapists are required to adhere to physicians’ prescriptions, which limits their therapeutic autonomy. Currently, two national CPGs26 27 are available to guide physiotherapists in the management of hip and knee OA. However, these are consensus-based and thus without a systematic literature search (although both guidelines are currently being updated to be evidence-based) and primarily address patient management by physicians.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore barriers and facilitators to the use of CPGs in OA care among German physiotherapists.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative design involving semistructured interviews among German physiotherapists was chosen to explore barriers and facilitators to the use of CPGs in OA care. Data were collected between February and September 2023. The study was a priori registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS-ID: DRKS00030800), which is linked to the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform from the WHO.28 This interview study is part of a larger project aimed at developing a theory-driven implementation intervention to facilitate the transfer of evidence from hip and knee OA guidelines into physiotherapy practice. The study protocol has been published elsewhere.29 The study deviated from the protocol, as the planned purposive sampling could not be carried out due to a low number of respondents. As a result, all participants were included until data saturation was reached, with the aim of achieving diversity in the sample in terms of age, gender, work experience and professional degree.

Reporting of the study followed the Consolidated Criterion for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.30

Participants

Physiotherapists working in an outpatient setting in Germany and involved in the management of patients with hip and knee OA were eligible to participate. Recruitment was initiated through announcements and calls on social media, various physiotherapy networks and personal contacts of the study team. Interviewed persons were allowed to suggest other potential participants (snowball sampling approach). A participant information sheet and consent form were emailed to those interested in taking part.

Recruitment continued until data saturation was reached (at least 10 participants). As proposed by Francis et al,31 data saturation was defined as the point at which three consecutive interviews had been conducted and no additional information about barriers and facilitators had emerged.

Data collection

A semistructured interview topic guide (see online supplemental file 1) was developed based on the TDF22 with at least one question for each of the 14 theoretical domains. The questions were informed by the results of a recently published survey of German physiotherapists23 and previous literature on barriers and facilitators to guideline use and evidence-based practice.24 32 33 Open-ended questions and prompts were used to ensure participants’ own experiences and views, followed by an increasing focus on specific issues based on the TDF. Participants who were unfamiliar with the content of CPGs for hip and knee OA were given a brief verbal summary of the recommendations to allow these participants to discuss potential barriers and facilitators. Demographic information was collected at the end of the interview using a short questionnaire. The interview topic guide was pilot tested with two physiotherapists to ensure clarity and avoid redundancy. The pilot interviews were not included in the data analysis of this study.

The interviews were conducted via online video technology (Cisco WebEx, version: 43.6.0.26407) or by telephone, with each interview lasting approximately 45–60 min. The date and time of the interview was arranged according to the participants’ preferences. All interviews were led by the first author (CB), a female physiotherapist (MSc) working as a research associate at the university, who was familiar with the TDF and trained to conduct interviews. There was no prior relationship between the interviewer and the participants. All interviews were audio-recorded. Apart from the first author and the participant, no other person was present during the interviews. No additional field notes were collected and no repeat interviews were carried out.

Data analysis

All interviews were automatically transcribed verbatim using Amberscript software (www.amberscript.com) and checked for accuracy against the original recordings by a research assistant and the first author. Personal identifying information was removed and pseudonyms were assigned to all participants to anonymise the content. Transcripts of the interviews were emailed to each participant for review, but participants were not asked for feedback on findings.

The interviews were analysed using both an inductive and a deductive analytical approach. A thematic analysis was performed following the six stages from Braun and Clarke34: (1) familiarisation with the data; (2) generating initial codes; (3) generating themes; (4) reviewing potential themes; (5) defining and naming themes; and (6) producing the report. After reading each transcript several times, two researchers (CB and LH) independently pilot coded two interviews. Initial codes were organised into common themes and subthemes, resulting in an initial coding framework. Any disagreements were discussed and recoded until consensus was reached. The remaining interviews were iteratively coded by the first author and regularly discussed with the research team for refinement. Finally, emerging themes were deductively mapped to relevant domains of the TDF and categorised as barriers and facilitators. The data were analysed using MAXQDA 2022 software.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Brandenburg University of Technology Cottbus-Senftenberg (EK2022-15). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.35 Study participation was anonymous and voluntary, with the option to withdraw/drop-out at any time. No incentives were provided for participation. All participants gave written informed consent before being interviewed.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Participants

Eighteen potential participants were sent participant information sheets and consent forms. Six potential participants did not respond to the second contact and one was excluded as he was not involved in OA care. Finally, interviews were conducted with 11 physiotherapists (7 females, 4 males) from six different German federal states. The mean age was 34.2 years (SD 9.9, range 23–57), the mean work experience was 11.2 years (SD 10.1, range 1.5–34). All but one of the participants had been trained as a physiotherapist at a vocational school. At the time of the interviews, three of the participants had already completed a bachelor’s degree (one was pursuing a master’s degree), while five were still enrolled in their bachelor’s programmes. All of the physiotherapists worked in an outpatient practice, treating between 2 and 15 patients with hip or knee OA per week. Three participants had a leading function in their workplace.

Nine interviews were conducted using online video technology (Webex App, version 43.8.0.26955), and two were conducted by telephone. The interviews lasted on average 48 min (range 31–67 min).

Barriers and facilitators

Key factors perceived by physiotherapists to influence the use of CPGs in the management of hip and knee OA were categorised into 13 TDF domains. The domain ‘goals’ was not applicable. No new categories were created beyond the existing TDF domains. A summary of barriers and facilitators is presented in online supplemental file 2.

Knowledge

Most participants stated that they were aware of OA guidelines or had at least heard of them without knowing the content in detail. However, they felt that CPGs generally do not play a role at all in clinical practice in Germany and that many colleagues lack knowledge about guidelines and their specific recommendations.

Yeah, and I know that in the practice where I work, clinical practice guidelines are not an issue… and that’s actually sad or sobering. (PT7)

The physiotherapists summarised that the professional degree has a major influence on guideline use in clinical practice, as CPGs are often not part of the training in vocational schools and only physiotherapists who graduated from university are familiar with them. What has been learnt in both professional education and postgraduate courses can be a major barrier. Many physiotherapists have developed their own routines over the years, and the guideline recommendations may contradict what they previously considered effective, increasing resistance to the guidelines. They stated that it is difficult to realise that what they have learnt and provided to patients with OA for years is not up to date and may not be evidence-based.

When I first started dealing with it, it sometimes hurt. To realise that, in terms of manual therapy, I was at best providing short-term pain relief, but otherwise achieving little or nothing structurally. Of course, once you learn that you can heal with your hands, it’s something you have to process, and I think a lot of people don’t process it. (PT1)

Some physiotherapists would like the OA guidelines to be more specific and provide clearer recommendations, while others find the CPGs very complex as they contain too much content, especially on interventions not relevant to physiotherapy. In addition, some physiotherapists expressed concerns because not all recommendations are based on the results of high-quality studies but on expert consensus, and the CPGs quickly become outdated. As in Germany physiotherapists are rarely represented in guideline development, some participants prefer to use CPGs from other countries as they are perceived as more trustworthy and are provided by physiotherapy associations (eg, from the Royal Dutch Society for Physiotherapy (KNGF)).

I always put German guidelines in big, bold brackets, even among colleagues, because the S2k [consensus-based recommendations] guidelines in Germany in particular should be treated with a bit of caution. (PT1)

Although the German OA guidelines are freely available on the internet, many physiotherapists do not know about the website where they can find them. Instead, physiotherapists obtain new knowledge from postgraduate courses where, according to the participants, the evidence from CPGs is often not taken into account. Physiotherapists with higher educational degree search databases for studies to keep up to date. Most participants also use social media and podcasts to receive evidence preprepared and easily accessible in the form of ‘information snacks’. However, they see misinformation and trends of unknown value as a major barrier to treating patients according to CPGs.

I see the influence of pseudo-science in physiotherapy as the biggest factor here. Yeah. Not even the conditions, but mainly this. (PT1)

Some physiotherapists stated that they would be more motivated to engage with CPGs if they were shorter and limited to the essential aspects for physiotherapy. Infographics and checklists could also be helpful to make the content easier to understand. Many felt that physiotherapy associations and employers in particular have a duty to inform physiotherapists about CPGs. Printed materials (eg, booklets) have been requested more often as they can be consulted quickly.

Or maybe get the associations on board. Well, we have the physiotherapy associations. If you’re a member, and I think most superiors are, that’s largely the case. That maybe you go back to them. That you make flyers or things like that available again and say: ‘Man, just put this in your practice.’ Or I don’t know, that- well, I could imagine that. Yeah. (PT11)

However, as one of the most important approaches to making physiotherapists aware of CPGs, the participants saw a renewal of the professional education, with most preferring that physiotherapists only graduate from university in the future.

Skills

Physiotherapists agreed that basic clinical skills, for example, in terms of exercise planning and dosage, are particularly important in implementing OA guideline recommendations. Some participants indicated that the ability to find, read, understand and critically appraise CPGs was also a useful skill.

But also, a certain scientific understanding. Just to understand- okay, if now- I mean, it’s always these S1 [expert opinion-based recommendations], S2k [consensus-based recommendations] or whatever it says. That you can work it out for yourself. Or what does it mean when it’s just a consensus, an expert consensus? What does that mean for the situation now? (PT2)

Alongside a range of soft skills such as empathy, respect, self-confidence and self-reflection, the role of communicative soft skills was highlighted. For many participants, the ability to lead conversations, educate and motivate patients is an important skill for initiating lifestyle changes, but one that is rarely taught in professional training.

Then I think I need- well, it’s not for nothing that I’ve had training or further training in communication, because I think that’s often referred to as a soft skill. That we therapists lack it far too much and get far too little training in it. […] Yeah, I don’t even know how to listen. How to listen actively. In other words, how to make the patient feel heard. (PT5)

Social/professional role and identity

Overall, the participants perceived the use of CPGs as part of their professional role, as all physiotherapists, whether they have an academic background or not, want the best possible outcome for their patients. The participants saw themselves as playing a key role in the management of OA. However, some physiotherapists discussed that they are considered inferior to physicians and that patients lack confidence in their knowledge and skills. Being tied to physicians’ prescriptions often limits the implementation of interventions recommended in CPGs. For example, if manual therapy, electrotherapy or lymphatic drainage is prescribed, they have to follow it, knowing that active therapy options would have been a better choice. Collaboration with physicians is perceived as difficult. Although younger physicians in particular are interested in increasing collaboration, there is often no time for interprofessional communication. In addition to collaborating with physicians, greater involvement of psychologists and nutritionists can contribute to guideline adherence and thus improved care for patients with hip and knee OA.

And that is of course a huge ethical dilemma, which is incredibly exhausting when you’re constantly faced with the dilemma of ‘I’m somehow bound by prescriptions. I’ve got a referrer here on whom we’re financially dependent to some extent, and he’s telling me not to do the right thing.’ (PT1)

Beliefs about capabilities

Most participants reported that they felt able to provide the recommended treatments for people with hip and knee OA guidelines. Some noted that physiotherapists who are physically active themselves and enjoy exercise may find it easier to implement the CPGs, as they can better identify with the recommendations and appear more authentic to their patients. Overall, providing exercise therapy and education seems to be easier than implementing weight management for overweight people. Physiotherapists feel that this topic has often already been addressed by the physician. In addition, it is a sensitive topic and therapists are concerned about offending people and thus losing therapeutic relationship. In this context, it was considered important that physiotherapists know their professional limits and refer patients to another profession if their skills are not sufficient.

I find it relatively easy in this case. Precisely because it is about education and physical activity. That’s what physiotherapy is all about. (PT2)

Optimism

Although CPGs do not currently play a role in many physiotherapy practices, participants felt that they were important in guiding the management of patients with hip and knee OA. It was therefore reported that there is a need for greater awareness of the existence of CPGs, the need to use them and the opportunities they offer. Overall, participants were optimistic that CPGs will become more important and that their use will improve the quality of care in the future.

Beliefs about consequences

A wide range of potential benefits associated with following guideline recommendations in the management of patients with hip and knee OA was reported. Most physiotherapists expected improved patient outcomes (eg, pain, function, quality of life) and increased patient self-efficacy, as well as savings in staff, time and money. In addition, all participants emphasised that OA guidelines support and protect their clinical decisions, and can also be used as a basis for discussions with physicians and patients. Providing evidence-based therapy gives therapists confidence and satisfaction, and can also enhance the reputation of the profession.

You basically have the protection, if you like, for yourself to be able to say: ‘OK, this isn’t something I just make up, it’s written there in black and white.’ And of course, I can show it to the patients if they’re interested. That’s certainly the helpful thing about it. (PT8)

CPGs were considered to be a good starting point for treatment planning in patients with hip and knee OA. Participants discussed the aspect of standardisation as both a benefit and as a potential harm. If all physiotherapists adhere to CPGs, there will be a high-quality standard across all physiotherapy clinics. This would also lead to less confusion for patients, who are currently often surprised when physiotherapists in different practices or even in the same practice treat their health condition in completely different ways. However, participants emphasised the importance of individuality in treatment and expressed concern that following guidelines may result in losing sight of the patient as an individual and applying a kind of standard programme that is not appropriate for all patients.

There can also be negative consequences: I focus too much on the clinical practice guidelines and don’t pay attention to the patient in front of me. And with their individual problems and individual conditions. (PT6)

When asked about the consequences of not following the CPGs, some physiotherapists were concerned that providing interventions with no or conflicting evidence instead could lead to their patients’ pain becoming chronic and a dependency on physiotherapy treatment.

Reinforcement

Some participants felt that financial incentives would increase the willingness of physiotherapists to implement guideline recommendations in clinical practice. There is currently no reward system for effective and efficient work and the current rates paid by health insurances tend to favour non-guideline-adherent therapy.

And in general, of course, it has to be said: Whether I do 15 minutes of guideline-adherent therapy or not, I get paid the same. So, if you do a good job, you don’t get anything out of it, and in the worst case you get even less money. (PT1)

Intentions

One of the most important factors influencing the implementation of guideline recommendations in clinical practice is the motivation of physiotherapists. There are interactions between motivation and various barriers and facilitators discussed in the other TDF domains. For example, physiotherapists mentioned that it takes a lot of time and energy to keep up to date with the evidence. In addition, some felt a sense of resignation towards the system and patients leading to a lack of motivation. A few participants also suggested that there are therapists who do not want to change their behaviour because they are satisfied that things are running smoothly and patients continue to come to physiotherapy whether they are being treated according to CPGs or not. However, some of the participants expressed their intention to engage more with the OA guidelines after the interview and to implement the recommendations into clinical practice.

Yeah, and then there’s also the resignation that, well, the system and the physicians don’t want it [OA management according to clinical practice guidelines]. Then that’s the way it is. Maybe there’s also a certain amount of laziness. Of course, it’s relaxing to give patients a massage every 20 minutes. That’s a really nasty thing to say, but that’s probably what I meant. I don’t know. (PT6)

Memory, attention and decision processes

The physiotherapists indicated that working according to CPGs is something that is mostly done automatically. Education and exercise therapy in particular are key interventions in physiotherapy, so even those who are not aware of the specific content of the OA guidelines are in line. However, some kind of visual reminders (eg, posters, checklists) that summarise the content of the CPGs was mentioned as beneficial in order not to forget important aspects.

But I also have the feeling that I’ve always implemented a bit of it [the clinical practice guideline], even though I didn’t know it. So, yeah? Well, if you work in a reasonably serious way, then you make a diagnosis and you also make a plan somehow. What do you want to work on? And then you get a little closer to that, I’d say, by working with strengthening. How you implement strengthening is up to you. (PT10)

Environmental context and resources

All participants reported environmental factors that influence whether or to what extent guideline recommendations for patients with hip and knee OA are followed in clinical practice. An important factor that was discussed by the physiotherapists was the size and equipment of the rooms: while small rooms with only a treatment table encourage patients and therapists to engage in passive treatment, larger rooms with exercise equipment offer many more opportunities to activate patients.

And what I find really bad is the practice environment. As long as I think there is a treatment table in every room, you have to ask yourself what is implied by that. And if there was an exercise mat in every treatment room with maybe a few dumbbells, things would be different. (PT4)

One of the most commonly mentioned barriers was lack of time and participants stated that 20 min, which is often the standard time for a treatment session in Germany, was too short to provide therapy according to CPGs. They also lack the time to read articles and CPGs, so they have to do this in their spare time, which requires a lot of motivation and discipline. Many participants also stated that they felt restricted by the frequency of therapy prescribed. They would like more flexibility in scheduling of physiotherapy sessions, as effective long-term therapy cannot be achieved if the patient only attends twice a week and the treatment ends after 3 weeks.

I have 15 minutes for the patient- so we have 15 to 20 min, and after 15 minutes I actually have to kick the patient out to document. Then I can really only give them a short coaching session and that’s it. That’s far too little for the behavioural change we need to achieve. (PT1)

Some therapists indicated that digitalisation would make it easier for them to implement recommendations from OA guidelines. In particular, apps that could be used to plan training sessions and to generate a home exercise programme for patients were seen as beneficial.

Social influences

Various social impacts from patients, colleagues and employers influence the implementation of guideline recommendations for OA care. Almost all participants reported that patients’ expectations, previous experiences and motivation influence the choice of interventions and lead to discussions. Patients often ask for passive treatments, especially if they have already received them before, and react with surprise when they have to do exercise therapy. Patients also do not expect or perceive education as part of physiotherapy. Some participants reported that despite knowing that CPGs recommend active treatment, they sometimes provide passive treatment because they are afraid of losing the therapeutic relationship if they try to convince patients to exercise. While there are patients who have unrealistic goals, such as complete pain relief, others come to physiotherapy with no expectations and see surgery as inevitable. What patients expect from physiotherapy and how they manage their OA depends largely on their physician’s communication and treatment decisions, but also on what they have heard from friends and relatives or read on the internet. One difficulty for physiotherapists was dealing with patients who were not been sufficiently informed about their condition and describe OA with terms such as ‘wear-and-tear’ or ‘bone-on-bone’ suggesting that the joint was completely damaged. Patients who are anxious and worried, and who expect that physiotherapy will not improve their symptoms anyway, are difficult to motivate doing exercises. Contrarily, motivated patients who have a positive attitude or are physically active can facilitate OA management according to guideline recommendations, with the physiotherapist taking more of a coaching role. Participants want their patients to be better informed, as discussing popular myths or the effectiveness of treatments with no or conflicting evidence takes a lot of time and energy. Possible tools include leaflets or brochures that patients can get from their health insurance company or physician, while at the same time limiting misinformation on the internet.

Exercises? I think very few people expect that because they tend to think: ‘OK, the therapists will make it better. The physician will make it better. The medication will make it better.’ But a lot of people underestimate that they can and have to do something themselves and then they are quite surprised: ‘What? We do exercise?’ (PT9)

The social influence of colleagues was also discussed. Participants found it very beneficial when several or even all team members had the same positive attitude and the same interest in implementing guidelines in clinical practice and highlighted team meetings or internal training sessions where CPGs can be discussed. However, some participants also reported problems when working with colleagues who are unaware of the CPGs and sceptical about them. Therapists are often reluctant to inform their colleagues about the use of interventions for which there is no or conflicting evidence, because they do not want to lecture their colleagues or cause conflicts in the team. Instead, they develop strategies to keep their patients away from colleagues who do not work according to guideline recommendations, as they want to avoid the internal conflict between informing the patient about the best treatment and embarrassing colleagues.

On the one hand, I find it difficult- for example, when I see my colleagues who have had no contact with evidence-based practice, and who, unfortunately, are learning and being taught the wildest things in postgraduate courses, and then I come across as an exotic and say: ‘Nope!’ I find that fundamentally difficult. (PT4)

Other social influences on the use of CPGs include the employer and the way the physiotherapy practice is managed. Participants described employers who tended to focus on passive therapy approaches in clinical practice, employers who did not care about the way their team members worked and employers who valued and promoted evidence-based practice in physiotherapy. Physiotherapists often follow the employer’s instructions because they expect negative consequences if they disagree. However, the employer can also promote the implementation of guidelines in clinical practice in a number of ways also mentioned in other TDF domains, for example, by organising team meetings, providing additional time for research or funding (evidence-based) postgraduate courses.

Well, my former employer - it was a classic physiotherapy practice - and the boss was a total manual therapy freak and anything other than manual therapy wasn’t effective to him. And well, if you did anything other than the classic stuff, it was always difficult. You were always looked at strangely: ‘What are you doing to my patient now?’ And that was the problem, on the one hand. (PT4)

Emotion

Organisational factors such as lack of time, workload or limited resources can sometimes lead to stress and exhaustion in physiotherapists. The therapists acknowledged that these emotions can have a negative impact on their therapy. However, they believe that their professional commitment should be such that patients do not perceive any influence of moods, emotions and feelings on the therapy decisions.

When I realise that I’m not in a good mood today, I might also ask myself: ‘What’s the reason?’ Because even though we are people at work, we should be so professional that a patient doesn’t necessarily realise when we aren’t doing well ourselves. (PT4)

Behavioural regulation

Some participants agreed that there should be more pressure from employers and especially health insurers to increase the number of therapists working according to guideline recommendations. There are currently no monitoring or quality indicators for evidence-based physiotherapy. A few physiotherapists reported that some quality control could be achieved by introducing standardised assessments to record patient outcomes. This could be beneficial and motivating for both the patient and the therapist.

We collect the HOOS and KOOS questionnaires as standard. Establishing this process was a lot of work at the beginning. Of course, analysing these things costs me time as a subject leader. It benefits us in the sense that we can make our work measurable, give people something to show how they have improved and also write reports. (PT1)

Another approach discussed was mandatory evidence-based postgraduate courses. These could include courses that teach the content of OA guidelines and CPGs for other diseases, as well as courses that teach skills in searching, reading and critically appraising scientific literature.

Discussion

The aim of the current study was to explore the perceived barriers and facilitators to the use of CPGs in OA care among German physiotherapists. Using the TDF, influencing factors were identified across 13 domains, most of which were categorised into ‘knowledge’, ‘beliefs about consequences’, ‘environmental context and resources’ and ‘social influences’. For example, key barriers in these domains included lack of awareness of existing CPGs, concerns that treatment will lose individuality, time constraints and dissonant patient expectations. Facilitators identified in this study included the discussion of guideline recommendations within the team and the expectation of improved patient outcomes and resource savings. The findings of this qualitative interview study are broadly consistent with previous research examining factors influencing healthcare professionals’ use of guideline recommendations14 and the use of evidence-based practice.15 32 33 However, this study provides new insights by specifically highlighting how these factors affect the context of OA care among physiotherapists in Germany.

In a systematic review, Egerton et al24 synthesised evidence from qualitative studies among healthcare professionals to identify barriers and facilitators to providing recommended management specifically for patients with OA. Four main themes were considered to have an impact on the use of OA guidelines: (1) ‘OA is not that serious’, (2) ‘Clinicians are, or perceive they are, under-prepared’, (3) ‘Personal beliefs at odds with providing recommended practice’ and (4) ‘Dissonant patient expectations’. However, the perspective of physiotherapists was little considered. Egerton et al24 summarised that clinicians do not feel adequately prepared to manage patients with OA due to a lack of knowledge of recommended practice and problems with the clarity and specificity of CPGs. This is in line with the results of the current study, as participants also reported a lack of awareness of existing CPGs as a problem in physiotherapy. Findings from a previous nationwide web-based survey showed that only 49% of participating physiotherapists were aware of an OA guideline and 36% were aware of the German guidelines for hip or knee OA.23 In Germany, an academic level of physiotherapy education is not mandatory and most physiotherapists are trained in vocational schools according to the German Training and Examination Regulations for Physiotherapists, which do not address specific competencies related to the implementation of CPGs and evidence-based practice.36 This was confirmed by some participants interviewed, who had no contact with CPGs until their bachelor’s programme. A standardised and consistent inclusion of evidence-based practice and CPGs in the professional training of physiotherapists in Germany seems necessary. In addition, tailored guideline dissemination could help to reach physiotherapists working in clinical practice who completed their professional training years ago and are not aware of CPGs. As physiotherapists lack the time and often the ability to find, read and critically appraise scientific literature, the participants of the current study stated that employers and the health insurers should be responsible for providing CPGs in user-friendly formats.

According to Egerton et al,24 physicians perceive patient expectations that are inconsistent with their own views as an important barrier influencing the guideline-adherent management of OA. Dissonant expectations regarding specialist referrals or medication prescriptions were mainly attributed to external influences such as the media and common beliefs about the causes of the disease. For example, patients may expect unnecessary tests or immediate and aggressive treatment options, which may conflict with the evidence-based approaches advocated by physicians. The influence of patients’ expectations and goals on disease management and attitudes towards OA were also discussed in detail by almost all participating physiotherapists in the current study. Physiotherapists identified previous management strategies and communication from physicians and colleagues as the most influential factors in patients’ beliefs. Language is a powerful tool that has the potential to influence patients’ thoughts and actions. For example, terms used to describe the same condition may influence the patient’s perception of condition severity and therefore treatment preference. The more medical the term, the less control a person may feel they have over the situation.37 The terms ‘wear and tear’ or ‘degenerative changes’ are perceived by patients as strongly negative and are associated with a sign of ageing and a disease that cannot be cured.38 39 Physicians who present osteoarthritic joint damage as normal ageing phenomenon and downplay the seriousness of the disease can reduce patients’ motivation to engage with their condition and take the necessary steps to change their lifestyle.40 Patients prefer if clinicians discuss the impact of OA on their lives rather than explain the biomechanical mechanisms that cause OA.38 In general, a rethink of the management of persistent non-traumatic musculoskeletal pain seems desirable, moving away from ‘we can fix and cure you’ to more person-centred care focusing on education, lifestyle changes and increasing physical activity, with clinicians suggested as health coaches.41 42

The participants had a positive attitude towards the use of CPGs in OA management, consistent with other studies that have found similarly positive attitudes towards evidence-based practice among German physiotherapists.43 44 In contrast to other clinicians, they did not trivialise OA, express doubts about treatment effectiveness, or hold negative attitudes towards the disease, as described by Egerton et al.24 Providing evidence-based care was considered as part of their professional role, and they expected predominantly positive consequences from following guideline recommendations, including improved patient outcomes and self-efficacy, resource savings and increased therapist satisfaction. There is currently limited evidence on facilitators to guideline use specific to OA care.24 Egerton et al24 suggested that the lack of reported facilitators might reflect a gap in study design rather than their absence in practice. In line with the findings of a systematic review summarising facilitators for implementing CPGs across different healthcare professionals,14 the present study identified several beneficial factors, including technical support, the use of simplified and time-efficient guideline versions, targeted training courses, a positive attitude towards CPGs, early patient education and the use of audit and feedback tools. Recognising facilitators is as important as identifying barriers. Future studies should focus on both, as they can inform and enhance implementation strategies.

In implementation science, the concept of tailored interventions is becoming increasingly important. This approach suggests that the success of disseminating and implementing CPGs is significantly increased when strategies are specifically designed to address local barriers and facilitators.45 The findings of this study identify several key factors that can inform the development of such tailored interventions. To enhance this field, comparative studies are needed to assess the effectiveness of various tailored approaches across different contexts. In addition, a deeper understanding of how local contextual factors, such as cultural and regional differences, affect guideline implementation could provide valuable perspectives for refining these strategies. According to Swaithes et al,46 evaluations of implementation activity should involve patient and healthcare practitioners together.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore barriers and facilitators to the use of CPGs in OA care among German physiotherapists using a qualitative approach. A major strength of this study is that the methods and results were guided by the validated TDF as a theoretical framework to comprehensively identify factors influencing target behaviour, which can subsequently inform the development of implementation interventions, for example, using the BCW. The interview guide was developed with at least one question per domain, which may have given participants little opportunity to express opinions that were not relevant to the TDF, even though the interviews were guided by open-ended questions and prompts before more specific questions about the predefined domains were asked. In addition, both an inductive and deductive analysis approach was used to ensure that any factors not covered by the TDF were identified.

Recruitment was challenging and the number of participants interviewed was small compared with the proportion of German physiotherapists working in outpatient OA care. Consequently, it was necessary to deviate from the study protocol and include all interested participants rather than of conducting purposive sampling. However, data saturation was reached after 11 interviews and a wide range of opinions on the use of CPGs can be assumed, as the sample included participants of different ages, genders and educational backgrounds. In addition, participants worked in different federal states of Germany and both physiotherapists who regularly used guideline recommendations and those who reported not knowing any CPG were included. Social desirability bias cannot be ruled out. However, this risk was minimised because there was no prior relationship between the interviewer and the participants.

Only physiotherapists working in outpatient practices were interviewed about their use of CPGs, particularly in relation to OA care, limiting generalisability. Additional influencing factors may exist in other settings, such as inpatient facilities, where different resources and organisational structures may affect the implementation of guideline recommendations. For example, physiotherapists working in a hospital may face different constraints, such as different levels of interdisciplinary collaboration, which may affect their use of guidelines compared with those working in outpatient practices. Other barriers may exist among other healthcare professionals and in relation to other health conditions. Although this study focused on the German context, and the specific findings are tailored to outpatient physiotherapy for OA, many of the barriers and facilitators identified are consistent with those reported in the literature.14 Therefore, the results may be broadly transferable and useful for other implementation projects aimed at increasing the use of guidelines in clinical practice, including settings and countries beyond Germany.

Conclusion

Using a theory-driven approach, this study provided valuable data on barriers and facilitators influencing the use of CPGs in the management of hip and knee OA among German physiotherapists. Guideline use in OA care appears to be influenced by a number of factors across several TDF domains, including ‘knowledge’, ‘beliefs about consequences’, ‘environmental context and resources’ and ‘social influences’. Key barriers identified, such as lack of awareness of existing guidelines, concerns about losing individuality in treatment, time constraints and dissonant patient expectations underscore the challenges physiotherapists face in integrating CPGs into clinical practice. Facilitators such as team meetings to discuss guideline recommendations suggest pathways for enhancing guideline uptake. The identified barriers and facilitators can be used to guide the development of a theory-based implementation intervention aimed at increasing the uptake of CPGs by physiotherapists in clinical practice to improve the quality of care for patients with hip and knee OA.
