The impact of structured assessment attempts and instructor feedback on clinical competency development in dental assistant training
Authors: Ranya F Elemam, Heba M Abdelaal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis research investigated the effectiveness of a structured three-attempt formative assessment model combined with instructor feedback in improving clinical competency among first-semester dental assistant diploma students.MethodsTen dental assistant students underwent repeated assessment attempts across eight operative and endodontic procedures during the Fall 2023 semester of the Dental Assistant Diploma program. After each attempt, students were given performance feedback reinforcing elements they performed well, and guiding their improvement in areas that were less well executed. Outcomes across the three attempts were then compared to determine if repeated feedback was associated with improved performance over time.ResultsStudent progress with this structured feedback approach was associated with significant and consistent improvement of performance scores across the three assessments (F = 577.74; p < 0.000), along with universally high logbook grades across students and a 100% pass rate in the final competency exam. Statistical analysis revealed a strong correlation (Pearson’s r = 0.94, p < 0.000) between logbook scores and final competency exam scores, which is notable given these two assessment types were conducted by independent examiners.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that structured feedback-driven assessment plays a pivotal role in competency-based education for dental assistants. In addition, this research highlights specific design elements that may be fundamental in the success of such an approach.Keywords: Competency-Based education, Formative assessment, Structured feedback, Dental assistant, Course design

Background

Competency-based education in dental training emphasizes skill mastery through structured assessment and continuous feedback. The use of logbooks, formative assessments, and structured evaluation models has been increasingly integrated into medical and dental education to enhance skill acquisition and ensure clinical competency on specific tasks. A number of articles have described the models/methods [1, 2], theory [1, 3], qualitative features [4, 5]. and student/teacher satisfaction with structured feedback approaches in education [4, 6, 7]. However, few studies have reported direct quantitative, empirical evaluations of the learning/performance improvement effects associated with structured, multi-attempt assessment models with instructor feedback in dental assistant programs.

This research aims to bridge this gap by evaluating whether a structured, three-attempt system and assessor feedback contributed to the high success rate observed among first-semester dental assistant diploma students. Specifically, in addition to the typical cumulative evaluations during the semester, the course included three structured, formative assessments with feedback from one of the course instructors, which allowed specific targeting of strengths and weaknesses so that students could address them. The assessment task structure followed the “Entrustable Professional Activities” (EPA) model, which has gained substantial popularity in recent years [8–10]. While the concept of EPA models has been discussed in the literature, the guidelines for specific tools, structure, and implementation of EPA-based approaches in teaching have not been studied or defined systematically [10].

Methods

The aim, design, and setting of the research

This research retrospectively analyzes data extracted from feedback reports, logbook grades, and final competency exam results in the Fall 2023 semester of the Dental Assistant Diploma program at Gulf Medical University. The course spanned 15 weeks. Students received training in operative and endodontic procedures and practiced them throughout the semester. Logbook grades were computed based on clinical performance in the classroom; these grades were collected as cumulative scores based on students’ progressive performance in practical tasks, evaluated over the semester by clinical instructors and averaged into a single score at the end of the semester.

During weeks 9, 10, and 11 of the semester, each student took part in a formative assessment in which they performed eight procedural tasks, which were identified as key entrustable professional activities (EPAs), and had been learned as part of normal clinical coursework. EPAs evaluated during the assessment were defined using the following approach: During internal course development, we mapped existing core dental assisting clinical duties to define EPAs by breaking down key dental assisting procedures (operative and endodontic) into measurable competency units that aligned with course evaluation criteria.

The three formative assessments were conducted by one of the course instructors (the same Instructor for every student) after each task was taught and practiced by students. Each assessment was identical in structure and tasks evaluated. The instructor provided same-day, brief, written feedback and asked students to submit a self-reflection after receiving the feedback. Following the third and final assessment, students received a more detailed, written report that included feedback on the tasks attempted in each of the three assessments, highlighting strengths, areas for improvement, and overall performance. Because the more detailed feedback after the third assessment was given after the last assessment was completed, it did not affect assessment performance or scores (i.e., it did not influence assessment outcomes).

Between assessments, students practiced and refined their clinical skills based on the feedback they received at each assessment. Their cumulative performance on the primary eight skills that were independently assessed and scored as part of the formative assessments, plus performance on 15 other skills, was documented in logbooks and contributed to their final logbook grade.

At the end of the semester, during week 15 of the course, all students took part in a final competency exam, scored by an independent assessor certified by a United Kingdom Northern Council for Further Education (UK NCEF) as an assessor in vocational training.

To test the impact of formative assessments on performance, in this study we examined the within-subject changes in formative assessment scores across the three, identical assessments. As a control for inter-rater reliability of performance assessment, we also looked at the correlation of the final logbook scores with the final competency exam scores, which were conducted by independent evaluators.

The characteristics of participants or description of materials

Participants

The research cohort consisted of 10 trainees enrolled in the Fall 2023 semester of the Dental Assistant Diploma program at Gulf Medical University. All participants received the formative assessments.

2. Logbook grades

Logbook grades

The logbook grade was a clinical performance grade that was computed independent of written exams, homework, or class participation. It was based solely on clinical task performance during patient care sessions and simulation activities, including instructors’ evaluation of students’ procedural skills, instrument handling, infection control, and professionalism during chairside assistance. Students practiced and performed these same tasks in daily clinical sessions. The logbook tracked 8 specialized tasks—which corresponded to the tasks evaluated in the formative assessment—and 15 fundamental tasks together to generate a single score during each classroom session (i.e., scores were not segmented per task as the formative assessments were). Performance was scored using a 10-point rubric (competent, needs improvement, or not yet competent). At the end of the semester, scores across all tasks were averaged and scaled to be a percentage (out of 100). The specialized tasks were identical to those evaluated during the three structured assessments, but grades from the assessments themselves were not included as part of the logbook grade.

Logbook grades were computed by the two course instructors; one of the instructors who recorded grades for the logbook was the same instructor who conducted the three formative assessments. However, these instructors were distinct from the assessor who conducted, scored, and gave feedback on the final competency exam.

For the purposes of the current study, final logbook grades were retrieved from student performance records over the course of the semester (Table 2 in Results). Scoring for logbook grades was computed in the following manner:

◦ A cumulative numerical score (out of 100) of learning/performance over the course of the semester, reflecting performance across the 8 tasks performed as part of the formative assessments (but evaluated independently) and 15 others.

Final competency exam score

In addition to the measures evaluating the efficacy of structured feedback on student performance, we also asked students, at the end of the course, to rate their satisfaction with the course using eight different satisfaction categories. Responses used a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree. Satisfaction categories were recorded as percent of students who selected 5, or Strongly Agree for each response. Categories included: [1] The course was well organized [2]. The course learning outcomes were achievable [3]. The Practical |Lab sessions |experiential learning sessions were useful in developing and acquiring skills [4]. The assessments used measured my learning accurately [5]. The course included interactive sessions which stimulated learning [6]. The recommended learning resources (e-Books|Journals|Websites) were useful [7]. The course instructor(s) gave feedback on assessments within 1-week after the exam/assignment deadline [8]. The practice questions (formative assessments) were helpful in improving my performance in the course. In particular, the last two categories directly addressed student feedback.

Statistical comparisons

To evaluate the relationship between the formative assessment variable and performance over time, a one-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to evaluate change in formative assessment scores across the three assessments. In parallel, a Pearson’s correlation was performed to evaluate the relationship between logbook grades and final competency exam score; because semester grade evaluation and final competency exam evaluations were conducted by separate raters, this analysis was used to show objective consistency in evaluation measures across measures and time, and also to show that learning during the semester was effectively learned and successfully achieved by the time students were assessed for abilities in real-world clinical skills.

Discussion

The findings in this research highlight the effectiveness of repeated formative assessments with structured feedback over the semester in enhancing dental assistant trainees’ competency. Students showed substantial and highly consistent improvement across the three assessments, and all students performed at an outstanding level in the independently conducted final competency examination. The structure of our teaching tool combined the strengths of both feedback and structure of the EPA model of assessment. This likely boosted the positive impact of the approach on student outcomes, by optimizing features of feedback such as making course objectives clear.

Past studies have suggested that, although students value feedback, it is often lacking [3] or delivered in a poor manner [5]. For example, in a survey of medical residents’ perspectives on feedback in medical training, only 25.6% reported routinely receiving feedback [11]. Other studies have indicated that, often, trainees who do receive feedback feel that it is structured more for program evaluation than for helping them to improve their skills, and that feedback was often perceived as more for the purpose of checking competency boxes than for promoting clinical excellence [12].

Compared to conventional single-attempt assessments, the three-attempt model method here enabled students to refine their skills progressively. The uniform improvements observed over time and uniform success in the end-of-semester competency exam results illustrated the effectiveness of this approach. The assessments served as a valuable reflective tool, reinforcing learning and procedural accuracy. These results align with competency-based educational principles emphasizing skill mastery through iterative learning.

An important feature of this report is that we itemized the specific content and structure of the formative assessments to maximize accessibility and adaptability of our approach to other programs. We divided tasks into well-delineated categories (operative and endodontic) and repeated the same activities with the same structure at each assessment. Importantly, past research has suggested the format of feedback is critical to whether it improves or hinders performance [5, 13]. A review of studies evaluating student feedback across a number of domains identified several successful feedback elements that overlapped with our feedback structure, including optimized timing (immediate feedback following task performance) [5] and articulation of actionable revisions to performance that can guide future practice and student understanding of expectations [13, 14]. In addition, other studies have shown the importance of positive feedback in fueling student motivation [5], which in turn, leads to better performance [15]. Our approach aimed to motivate students by providing them with feedback about what they had done well during each assessment.

While we did not include a separate control group in this study because it was a retrospective evaluation, this was partially offset by the fact that each student served as his/her own control, since measures were made within-subject, across assessments. While this did not serve as a perfect control, since practice over the semester in the absence of assessments might have led to similar improvements, an important benefit of conducting this as a retrospective study was that this minimized bias in delivery and outcome of the assessment/feedback procedures because the formative assessments were not conducted with the aim of proving their efficacy; they were simply included to assist the students. A control group could have only been used if it had been designed prospectively with the intention of proving efficacy, which would have then led to a greater likelihood of bias in the assessments. Furthermore, the design of control groups in educational research is more challenging in educational environments where the research is secondary to student success. This environment differs from research volunteers who agree to be part of a clinical trial where they are aware that there is a chance they will be placed in the placebo/control group. As a result, within-subject controls would be optimal for follow-up studies; for example, one could segregate components of the evaluations that received versus did not receive structured feedback for each student, and compare within-subject differences in improvement over time for tasks with versus without feedback.

Our research shows that the measures and outcomes implemented during the semester, including both formative assessment improvements and logbook grades, were objective measures that translated to highly successful performance on the final competency exam.

Specifically, the final competency exam was conducted by an outside assessor who had no knowledge of individual grades or feedback scores during the course. The correlation between final competency exam (done by independent assessor) and logbook grades (done by course Instructors) showed a Pearson’s R of 0.94, indicating strong inter-rater reliability, robustness, and objectivity of the assessment measures.

Importantly, students uniformly felt the feedback, as well as other aspects of the course, was effective and helpful. All 9 out of 10 survey respondents answered, “strongly agree” to statements regarding timeliness of the feedback and that the formative assessments were helpful in improving their performance in the course. The positive response to this feedback is critical to its efficacy, as the psychological component of feedback is critical [7]. We believe that the formative assessment approach used here can serve as a valuable example and template for future dental assistant programs.

There were some minor logistical challenges faced as part of implementing the formative assessments, which we managed to the best of our ability. These included [1] Faculty workload: A high degree of clinical instructor involvement was required for conducting three rounds of formative assessments and continuous logbook evaluations [2]. Time constraints: Scheduling all assessments within the busy clinical timetable while ensuring patient flow required careful planning [3]. Patient access: There were occasional challenges ensuring adequate patient numbers for certain procedures, but students were able to meet clinical requirements. We managed these through flexible scheduling. These challenges might be amplified in larger-scale classrooms, but with standardization, processes might be streamlined and made more efficient.

For future implementation in larger classrooms, some of the challenges that might arise are: [1] the volume of work for the Instructors, which would likely be amplified in larger-scale classrooms [2], standardization of the quality of feedback across course Instructors, and [3] the inability to control for differences in student perception of feedback across cohorts [12]. For future implementation in other healthcare settings, such as medical school training, there would be a need to standardize the content and method of EPA segmentation and definition for implementation consistency. Future research development in these domains could offset these challenges; for example, EPA templates could be developed and shared to achieve a degree of standardization across institutions.

The limitations of our analyses in the current study include a small sample size, the absence of a control group, and the absence of longer-term measures to evaluate task performance retention. Future studies should evaluate this approach in larger cohorts, compare it to cohorts who do not receive formative assessments, and explore long-term retention of competency and its impact on clinical practice. While challenges in larger cohorts might be present, the value added to clinical programs by developing effective feedback methods is one that has long been desired in the field [3, 11, 12, 16].

Conclusion

This research provides empirical evidence that structured assessment models incorporating multiple attempts and instructor feedback significantly enhance competency development in dental assistant training. Integrating similar methodologies into broader dental education programs may further optimize skill acquisition and professional preparedness.
