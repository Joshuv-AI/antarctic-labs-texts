Brain Atrophy and Leukoencephalopathy Following Tarlatamab: Case Report
Authors: Kiriko Onishi, Toki Kitano, Yusuke Fujioka, Moeko Ota, Fumie Onishi, Kohei Miyake, Hisaaki Tanaka, Nobukazu Fujimoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe describe the case of a 74‐year‐old female diagnosed with extensive‐stage small cell lung cancer. Initially, she received carboplatin, etoposide and atezolizumab, followed by amrubicin as second‐line treatment and nogitecan as third‐line treatment. Stereotactic radiotherapy was delivered to subsequent brain metastases. Two years after the initial treatment, new brain and lung metastases developed. Whole‐brain radiotherapy was administered, followed by tarlatamab (1 mg on Day 1, 10 mg on Days 8 and 20). After the third dose of tarlatamab, the patient developed disorientation. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed shrinkage of the metastatic lesions, rapid cerebral atrophy, ventricular enlargement and periventricular hyperintensity on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery imaging. Dexamethasone was initiated without neurological improvement. The early‐onset cerebral atrophy and leukoencephalopathy in the current case was attributed to whole brain radiotherapy and tarlatamab. Patients with brain metastases may be at increased risk of neurological events associated with tarlatamab.

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is an aggressive malignancy. Although the responses to initial treatment are usually good, the outcomes after second‐line treatment are often poor [1]. Tarlatamab, a bispecific T‐cell engager directing T cells to SCLC cells expressing delta‐like ligand 3, has a longer response duration than the previous chemotherapy standards [2]. However, immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) and neurological events often occur with tarlatamab and require careful attention. Here, we describe a patient who developed rapid brain atrophy and leukoencephalopathy after treatment with tarlatamab.

Case Report

A 74‐year‐old woman presented with dyspnea and morning cough. She had a smoking history of 5 pack‐years. She also had untreated hypertension and dyslipidemia. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a right hilar mass, mediastinal lymphadenopathy and hepatic tumours. Transbronchial biopsy confirmed small cell carcinoma, and she was diagnosed with extensive‐stage SCLC (T2aN2M1c). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) did not reveal brain metastases. The patient received four cycles of chemo‐immunotherapy consisting of carboplatin, etoposide and atezolizumab, followed by atezolizumab maintenance. The right hilar lymph nodes grew after one cycle of atezolizumab maintenance. Eleven cycles of amrubicin were administered as second‐line treatment. After tumour progression in the mediastinal lymph nodes, which caused superior vena cava syndrome, she received palliative radiotherapy. Although it was initially effective, the lymph nodes grew again, and nogitecan was administered as the third‐line treatment. However, during the nogitecan treatment, the patient experienced dizziness and brain MRI revealed cerebellar metastases. Stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) was delivered to the metastases. Tarlatamab was suggested as salvage treatment, but the patient was concerned about the potential side effects and wanted to continue with her current treatment of nogitecan.

Seven months after SRT, the patient presented with a headache and brain MRI revealed multiple brain metastases and leptomeningeal dissemination (Figure 1A,B). She received whole‐brain radiotherapy (WBRT) at a dose of 3 Gy in 10 fractions. Chest CT revealed progression of the primary tumour and multiple intrapulmonary metastases. Six days after the completion of WBRT, tarlatamab was initiated; the patient received 1 mg on Day 1 and 10 mg on Day 8. After each administration, she experienced a high fever (> 39°C), suggesting cytokine release syndrome, which was managed with acetaminophen, dexamethasone and tocilizumab. From Day 11, she developed persistent Grade 3 Anorexia, delaying her third dose to Day 20.

(A, B) Brain MRI revealed multiple brain metastases. (C, D) Brain MRI revealed multiple brain metastases shrunk after whole‐brain radiation and treatment of tarlatamab. MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

Following the third administration, cognitive symptoms emerged, including disorientation and inconsistent speech. On Day 27 of tarlatamab, brain MRI revealed shrinkage of the brain metastases and leptomeningeal lesions (Figure 1C,D), but it also demonstrated marked cerebral atrophy, ventricular enlargement and periventricular hyperintensity on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery imaging (FLAIR) (Figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed no elevated cell count or pressure, and cytology was negative for malignant cells. The immune effector cell‐associated encephalopathy score declined from 10 to 7, with increasing errors in date orientation and calculations. The revised Hasegawa Dementia Scale declined to 15/30, resulting in suspicion of Grade 2 ICANS. Dexamethasone was initiated, but subsequent MRI scans on Days 34 and 48 showed persistent cerebral atrophy with no cognitive improvement.

(B) MRI undergone 32 days after the completion of WBRT and 26 days after tarlatamab treatment revealed cerebral atrophy, ventricular enlargement and periventricular hyperintensity. There is a 57‐day interval between these two scans. FLAIR, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging; WBRT, whole brain radiation therapy.

Although a partial response was observed in the primary tumour and intrapulmonary metastases, the patient's cognitive dysfunction persisted, leading to discontinuation of tarlatamab after just one cycle. She is now receiving supportive care.

Discussion

In an International Phase II Trial (DeLLphi‐301), ICANS and associated neurological events were observed in 11 of 133 patients (8.3%) who received tarlatamab: ICANS in six patients (4.5%), muscle weakness in four patients (3.0%) and aphasia in one patient (0.8%) [2]. ICANS was also observed in 9 patients (40.9%) in a cohort study of 21 patients with SCLC treated with tarlatamab [3]. The cohort included patients with symptomatic untreated brain metastases and those with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status ≥ 2, who would not have met the eligibility criteria of the DeLLphi‐301 trial. All cases of ICANS in the study were reversible, and no patients experienced permanent neurological deficits. To the best of our knowledge, our case is the first report of early‐onset cerebral atrophy following tarlatamab treatment.

In the current case, the patient had been treated with SBT and WBRT before the administration of tarlatamab. SBT was delivered to only three small brain metastases. Cerebral atrophy may occur following WBRT, but it typically develops several months after the treatment. The brain atrophy in the current case developed only 1 month after WBRT, so we attributed the early‐onset cerebral atrophy and leukoencephalopathy to tarlatamab. It was necessary to differentiate other diseases such as Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) or Alzheimer's disease (AD). CJD typically progresses over several weeks to a few months, often accompanied by myoclonus and extrapyramidal symptoms. However, the present case showed a more rapid progression and did not exhibit myoclonus or extrapyramidal signs. While CJD is characterised by hyperintensity in the cerebral cortex and striatum on diffusion‐weighted imaging and FLAIR MRI sequences, such findings were not observed in this case. AD generally progresses over several years and thus does not correspond to the clinical course observed in this patient.

Radiation‐induced central nervous system injury is considered to be the result of both vascular endothelial damage—leading to vasogenic edema and disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB)—and direct injury to radiation‐sensitive neural cells, particularly oligodendrocytes, resulting in demyelination [4]. Although the pathophysiology of ICANS is not completely understood, endothelial injury caused by elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and increased BBB permeability may lead to elevated cytokine concentrations in CSF, contributing to neurotoxicity [5]. It is conceivable that the combination of SBT/WBRT and tarlatamab in the current case led to a synergistic increase in BBB permeability and further elevation of inflammatory cytokines in the CSF, ultimately resulting in irreversible neurological damage.

A higher frequency of ICANS in patients with untreated brain metastases has been suggested [3]. The findings in the current case raise the possibility that patients with brain metastases, regardless of treatment history, may be at increased risk of neurological events. Close monitoring and prompt management of adverse neurological events are warranted in such cases.

In conclusion, we reported a case of rapid brain atrophy and leukoencephalopathy development after treatment with tarlatamab.
