Evaluation of different culture media supporting the growth and melanin production in Cryptococcus
Authors: Pavithra Amrath Jain, Malledevarapura D. Spandan, Ganavalli S. Ajantha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesMelanin, as a virulence factor, plays an important role in protecting Cryptococcus against the host immune response. The demonstration of a melanin-producing colony in vitro helps in proper identification of this fungus. This study aimed to assess 3 locally available natural seeds in designing a culture medium for supporting growth of Cryptococcus and melanin production.MethodsCryptococcus neoformans previously identified and stocked were tested on flaxseed, black til, and Garden cress containing culture medium separately, where Niger seed agar served as positive control. Melanin production was visually measured using direct observation and also using Whatman No.1 filter paper. The quantitative measurement was done by using spectrophotometric analysis after 48 h of incubation at 37℃.ResultsAll three seed-based culture media developed for Cryptococcus supported its growth, and melanin production was significantly more in colonies grown on garden cress agar than on black til and flaxseed agar. Difference between garden cress medium and Niger seed medium was not significant (P=0.818).Interpretation and conclusionsGarden cress seed agar was found to be suitable for supporting melanin production in Cryptococcus neoformans. Further use of these seeds in preparation of readymade dehydrated medium can also be elucidated.

Cryptococcus neoformans is an encapsulated fungal pathogen that primarily affects immunocompromised individuals, such as those with HIV/AIDS or organ transplant recipients. It causes opportunistic infections, most notably severe disseminated meningoencephalitis.1 Infection typically occurs through the inhalation of airborne spores or yeast cells, initially leading to pulmonary involvement, which can progress to central nervous system (CNS) disease, particularly cryptococcal meningitis. This condition is associated with high morbidity and mortality, with reported CNS infection mortality rates ranging from 41 to 61%.2

Niger seed agar medium, developed in 1962, by has long been used as the standard medium for cultivating Cryptococcus species and observing melanin production.3 The potential use of other natural materials to create more affordable and locally available media has been attempted by various researchers.4-7 The objective of this study was to develop new culture media for the demonstration of melanin production in Cryptococcus, using garden cress seeds, black sesame (black til), and flax seeds separately, and to compare their melanin-production efficacy with that of standard Niger seed agar using direct visual observation and spectrophotometry.

Methods

This study was conducted at the department of Microbiology, SDM College of Medical Sciences and Hospital, Dharwad, Karnataka from August 2025 till September 2025 after obtaining Institutional Ethical Committee approval from the institute.

We used four types of seeds in our study: flax seed (Linum usitatissimum), Niger seed (Guizotia abyssinica), black til seed (Sesamum indicum), and garden cress seed (Lepidium sativum), with Niger seed agar serving as the standard medium for melanin production.3 All four varieties of seeds used in the study were purchased in the month of August 2025, from the authorised grocery shop, cleaned properly, and the same batch of seeds was used for the entire experiment.

Preparation of seed-based culture media

Firstly, Niger seed agar was prepared according to the standard guidelines.3 A minimum modifications in quantity of seeds to be taken and filtration methods etc., was done based on our preliminary optimisation work. Briefly, three grams of Niger seeds (Guizotia abyssinica) were crushed and mixed with 50 mL of distilled water. The mixture was boiled for 30 min to extract the active components. After boiling, the solution was filtered through filter paper to remove solid residues. To the filtrate, 50 mg each of creatinine, dextrose, and KH₂PO₄, and 800 mg of agar were added. The mixture was stirred until all components were fully dissolved and the pH was adjusted to 6.5. It was then autoclaved at 121°C for 15 min. The medium was poured into 90 mm Petri dishes, allowed to solidify, and stored in a refrigerator after performing a sterility check. A similar procedure was followed for the preparation of three other seed media, namely, garden cress seeds (Lepidium sativum), black til seeds (Sesamum indicum), and flax seeds (Linum usitatissimum) agar, where 3 g of the seeds were incorporated in the respective medium. Since garden cress seeds were getting into a jelly-like consistency, we tried filtering them through a sterile gauze instead of filter paper.

Melanin production on various seed-based media

After preparing all four seed-based agar media, the petri plates were inoculated with four different isolates of Cryptococcus neoformans var neoformans and a Candida albicans isolate, which were previously identified both by standard manual methods.8 Manual identification was further confirmed by automated systems (YST cards, BioMérieux’s by VITEK 2 system). While Cryptococcus was used for detecting melanin production on each agar medium, a non-pigment-producing Candida albicans isolate was inoculated as a negative control for melanin production. Each isolate was tested in triplicate on each type of medium.

Visual observation

The petridishes were incubated at 37℃ for 48 h, and melanin production was observed by colour changes in the colonies, compared to the colonies in the Niger seed plate. Whatman no. 1 filter paper was used to enhance the visibility of brown pigmentation. Colonies from all plates were smeared onto the filter paper using a sterile glass rod, and the dark brown pigment colour was compared visually.

Spectrophotometric observation

Since visual observation alone was insufficient, we performed a semi-quantitative assessment by measuring optical density (OD) at 450 nm for the melanin-producing colonies by developing an in-house method. Briefly, Cryptococcus neoformans colonies from each medium were suspended in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) (pH: 7.4) in a concentration of 107 cells/ml, and cell numbers were determined using a Neubauer chamber. Similarly, suspensions of the cryptococcal colonies from brain heart infusion (BHI) agar were also prepared. The PBS suspensions of cryptococcal cells were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and pellets were washed three times with PBS to remove residual media and debris. The sediment was transferred to sterile Petri plates and allowed to dry for 16 to 24 h at 37℃ in the incubator. Once completely dried, the melanin-containing material was scraped off and resuspended in PBS, and the optical density was measured at 450 nm. The differential OD for melanin production from each seed-based medium for Cryptococcus was obtained by subtracting individual OD values from each seed medium from the average OD reading obtained from cryptococcal colonies grown on BHI agar.

Statistical analysis

All the differential OD readings from each type of seed-based medium were compared by applying one-way analysis of variance test (ANOVA) followed by the post-hoc test.

Results

Visual observation of melanin production

After 48 h of incubation, plates were examined for melanin production in Cryptococcus colonies. Garden cress seed agar showed visually appreciable, distinct brown colonies. Black til seed agar and flax seed Agar also showed melanin-producing colonies, which were less intense compared to the garden cress medium. Niger seed agar, which was used as a standard medium in the present study, produced dark brown colonies.

Filter paper observation

Whatman filter paper (No. 1) allowed for clearer distinction of melanin-producing (brown) colonies against a white background. Garden cress seed agar produced the clear brown colonies, suggesting the highest melanin production compared to black til and flax seed agar. Black til seed agar and flax seed agar displayed less brown pigmentation, with flax seed agar exhibiting the faintest hue, indicating the lowest melanin production. These observations visually proved that garden cress seed agar is an effective medium for melanin production in Cryptococcus, followed by black til seed and flax seed agar media in the present experiment.

Spectrophotometric analysis of melanin production (OD at 450 nm)

Semi-quantitative assessment by measuring optical density (OD) at 450 nm provided a more precise and objective evaluation of melanin production. Colonies of Cryptococcus obtained from Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) agar showed a mean OD value of 1.02. Direct OD measurement obtained from each of the seed medium was subtracted from the above mean value (1.02), to obtain the differential OD values for individual seed-based media. Cryptococcus colonies grown on garden cress seed agar showed the highest mean differential OD value of 0.7511, indicating excellent melanin production, compared to colonies on niger seed agar (mean differential OD value of 0.6452), followed by black til agar (mean differential OD of 0.4197) and flax seed agar (mean differential OD of 0.2915).

Statistical analysis

Cryptococcal colonies from garden cress medium had significantly higher mean melanin production compared to black til and flaxseed-based medium, with P values of 0.0433 and 0.0025, respectively. However, the difference in melanin production between the garden cress medium and the Niger seed medium was not statistically significant (P=0.818), though the mean of differential OD values of melanin-producing colonies of Cryptococcus on Garden cress medium was numerically slightly higher than Niger seed.

Discussion

The production of melanin is a key virulence factor in Cryptococcus neoformans, helping the organism evade host immune defences.9,10 In the present study, all three seed-based media supported the growth and melanin production of C. neoformans. Among them, garden cress seed agar demonstrated superior melanin production, both visually and spectrophotometrically. Black til seed agar also supported appreciable melanin production, whereas flax seed agar demonstrated comparatively lesser pigment production than the other two media.

Several plant-based media have previously been studied for melanin production in Cryptococcus, including tobacco agar, mustard seed agar, banana blossom agar, and sunflower seed agar.4–7,11 Previous studies have confirmed that Niger seed agar is an effective medium due to its abundant phenolic compounds, which are essential for melanin synthesis. Similarly, tobacco agar and mustard seed agar have also been shown to contain phenolic compounds that support melanin production.5,6 In the present study, garden cress seed agar showed the highest melanin production, comparable to Niger seed agar. Based on available literature, there are no previous reports describing the use of garden cress seed agar, black til seed agar, or flax seed agar for demonstrating melanin production in Cryptococcus. The phenolic and bioactive compounds present in these seeds may have contributed to enhanced melanin synthesis.12-14

The present study had certain limitations. Only a limited number of clinical isolates of C. neoformans were included in this study, and environmental isolates or standard reference strains were not tested. In addition, the study was limited to phenotypic and spectrophotometric assessment of melanin production; molecular analysis was not performed.

The findings of the present study may have practical applications in routine microbiology laboratories, particularly in resource-limited settings. Garden cress seed agar can serve as an affordable, locally available, and eco-friendly alternative medium for demonstrating melanin production in Cryptococcus. The use of easily available culinary seeds may reduce dependence on commercially available specialised media. Further studies can focus on developing dehydrated, ready-to-use formulations.
