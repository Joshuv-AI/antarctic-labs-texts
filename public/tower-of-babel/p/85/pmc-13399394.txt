Crosstalk Between Parkinson's Disease and Colorectal Cancer: Genetic Mechanisms, Gut Microbiota, and Therapeutic Insights
Authors: Jiacheng Ying, Zhengyuan Fang, Cheng Zhang, Xu Liu, Jianzhi Cui, Zezhi Li, Jiayin Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis review summarizes the potential genes involved in both Parkinson's disease (PD) pathogenesis and colorectal cancer (CRC) carcinogenesis, focusing on the oncogenic mechanisms that may arise from PD gene dysfunctions. By investigating genes such as PRKN, PINK1, and DJ‐1, which influence pathways primarily related to oxidative stress, mitophagy, cell cycle regulation, and inflammation, this review provides insight into how PD genes may contribute to CRC tumorigenesis through interactions with gut microbes such as Bacillus subtilis and Clostridium butyricum. This review highlights a mechanistic bridge between neurodegeneration and cancer centered on mitochondrial dysfunction, mitophagy, and inflammation. The convergence of molecular mechanisms and gut microbiota in PD and CRC highlights promising avenues for microbiome‐targeted interventions alongside genetic and molecular approaches to advance novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for these two diseases. Importantly, therapeutic strategies should be considered in a holistic manner, as interventions for one disease may influence the course of another, and probiotics represent a unique modality with the potential to confer preventive and therapeutic benefits across both conditions.Keywords: colorectal cancer, genetic susceptibility, gut microbiota, oxidative stress, Parkinson's disease

Background

Parkinson's disease (PD), recognized by motor dysfunctions [1, 2], is one of the most common neurodegenerative diseases, affecting approximately 572 out of every 100,000 individuals aged over 45 years [3]. Neuronal loss in the substantia nigra is the neuropathological hallmark of this disease. Multiple mechanisms and pathway dysfunctions have been identified as playing a role in the pathogenesis of PD, including oxidative stress, dysfunctional mitochondria and inflammation [4]. So far, numerous genes have been implicated in the development of PD, with more than a dozen of these genes, such as SNCA, PRKN, and PINK1, confirmed as causative [5].

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy globally and the second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide [6], highlighting the considerable global burden of the disease [7, 8]. Interestingly, some epidemiological studies have uncovered an inverse relationship between PD and CRC risk [9, 10]. Our recent meta‐analysis, which encompassed 17 studies with a total of 375,964 PD patients and 879,307 cancer patients, revealed a 22% reduced risk of CRC among PD patients in both cohort and case‐control studies [11]. These epidemiological findings suggest that shared molecular mechanisms might underlie both conditions. Several PD‐associated genes have been implicated in CRC, indicating potential links between these two diseases [12]. The gut‐brain axis is a bidirectional linkage between the central nervous system and the gastrointestinal tract. Disruptions in gut microbiota composition and their associated metabolites, such as short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and bacterial amyloids, have been linked to neurodegeneration and tumorigenesis. These findings suggest that gut microbial dysbiosis may play a potential role in both PD and CRC, in terms of disease susceptibility and progression via shared pathways.

Collectively, current evidence suggests that the association between PD and CRC is unlikely to be incidental. Beyond epidemiological observations, studies increasingly indicate that PD‐related biological alterations may extend beyond the nervous system and intersect with processes relevant to colorectal tumor biology [13, 14, 15]. At the same time, gut microbial dysbiosis and microbe‐derived metabolites have emerged as a plausible interface linking these two disorders, further supporting the idea that the PD‐CRC axis may involve coordinated host‐microbe interactions rather than isolated organ‐specific events [16].

However, the current evidence base remains fragmented. Epidemiological findings, PD‐associated genes, tumor‐related molecular changes, and gut microbial factors are often discussed in parallel rather than within a unified framework. In addition, although PD has been examined in relation to multiple malignancies, the specific relevance of CRC has not been sufficiently clarified in a focused and mechanistically integrated manner. In particular, how PD‐related genes and gut microbial factors may converge in the context of colorectal carcinogenesis has not yet been systematically synthesized.

In this context, the present review provides a CRC‐focused overview of the crosstalk between PD and colorectal tumorigenesis by integrating evidence from PD‐associated genes, host molecular alterations, and implicated gut microbes and their derivatives (Table 1). We further discuss the potential therapeutic implications of this interplay, with particular attention to microbiome‐targeted strategies, as interventions aimed at one disease may influence the course of the other. By organizing these previously dispersed findings into a more coherent conceptual framework, this review seeks to clarify the potential biological links between neurodegeneration and CRC and to highlight perspectives for future mechanistic and translational research.

Molecular Crosstalk and Mechanistic Intersections

Oxidative Stress and Mitophagy

Oxidative stress and mitophagy play significant roles in the pathogenesis and development of PD and cancer. In PD, oxidative stress arises from an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body's ability to eliminate these damaging molecules. This stress primarily affects dopaminergic neurons, contributing to their degeneration. Neurotoxins like rotenone and 6‐hydroxydopamine, which inhibit mitochondrial complex I, are commonly employed in models to replicate this oxidative damage [35]. Mitophagy, the process by which damaged mitochondria are removed, is crucial in maintaining cellular health. Impaired mitophagy in PD leads to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, exacerbating oxidative stress and further promoting neuronal death [36, 37]. Growing evidence indicates that impaired mitophagy [38, 39] and increased oxidative stress [40, 41] are major drivers of the ongoing neurodegeneration in PD.

In the context of cancer, oxidative stress and mitophagy are also involved in the carcinogenesis and development. Cells under oxidative stress may undergo mutations and genomic instability, thereby leading to uncontrolled growth and tumor progression [42, 43]. Mitophagy has been demonstrated to play a dual role in cancer. While it has the capacity to eliminate damaged mitochondria, thereby prevent further oxidative damage, it can also support the survival of cancer cells by maintaining energy production and metabolic flexibility [44]. This dynamic allows cancer cells to thrive even in stressful environments, highlighting the intricate role of mitophagy in tumor development. Understanding these molecular connections could offer valuable insights into CRC. PRKN and PINK1

PRKN, also known as PARK2, is located on the long arm of chromosome 6 (6q25.2‐q27). It was initially identified in association with juvenile‐onset PD [45]. Parkin, the protein encoded by the PRKN gene, is a cytosolic E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for tagging proteins with ubiquitin for degradation via the ubiquitin proteasome system [46]. Additionally, as a crucial regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis, Parkin's increasingly pivotal role in CRC has been reinforced by numerous studies, emphasizing its involvement in maintaining mitochondrial function and its potential as a tumor suppressor in CRC [47]. Interestingly, PRKN is situated within the common fragile site FRA6E, which is a well‐documented mutational hotspot that is frequently deleted in multiple tumor types [48, 49, 50]. Recent evidence showed that structural variations involving PRKN were commonly observed in CRC, particularly associated with chromosomal rearrangements at fragile sites [51, 52]. Additionally, PRKN‐related single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), frameshift mutations, deletions, and promoter hypermethylation have been widely reported in CRC patients [53, 54, 55, 56]. A growing number of studies have suggested that PRKN functions as a tumor suppressor in CRC. For instance, a data analysis study based on The Cancer Genome Atlas and Gene Expression Omnibus identified the significant downregulation of PRKN in CRC samples as compared to normal samples [57]. On the other hand, overexpression of PRKN was found to suppress CRC proliferation in vitro in another study [14]. Parkin protein, whose loss has been significantly observed in CRC cases carrying PRKN mutations, was notably downregulated in CRC tissues [58]. This reduction in Parkin expression has been strongly associated with poorer prognosis in CRC patients [59, 60]. Wu et al. demonstrated that restraining the activation of the PRKN‐mediated mitophagy pathway could ultimately drive CRC tumorigenesis [61]. All of these evidence collectively suggests a strong relationship between PRKN and CRC.

PTEN‐induced kinase 1 (PINK1), also known as PARK6, is the second most common gene causing autosomal recessive PD after PRKN [62]. The PINK1 protein is a serine/threonine kinase that is primarily localized in mitochondria and plays a pivotal role in preserving functional mitochondrial networks and selectively removing damaged mitochondria via mitophagy. This ensures cellular homeostasis and protection against oxidative stress [63]. Several studies have revealed the alteration of PINK1 expression in CRC patients. Two microarray‐based studies have identified significant downregulation of PINK1 in CRC tissues [57, 64]. One of these studies further demonstrated that PINK1 expression was consistently reduced across all clinical stages of CRC (I–IV) [64]. An in vivo animal experiment further provided evidence that overexpression of PINK1 suppressed colon tumor growth by promoting mitophagy, decreasing glycolysis, and enhancing mitochondrial respiration [65]. Additionally, PINK1 was also found to be an independent prognostic factor for CRC [66]. Patients with higher or lower PINK1 immunoexpression were found to be associated with worse overall survival. Moreover, the detrimental effect of high PINK1 expression on CRC prognosis was replicated in two studies [15, 67]. Despite the contradictory effects of PINK1 on CRC progression and prognosis, the positive role of PINK1‐related SNPs in adjuvant chemotherapy has been well‐established [68]. These SNPs have been shown to contribute to the effectiveness of chemotherapy in CRC patients, which was hypothesized to be achieved by inhibition of mitophagy in stage III and IV CRC patients, thereby reducing drug resistance in cancer cells.

The PINK1/Parkin pathway is widely acknowledged as an important pathway regulating mitophagy in the context of cellular stress [69, 70, 71]. Under normal circumstances, PINK1 is imported to the mitochondrial inner membrane and then degraded by the intramembrane protease PARL. However, when mitochondria lose membrane potential, PINK1 accumulates on the mitochondrial outer membrane, leading to Parkin recruitment and phosphorylation activation [72, 73]. Upon activation, Parkin ubiquitinates mitochondrial proteins, thus initiating p62/SQSTM1‐mediated autophagosome encapsulation and subsequent lysosomal degradation [74, 75, 76]. The pivotal role of PINK1/Parkin pathway in relation to mitophagy regulation was widely researched in the context of tumorigenesis. The enhancement of the PINK1/Parkin signaling‐mediated mitophagy pathway was identified as one of the mechanisms through which sodium butyrate (NaB) inhibits CRC cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis [17]. Similarly, PINK1/Parkin‐dependent mitophagy was also found to participate in CRC apoptosis induced by dietary δ‐Valerobetaine [18]. In contrast, inhibition of PINK1/Parkin signaling, as induced by piR‐823, may play a role in regulating CRC tumorigenesis, further indicating the role of this pathway in regulating cellular death processes in CRC [19]. It is speculated that the tumor‐suppressing functions of Parkin and PINK1 are closely tied to their roles in modulating mitochondrial biology. By clearing depolarized mitochondria, they might help prevent the excessive accumulation of ROS and the shift towards Warburg metabolism, both of which are hallmarks of tumor progression [77, 78, 79, 80].

DJ‐1

DJ‐1, encoded by the PARK7 gene, is a multifunctional protein composed of 189 amino acids, which typically appears in the form of dimers and is expressed across a wide range of cell types, including neurons, glial cells, and cancer cells [81]. This protein plays a crucial role in oxidative stress response and mitochondrial regulation, acting as a cellular sensor against oxidative damage through its cysteine residues C46, C53, and C106 [24, 82, 83]. Mutations in DJ‐1, such as L166P, often lead to the loss of function and conformational changes in the protein, which have been identified in 1%–2% of early‐onset PD cases [84, 85, 86, 87, 88]. Beyond its role in neuroprotection, DJ‐1 also contributes to the regulation of transcription factors such as Nrf2 and p53, influencing processes such as mitochondrial homeostasis, signal transduction, and anti‐apoptotic responses [81, 89, 90, 91]. DJ‐1 is overexpressed in reactive astrocytes in sporadic PD and other neurodegenerative diseases, further emphasizing its important involvement in stress response mechanisms [81]. This complex function highlights the importance of DJ‐1 not only in PD but also in broader cellular stress mechanisms.

DJ‐1, initially considered as a putative oncogene due to its role in antioxidative stress responses and cancer progression, has been shown to be overexpressed in CRC tissues [82, 92, 93]. Proteomic analyses revealed significantly elevated DJ‐1 levels in CRC, particularly in advanced‐stage tumors [94, 95]. Its overexpression is associated with ROS‐induced metastasis and cancer progression [96]. Recent studies demonstrated that DJ‐1 promoted mitophagy, enabling CRC cell survival [22], whereas its inhibition led to mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS accumulation, impeding tumor growth [23]. DJ‐1 also enhances CRC survival in hypoxic conditions via the PI3K‐AKT‐HIF‐1α pathway [24]. These findings highlight the pivotal role of DJ‐1 in sustaining mitochondrial stability and redox equilibrium, thereby promoting CRC pathogenesis by supporting cancer cell survival and resistance to apoptosis under oxidative stress.

ATP13A2

ATPase cation transporting 13A2 (ATP13A2, also known as PARK9) encodes a lysosomal P5‐type ATPase, which plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular ion homeostasis by transporting cations during ATP hydrolysis [97]. Mutations in ATP13A2 cause Kufor‐Rakeb syndrome, a rare autosomal recessive, juvenile‐onset PD associated with spasticity, dementia, and supranuclear gaze palsy [98, 99]. In PD, ATP13A2 is significantly expressed in nigral dopaminergic neurons [100]. Its dysfunction is implicated in lysosomal impairment, leading to α‐synuclein accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction, both of which are key pathological features of PD [98].

Lower expression of ATP13A2 has been linked to reduced CRC tumor volume and enhanced response to treatment in colon cancer models [31]. Additionally, Zhang et al.'s study suggested that upregulation of ATP13A2 might promote CRC progression by regulating oxidative stress and metabolic pathways, particularly the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) [30]. As a critical route for glucose processing, the PPP plays a pivotal role in cellular metabolism and maintaining antioxidative defenses: it generates ribose‐5‐phosphate, essential for nucleotide synthesis, and NADPH, crucial for neutralizing ROS [101]. Thus, it is hypothesized that ATP13A2's influence on the PPP could improve cancer cell resistance to oxidative stress, thereby contributing to overall tumor progression. Beyond its involvement in oxidative stress and metabolic pathways in relation to PPP, Chen et al.'s study highlights the role of ATP13A2 in promoting colon cancer progression via autophagy regulation [31]. The study demonstrated that ATP13A2 knockdown reduced autophagy levels, thus leading to a decrement in CRC cell stemness and invasiveness. In terms of prognosis, high expression of ATP13A2 was correlated with poorer overall survival in CRC patients.

SNCA

SNCA (also known as PARK1 and PARK4), mapped on 4q21.3‐q22, is the first gene reported to cause autosomal dominant PD [102]. Its encoded protein α‐synuclein structure features an N‐terminal region with incomplete KXKEGV motifs, a highly hydrophobic NAC domain, and a highly acidic C‐terminal domain [103]. Under physiological conditions, α‐synuclein is thought to exist either as an intrinsically disordered monomer or as a helically folded tetramer [104]. As part of the synuclein family, α‐synuclein is prone to aggregation, a characteristic linked to severe human diseases, emphasizing its role as an intrinsically disordered protein [105]. α‐Synuclein, the primary component of Lewy bodies, has been closely linked to mitochondrial disruption [106]. Recent studies have identified the specific mitochondrial targets affected by α‐synuclein and the particular forms of this protein responsible for such effects [107, 108, 109]. The discovery that Lewy bodies contain fragmented mitochondria, along with lipids and lysosomes, underscores α‐synuclein's intrinsic role in mitochondrial dysfunction [110].

While predominantly expressed in brain tissues, α‐synuclein is also widely detected across various tissues (including colorectal tissue) throughout the body [111]. In CRC, early research identified SNCA as frequently hypermethylated in patient stool, blood, and tumor tissues, suggesting it as a potential tumor suppressor gene and prognostic factor [112, 113, 114]. More recently, a study conducted by Firouzjaei et al. has shown that SNCA expression is significantly downregulated in CRC [115]. However, higher SNCA expression levels have been associated with worse prognosis compared to lower levels, indicating a complex and multifaceted role of SNCA in CRC onset and progression. Investigation into the mechanisms by which SNCA influences CRC progression is critically lacking; further research is necessary to clarify its precise role in tumor biology and patient prognosis.

Taken together, the mechanisms summarized in Figure 1 can be organized into three interconnected layers: mitophagy and mitochondrial quality control, redox homeostasis and oxidative stress, and hypoxia‐adaptive survival signaling. In this framework, PINK1/Parkin primarily regulate mitochondrial integrity and ROS balance, whereas DJ‐1 and ATP13A2 appear to support CRC cell adaptation to oxidative and metabolic stress. This layered summary helps clarify how PD‐associated genes converge on CRC‐related mitochondrial and redox vulnerabilities.

Cell Cycle Regulation

Beyond mitochondrial quality control and redox homeostasis, several PD‐associated genes also intersect with oncogenic programs that govern cell‐cycle progression. The cell cycle is a fundamental process regulating growth, differentiation, proliferation, and death of cells. Although neurons are classically considered terminally differentiated, recent findings reveal that post‐mitotic neurons attempt to re‐enter the cell cycle under pathological conditions, particularly in neurodegenerative diseases like PD [116, 117, 118]. Studies have shown that cell cycle dysregulation plays a pivotal role in the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra of PD. Hyperphosphorylation of the retinoblastoma protein has been observed in the substantia nigra and hippocampus from PD patients’ autopsy tissues [119]. This hyperphosphorylation indicates aberrant cell cycle activation, as confirmed by increased PCNA and E2F‐positive cells, as well as elevated DAPI staining. Experiments using MPP+ (a neurotoxin) on rat midbrain neurons further support this, showing increased levels of cell cycle markers like cyclins D, A, E, and B, and hyperphosphorylated retinoblastoma protein [120]. As shown by the colocalization of caspase‐3 and BrdU‐positive cells, this unusual re‐entry into the cell cycle leads to apoptosis. Moreover, two crucial cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs), CDK5 and CDK2, also have been implicated in the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in PD models [121]. Treatment with the CDK inhibitor flavopiridol reversed some of the degenerative effects, highlighting the potential therapeutic role of targeting cell cycle dysregulation in PD. Collectively, these findings link aberrant reactivation to neurodegeneration, highlighting the significance of cell cycle control in PD. On the other hand, cancer is characterized by uncontrolled cell division, where cells continuously initiate the cell cycle without properly exiting it. Dysregulation in cell cycle control pathways—such as those involving cyclins, CDKs, and checkpoint signaling—can disrupt normal cell cycle progression [122]. These disruptions contribute to conditions that favor uncontrolled proliferation, which drives cancer development. Any perturbation in these regulatory mechanisms may compromise the ability of cells to properly manage growth signals, leading to tumor formation and disease progression. This intersection suggests that dysregulation in cell cycle regulation may be a key convergence point, implying that PD‐associated genes may play a role in cancer pathogenesis by disrupting normal cell cycle regulation (Figure 2).

PRKN

As previously mentioned, PRKN mutations are frequently observed in CRC patients, suggesting its role as a potential tumor suppressor gene. In addition to its function in mitophagy regulation, PRKN may also play a role in cell cycle control. A study by Ikeuchi et al. demonstrated that Parkin expression in colonic epithelial cells was regulated in a cell cycle‐dependent manner through the EGF‐PI3K‐Akt pathway [20]. Notably, the study revealed that Parkin promoted the degradation of cyclin E, an activator of CDK2, necessary for the G1‐S phase transition of the cell cycle [123]. Disruption of Parkin function could impair cyclin E degradation, resulting in excessive cyclin E‐CDK2 accumulation, which might drive uncontrolled cell cycle activation and CRC progression.

In a subsequent study, Parkin overexpression in HCT‐15 CRC cells was found to significantly reduce cell viability by facilitating G2/M cell cycle arrest [21]. The mechanism involves Parkin upregulating p53, which in turn increases p21 levels and downregulates cyclin B1, a critical regulator of the G2/M phase transition. The reduction in cyclin B1 was previously linked to G2/M arrest, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation [124, 125, 126].

Additionally, broader studies on PRKN have highlighted its role in cell cycle regulation beyond CRC. For instance, PINK1/Parkin influences cell cycle by sequestering TBK1 at damaged mitochondria, thereby inhibiting mitosis [127]. Another review emphasized Parkin's regulation of mitosis through Cdc20/Cdh1, coactivators of anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome, which is essential for genomic stability [128]. While these studies are not CRC‐specific, they indicate possible mechanisms through which PRKN might exert control over cell cycle regulation in various cancers.

These studies collectively suggest that PRKN mutations may influence CRC tumorigenesis via several cell cycle regulatory mechanisms, such as involving cyclin E and through p53‐mediated regulation of p21 and cyclin B1, leading to dysregulated cell cycle control.

DJ‐1

DJ‐1 has consistently emerged as a critical regulator of cell cycle dynamics in CRC, impacting tumor progression through diverse signaling pathways and modulation of cell cycle‐related proteins. Initial studies highlighted DJ‐1's role in enhancing CRC cell proliferation and invasiveness by activating the PTEN‐AKT pathway, establishing it as a potent oncogenic factor linked to poor prognosis [25]. Additionally, DJ‐1 upregulates WNT/β‐catenin signaling, subsequently promoting BMP4 expression to drive cell migration and invasion, while simultaneously boosting cyclin D1 expression to facilitate cell cycle progression [27].

Recent findings in 2022 have unveiled further complexities in DJ‐1's regulatory roles in CRC [28]. Zhu et al. demonstrated that DJ‐1 indirectly inhibited p53 by upregulating MDM2, thus repressing p53‐driven tumor suppression. DJ‐1 also promotes CRC proliferation by upregulating cyclin D1, facilitating uninterrupted cell cycle progression, and enhances cell survival by regulating p53 downstream molecules, increasing anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 levels while reducing pro‐apoptotic Bax and Caspase‐3 levels. Beyond these mechanisms, DJ‐1 was shown to activate fibroblast growth factor 9 via WNT signaling, enabling epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition and further amplifying CRC cell invasiveness [29].

Adding to these insights, Wang et al. revealed DJ‐1's modulation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, enhancing phosphorylated PI3K and Akt levels, which reinforces CRC invasiveness across both in vitro and in vivo models [26]. Notably, DJ‐1's regulation of p27 and cyclin E showed a paradox between in vitro and xenograft models: suppressing these proteins in vitro, while upregulating them in vivo, indicating a context‐specific role in CRC progression. Collectively, these findings underscore DJ‐1's multifaceted influence on CRC pathogenesis in relation to cell cycle regulation.

FBXO7

F‐box only protein 7 gene (FBXO7), also known as PARK15, is a gene encoding an F‐box‐containing protein that serves as a specificity subunit in the SKP1‐cullin‐F‐box E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. FBXO7 is essential for cellular functions involving protein degradation via the ubiquitin proteasome system, cellular stress response, and mitochondrial quality control [129, 130, 131]. Mutations in FBXO7 cause PARK15, a juvenile parkinsonism, resulting in disrupted neuroprotective functions and impaired mitophagy [132]. These mutations lead to impairment of neuroprotective function and aggravation of mitochondrial proteotoxicity by promoting the formation of parkin protein aggregates [131]. While primarily linked to PD pathogenesis, emerging evidence suggests that FBXO7's regulation of proteostasis and cellular stress response mechanisms may play a role in tumorigenesis and oncogenic pathways [133, 134].

FBXO7 was classically considered oncogenic in CRC due to its frequent overexpression in CRC tissues, where it appears to contribute to cell cycle regulation through its specific interactions with the cyclin D/CDK complexes, particularly cyclin D/CDK6 [32]. The study showed that FBXO7 knockdown selectively decreased the association of cyclin D3 with CDK6 but did not affect CDK4, suggesting a selective role in stabilizing cyclin D/CDK6 complexes essential for G1/S phase progression. This specific regulation of the cyclin D/CDK6 complex enables FBXO7 to potentially disturb cell cycle advancement, implicating its role in promoting tumorigenic activity in CRC.

As summarized in Figure 2, PD‐associated genes influence CRC through three major functional outputs: cell‐cycle progression and checkpoint control, apoptosis and survival regulation, and migration and invasive signaling. PRKN mainly acts through cyclin‐ and p53‐related checkpoint pathways, whereas DJ‐1 and FBXO7 are more strongly associated with sustained proliferation, resistance to cell death, and invasive phenotypes. Inflammation

In parallel with oxidative and proliferative abnormalities, inflammatory signaling provides an additional layer through which PD‐related genes may shape CRC‐relevant biology. Inflammation, a vital response to cellular damage, is pivotal in neurodegeneration. In PD, neuroinflammation is increasingly seen as a driver of the disease rather than a consequence, as chronic inflammation and sustained cytokine release promote neurodegeneration and early non‐motor symptoms such as gastrointestinal problems and sleep disturbances [135, 136, 137, 138]. This chronic inflammatory state is intensified by age‐related immune changes, particularly “inflammaging,” further exacerbating PD's pathology [139, 140, 141]. In oncology, the role of inflammation was first noted by Rudolf Virchow in the 19th century, and recent research recognizes an inflammatory microenvironment as a hallmark of all tumor types [142, 143]. For instance, chronic inflammatory diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease, significantly elevate the risk of CRC [144]. Comparing PD's inflammatory mechanisms with those in CRC may shed light on shared therapeutic pathways, offering promising intervention points across these two seemingly distinct diseases (Figure 3).

LRRK2

Mutations in the leucine‐rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) gene are the single most common cause of inherited PD [145]. LRRK2 protein stands as a pivotal serine‐threonine protein kinase within the expansive ROCO protein family, which includes proteins with a Ras of complex proteins domain and a kinase domain [146]. In LRRK2, these two domains interact to regulate cellular functions like inflammation, protein degradation, autophagy, mitochondrial functions and modulation of the Golgi network [147, 148, 149, 150]. Mutations in these domains, such as the G2019S mutation in the kinase domain, can lead to dysregulated protein activity and are implicated in PD [151].

Interestingly, an observational study revealed that PD patients carrying the LRRK2‐G2019S variant were prone to colon cancer compared to those with idiopathic PD [152]. This association was subsequently confirmed in a meta‐analysis, which reinforced the link between the LRRK2‐G2019S mutation and an increased risk of colon cancer [153]. Despite the growing evidence linking LRRK2‐G2019S with an increased risk of CRC, the mechanisms driving LRRK2‐G2019S‐mediated tumorigenesis remain largely unknown. Wang et al. utilized a colitis‐associated cancer model to study the impact of the LRRK2‐G2019S mutation on CRC development [33]. The colitis‐associated cancer model, which is induced by dextran sulfate sodium, emulates chronic inflammation, a known CRC risk factor. In this context, LRRK2‐G2019S knock‐in mice exhibited an increased tumor load and size, highlighting the role of LRRK2 mutation in CRC progression. Further, the LRRK2‐G2019S mutation was found to intensify intestinal inflammation, which was evidenced by elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and IL‐18, as well as downstream markers of inflammasome activation such as GSDMD cleavage. The knock‐in mice also displayed increased epithelial cell proliferation, which is a critical factor in early tumor development. Notably, treatment with GSDMD inhibitors mitigated inflammation severity, implying the role of the LRRK2‐GSDMD signaling axis in CRC pathogenesis by promoting both inflammation and epithelial cell proliferation in the gut.

PLA2G6

The PLA2G6 gene, located on chromosome 22q13.1, encodes iPLA2β, a calcium‐independent phospholipase A2 essential for lipid metabolism, mitochondrial dynamics, immune responses, and autophagy [154, 155, 156]. iPLA2β generates critical lipid mediators such as docosahexaenoic acid and arachidonic acid (AA), which are involved in maintaining phospholipid homeostasis and regulating inflammatory signaling pathways [157]. This enzyme is found in multiple cellular compartments, including the mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and plasma membrane, indicating its extensive role across diverse cellular processes [158, 159]. Mutations in PLA2G6 are associated with recessive juvenile‐onset PD, PARK14, characterized by motor symptoms, cognitive decline, and other neurological impairments [160]. Such mutations reduce iPLA2β activity, resulting in lipid metabolism disruptions, oxidative stress, and impaired mitochondrial function, which contribute to neurodegeneration [161, 162]. Additionally, abnormal lipid accumulation in PD brains with PLA2G6 mutations has been linked to α‐synuclein aggregation in Lewy bodies, suggesting that lipid dysregulation may contribute to PD pathogenesis by promoting neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction [163, 164].

In the context of CRC, recent studies have also recognized PLA2G6 as a significant factor in CRC risk and progression due to its involvement in fatty acid metabolism and inflammatory pathways. Polymorphisms within PLA2G6 were associated with an increased risk of CRC, likely through their effects on AA metabolism—a pathway that triggers AA release, subsequently metabolized into pro‐inflammatory prostanoids [165]. Research in CRC cell models has demonstrated that iPLA2 enhances AA release from phospholipids, leading to the synthesis of pro‐inflammatory eicosanoids, such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) [34]. Elevated PGE2 levels promote cellular growth, angiogenesis, and inhibit apoptosis, thereby fostering a pro‐tumorigenic environment within the colon. These findings emphasize the dual role of PLA2G6 in lipid remodeling and as a mediator of inflammation. By intensifying inflammatory responses and creating a CRC‐supportive environment, PLA2G6‐encoded iPLA2 emerges as a critical metabolic and inflammatory contributor to CRC, marking it as a potential therapeutic target for disrupting the lipid signaling and inflammation that drive CRC progression.

Implicated Gut Microbiota and Their Derivatives

Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis)

Recent studies suggest that B. subtilis may modulate α‐synuclein aggregation and influence mitochondrial quality control through the PINK1/Parkin pathway, both of which are implicated in the pathogenesis of PD. Studies have shown that B. subtilis PXN21 reduces α‐synuclein aggregation in Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) models by activating protective pathways such as DAF‐16/FOXO and sphingolipid metabolism. Through its biofilms and metabolic products, this bacterium alters host metabolic patterns, mitigating α‐synuclein aggregation and potentially slowing PD progression [166]. Further supporting this neuroprotective function, B. subtilis R0179 has been shown to alleviate Aβ‐induced paralysis, an effect significantly diminished when PINK1 was knocked out, suggesting that the bacterium may exert its effects at least in part through the PINK1‐mediated mitophagy [167].

Beyond its influence on mitochondrial quality control and protein aggregation, B. subtilis has demonstrated additional neuroprotective properties, including its ability to inhibit acetylcholinesterase, thereby reducing the degradation of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter crucial for cognitive function [168]. In PD models induced by haloperidol, administration of B. subtilis was associated with improved motor function, reduced dopaminergic neuron degeneration, and decreased α‐synuclein accumulation. These beneficial effects were accompanied by increased superoxide dismutase and dopamine levels, reduced monoamine oxidase B and malondialdehyde levels, and improved gut microbiota composition, marked by an increase in beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and a decrease in harmful bacteria such as Escherichia coli (E. coli) [169]. The modulation of gut microbiota further underscores the role of B. subtilis in the gut‐brain axis, providing an additional mechanism by which it may exert neuroprotective effects.

B. subtilis has also demonstrated significant potential in CRC, exerting both tumor‐suppressive and tumor‐promoting effects depending on its bioactive components. Certain strains of B. subtilis have been shown to induce apoptosis in CRC cells through multiple mechanisms. Its supernatant upregulates Bax and caspase‐3 expression, enhancing apoptosis and improving the efficacy of radiation therapy [170]. Additionally, B. subtilis‐derived Iturin A‐like lipopeptides initially promote autophagy, but this process later transitions into apoptosis via mitochondrial pathways, marked by increased Bax and Bad expression and decreased Bcl‐2 levels [171]. Since PRKN has been implicated in upregulating p53, which subsequently regulates Bax and caspase‐3, the pro‐apoptotic effects observed with B. subtilis suggest a potential involvement of PRKN‐mediated pathways. In addition to its direct effects on cancer cell death, B. subtilis has been shown to modulate the tumor microenvironment. In combination with Clostridium butyricum (C. butyricum), it has been reported to suppress CRC cell proliferation by reducing inflammation and restoring immune homeostasis [172]. However, despite these tumor‐suppressive properties, certain bioactive components of B. subtilis may contribute to tumor progression. The serine protease subtilisin, secreted by B. subtilis, has been shown to degrade key tumor suppressors such as Deleted in Colorectal Cancer and Neogenin, which are critical for cell adhesion and tumor suppression [173]. The loss of these factors may enhance cancer cell migration and invasion, highlighting a potential tumor‐promoting aspect of the bacterium.

C. butyricum

C. butyricum is also linked to the PINK1/Parkin pathway, playing a role in mitochondrial quality control, neuroprotection, and tumor suppression. In the context of PD, C. butyricum has demonstrated neuroprotective properties, particularly through its influence on mitochondrial quality control. Studies have shown that an engineered strain, C. butyricum‐GLP‐1, enhances PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitophagy, alleviating oxidative stress and improving motor function in PD models [174]. Additionally, C. butyricum plays a role in gut‐brain axis regulation by stimulating intestinal GLP‐1 secretion and activating GLP‐1 receptors in the brain, which are linked to improved synaptic function, reduced neuroinflammation, and enhanced neuronal survival [175]. Beyond its role in PD, C. butyricum has also been implicated in alleviating neuroinflammatory conditions, with evidence showing that it upregulates protectin D1, an anti‐inflammatory lipid metabolite, under antibiotic‐induced dysbiosis [176]. Furthermore, it strengthens gut barrier integrity by increasing the expression of tight junction proteins such as occludin and ZO‐1, further reducing systemic inflammation that may contribute to neurodegenerative progression [177].

Beyond neuroprotection, C. butyricum exerts significant tumor‐suppressive effects in CRC through multiple mechanisms. C. butyricum contributes to the production of SCFAs in the gut, which are protective in CRC [178]. Additionally, C. butyricum interacts with the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway, a critical oncogenic regulator in CRC, inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis [179]. These effects are further complemented by its ability to regulate bile acid metabolism, reducing the secretion of tumor‐promoting secondary bile acids while enhancing the activity of G protein‐coupled receptors (GPRs) such as GPR43 and GPR109A, both of which have been associated with anti‐inflammatory and tumor‐suppressive functions in the gut [180]. Furthermore, C. butyricum has been shown to reprogram CRC cell behavior by targeting MYC, a key transcription factor governing cell cycle progression and therapy resistance. Through enhanced proteasome‐mediated ubiquitination, C. butyricum promotes MYC degradation, thereby disrupting its oncogenic ability to drive cell cycle progression [181]. This mechanism not only suppresses CRC cell proliferation and migration but also enhances sensitivity to 5‐fluorouracil chemotherapy and improves responsiveness to anti‐PD1 immunotherapy [181].

SCFAs and Gut‐Derived Metabolites

SCFAs, primarily produced by gut microbiota through the fermentation of dietary fibers, play a crucial role in maintaining host metabolic homeostasis and influencing both neurodegenerative diseases and cancer. Among them, NaB and propionate have been reported to modulate key pathways involved in mitochondrial function, mitophagy, and inflammation, particularly in relation to the aforementioned genes.

NaB has been shown to enhance the PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitophagy pathway, which contributes to mitochondrial quality control and inhibits tumor progression in CRC [17]. Additionally, NaB restores PRKN expression by suppressing the RELA‐HDAC8 complex, thereby improving mitochondrial function and mitigating mitophagy dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis in high‐glucose‐induced stress conditions [182]. Similarly, propionate, a key metabolite produced by Akkermansia muciniphila, has been implicated in neuroprotection. Propionate enhances PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitophagy via GPR43 activation while simultaneously downregulating the mitochondrial fission protein DRP1 through GPR41 signaling, promoting mitochondrial homeostasis and counteracting Alzheimer's disease. Administration of propionate or Akkermansia muciniphila has been reported to improve cognitive function and mitochondrial activity in Alzheimer's disease models, further supporting the critical role of SCFAs in neurodegenerative disease modulation [183].

Beyond SCFAs, other gut‐derived metabolites such as hydrogen sulfide (H2S) have been linked to pathogenic processes, particularly in PD. Bacteria from the Desulfovibrionaceae and Enterobacteriaceae families, which are known for their H2S‐producing capabilities, have been implicated in α‐synuclein aggregation and PD progression. H2S readily penetrates intestinal cells, where it increases intracellular iron levels, disrupts mitochondrial function by releasing cytochrome c, and generates a pro‐oxidative environment that promotes α‐synuclein oligomerization and fibril formation [184]. In CRC, H2S plays a complex role, acting as a double‐edged sword in tumor progression [185]. Endogenously, H2S is primarily produced by cystathionine β‐synthase, which is overexpressed in CRC tissues, supporting bioenergetics, angiogenesis, and cancer‐associated fibroblast activation. However, at higher concentrations, exogenous H2S donors have demonstrated anti‐tumor effects, including inhibition of proliferation and induction of apoptosis.

Similarly, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a major endotoxin derived from Gram‐negative bacteria, has been shown to directly interact with α‐Syn, accelerating its aggregation and altering its structural dynamics [186]. Beyond its role in α‐Syn pathology, LPS from E. coli has been found to downregulate DJ‐1 expression in colorectal epithelial cells (HT‐29), a gene implicated in oxidative stress, mitophagy, and cell cycle regulation in PD [187]. Additionally, LPS exposure in intestinal organoids derived from canine intestinal mast cell tumors led to the downregulation of ATP13A2, another PD‐associated gene involved in lysosomal homeostasis [188]. In terms of CRC, the pathogenetic role of LPS in gut barrier dysfunction and inflammation has been widely investigated [189]. Elevated LPS levels have been linked to systemic inflammation and tumor progression, as well as its role in epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, cancer invasion, and metastasis. Targeting LPS‐mediated inflammatory pathways has emerged as a promising strategy for CRC prevention and therapy.

Functional Amyloids

Functional amyloids are protein aggregates that play essential roles in various biological processes, including biofilm formation, cell adhesion, and host‐microbe interactions [190]. Functional amyloids derived from gut bacteria have garnered increasing attention for their potential influence on neurodegenerative processes. Among these, biofilm‐associated proteins (BAPs) from Staphylococcus aureus and curli proteins from E. coli have been implicated in promoting the development of PD.

BAPs, membrane‐associated amyloid proteins from Staphylococcus aureus, have been shown to induce α‐synuclein aggregation and contribute to Lewy body‐like pathology. Experimental models, including C. elegans muscle models and SH‐SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells, indicate that BAP proteins accelerate α‐synuclein aggregation, particularly in neuronal cells. This aggregation appears to be mediated through a cross‐seeding mechanism, whereby BAP proteins facilitate the fibrillization of α‐synuclein, disrupting its degradation pathways, particularly chaperone‐mediated autophagy [191]. Similarly, curli proteins, extracellular amyloid fibers produced by E. coli, have been shown to influence α‐synuclein pathology through multiple mechanisms. Curli‐producing E. coli strains have been found to exacerbate α‐synuclein aggregation in PD mouse models, leading to enhanced pathology in both the gut and brain, accompanied by motor and gastrointestinal dysfunction [192, 193]. Notably, curli proteins appear to accelerate α‐synuclein aggregation via direct interaction, facilitating neuroinflammatory responses that may drive disease progression. In a C. elegans and SH‐SY5Y cell model, curli's primary subunit, CsgA, was found to co‐aggregate with α‐synuclein in a bidirectional manner—CsgA promoted α‐synuclein aggregation, while α‐synuclein stabilized CsgA within cells [193]. In vitro experiments show that CsgA proteins from different gut bacterial species differentially accelerate α‐synuclein aggregation by reducing the lag phase required for fibril formation [194]. Beyond α‐synuclein, curli proteins have also been implicated in promoting the aggregation of other neurodegenerative disease‐associated proteins, such as Aβ, superoxide dismutase 1, and huntingtin. Functional amyloids can also interact with genetic risk factors for PD. A study investigating E. coli‐derived curli found that while the LRRK2 R1628p risk variant alone was insufficient to induce α‐synuclein pathology, its combination with curli exposure significantly accelerated PD‐like pathological features [195]. This suggests that bacterial amyloids may act as environmental triggers that exacerbate genetic susceptibilities, highlighting a potential gene‐microbiota interaction in PD pathogenesis.

Functional amyloids are essential for biofilm formation and stabilization, providing structural integrity and resilience to bacterial communities [190]. Increasing evidence suggests that bacterial biofilms contribute to the pathogenesis of various human diseases, including CRC [196]. However, the specific role of functional amyloids, such as BAPs and curli, in CRC progression remains largely unexplored, underscoring a critical gap in current research.

Microbial Strains as Double‐Edged Modulators

When viewed across the PD‐CRC axis, the significance of specific microbial strains lies not only in their reported effects on each disease separately, but also in the fact that their biological actions are not necessarily uniform across contexts. In this sense, the term “double‐edged” does not simply imply opposite effects in PD and CRC, but rather reflects the possibility that the same microbial species may exert distinct consequences depending on the strain studied, the bioactive component involved, and the host setting in which the effect is observed.

B. subtilis illustrates this point most clearly. As discussed above, the available PD‐related studies predominantly support a neuroprotective role. In contrast, the CRC‐related evidence is less uniform: although several reports suggest anti‐tumor activity, other findings indicate that specific secreted products may also interfere with tumor‐suppressive pathways. Taken together, these observations argue against treating B. subtilis as a single functional entity at the species level. Rather, its biological relevance in the PD‐CRC framework appears to reside in its internal functional heterogeneity, with potentially beneficial and unfavorable effects arising from different microbial components or experimental contexts.

By comparison, the current evidence for C. butyricum is more directionally consistent, as the studies summarized above generally support protective associations in both PD‐ and CRC‐related models. However, this apparent consistency should still be interpreted with caution. Much of the evidence remains preclinical, and some mechanistic support relies on engineered strains or indirect pathway‐level inference. Therefore, its value in this review is not to establish a definitive cross‐disease therapeutic effect, but to suggest that certain microbial strains may converge on biological processes relevant to both neurodegeneration and colorectal tumorigenesis.

Thus, the key message is not that the same strain always produces opposite outcomes in PD and CRC, but that microbial effects are mechanistically bounded and context‐dependent. This perspective helps refine the interpretation of strain‐level evidence and underscores the need to distinguish species identity, strain specificity, and active microbial products when considering microbiota‐based mechanisms in the PD‐CRC axis.

Integrated Crosstalk Among Oxidative Stress, Inflammation, and Cell‐Cycle Dysregulation

The mechanisms discussed above should not be interpreted as fully independent modules. Rather, oxidative stress and mitophagy defects, inflammatory signaling, and cell‐cycle dysregulation appear to form an interconnected network that may help explain the molecular overlap between PD and CRC. In this framework, mitochondrial damage and redox imbalance may amplify inflammatory responses, whereas persistent inflammatory signaling may in turn aggravate oxidative stress and reshape proliferative behavior in colorectal epithelial cells. Thus, the shared genes reviewed here are better understood not only as pathway‐specific factors, but also as mechanistic nodes linking multiple biological processes.

Among these genes, PRKN and PINK1 occupy a central position because mitochondrial quality control lies upstream of both redox homeostasis and growth control. As discussed above, impairment of the PINK1/Parkin axis leads to defective mitophagy and accumulation of damaged mitochondria, which favors ROS elevation and stress adaptation. In parallel, PRKN is also linked to cell‐cycle regulation through cyclin E degradation and p53/p21/cyclin B1‐related control. This positions PRKN as a direct bridge between mitochondrial dysfunction and proliferative dysregulation, rather than a regulator confined to mitophagy alone. DJ‐1 represents an even broader cross‐mechanism hub. In the present review, DJ‐1 is implicated in oxidative stress responses, mitochondrial stability, hypoxia adaptation, and multiple growth‐related pathways, including PTEN‐PI3K‐AKT, WNT/BMP4, and MDM2‐p53/cyclin D1 signaling. These observations suggest that DJ‐1 may coordinate how redox adaptation is translated into enhanced survival and sustained cell‐cycle progression in CRC.

By contrast, LRRK2 and PLA2G6 more prominently illustrate the inflammatory arm of this network. LRRK2‐G2019S was discussed in relation to inflammasome‐associated cytokine production and epithelial proliferation in colitis‐associated tumorigenesis, while PLA2G6 promotes arachidonic‐acid‐derived inflammatory mediators such as PGE2, which are closely related to a tumor‐supportive microenvironment. Although these genes were introduced in the inflammation section, their effects are unlikely to remain restricted to inflammation itself, because chronic inflammatory signaling can reinforce oxidative stress, tissue injury, compensatory proliferation, and ultimately tumor progression. Taken together, these findings support the view that the PD‐CRC connection is unlikely to be mediated by any single pathway in isolation; instead, it may arise from coordinated shifts across interdependent processes centered on shared susceptibility genes.

Bidirectional or Unidirectional? Revisiting the PD–CRC Axis

Although the present review mainly addresses the PD‐to‐CRC direction, this asymmetry likely reflects both disease course and evidence structure: CRC usually follows a more time‐defined clinical trajectory, whereas PD is chronic and slowly progressive, making reverse‐direction longitudinal evidence harder to establish. Current support for a CRC‐to‐PD framework remains limited. First, CRC‐specific epidemiological evidence on subsequent PD risk is still scarce, and bidirectional Mendelian randomization studies have not produced a consistent reverse causal signal [197]. Second, in patients with cancer and pre‐existing PD, anticancer treatment and perioperative stress may be accompanied by worsening parkinsonian symptoms or functional decline, but direct evidence that these exposures accelerate PD progression remains insufficient; notably, in a 2025 multicenter cohort of patients with pre‐existing PD receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors, 21% showed worsening of parkinsonian symptoms, yet treatment‐attributable neurologic progression was not established [198]. Third, current mechanistic data are better interpreted as indicating plausible shared pathways rather than proving that CRC directly causes PD, with intestinal inflammation, barrier dysfunction, dysbiosis, and systemic immune activation all being biologically capable of influencing α‐synuclein pathology and neuroinflammation [16]. Therefore, under the current evidence landscape, focusing on PD‐CRC shared mechanistic links, particularly convergent genetic pathways and gut microbial metabolites, is likely more informative than overstating a direct CRC‐to‐PD causal model.

Scope and Generalizability of the PD‐CRC Framework

Notably, the currently available evidence does not suggest a clearly population‐restricted pattern. However, many microbiota‐related findings are still based mainly on preclinical studies rather than patient cohorts. Besides, although the present review mainly focuses on the PD‐CRC axis, accumulating epidemiological evidence suggests that inverse associations between PD and cancer may extend beyond CRC. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have reported an overall reduced cancer risk in patients with PD, with negative associations observed for several cancer categories, including digestive system, lung, and urinary system cancers [199, 200]. In addition, a recent Mendelian randomization study suggested a negative causal relationship between PD and gastric cancer, further supporting the possibility that the protective association linked to PD is not limited to CRC alone [201]. Therefore, the molecular and microbial overlap discussed in this review may not be entirely CRC‐specific, but may instead reflect broader oncogenic, inflammatory, or metabolic processes that could potentially be extrapolated to other malignancies and may offer clues for future therapeutic exploration across cancer types.

Conclusion and Perspective

This review explores the intricate convergence between PD and CRC, suggesting that the interplay between genetic susceptibility and gut microbial dysbiosis may serve as a critical nexus for these distinct pathologies.

Uncovering these molecular and microbial connections offers a deeper insight into the shared pathogenesis of neurodegeneration and carcinogenesis. The evidence summarized here indicates that PD‐associated genes—such as PRKN, PINK1, and DJ‐1—might extend their influence beyond neuronal health to modulate key oncogenic processes, including oxidative stress, mitophagy, and cell cycle regulation. Furthermore, the gut microbiota appears to function as a mechanistic bridge in this context. Specific taxa like B. subtilis and C. butyricum, along with their metabolites, may simultaneously affect α‐synuclein aggregation and tumorigenic pathways, highlighting the potential role of the gut‐brain axis as a shared pathological substrate.

A comprehensive understanding of these underlying mechanisms could, in turn, open new avenues for therapeutic intervention. As the biological pathways of PD and CRC intersect, therapeutic strategies may benefit from a more integrated perspective. In this regard, microbiome‐targeted approaches—ranging from probiotics and prebiotics to fecal microbiota transplantation—represent promising areas for future exploration [202]. By potentially restoring mitochondrial and immune homeostasis, such interventions might confer benefits across both disorders, paving the way for precision medicine strategies that bridge neurology and oncology.

Beyond therapeutic implications, these findings also point toward the potential value of early screening and proactive management. For individuals carrying pathogenic variants in genes like LRRK2, PINK1, or PRKN, dual surveillance programs covering both neurodegenerative and colorectal risks might be worthy of consideration. Additionally, monitoring fecal microbial profiles or inflammation‐associated biomarkers could offer a non‐invasive means to assess the risk of comorbidities. Implementing such personalized surveillance strategies may aid clinicians in detecting disease onset at an earlier stage, thereby facilitating more timely interventions for susceptible individuals.
