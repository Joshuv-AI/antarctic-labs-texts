Dissolvable silk microneedles delivering minocycline-loaded ZIF-8 nanoparticles for targeted modulation of sebocyte metabolism and improved acne therapy
Authors: Xuefan Yang, Dandan Ruan, Feng Ye, Xinlan Qiu, Sitong Li, Lei Huang, Christos C Zouboulis, Xiaohui Mo, Xiping Jiang, Qiang Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcne Vulgaris (AV) lesions exhibit an acidic, sebum-rich, and inflammation-prone microenvironment in which conventional minocycline therapy is constrained by the stratum-corneum barrier and systemic exposure. Here, we present the fabrication and application of dissolvable silk fibroin microneedles encapsulating minocycline-loaded zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs) to achieve sebaceous gland-targeted, pH-responsive delivery and multimodal therapy.ResultsThe Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs display uniform conical morphology, excellent mechanical robustness (peak failure force ≈ 0.4 N per needle), rapid dissolution (< 1 min), high drug loading (≈ 40.11%) and encapsulation efficiency (≈ 70.2%), and negligible hemolytic activity. Under mildly acidic conditions (pH 5.5), minocycline release was markedly accelerated compared to physiological pH 7.4, reflecting the acidified acne microenvironment. Functionally, Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs achieved > 95% inhibition of Cutibacterium acnes at 30 µg/patch and substantially attenuated sebocyte inflammatory responses. In heat-killed C. acnes-stimulated SZ95 sebocytes, the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs downregulated NFKB1, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and MMP9 expression, along with suppression of cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, IL-17 and NET-associated signaling pathways. RNA-sequencing also suggested that sebocyte metabolism may undergo reprogramming involving enhanced apoptosis, maintenance of mitochondrial oxidative activity, and modulation of PPARγ-related lipid metabolism, which could collectively contribute to reduced lipid droplet accumulation. In a C. acnes-induced acne-like mouse model, Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs markedly accelerated lesion resolution, reduced lesion elevation, eschar formation, and diameter scores, normalized epidermal thickness, and decreased sebaceous lipid accumulation, while exhibiting no detectable systemic toxicity as confirmed by histology, hematology, and serum biochemistry.ConclusionCollectively, this study establishes a nanobiotechnology platform that integrates metal-organic frameworks(MOF)-based pH-responsive delivery with antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic reprogramming activities, offering a precise and minimally invasive multimodal strategy for sebaceous gland-targeted acne therapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04221-8.

Background

Acne vulgaris is a prevalent inflammatory skin disorder that predominantly affects adolescents and young adults, exerting substantial physical and psychological burdens on patients [1]. Its pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving four hallmark processes: excessive sebum secretion, abnormal keratinization of the pilosebaceous unit, overcolonization of Cutibacterium acnes (C.acnes), and dysregulated immune-inflammatory responses involving Toll-like receptor (TLR) activation, neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, and excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [2–6]. Sebaceous glands play a central role in this cascade by providing a lipid-rich niche that favors C. acnes proliferation, generating pro-inflammatory lipids and mediators, and contributing to follicular occlusion [7]. Current therapeutic regimens for acne include topical retinoids, benzoyl peroxide, and antibiotics, as well as systemic isotretinoin, antibiotics, and hormonal antiandrogens.

Minocycline, a broad-spectrum tetracycline antibiotic, is widely employed for the management of moderate-to-severe inflammatory acne. In addition to its potent antibacterial activity, minocycline exhibits anti-inflammatory effects, suppresses sebum secretion, and modulates follicular keratinization to facilitate sebum excretion [8, 9]. Its pronounced lipophilicity enables preferential accumulation within the sebum‑rich environment of the pilosebaceous unit, thereby supporting its sebaceous gland-targeted therapeutic profile [10]. However, topical administration of minocycline is often hindered by the stratum corneum barrier, resulting in inadequate transdermal delivery, whereas systemic administration may induce antibiotic resistance and other adverse effects. These limitations highlight the urgent need for novel minocycline-based strategies that enhance cutaneous bioavailability while minimizing systemic exposure and associated toxicity.

Sebaceous glands play a pivotal role in acne pathogenesis, and their direct targeting presents a promising potential for effective therapy. Dissolvable microneedles (DMNs), fabricated from biocompatible materials, can painlessly breach the stratum corneum, dissolve rapidly, and release encapsulated drugs in a minimally invasive manner. This emerging platform serves as a promising strategy for sebaceous gland-targeted drug delivery with considerable translational potential [11, 12]. Among biopolymer matrices, silk fibroin (SF) is particularly advantageous owing to its excellent cytocompatibility, tunable biodegradability, and favorable mechanical strength, which ensures sufficient penetration into the dermis to reach the sebaceous glands [13]. Meanwhile, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), especially zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), provide unique features such as high surface area, tunable pore size, intrinsic biocompatibility, and pH-responsive degradation, thereby enabling intelligent strategies for drug delivery [14]. Notably, ZIF-8-based microneedle systems can sense the mildly acidic microenvironment of acne lesions to achieve site-specific and controlled drug release, significantly enhancing therapeutic precision and overall efficacy for sebaceous gland-targeted therapy.

In this context, we engineered an innovative dissolvable silk fibroin microneedle (MN) platform encapsulating minocycline@ZIF-8 nanocomposites (Mino@ZIF-8) to achieve sebaceous gland-targeted therapy. This system painlessly breaches the stratum corneum, dissolves rapidly to release Mino@ZIF-8, and effectively delivers minocycline into sebaceous glands. Beyond its intrinsic antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activities, minocycline also modulates sebocyte metabolism, offering synergistic potential for acne management. To validate this hypothesis, we fabricated and characterized Mino@ZIF-8 SF MNs, systematically evaluated their mechanical properties, drug release kinetics, skin penetration, and biocompatibility, and further investigated their regulatory effects on sebocyte lipid metabolism. Moreover, the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of Mino@ZIF-8 SF MNs was examined in a C.acnes-induced acne-like mouse model, focusing on their ability to suppress sebum secretion, mitigate inflammation, and accelerate lesion resolution. Collectively, this work establishes a high-efficiency, minimally invasive, and sebaceous gland-targeted delivery strategy for acne treatment. More importantly, it unveils a mechanism-based therapeutic approach that reprograms sebocyte metabolism, offering broad implications for precision dermatologic therapy and transdermal nanomedicine design.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and animals

Human sebaceous gland cells (SZ95) were generously provided by Professor Christos C. Zouboulis [15] and cultured in Sebomed medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, MA, USA), 5 ng/mL recombinant human epidermal growth factor (EGF; Gibco, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, MA, USA). Human keratinocytes (HaCaT) and human monocytic cells (THP-1), and human skin fibroblasts (HSF) were all obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). HaCaT and HSF cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, while THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, MA, USA) with the same supplements. All cells were incubated under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂).

Escherichia coli (ATCC25922) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC6538) were obtained from Shanghai Conservation Biotechnology Center (Shanghai, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Renji Hospital (RJ2025-068B). Female ICR mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Slac Animal (Shanghai, China). A total of 30 mice was randomly allocated to five groups (n = 6 per group) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. All procedures were performed in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. An additional three ICR mice were used for ex vivo dissolution testing of microneedles, skin irritation and recovery evaluation, and hemolysis assay.

Synthesis of ZIF-8 and Mino@ZIF-8 nanoparticles

ZIF-8 nanoparticles (NP) were synthesized via a co-precipitation method [16]. Briefly, 330 mg of 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM; Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in 10 mL of methanol under magnetic stirring (ZNCL-GS; Lingke, Shanghai, China) to obtain a clear solution. Separately, 150 mg of zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O, ≥ 99%) was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water. The zinc nitrate solution was then poured into the 2-MIM solution, and the resulting suspension was centrifuged, washed with methanol, and dried in a vacuum oven to yield ZIF-8 powder. Mino@ZIF-8 NPs were prepared following the same procedure, except that 40 mg of minocycline hydrochloride (Mino; Targetmol, MA, USA) was co-dissolved with 330 mg of 2-MIM in methanol prior to mixing with the zinc nitrate solution. After reaction and purification, the resulting yellow precipitate was lyophilized (Scientz-N; Ningbo, China) to obtain Mino@ZIF-8 powder.

Characterization of ZIF-8 and Mino@ZIF-8 NPs

The morphology of ZIF-8 and Mino@ZIF-8 NPs was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; FEI, Oregon, USA) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JSM-7500 F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The crystalline structure was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker, Bremen, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) scanned over a 2θ range of 10–80° at a rate of 10°/min. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Nicolet-460, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) was performed in the range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ to identify functional groups. The ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectrum of minocycline was recorded using a microvolume spectrophotometer (NanoDrop One; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and the maximal absorbance wavelength (λmax) was determined as 382 nm. All subsequent quantifications of drug loading and release were performed at this wavelength. To determine the drug loading content (DLC) and encapsulation efficiency (EE), a calibration curve of minocycline was established based on UV absorbance. After synthesis and centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured to determine the amount of unencapsulated drug. pH-responsive property in vitro drug release assay

The pH-responsive release behavior of minocycline from Mino@ZIF-8 NPs was evaluated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 5.5 and pH 7.4, simulating the acidic microenvironment of acne lesions and physiological conditions, respectively. In brief, 5 mg of NPs were dispersed in 5 mL of PBS at each pH condition and incubated at 37 °C under gentle shaking. At predetermined time intervals (2, 6, 12, 24 and 48 h), 2 µL of the release medium were collected and analyzed using UV-Vis at 382 nm to quantify the released minocycline. The cumulative release profile was calculated according to the standard calibration curve of minocycline. All experiments were performed in triplicate for each condition.

Preparation and morphological characterization of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs patches

All procedures were conducted under aseptic conditions within a laminar-flow biosafety cabinet. Sterile lyophilized water-soluble silk fibroin (SF; Yuxi Biotech, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in ultrapure water to obtain a 12% (w/v) SF solution. A 20% (w/v) polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution was prepared by dissolving sterile PVA (≥ 98% purity, Kelon Chemical Reagent, Chengdu, China) in distilled water at 90 °C under continuous stirring until completely dissolved, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min to remove entrapped air bubbles [17].

For MN fabrication, a 12% (w/v) SF solution containing Mino@ZIF-8 NPs (formulated to deliver 30 µg of mino@ZIF-8 equivalent per patch) was added to sterile polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) molds (EFL, Suzhou, China). The molds were subjected to vacuum degassing (-0.08 MPa, 15 min) and subsequently dried in a sterile vacuum chamber at room temperature for 6 h. This process was repeated three times with fresh solution added at each cycle to ensure complete filling of the microneedle cavities. Afterwards, 300 µL of 20% PVA solution was layered over the MN tips to form a uniform backing, followed by drying at 37 °C for over 12 h. The resulting patches were carefully demolded under sterile conditions and stored in sealed and light-protected containers until use. All procedures were conducted under minimal ambient light conditions to prevent photodegradation of minocycline.

Control microneedle formulations were prepared following the same procedure. For ZIF-8/SF MNs, an equivalent amount of ZIF-8 nanoparticles was incorporated into the 12% (w/v) SF matrix. For pure SF MNs, the 12% (w/v) SF solution without any nanoparticles was directly cast into PDMS molds and subjected to identical degassing and drying processes.

The overall morphology and surface microstructure of the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs were examined by SEM to evaluate MN sharpness and structural integrity.

Mechanical strength test of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs

The mechanical strength of the MNs were evaluated using a universal texture analyzer (Universal TA; Tengba Instruments, Shanghai, China) equipped with a cylindrical probe (P/6, 6 mm diameter). Each MN patch was trimmed to a 10 × 10 assay for compression testing. The probe was programmed with a trigger force of 0.049 N and a test speed of 5 mm/min. Compression was applied vertically until tip failure occurred or a maximum displacement of 900 μm was reached. The resulting force-displacement profiles were recorded to determine the fracture strength and mechanical integrity of the MNs. Following testing, deformed or fractured microneedle tips were examined using an optical microscope (CKX53; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) to visually confirm structural failure.

Ex vivo dissolution behavior of Mino@ZIF-8/SF microneedles

To evaluate the Ex vivo dissolution behavior of the microneedles, freshly excised dorsal skin from depilated ICR mice was secured onto a foam pad. Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN patches were gently applied to the skin and maintained in position for predetermined time intervals (0, 20 and 40 s). At each time point, the MNs were carefully removed and immediately examined under an optical microscope to assess the residual tip length. The initial full needle height prior to insertion was recorded as the baseline. All procedures were performed under aseptic conditions to prevent contamination. The dissolution extent was quantified by calculating the ratio of residual length to original needle height [17, 18].

HPLC analysis of minocycline stability

The chemical stability of minocycline during ZIF-8 encapsulation and microneedle fabrication was assessed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system (Agilent, USA). Free minocycline, Mino@ZIF-8 NPs, and Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs were analyzed using minocycline-equivalent amounts to enable direct comparison of drug recovery across formulations.

For nanoparticle and microneedle samples, EDTA-assisted decomposition followed by ultrasonication was performed to completely disassemble the ZIF-8 framework and release the encapsulated drug prior to HPLC injection. Chromatographic analysis was performed according to previously reported HPLC methods for tetracycline-class antibiotics, as described in a study on doxycycline-based microneedles [19]. Peak areas were used to quantify the relative recovery of minocycline.

Skin irritation and recovery assessment

Skin irritation and recovery following microneedle insertion were evaluated in depilated dorsal skin of ICR mice. Mino@ZIF-8/SF microneedle patches were applied to the skin and maintained for 5 min before removal. The treated sites were photographed using a digital camera immediately (0 min) and at 5, 10, 20, and 30 min post-removal to monitor skin recovery. The presence of erythema, microindentation, or edema was visually assessed to evaluate local irritation and the time required for complete restoration of the skin surface.

Cytocompatibility assessment

MN patches were fabricated with increasing Mino@ZIF-8 loading to achieve final drug-equivalent concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µg per patch. To specifically assess the cytocompatibility of the functional microneedle portion, only the needle tip was excised. Each MN(patch base area: 1 cm²) was immersed in 1 mL of culture medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain the extract [20].

HaCaT and THP-1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and exposed to 100 µL of each extract for 24 h. Cell viability was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

After extract exposure, the cells were washed with PBS and stained with Calcein-AM/PI working solution (2 µL each per 1 mL buffer; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse Ti-U microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), where green and red fluorescence indicated viable and dead cells, respectively. Quantification of live and dead cells was performed using ImageJ software through intensity thresholding and particle analysis.

Hemolysis assay

The hemocompatibility of the samples was evaluated by a hemolysis assay using fresh blood collected from ICR mice. Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min, washed three times with PBS, and diluted to a final concentration of 4% (v/v) in PBS. Test solutions of free Mino, ZIF-8 NPs, and Mino@ZIF-8 NPs were prepared in PBS. For each group, 0.5 mL of the RBC suspension was mixed with 0.5 mL of the test solution, PBS (negative control), or deionized water (ddH2O, positive control), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. Isolation and identification of Cutibacterium acnes from acne lesions

Facial acne lesion samples were obtained from patients at Renji Hospital dermatology outpatient clinic, following approval by the institutional ethics committee (approval no.LY2024-191-B). After cleansing the skin surface with 70% isopropyl alcohol, comedonal or pustular content were gently expressed or punctured with a sterile needle and collected using sterile swabs (COPAN, Italy). The samples were inoculated onto Gifu Anaerobic Medium (GAM) agar plates (Hopebio, China) and incubated in an anaerobic chamber (Don Whitley Scientific Ltd., UK) at 37℃for 48 h to 1 week. Plates were examined daily for the appearance of typical C. acnes colonies, which were subsequently picked for subculturing and purification [21].

To confirm species identity, 16S rRNA gene amplification was performed using universal primers 27 F (AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG) and 1492R (GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT). Amplicons were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and sequenced via the Sanger method. The resulting sequences were aligned to the NCBI GenBank database using BLAST, and strains exhibiting ≥ 99% sequence similarity were identified as C.acnes. Verified bacterial strains were cultured under anaerobic conditions at 37℃ and preserved at −80 °C in 25% glycerol for subsequent experiments.

Antibacterial effect of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs

Antibacterial performance of MNs at different concentrations

The antibacterial performance of Mino@ZIF-8 SF MNs was evaluated in vitro at five mino@ZIF-8 loadings (0, 10, 20, 30, and 50 µg per patch) using a standard dilution and colony-counting assay [18]. MN patches from each group were placed at the bottom of 48-well plates, and 200 µL of C.acnes, E. coli or S.aureus suspension (10⁵ CFU/mL) was added to each well, followed by 800 µL of the corresponding culture medium.

C. acnes was cultured in GAM broth under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h, whereas E. coli and S. aureus were cultured in LB broth (Hopebio, China) under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 12 h. After incubation, bacterial suspensions were serially diluted with sterile saline, and 50 µL were spread onto agar plates. Following further incubation, colony formation was recorded photographically, and colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ. To further confirm the antibacterial efficacy of the MNs, the optical density (OD600) of C. acnes, E. coli, and S. aureus cultures was measured after treatment under respective anaerobic or aerobic conditions using a microplate reader.

Antibacterial comparison among free Mino, ZIF-8, and Mino@ZIF-8

To compare the antibacterial efficacy of free Mino, ZIF-8 NPs, and Mino@ZIF-8 NPs, each sample was tested at an equivalent mino@ZIF-8 NPs concentration of 30 µg/mL. Based on the determined DLC of 40.11%, the actual minocycline content in Mino@ZIF-8 was calculated as approximately 12.03 µg per 30 µg of Mino@ZIF-8. Accordingly, the concentration of free minocycline in the control group was set at 12.03 µg/mL, while the ZIF-8 NPs group was adjusted to 30 µg/mL. This dosing strategy enabled independent assessment of the antibacterial effects of the drug, the carrier, and their combination under equivalent exposure conditions.

All groups were evaluated using the same antibacterial assay protocol described above, and bacterial suspensions were incubated under their respective culture conditions.

SEM visualization of bacterial morphology after MN treatment

To investigate the morphological changes in bacteria following MN treatment, C. acnes, E. coli, and S. aureus were co-cultured with Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN extracts for 24 h. After incubation, the bacterial suspensions were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), each for 10 min at room temperature, to remove residual moisture while preserving bacterial morphology. The samples were then air-dried, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged by SEM characterization to visualize surface morphology and structural damage [22].

In vitro scratch wound healing assay

The wound healing capacity of MN-derived extracts was evaluated using HaCaT keratinocytes and fibroblasts. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured in complete DMEM until reaching ~ 100% confluence. A linear scratch was generated using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip, and the wells were gently washed twice with PBS to remove detached cells.

Subsequently, the cells were incubated with serum-free medium containing extracts derived from Control (DMEM only), SF MN, or Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN patches. Wound closure was monitored and imaged at 0 h, 12 h, and 24 h using an inverted microscope (×100 magnification). The scratch area was quantified with ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and the percentage of wound closure was calculated. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

C.acnes was cultured anaerobically in GAM broth at 37 °C for 72 h. Bacterial suspensions were collected by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min at 4℃, washed three times with cold PBS, and adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0 (≈ 7.0 × 108 CFU/mL). The suspensions were heat-inactivated at 80 °C for 30 min [23]. SZ95 sebocytes were then treated either with heat-killed C. acnes alone or co-incubated with Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN extract for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and integrity were evaluated using a Qubit3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Agilent 5300 Analyzer (Agilent, USA). Only samples with RIN > 7.0 were used for subsequent library construction.

RNA library preparation and sequencing were performed by LC-Bio Technologies (China). Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA, fragmented into short fragments, and reverse-transcribed to generate cDNA. After second-strand synthesis, end repair, A-tailing, and adaptor ligation, the ligated products were enriched by PCR to construct strand-specific libraries with an average insert size of approximately 400 bp. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus platform following the vendor’s recommended protocol.

Raw reads were filtered to remove adaptors and low-quality sequences. Clean reads were aligned to the human genome using HISAT2, and gene expression levels were calculated as FPKM. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 software, with absolute fold change ≥ 2 and false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 as the significance thresholds. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/) databases to explore relevant biological processes and signaling pathways.

Quantitative real-time PCR for inflammatory cytokines

To evaluate the inflammatory response of SZ95 sebocytes, cells were divided into five groups: untreated control (C), stimulation with heat-killed C. acnes (HKC), and co-stimulation with heat-killed C. acnes plus either minocycline (HKC-M), ZIF-8 (HKC-Z), or Mino@ZIF-8 NPs (HKC-MZ). The concentrations of minocycline and ZIF-8 were consistent with those used in previous experiments.

Total RNA was extracted using the Total RNA Isolation Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using the PowerUp™ SYBR Green Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on a ViiA™ 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA).

The mRNA expression levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and MMP9, were quantified. In addition, NFKB1, an upstream transcriptional subunit of the NF-κB pathway identified through RNA-seq analysis, was also quantified to assess whether MN treatment modulates inflammation at a higher regulatory level. GAPDH was used as an internal reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Relative gene levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in triplicate.

In vivo anti-acne evaluation of MNs

To mimic the sebum-rich microenvironment of acne-prone skin, artificial sebum was prepared according to a previously reported formulation (17% fatty acids, 44.7% triglycerides, 25% jojoba oil, 12.4% squalene) [24] and stored at -20℃. Beginning five days prior to acne induction (day −5), 20 µL of artificial sebum was applied once daily to the shaved ICR mice dorsal skin and continued throughout the modeling and treatment periods, including during MN application. After a 7-day acclimation period and a 5-day preconditioning phase with daily artificial sebum, microneedle treatment was initiated when the animals were 8–10 weeks of age, which is consistent with commonly applied protocols for acne-like mouse models [25]. At this stage, the dorsal lesions exhibited characteristic epidermal thickening. The dissolving silk fibroin microneedles dissolved within minutes after insertion, resulting in only transient superficial penetration without persistent mechanical stress.

On days −2 and −1, mice received intradermal injections of C.acnes (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, 50µL) on the dorsal skin to establish an acne-like inflammatory model [12]. After model establishment, animals were randomly assigned into five groups (n = 6 per group): Model (untreated control), SF MN, ZIF-8 MN, Mino (topical minocycline solution) and Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN. For MN-treated groups, patches were applied directly to the inflamed sites and secured with medical adhesive tape. After complete dissolution within approximately 1 min based on in vivo observation, the residual patch base was gently removed. For the Mino group, 12 µg of minocycline solution was topically applied to the lesion site in a 30 µL volume once daily to match the drug dose delivered by the MNs. To ensure a clean pharmacological comparison, minocycline was administered as a simple aqueous solution without penetration-enhancing excipients, allowing the specific effects of microneedle-mediated delivery and ZIF-8 encapsulation to be evaluated without confounding formulation factors.

Treatments were administered on days 0, 2, and 4. Clinical photographs were captured at day 0, 2, 4, and 7, and lesion severity was evaluated based on diameter, eschar, and elevation scores [21, 26]. Scoring was performed independently by two blinded dermatologists.

On day 7, mice were sacrificed for histological and microbiological analyses. Dorsal skin tissues were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess histopathological alterations, including epidermal hyperplasia and dermal inflammatory infiltration.

For lipid analysis, frozen dorsal skin sections were subjected to Oil Red O staining to visualize neutral lipid accumulation in sebaceous glands. In parallel, BODIPY 493/503 staining was conducted on cryosections to label intracellular lipid droplets. Fluorescent images were acquired under identical exposure parameters using a confocal laser scanning microscope to ensure comparability across groups.

In vivo systemic toxicity evaluation

After completion of treatment, three mice from each group were randomly selected and sacrificed to evaluate systemic toxicity assessment. Whole blood samples were collected for routine hematological and serum biochemical analysis. Major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were harvested and stained with H&E for histopathological examination. Comprehensive assessment of these parameters was performed to determine the systemic biosafety of the MN treatments.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA). Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. For datasets meeting the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For ordinal or non-normally distributed data, Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test applied to evaluate intergroup differences. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Mino@ZIF-8 NPs

The morphology of the nanoparticles was examined by TEM and SEM. ZIF-8 NPs displayed a uniform rhombic dodecahedral structure with smooth surfaces and narrow size distribution of 50–90 nm, consistent with previously reported morphologies. In contrast, Mino@ZIF-8 NPs appeared slightly larger (60–130 nm) with irregular outlines and rougher surfaces, indicative of minocycline incorporation (Fig. 1A-C). Both types of NPs showed good monodispersity without detectable aggregation, indicating high colloidal stability under ambient conditions.

The crystalline structure of ZIF-8 was preserved after minocycline loading, as confirmed by XRD patterns. Both ZIF-8 and Mino@ZIF-8 displayed characteristic peaks at 7.4°, 10.3°, 12.9°, and 18.0°, corresponding to the (011), (002), (112), and (222) planes, respectively (Fig. 1D). FT-IR spectra were used to investigate the interaction between minocycline and ZIF-8 (Fig. 1E). Compared with pristine ZIF-8, the Mino@ZIF-8 spectrum exhibited a new absorption band at 3426 cm⁻¹ consistent with the O–H stretching of minocycline. Meanwhile, the characteristic C = O peak of ZIF-8 at 1678 cm⁻¹ disappeared, as did the typical C = O band of minocycline at 1652 cm⁻¹. The concurrent disappearance of these vibrational signals suggests coordination between the C = O oxygen of minocycline and the Zn²⁺ centers of ZIF-8, confirming successful incorporation of the drug within the MOF framework [27, 28].

The DLC was further quantified using UV-vis spectrophotometry. A standard curve established at 382 nm yielded the linear equation y = − 14.32586 + 65.58696x (R² = 0.9987). (Fig. 1F) Based on this curve, the EE and DLC were calculated to be approximately 70.2% and 40.11%, respectively, confirming efficient minocycline entrapment within the ZIF-8 matrix.

pH-responsive release behavior of Mino@ZIF-8 NPs

The pH-responsive release profile of Mino@ZIF-8 NPs was evaluated under mildly acidic (pH 5.5) and neutral (pH 7.4) conditions. Drug release occurred significantly faster at pH 5.5, with over 50% of minocycline released within 12 h, compared to less than 20% at pH 7.4 (Fig. 1G). This behavior is consistent with the well-recognized pH-dependent structural instability and biodegradability of ZIF-8, which maintains its framework under neutral conditions but undergoes protonation-induced weakening and partial disassembly of its imidazolate linkers in acidic environments, thereby promoting drug diffusion [29, 30].

Such acid-triggered release behavior aligns well with the pathological microenvironment of acne lesions. The normal skin surface typically exhibits an acidic pH ranging from 4.0 to 6.0, and C.acnes further lowers the local pH during metabolic activity and biofilm formation, with reported values as low as 4.85 [31]. Importantly, microneedle-based transdermal delivery studies have shown that ZIF-8 nanostructures undergo gradual biodegradation within cutaneous tissue under mildly acidic conditions, releasing Zn²⁺ as the framework collapses [32]. Together, these characteristics highlight the suitability of ZIF-8 as an acid-responsive and physiologically degradable nanocarrier for acne therapy. By remaining stable under physiological pH while rapidly disassembling within acidic lesions, Mino@ZIF-8 NPs enable targeted, microenvironment-responsive delivery of minocycline to sebaceous glands in a manner compatible with great biodegradability.

Biocompatibility and hemocompatibility of Mino@ZIF-8 NPs

To comprehensively assess the biosafety of Mino@ZIF-8 NPs, two representative cell lines were employed to model the skin and immune interfaces: HaCaT and THP-1. HaCaT cells are widely used to assess epidermal barrier cytotoxicity in dermatological studies [33], whereas THP-1 cells serve as a representative model of innate immune surveillance and are particularly suitable for early safety evaluation of nanomaterials due to their stable phenotype and sensitivity to inflammatory stimuli [34].

CCK-8 assays demonstrated that Mino@ZIF-8 exhibited negligible cytotoxicity toward both HaCaT and THP-1 cells at concentrations of 1–30 µg/mL, maintaining cell viabilities above 80% (Fig. 2B and C). A moderate decline in viability was observed at 40 and 50 µg/mL, indicating dose-dependent cytotoxicity at higher concentrations. Live/Dead staining further confirmed the excellent cytocompatibility, showing over 95% viable cells across all tested concentrations (Fig. 2A-C) and minimal evidence of membrane damage. Based on these results, 30 µg/mL was selected as the optimal working concentration for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments, balancing safety with therapeutic efficacy.

In addition, hemocompatibility was evaluated by a standard hemolysis assay using RBCs isolated from ICR mice. The hemolysis ratio of all groups remained below 1%, demonstrating excellent blood compatibility and negligible erythrocyte disruption. (Fig. 2D)

Collectively, these findings confirm that the developed Mino@ZIF-8 NPs possess favorable biocompatibility and hemocompatibility within the effective concentration range, supporting their suitability for further biological and therapeutic investigations.

Fabrication, morphology and dissolution behavior of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs

To achieve efficient and minimally invasive transdermal delivery of Mino@ZIF-8 NPs, a dissolvable SF MN patch was fabricated via a multi-step, vacuum-assisted mold-filling approach (Fig. 3F). The drug-loaded SF solution was repeatedly introduced into PDMS molds under vacuum to ensure complete filling of the needle cavities, followed by casting a 20% PVA backing layer to form the base substrate. This two-layered casting technique enabled the fabrication of mechanically robust and uniformly shaped MN arrays with precise spatial distribution of the drug-loaded compartment.

SEM imaging revealed well-defined conical MNs with sharp tips, each appoximately 600 μm in height and 250 μm in base diameter, consistent with the mold geometry (Fig. 3 A). The MNs were uniformly distributed across the array, exhibiting smooth surfaces without visible defects, thereby confirming the high structural fidelity and reproducibility of the fabrication process.

Time-lapse optical microscopy further demonstrated rapid dissolution of the MN tips upon exposure to aqueous medium, with progressive tip blunting observed within 40 s (Fig. 3B). This finding aligns with previous reports showing that SF-based MNs can completely dissolve within 60 s under physiological conditions [35]. Collectively, these results highlight that the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs combine structural robustness with rapid dissolution behavior, enabling reliable skin penetration and timely drug release.

Minocycline stability during nanoparticle encapsulation and microneedle fabrication

HPLC analysis was performed to determine whether the multi-step preparation of Mino@ZIF-8 and Mino@ZIF-8/SF microneedles caused chemical degradation of minocycline. All three chromatograms exhibited a single peak at ~ 5.6 min with no secondary peaks or shoulders (Fig. 3D).

Compared with the free-drug control, both the Mino@ZIF-8 and MNs retained approximately 90% of the original peak area, indicating that about 10% of minocycline was lost during the formulation process. Given the well-known photosensitivity of minocycline, this reduction is most plausibly attributed to unavoidable photodegradation during nanoparticle synthesis and solution handling, even though all steps were carried out under dark conditions and with minimized exposure time. The rapid coordination of minocycline into the ZIF-8 framework, together with subsequent handling in the dark, effectively limited the extent of photodegradation to this relatively small fraction. This is consistent with previous observations that minocycline can maintain most of its chemical integrity under dark or low-light conditions in dissolving microneedle formulations, even in the absence of MOF-based protection [36].

Notably, the peak area of the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN was similar to that of the Mino@ZIF-8 nanoparticles, indicating that the microneedle casting and drying steps did not introduce much additional degradation beyond that incurred during nanoparticle preparation. Together, these findings confirm that minocycline remains largely chemically intact throughout encapsulation and microneedle processing, and that the observed ~ 10% decrease mainly reflects controlled photodegradation during the early solution-phase steps.

In vitro insertion performance and skin tolerance of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs

The mechanical strength of MNs is critical for ensuring successful skin penetration without tip deformation or breakage. The force-displacement profile of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs was recorded using a texture analyzer. The applied force per needle increased progressively with displacement, reaching a peak failure force of approximately 0.4 N without any abrupt fracture before failure (Fig. 3C). Notably, this value surpassed the previously documented minimum threshold of 0.05 N required for efficient skin penetration [37]. Compared with blank SF MNs, the incorporation of Mino@ZIF-8 slightly enhanced fracture resistance, likely due to the mechanical reinforcement effect of the rigid nanoparticle network within the silk fibroin matrix.

Skin irritation and recovery were further evaluated following MN application. Immediately after insertion (0 min), mild erythema and micro-indentations were observed at the application site, consistent with prior studies showing that SF MNs can successfully pierce the stratum corneum and leave transient puncture marks [38]. These signs gradually subsided over time, with most visible redness fading within 10 min and the majority of the puncture marks substantially diminished by 30 min (Fig. 3E). To more comprehensively characterize the recovery process, extended monitoring at 1–24 h confirmed that the transient erythema and puncture marks progressively diminished and fully resolved within 24 h. No persistent irritation, edema or scarring was observed, indicating rapid skin recovery and good biocompatibility of the dissolving MNs (Supplementary Fig. S1).

In vitro antibacterial activity of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs

The antibacterial efficacy of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs was first evaluated against three representative acne-associated and opportunistic pathogens: C. acnes, E. coli, and S. aureus. Colony-counting assays revealed a clear dose-dependent inhibition across all three strains, with the antibacterial rate exceeding 95% for C. acnes at mino@ZIF-8 NPs dose of 30 µg/patch and above(Fig. 4A and D). Comparable inhibition levels were observed for E. coli and S. aureus, confirming the broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of the MN platform.

To elucidate the contribution of each component, the antibacterial effects of free minocycline, ZIF-8 NPs, and Mino@ZIF-8 NPs were compared at equivalent drug concentrations. Free minocycline exhibited potent inhibition against all tested strains, consistent with its established broad-spectrum antibiotic properties. ZIF-8 NPs also demonstrated measurable antibacterial activity, likely due to the release of Zn²⁺ ions that disrupt bacterial membranes. Notably, Mino@ZIF-8 achieved the highest inhibition rates across all strains, surpassing either component alone. This enhanced effect is attributed to a synergistic mechanism, in which minocycline inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30 S ribosomal subunit [39], while ZIF-8 induces membrane destabilization and oxidative stress through zinc ion release, thereby amplifying bacterial cell damage [40] (Fig. 4B and E).

SEM further further confirmed these findings at the morphological level. Untreated bacteria displayed intact and smooth surfaces, whereas those exposed to Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs exhibited severe membrane deformation, surface collapse, and cytoplasmic leakage (Fig. 4F). In C.acnes and E. coli, the characteristic rod-shaped structures were disrupted into fragmented debris, while S. aureus displayed roughened and wrinkled surfaces indicative of catastrophic cell wall damage.

Notably, at the mino@ZIF-8 dose of 30 µg per patch, the antibacterial effect of the Mino@ZIF-8 SF microneedles appeared slightly weaker than that of the free Mino@ZIF-8 nanoparticles. This difference is likely related to the exposure geometry of the plate-based assay rather than the intrinsic antibacterial activity of the microneedle system. After dissolution, the nanoparticles are released locally at the bottom of the well and must diffuse upward through the culture medium, whereas the free nanoparticle suspension is homogeneously dispersed from the outset and provides immediate and uniform bacterial contact. Previous studies have emphasized that the antibacterial performance of dissolving microneedles is strongly influenced by material composition, device geometry, and diffusion-controlled drug release rather than drug loading alone [41].

Collectively, these results demonstrate that Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs exert potent, dose-dependent, and broad-spectrum antibacterial activity through the combined actions of minocycline and ZIF-8. This broad-spectrum efficacy is particularly relevant for acne therapy, as C. acnes often resides within polymicrobial communities alongside other skin commensals. Dysbiosis within these communities has been associated with lesion persistence and exacerbation, and targeting both C. acnes and co-colonizing species may help suppress primary infection and reduce the risk of secondary colonization, thereby improving overall therapeutic outcomes [42, 43].

Anti-inflammatory effects of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs in C. acnes-stimulated sebocytes

To assess the anti-inflammatory efficacy of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs, SZ95 sebocytes were stimulated with heat-killed C. acnes (HKC) in the presence or absence of MN-derived extracts. Beyond their role in sebum production, sebocytes act as innate immune regulators that express pattern recognition receptors and produce cytokines and antimicrobial peptides. These mediators coordinate leukocyte recruitment and promote Th17 polarization, processes central to acne lesion inflammation [44].

Transcriptomic profiling revealed that HKC stimulation markedly upregulated a broad panel of pro-inflammatory mediators, including chemokines (e.g., CXCL1, CXCL8, CXCL10, CXCL11), cytokines (e.g., IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, IL-32), and transcriptional regulators (e.g., NFKB1, FOS) (Fig. 5A). These molecules are canonical downstream targets of C. acnes-activated NF-κB and MAPK signaling, both of which are well-established drivers of acne lesion inflammation. Treatment with Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs (HKC-MN) substantially reversed these transcriptional changes, suggesting broad immunomodulatory effects [45].

KEGG enrichment analysis further demonstrated that MN treatment suppressed multiple acne-relevant inflammatory pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, which orchestrates leukocyte recruitment and amplifies local cytokine cascades in acne lesions [46]; neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, which contributes to pustule development and histone-mediated tissue damage in inflammatory skin diseases [4, 47]; and IL-17 signaling, a Th17-driven axis sustaining chronic cutaneous inflammation and keratinocyte activation [48] (Fig. 5B). MN treatment significantly downregulated gene expression within these pathways, indicating a broad dampening of upstream immune activation and a consequent reduction in C. acnes-induced inflammatory output.

Consistent with the transcriptomic and pathway analyses, qPCR quantification showed that HKC stimulation triggered a robust upregulation of NFKB1 and inflammatory mediators including IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and MMP9 compared with unstimulated controls (Fig. 5C & D). All treatment groups attenuated this response. Notably, NFKB1 elevation was also partially normalized by MN treatment, indicating that the MN extracts modulate inflammation at an upstream regulatory level. Among all interventions, Mino@ZIF-8 (HKC-MZ) produced the most profound suppression, surpassing the effects free minocycline (HKC-M) or ZIF-8 alone (HKC-Z), thereby confirming a synergistic anti-inflammatory action mediated by the combined antibiotic and nanoparticle components.

Collectively, these findings identify Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs as an effective intervention for mitigating C.acnes-driven inflammation in sebocytes. By converging on multiple acne-relevant signaling axes — including NF-κB/MAPK, IL-17/JAK-STAT, and NET-associated pathways — this MN platform provides a mechanistic rationale for attenuating sebocyte-driven immune amplification. Given the central role of sebocytes in cutaneous immune homeostasis, such multi-nodal suppression may also hold therapeutic potential for other Th17- or NET-driven skin disorders such as rosacea, hidradenitis suppurativa, and psoriasis.

Possible mechanisms of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs in acne treatment

KEGG enrichment indicated that apoptosis, oxidative phosphorylation, and PPAR signaling pathways were significantly modulated by Mino@ZIF-8/SF treatment, suggesting a multifaceted mechanism underlying its therapeutic effects.

Among the enriched pathways, those related to cell survival and apoptosis are particularly relevant in the context of sebaceous gland regulation. Controlled apoptosis or growth arrest can reduce sebaceous gland size and limit sebum output, a process well exemplified by isotretinoin through p53- and TRAIL-dependent pathways [49]. Minocycline may partially converge on this axis by enhancing retinoid signaling and p53 activity, while related tetracyclines such as doxycycline have been shown to decrease SZ95 proliferation and promote differentiation [50]. Thus, Mino@ZIF-8/SF microneedles may, at least in part, act by engaging apoptosis-related or cell-cycle regulatory processes that normalize sebocyte turnover.

The enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation-related genes indicates that mitochondrial metabolism may also be affected by MN treatment. Mitochondrial homeostasis is essential for maintaining lipid balance and redox stability, and dysregulation of this process has been linked to lipid peroxidation and inflammation in acne [51]. Prior studies have shown that minocycline can stabilize mitochondrial function and attenuate oxidative stress [52], while ZIF-8 derived Zn²⁺ can support antioxidant defense and attenuate oxidative injury in cutaneous models [16]. These precedents raise the possibility that the MN system may influence mitochondrial pathways to restore metabolic equilibrium and reduce oxidative stress in acne lesions.

In addition, the enrichment of the PPAR signaling pathway underscores its potential role in mediating lipid metabolic regulation. PPARγ acts as a central regulator of sebocyte differentiation and lipogenesis, and dysregulation of this pathway is a hallmark of acne pathophysiology [53]. Previous studies reported that minocycline suppresses lipogenic genes such as SREBP1, FAS, and ACCα via inhibition of p300 activity [8], suggesting a plausible link between PPARγ modulation and reduced lipid synthesis.

Together, these findings suggest that Mino@ZIF-8/SF microneedles may affect multiple interconnected pathways, apoptotic regulation, mitochondrial metabolism, and lipid biosynthesis. thereby contributing to a more balanced sebocyte phenotype. While mechanistic validation is required, these transcriptomic insights provide a framework for understanding how MN treatment may orchestrate metabolic and functional reprogramming in acne therapy.

In vitro wound healing and cell migration assessment of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs

Scratch wound-healing assays were performed to evaluate the pro-regenerative effects of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN extracts on keratinocytes and fibroblasts. As shown in the representative images (Fig. 6A), HaCaT monolayers treated with Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN extracts exhibited markedly accelerated migration compared with the Control and SF MN groups. The scratch gap was nearly closed by 24 h in the Mino@ZIF/SF MN groups, whereas the other groups still displayed clear wound areas. In HSFs, differences were modest at 24 h but became evident by 48 h, when the Mino@ZIF-8/SF group resulted in substantially narrower wound regions. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 6B) confirmed these observations, closure rates in the Mino@ZIF-8/SF group were significantly higher at 12 and 24 h in HaCaT cells and at 48 h in HSFs (p < 0.05).

It is well established that scratch wound closure primarily reflects extrinsic, matrix-dependent keratinocyte migration, involving cytoskeletal remodeling, focal-adhesion dynamics, and cell-ECM interactions rather than intrinsic proliferative activity [54]. Clinically, efficient keratinocyte migration is essential for re-epithelialization and barrier restoration, and impaired migration has been associated with delayed healing and the development of atrophic acne scarring [55]. Consistent with these principles, each component of our formulation, including silk fibroin [56], minocycline [57], and zinc [58], has been independently reported to promote wound repair. Beyond accelerating wound closure, these activities are also known to support orderly tissue remodeling, thereby helping to reduce the risk of abnormal repair such as atrophic scarring.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs in a C. acnes-induced acne-like lesion mouse model

To assess the In vivo anti-acne potential of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs, a C. acnes-induced acne-like lesion mouse model was established in ICR mice via intradermal injection of C. acnes combined with topical artificial sebum application (Fig. 7A). This approach reproduces two key pathogenic components of human acne: the presence of C. acnes within the pilosebaceous unit and the lipid-rich microenvironment that supports bacterial growth and modulates the host inflammatory response. By integrating microbial and metabolic triggers, the model generates persistent perifollicular inflammation and papulopustular lesions that closely mimic the clinical morphology of human acne, thereby providing a physiologically relevant platform for testing localized therapeutic interventions [59].

Representative lesion images (Fig. 7B) revealed clear therapeutic distinctions among the treatment groups. Untreated model mice exhibited persistent erythema, swelling, and pustule formation throughout the 7-day observation period. In contrast, Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs markedly accelerated lesion resolution, with significant reductions in redness, elevation, and purulent content evident by day 4, with near-complete recovery by day 7. Minocycline solution and ZIF-8/SF MNs induced moderate improvement, partially reducing swelling and erythema but leaving residual pustules at the endpoint, whereas SF MNs produced only minimal improvement relative to the untreated controls. These outcomes underscore the necessity of nanoparticle loading and microneedle-mediated delivery for achieving optimal therapeutic efficacy.

Quantitative scoring using the Leeds revised acne grading system (Fig. 7C-E) corroborated the visual findings. Across all three morphological parameters (elevation, eschar, and diameter), the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN group consistently outperformed all comparators. In elevation scores, the untreated model group displayed sustained swelling until day 7, whereas Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs elicited a rapid and sustained decline, indicating early suppression of inflammatory edema. For eschar scores, only the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN group achieved near-complete clearance by day 7, reflecting accelerated re-epithelialization and surface healing. Regarding diameter, lesion size in all groups peaked between days 2 and 4 before gradually decreasing; however, Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs induced the steepest contraction, suggesting effective limitation of inflammatory spread through coordinated antibacterial and anti-inflammatory actions. These results align with previous reports of antibiotic-loaded microneedles such as clindamycin MNs [32].

These multidimensional improvements — attenuation of edema, acceleration of epidermal recovery, and limitation of lesion expansion— mirror the in vitro findings of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs, including potent anti-inflammatory effects, antibacterial, and wound-healing effects. Together, they highlight the capacity of this microneedle platform to address the multifactorial pathology of acne in a localized, minimally invasive, and mechanistically integrated manner. Although the present study focused on mechanistic evaluation rather than benchmarking against commercial topical formulations, future work directly comparing this microneedle-nanocarrier platform with clinically approved minocycline gels or foams may provide additional translational insight.

Histological and lipid staining analysis of dorsal skin

H&E staining was first performed to evaluate epidermal remodeling and inflammatory infiltration. In the untreated model group, skin sections showed pronounced epidermal hyperplasia, disorganized architecture and dense dermal inflammatory cell infiltration. Considering that the normal murine dorsal epidermis measures approximately 15 μm in thickness [60], such marked thickening reflects substantial pathological remodeling. Treatment with SF MN or ZIF‑8/SF MN offered little improvement, while topical minocycline moderately reduced epidermal thickening and inflammatory infiltration. In sharp contrast, Mino@ZIF‑8/SF MNs restored epidermal organization and markedly decreased inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 8A). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the epidermal thickness in this group was significantly lower than in all others, demonstrating that MN-mediated delivery of minocycline effectively normalizes epidermal architecture while suppressing local inflammation (Fig. 8B).

To determine whether sebocyte lipid metabolism was also affected, BODIPY staining was performed to visualize intracellular neutral lipid droplets [61]. The model group exhibited intense green fluorescence throughtout the sebaceous lobules, indicative of abundant lipid droplet accumulation. SF MNs and ZIF‑8/SF MNs produced little change, whereas topical minocycline led to a moderate reduction in fluorescence intensity. In contrast, Mino@ZIF‑8/SF MN treatment significantly diminished BODIPY signals (Fig. 8A). Quantitative analysis corroborated that BODIPY fluorescence intensity was lowest in the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN group (Fig. 8B), confirming effective suppression of sebocyte lipogenesis beyond that achieved by free drug or blank MNs.

Oil Red O staining was subsequently performed to visualize neutral triglycerides and cholesteryl esters [62]. Sections from the untreated model group displayed extensive red‑positive areas within sebaceous glands, consistent with excessive neutral lipid accumulation. SF MNs and ZIF‑8/SF MNs again had minimal impact, and topical minocycline induced only partial lipid reduction. Strikingly, Mino@ZIF‑8/SF MNs led to the most substantial decrease, with sebaceous lobules exhibiting markedly diminished Oil Red O positivity (Fig. 8A). Quantitative image analysis confirmed a significant reduction relative to all other groups (Fig. 8B).

The collective histological and lipid staining results provide mechanistic insight into how Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs exert their therapeutic effects. Beyond direct antibacterial activity against C. acnes, minocycline has shown the ability to reduce sebum output [8]. The current findings, supported by transcriptomic analyses, suggest that the microneedle platform further modulates sebocyte metabolism through coordinated regulation of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, apoptosis, and PPARγ-mediated lipogenesis [63]. Localized and sustained delivery of Mino@ZIF-8 within the pilosebaceous unit enables simultaneous suppression of bacterial growth, attenuation of inflammation, and rebalancing of lipid synthesis. The resulting normalization of epidermal thickness and substantial reduction in sebaceous lipid accumulation highlight the dual structural and metabolic benefits of this system. Together, these findings establish a mechanistically integrated strategy in which antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic reprogramming effects converge through microneedle-based delivery to achieve comprehensive treatment of acne lesions.

In vivo biosafety assessment of Mino@ZIF-8/SF MNs

To evaluate the In vivo biosafety of the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN platform, histopathological analysis of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were performed after the 7-day treatment course. H&E staining revealed no signs of structural damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, hemorrhage, or necrosis in any group, including the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN-treated mice (Fig. 9A). The preservation of normal tissue morphology across all samples indicates that neither the silk fibroin matrix nor the incorporated ZIF-8 nanocarriers and minocycline payload induced detectable systemic toxicity.

Hematological and serum biochemical parameters further substantiated these findings (Fig. 9B). White blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and platelet count (PLT) counts remained within normal physiological ranges, with no statistically significant differences compared to untreated controls. Similarly, serum markers of hepatic function alanine aminotransferase(ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), renal function creatinine (CREA) and uric acid (UA) showed no abnormal changes.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that the Mino@ZIF-8/SF MN system achieves therapeutic efficacy in acne-like lesions without compromising systemic safety, supporting its translational potential for clinical dermatologic applications.

Conclusion

In this study, we designed a pH-responsive MN system based on MOFs to achieve painless transdermal delivery of minocycline. The fabricated drug-loaded microneedles (Mino@ZIF-8 MNs) exhibited a well-defined conical morphology, effectively penetrated the stratum corneum, and dissolved rapidly. This MN system demonstrated high drug-loading capacity, excellent biocompatibility, and pronounced pH-responsive drug release. The silk fibroin matrix facilitated the migration of human immortalized human keratinocytes (HaCaT) and fibroblasts, thereby promoting skin repair. Simultaneously, the incorporated Mino@ZIF-8 not only demonstrates potent antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activites but also possesses the capability to regulate sebaceous gland functions from multiple perspectives. Specifically, Mino@ZIF-8 exert therapeutic effects on acne by targeting its pathological core through multiple pathways, including inducing apoptosis of sebaceous gland cells, regulating the oxidative phosphorylation process, and inhibiting lipid synthesis, thereby reshaping the lipid metabolism of sebaceous glands. Moreover, we successfully established a mouse acne model to further validate the antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and lipid regulation mechanisms of the microneedles in vivo. The experimental results confirmed that Mino@ZIF-8 MNs markedly alleviated inflammation and promoted the repair of acne lesions. In summary, the Mino@ZIF-8 MNs developed in this study exhibited outstanding antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activities both in vitro and in vivo, while also regulating lipogenesis, underscoring their great potential as a promising therapeutic strategy for acne treatment.
