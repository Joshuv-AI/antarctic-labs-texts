Navigating digital resources in dental education: a cross-sectional comparison of the educational quality of rubber dam videos on Youtube and Instagram
Authors: Eray Ceylanoglu, Dilek Hancerliogullari, Bengisu Yuksel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundApplying a rubber dam (RD) is fundamental in both endodontic and restorative procedures to ensure a sterile working area, achieve optimal moisture control, and minimize microbial contamination. While platforms like YouTube and Instagram have become go-to resources for dental education, the actual quality of their content needs closer inspection. Therefore, this study sets out to assess how accurate and reliable the information on RD application techniques is across YouTube videos and Instagram Reels.MethodsSystematic searches for rubber dam application content were conducted on both platforms on January 18, 2026. After filtering, 39 relevant videos from each source were included. Three independent endodontists evaluated these videos using the Global Quality Scale (GQS), JAMA benchmarking criteria, and the modified Patient Educational Materials Evaluation Tool (mPEMAT). For statistical evaluation, Fleiss Kappa was used to check inter-rater reliability, Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare platforms, and Spearman correlation analysis (p < 0.05) was used.ResultsA strong consensus was observed among the observers; inter-rater reliability across all measures fell within the “good” to “excellent” categories (p < 0.001). YouTube significantly outperformed Instagram Reels, scoring higher on the GQS, JAMA, and mPEMAT scales (p < 0.001 for all). Content uploaded by dentists received the highest scores, regardless of the platform. Longer videos tended to have higher GQS and mPEMAT scores on both platforms.ConclusionIn this limited cross-sectional study, YouTube videos exhibited higher instructional quality and reliability scores compared to Instagram Reels. Rather than establishing an absolute superiority of one platform over the other, it appears that YouTube currently provides a more comprehensive learning resource.Keywords: Instagram, YouTube, Social media, Rubber dam application, Education

Introduction

Rubber dam (RD) application, introduced by Barnum in 1864, protects against instrument aspiration, reduces microbial contamination, and provides an operating area under sterile conditions [1]. While the necessity of using RDs for treatment that meets gold standards in endodontics is clearly evident, it is equally critical in restorative dentistry for achieving strict moisture control and ensuring the long-term success of adhesive protocols [2, 3]. This practice not only enhances clinical success but also forms the foundation of preventive care by ensuring patient safety during dental procedures [4]. The clinical importance and application of RDs in dental undergraduate education are taught in terms of their contributions to visibility, accessibility, isolation, and retraction [5]. Textbooks, brochures, presentations, and other text- and still image-based materials may be insufficient for conveying the rationale for practical procedures to students who receive training in laboratory applications prior to clinical treatment [6].

Digitalization has increased the popularity of social media, making it the preferred choice of users in various fields, including education [7]. Instagram stands out among popular social media platforms in terms of the user growth rate, with the number of users estimated to reach 1.5 billion by 2026 [8, 9]. The video-sharing network YouTube has made rapid progress since 2005, reaching over 2 billion daily views by the end of 2005 and doubling that number after October 2009. Given their popularity and accessibility, YouTube videos and Instagram Reels can be integrated into modern teaching frameworks to enhance learning; however, a critical analysis of their digital content quality remains essential [10, 11].

Despite the growing literature evaluating digital platforms in dental education, there is a significant research gap regarding the multidimensional assessment of these visual resources. While previous studies have largely focused on general information quality, few have delved deeply into the concepts of ‘understandability’ and ‘applicability’ from a procedural learning perspective. To address this gap in the literature, the present study aims to comprehensively assess these educational videos by combining established reliability tools (GQS and JAMA) with the modified PEMAT (mPEMAT) scale. The null hypothesis (H0) of this study is that there is no significant difference in terms of the quality, reliability, comprehensibility, and applicability scores of rubber dam application videos between YouTube and Instagram platforms and between different sharing sources.

Methods

Data collection

To ensure high reproducibility of the methodology, the search strategy and parameters were precisely defined. To eliminate temporal variations arising from the dynamic nature of social media algorithms and to capture a real-time snapshot, the data collection process was executed as a single cross-sectional iteration on January 18, 2026. To minimize selection bias from personalized algorithms, YouTube searches were performed in an unauthenticated session using only the search string “rubber dam application,” after browser history and cookies were completely cleared [12]. Due to Instagram’s closed architecture, which prohibits unauthenticated searches, a fake account with no prior search or viewing history was used, and the search was strictly limited to the English hashtags #rubberdam and #rubberdamapplication. Furthermore, to prevent any algorithmic discrepancies caused by different IP addresses, device-specific configurations, or geographic tracking, all searches across both platforms were executed consecutively from a single computer (Windows 11 Home Single Language OS, Google Chrome browser) located in Kirikkale, Turkey. No additional filters were applied, allowing the platforms’ native algorithms to naturally rank visibility [13].

Initially, a predetermined sample size of approximately 300 videos was planned, aiming for 85% statistical power to detect an effect size of 0.35. Videos listed in the search results were evaluated sequentially, starting from the top. However, a methodological limitation emerged on Instagram; content under the relevant hashtags became completely irrelevant after the 100th video. Due to this practical limitation, data collection was stopped; to ensure statistical balance and comparative fairness, the top 100 videos with the highest visibility from each platform were selected for screening.

From this pool, videos were excluded if they did not directly demonstrate clinical rubber dam application, lacked clear English audio, subtitles, or text descriptions, or contained repetitive content. Conversely, videos were strictly included in the final analysis only if they were in English and clearly demonstrated key clinical steps specific to rubber dam application, such as equipment introduction, dam perforation, single- or two-stage placement techniques, inversion, flossing, and safe removal procedures.

In the sample selection process, after applying exclusion criteria, exactly 39 videos from the top 100 videos screened on Instagram were found suitable for analysis. Although a significantly larger data pool was available on YouTube, the sample size was limited to match the Instagram data to ensure statistical balance, robustness against potential heterogeneity of variance, and comparative fairness in the cross-platform analysis [14]. Accordingly, the top 39 videos that ranked highest in YouTube search results and met the study criteria were identified and included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the video selection and screening process

Reels and videos were examined in terms of duration, likes, comments, reposts, days since initial posting, and views. The number of people followed by the sharing sources, their followers, and the number of posts and videos they shared were recorded. The view rates of the videos were calculated as views/days since upload × 100 [15].

Categorization of sharing sources

Content creators have been divided into four main categories based on their professional qualifications: dentists; health-related businesses (dental equipment manufacturers, continuing education platforms, and corporate healthcare agencies, etc.); private hospitals/university hospitals/clinics; and others (dental supply stores, commercial websites, or individual users) [15]. This classification was created to analyze the differences in expertise levels between institutions and individuals regarding information shared about rubber dam applications.

Measuring video quality

The reviewers evaluated the educational quality by focusing on how thoroughly and accurately the content creators executed the clinical sequence previously defined in the inclusion criteria.

Video quality was assessed by three endodontists specializing in the field via the Global Quality Scale (GQS), the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria, and the modified Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (mPEMAT).

The GQS consists of five elements that define the quality, accuracy, and usefulness of the content [16]. Videos were scored from a minimum of 1 to a maximum of 5 on the basis of their quality (Table 1).

PEMAT ensures that written, visual, and audio materials are evaluated in terms of comprehensibility and actionability. It demonstrates how well the material enables consumers with diverse backgrounds and varying literacy levels to process and explain the core messages and understand what they can do with the information provided [18]. In this study, we used the visual and auditory material comprehensibility table of the modified PEMAT prepared by the Minnesota Center of Excellence in Newcomer Health and Nationalities Service Center [19]. This study aimed to evaluate the quality of information accessible to dental students on video sharing platforms; therefore, the items were interpreted in a way that would evaluate material quality from the students’ perspective. The relevant items received scores between 0 and 1, whereas irrelevant items 5, 6, 9, and 10 were not included in the scoring. Eight of the 12 items were scored. The score was calculated as “(total score / maximum possible score) × 100” ((total score / 8) × 100) (Table 3).

Statistical analysis

The normality of the variables was examined via the Shapiro-Wilk test, which revealed that the data did not meet the parametric assumptions. Accordingly, the Mann-Whitney U test, a nonparametric method, was used to evaluate the differences between two independent groups. The direction and strength of the relationships between the variables were determined via Spearman correlation analysis (rho). The correlation coefficient (r) was classified as follows: 0.00–0.19 very weak, 0.20–0.39 weak, 0.40–0.59 moderate, 0.60–0.79 strong, and 0.80–1.00 very strong. The Fleiss’ Kappa coefficient was calculated to determine the level of interrater agreement. To interpreting the kappa values, the following criteria were used: ≤0.20 is poor, 0.21–0.40 is fair, 0.41–0.60 is moderate, 0.61–0.80 is good, and 0.81–1.00 is excellent agreement. For all analyses, the statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05. To control for Type I error inflation (false positives) arising from the simultaneous evaluation of three distinct dependent variable scales (GQS, JAMA, mPEMAT), a Bonferroni correction was applied, adjusting the statistical significance threshold to p < 0.016 (0.05 / 3).

The GQS, JAMA, and mPEMAT scores of the videos were evaluated blindly by three independent endodontists. Inter-rater reliability analysis revealed statistically significant Fleiss Kappa coefficients for all scales (p < 0.001), exhibiting values within the ‘good’ to ‘excellent’ agreement range (Table 5). However, while there was complete absolute agreement between observers for the JAMA scores on the Instagram platform (p = 1.000), the corresponding p-value remained above the significance threshold (p = 0.075). This statistically significant situation is explained by the ‘prevalence paradox,’ which is also influenced by the relatively small sample size (n = 39). In cases where a single score is excessively dense and there are a limited number of observations, the Kappa statistic may not reach its traditional level of significance because it becomes mathematically difficult to distinguish perfect agreement from random agreement [20]. Based on this high level of consistency achieved among observers, final scores determined by the consensus of experts were used instead of individual scores in the later stages of the analysis.

Discussion

Recent technological developments have led to a digital transformation in educational materials, with innovative approaches replacing traditional methods. Video sharing platforms are considered a primary source of information among the younger generation because of their ability to provide quick and easy access to knowledge. Social media is widely used and viewed as a material that can support education by dental students [21]. Digital 2026: Turkiye data show that YouTube ranks first in user preference in terms of information and entertainment-based content, while Instagram ranks first for visual interaction-focused sharing [22]. This study aimed to evaluate the quality and scientific accuracy of videos shared on these platforms and containing content related to “rubber dam application” via objective criteria.

GQS, which is used in the evaluation of health-related websites, has recently become one of the methods used to evaluate the quality of digital content on online platforms [16, 23]. While the GQS generally evaluates content quality, the JAMA assesses reliability and transparency (authorship, citations, recency, etc.) [24]. The PEMAT is a tool developed to evaluate patient education materials [25]. However, the “Rubber Dam application,” which is the focus of the current study, is a clinical procedure performed directly by a dentist. In this context, the PEMAT scale could be adapted to this group to determine the levels of ‘understandability’ and ‘applicability’ of the videos used by dental students in their clinical training process, and the scale was included in the study protocol.

Educating individuals who lack medical terminology and specific field knowledge and ensuring that knowledge is translated into practice is a complex process. PEMAT is a critical tool that provides evidence-based data for the evaluation and development of such materials [26–28]. In this study, the ability of the PEMAT criteria to simplify complex procedures was simulated through the ‘learner’ role of dental students who have not yet achieved clinical competence. Although videos on platforms receive high scores for comprehensibility, the lack of full competence persists because of low information quality and reliability [29, 30]. Chen et al., who compared different platforms, reported that YouTube had a higher PEMAT score than other platforms did [31]. Studies evaluating Instagram alone reported high PEMAT comprehensibility scores, whereas other studies comparing it with other platforms reported that YouTube lagged behind [32–34]. Our results revealed that YouTube and Instagram had lower scores than did compared to similar studies in the literature; however, the better performance of YouTube compared with Instagram is consistent with the literature. On Instagram, the fast-paced delivery of information in short Reels makes comprehension difficult, particularly because the video durations are significantly shorter than the actual clinical time required for rubber dam application. Insufficient video duration may restrict the comprehensive demonstration of the procedure, potentially affecting both comprehensibility and actionability.

Videos on dentistry topics have generally performed poorly in studies evaluating information quality and accuracy, with low GQS and JAMA scores on Instagram and YouTube [35–39]. The findings of our current study align with this trend of low performance in the literature, specifically with respect to Instagram Reels content; however, YouTube videos demonstrated higher success in GQS scoring than did previous studies [15, 40]. In this study, there was also a difference between platforms, with YouTube videos achieving the highest value in both scores. The higher GQS and JAMA scores on YouTube than those reported in studies in the literature on dental topics can be attributed to the adequacy of the sharing sources regarding “rubber dam application” and the improvement in the quality of information by sharing sources to attract the attention of the audience each year [41, 42]. The lack of positive increases across all the scores examined on Instagram can be attributed to the platform being used more for social interaction than information sharing, and to target audiences tending to consume real-life videos for quick visual engagement rather than in-depth academic learning. Consequently, it is crucial to acknowledge that these score disparities were likely influenced by unmeasured variables, such as video production quality and the presenter’s communication skills. Furthermore, since the videos evaluated were selected based on search visibility rather than random sampling, platform-specific algorithmic biases undoubtedly shaped the sampling. Therefore, these findings reflect a broader digital ecosystem where algorithms, creator intent, and audience expectations collectively determine the educational value of medical content.

Video sharing platforms offer a wide range of content across various disciplines owing to their heterogeneous uploader structure. Recent studies evaluating health videos on different platforms have shown that quality scores and reliability are higher for content shared by experts in the field or academic institutions [39, 43, 44]. One of the notable findings of this study is that videos uploaded by individual dentists received higher quality scores than those from academic sources. This is thought to be due to a significant disparity in sample sizes between the two sources of content sharing. The fact that content produced by individual dentists is far more numerous than that from academic institutions naturally increases the likelihood of encountering high-quality videos within this larger pool.

In the literature, studies have reported that YouTube video GQS scores increase in direct proportion to duration [24, 45]. Rachmawati et al. reported that video quality scores for videos longer than 6 min were higher than those for videos shorter than 6 min [46]. The findings of this study are consistent with these data; both the GQS and PEMAT scores are positively correlated with video duration on both platforms. The number of studies evaluating the correlation between video characteristics and Instagram quality scores is limited, with some studies reporting a positive correlation and others reporting no correlation [15, 47]. Video duration appears to be a strongly associated factor with information quality in terms of conveying technical details and content depth. In this study, Instagram Reels videos were found to last a minimum of 2 s, whereas this duration was significantly greater for YouTube videos. The long video format not only increases the sharing of theoretical knowledge but also provides advantages in terms of the detailed demonstration of different clinical techniques and the capacity to guide the viewer. Therefore, the lower GQS scores on Instagram are likely intertwined with the severe time constraints inherent in the Reels format.

The main limitation of this study is the limited sample size (n = 39 per platform). Because completely irrelevant content was obtained after the first 100 search results on Instagram, only 39 videos met the inclusion criteria. To maintain statistical balance and ensure a fair comparison of high-visibility content, the YouTube sample was matched to 39. Consequently, while this allows for direct comparison of top-ranking results, our findings do not fully represent the large number of educational videos found on YouTube. Furthermore, the scope was limited to YouTube and Instagram; another methodological limitation is that the modified PEMAT (mPEMAT) scale used in the assessment has not undergone formal validation in the field of professional clinical education.

Conclusion

The findings of this cross-sectional study indicate that YouTube videos exhibit higher scores in terms of overall quality, reliability, and comprehensibility compared to Instagram Reels. However, due to the substantial variability in content quality across both platforms, social media should be viewed as a supplementary tool rather than a definitive guide. To safely harness the potential of digital learning, it is essential for academic institutions to foster critical evaluation skills among dental students and for experts to actively contribute evidence-based clinical resources to these platforms.
