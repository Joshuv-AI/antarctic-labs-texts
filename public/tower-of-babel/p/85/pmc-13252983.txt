Contributions of testosterone to excessive alcohol drinking in males: Potential role for interactions with the HPA axis
Authors: Roman A. Zegarelli, Anna K. Radke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Excessive alcohol drinking and alcohol use disorders (AUDs) are a significant and increasing public health concern in the United States and worldwide, with men historically having higher rates of alcohol consumption and AUD diagnoses. Despite this, the biological mechanisms underlying sex differences in these outcomes are not fully understood. Endocrine systems are critical regulators of behavior and testosterone has a bidirectional relationship with alcohol in males. Baseline levels of testosterone (T) may bias males toward approach behaviors that increase the tendency to engage in risky drinking behaviors in adolescent and adult men. At the same time, T appears to protect males (particularly rodents) against elevated levels of initial alcohol consumption. T levels also change with alcohol exposure, decreasing with acute and chronic use. This review synthesizes findings from both human and animal studies, highlighting how interactions between the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axes may underlie male-specific patterns of alcohol use and risk for AUD. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for preclinical and clinical researchers conducting research in males and for the development of more effective interventions for AUD in men.

Excessive alcohol drinking and alcohol use disorders (AUD) are a severe problem in the United States of America and have only been increasing in frequency within recent years (Grant et al., 2017). This is exemplified by a 2020–2021 report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in which over 178,000 deaths were attributed to excessive alcohol use (CDC, 2024). Although women may be at greater risk for the negative consequences of alcohol use, men are more likely to engage in binge drinking and heavy drinking and to meet criteria for AUD (NSDUH Detailed Tables, 2023). This influence of sex and gender on alcohol use is likely due to a combination of biological and social factors. Furthermore, known sex differences in the development and maintenance of AUD complicate research into the underlying mechanisms, thereby making it more challenging to develop effective treatment options (Flores-Bonilla & Richardson, 2020). This is especially apparent when considering the limited number of drugs approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for AUD, as well as their limited efficacy (Leggio et al., 2020).

One potential mechanism contributing to sex differences in AUD are gonadal hormones. Ovarian hormones, for example, are known to facilitate alcohol consumption in animals (Arnold et al., 2023; Carroll & Anker, 2010; Finn, 2020; Satta et al., 2018; Sneddon et al., 2023a). In males, testosterone may have a critical function. Many studies have demonstrated that testosterone levels decrease in a dose-dependent manner in men and male rodents following chronic alcohol consumption. However, less attention has been given to the implications of low testosterone on subsequent alcohol consumption behaviors. Because the effects of ovarian hormones in females have already been extensively reviewed (Carroll & Anker, 2010; Finn, 2020), this paper will examine the available evidence for testosterone’s effects on alcohol drinking in males and potential underlying mechanisms, including interactions with the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis that may contribute to a cycle of continued alcohol consumption. Considering that a range of factors, including age, circadian rhythms, social factors, stress, and physical activity influence testosterone levels, understanding the bidirectional relationship between this hormone and alcohol is important for all alcohol research using male rodents or humans.

Excessive alcohol drinking: definitions and models

Excessive drinking includes any consumption of alcohol that increases the risk of illness, injury, chronic disease, or social, emotional, or mental health challenges (CDC, 2024). In men, binge drinking, heavy drinking, and underage drinking are all included in this definition. Binge drinking is defined differently across species and experimental contexts, but for men it is typically characterized as consuming five or more drinks in one sitting (Wechsler & Austin, 1998). The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) defines binge drinking as a pattern of drinking resulting in blood ethanol concentrations of 0.08 % (80 mg/dL) or greater (Understanding Binge Drinking). Heavy drinking occurs when men consume 15 or more drinks per week. Alcohol is metabolized relatively quickly within humans and rodents in the liver, where it is converted to acetaldehyde by the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase (Holmes, 1994). When drinking is excessive, alcohol clearance is overwhelmed and alcohol and its metabolites accumulate throughout the brain and peripheral tissues, causing multiple deleterious effects and increasing the risk of developing AUD (Carr et al., 2024; Li, 2008). With higher rates of alcohol use, these negative consequences are exacerbated in men. For example, roughly 3 million deaths were attributed to excessive alcohol drinking worldwide, with 2.3 million deaths for men and 0.7 million deaths for women (White, 2020).

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5) lists eleven criteria for AUD, where the presence of two to three of the symptoms is considered mild AUD, four to five symptoms is considered moderate AUD, and having six or more symptoms is considered severe AUD. These symptoms include drinking more or longer than intended, having trouble cutting down on drinking, continuing to drink despite negative consequences, a preoccupation with drinking, and dependence and withdrawal (Association & Others, 2013). Leading researchers in the field further define the cycle of addiction to include binge/intoxication, withdrawal/negative affect, and preoccupation/anticipation (e.g., cravings) (Becker & Koob, 2016; Koob & Volkow, 2010). A core feature of the DSM-5 criteria and addictive behavior is continuing to drink despite negative consequences, which is often termed “compulsive” or “aversion-resistant” drinking (Garrison et al., 2025; George et al., 2022; Hopf & Lesscher, 2014; Koob & Volkow, 2010; Radke, Sneddon, & Monroe, 2021).

The neural mechanisms that contribute to the development of addictive behavior, including aversion-resistant alcohol drinking have been extensively reviewed (Barker et al., 2015; Domi et al., 2021; Gilpin & Koob, 2008; Koob & Volkow, 2010; Radke, Sneddon, Frasier, & Hopf, 2021). The involvement of key neural systems such as the motivation and reward systems, prefrontal control systems, and hypothalamic and extrahypothalamic stress circuits shifts as dependence develops. Alcohol-induced release of endogenous opioids and mesolimbic dopamine is important for the positive reinforcing actions of alcohol, particularly early in drug use. Over time and as alcohol use becomes excessive, drinking shifts from a goal-directed behavior reliant on the ventral striatum and subject to prefrontal regulation to an uncontrolled behavior that recruits the dorsal striatum. There is also a profound dysregulation of stress circuits, including changes in neuropeptides such as CRH and NPY in the amygdala, that are thought to contribute to negative reinforcement in the later stages of addiction. Together, these brain-wide changes produce the constellation of symptoms that characterize AUD.

Lab-based studies of alcohol effects in humans

While epidemiological and field studies are important for assessing drinking patterns and motivations, an understanding of how biological mechanisms contribute to alcohol drinking and its effects require a more controlled setting. To make the experiment as naturalistic as possible, lab-based studies in humans often involve voluntary drinking in a bar-like setting, and there is evidence to suggest that these efforts are largely successful (DeMartini et al., 2020). These studies generally involve mixed drinks (alcohol with soda or juice) or a placebo that is soda or juice alone and is sprayed with ethanol (EtOH) to mimic the scent (Patchev & Patchev, 2006; Sarkola & Eriksson, 2003; Vatsalya et al., 2012). Other studies using human participants use catheters inserted into the arm of participants to control the amount of EtOH administered while breath-alcohol concentrations (BrAC) are measured (Plawecki et al., 2022). These studies often involve concurrent surveys assessing how participants feel during EtOH administration, and AUD histories (defined as alcoholism in older reports) are often collected as well (Plawecki et al., 2022). These types of studies have provided foundational information about how alcohol affects endocrine systems, including testosterone levels, in men. Associations between testosterone levels and alcohol use have also been reported and will be reviewed below.

Modeling excessive alcohol drinking in rodents

Studies using animal models have provided important insights into how biological factors such as hormones are involved in alcohol consumption and the development of addiction-like behavior. Rodents are the most common model organism used in preclinical studies, although non-human primates are also used and can be especially informative due to their tendency to consume alcohol in a manner similar to humans. Rats and mice, on the other hand, consume alcohol in much lower quantities. For this reason, selectively-bred lines may be used. In mice, the C57BL/6J line is known to prefer rewarding substances and will generally consume high levels of EtOH ad libitum (Bagley et al., 2021; Lewis et al., 2005; Sneddon et al., 2019). High alcohol preferring (HAP) and high Drinking in the Dark (HDID) lines of mice are also commonly used (Crabbe et al., 2022; Grahame et al., 1999; Savarese et al., 2022; Sneddon, Schuh, et al., 2022). In rats, many studies have been done in the alcohol preferring P and AA lines (Moore & Lynch, 2015; Timme et al., 2019).

No singular drinking paradigm can fully encompass the entire cycle of addiction or all eleven of the DSM-5 criteria for AUD. Therefore, drinking paradigms have been carefully developed over time to model specific alcohol drinking patterns and symptoms of AUD. Home-cage drinking paradigms are commonly used in order to assess binge-like, withdrawal precipitated, and continuous access drinking. Binge-like drinking is commonly modeled with the limited access “Drinking in the Dark” (DiD) paradigm, where rodents receive 15–20 % EtOH for 2–4 h during the dark cycle (Crabbe et al., 2022; Lei et al., 2020; Lesscher et al., 2010; Sneddon et al., 2019; Thiele & Navarro, 2014). This typically yields blood ethanol concentrations (BECs) near 150 mg/dL, above the previously defined 80 mg/dL “binge” threshold (Thiele & Navarro, 2014). A two-bottle choice (2BC) variation, with water available alongside EtOH, allows measurement of preference and water intake (Lesscher et al., 2010; Sneddon et al., 2019). Other widely used 2BC models include intermittent access (IA), where EtOH is provided for 24 h every other day, capturing escalation after abstinence and withdrawal (Hwa et al., 2011; Kimbrough et al., 2017), and continuous access (CA), where constant EtOH/water availability generally produces lower intake and preference (Files et al., 1997; Kimbrough et al., 2017; Lopez & Becker, 2005; Samson et al., 1992). Operant response tasks extend these models by requiring lever presses or nose pokes for EtOH, with response requirements manipulated to assess alcohol seeking and motivation (Augier et al., 2018; Bluitt et al., 2025; Radke et al., 2017; Sneddon et al., 2020). In any of these tasks, EtOH consumption or responding can be punished with aversive stimuli such as quinine, foot shocks, or air puffs. Induction of such “aversion-resistant” drinking models DSM-V AUD criteria (symptoms 5–9).

Passive methods of exposure are useful for modeling heavy alcohol use, dependence, and withdrawal. Chronic intermittent EtOH (CIE) vapor exposure maintains BECs at 150–250 mg/dL over extended periods and is often combined with home-cage access to escalate intake (Forquer et al., 2011; Griffin et al., 2014; Kimbrough et al., 2017; Lopez & Becker, 2005). Liquid diets, in which calories are partially replaced by EtOH, provide another route for chronic administration (Cicero & Badger, 1977a; Cicero, Greenwald, et al., 1990; Gordon et al., 1978; Rasmussen et al., 2003; Tredger et al., 1984). Additional methods include EtOH injections, intragastric inutbation, or cannulas are also used to study the effects of passive exposure on tissue, hormone or EtOH levels, and behavior (Keyes et al., 2019, 2021; Mello et al., 1985; Slade et al., 2016; Stelander et al., 2021; Tilbrook & Clarke, 2001; Tredger et al., 1984; Wilsnack et al., 2018; Winslow et al., 1988). Similar chronic methods also escalate intake in non-human primates (Mello et al., 1985; Tilbrook & Clarke, 2001; Winslow et al., 1988).

Factors driving sex differences in alcohol use

Data from both humans and animal models point toward clear sex differences in patterns and levels of alcohol drinking. For example, rates of binge drinking are consistently higher in men vs. women across age groups and cultures (Wilsnack et al., 2018). Historically, men have consumed alcohol at higher rates and received more diagnoses of AUD than women. However, in recent years, this gender gap has closed as rates of alcohol drinking have increased in women in the United States (Grant et al., 2017; Keyes et al., 2019; White, 2020) and elsewhere (Slade et al., 2016; Stelander et al., 2021). Furthermore, there is some evidence that women may escalate drinking faster, moving from casual drinking to dysregulated drinking in less time (Becker & Koob, 2016; Radke, Sneddon, & Monroe, 2021). In humans, sex and gender differences in drinking can be partially attributed to cultural and social norms regarding the acceptability of alcohol use among men vs. women (Keyes et al., 2021). Shifts in these social norms may be part of the reason as to why the gender gap in drinking and rates of AUD have been changing in recent times.

Use of rodent models avoids the confounding influence of the complex social environments experienced by humans. While men have historically had higher rates of drinking and AUD diagnoses, these patterns do not hold in the animal literature. In fact, in most studies, female rodents are more vulnerable than males. Female rats and mice consume higher quantities of EtOH per kg of bodyweight, develop binge-like drinking faster, and are more likely to demonstrate aversion-resistant drinking than males. These observations, which have been reviewed in depth elsewhere (Becker & Koob, 2016; Finn, 2020; Radke, Sneddon, & Monroe, 2021), prompt an examination of the factors driving sex differences in alcohol consumption.

Biological sex influences behavior via two primary mechanisms: differential gene expression arising from the sex chromosomes, and gonadal hormone release. Because males typically possess one X chromosome and one Y chromosome and females two X chromosomes, sex differences in alcohol consumption may be partially due to the differential expression of genes encoded on these chromosomes. Males contain some unique genes found on the Y chromosome that may affect alcohol drinking or other related behaviors, referred to as direct genetic effects (Ngun et al., 2011). Additionally, some X chromosome genes can escape the process of X chromosome inactivation, resulting in greater expression in females (Berletch et al., 2015; Ngun et al., 2011; Payer & Lee, 2008). The Four Core Genotypes (FCG) mouse model has been used to examine the independent influence of sex chromosome complement to alcohol drinking behaviors (Arnold, 2009). These studies demonstrate that EtOH consumption and preference differs in XX vs. XY individuals in continuous access and limited access drinking paradigms, with XY mice with testes having the higher rates (Sneddon et al., 2022b, 2023b). Habitual responding for EtOH and aversion-resistance are also impacted, with genetically XY individuals demonstrating reduced flexibility (Barker et al., 2010; Sneddon et al., 2023b).

Gonadal hormones are also known to have a profound influence on behavior in males and females. Levels of gonadal hormones are quite different between males and females, with males typically having much higher levels of androgens (e.g., testosterone), while females have higher levels of estrogens. The influence of gonadal hormones on drinking has been studied through the surgical removal of gonads (gonadectomy, GDX), administration of supplementary hormones, or antagonism of androgen or estrogen receptors (Agrawal & Dwivedi, 2020; Alomary et al., 2003; Badr et al., 1979; Bertholomey & Torregrossa, 2019; Lisciotto et al., 1990; Maddern et al., 2024; Tang et al., 1994). The surgical removal of ovaries is referred to as ovariectomies or oophorectomies (OVX) and the surgical removal of testes is referred to as orchiectomies, orchidectomies, or castration (ORX). Studies examining the influence of ovarian hormones demonstrate a facilitatory influence on various measures of alcohol drinking. This effect holds true for binge-like (Finn, 2020; Satta et al., 2018; Sneddon et al., 2023a) consumption, while, interestingly, both increases and decreases in aversion-resistance have been observed following OVX (Arnold et al., 2023; Sneddon et al., 2023a). In regard to mechanism, greater binge-like consumption in female vs. male mice has been tied to estrogen receptor (ER) enhancement of VTA DA neuron responsivity to EtOH (Vandegrift et al., 2017, 2020). Although the effects of ovarian hormones are significant, it is important to recognize that the estrous cycle does not reliably influence drinking in 2BC models (Arnold et al., 2023; Fulenwider et al., 2019; Satta et al., 2018; Sneddon et al., 2023b) or increase behavioral variability in females (Becker et al., 2016; Kaluve et al., 2022). As the influences of female gonadal hormones on alcohol drinking and addictive behavior have been extensively reviewed previously (Carroll & Anker, 2010; Finn, 2020; Radke, Sneddon, & Monroe, 2021), the remainder of this article will focus on mechanisms driving drinking in males.

Testosterone and the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis

Testosterone is regulated and synthesized in a three-hormone axis named the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis (Fig. 1). Release of gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus is stimulated by the neuropeptide kisspeptin binding to its G-protein coupled receptor GPCR54 (also known as GPR54 or Kiss1R) (Clarkson & Herbison, 2016; Corradi et al., 2016). GnRH binds to receptors in the anterior pituitary, which activates calcium-dependent protein kinase C (PKC) and triggers the release of follicular stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) (Wanderley & Udrisar, 1994). LH is required for the secretion of testosterone (hereafter referred to as T) whereas FSH is involved in the production and maturation of sperm, both of which occur in the testes (Amory & Bremner, 2003; Clarkson & Herbison, 2016; Corradi et al., 2016; Emanuele & Emanuele, 1998). These hormones work together in a homeostatic process referred to as negative feedback (Tang et al., 1994). Levels of T in the blood suppress GnRH release from the hypothalamus and LH release from the pituitary (Amory & Bremner, 2003; Corradi et al., 2016; Pieper et al., 1987).

In the testes, LH binds to receptors on the membrane of Leydig cells to initiate the production of T (Amory & Bremner, 2003). Once in the blood, T can be metabolized into other hormones such as dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which has a 5–10 times greater affinity for androgen receptors than T, or estrogen (Naam neh et al., 2022). Estrogens work to suppress T by providing negative feedback to the hypothalamus and pituitary.

The majority of T (around 98 %) is bound by proteins that help to regulate the bioavailability of T (Dohle et al., 2003). Studies examining T levels most often measure total T in the blood, but sometimes bioavailable (or “free”) T is reported. It is also important to note that while 95 % of circulating T arises from the testes, adipose tissues and the adrenal glands also produce smaller amounts of androgens and estrogens (Corradi et al., 2016; Naamneh et al., 2022). For these reasons, T does not immediately drop and is never fully eliminated following gonadectomy.

Alcohol suppresses testosterone levels in males

The relationship between T and alcohol was first characterized in the 1970s after researchers noted the apparent “feminization” of men diagnosed with alcoholism (Badr et al., 1977, 1979; Cicero and Badger, 1977a, 1977b; Gordon et al., 1976, 1979; Mendelson et al., 1978; Van Thiel & Lester, 1976). Men experiencing feminization were described to have symptoms of gynecomastia, sterility, impotence, and a change in the distribution of body hair (Van Thiel & Lester, 1976). Many of these early studies used rodent models to assess the effect of alcohol on blood levels of T and other HPG hormones (Cicero and Badger, 1977a, 1977b), paving the way for future research assessing T’s response to acute, chronic, and even fetal exposure to alcohol. As reviewed below, there is a wealth of convincing evidence to suggest that acute and chronic EtOH exposure decreases T levels in males (Table 1).

Effects of alcohol on gonadotropin releasing hormone

A possible mechanism through which alcohol could affect T levels is GnRH. An experiment by Rasmussen and colleagues (2003) that used chronic alcohol administration via liquid diet found no change in GnRH gene expression (Rasmussen et al., 2003). However, other studies, as reviewed by Smith et al. (2023) report decreases in GnRH (Smith et al., 2023). For example, Kim et al. (Kim et al., 2003) administered 3 g/kg of 15 % EtOH by intraperitoneal injection for 10 days in adult male rats and found a decrease in GnRH mRNA levels in the hypothalamus. Similar findings have resulted from in vitro studies (Lomniczi et al., 2000; Rettori & McCann, 1997). However, the number of studies with findings related to GnRH are limited compared to those of other hormones in the HPG axis.

Effects of alcohol on luteinizing hormone

In comparison to GnRH, the influence of alcohol on LH levels has been more extensively examined. Acute intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of EtOH resulted in no change to LH levels in rats (Adams et al., 1991). The same can be said for human studies utilizing acute EtOH administration, as shown by Ida and colleagues (1992), where LH actually slightly increased in response to EtOH (Ida et al., 1992). Other studies suggest that acute EtOH administration may decrease LH (Adams & Cicero, 1991; Cicero & Badger, 1977b; Gordon et al., 1976; Hadi et al., 1987; Smith et al., 2023). However, these effects are often dose-dependent, finding that low doses of EtOH tend to increase LH and high doses may decrease LH (Cicero & Badger, 1977b; Smith et al., 2023).

Findings are also mixed when it comes to chronic administration of EtOH and the effects on LH. For example, Aprioku and colleagues (Aprioku et al., 2022) demonstrated that LH decreases in response to chronic EtOH administration. However, the majority of studies examining the effects of chronic EtOH on LH levels suggest that EtOH does not directly affect LH (Badr et al., 1979; Little et al., 1992; Mello et al., 1985; Rasmussen et al., 2003) or may even increase it (Ida et al., 1992; Kley & Teschke, 1985; Mendelson et al., 1978). This relationship is made even more apparent when considering findings from Cicero and Badger (Cicero & Badger, 1977b) where LH was administered alongside EtOH and T still decreased, suggesting that EtOH affects the testes directly. Furthermore, work by Rivier (1999) used the GnRH antagonist Azaline B and found that alcohol’s effects on T are independent of a decrease in LH (Rivier, 1999). Thus, the effects of alcohol on LH levels do not likely contribute to alcohol’s influence on T.

Effects of alcohol on follicle stimulating hormone and spermatogenesis

Although not directly responsible for T production, the effects of alcohol on FSH and subsequent spermatogenesis could influence testicular function. Most studies demonstrate that levels of FSH do not change in response to EtOH (Aprioku et al., 2022; Badr et al., 1979; Gordon et al., 1976). Spermatogenesis and sperm motility, on the other hand, do seem to be affected by EtOH. In general, EtOH reduces sperm count/density (Aprioku et al., 2022; Trautman, 2023), disrupts spermatogenesis (Perreault et al., 2023), reduces weight of seminal vesicles (Sousa et al., 1994), and decreases sperm motility (Tangsrisakda & Iamsaard, 2020). Relatedly, when male mice and rats were exposed to EtOH, T was found to be low in their offspring (Cicero, Adams, et al., 1990). This finding was generally the same for female dams exposed to EtOH, with offspring typically having a delayed postnatal T surge (McGivern et al., 1993) and lower levels of T (Dalterio et al., 1981; Lugo et al., 2006). Conversely, one study by Carter and colleagues (Carter et al., 2014) reported that both male and female offspring of dams exposed to EtOH have higher levels of T.

Effects of alcohol on testosterone

Unlike the rest of the HPG axis, the vast majority of papers published find that alcohol decreases T dose-dependently (i.e., the higher the dose, the greater the decrease in T). Acute doses of EtOH, ranging from 0.5 g/kg to 3 g/kg, almost always decrease T in vivo (Adams et al., 1991, 1997; Cicero & Badger, 1977b; Eriksson et al., 1983; Ida et al., 1992). For example, Adams and colleagues, 1991, demonstrated that 3 g/kg injections of 25 % EtOH resulted in significant decreases in T after 1 h (Adams et al., 1991). In vitro studies in which the testes are removed from rats, decapsulated, and the extracted Leydig cells are exposed to EtOH or its metabolites are generally in agreement; three of these studies found that EtOH decreases T synthesis and secretion (Gordon et al., 1979; McGivern et al., 1993). On the other hand, Johnston and colleagues (Johnston et al., 1981) found that EtOH in concentrations below 600 mg/dl does not decrease two out of four enzymes (17Beta-dehydrogenase and a complex of 3Beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase and steroid Alpha4-5-isomerase) involved in T biosynthesis. However, the enzyme 17Alpha-hydroprogesterone aldolase was significantly reduced by EtOH in concentrations of 150 mg/dl or greater. Wanderley and Udrisar (1994) reported that T levels were unaffected by EtOH alone; however, T secretion was significantly reduced when EtOH was combined with GnRH or phorbol ester (PDBu), both of which can trigger release of LH and FSH through intracellular PKC. Additionally, they found that acetaldehyde, a primary metabolite of EtOH, significantly reduced T levels both independently and in combination with GnRH or PDBu (Wanderley & Udrisar, 1994).

There were three exceptions identified within the reviewed papers where T increased from acute alcohol administration. The first example was found only within young Sprague-Dawley rats (PND 25–30), whereas T was unchanged in rats between the ages of PND 35–45, and EtOH decreased T in rats that were PND 45+ (Little et al., 1992). This was the only study identified where different cohorts of rats were tested by age for alcohol administration and may point to effects of acute alcohol administration dependent on pubertal status. The second exception where acute administration resulted in an increase in T was from a study by Alomary and colleagues (2003) where T increased within the frontal cortex of the brain, as well as in plasma, 30 min following a 2 g/kg injection of EtOH (Alomary et al., 2003). Finally, Sarkola & Eriksson, 2003, found that men who were light-drinkers had an increase in T in response to low doses (0.5 g/kg) of alcohol (Sarkola & Eriksson, 2003). This result suggests that low doses of alcohol may not decrease T when there is no history of alcohol abuse.

Findings from chronic alcohol administration and its effects on T are generally the same as those from acute studies, regardless of the method of administration. Whether the subjects are men (Emanuele & Emanuele, 1998; Gordon et al., 1976, 1978; Ida et al., 1992; Vatsalya et al., 2012), male rats (Abarikwu et al., 2016; Adams & Cicero, 1991; Adams et al., 1991, 1997; Aprioku et al., 2022; Apter & Eriksson, 2003; Cicero & Badger, 1977b; Cicero, Adams, et al., 1990; Eriksson et al., 1983; Lugo et al., 2006; Santucci et al., 1983; Tangsrisakda & Iamsaard, 2020), male mice (Badr et al., 1977, 1979; Dalterio et al., 1981), or male monkeys (Mello et al., 1985), T reliably decreases with chronic EtOH administration. These findings have been shown across species, concentrations, routes of administration, and length of exposure. T decreases in men when alcohol is administered intravenously or orally for anywhere from 7 to 40 days and in monkeys receiving intragastric inhibition of EtOH for 18–20 h. In rodents, similar results are found with oral gavage (e.g., 0.5–5 g/kg for 4–5 weeks), i.p. injection over multiple sessions and concentrations (e.g., 0.5–4 g/kg), or chronic alcohol diets.

There are very few exceptions to T’s response to chronic EtOH administration. For starters, one study found no differences in T levels following chronic consumption of 10 % EtOH for 1 h/day in male Long-Evans rats (Duncan et al., 2006) while another found no changes in T, LH, or FSH after rats were given 2 g/kg of EtOH via oral gavage for 63 days (Siervo et al., 2015). Another study using male F2 rats, which are bred from AA and ANA rats, found decreases in T in response to acute EtOH injection develop tolerance after 3 weeks of continuous access to 10 % EtOH (Etelälahti et al., 2011).

Finally, withdrawal is a significant aspect of human addiction, therefore, changes in T in response to withdrawal could contribute to alcohol drinking behaviors. Since alcohol exposure suppresses T levels, a rebound in activity might be expected during withdrawal. However, the available evidence is currently inconclusive. Some authors have found that T decreases during withdrawal periods (Forquer et al., 2011; Heberlein et al., 2016) while others have found the opposite (Rasmussen et al., 2003; Walter et al., 2007).

Although several authors have posited theories, there is no agreed upon mechanism for how alcohol reduces T. One possibility is that EtOH-induced secretion of endorphin into testicular interstitial fluid (TIF), which correlates with a decrease in T, may have autocrine or paracrine effects on testicular steroidogenesis (Adams et al., 1997). There could also be a change in catabolism in the liver that would result in less androstenedione being converted into T, resulting in less T detected in plasma (Alomary et al., 2003; Sarkola & Eriksson, 2003). Other theories from early in vitro studies suggest that EtOH and/or acetaldehyde directly affect the enzymes that synthesize T (Badr et al., 1977; Johnston et al., 1981; Wanderley & Udrisar, 1994). Further research will be necessary to resolve this issue.

Testosterone may suppress alcohol intake

Although there are many studies examining the effect of alcohol on T in males, studies approaching the relationship from the other directions are far less common. This lack of research into how T influences alcohol intake is especially concerning when considering T’s potential role in the development and maintenance of excessive alcohol drinking. Below, we review the available evidence and discuss gaps in current knowledge that may guide future lines of research.

Individual differences in T levels are one way to assess whether T influences alcohol drinking behaviors. There is evidence to support the idea that higher T is correlated with greater drinking in adolescent boys, with multiple studies showing that salivary or blood T levels positively correlate with metrics such as alcohol use, age of onset of use, and symptoms of alcohol dependence (Braams et al., 2016; Costello et al., 2007; Eriksson et al., 2005; Walter et al., 2007). Blood T levels (both total and bioavailable) also positively associate with higher alcohol use in adult men (Grange et al., 1995; Hansen et al., 2012; Ho et al., 2023; Jensen et al., 2014; Stålenheim et al., 1998), although notably not in older men (Wu et al., 1995). In men with alcohol dependence, there is also a correlation between total T and alcohol craving (Ho et al., 2019) while the androgen dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a metabolite of T, is associated with AUD relapse (Lenz et al., 2017). This suggests that T signaling may contribute not only to the initiation and maintenance of drinking but also to vulnerability during abstinence, when craving and relapse risk are heightened. One possibility is that elevated androgen activity enhances sensitivity to alcohol-related cues or increases motivation to seek alcohol following withdrawal. Interestingly, lower T predicts a higher severity of withdrawal symptoms in men with alcoholism (Ruusa & Bergman, 1996). Similar trends have been observed in high and low consuming selectively-bred rats (Etelälahti et al., 2011). Although, collectively, this evidence suggests that higher levels of T promote excessive alcohol use, the mechanism for this relationship is not straightforward. For example, associations between T levels and drinking have been proposed to be mediated by an increase in risk-taking (Erol et al., 2019; Finn, 2020; Peters et al., 2015). Higher bioavailable T is also associated with conduct disorder, which may influence drinking independently of gonadal mechanisms (Eriksson et al., 2005).

To establish a more direct connection between T levels and risk for alcohol intake and addictive behavior, direct manipulations using animal models are required (Table 2). An experiment by Bertholomey and Torregrossa (2019) involved ORX or Sham surgeries on male Sprague-Dawley rats, where half of the castrated rats also received T injections. Following ORX, rats were trained in an operant drinking paradigm where reinforcers earned and EtOH intake were measured. ORX significantly increased both reinforcers earned as well as EtOH intake when compared to Sham rats. Furthermore, T replacement in ORX mice significantly decreased reinforcers earned and EtOH intake when compared to ORX mice who received vehicle injections (Bertholomey & Torregrossa, 2019). Vetter-O’Hagen and Spear (2011) likewise found that T administration suppresses sweetened EtOH consumption and preference in 2BC limited access drinking. These studies suggest that T suppresses alcohol drinking behaviors in males.

There are some studies, however, that have found no changes in EtOH consumption following ORX. For example, Almeida et al. (1998) demonstrated that castration does not affect EtOH consumption in Wistar rats during a 23-h oral self-administration paradigm. However, when castrated rats were treated with DHT, their EtOH consumption significantly decreased (Almeida et al., 1998). Other studies using extended, continuous free-choice drinking have also found no significant changes in ad libitum EtOH consumption following ORX (Begg & Weisinger, 2008; Cailhol et al., 2001), perhaps suggesting that gonadal hormone effects in males are specific to models of binge drinking and limited access.

Overall, these studies in rodents suggest that T serves as a protective factor by suppressing alcohol consumption. As with studies correlating individual differences in T to alcohol drinking behaviors, the mechanism underlying these effects is not known. One possibility is through interactions with the mesolimbic DA system, which is influenced by androgens (Tobiansky et al., 2018). Although no studies examining this possibility have been conducted with alcohol, ORX does increase the dopaminergic response to amphetamine in rats (Hernandez et al., 1994). Another possibility to consider is that ORX affects EtOH metabolism (Alomary et al., 2003), specifically increasing liver ADH activity and EtOH clearance. T administration reduces ADH activity to normal levels (Cicero & Bell, 1980; Mezey et al., 1980; Rachamin et al., 1980). This is an important consideration, as EtOH metabolism significantly influences both the amount of EtOH that can be consumed and the physiological effects experienced. Rodents with ORX may be consuming more EtOH in order to compensate for this increase in EtOH metabolism. Another potential mechanism involves interactions between testosterone and the HPA axis stress response, which are discussed in detail next.

Interactions between HPG and HPA axes may contribute to alcohol drinking

Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and alcohol

The HPA axis is a three-hormone axis that is similar to the HPG axis. The HPA axis starts with the release of corticotropin releasing hormone (CRH) from the hypothalamus, which travels by blood through the portal vein to the anterior pituitary to trigger the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). From there, ACTH travels by blood to the adrenal glands, which are situated on top of the kidneys, where it then binds and triggers the release of cortisol in humans, or corticosterone in rodents (Oyola & Handa, 2017). Similar to the HPG axis, the HPA axis maintains homeostasis by short-loop and long-loop feedback from cortisol/corticosterone which occurs at the level of the hypothalamus, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex. (Fig. 2).

Interactions between alcohol, stress, and the HPA axis have been thoroughly studied, revealing an important role for this system in initiation, maintenance, and relapse to drinking (Blaine & Sinha, 2017; Dunne & Ivers, 2023; Stephens & Wand, 2012). Stress is a significant motivator of alcohol use, especially as a coping mechanism (Chaplin et al., 2008; Cooper et al., 1992; Dawson et al., 2005; Esper & Furtado, 2013; Holahan et al., 2001; Sacco et al., 2014; Temmen & Crockett, 2020), and HPA axis activation may be an important contributor to these effects. For example, individuals with a family history of AUD tend to show a stronger cortisol response to opioid receptor antagonism and psychosocial stressors (King et al., 2002; Uhart et al., 2006; Wand, 2008), suggesting HPA hyperactivity is a risk factor for AUD. Preclinical evidence supports this association as well, although stress exposure in animals can both increase and decrease alcohol drinking behaviors (Becker et al., 2011; Perry et al., 2025; Radke et al., 2014, 2020). Corticosterone treatment in rats increases drinking (Besheer et al., 2012; Fahlke et al., 1995) while adrenalectomy (ADX) reduces EtOH consumption and preference (Fahlke & Eriksson, 2000; Fahlke et al., 1995; Lamblin & De Witte, 1996). Some rodent lines selected for alcohol drinking behaviors (e.g., AA rats, HDID mice) display higher levels of corticosterone at baseline or in response to stress (Apter & Eriksson, 2006; Savarese et al., 2022). Corticosterone has also been found to mediate the relationship between repeated stress exposure and enhanced EtOH-induced locomotor sensitization in mice (Roberts et al., 1995).

Acute exposure to alcohol activates the HPA axis by stimulating the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN) (Ida et al., 1992; Richardson et al., 2008; Tabakoff et al., 1978) while chronic use produces neuroendocrine tolerance - a dysregulated system characterized by elevated glucocorticoid levels that lack normal circadian rhythmicity and a blunted response to stressors (Becker et al., 2011; Richardson et al., 2008; Stephens & Wand, 2012). In rodents, alcohol or stress exposures that produce this type of HPA dysregulation also escalate EtOH consumption and produce compulsive-like drinking (Butler et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2000; Logrip et al., 2013; Richardson et al., 2008; Vendruscolo et al., 2012). These changes are thought to contribute to excessive alcohol drinking and the development of compulsive drinking patterns through interactions with the mesolimbic dopamine system and extrahypothalamic stress systems (Blaine & Sinha, 2017).

HPG regulation of the stress response

The role of the HPA axis and stress in alcohol drinking is relevant because sex hormones are important regulators of this system, with estrogens increasing HPA axis activity and androgens suppressing it (Heck & Handa, 2018; McCormick et al., 2002; Zuloaga et al., 2020). In adult males, ORX and hormone replacement studies demonstrate that T directly suppresses HPA axis function (Handa & Weiser, 2014; Seale et al., 2004a, 2004b). For example, ORX increases stress-induced CRH and ACTH release while T or DHT administration lowers these hormones back to more typical levels (Almeida et al., 1992; Bingaman et al., 1994; Handa et al., 1994; Viau & Meaney, 1996). In animals, glucocorticoid release following stress or alcohol is greater in females than males, an effect that relies at least partly on organizational effects of sex hormones (McCormick et al., 1998; Ogilvie and Rivier, 1996, 1997; Rivier, 1993). However, sex differences in HPA axis activity in humans is more nuanced, especially for psychosocial stressors where evidence for greater reactivity exists for both men and women (Kudielka & Kirschbaum, 2005; Zuloaga et al., 2020) and binge alcohol intake has been reported to increase ACTH and cortisol more in men than in women (Anthenelli et al., 2025).

HPA regulation by T occurs throughout the axis, with T being demonstrated to suppress hypothalamic CRH as well as alter glucocorticoid receptor (GR) levels in the medial preoptic nucleus (MPN) of the hypothalamus and hippocampus (Kerr et al., 1996; Viau & Meaney, 1996). T and DHT infusions in the MPN decrease corticosterone levels in ORX male rats (McCormick et al., 2002) while lesions to the MPN can disrupt typical regulatory effects of T on the HPA axis (Williamson & Viau, 2008). The MPN is densely populated with intracellular androgen receptors (ARs), and when T binds to these receptors, it initiates a cascade of genomic events that alter gene expression within the neurons. The result is enhanced inhibitory signaling from the MPN to the PVN, which plays a central role in regulating the HPA axis, producing a decrease in CRH release (Handa & Weiser, 2014; Williamson & Viau, 2008). This alteration of HPA axis activity by T has behavioral consequences, as T has also been shown to reverse increases in depression and anxiety in men with hypogonadism (McHenry et al., 2014). Likewise, anxiety-like behaviors are elevated following ORX and reduced following T administration. Notably, T was as effective as the antidepressant imipramine in reducing anxiety-like behaviors when administered to rodents following social isolation (Carrier & Kabbaj, 2012).

Implications for alcohol drinking

It is interesting to speculate that the effects of T on HPA reactivity may contribute to its suppressive effects on alcohol consumption in males. In animals, there is a clear vulnerability to alcohol drinking behaviors in females, including higher consumption across drinking paradigms and an increased likelihood of engaging in aversion-resistant drinking (Finn, 2020; Fulenwider et al., 2019; Radke et al., 2021a, 2021b; Sneddon et al., 2020, 2023c). Indeed, the alcohol-induced glucocorticoid response is lower in male rats than in females or ORX males (Ogilvie and Rivier, 1996, 1997). Considering the role of glucocorticoids in promoting alcohol consumption, this lower HPA reactivity in the presence of T may contribute to reduced vulnerability of males in animal models. Lower HPA activity in males vs. females may also protect against the development of the neuroendocrine tolerance associated with heavy, chronic alcohol use. Though explorations of sex differences in this outcome are notably lacking from the literature, one recent study reporting higher morning cortisol levels in binge vs. light-moderate drinkers found no interaction with sex (McGowan et al., 2024).

Another consideration is that reductions in T following acute and/or chronic alcohol use could alter regulation of the HPA axis, resulting in increased glucocorticoid release and promotion of alcohol drinking through an elevated stress response in males (Fig. 3). Although T may serve as a protective factor with limited use, the ability of alcohol to suppress T may attenuate or eliminate this effect and even contribute to HPA dysregulation in men who drink alcohol excessively. Interestingly, CIE exposure, which produces HPA axis dysregulation in male rodents (Lee et al., 2000; Richardson et al., 2008), escalates EtOH consumption in male but not in female mice (Jury et al., 2017) and rats (Morales et al., 2015). Adult females are resistant to a number of other behavioral and physiological outcomes following CIE, such as hyperalgesia, cardiac responses to stress, and behavioral flexibility (Barker et al., 2017; Bianchi et al., 2021; Brandner et al., 2023). It is interesting to speculate that sex hormone signaling may be an important contributor to these sex differences in response to CIE, although future studies will be needed to confirm these ideas. Relatedly, some studies in rodents with prior intermittent exposure to EtOH show stress effects in males but not females (Albrechet-Souza et al., 2020; Finn et al., 2018; Quadir et al., 2019), an effect with parallels in the human literature (Chaplin et al., 2008; Cooper et al., 1992; Esper & Furtado, 2013; Sacco et al., 2014; Temmen & Crockett, 2020). For example, a systematic review of studies examining gender differences in the relationship between stress and alcohol consumption found that while 95 % of the reviewed studies reported an association between stress and drinking, 35 % of those reported effects only in men (Esper & Furtado, 2013). Thus, sex-specific endocrine profiles may lead to differences in how stress interacts with chronic alcohol exposure to affect consumption.

Gaps in research and future directions

The reviewed studies highlight important interactions between the alcohol and the HPG axis, particularly T. Although there is a significant amount of research demonstrating that alcohol reduces levels of T in males, research focusing on how T affects alcohol drinking is much more limited. Correlational data from humans suggests that T may promote alcohol use in men (Eriksson et al., 2005; Ho et al., 2019; Lenz et al., 2017), but the mechanisms behind this effect are not clear. In contrast, ORX studies in rodents demonstrate that T may be a protective factor that lowers levels of alcohol consumption (Bertholomey & Torregrossa, 2019; Vetter-O’ et al., 2011; Almeida et al., 1998). The available evidence suggests that this effect may be limited to certain types of alcohol drinking paradigms, such as binge-like consumption. Studies examining the effect of T on aversion-resistant drinking or escalation induced by intermittent access would be informative. Here again, the mechanisms underlying the suppressive effects of T have not been tested. Thus, the field could benefit from additional investigations into the extent that effects of T are mediated by changes in metabolism vs. interactions with brain systems involved in reward and stress. This could be achieved by incorporating BEC and other metabolic measures into studies examining the influence of T on alcohol drinking in rodents.

We also found compelling evidence for interactions between the HPG and the HPA axes that likely influence alcohol drinking and related behaviors (Apter & Eriksson, 2006; Eriksson et al., 2017; Etelälahti et al., 2011; Handa & Weiser, 2014; Ida et al., 1992; Williamson & Viau, 2008). Specific experiments designed to test whether male-specific alcohol-induced reductions in T contribute to drinking behaviors, potentially through effects on the HPA axis, are needed. Studies examining T should also assess levels of corticosterone and other HPA axis hormones and correlate these measures to behavior. Targeted manipulation of androgen receptors in the MPN could help determine whether T is important for escalated EtOH consumption or other effects of CIE. A thorough exploration of sex differences in HPA axis dysregulation following chronic EtOH exposure in human and animal models is also needed.

While this review highlights the critical importance of considering sex as a biological variable in alcohol research, there are other important and potentially interactive factors that cannot be ignored. For example, it is important to consider age and to include multiple age groups when possible, as it has been shown that T’s response to EtOH is potentially age-dependent (Little et al., 1992). This is particularly significant considering that EtOH metabolism changes with age, with metabolism typically being higher in younger animals (Rachamin et al., 1980). Therefore, further research on the developmental timing of alcohol exposure (e.g., adolescence, adulthood, late-adulthood) is necessary to clarify how alcohol interacts with the status of the HPG axis. Social housing status also modifies levels of T in animals and leads to dominance hierarchies associated with variations in T (Machida et al., 1981; Williamson et al., 2017a, 2017b). There are also circadian rhythms in T levels that could impact the results of studies (Bremner et al., 1983; Iwahana et al., 2008; Piro et al., 1973; Thorpe et al., 2012). Factors such as stress, diet, and physical activity are all known to affect T levels across species (Cano et al., 2008; Perreault et al., 2023; Retana-Má et al., 2003; Whittaker & Wu, 2021; Wrzosek et al., 2020). Awareness of these endocrine effects could help alcohol researchers prevent confounds in their studies and identify potential mechanisms contributing to observed effects. For example, lower alcohol consumption in socially dominant mice could be explained by higher levels of T in these animals (Anacker & Ryabinin, 2010; Duncan et al., 2006).

Finally, factors related to exposure need to be considered when studying alcohol and T. Conflicting findings regarding the effects of EtOH on the HPG axis may be the result of studies using different doses of alcohol (Abarikwu et al., 2016; Adams et al., 1991; Smith et al., 2023). Differential effects may also depend on whether alcohol exposure is acute or chronic or whether the study was conducted in heavy or light drinkers. Human studies that collect survey data on alcohol use, frequency, and related behaviors should consider additional questions regarding the specific types of alcohol consumed as well to account for how other compounds in alcoholic beverages may impact T or its metabolism (Jenkinson et al., 2012).

Conclusions

The current review of the literature demonstrates that T has multiple, interactive effects with alcohol drinking behaviors in humans and animals. Baseline levels of T may bias males toward approach behaviors that increase the tendency to engage in risky drinking behaviors in adolescent and adult men. At the same time, rodent studies tell us that T protects males against elevated levels of alcohol consumption. T levels also change with alcohol exposure, decreasing with both acute and chronic use. In heavy drinkers and individuals with severe AUD, high levels of consumption can even promote hypogonadism and physiological signs of feminization.

The mechanisms responsible for the effects of T on alcohol drinking are not known, although influences on the mesolimbic DA systems and HPA axis are possible. Evidence for interactions between the HPA and HPG axes is particularly strong, and supports the hypothesis (illustrated in Fig. 3) that alcohol-induced changes in T in males may be a factor in escalated consumption through HPA dysregulation. Therefore, in males, alcohol use may establish a harmful feedback loop where decreased T reduces HPA axis inhibition, amplifying negative affect and driving continued drinking.

In conclusion, excessive alcohol intake and AUD remain significant public health issues in the United States and worldwide. Despite ongoing research efforts, the development of new and effective treatments for AUD has been slow, and the few currently available options are often limited in their efficacy. Sex differences contribute to rates of alcohol use, alcohol-related harms, and biological mechanisms driving drinking. Gaining a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying lower alcohol consumption in male vs. female rodents is crucial for informing treatment strategies in humans. In particular, exploring the interactions between the HPG and the HPA axes may help explain these sex differences and ultimately guide the development of more targeted, sex-specific pharmaceutical and behavioral interventions for excessive alcohol drinking and AUD.
