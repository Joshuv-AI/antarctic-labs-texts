Formyl Peptide Receptor-2-Suppressed Autophagy Promotes the Migration and Invasion of Human Glioblastoma Cells Through PI3K/Akt Signaling
Authors: Zhenzhao Luo, Deyong Kong, Guangjian Qi, Chao Zheng, Wushi Zhao, Zhongxin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFormyl peptide receptor-2 (FPR2) belongs to the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family and plays a critical role in the development of various tumors. However, the roles and mechanisms of FPR2 in glioblastoma (GBM) remain poorly understood. In this study, we observed significant upregulation of FPR2 in glioma cell lines and tissues, and elevated FPR2 expression levels are correlated with poor patient survival. Furthermore, we found that FPR2 suppresses the autophagy mediated by BECN1 and ATG5 in GBM cells. Using Western blot analysis, we revealed that FPR2 regulates GBM cell invasion via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Additionally, we demonstrated that knocking down FPR2 expression in GBM cells reduced tumor cell migration and invasion in vitro and tumor growth in vivo. The inhibition of FPR2 led to cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase and increased apoptosis. Finally, our findings indicate that FPR2 may prevent autophagy-induced epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT)-like changes by preventing autophagy-induced degradation of Snail. Our findings suggest that FPR2 promotes GBM cell migration and invasion through the inhibition of autophagy and the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, highlighting the potential of inducing autophagy as a therapeutic approach to inhibit invasion in GBM with high FPR2 expression.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11481-026-10284-z.Keywords: Glioblastoma, Autophagy, FPR2, Cell growth, Migration and invasion, Snail

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is among the most prevalent and fatal intracranial malignancies in the human brain (Wang et al. 2023). Although multiple therapies, including surgical removal, adjuvant radiation therapy and chemotherapy with temozolomide, have been accepted as the standard therapeutic treatment for GBM, the prognosis remains poor because of its diverse heterogeneity, with a five-year survival rate not exceeding 10% (Ren et al. 2022). To date, few biological risk factors and effective therapeutic targets have been identified to improve GBM treatment (Moody et al. 2021). Therefore, investigating new prognostic and promising therapeutic objectives to prolong the survival time of GBM patients is imperative.

Chemoattractant receptors are seven transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor (GPCRs) that are involved in a broad spectrum of pathophysiologic processes, including hematopoiesis, inflammation, wound healing, development (Hou et al. 2022), and malignant tumor progression (Dorsam and Gutkind 2007; Liang et al. 2020). Formyl peptide receptors (FPRs) and their variants, including FPR-like1 (FPR2) and FPR-like 2 (FPR3), are GPCRs that were originally isolated from phagocytic leucocytes and mediate the activation and chemotaxis of these cells during interactions with bacterial formylated chemotactic peptides (Busch et al. 2022). Previous studies have shown that other cell types, including astrocytoma cells, also express functional nFPRs (Le et al. 2000). Among these receptors, FPR2 is the most attractive owing to its diverse signaling pathways and a range of structurally diverse agonists, which contain high concentrations of fMLF, bacteria, viruses, and synthetic peptides (Tylek et al. 2023). In addition to formylated peptides, FPR2 also recognizes large proteins and structurally unrelated lipids as internal agonists and exhibits potent pro- or anti-inflammatory responses on the basis of the ligands that activate it. Annexin-A1 serves as an FPR2 ligand, and previous studies have confirmed that annexin-A1 activates FPR2 to promote breast cancer proliferation (Moraes et al. 2017). Our previous data demonstrated that Tat-NTS, a blocker of annexin-A1 nuclear translocation, inhibits glioblastoma cell proliferation and invasion in a dose-responsive manner (Luo et al. 2022). Agonist binding to FPR2 in leucocytes leads to the activation of phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase C (PLC) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) signaling cascades, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways as well as the nuclear translocation of NF-κB (Li et al. 2021). More importantly, FPR2 plays a crucial role not only in many inflammatory-associated diseases but also in the progression of malignant gliomas (Tadei et al. 2018).Previous research has shown that functional FPR2 is endogenously expressed in the U87 astrocytoma cell line (Le et al. 2000), suggesting its involvement in the development of brain cancer. A previous study revealed that FPR activation in U-87 cells also enhanced the phosphorylation of Akt (Zhou et al. 2005), also known as protein kinase B. Akt is a downstream component of the PI3K pathway and has been shown to promote tumor cell survival and proliferation. Highly malignant human GBM cells express functional FPRs associated with their malignant phenotype, which is elicited by potential agonists secreted by necrotic tumor cells (Yang et al. 2011). Although FPR2 in glioblastoma tissue and glioma cells directly promotes tumor cell invasion and growth and the synthesis of matrix metalloproteinases and angiogenic vascular endothelial growth factor (Yao et al. 2008), it also transactivates EGFR, synergistically enhancing the aggressive properties of GBM cells (Huang et al. 2007). Although prior research has demonstrated that FPR2 is significantly upregulated in highly aggressive GBM cells and plays a vital role in facilitating cancer development, the clinical value of FPR2 and its role in human gliomas still need further clarification.

Autophagy is the main cytoplasmic degradation system through which cytoplasmic macromolecules and damaged organelles are transported to and digested in the lysosome by double-membrane vesicles (Lei and Klionsky 2021). Autophagy can be either constitutive or induced in response to different degrees of stress. Autophagy not only maintains cellular homeostasis but is also involved in various physiological and pathological processes, including apoptosis, aging, and tumor growth (Debnath et al. 2023). The regulation of autophagy is complicated and varies on the basis of tumor cell type. Autophagy suppresses growth and development while promoting cell death in certain tumors. Moreover, autophagy promotes the survival and proliferation of other tumors, making it a promising therapeutic target for cancer. Prior research has indicated that autophagy is capable of inhibiting GBM progression in the initial stages but facilitating GBM cell proliferation in the later stages. During autophagic flux, many dynamic membrane reorganizations are initiated with the elongation and closure of autophagosomes, resulting in the formation of autophagosomes, which ultimately effectively fuse with lysosomes to form autolysosomes. One FPR2 agonist, namely, annexin-A1, plays a crucial role in the vesiculation of multivesicular bodies (Diakonova et al. 1997) and membrane trafficking (White et al. 2006), indicating that FPR2 might also be involved in autophagy (Liu et al. 2018).

Despite several studies on the role of autophagy in GBM, the pathological role of FPR2 in regulating autophagy in human GBM has not been well studied (Wan et al. 2023). In this study, we discovered that FPR2 is selectively overexpressed in glioma cells and brain tissues compared with normal astrocytes and normal brain tissues, respectively. Knockdown of FPR2 impaired the proliferation, migration and invasion of GBM cells. More importantly, the inhibition of FPR2 through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway facilitated autophagy in GBM cells, and the process was dependent on BECN1 and ATG5. In addition, the inhibition of autophagy by FPR2 enhanced the migration and invasion of GBM cells by suppressing the degradation of Snail. Our results provide the first and most definite evidence that the inhibition of autophagy by FPR2 actively promotes the migration and invasion of GBM tumor cells.

Clinical Specimens

From June 2013 to August 2019, we collected 58 paraffin-embedded glioma samples from the Neurosurgery Department at the Central Hospital of Wuhan. All the cases were confirmed by a pathologist, and 6 nontumor brain tissue samples (acquired through decompressive surgery) were utilized as normal controls. All the glioma samples included elaborate information related to clinical and pathological features. In accordance with the 2000 World Health Organization central nervous system tumor grading system, 15 cases were Grade II, 16 cases were Grade III, and 27 cases were Grade IV. Additionally, 6 Grade IV samples and 6 nontumor brain tissue samples were procured and preserved in liquid nitrogen for immunoblot analysis. The research protocol was endorsed by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Central Hospital of Wuhan, and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Stable FPR2 Knockdown and Overexpression and Transient Transfection

Lentiviral vectors, including LV2-pGLV constructs with either FPR2 shRNA or scramble nontarget shRNA and LV5 vectors harboring FPR2, were developed by GenePharma Co. (Shanghai, China) and validated through sequencing. The target sequence for the human FPR2 shRNA was designed as follows: 5′-UAUAAGGGCCACCACCUGAUAUGGG-3′. The efficiency of the knockdown was confirmed in previous studies (Khau et al. 2011). U87 and LN-229 cells were transfected with lentivirus particles and subsequently screened with puromycin for a two-week period. Glioma cell lines with stable knockdown or overexpression of FPR2, along with corresponding control cell lines, were established. siRNAs targeting BECN1 (sc-29797; Santa Cruz, USA), ATG5 (sc-41445; Santa Cruz, USA), control siRNA (sc-37007; Santa Cruz, USA) or SQSTM1 (sc-29679; Santa Cruz, USA) were transiently transfected into stable FPR2-knockdown and FPR2-overexpressing glioma cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Real-Time RT‒PCR

Total RNA was extracted from glioma cells with TRIzol, and quantitative real-time PCR was subsequently performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and our previous study (Wei et al. 2021). The housekeeping gene GAPDH was used as an internal control. The sequences of primers used were as follows: FPR2, 5’-CTTGTGATCTGGGTGGCTGGA (sense) and 3’-CATTGCCTGTAACTCAGTCTC (antisense) and GAPDH, 5’-GCCAAAGGGTCATCATCTC (sense) and 3’-GTAGAGGCAGGGATGATGTTC (antisense) (Rossi et al. 2015).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) Assay

Cell growth was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and as previously described (Wei et al. 2021). Briefly, stable transfected U87 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well. Cells from each group were cultured for 24, 48, 72 h and 96 h before CCK-8 assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was applied and 10 µl reaction solution was added to every well and subsequently incubated for 1 h. The optical density was then detected by an EnSpire Manager spectrophotometer plate reader (Turku, Singapore) at 450 nm (excitation) and 600 nm (emission).

Flow Cytometry Analysis

During the exponential growth phase, U87 cells were plated at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well in 6-well culture plates. Glioma cells transduced with different vectors were prepared for flow cytometry analysis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cell cycle progression and apoptosis was assessed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). To examine the cell cycle, a PI/RNase Staining Buffer Solution Cell Cycle Analysis Kit (#550825; BD Bioscience, USA) was used. To measure the rates of apoptosis, an apoptosis detection kit (#C1062M, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used. The percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated by adding together the early apoptotic cells in the lower right quadrant and the late apoptotic cells in the upper right quadrant.

Western Blot Analysis

The proteins from the cells or tissues were extracted in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Germany). Following centrifugation at 14,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, proteins were combined with 5× loading buffer (#P0015, Beyotime) and heated to boiling for 10 min. Equal quantities of protein (30 µg) were loaded per lane for immunoblot analysis. The proteins were separated and then blotted onto PVDF (#IPVH00010; Millipore, Billerica, USA) membranes. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were blocked with TBST containing 5% nonfat milk, followed by overnight incubation with the appropriate primary antibodies in TBST at 4 °C. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, which were diluted to a 1:10000 ratio in TBST, at room temperature for 60 min. After three washes with TBST, the protein blots were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Pierce Biotech, Rockford, IL) and quantified using NIH ImageJ 1.4.3 (Bethesda, MD, USA) after normalization to the internal control.

Migration and Invasion Assays

First, the Transwell chamber was immersed in complete medium for 2 h to ensure proper hydration. GBM cells were collected and quantified under a microscope and then plated into the upper wells at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. The chamber medium was composed of DMEM enriched with 0.1% bovine serum albumin, while the lower part contained DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The migration pattern of glioblastoma cells was subsequently examined. After 24 h of cultivation, the chamber was removed. The cells that penetrated the chamber membrane were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The cells were visualized under a microscope (Olympus, USA), and five random microscopic fields were imaged. For invasion assays, the chambers were precoated with a uniform layer of Matrigel (BD Biosciences), and the subsequent steps were similar to those used in the migration assays.

Assay of Cytosolic Phospholipase A2 (cPLA2) Activity

cPLA2 activity was evaluated via sonicated liposomes prepared with 1-stearoyl-2-[1-14 C]-arachidonyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine as the substrate, in accordance with a previously established methodology(Kim et al. 2001).

Immunohistochemical Analysis

Immunohistochemical staining of FPR2 was performed on brain tissue sections that were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin wax, and cut into 5-µm sections. The tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene and subsequently rehydrated through a series of graded alcohol solutions. The samples were then incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min to inhibit the activity of endogenous peroxidases. The slices were heated in citrate buffer for 20 min to facilitate antigen repair. Afterward, the tissue slices were incubated with an anti-FPR2 antibody (1:200, #30989-1-AP, Proteintech Technology) overnight at 4 °C. The sections were then incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody, developed using an avidin-biotin peroxidase complex and counterstained with Harris’ modified hematoxylin. After imaging, two pathologists independently evaluated the samples. The staining intensity was categorized as follows: strong, 3; moderate, 2; weak, 1; and no staining, 0. The score was determined on the basis of the proportion of FPR2-positive cells: if the percentage of positive cells was less than 5%, a score of 0 was assigned; if it was between 5% and 10%, the score was 1; if it was between 10% and 50%, the score was 2; if it was between 50% and 80%, the score was 3; and if it was greater than 80%, the score was 4. The total score ranged between 0 and 12. The above two scores were multiplied; a score equal to or greater than 4 was considered positive, and a score less than 4 was considered negative.

Nuclear Factor NF-κB p65 Transcription Factor Activity Assay

Nuclear extracts from stably transfected U87 cells were extracted using NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, USA). The activity of NF-κB was evaluated by employing 9 µg of nuclear extract along with an NF-κB (p65) Transcription Factor Assay Kit (KA1341;Abnova, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocols. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an EnSpire Manager spectrophotometer plate reader (Turku, Singapore).

Tumor Xenografts in Nude Mice

Following the NIH guidelines and the requirements of the “Ethical Review System for Laboratory Animal Welfare of the Myhalic Biotechnology Co., Ltd” (approval number HLK-20241115-001), mouse experiments was performed. The parental U87 cells, which had been stably transfected, were adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/ml. A volume of 200 µl containing 2 × 106 cells with either the sh-Scramble or FPR2 knockdown sequence in PBS was subcutaneously injected into both regions. Starting from day 3, the tumor volume was measured every 3 days. The tumor volume was determined using the following formula: tumor width2 × length × 0.5. On the 30th day, tumor tissues were acquired for weighing or Western blot analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0) software. The data are expressed as the means ± SDs. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test was used to evaluate differences among groups. A P value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves were constructed and evaluated using the log-rank test for comparative analysis, utilizing data from the CGGA glioma cancer dataset. The correlation between FPR2 and SQSTM1 in glioma patient tissues was assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn).

Results

FPR2 is Selectively Upregulated by More Highly Malignant Glioblastoma Cells and Tissues

To evaluate the function of FPR2 in glioma, we determined FPR2 expression in glioblastoma cells and brain tissues. First, Western blot results revealed that FPR2 protein levels were clearly higher in four human glioma cell lines, U87, U251, U118 and A172, compared with another glioma cell line, LN-229, and normal human astroglial cells (NHAs) (Fig. 1A and B). Of these, U87 cells presented the highest levels compared with those of other glioma cell lines; therefore, we chose the U87 cell line for subsequent experiments. Additionally, immunohistochemical staining for FPR2 was conducted on glioma tissues at various stages and normal brain tissues. The findings indicated that FPR2 expression increased with increasing glioma grade (Fig. 1C and D). Similarly, the Western blot results revealed that the expression of FPR2 was clearly higher in GBM tissues than in normal brain tissues (Fig. 1E). We also analyzed FPR2 expression using a human glioma database (https://gliovis.bioinfo.cnio.es/) and reported that high FPR2 levels were associated with poor prognosis in glioma patients (p = 0; Fig. 1F). Taken together, these results suggest that FPR2 plays an oncogenic role in GBM.

FPR2 Knockdown Restrains Cell growth, Migration and Invasion

FPR2 is upregulated in glioma compared with normal brain tissues; therefore, we hypothesized that FPR2 might play a role in regulating cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. To confirm this supposition, three shRNAs were designed to study the function of FPR2, and the knockdown efficacies were verified using RT‒qPCR and Western blotting in U87 cells (Fig. 2A and B).Among the three shRNAs, shRNA2 had the highest knockdown efficacy (Fig. 2C) and was thus chosen for subsequent studies (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A). To assess the impact of FPR2 on glioma cell proliferation, we performed CCK-8 and cell cycle assays. We observed that compared with the Scr-shRNA and control treatments, the knockdown of FPR2 inhibited U87 cell growth (P < 0.05, Fig. 2D). The results of the cell cycle assays revealed that silencing FPR2 increased the number of cells in the G2 phase but decreased the number of cells in the G1 phase population compared with those in the Scr-shRNA-treated and untreated cells (Fig. 2E and F).To further elucidate the mechanisms through which FPR2 influences glioma cell proliferation, we analyzed apoptosis using flow cytometry. These findings indicated that FPR2 inhibition led to a marked accumulation of peak apoptotic cells, in contrast to the peak in U87 cells infected with Scr-shRNA and untreated U87 cells (Fig. 2G and H). These results suggest that FPR2 is involved in the proliferation of glioma cells. We further examined the function of FPR2 in glioma cell migration and invasion. After FPR2 expression was knocked down in U87 cells, the number of migrated and invaded cells notably decreased compared with that of cells transfected with Scr-shRNA or untreated cells (Fig. 2I to K). To investigate the potential mechanisms by which FPR2 participates in the apoptosis of glioma cells, Western blotting was performed, and the results further revealed that the suppression of FPR2 promoted U87 apoptosis and increased the levels of apoptosis-related proteins, such as cleaved caspase-8, cleaved caspase-7, cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP (P < 0.05; Fig. 2L and M).

FPR2 Knockdown Inhibits Tumor Growth in Vivo

Once the causal connection between the suppression of FPR2 expression and glioma cell growth was established, we verified whether a reduction in FPR2 expression could inhibit tumor growth in vivo. U87 cells that were stably infected with Scr-shRNA or FPR2 KD were subcutaneously injected into either side of the same nude mouse. The tumor growth curves demonstrated that the knockdown of FPR2 clearly suppressed the growth of glioma tumors in vivo (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A). Four weeks after injection, the weight of tumors formed by U87/FPR2-KD cells was clearly lower than that formed by U87/Scr-shRNA cells (Fig. 3C). FPR2 knockdown in tumors generated from U87/FPR2-KD cells was also confirmed by Western blot analysis (Fig. 3D and E). The tumor growth curves revealed that inhibition of FPR2 markedly reduced the growth of glioma tumors in vivo (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B). Tumor samples obtained from nude mice were subjected to Western blotting for cleaved caspase-3. Our results suggested that the inhibition of FPR2 enhanced the apoptosis of U87 cells in nude mice with elevated levels of cleaved caspase-3, whereas reduced the expression of caspase-3 (P < 0.05; Fig. 3F and G). Collectively, our results suggest that the inhibition of FPR2 significantly inhibits glioma cell growth via the induction of apoptosis.

Inhibition of FPR2 Promotes Apoptosis by Inhibiting the PI3K/Akt Signaling Pathway

To identify the underlying molecular mechanisms through which FPR2 regulates glioma cell proliferation and migration, we investigated the effects of FPR2 on the expression of proteins involved in the cell cycle and key signaling molecules associated with these biological processes (Chen et al. 2019a; Wei et al. 2019). Our results demonstrated that FPR2 had a significant effect on cdc2, cyclin B1, and cdc25C protein levels, as well as the PI3K/Akt signaling cascade, but had no obvious effect on cyclin D1, β-catenin or GSK-3β signaling in U87 glioma cells (Fig. 4A to D). Additionally, since the role of FPR2 in regulating NF-κB and phospholipase A2 (PLA2) activity in GBM remains unclear, we also evaluated the activity and expression of NF-κB and cytosolic phospholipase A2 (cPLA2) in U87 cells with FPR2 knockdown. Although NF-κB activity did not significantly differ across the groups, the suppression of FPR2 expression significantly increased both the activity and expression of cPLA2 (Fig. 4E to H). Overall, the downregulation of FPR2 expression led to decreased expression of cdc2, cyclin B1, and cdc25C, as well as inactivation of the PI3K/Akt pathway. These changes may lead to impaired cell growth and invasive capabilities both in vitro and in vivo.

FPR2 Regulates SQSTM1 Expression Through Autophagy in Glioma Cells

PI3K/Akt signaling clearly plays an essential role in facilitating cell survival and restraining autophagy (Xu et al. 2020); thus, we further explored whether FPR2 regulates Akt-mediated autophagic flux signaling in GBM cells. SQSTM1, a negative marker of autophagic flux, is degraded by autophagy. Investigating whether FPR2 promotes Akt activation through autophagy is interesting because FPR2 selectively activates Akt to promote glioma cell tumor growth. FPR2 expression was increased in U87 glioma cells compared with LN-229 cells (Fig. 1A); therefore, we reduced the expression of FPR2 in U87 cells and increased its expression in LN-229 cells using a lentiviral vector that expresses FPR2 shRNA or an FPR2 plasmid. We found that FPR2 positively modulated the protein expression of SQSTM1 in glioma cells (Fig. 5A and B). Following the transduction of U87 cells with lentiviral vectors expressing either FPR2 shRNA or scramble shRNA, the cells were subsequently treated with the autophagy inhibitors bafilomycin A1 (BAF, 40 nM) or chloroquine (CQ, 40 µM) to prevent the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes as well as the degradation of lysosomal proteins (Yang and Kimmelman 2014). Western blot analysis revealed that both autophagy inhibitors abolished the reduced the expression of SQSTM1 in FPR2-knockdown glioma cells but had a minor effect on SQSTM1 levels in shRNA-treated glioma cells (Fig. 5D and E). Additionally, FPR2 expression was positively correlated with SQSTM1 expression in human glioma tissues (Fig. 5C). Moreover, we employed siRNAs against Beclin 1 (BECN1) or ATG5 to block the initiation of autophagy, and both small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) abolished the decrease in SQSTM1 expression in the FPR2-knockdown glioma cells with only a minor effect on SQSTM1 expression in the shRNA control glioma cells (Fig. 5F and G). In summary, these data indicate that FPR2 regulates SQSTM1 expression through autophagy to activate the Akt pathway.

FPR2-Induced Autophagy Induces EMT-Like Alterations in Glioma Cells

Epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a critical process in tumor invasion. To evaluate the potential functions of FPR2 in regulating EMT, we investigated the effects of FPR2 on the expression levels of key EMT markers in glioblastoma cells through Western blotting. Our results revealed that the silencing of FPR2 clearly reduced the expression of the mesenchymal markers vimentin and N-cadherin while increasing the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin. Furthermore, overexpression of FPR2 had the opposite effect on the expression levels of these EMT markers in glioblastoma cells (Fig. 6A to C). These findings demonstrate that FPR2 triggers EMT-like modifications in glioma cells.

Previous investigations have demonstrated that autophagy is intimately associated with EMT (Behrooz et al. 2024). To determine whether the suppression of autophagy by FPR2 results in EMT, we used siRNA to knockdown the expression of BECN1 or ATG5 and 3-methyladenine (3-MA) to inhibit autophagy in shRNA control cells and FPR2-KD glioma cells, respectively. The levels of typical EMT markers were subsequently determined. The results demonstrated that genetic or chemical inhibition of autophagy resulted in decreased E-cadherin levels and elevated N-cadherin and vimentin expression in glioma cells with FPR2 knockdown. However, FPR2 had only a minor effect on the expression of these EMT markers in glioma cells transfected with scramble shRNA (Fig. 6D to G), suggesting that the suppression of autophagy by FPR2 causes EMT-like changes in glioma cells.

Autophagy defects promote EMT through the inhibition of Snail degradation, thereby promoting metastasis (Lv et al. 2012; Grassi et al. 2015). Intriguingly, FPR2 knockdown markedly decreased Snail expression, whereas the substantial upregulation of FPR2 significantly increased Snail expression (Fig. 6A to C). Moreover, genetic and pharmacological suppression of autophagy blocked the decrease in Snail protein levels in glioma cells with FPR2 knockdown. However, interestingly, these inhibitory measures had relatively minor effects on the expression of Snail protein in glioma cells transfected with the shRNA control (Fig. 6D to G). These data indicate that the suppression of autophagy by FPR2 prevents the degradation of the Snail protein in glioma cells. More importantly, our results indicated that overexpression of SQSTM1 upregulated Snail protein expression in FPR2-knockdown glioma cells (Fig. 6H and I), whereas SQSTM1 knockdown reduced Snail levels in FPR2-overexpressing glioma cells (Fig. 6J and K), suggesting that the suppression of autophagy by FPR2 is involved in the degradation of Snail in glioma cells through a SQSTM1-mediated pathway.

Discussion

Glioblastoma (GBM) represents the most prevalent malignant tumor of the central nervous system. Emerging evidence indicates that FPR2 is involved in multiple cancers (Chen et al. 2023). Elevated FPR2 expression is common in diverse cancers, including colorectal cancer (De Matteis et al. 2022), gastric cancer (Hou et al. 2017) and glioblastoma(Tadei et al. 2018). To the best of our knowledge, few studies have focused on the clinical significance of the association between FPR2 and autophagy in glioma. Therefore, a systematic study of FPR2 in gliomas may be useful for characterizing this fatal disease. This study revealed the expression pattern of FPR2 during the development of GBM and its potential prognostic significance in patients with glioma and further explored its role and mechanism in glioma cells. Because SQSTM1 is a widely recognized autophagic substrate (Lee et al. 2023), we sought to determine whether FPR2 regulates autophagy by measuring the expression of SQSTM1. Our results demonstrate that FPR2 inhibits autophagy in glioma cells and that the autophagy triggered by FPR2 knockdown is mediated through ATG- and BECN1-dependent pathways. Despite the possible link between FPR2 and autophagy, the function of FPR2-mediated autophagy in glioblastomahas not been comprehensively studied. To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first evidence demonstrating that FPR2 modulates autophagy in glioblastoma.

Approaches that induce G2/M phase arrest in glioma cancer cells could be beneficial for the treatment of GBM (Gortany et al. 2021). Apoptosis represents a common pattern of programmed cell death detected in tumor cells(Kashyap et al. 2021). Our flow cytometry analysis revealed that FPR2 knockdown induces apoptosis in glioma cells and results in G2/M phase arrest. Apoptosis occurs through two main pathways: the intrinsic pathway and the extrinsic pathway (Kashyap et al. 2021). In the intrinsic pathway mediated by mitochondria, caspase-9 and other caspase molecules, such as caspase-3, caspase-6, and caspase-7, are activated. The extrinsic apoptotic pathway is another pathway in which caspase-8 is initially activated, resulting in subsequent downstream cascade activation. These two pathways ultimately result in the cleavage of caspase-3, leading to apoptosis. In the present study, FPR2 KD in U87 cells resulted in the activation of cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-8, cleaved caspase-7 and cleaved PARP.

Previous studies have shown that autophagy may have either a GBM-promoting or a GBM-suppressing effect (Chryplewicz et al. 2022). On the one hand, autophagy inhibits GBM by eliminating carcinogenic metabolic substrates, toxic unfolded proteins, and damaged organelles and preventing chromosomal instability (Jee and Cheong 2023). On the other hand, autophagy also maintains mitochondrial function and support metabolism by recycling intracellular substrates, thereby promoting tumor growth (Zhang et al. 2024). Some studies have reported that the role of autophagy in tumorigenesis depends on the tissue and genetic context, and understanding its controversial role in GBM is critical. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway promotes cell survival and suppresses autophagy. In the context of Crohn’s disease, macrophage FPR2 modulates neutrophil function through the activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway (Prescott and McKay 2011). Alisertib (ALS) inhibits cell proliferation and induces mitochondria-dependent apoptosis and autophagy through the participation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR- and p38 MAPK-mediated signaling pathways in human glioblastoma cells (Liu et al. 2017). Hypoxia induces the activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, which is related to the increased migration and invasion capabilities of human glioblastoma U87 cells (Huang et al. 2018). The ANXA1 mimetic peptide Ac2-26 activates formyl peptide receptors (FPRs), leading to the degradation of IKKβ through chaperone-mediated autophagy mediated by HSPB1 in microglia treated with NCM (Liu et al. 2018). All these results indicate that the PI3K/Akt/mTOR cascade is closely related to survival, growth, angiogenesis, autophagy, and chemotherapy resistance in GBM.In addition, our earlier research revealed that ANXA1 activates PI3K/AKT signaling to promote glioma cell proliferation, migration and invasion both in vitro and in vivo (Wei et al. 2021). Ren et al. reported that Annexin A1 induces oxaliplatin resistance in gastric cancer through autophagy by targeting PI3K/AKT/mTOR (Ren et al. 2023), and Yang et aldemonstrated that CircCUL1 inhibits trophoblast cell migration and invasion and promotes cell autophagy by sponging hsa-miR-30e-3p to restrict fetal growth via the ANXA1/PI3K/AKT axis (Yang et al. 2024).Ganesan et al. demonstrated that the cytotoxic effects of punicalagin are mediated through the modulation of ANXA1, highlighting its role in the interplay between apoptosis and autophagy (Ganesan et al. 2020). Importantly, ANXA1 primarily exerts its effects through its interaction with FPR2; however, the role of FPR2 in promoting glioma development via the autophagy/PI3K/AKT pathway remains unclear. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate whether the modulation of autophagy by FPR2 is contingent upon the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

FPR2 influences the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway to regulate autophagy in glioblastoma through complex molecular interactions. As a key player in inflammation and cancer progression, FPR2 affects cell survival, proliferation, and autophagy and is crucial for tumor growth and therapeutic resistance. The PI3K/Akt pathway, which is vital for autophagy regulation, typically suppresses autophagy to promote cell survival and proliferation. However, under stress conditions such as nutrient deprivation or therapy, the pathway is altered to induce autophagy, potentially leading to cell death or survival.

Research has indicated that targeting the PI3K/Akt pathway and its regulation of autophagy could offer new treatments for glioblastoma. Studies have shown that the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway mediates autophagy, as observed in ivermectin-treated glioma cells (Liu et al. 2019). In addition, hypoxia-induced autophagic apoptosis is achieved by targeting the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway (Lee et al. 2021). Additionally, the interaction of PD-L1 with Akt in glioma cells enhances invasion through the Akt/autophagy/F-actin pathway (Chen et al. 2019b), highlighting the role of this pathway in glioblastoma progression. In the context of FPR-2, its suppression of autophagy via the PI3K/AKT pathway suggests a mechanism through which glioblastoma cells might increase their migratory and invasive potential. These findings align with those from other studies in which modulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway was shown to affect glioblastoma cell behavior. For instance, a study on the PI3K p110β isoform revealed that its inhibition, in combination with JNK inhibition, reduces glioblastoma cell proliferation and migration, further supporting the critical role of PI3K/AKT signaling in these processes (Zhao et al. 2016). We focused only on the biological function of FPR2 in GBM cells. However, as FPR2 is not expressed only in glioma cells, we cannot eliminate the possibility that the effect of FPR2 on the development of GBM originates from the cooperative effects of multiple cells in the tumor microenvironment, especially tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), because TAMs are the major innate immune component in the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, further studies are needed to determine whether FPR2 promotes the differentiation of TAMs into an immunosuppressive phenotype to promote GBM progression.

Epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) plays a critical role in the processes of tumor invasion and metastasis. FPR2 promotes EMT-like alternations in glioma cells. FPR2 enhances the induction of EMT, thereby facilitating the invasion and metastasis of other cancers (He et al. 2023). Therefore, FPR2-induced EMT may also contribute to the invasion of glioma cells. Previous reports have shown that inhibiting autophagy promotes EMT in tumors (Gundamaraju et al. 2022).Both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of autophagy restore the protein expression levels of EMT markers in glioma cells with FPR2 knockdown, indicating that FPR2 increases EMT in glioma cells by inhibiting autophagy.

Recent investigations have shown that autophagy defects promote EMT by blocking the degradation of its two main regulatory factors, Snail and Twist, and subsequently inducing EMT (Jacob et al. 2024). Further studies are necessary to elucidate whether FPR2-induced suppression of autophagy results in EMT in glioma cells. The findings of this study suggest that genetic and pharmacological inhibition of autophagy eliminates the decrease in Snail protein expression levels in autophagy-activated FPR2-KD glioma cells, suggesting that FPR2-mediated suppression of autophagy may induce EMT, potentially by blocking the degradation of Snail.

SQSTM1 (p62) acts both as an autophagy substrate and as a cargo receptor that typically binds to proteins that must be eliminated and degraded into autophagosomes (Ieni et al. 2022). Autophagy deficiency leads to the accumulation of SQSTM1, which interacts with Snail proteins to increase their stability and inhibit their degradation (Grassi et al. 2015). To determine whether SQSTM1 affects the ability of FPR2-inhibited autophagy to affect Snail protein levels, we up-and downregulated the expression of SQSTM1 in glioma cells. We found that the overexpression of SQSTM1 led to an increase in Snail protein levels in FPR2-KD glioma cells, whereas SQSTM1 knockdown (KD) reduced Snail protein expression in FPR2-OE glioma cells, suggesting that FPR2-inhibited autophagy abolishes the degradation of the Snai1 protein via a SQSTM1-mediated pathway. Taken together, our data indicate that FPR2 potentially promotes glioma migration and invasion, possibly through autophagy deficiency-induced EMT.

Our data demonstrate that FPR2 inhibits autophagy through specific PI3K/Akt activation. Autophagy plays a key role in various pathological situations, but its interaction with apoptosis remains unclear (Strickland and Stoll 2017). Autophagy occurs prior to apoptosis and is necessary for apoptosis. Autophagy induces cell death by activating proapoptotic factors and downregulating the expression of oncogenes, thereby inhibiting tumor development (Edinger and Thompson 2003). However, autophagy does not lead to cell death but rather acts to weaken apoptosis by promoting cell survival. Autophagy can maintain endoplasmic reticulum function and mitochondrial function to limit apoptosis. Radiotherapeutic and chemotherapeutic resistance are two of the most prevalent issues associated with anticancer treatment, and autophagy is closely associated with these processes because it helps prevent radio/chemotherapy-induced apoptosis in various tumors. In addition, autophagy supplies nutrients to cells in oxygen-deficient or nutrient-depleted environments, protecting GBM tumor cells from death. Overall, apoptosis and autophagy are antagonistic or synergistic, depending on the stimulus and cell setting. Autophagy and apoptosis can coexist and even act synergistically to trigger cell death, whereas apoptosis can also accelerate the transition of cells to autophagic cell death (Denton and Kumar 2019).

In conclusion, the findings of our research suggest the following model for the actions of FPR2 in GBM (Fig. 7). FPR2 activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, leading to the suppression of ATG5- and BECN1-mediated autophagy. In addition, the suppression of autophagy leads to EMT-like alterations and subsequently enhances the migration and invasion of glioma cells. Our data demonstrate that FPR2 is a key regulator of glioma progression and should be further considered a potential target for the treatment of GBM.

The proposed model explains how the suppression of autophagy by FPR2 facilitates the migration and invasion of GBM cells. In this model, FPR2 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, leading to the inhibition of autophagy through BECN1 and ATG5. This results in the accumulation of SQSTM1, which inhibits the degradation of Snail. Increased Snail induces EMT-like alterations, thereby facilitating GBM cell migration and invasion
