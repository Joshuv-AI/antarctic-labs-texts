Investigation of spore infection mechanism in sunflowers infected by Puccinia helianthi
Authors: Jiayun Wang, Yan Lu, Xiangjiu Kong, Yang Song, Lan Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sunflower rust, caused by the fungus Puccinia helianthi, poses a significant threat to global sunflower production. However, the fundamental mechanisms of P. helianthi infection and development remain poorly understood. This study aims to elucidate the infection process of P. helianthi in sunflower leaves through histological and cytological observations. P. helianthi is an autoecious fungus that undergoes five spore stages: teliospores, basidiospores, pycniospores, aeciospores, and urediospores. Our findings revealed that basidiospores germinated by producing a germ tube at 24 hours post-inoculation (hpi). This germ tube entered the leaf through the stomata or directly penetrated the epidermal cells without forming an appressorium, resulting in the formation of monokaryotic intercellular hyphae. The hyphae grew along the cell wall, which thickened when the hyphae came in touch with the mesophyll cells. The monokaryotic haustorium formed within the host cells at 48 hpi. Chlorotic dots began to appear on the upper surface of the leaves five to six days after the basidiospores were inoculated. After 14 days post-inoculation (dpi), flask-shaped orange pycnia developed. By approximately 18 d, honeydew started appearing on the pycnia. Aecia began to develop six or seven days after fertilization; they were cylinder-shaped and adorned with coglike ornaments. Aeciospores and urediospores germinated by producing a germ tube at 4 hpi, which subsequently developed a dome-shaped appressorium that penetrated the sunflower leaves through the stomata at 6 hpi. Intercellular hyphae produced septa apically and further differentiated into haustorial mother cells, which invaded the host cell and formed multinucleate haustoria at 24 hpi, with all the cytoplasm from the haustorial mother cells flowing into the haustorium. Aeciospores with warty surfaces invaded the host and produced oval-shaped urediospores at 14 dpi, which exhibited dense, thorny protrusions on their surfaces. By regulating the temperature, the urediospore-infected leaves were induced to produce teliospores after 20 days, which consisted of two cells with smooth surfaces.

Introduction

Sunflower (Helianthus annuus) is a vital oilseed crop, with China ranking as the fourth-largest producer of sunflowers in the world, following Russia, Ukraine, and Argentina [1]. The main sunflower-producing areas in China are located in Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning, Gansu, Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, and other places. The largest producing area is Inner Mongolia, where mainly edible sunflowers are planted [2]. Sunflower rust, caused by the fungus Puccinia helianthi, is a highly destructive disease that impacts global sunflower cultivation. The first report of this disease in 1872 confirmed its widespread distribution across all continents except Antarctica [3]. As the area cultivated with sunflowers has expanded, the geographical spread of this disease has also increased, resulting in a rise in both the severity and frequency of outbreaks. Consequently, significant losses have been incurred in sunflower production [4, 5]. The pathogen is present in all major sunflower-producing regions across the globe [6]. Variation among global P. helianthi isolates was first observed in 1923; however, the races were not formally described until 1962 [7]. Between 1988 and 1989, Race 3 was widely prevalent in the Manitoba region of Canada and the north-central region of the United States [8]. Australia reported 16 races in 1999 [9] and six races were reported in Argentina in 2011 [10]. The USA reported a total of 29 races in 2015 [11] and China reported 17 in 2015 [12]. According to Araslanova’s research, seventeen races of the P. helianthi were identified across three regions of the Russian Federation between 2018 and 2020, of these, eleven races were newly documented [13].

Research on plant histopathology began in the early 20th century with investigations into the infection process of Puccinia dispersa in the leaves of brome (Bromus japonicus) [14]. The development of wheat stem rust (Puccinia graminis) was systematically studied using the tissue slice technique and described for the first time in 1915, laying the foundation for future histopathological studies [15]. Upon infecting the plant, rust fungi expanded and grew within intercellular spaces, forming intercellular hyphae. Kang et al. [16] investigated the infection process of wheat stripe rust (Puccinia striiformis) on wheat leaves and discovered significant variation in nuclear numbers within intercellular hyphae; some were binucleated, while most were multinucleated. This observation indicated distinct cytological characteristics exhibited by wheat stripe rust compared to other rusts. Observations using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that substances surrounded the intercellular hyphae after Puccinia malvacearum infected its host, the hollyhock (Alcea rosea). These substances were speculated to contain secreted products from the fungus and components derived from degraded host cell walls [17]. Kang et al. [18] examined the ultrastructure of wheat stripe rust infection and identified significant diversity in the nuclear number within the haustorial mother cells. Typically, these cells contained no more than six nuclei, with most exhibiting an even distribution. During the formation of haustoria and their subsequent invasion of the host cell wall, cytoplasmic material from the haustorial mother cells flows into the developing haustorium, forming a cavity. In a related study on wheat stem rust, Chong et al. [19] discovered that the cell wall of haustorial mother cells was more complex than that of the intercellular hyphae. Specifically, the cell wall of the intercellular hyphae consisted of only four layers, while the cell wall of the haustorial mother cells comprised six. Rohringer et al. [20] employed cytochemical methods to examine the presence of galactose, glucose, mannose, fructose, and other glycosyl groups in the host cell walls of the wheat stem rust and stripe rust pathogens. Baka studyed the micromorphology of pycniospores and aeciospores of P. helianthi on its host [21].

Following the invasion of the host cell wall by the haustorial mother cells of rust fungi, these cells continue to develop within the host cell, leading to the formation of haustoria. The haustorium consists of a neck and a body, with the neck serving as a tubular structure that connects the haustorial mother cell to the haustorium [22]. The findings indicate that haustoria play a crucial role in maintaining the parasitic state of the pathogen by facilitating the exchange and transmission of nutrients and information between the host and the pathogen, as well as regulating the fundamental metabolism of the pathogen [23]. Sunflower rust significantly impacts sunflower production worldwide. To address the scientific gap in understanding the life cycle of sunflower rust, this study employed a combination of microscopy techniques, including fluorescence microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and TEM, to investigate spore morphology, germination, the infection process, and infection structures. The findings provide a vital scientific foundation for elucidating the interaction between P. helianthi and its host at the cellular level.

Results

Teliospore germination and basidiospore formation

Mature teliospores of P. helianthi exhibit a dormant period. To promote the germination of teliospores, they must be stored at − 20℃ for at least two weeks and subjected to both wet and dry treatments [24]. Following this, the teliospores germinated within 48 h on water agar. When observed under a SEM, the teliospores on leaves germinated by producing a metabasidium (Fig. 1A). The development of the metabasidium was associated with the formation of three septa, dividing it into four contiguous cells, each of which gave rise to a conical sterigma (Fig. 1B). A basidiospore was formed at the tip of each sterigma. Initially, the basidiospores were nearly spherical (Fig. 1C). Upon maturation, the basidiospores became oval with a wrinkled surface; each metabasidium produced four basidiospores (Fig. 1D).

Basidiospore development and infection

SEM examination revealed that basidiospores germinated by producing a single unbranched germ tube of variable length on the leaf surface at 24 hours post-inoculation (hpi). Some basidiospores entered through the stomata (Fig. 2A), while most germ tubes penetrated the host cuticle and epidermal cells directly (Fig. 2B, C). In fluorescence microscopy, we observed that basidiospores germinated into germ tubes at 24 hpi (Fig. 2D), followed by the formation of haustorial mother cells and haustoria within the host cells at 36 hpi and 48 hpi (Fig. 2E, F). TEM observation indicated that after entering the host, the pathogen’s hyphae grew in the intercellular spaces between leaf cells at 36 hpi, with cell wall thickening occurring at the points of contact between the hyphae and the cell wall. Each hyphal cell contained only one nucleus (Fig. 2G). After the intercellular hyphae formed the haustorial mother cells, haustoria developed from external haustorial mother cells with a slender neck at 48 hpi (Fig. 2H, I).

Pycnia development

At five to six days post-inoculation (dpi) with basidiospores, chlorotic spots appeared on the leaves, which subsequently transitioned to yellow. By 14 dpi, yellow-orange spots (pycnia) were observed on the upper side of the infected cotyledons, the size of the pycnia is 96–112 μm, and they grew in clusters. (Fig. 3A). The surface of the stem bearing cotyledons is smooth. Under conditions of high basidiospore inoculum density and the presence of water on the seedling stem, basidiospores can readily adhere to the surface of the sunflower stem, causing infection and leading to stem enlargement and deformity. In contrast, the upper stem regions possess longer trichomes, which serve as an effective barrier against basidiospores infection (Fig. 3B). SEM examination indicated that bundles of flexuous hyphae and paraphyses penetrated the host epidermal cells, extending out of the surface (Fig. 3C). Under conditions of elevated environmental humidity, pycnial nectar began to exude approximately four days after the appearance of pycnia (Fig. 3D). Using light microscopy and the semi-thin section technique, we observed pycniospores aggregating beneath the epidermal cells (Fig. 3E), breaking through the epidermis to form flexuous hyphae and paraphyses, with pycniospores scattered on the leaf surface (Fig. 3F). Pycniospores formed in the pyciniosporophore surged to the host surface through the paraphyses gap as observed under SEM (Fig. 3G). The surface of the oval pycniospores exhibited folds (Fig. 3H, I).

Aeciospore development and infection

About seven days after the cross-fertilization of pycniospores, tubular aecia developed on the abaxial surface of the leaf, directly opposite the pycnia, producing dikaryotic aeciospores (Fig. 4A). In light microscopy, the aecium was formed by hyphae that aggregated beneath the host epidermis, eventually breaking through the epidermis as they grew (Fig. 4B). It was also observed that the peridium surrounding the aeciospores consisted of a single layer of cells, as seen in the cross-section of the aecia (Fig. 4C). SEM examination revealed that cylindrical, short aecia with irregular openings at the apex were arranged in a circular pattern on the leaves (Fig. 4D), with numerous aeciospores produced within each aecium (Fig. 4E, F). Upon maturation, many aeciospores were released from the opening at the apex of the aecia (Fig. 4G). The aeciospores were nearly circular (Fig. 4H) and densely packed within the aecia, measuring 17.2–28.1 μm in length and 16.7–26.9 μm in width. The surface of the aeciospores was heavily covered with warts (Fig. 4I).

SEM examination showed that the aeciospores germinated by producing a germ tube at 4 hpi (Fig. 5A), which extended and grew along the epidermal cells of the leaves, with its tips forming appressoria that penetrated through the stomata (Fig. 5B). Germ tubes exhibited directional growth on the leaf surface, often extending toward the stomata. However, some germ tubes bypassed adjacent stomata and extended toward distant stomata (Fig. 5C). In fluorescence microscopy, samples at 12 hpi showed that the substomatal vesicle formed first, followed by the development of intercellular hyphae and haustorial mother cells (Fig. 5D, E). The haustorium was formed at 24 hpi (Fig. 5F). TEM observation indicated intercellular hyphae were formed at 12 hpi, and the hyphal cells were typically binucleate (Fig. 5G). Subsequently, intercellular hyphae invaded host cells and formed haustoria at 24 hpi (Fig. 5H).

Urediospore infection and teliospore development

Mature uredia were observed 14 d after inoculation with aeciospores. Subsequently, urediospores were inoculated onto sunflower leaves. SEM analysis revealed that germination of the urediospores began at four hours post-inoculation, during which germ tubes were produced. Some of these germ tubes branched out and spread freely across the leaves (Fig. 6A). Upon encountering open stomata, appressoria with varying morphologies developed at the ends of these germ tubes to facilitate host invasion (Fig. 6B, C). In fluorescence microscopy, we observed that infection by urediospores initially led to the formation of substomatal vesicles at 6 hpi (Fig. 6D), followed by the production of intercellular hyphae, which subsequently differentiated into haustorial mother cells at 12 hpi (Fig. 6E). Twenty-four hours after inoculation, host cell invasion occurred through haustorium formation (Fig. 6F). TEM examination revealed early-stage hyphal formation within the intercellular spaces of leaf cells at 12 hpi (Fig. 6G, H), as well as the development of haustoria within the host cells at 24 hpi. (Fig. 6I). The morphology of haustoria formed by the infection of both urediospores and aeciospores was found to be similar (Figs. 5H and 6F).

After six to seven days of inoculation with urediospores, chlorotic spots appeared on the leaves. Fourteen days post-inoculation, cinnamon-brown uredial pustules were observed (Fig. 7A). SEM examination showed some spores exhibited a concave shape on both sides and lacked spines, possibly due to inter-spore compression (Fig. 7B). The urediospores were echinulate, with the conical spines distributed across the entire surface. The oval-shaped urediospores measured between 22.0 μm and 31.2 μm in length and 20.8 μm to 27.9 μm in width (Fig. 7C). After 20 d of continuous cultivation in the greenhouse following the formation of uredia, dark-brown or black telia developed. The telia were denser and firmer than the uredia and did not easily rub off (Fig. 7D). The telia broke through the leaf epidermis, exposing a large number of teliospores (Fig. 7E). Each teliospore consists of one upper cell, one lower cell, and a stipe. The lengths of the teliospores ranged from 43.7 μm to 57.9 μm, while their widths ranged from 15.5 μm to 22.7 μm. The stipe length measured between 94.3 μm and 105.6 μm. The apex of the upper cells featured a slightly convex cap structure (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

The teliospore germinates to produce a metabasidium, which subsequently develops four sterigmata, each generating a basidiospore. This phase of basidiospore germination and the resulting infection is crucial for the sexual reproduction of rust fungi. There are two forms of basidiospore germination: the direct formation of germ tubes and the secondary formation of basidiospores through the production of sterigma. The formation of secondary basidiospores is observed in many rust fungi. The teliospores of larch-poplar leaf rust (Melampsora larici-populina) can produce not only primary basidiospores and secondary basidiospores but also exhibit phenomena associated with the formation of secondary basidiospores [25]. Southern fusiform rust (Cronartium fusiforme) typically produces secondary basidiospores in water, and the infection process of these secondary spores is similar to that of the primary basidiospores [26]. In this experiment, the germination of teliospores from sunflower rust resulted in the production of basidiospores, with no secondary spores observed.

Our SEM examination revealed that the basidiospores of P. helianthi did not form appressoria after germination and mostly invaded directly through the leaf epidermis, with stomatal invasion observed only once. However, germinated basidiospores of Puccinia xanthii with a germ tube produced a highly differentiated appressorium and invaded the host through the leaf epidermis rather than the stomata [27].

The electron microscope is an essential tool for examining the ultrastructure and microscopic morphology of plant diseases, significantly advancing our understanding of their developmental processes and structural characteristics. By investigating the infection processes of various rust fungi, such as corn rust (Puccinia sorghi) [28] and wheat stripe rust [22], the infection process of these pathogens can be summarized as follows: The urediospores of rust fungi first attach to the host surface and germinate under favorable temperature and humidity conditions, generating germ tubes that elongate throughout the host tissue. Upon encountering stomata, these tubes form appressoria above them. The appressorium develops penetration structures that invade the stomata, resulting in the formation of substomatal vesicles. The infected hyphae continuously branch and expand between cells, forming secondary hyphae and establishing a colony. Meanwhile, haustorial mother cells and haustoria are persistently produced to absorb nutrients from the host cells. Once the colony reaches a specific developmental stage, it breaks through the host epidermis and produces uredia [29].

The invasion modes of various rust fungi exhibited inconsistencies, and the different types of spores of the same fungus also displayed distinct characteristics. In this study, our results indicated that the urediospore and aeciospore of P. helianthi germinated by producing a germ tube that extended vertically along the leaf veins and targeted the stomata. Upon reaching the stomata, the tip of the germ tube swelled, forming a dome-shaped appressorium above the stomata. The appressorium subsequently invaded the stomatal cavity, forming substomatal vesicles. This mode of infection differs from that of the bean rust pathogen Uromyces appendiculatus [30] and the poplar rust pathogen M. larici-populina [31]. When poplar rust infects leaves, two modes of urediospore invasion were observed: one occurred through the stomata with the formation of an appressorium, while the other involved direct invasion of the leaf epidermis without the formation of an appressorium. In most cases, bean rust primarily invades host cells through the stomata, with only a few instances of direct invasion of the host epidermis, which does not necessarily require the formation of an appressorium prior to infection.

After the haustorial mother cells of rust fungi invade the host cell wall, the cells continue to spread and grow, ultimately forming the haustorium. Bushnell [32] posited that the haustorium is a specialized component of the intercellular hyphae, a structure that develops within the host cell following fungal infection and facilitates the exchange of materials between the fungus and the host. Studies on haustoria and their relationship with host plants have been reported [33]. Due to the differences in the number of nuclei present in haustoria formed by urediospores, aeciospores, and basidiospores, the haustoria produced by rust fungi can be classified into two types: binucleate haustoria and mononucleate haustoria. Using an optical microscope, Allen [34] found that the haustoria formed by basidiospore invasion were linear in structure. The formation of these mononucleate haustoria, as observed under transmission electron microscopy, depended on the mononucleate hyphae produced by basidiospores invading the host without exhibiting host specificity [35]. Our investigation revealed that following the invasion of basidiospores, haustoria exhibited significant variability, and not all mononucleate haustoria conformed to the morphology of intercellular hyphae. The presence of septa in the mononucleate haustoria of sunflower rust was consistent with observations made in the study of Puccina coronata [36]. Additionally, it was noted that when P. helianthi formed haustoria following infection, septa developed first in the haustorial mother cells. The structure of the cell wall and septa of the haustorial mother cells were indistinguishable from those of mononucleate hyphae. During the process of the haustorial mother cell invading the host cell, no specialized penetration peg structures were observed. Instead, the entire hyphal wall participated in the invasion process, with localized thickening occurring at the site of invasion.

This study demonstrates that P. helianthi completes an entire life cycle on sunflowers. Furthermore, our understanding of sunflower rust fungi has been enhanced, providing a theoretical foundation for future investigations into sunflower-rust interaction mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study utilized basidiospores derived from teliospores to infect a susceptible cultivar of sunflower. Following successful infection, the pycniospore and aeciospore stages of sunflower rust were completed, and the resulting aeciospores were inoculated into sunflowers to effectively generate uredia. The experimental results indicated that after germination, the basidiospores do not produce appressoria but directly penetrate the host surface, the monokaryotic intercellular hyphae and haustoria formed after infection by basidiospores. Upon contact between the intercellular hyphae and the host cell wall, localized thickening of the cell wall is observed. Both aeciospores and urediospores germinated with a germ tube, subsequently developing an appressorium that penetrated the sunflower through the stomata. Intercellular hyphae produced septa apically and differentiated into haustorial mother cells at 12 hpi, ultimately forming multinucleate haustoria. Our investigation demonstrated that sunflower rust comprises five spore stages: teliospores, basidiospores, pycniospores, aeciospores, and urediospores. Chlorotic spots begin to develop approximately five to six days following the germination of teliospores and the subsequent production of basidiospores that infect sunflowers. Around 14 days post-inoculation, orange pycnia are formed, followed by the appearance of honeydew on the pynia surface approximately four days later. Following artificial fertilization using the honeydew, aecia commence formation within six to seven days. Upon re-inoculation of the aeciospores onto sunflower, the uredia are observed around 14 days post-inoculation. These sequential developmental events collectively signify the completion of the life cycle of the sunflower rust fungus.

We observed their infection and development using histological and cytological methods. The entire life cycle was completed on a single host, encompassing both asexual and sexual stages, thereby establishing a basis for understanding the patterns of occurrence and developing prevention methods for sunflower rust. It also provides technical approaches for future studies on the virulence variation among physiological races of the sunflower rust fungus.

Materials and methods

Plant and pathogen

Susceptible sunflower seeds, Heidapian, were provided by the Plant Pathology Laboratory at Inner Mongolia Agricultural University. Sunflower leaves bearing teliospores were collected from a farm associated with the university in winter, 2021. The seeds were planted in potting soil composed of a mixture of turf, perlite, and vermiculite. The plants were cultivated in a rust-free greenhouse maintained at a temperature of 20℃ with a 16 h light (8000 lx) and 8 h dark cycle.

Inoculation of sunflower plants with basidiospores of P. helianthi

Heidapian seedlings with four leaves were used for inoculation. Sunflower leaves were inoculated with basidiospores as previously described by Zhao et al. [37], with slight modification. Leaves of sunflowers bearing teliospores of P. helianthi underwent an alternating 24 h wet/24 h dry treatment (soaked in water for 24 h and air-dried for 24 h in a 90 mm plastic Petri dish at 15 °C after being stored at − 20 °C for more than 14 d. The leaves with teliospores were then cut into approximately 2 cm pieces in both length and width, with the teliospores facing upwards, and placed on 1.5% (w/v) water agar in a plastic Petri dish, which was maintained at 15 °C. Teliospores were periodically observed for germination using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100, Japan). Once the teliospores began to germinate, the uncovered Petri dish was placed 20 cm above the seedlings and incubated for one day at 15 °C in a dew chamber. Following incubation, the inoculated sunflower plants were transferred to a growth chamber at a temperature of 20 °C, with a 16 h light (8000 lx) and 8 h dark cycle.

Inoculation of sunflower plants with aeciospores of P. helianthi

Fertilization was performed by transferring nectar from one pycnium to another using a clean plastic inoculation loop. The plant was covered for two days, after which the cover was removed. Approximately seven days after fertilization, aecial cups were excised using a sterile razor blade, and aeciospores were collected to inoculate sunflower plants. Fourteen-day-old sunflower seedlings were inoculated with a suspension of aeciospores (1 × 107 spores·mL−1), which is then evenly brushed onto the leaf surfaces. The inoculated seedlings were kept for 24 h at 10 °C in a dew chamber with 100% humidity in the dark and then transferred to a greenhouse maintained at 15℃, with a 16 h light (8000 lx) and 8 h dark cycle.

Inoculation of sunflower plants with urediospores of P. helianthi

Pure urediospores were mixed with sterile water to create a suspension of 1 × 107 spores·mL−1. Sunflower plants were then inoculated by applying the suspension to both sides of the leaves and were placed in a dew chamber with 100% humidity at 10 °C in the dark for 24 h. Afterward, the plants were transferred to a greenhouse maintained at a temperature of 15 °C, with a 16 h light (8000 lx) and 8 h dark cycle.

Teliospores production

The induction of teliospore production was modified following the procedure previously described by Wang et al. [38]. Once the infection resulted in significant uredial sporulation on the leaves, the greenhouse temperature was increased to 25 °C to promote telia formation.

Observation of P. helianthi infection by scanning electron microscopy

Leaf samples inoculated with basidiospores were collected at 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h, while samples inoculated with aeciospores and urediospores were collected at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h. Additionally, leaf samples with telia, pycnidia, aecia, and uredia were also collected for examination of fungal structures. Telia were sampled 34 days after inoculation with urediospores, pycnidia were collected 7 days after inoculation with basidiospores, aeciospores were sampled 14 days after inoculation with basidiospores, and urediospores were collected 14 days after inoculation with urediospores. All samples were cut into pieces measuring 0.5 × 0.5 cm and quickly placed in 2 mL of a 4.0% glutaraldehyde solution (phosphate buffer, pH 6.8) for more than 6 h at 4 °C. The samples were rinsed with the same buffer three times for 10 min each and dehydrated in a graded series of 2 mL ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, and 90%, each for 15–20 min, followed by 100% ethanol for 30 min, with rinsing three times with distilled water after each ethanol treatment). They were then soaked in 1 mL of isoamyl acetate for 10 min and dried under a CO2 atmosphere for 20 min using the critical point dryer EM CPD300 (Leica, Germany). The samples were subsequently sputter-coated with gold for 80 s using the sputter coater EM ACE200 (Leica, Germany). Finally, the samples were placed on conducting resin, examined, and photographed under a U-8010 field-emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan) at a voltage of 5.0 kV.

Observation of P. helianthi infection by transmission electron microscopy

Leaf samples inoculated with basidiospores, aeciospores and urediospores were collected at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 4 d, 6 d, and 7 d. The TEM samples of inoculated sunflower leaves were immersed in 4.0% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in a 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) for over 12 h at 4 °C. After fixation, the samples were washed four times with the same buffer, with each wash lasting 10 min. The samples were then dehydrated using a series of ethanol solutions with increasing concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, and 90%, each for 15 min, and 100% ethanol for 30 min). Subsequently, the samples were treated with 100% acetone for 20 min. The embedded samples were sliced using an ultramicrotome (EMUC7) to obtain ultrathin sections approximately 1 nm thick. Semithin sections were stained with toluidine blue and examined using a BX51/DP70 microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan). The ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and then observed with an HT-7700 transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan) at an operating voltage of 80.0 kV.

Observation of P. helianthi infection by fluorescence microscope

Leaf samples inoculated with basidiospores were collected at 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h, while samples inoculated with aeciospores and urediospores were collected at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h. The sunflower leaf segments infected by P. helianthi were fixed and decolorized in a decolorizing solution (100% ethyl alcohol and acetic acid glacial, 1:1 vol/vol), followed by overnight immersion in chloral hydrate. The decolorized samples were then autoclaved in 1.5 mL of 0.5 mol·L−1 NaOH for 5 to 6 min. Those samples were washed three times with 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) for 15 min. They were then stained with a 20 µg·mL−1 solution of wheat germ agglutinin conjugated to Calcofluor White (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Using an Olympus BX-53 microscope and CellSens Entry software (version V1.7), the fluorescent-stained samples were examined under blue light excitation (excitation wavelength: 450 to 480 nm, emission wavelength: 515 nm).
