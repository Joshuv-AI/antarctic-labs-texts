Janus microneedles enabling orderly vitiligo treatment through spatiotemporal sequential promoting melanogenesis to transfer via PAR2 activation
Authors: Yan Tang, Tiantian Liu, Chuan Wang, Jiajun Xu, Guozhi Zhong, Qian Jing, Jingxin Liu, Fei Gao, Rujing Wang, Mengnan Zhao, Sanjun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVitiligo is characterized by a complex pathogenesis involving multiple factors, necessitating therapeutic strategies that simultaneously achieve immune regulation, melanocyte regeneration, and melanosome transfer modulation. Here, we designed a Janus microneedle (Janus MN) based on a core-shell bilayer structure, inspired by the melanin metabolism pathway and the stratified architecture of the skin. The shell of the MN was loaded with a complex of hypericin–assembled nanoparticles (HY-NPs) and astragalus polysaccharides, which preferentially dissolve in the epidermal hair follicle region and basal layer. This intervention ameliorated the microenvironment by scavenging reactive oxygen species and inhibiting CD8+ T-cell-mediated immune attacks on melanocytes for repair, while simultaneously activating the MAPK pathway to enhance melanin synthesis in melanocytes for regeneration. The MN core, encapsulated with stabilized trypsin in a hyaluronic acid matrix, targeted the upper epidermal keratinocytes. It activated the PAR2 signaling pathway to enhance melanosome transfer and phagocytosis, thereby promoting visible pigmentation. It integrated dual spatially heterogeneous targets within the epidermis and hair follicles for the first time, achieving multidimensional synergistic intervention through a sequence of “repair, regeneration, and transfer”. Our study provides a novel, spatiotemporally sequential drug delivery strategy for the precise treatment of vitiligo.Keywords: Vitiligo, Janus microneedle, Hypericin-assembled nanoparticles, Trypsin, Repair-regeneration-transfer spatiotemporal sequence

Introduction

Vitiligo is a prevalent autoimmune disorder, characterized by the loss of functional melanocytes (MCs) in hair follicles (HFs) and the epidermis, leading to depigmentation of the skin and exerting profound impacts on patients’ physical and mental health as well as their quality of life [1]. The skin pigmentation process is predominantly mediated by MCs, which determine the color characteristics of the skin and hair through melanin synthesis [2]. From a developmental biology perspective, the MC lineage originates from the melanoblast lineage derived from the neural crest. During embryonic development, these precursor cells undergo a trans-germ layer migration process and ultimately colonize the basal layer of the epidermis and the structures of HFs, especially within HFs [3]. Melanoblasts exhibit significant heterogeneity in the histological compartmentalization of the mammalian HF system. The mature differentiated subpopulation is localized in the HF bulb, directly participating in hair pigment deposition; in contrast, melanocyte stem cells (McSCs) predominantly reside in the HF bulge region and are responsible for the self-renewal of the MC reservoir during hair cycle regeneration [4]. This implies that vitiligo treatment needs to break through the traditional epidermal MC treatment paradigm and urgently requires synchronous targeting of HF MCs.

The currently accepted pathogenesis of vitiligo can be summarized as three spatiotemporally dynamic biological events. Initially, at the onset of vitiligo, oxidative stress [5] and immune system dysregulation [6] lead to erroneous attacks on MCs, damaging not only those in the basal layer but also those in the HFs. Subsequently, the functional loss of MCs in the basal layer and HFs impairs their ability to synthesize melanin normally. Finally, the transport of melanin granules to keratinocytes is obstructed, preventing normal skin pigmentation. It is noteworthy that these three stages are not isolated. Oxidative stress and immune microenvironmental dysregulation exacerbate the survival conditions of MCs, thereby intensifying their functional impairment. Furthermore, if melanin cannot be transported to epidermal keratinocytes, the therapeutic efficacy of vitiligo treatment is severely compromised [7].

Current treatments, including topical corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, and NB-UVB phototherapy, offer short-term repigmentation by suppressing local immune responses or boosting residual MC proliferation. However, their efficacy is limited [8]. Traditional drugs suffer from poor transdermal absorption, failing to reach effective concentrations in the skin or penetrate deeply into HFs. Phototherapy, meanwhile, is constrained by its shallow penetration depth, affecting only the epidermal basal layer and unable to modulate the deep immune microenvironment or MC regeneration niche within HFs. Moreover, these therapies primarily target a single pathological aspect, such as inhibiting T-cell activation or promoting melanogenesis. They fail to regulate the entire cascade of “oxidative stress/immune attack-melanin synthesis-melanin transport” and overlook the importance of melanosome upward transport. Consequently, they suffer from non-durable repigmentation and high relapse rates post-treatment discontinuation [9]. Thus, developing novel therapeutic strategies that provide spatiotemporal synergistic intervention based on “protecting MCs-melanin synthesis-melanin transport” is urgently needed. These strategies should repair oxidative damage, restore immune homeostasis to enhance MC survival, promote stable melanogenesis, and accelerate melanosome transfer into keratinocytes.

Hypericin (HY), a natural photosensitive dianthrone compound with a unique tetracyclic conjugated structure, exhibits significant antioxidant activity and has shown potential in treating skin diseases [10,11]. This aligns with the apoptosis of basal MCs in vitiligo, which is driven by reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. We explored its role in protecting cells from oxidative damage, though its application in vitiligo treatment remains unreported. Our preliminary research, via network pharmacology and pre-experiments, suggests that HY may promote melanogenesis by modulating the MAPK signaling pathway, providing a theoretical basis for its use in vitiligo. In addition, astragalus polysaccharides (APS) function dually as an ideal matrix material for microneedle (MN) fabrication and a bioactive immunomodulator, which can modulate the pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and attenuate CD8+ T cell activation and infiltration in autoimmune pathologies [12,13]. Given that abnormal CD8+ T cell activation in the vitiliginous microenvironment exacerbates MC apoptosis, we combined HY with APS to harness its immunosuppressive capacity. This APS-HY complex forms a multitarget system for “antioxidation-immunomodulation-melanogenesis promotion”, protecting MCs and promoting melanin synthesis when applied to HFs. Ultimately, the upward transfer of melanin granules is critical for epidermal pigmentation [7].

The protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2) signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the melanosome transfer process, with its activation promoting melanosome uptake, transfer and microtubule network reorganization, and it can also relieve inflammatory responses [[14], [15], [16]]. This indicates that enhancing PAR2 activity can facilitate the transfer of the aforementioned melanin granules to the epidermis for subsequent pigmentation. Trypsin, a serine protease that specifically cleaves peptide bonds at the carboxyl side of basic amino acids such as arginine and lysine, has been found to activate PAR2 selectively. This suggests that trypsin immobilized in the epidermis can activate PAR2 to induce melanosome uptake and transfer. Given that the human epidermis and dermis are approximately 1–5 mm thick, with HFs primarily located at a depth of 2–4 mm, and considering that the dermis of a growing mouse reaches about 390 µm with active follicular basal cells at approximately 450–550 µm, where melanin is predominantly synthesized, and that pigmentation typically occurs in the epidermis [17,18], the design of a spatiotemporal drug delivery system by the skin’s spatial architecture is of paramount importance.

In this study, we designed a Janus MN based on a core-shell bilayer structure, guided by a spatiotemporal drug delivery strategy to address pathological progression in stages. This design enabled the sequential “repair-regeneration-transfer” of MCs and melanin in a mouse model (Fig. 1). Initially, the MN shell released HY- assembled nanoparticles (HY-NPs) deep into the HFs, where oxidative stress triggered the release of HY molecules. This alleviated immune attack on MCs, while HY concurrently activated cell viability and melanogenesis. Subsequently, the MN core, localized in the epidermis, released trypsin to activate PAR2 in keratinocytes. Under PAR2 regulation, newly synthesized melanosomes were then transported via tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) from the HF base to the epidermis, completing visible pigmentation. This core-shell structure of Janus MNs is reminiscent of the Roman god Janus. Its asymmetry embodies “dual properties”, with distinct chemical compositions [19,20], morphologies and functionalities on opposing sides, thus achieving spatial duality. Results demonstrated that this Janus MNs design achieved multidimensional synergy in vitiligo treatment for the first time, following a spatiotemporal sequence: temporally, it adheres to the pathological reversal order of “repair first, regenerate next, and transfer last”; spatially, it matches the cellular targets from HF MCs to epidermal keratinocytes. This spatiotemporal sequential drug delivery system not only provides a novel multidimensional synergistic platform for vitiligo treatment but also offers an innovative paradigm for developing transdermal drug delivery systems for other pigmentation disorders.

Preparation of HY-NPs

In a 1.5 mL EP tube, 2 mg HY was precisely weighed. 1 mL organic phase, composed of 0.5 ml dichloromethane and 0.5 ml anhydrous ethanol, was added to the tube. The mixture was sonicated to completely dissolve the HY. Subsequently, the organic phase was slowly introduced into 5 mL aqueous phase while vortexing for 2 min to form a crude emulsion. The crude emulsion was then transferred to a cell ultrasonic pulverizer (Ningbo Xinyi, JY92-IIN) and sonicated for 25 min at 30% power, using a pulse mode of 5 s on and 5 s off. Finally, 5 mL HY-NPs was obtained by transferring the oil-in-water (O/W) nanoemulsion to a rotary evaporator and removing the organic phase at 37 °C (−0.1 MPa) for 3 min.

Preparation of Janus MNs

A PDMS template was filled with 5% APS solution loaded with HY-NPs, followed by 20 min vacuum treatment to promote solution infiltration into the micropores. Residual excess solution on the surface was gently scraped away, and the template was dried at 65 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, a 20% HA gel solution (MW: 100–200 kDa) containing trypsin was added to the mold. After an additional 15 min of vacuum treatment, the gel that had not been pushed into the pinholes was removed by scraping. A PVP-PVA gel solution (10% w/v, 1:1) was used as the soluble backing layer. The mold was allowed to dry naturally overnight to obtain the Janus MNs.

Morphologies and mechanical properties

All MN samples were sputter-coated with gold prior to scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observation (ZEISS, EVO10). A core-shell Janus MN was fabricated using FITC dye instead of trypsin. Subsequently, a row of MNs was excised with scissors and mounted onto a glass slide. The core-shell structure of the MN was then examined via a laser confocal microscope (Leica SP8 ATE).

Then, the MN was placed on the sample stage of the Texture Analyzer (Shimadzu, EZ-X) with its orientation upward. Subsequently, the tip was compressed steadily at a speed of 0.01 mm/s [23,24]. To create a pressure versus displacement curve, the IPP6.0 program monitored the changes in MN height within a pressure range of 0 to 20 N. The mechanical load borne by a single MN was calculated using three independent patches, each arranged in a 12 × 12 MN array.

Penetration capability

To evaluate the skin penetration capability of the Janus MNs, in vivo penetration tests were conducted on the dorsal skin of mice. After MN patches were attached and peeled off, trypan blue solution was smeared onto the treated skin sites. After 1 min, the excess dye solution on the skin surface was removed by rinsing with isopropanol. The formation of microchannels within the skin was then visually confirmed by the presence of distinct blue-stained puncture points, indicative of successful MN penetration.

Puncture depth measurement

Fresh porcine skin was collected for penetration depth experiments. The core region of MNs was labeled with FITC, and the shell region was identified via the inherent fluorescence of HY. MNs were pressed tightly against porcine skin to complete skin insertion. The penetrated skin tissues were isolated, embedded in OCT medium, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen sections were prepared longitudinally using a cryostat, and penetration depth was determined under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6B). Fully dried MNs were inserted into porcine skin, and Z-axis scanning was performed at 10 µm intervals via laser confocal microscopy (FV4000) for subsequent three-dimensional image reconstruction [25].

Transdermal release

Three female SPF-grade C57BL/6 mice were employed for the ex vivo skin penetration study. The mice were euthanized, and the dorsal skin was excised and carefully trimmed to remove excess subcutaneous fat, which was then used as the skin barrier for the penetration assay. In the Franz diffusion cell’s receiving compartment, 4 ml release medium (PBS buffer containing 20% PEG-400 and 20% ethanol) was introduced. Mouse skin, with the stratum corneum facing upward, was affixed to the upper part of the receiving cell using a clip. A single Janus MN patch or 1 ml Janus MNs precursor gel (NPs + APS/trypsin + HA) was applied separately onto the excised skin, with a dosage of 16.33 ± 2.04 µg HY and 84 nmol trypsin per specimen. Magnetic stirring was conducted at 300 rpm. At predetermined time intervals of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6 and 12 h, 0.2 ml receiving medium was sampled, and an equivalent volume of fresh release medium was replenished. The samples were filtered through a 0.22 µm filter, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was employed for analysis [26,27]. Microsphere phagocytosis assay

HaCaT cells were seeded onto slides at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The cells were then treated with the designated administration for 2 h. Subsequently, carboxylic acid-modified fluorescent microspheres (Merck, 1 µm in diameter, emitting yellow-green fluorescence) were added at a density of 1 × 108 per well and incubated at 37 °C for 22 h. After incubation, the plates were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and washed again with PBS three times. The cells were then stained with DAPI, treated with an anti-fluorescence quencher (Beyotime, P0126-5 ml), sealed, and stored at 4 °C in the dark [32]. The samples were subsequently observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6B).

Melanosome transfer evaluation

Melanosome transfer between MCs and keratinocytes is a critical process occurring in skin tissues. To elucidate the impact of Janus MNs on melanin transfer, we established two distinct co-culture methods.

In the first method, B16F10 melanoma cells and HaCaT keratinocytes were co-cultured in 6-well plates at a 1:4 ratio (Corning, Lowell, MA) overnight [33]. Subsequently, trypsin was applied for 6 h, followed by fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, drying, and gold sputtering. The cellular interactions were then visualized using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) to observe the connections between B16F10 and HaCaT cells.

For the second method, a co-culture system was established using six-well plate chambers (Corning, Lowell, MA) [34]. Initially, the chambers were inverted, and B16F10 cells were seeded onto the lower surface at a density of 1 × 104 cells/cm2. After cell adhesion, the chambers were re-inverted, and HaCaT cells were seeded at the same density (1 × 104 cells/cm2) into the chambers. This arrangement allowed both cell types to adhere to the chamber walls on opposite sides, facilitating intercellular communication through 0.4 µm membrane pores. The insert wells were supplied with HaCaT-specific medium (1.5 ml/well), while the bottom wells were cultured with DMEM medium for B16F10 cells (2.6 ml/well). Extracts from the MN core and shell layers were applied to the upper and bottom layers of the chambers, respectively, according to the experimental design. After 3 d of co-culture, the intracellular melanosome content in B16F10 and HaCaT cells was independently assessed by cell digestion and subsequent analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was meticulously extracted from the cells utilizing the Trizol reagent (Acres Biologicals). The exact concentration of the extracted total RNA was then quantified using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and uniformly diluted with enzyme-free water to a final concentration of 200 ng/µl. Next, cDNA was synthesized using the EvoM-MLV reverse transcription kit. Ultimately, the relative mRNA levels were evaluated by employing SYBR Green Pro HS in conjunction with specific primer-based quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays. The detailed qRT-PCR procedures were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from the cells using a lysis buffer. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0010S). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on a 10% polyacrylamide gel and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were incubated with specific monoclonal antibodies against HO-1, MITF, TYR, p38, p-P38, ERK, P-ERK, P-JNK, JNK, PAR2, CD8α, GAPDH or β-actin overnight at 4 °C (dilution ratio 1:1000). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (Servicebio) at room temperature for 2 h. Protein expression was visualized using a chemiluminescence assay (Uelandy).

Depigmentation area score assessment

All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Laboratory Animal Research Center of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (2025061). C57BL/6 mice (4-week-old females, weighing approximately 13 g) were procured from Shanghai Spectrum Laboratory Animals Co. A mouse model of vitiligo was established using 40% monobenzone, as previously described. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 0.5% pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 50 mg/kg. The dorsal skin was shaved to expose a 2 cm × 2 cm area, which was subsequently treated daily with a thin layer of 40% monobenzone for 2 weeks. During this period, mice were allowed free access to food and water. After the establishment of the vitiligo mouse model, mice were randomly assigned into six groups (n = 6 per group) according to the average depigmentation degree: control group, 1% tacrolimus ointment group, blank MN group, core MN group, shell MN group and Janus MN group. The treatment regimen was as follows: every 3 d, the tacrolimus-positive group received a thin layer of 1% tacrolimus ointment applied to the depigmented skin lesions, the MN groups received two MNs administered to the dorsal region per mouse, and the control group received a saline coating. Treatment continued for 4 weeks. The severity of vitiligo was assessed by calculating the percentage of white spots in the exposed dorsal skin area using the following scoring system: 0% depigmentation, score 0; >5% depigmentation, score 1; >5%−25% depigmentation, score 2; >25%−50% depigmentation, score 3; >50%−75% depigmentation, score 4; and 75%−100% depigmentation, score 5 [35].

Histopathological evaluation of melanogenesis, melanin transfer and immune microenvironment

Following euthanasia at 4 weeks post-treatment, dorsal skin tissues (2 × 2 cm) from vitiligo model mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (>24 h) and processed for paraffin embedding, sectioning and staining by Chengdu Aochuang Biotechnology Co. H&E staining was employed to analyze skin thickness and HF morphology, with mature HFs and melanin deposition serving as morphological indicators of MC activation. Melanin synthesis and distribution were specifically assessed via Fontana-Masson staining, which exploits melanin’s argentaffin properties (reduction of silver ammonia to metallic silver). This method enabled dual quantification: (1) melanin density within HFs to evaluate melanogenesis efficiency, and (2) melanin granule distribution in the epidermis to confirm keratinocyte-targeted transfer. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining further detected CD8+ T-cell infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ) to assess immune microenvironment regulation. In addition, the spleen index was measured to assess systemic immune hyperactivation status.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Dorsal skin tissues (1 × 1 cm) were excised from both the control group (Ctrl, treated with saline) and the Janus MNs-treated group (Janus MNs) of vitiligo model mice, with three biological replicates per group. The harvested tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA sequencing by Novogene Co., Ltd.

For bioinformatics analysis, raw sequencing data were processed with Fastp to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads (Q < 20). Clean reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were normalized based on FPKM values, and differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 with the threshold set at |log2(fold change)| >1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis via the Novo Magic Cloud Platform (Novogene), with significance defined as P < 0.05. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks of target genes were retrieved from the STRING database (version 12.0) using a confidence threshold >0.4, and core network modules were visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.7.1). Fourteen key genes significantly associated with melanogenesis, MC migration, antioxidant response, and immune regulation (P < 0.05) were selected for hierarchical clustering analysis, and their standardized expression profiles were visualized as a heatmap using Origin (2012).

Flow cytometry analysis

Dissociative enzymes were employed to digest the 2 × 2 cm dorsal skin samples from mice at 37 °C for 2 h. Spleen tissues from each group were similarly processed to obtain single-cell suspensions enriched with immune cells. The resultant single-cell suspensions were subsequently filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (Biosharp, BS-70-XBS), washed three times with PBS, and incubated with a panel of fluorescence-conjugated antibodies. The phenotypes of mouse immune cells were characterized using various combinations of the following fluorescence-labeled anti-mouse monoclonal antibodies: CD3, CD4, CD8, CD11c, CD80, CD86, CD69, IFN-γ and Granzyme B. The stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using a FACSCanto II instrument (BD Biosciences, USA) and the FlowJo (v10.8.1).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with a minimum of three parallel samples per group. The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (10.1.2), employing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. A P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance, while a P < 0.01 was deemed to represent highly significant differences.

Results and discussion

Self-assembly of HY into HY-NPs

Localizing the drug at the MN tip maximizes payload, demanding complete solubility and uniform dispersion in the MN solution to eliminate any undissolved heterogeneity. To improve the loading efficiency of HY in MNs, we avoided using extra excipients and directly dissolved HY in the organic phase [36]. Subsequently, we successfully fabricated HY-NPs via the emulsion solvent evaporation method (Fig. 2A). During self-assembly, HY precipitated in water yet dissolved in an ethanol-dichloromethane (1:1, v/v) phase to yield a ruby-red solution. Ultrasonic dispersion of this organic phase into an aqueous medium instantaneously generated an O/W emulsion that remained stable for ≥5 min (Fig. 2B). Subsequent vacuum evaporation of the solvent afforded HY-NPs, demonstrating that HY not only stabilizes the emulsion but also drives nanoparticle formation through O/W templated self-assembly. As shown in Fig. 2C, the resultant HY-NPs exhibited uniform dispersion compared with free drug, with a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 57.27 ± 4.25 nm, a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.324 ± 0.089 (Fig. 2D), and a zeta potential of −15.53 ± 0.13 mV (Fig. 2E). The morphology of HY-NPs was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), revealing a remarkably uniform spherical structure and a distinctly well-defined surface morphology (Fig. 2F). Stability studies revealed that HY-NPs maintained a stable size profile over a period of 30 d (Fig. 2G), thereby demonstrating their remarkable stability.

To further elucidate the self-assembly mechanism, ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometry was first employed to detect the absorption wavelengths of HY and HY-NPs. A distinct red shift was observed for HY-NPs compared to free HY (Fig. 2H), indicating enhanced electronic interactions in the self-assembled nanostructures. Subsequently, ¹H NMR spectroscopy analysis was performed (Fig. 2I). A minute downfield shift was observed in the phenolic hydroxyl peaks of HY-NPs (δ 14.66→14.67 ppm; δ 14.03→14.04 ppm), while other characteristic groups remained essentially unchanged. This suggests that the phenolic hydroxyl groups likely participate in the self-assembly process through hydrogen bonding interactions. In summary, the self-assembly mechanism of HY-NPs may be through hydrogen bonding interactions between HY phenolic hydroxyl groups or π-π conjugation, and the self-assembly process did not change the main chemical structure of HY. This indicates that the nanosizing process did not alter the fundamental chemical structure of HY. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis further confirmed that, compared with free HY, HY-NPs exhibited a smoother thermal flow curve during programmed heating (Fig. 2K). This suggests that the nanosizing treatment significantly enhanced the thermal stability of the system.

Building on the successful fabrication of HY-NPs, this study systematically evaluated their drug-loading performance and H2O2-responsive release behavior. HPLC analysis revealed that HY-NPs exhibited remarkable drug-loading capacity, with an encapsulation efficiency as high as 95.48% ± 0.03% and a corresponding drug concentration of 381.91 ± 10.41 µg/ml (Fig. S1A–S1C). TEM observations revealed that HY-NPs’ morphology was influenced by the concentration of H2O2 solution. Specifically, HY-NPs were more prone to rupture in high-concentration H2O2, thus enabling drug release (Fig. 2L). To explore the oxidative stress-responsive properties of HY-NPs, their drug release profiles were investigated in pH 7.4 PBS simulating pathological microenvironments containing different concentrations of H2O2 (0, 0.1, 0.5 and 1 mM). The results revealed that the drug release behavior of HY-NPs exhibited a prominent concentration-dependent response to H2O2. Under 1 mM H2O2 conditions, the cumulative release rate reached 69.8% ± 2.1% within 24 h, which was markedly higher than that of the H2O2-free group (34.5% ± 1.7%) (Fig. 2M). This indicates that H2O2 can trigger drug release from the nanoparticles. This feature is highly consistent with the pathological characteristic of elevated H2O2 levels in the local oxidative stress of vitiligo-affected skin, suggesting that HY-NPs have the potential to target pathological microenvironments.

Kinetic fitting of the cumulative release curves (Fig. S1D) revealed that the first-order kinetic model provided a significantly better fit than the zero-order model, Higuchi model and Ritger-Peppas model. This indicates that the drug release rate is primarily driven by the concentration of the remaining drug, consistent with a diffusion-dominated release mechanism. This may be attributed to the H2O2-induced disassembly of the nanoparticle structure, which facilitates the continuous diffusion of the drug from the matrix. Given the hydrophobic nature of HY, direct loading onto MNs is challenging. Moreover, considering the limited drug-loading capacity of MNs, loading HY particles without excipients into MNs can help enhance the drug-loading efficiency.

Amelioration of redox environment by HY-NPs

Elevated ROS levels in the vitiligo microenvironment induce MC apoptosis, and oxidative stress is a key etiological factor in the pathogenesis of vitiligo. To evaluate the regulatory effect of HY-NPs on the oxidative stress microenvironment in vitiligo, we assessed their antioxidant capacity using DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays (Fig. 3A & B).

HY-NPs exhibited concentration-dependent radical scavenging activity across the tested range of 100–800 µM in both assays. In the DPPH assay (Fig. 3A), HY-NPs showed scavenging activity comparable to that of the positive control Vc at higher concentrations, and significantly outperformed free HY at the same concentrations. In the ABTS assay (Fig. 3B), HY-NPs also displayed notable scavenging activity that increased with concentration. Additionally, free APS showed marginal radical scavenging activity in both assays, and its combination with HY-NPs did not compromise the inherent antioxidant efficacy of HY-NPs. The superior antioxidant performance of HY-NPs relative to free HY may be attributed to their nanoscale structure, which provides a larger specific surface area and thereby enhances contact with free radicals.

Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), a well-known antioxidant enzyme [37], was further investigated to elucidate the antioxidant potential of HY-NPs. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was used to induce excessive ROS production in B16F10 cells (Fig. S2). Subsequently, the effects of HY-NPs on HO-1 were examined at both the mRNA and protein expression levels. At the mRNA level (Fig. 3C), HY-NP treatment partially restored HO-1 expression under oxidative stress, with no significant difference compared with the positive control. In parallel, Western blotting analysis (Fig. 3D) further confirmed that HY-NPs significantly upregulated HO-1 protein expression under oxidative stress, exhibiting a therapeutic efficacy comparable to Vc. Collectively, these results demonstrated that HY-NPs possess a regulatory effect on the HO-1 pathway equivalent to that of Vc. Therefore, as an antioxidant agent, HY-NPs hold considerable potential for alleviating oxidative stress in vitiligo when incorporated into MNs. Such alleviation of oxidative stress is conducive to promoting the viability of MCs.

Melanogenesis-promoting effects of HY-NPs

To investigate the melanogenesis-promoting effects of HY-NPs, we utilized the melanin-producing characteristics of mouse melanoma cells (B16F10) as a model for MCs [38]. MTT assays demonstrated that HY-NPs had no significant impact on B16F10 cell viability within the concentration range of 2.5–60 µM (Fig. S3A). Confocal imaging revealed a concentration-dependent cellular uptake of HY-NPs, with red fluorescence intensity in the PI channel progressively increasing as the drug concentration was raised from 5 to 30 µM (Fig. S3B-S3C).

Given its established efficacy in promoting melanogenesis, 8-MOP was employed as the positive control [39]. B16F10 cells were treated with 20 µM 8-MOP and 10–30 µM HY-NPs for 48 h. Subsequent optical microscopy imaging and melanin content determination using the NaOH dissolution method (405 nm) showed that 20 µM HY-NPs increased melanin production by 21% compared to the control group, while 30 µM HY-NPs achieved a 36% increase (Fig. 3E & F). Notably, the melanin production by 20 µM HY-NPs was comparable to that of the 8-MOP group. Additionally, TYR activity assays (475 nm) using the levodopa method indicated that HY-NPs enhanced TYR activity in a concentration-dependent manner, with the 30 µM HY-NPs group achieving activity levels similar to those of the 8-MOP group (Fig. 3G).

We further examined the expression of microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) and TYR in B16F10 cells treated with 8-MOP and HY-NPs using immunofluorescence confocal microscopy (Figs. 3H, I and S4C-S4D). MITF is a key regulator of MC development and function, controlling the expression of various genes essential for normal melanin synthesis [40]. TYR, as the rate-limiting enzyme in melanogenesis, catalyzes the initial steps of melanin production [41]. Results showed that both 8-MOP and HY-NPs significantly upregulated the expression of MITF and TYR, with a more pronounced effect on MITF. Flow cytometry quantitatively confirmed these findings, demonstrating that both 8-MOP and HY-NPs enhanced the activity of MITF and TYR (Fig. 3J & K). To further elucidate the effects of 8-MOP and HY-NPs on melanogenesis-related proteins and genes, we compared the expression of MITF and TYR proteins, as well as the mRNA levels of MITF, TYR, TYRP1, and TYRP2, in B16F10 cells treated with 20 µM 8-MOP and 20 µM HY-NPs for 48 h. TYRP1 and TYRP2, as key auxiliary enzymes in melanin synthesis, together with TYR, form the core catalytic network for melanogenesis [42]. Western blotting analysis and quantitative PCR revealed that both 8-MOP and HY-NPs significantly upregulated the protein expression of MITF and TYR (Fig. 3L & M), as well as the mRNA levels of MITF, TYR, TYRP1 and TYRP2 (Fig. 3N), compared to the control group. These findings suggest that HY-NPs enhance the expression of key transcription factors and enzymes involved in melanogenesis.

Based on network pharmacology predictions (Fig. S5) and literature integration [43], we hypothesized that HY might promote melanogenesis by modulating the MAPK signaling pathway. To validate this hypothesis, we systematically evaluated the effects of HY-NPs on MAPK pathway activity and downstream melanogenesis-related factors. Using Western blot analysis, we semi-quantitatively assessed the phosphorylation levels of core MAPK pathway members (p38, ERK and JNK) and the protein expression of MITF and TYR (Fig. 3O & P). These results showed that HY-NPs dose-dependently activated the MAPK pathway, with significant increases in the phosphorylation ratios of p38, ERK and JNK. This indicates that HY-NPs activate the MAPK pathway by phosphorylating its members. The expression levels of MITF and TYR were positively correlated with HY-NPs concentration and synchronized with the phosphorylation levels of MAPK, suggesting that MAPK pathway activation drives TYR expression by enhancing the transcriptional activity of MITF.

In summary, HY-NPs upregulate HO-1 expression, scavenge free radicals, exert antioxidant effects, and improve the survival state of MCs. They also dose-dependently activate the MAPK signaling pathway, leading to the upregulation of MITF and TYR, thereby systematically regulating melanogenesis and achieving a synergistic “antioxidant-melanogenesis-promoting transfer” effect (Fig. 3Q). This discovery provides a molecular basis for the application of HY-NPs in pigment regeneration therapy. Therefore, incorporating HY-NPs into MNs holds promise for the multidimensional treatment of vitiligo through “antioxidant-melanogenesis-promoting transfer” mechanisms.

Preparation and comprehensive characterization of HY-NPs-loaded MNs

To achieve optimal penetration and drug delivery in mouse skin, Janus MNs were meticulously designed and fabricated using a two-step vacuum casting method (Fig. 4A). The shell layer was fabricated by mixing APS with HY-NPs. Once the mixture had dried, the trypsin-hyaluronic acid gel was cast as the core layer. The base layer was made of PVP-PVA. SEM analysis confirmed that the Janus MNs exhibited a 100% de-molding efficiency, with the needle tips maintaining their integrity and uniform shape throughout the fabrication process (Fig. 4B). Each MN featured a quadrangular pyramidal shaft, arranged in a 12 × 12 array. The base diameter of each needle was 400 µm, with a height of 800 µm. Each needle has a base spacing of 200 µm and a tip spacing of 600 µm. These dimensions of the MNs were specifically tailored to match the structural characteristics of the HFs in mouse skin.

To visualize the core-shell structure of the MNs, the core was labeled with FITC, while the shell was labeled with HY-NPs, which exhibit intrinsic red fluorescence. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was employed to capture vertical cross-sectional images of the Janus MNs. The images revealed that the red fluorescence from HY-NPs was localized in the outer shell, while the green fluorescence from FITC was confined to the core (Fig. 4C), thereby confirming the successful construction of the core-shell structure. Consistent with these findings, cross-sectional observations showed pure green fluorescence within the core of the MNs (Fig. 4D), with HY fluorescence confined to the shell layer. Additionally, Fig. 4E displays fluorescence images of the MNs’ cross-sections, revealing red fluorescent square rings at different heights within the shell region. Moreover, the shell thickness was sufficiently substantial to accommodate drug encapsulation (Fig. S6). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the fabricated MNs possess a well-defined core-shell structure, with the shell layer containing HY-NPs (Fig. 4F & G). This design is tailored to meet the requirements of pre-releasing HY in the HF regions affected by vitiligo, followed by the exposure and subsequent release of the core drugs.

Penetrability and biocompatibility of Janus MNs

The mechanical properties and skin-penetration compatibility of MNs were subsequently characterized. We fabricated HA-MNs using the same method. Under identical pressure, the displacement of Janus MNs was significantly lower than that of HA-MNs, indicating that Janus MNs underwent less deformation than HA-MNs. This suggests that Janus MNs possess superior mechanical strength compared to HA-MNs (Fig. 4H). It has been reported that MNs with a single-needle hardness greater than 0.08 N can penetrate the skin [44]. This finding indicates that the Janus MNs prepared by this method have sufficient stratum corneum penetration capability and are significantly stronger than HA-MNs. To further investigate the skin-penetrating effect of MNs, we performed trypan blue staining on mouse skin (Fig. S7). Trypan blue staining revealed well-defined microchannels across the mouse skin following MN application, confirming 100% penetration efficiency.

Both HA and APS are safe and biocompatible materials for fabricating MN matrices [[45], [46], [47]]. After application, the microchannels created by MN penetration gradually closed over time. Mild erythema appeared at 1 h, which was attributed to transient skin responses to penetration, and the skin fully recovered to its normal state at 2 h. By 24 h, the micropores completely disappeared without causing bruising, swelling or persistent irritation (Fig. 4I). These results confirm that the MNs cause only minimally invasive damage that resolves rapidly. Furthermore, MN extracts maintained high viability in HaCaT cells at 24, 48 and 72 h, demonstrating excellent cytocompatibility (Fig. S8A). The scratch wound-healing assay further revealed that MN extracts, especially Janus MNs, significantly promoted HaCaT cell migration and repair (Fig. S8B-S8C). Meanwhile, the hygroscopicity test verified that the Janus MNs exhibited moderate moisture absorption, with no obvious weight change or deliquescence after storage in a desiccator for 30 days (Fig. S9). In addition, APS exerted no discernible impact on either the colloidal stability or the structural integrity of HY-NPs (Fig. S10).

Janus MNs enable spatially programmed drug delivery

To investigate the retention of MNs in skin, Janus MNs were labeled with FITC for the core layer and HY-NPs for the shell layer, and then inserted into porcine skin. Cryosection images revealed that the shell layer (red) penetrated deeper than the core layer (green), with the red layer extending approximately 600 µm into the skin and the green layer residing at a depth of about 330 µm (Fig. S11). This observation suggests that the core-shell structure leads to spatially distinct drug distribution within the skin. Specifically, the shell layer reached the HF region of the skin, while the core layer was positioned within the epidermis and dermis. Generally, in vivo skin is more flexible, and the penetration depth achieved in human skin may not match that in porcine skin. Therefore, the depth of MN molds can be adjusted according to the actual skin structure to develop MN patches suitable for vitiligo treatment. Tomographic imaging showed that each MN created a square pore in the cross-section of porcine skin, reaching a depth of 640 µm (Fig. 4J), which corresponds to the region where HFs are densely distributed. As shown in the 3D reconstructed side-view images of MNs (Fig. 4K), the overall penetration depth reached around 640 µm. Specifically, the core layer penetrated to nearly 300 µm, while the shell layer achieved a depth of 640 µm. The lengths of the shell and core layers in these MNs precisely correspond to the depths at which they penetrate the skin.

In mouse skin, when HFs are in the anagen phase, the dermal thickness doubles to approximately 390 µm, and the depth of HFs becomes more pronounced, with active HF basal cells reaching depths of 450–550 µm [17]. This suggests that drugs promoting HF or MC activity should be precisely delivered to a subcutaneous depth of 500–600 µm to enhance melanin production. Conversely, drugs facilitating melanin transfer should be targeted to a depth of 0–400 µm to promote melanin migration from the HF layer to the epidermis, thereby enhancing skin pigmentation. The Janus MNs we developed, as shown in Fig. 4K, demonstrated distinct penetration layers corresponding to the depths of HFs in mouse skin. This validates that our Janus MNs can deliver drugs specifically to the HF region and the basal and cortical medullary keratinocyte layers, achieving efficient site-specific drug delivery.

Janus MNs enable temporally sequential drug delivery

To study the dissolution of MN patches in mice, MNs were inserted into mouse skin and retrieved at different time points for optical microscopy imaging of both the overall (Fig. 4L) and localized views (Figs. 4M and S12). The results clearly showed that the outer shell layer commenced progressive flattening and dissolution from the 30-min time point onward, whereas the inner core remained substantially intact. This sequential dissolution of Janus MNs achieves temporally controlled drug delivery.

At 37 °C, the activity of free trypsin declined gradually in a time-dependent manner and dropped by nearly 50% within 1 h. However, when trypsin was mixed with HA, its activity was significantly enhanced. After 24 h, the activity retention rate of trypsin in Trypsin-HA gels increased from 1795 ± 627 U (free trypsin) to 4109 ± 446 U, representing a 2.3-fold enhancement (Fig. 4N). This improvement is likely attributed to the protective microenvironment formed by the large molecular structure of HA, which shields trypsin from external factors such as high temperatures, thereby stabilizing its activity. Consequently, HA was selected as the core material for Janus MNs.

To investigate the transdermal diffusion profile of drugs from MN patches, we compared the transdermal release of HY and trypsin from Janus MNs and the raw material gel mixture used to make Janus MNs using a Franz diffusion cell (Fig. 4O). HPLC analysis revealed that each Janus MN patch contained 16.33 ± 2.04 µg HY. The results indicated that the release of HY and trypsin from MNs was more than twice as fast as from the gel (Fig. 4P), likely because MNs penetrate the skin and provide better contact with the release medium, facilitating drug dissolution and release. In contrast, the gel has difficulty permeating the stratum corneum. Furthermore, HY from the outer shell of the Janus MNs was released earlier than trypsin from the inner core. Within 1 h, trypsin release was negligible, while HY release reached approximately 18.5%, suggesting that the drug in the core layer is released later than that in the shell layer. These findings confirm that our Janus MNs are designed to achieve sequential drug delivery. Kinetic fitting results showed that drug release from the MNs followed a first-order kinetics model (Fig. S13), indicating that drug release is proportional to the drug concentration in the needle matrix.

These results demonstrate that Janus MNs can create a high-concentration HY environment in the HFs region by initially releasing the shell-layer drug. This environment not only improves the survival conditions of MCs by reversing immune suppression but also promotes melanin production by activating MCs. Subsequently, the gradual release of trypsin from the core layer in the epidermis and dermis activates PAR2 function, facilitating the upward transfer of melanin and ultimately achieving spatially controlled treatment for vitiligo.

Shell MNs-driven antioxidation and melanogenesis

The antioxidant capacity of Janus MNs was primarily attributed to Shell MNs, as evidenced by the DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging assays (Fig. S14). The free radical scavenging ability of Shell MNs exhibited a concentration-dependent enhancement, thereby demonstrating their potential for antioxidant applications. The core component HY-NPs exhibited melanogenesis-promoting activity comparable to that of the positive control 8-MOP at a concentration of 20 µM, with a 21% increase relative to the control group (Fig. 3E). This effect was associated with the significant upregulation of key regulatory factors such as MITF and TYR via the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 3O & P). When HY-NPs were encapsulated within Shell MNs and precisely delivered to the MCs, they could accumulate locally at high concentrations, potentially creating a “drug reservoir” effect and releasing HY molecules to promote melanogenesis. Driven by the elevated H2O2 levels in the vitiligo microenvironment, HY-NPs could continuously release the drug in response to the local H2O2 concentration, thus achieving a sustained-release effect. Compared with the transient exposure pattern of conventional 8‑MOP administration, such site‑specific delivery of melanogenesis‑promoting agents to the immediate vicinity of MCs better matches the natural biological process of sequential melanin synthesis and transfer.

Core MNs-promoted melanosome transfer

Trypsin, a serine protease, activates downstream signaling pathways by cleaving PAR2, thereby modulating cellular phagocytic functions [48]. It has been found to regulate the rate at which melanosomes are transferred from MCs to keratinocytes, thereby modulating the amount of melanin in human epidermis and determining the depth of human skin color [49]. However, the potential of trypsin in the treatment of vitiligo remains to be explored. In this study, we innovatively encapsulated trypsin within the core of Janus MNs, leveraging HA to protect trypsin from inactivation and to delay its release, precisely targeting the spinous and granular layers of keratinocytes. This design not only prevented premature degradation of trypsin in the upper skin layers (with a 2.3-fold increase in enzyme activity retention) (Fig. 4N) but also integrated PAR2 pathway activation with the intervention of melanosome transfer impairment in vitiligo, overcoming the limitations of traditional therapies that overlook pigment transfer and providing a new strategy for synergistic treatment. To determine the optimal trypsin concentration, we first assessed the cytotoxicity of various trypsin concentrations (0–5000 nM) on HaCaT cells using MTT assays after 24 h of treatment (Fig. 5A). Low concentrations of trypsin (≤500 nM) showed negligible cytotoxicity, with cell viability maintained above 90%, whereas high concentrations (1000–5000 nM) significantly reduced cell viability to below 70%, likely due to excessive non-specific proteolysis. Consequently, concentrations ranging from 0 to 500 nM were selected for subsequent experiments as the safe concentration range. Subsequent qRT-PCR analysis revealed that trypsin upregulated PAR2 gene expression in HaCaT cells in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5B). Treatment with 50 and 100 nM trypsin increased PAR2 expression by 74% compared to the control group, while 500 nM trypsin elevated PAR2 expression by 129%, indicating that trypsin activates the PAR2 signaling pathway through transcriptional regulation. Based on these findings, 50 nM trypsin was chosen for further experiments. According to the PCR results, the PAR2 gene expression level peaked when HaCaT cells were treated with 50 nM trypsin for 6 h (Fig. S15). Based on this experimental result, 50 nM trypsin treatment for 6 h was chosen as the standardized experimental condition for subsequent studies.

To investigate whether trypsin encapsulated in MNs retained its ability to activate PAR2, Western blot analysis was performed (Fig. 5C). Both free trypsin (50 nM) and trypsin-loaded Core MNs significantly enhanced PAR2 protein expression, increasing levels by 62% and 55% compared with the control group, respectively. These results confirmed that MN encapsulation did not impair the ability of trypsin to induce PAR2 expression.

To validate the specificity of PAR2 activation by Core MNs, HaCaT cells were co-treated with the PAR2-specific agonist 2f-LIGRL-NH2 (10 µM) and the PAR2-selective antagonist P2pal-18S (20 µM) (Fig. 5D & E). Compared with the control group, the PAR2 agonist alone and Core MNs treatment increased PAR2 protein expression by 61% and 41%, respectively. In contrast, pre-treatment with the PAR2 antagonist completely abolished the upregulation of PAR2 induced by Core MNs, directly confirming that Core MNs enhance PAR2 expression specifically through trypsin-mediated PAR2 activation.

To directly visualize and quantify the effects of PAR2 activation on keratinocyte phagocytosis, 1.0 µm fluorescent polystyrene beads were used as melanosome-mimicking particles. CLSM showed that both the PAR2 agonist and Core MNs treatment markedly enhanced the uptake of fluorescent beads by HaCaT cells (Fig. 5F). Quantitative flow cytometric analysis further supported these results: the agonist group and Core MNs group exhibited 83% and 32% increases in intracellular bead fluorescence intensity relative to the control group, respectively (Fig. 5G & H). Pre-treatment with the PAR2 inhibitor P2pal-18S significantly attenuated bead internalization, demonstrating that Core MNs enhance the phagocytic capacity of HaCaT cells in a PAR2‑dependent manner, thereby facilitating the uptake and transfer of melanin-containing melanosomes.

Mechanisms of melanosome transfer

In the physiological architecture of the skin, MCs and keratinocytes are spatially segregated [50]. To elucidate the intercellular interactions involved in melanosome transfer, B16F10 melanoma cells and HaCaT keratinocytes were co-cultured on coverslips at a ratio of 1:4. Here, HaCaT cells were employed as a model for epidermal keratinocytes to evaluate their capacity for melanosome transfer. Using B16F10 cells as a melanogenic model and co-culturing them with HaCaT cells, we observed that B16F10 cells treated with HY and trypsin transferred melanin to HaCaT cells (Fig. 5I). The results illustrate that melanin synthesized by B16F10 cells primarily accumulates around the cell nucleus, with a minor portion of melanin being transferred from the synapse to HaCaT cells or extracellularly. In contrast, no accumulation of melanin granules was observed in untreated HaCaT cells (Fig. S16). FESEM revealed the formation of TNTs between B16F10 and HaCaT cells following trypsin treatment (Fig. 5J). Given that MCs exhibit low expression of PAR2 receptors, whereas keratinocytes display high levels of PAR2 receptor expression [51], it is hypothesized that trypsin activates PAR2 in HaCaT cells, prompting these cells to signal a “need for melanin” to B16F10 cells. This signaling cascade likely induces B16F10 cells to form TNTs that connect with HaCaT cells, facilitating the transfer of melanosomes from B16F10 cells to keratinocytes, ultimately leading to pigmentation (Fig. 5J). This finding suggests that trypsin may promote melanin transfer through phagocytosis and TNT processes (Fig. 5K).

To simulate the dynamic melanosome metabolism in the skin, a two-layer Transwell co-culture model was established (Fig. 5L). The upper layer, comprising HaCaT cells, mimicked the epidermis and was treated with Core MNs to activate PAR2. The lower layer, consisting of B16F10 cells, represented the MCs and was treated with Shell MNs to promote melanin synthesis. After treatment, both B16F10 and HaCaT cells were harvested, imaged to observe melanin deposition, and subsequently lysed with NaOH to quantify melanin content (Fig. 5M & N). Given that B16F10 cells intrinsically produce melanin even in the absence of drug treatment, no discernible color differences were observed among the groups following centrifugation. However, HaCaT cells co-cultured with B16F10 cells exhibited a pronounced darkening in color after treatment with Janus MNs. Melanin content analysis using NaOH lysis revealed that in B16F10 cells, both Shell MNs and Janus MNs groups exhibited significantly higher melanin levels compared to the control and Core MNs groups. In HaCaT cells, the Janus MNs group showed a 20% increase in melanin content compared to the Shell MNs group. This indicates that under comparable melanin synthesis in the lower layer, the upper layer exhibited a melanin content order of Shell MNs < Janus MNs, suggesting that Trypsin-loaded Core MNs facilitate melanin transfer from B16F10 to HaCaT cells. Western blot analysis (Fig. 5O & P) confirmed that in HaCaT cells, both Core MNs and Janus MNs groups significantly upregulated PAR2 protein expression, attributable to the presence of Trypsin. In B16F10 cells, MITF expression peaked at approximately 60% in the Shell and Janus MNs groups, indicating that the shell layer MNs enhance melanin production. Collectively, these results demonstrate that in the Janus MNs system, the shell layer HY-NPs act on MCs to stimulate melanin synthesis, while the core layer Trypsin activates keratinocytes to promote the transfer of melanin and melanosomes to the epidermis.

In summary, the Core MNs system developed in this study achieves targeted delivery and functional maintenance of trypsin in the epidermal layers of the skin. It specifically activates the PAR2 signaling pathway in keratinocytes, primarily regulating the transfer of melanosomes through the transport via TNTs. This design initially delivers the melanogenesis-promoting HY precisely to the location of HFs in the deep dermis, stimulating melanogenic cells to produce a substantial amount of melanin. Subsequently, the trypsin in the core layer activates PAR2 in the epidermis, facilitating melanosome transfer. Ultimately, this approach unifies both temporal and spatial aspects, for the first time synchronously addressing the dual barriers of melanin generation and transfer in the therapeutic dimension.

Therapeutic efficacy of Janus MNs for vitiligo

In a vitiligo model established using C57BL/6 mice, the low content of melanin in the epidermis makes it difficult to observe changes in epidermal pigmentation. Therefore, the pathological damage of vitiligo primarily occurs in the HFs [52]. As shown in Fig. 6A, to induce vitiligo, we applied 40% monobenzone cream (area 2 × 2 cm2, dosage 50 mg/cm2) to the shaved backs of C57BL/6 mice for 2 consecutive weeks. The appearance of persistent depigmentation in new hair growth was taken as a sign of successful model induction [35]. During the treatment phase, the experimental group applied two MN patches every 3 d, in combination with tacrolimus ointment (concentration 0.1%, dosage 20 mg/cm2) as a positive control. After skin is exposed to ultraviolet radiation, melanin begins to form within a few minutes to several hours; however, the full effect may take several days to become apparent [53,54]. Therefore, a dosing schedule of once every 3 d is adopted here to effectively facilitate the “repair-regeneration-transport” cascade reaction. Furthermore, while melanin synthesis is a relatively rapid process, melanin transport involves the gradual upward migration of melanin and the upward displacement of the epidermis to achieve pigmentation, which is a slower process [55]. Too frequent administration may lead to drug accumulation and cause toxic reactions due to overdose. Conversely, an administration schedule that is too infrequent may result in insufficient efficacy, preventing skin pigmentation. Therefore, our 3-d administration schedule is highly consistent with this natural physiological rhythm.

With the treatment of tacrolimus ointment and Janus MNs, the depigmented fur of the vitiligo mouse model gradually darkened, which was significantly more pronounced than that of Shell MNs and Core MNs, and also superior to blank MNs (Fig. 6B). The vitiligo severity score results indicated that after the successful induction of the model, the vitiligo severity scores of all groups reached above 4 points (Fig. 6C). Following the treatment, the Janus MNs group exhibited a significant regeneration of hair pigmentation as early as the second week, showing a statistically significant difference from the model control group. By the 3rd week, both the Shell MNs, Janus MNs, and tacrolimus ointment groups demonstrated significant therapeutic effects. When the treatment was continued to the 4th week, the scores of the Janus MNs and tacrolimus ointment groups were both <1, with the dorsal depigmentation essentially returning to normal levels. The scores of the blank MNs, Core MNs and Shell MNs groups were close to 2, respectively. It is worth noting that the model control group maintained a stable depigmented state throughout the observation period, with the score consistently remaining above 3.5. Overall, Janus MNs exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy compared to the single-layer structured MNs (Core MNs and Shell MNs). This disparity may be attributed to the Janus bilayer structure of Janus MNs, which can exert distinct functions according to the entire pathological process of vitiligo. Specifically, the lower layer promotes melanogenesis, while the upper layer releases Trypsin to activate the PAR2 pathway and facilitate the transfer of melanin to the epidermis.

The temporal-spatial efficacy of Janus MNs in melanogenesis and melanin transfer

Vitiligo is a depigmenting skin disorder caused by the loss of epidermal MCs [56]. In human skin, MCs are primarily located in the HFs and the basal layer of the epidermis, where they maintain normal skin color by transferring melanin granules or melanosomes to keratinocytes. However, the C57BL/6 mouse model exhibits significant interspecies differences. Its epidermis contains only trace amounts of melanin and lacks epidermal MCs entirely, resulting in the characteristic pinkish skin color. The majority of MCs are concentrated in the HFs, so the pathological phenotype of vitiligo is mainly manifested as hair whitening, with no obvious aggregation of melanin granules observable in the epidermis [57].

The growth cycle of HFs consists of anagen, catagen, and telogen phases, with maximal melanin synthesis occurring in the anagen phase [58,59]. H&E staining results indicate that HFs in the Ctrl and blank MN groups were predominantly in the telogen phase, exhibiting minimal melanin synthesis. The mild therapeutic effect of blank MNs stems from mechanical stimulation-induced improvement of the skin microenvironment, which is only a non-specific physical auxiliary effect without any therapeutic drugs [60]. In contrast, those treated with Janus MNs were in the anagen phase, which is characterized by robust melanogenesis (Fig. 6D & E). Furthermore, the Janus MNs group displayed more enlarged and robust follicles, with enhanced melanin synthesis, compared to the positive control of Tacrolimus, Core MNs and Shell MNs groups. The quantitative results showed that the Janus MNs group had the highest number of HFs, and the count at 4 weeks post-treatment significantly surpassed that observed at 2 weeks (Fig. 6F), indicating a progressive increase in follicle density over time. Groups containing HY exhibited a significant enhancement in HF formation, suggesting that HY not only facilitates the generation of HFs but also stimulates melanogenesis.

To better visualize melanin production and transfer within the skin, Fontana-Masson staining was performed (Fig. 6G & H). First, we examined the changes in the HF regions. The control, Blank MNs and Core MNs groups exhibited sparse HFs with limited melanin synthesis. In contrast, the Shell MNs, Janus MNs, and tacrolimus ointment groups displayed mature spherical HF structures with significant melanin deposition. Subsequently, as the skin’s spatial structure shifted upward, the Fontana-Masson staining of the epidermis showed that melanin transfer was absent in the control, Blank MNs and Shell MNs groups. Although the Core MNs group exhibited partial melanin transfer, it was restricted due to the limited number of HFs and melanin content. Consequently, only a small amount of melanin was transferred to the skin. The Janus MNs group demonstrated significantly better melanin transfer compared to the Core MNs group, likely because the HY released from the Shell layer promoted melanin production in the HFs, resulting in sufficient melanin content for effective transfer. Quantitative results of Fontana-Masson staining grayscale values showed that the melanin content in the Janus MNs group was significantly higher than in the positive control group after 4 weeks of treatment (Fig. 6I).

To further verify whether the superior therapeutic efficacy of Janus MNs arises from the integrated core-shell structure rather than simple sequential drug delivery, we compared the in vivo performance of Janus MNs with a control group in which Shell MNs and Core MNs were applied sequentially (Fig. S18). Both treatment groups eventually achieved skin repigmentation, confirming the therapeutic potential of combining HY-NPs and trypsin. However, the Janus MNs group showed faster and more uniform repigmentation throughout the treatment period, with no visible signs of skin irritation or inflammation. In contrast, the sequential Shell MNs + Core MNs group exhibited delayed and uneven repigmentation, accompanied by mild local inflammation at the repeated application sites. These findings demonstrate that the integrated core-shell structure of Janus MNs possesses distinctive superiorities. It realizes programmed sequential drug release via a single administration, reduces repeated skin damage, and improves skin tolerance, all of which contribute to favorable long-term therapeutic effects. In other words, the Janus MNs, specifically designed based on the thickness of mouse skin, can achieve the following effects: The shell layer (the lower layer) can reversibly reverse the over-activated pathological immune state and promote the synthesis of melanin by MCs in the HF layer. The core layer (the upper layer) assists in the upward transfer of melanin, thereby achieving skin pigmentation. Both layers are indispensable. The Core MNs group, which lacks the shell layer, has limited transfer due to insufficient melanin sources. The Shell MNs group, which lacks the core layer, can synthesize melanin but is unable to transfer it upward, with most of it deposited in the HFs. Therefore, the Janus MNs have realized the unity of the opposing functions of the upper and lower layers, achieving spatiotemporal sequential treatment for vitiligo.

In vivo mechanisms underlying melanin production and transfer

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the effects of Janus MNs on vitiligo in mice, we performed transcriptome sequencing on the skin of Janus MNs-treated vitiligo mice and vitiligo model mice (Ctrl). Compared with the control, the Janus MNs group exhibited 3562 DEGs, including 1184 significantly upregulated genes (P < 0.05 and log2(fold change)>1) and 2378 significantly downregulated genes (P < 0.05 and log2(fold change)<−1) (Figs. 7A and S20). As shown in Fig. 7B, the top 20 pathways ranked by P-value were presented, and the transcriptome KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted the MAPK signaling pathway, which aligns with the findings from the network pharmacology analysis (Fig. S5), thereby validating the previous finding that HY promotes melanogenesis via the MAPK pathway. Studies have shown that the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is associated with melanin transfer [61]. We hypothesize that PAR2 may influence melanin transfer through the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. Therefore, we constructed a PPI network (Fig. S21) of PAR2 and genes related to the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, revealing that PAR2 (also known as F2Rl1) is connected to three genes (TGFA, VTN, EGF). In the GO enrichment analysis, the GO terms further highlight biological processes such as oxygen levels, the Erk2 cascade, Wnt signaling, and the regulation of melanin biosynthesis, as well as cellular functions related to melanin transfer, such as the cytoskeleton and actin, and molecular functions associated with antioxidant activity, such as oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 7C). The above enrichment analyses demonstrate that the Janus MNs group, compared with the control group, exerted comprehensive therapeutic effects on vitiligo mice through the regulation of the immune microenvironment, antioxidant activity, melanogenesis, and melanin transfer.

To further elucidate the effects of our Janus MNs at the genetic and protein levels, we selected 14 genes of interest from the DEGs and constructed a heatmap (Fig. 7D). The selected genes are involved in melanogenesis (AP3D1, HPS1, MITF), melanin transfer (Stx2, Mlph, Myo5a), antioxidant activity (Foxo3, Hmox1, Nrf1), and T-cell recruitment (Il10, Il18, Ccr2, Cxcl10) [[62], [63], [64]]. As shown in Fig. 7E, the levels of melanogenesis-related proteins (MITF, TYR), antioxidant-related proteins (HO-1), and melanin transfer-related proteins (PAR2) in mice were upregulated after treatment with Janus MNs, while the expression of vitiligo-related immunological overactivation protein (CD8α) was downregulated (Fig. S22). The above results indicate that genes and proteins associated with antioxidant activity, melanogenesis, and melanin transfer in mice were upregulated, while those related to CD8+ T cells were downregulated, validating the therapeutic effects of Janus MNs on vitiligo.

To explore the potential therapeutic targets of HY in the treatment of vitiligo, we identified six intersecting genes from the network pharmacology intersection genes (Fig. S5A) and the DEGs (Fig. S20) obtained from the transcriptome analysis (Fig. 7F). We then conducted molecular docking experiments between these six proteins and HY, which indicated that only ALOX12, CASR and PTGS2 exhibited binding affinities with HY that met the criterion of a binding energy less than −6 (Figs. 7G and S23). Subsequently, we conducted a literature review to investigate the relationship between these three genes and the MAPK signaling pathway, which we had previously demonstrated to be associated with melanogenesis (Fig. S5). Our review revealed that the PTGS2-MAPK axis is well-supported by a substantial body of literature [65], with PTGS2 activation being shown to inhibit the MAPK pathway. Based on these findings, we constructed a PPI network between PTGS2 and proteins in the MAPK pathway, revealing a strong interconnectivity among these genes (Fig. S24). As shown in Fig. 7H, the expression of PTGS2 was downregulated by >5-fold in the Janus MNs group compared with the control group. These results suggest that HY may exert its therapeutic effects by inhibiting PTGS2, thereby activating the MAPK pathway. This activation may subsequently influence biological activities related to melanogenesis (positive regulation of the Wnt signaling pathway, regulation of the melanin biosynthetic process) and antioxidant responses (response to oxygen levels). We hereby propose the hypothesis that HY acts on the PTGS2-MAPK-MITF axis to promote melanogenesis (Fig. 7I).

Influence of Janus MNs on the skin immune microenvironment and spleen in vitiligo mice

In the pathological state of vitiligo, dendritic cells (DCs) typically migrate to the spleen after capturing antigens in the skin, where they activate T cells. These activated T cells may then recirculate to the skin, secrete IFN-γ and Granzyme B, and participate in the attack on MCs (Fig. 8A) [66]. Therefore, an ideal therapeutic approach for vitiligo should not only promote melanogenesis and melanin transfer but also consider strategies for modulating the pathological immune response. To investigate the systemic and local immunomodulatory effects of MN treatment on the vitiligo model, we analyzed the expression changes of key immune cell subsets in the spleen and skin of mice using flow cytometry.

Scale bar: 250 µm (n= 5); (D) Expression of skin DC maturation markers CD80 and CD86 (n= 5); (E and F) Expression of CD69, IFN-γ and Granzyme B in CD8 T cells (n= 5); (G) Expression of spleen DC maturation markers CD80 and CD86 (n= 5); (H and I) Infiltration of splenic CD8 T and CD4 T cells (n= 5).

Vitiligo is a common autoimmune-related disease caused by the loss of epidermal MCs. In its pathologic microenvironment, autoreactive cytotoxic CD8+ T cells are overactivated and destroy MCs by locally releasing IFN-γ and TNF-α, leading to vitiligo. Both local and systemic treatments that block IFN-γ and TNF-α signaling can effectively reverse vitiligo in humans [67]. Given the close association between vitiligo and autoimmune responses, we measured the spleen index and the expression of immune-related markers. The results showed that the spleen index in the Janus MNs group was significantly reduced (Fig. 8B), indicating an improvement in the state of excessive immune activation. As shown in Fig. 8C, the Janus MNs group, the tacrolimus group, and the Shell MNs group could significantly inhibit the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and the expression of TNF-α and IFN-γ, demonstrating that formulations containing HY can modulate the immune microenvironment.

In skin tissues, the co-expression of CD80 and CD86 on DCs was markedly upregulated in the saline control group (Fig. 8D). This result indicated excessive maturation and activation of DCs within vitiligo lesions, which further triggered and amplified subsequent adaptive immune responses. After intervention with Janus MNs, the proportion of CD80+CD86+ expression decreased by 52.9% compared to the Control group and was significantly better than other MN groups. Notably, the expression of the activation marker CD69, effector molecule IFN‑γ, and cytotoxic molecule Granzyme B in skin CD8⁺ T cells was abnormally elevated in the model group (Figs. 8E, F and S25-S26), reflecting that CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells were highly activated and could directly mediate immune injury and lysis of MCs. Treatment with Janus MNs significantly inhibited the expression of these molecules, and its inhibitory effect on Granzyme B⁺CD8⁺ T cells was significantly stronger than that of the tacrolimus group. These flow cytometry results confirmed that Janus MNs could effectively inhibit DC maturation and CD8⁺ T cell activation. This effect weakened the specific killing of MCs by cytotoxic T cells, thereby relieving impaired melanogenesis and skin tissue injury, and ultimately preventing the formation of depigmented lesions.

In the spleen tissue, the expression levels of the maturation markers CD80 and CD86 on DCs were also significantly elevated in the Control group (Fig. 8G), indicating that vitiligo progression is accompanied by not only local skin immune disorders but also systemic immune imbalance, with abnormal DC activation in the spleen as a major peripheral immune organ. After intervention with various treatment groups, CD80/CD86 expression showed varying degrees of decline. Among them, the inhibitory effect of Janus MNs was the most significant, with a 58.1% reduction in CD80+CD86+ expression compared to the model group and no statistical difference from the positive control tacrolimus group. The Core MNs and Shell MNs groups also exhibited noticeable inhibitory effects, while the Blank MNs group had limited efficacy. Further flow cytometric analysis of T lymphocyte subsets revealed that the proportion of CD8+ T cells was significantly increased in the Control group (Fig. 8H & I), suggesting an imbalanced CD4/CD8 ratio and a shift toward hyperactivated cellular cytotoxic immunity. After treatment with Janus MNs, the proportion of CD8⁺ T cells decreased to 13.3%, which was markedly superior to that of single-layer MN groups. The proportion of CD4+ T cells increased to 15.0% in the Janus MNs group, suggesting that it can restore immune homeostasis by reshaping the CD4/CD8 balance and ameliorating systemic immune disorders in vitiligo.

These results also indicate that, in both spleen and skin tissues, Shell MNs have a stronger effect on improving the immune microenvironment than Core MNs, suggesting that the shell layer of Janus MNs primarily exerts immunomodulatory effects. Skin lesions of vitiligo develop a microenvironment with excessive immune activity, and shell-layer MNs are capable of specifically releasing HY within this region. This coincidence allows Shell MNs to exert immunomodulatory and antioxidant functions in this area, relieving the immune suppression of MCs and promoting melanogenesis. Core MNs, located approximately 0–400 µm above Shell MNs, sequentially release the PAR2 activator Trypsin after the Shell layer has been released, thereby promoting melanin transfer.

Clinical observations have consistently demonstrated that patients with vitiligo exhibit elevated levels of peripheral immune cells compared to healthy individuals. In a murine model of vitiligo, the administration of Janus MNs resulted in the normalization of leukocyte, neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte levels (Fig. S27). This finding indicates that Janus MNs can modulate the immune system in mice, which may be beneficial for the treatment of vitiligo. Furthermore, topical administration of HY and trypsin to mice did not have any adverse effects on liver or kidney function (Fig. S28), suggesting that these treatments possess potential safety for clinical application.

Conclusions

In summary, we have pioneered the development of a spatiotemporally sequenced drug delivery system based on a core‑shell Janus MN platform, which orchestrates a synergistic therapeutic regimen encompassing MC preservation, melanogenesis promotion, and melanosome transfer, thereby presenting a novel, pathologically rational strategy for vitiligo treatment. The shell layer loaded with HY‑NPs and APS penetrates deep into the HF layer (640 µm) to scavenge ROS, suppress excessive CD8⁺ T‑cell activation, and boost melanogenesis via the PTGS2‑MAPK‑MITF pathway. The core layer encapsulates trypsin in HA, which releases sequentially in the upper epidermis (300 µm) to activate keratinocyte PAR2 signaling, promoting melanosome uptake and intercellular transfer. This spatially targeted and temporally ordered “repair‑regeneration‑transfer” cascade synergistically restored pigmentation in a mouse vitiligo model, with efficacy comparable to tacrolimus. This Janus MN system provides a multidimensional, minimally invasive, and precise transdermal delivery strategy for vitiligo management and offers a design paradigm for stratified skin disorders, including but not limited to psoriasis and atopic dermatitis.
