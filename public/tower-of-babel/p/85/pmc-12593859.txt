Early use of neutral Protamine Hagedorn (NPH) insulin in the management of diabetic ketoacidosis: an open-label randomized controlled trial in a resource-limited setting
Authors: Naveen Baby, Mukta Wyawahare, Dukhabandhu Naik, Nishaant Ramasamy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRegular insulin infusion and intravenous fluids are the cornerstones of managing diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). To shorten the time taken to resolve DKA in known diabetic patients, the concomitant administration of glargine with regular insulin infusion has been tried, with promising results. This study primarily aimed to assess whether the addition of Neutral Protamine Hagedorn (NPH) insulin to continuous insulin infusion (CII) hastens the resolution of DKA. The secondary objectives were to compare the time to rebound hyperglycemia, frequency of hypoglycemia and duration of hospital stay between the early NPH insulin and control groups.ResultsAn open-label, randomised controlled trial (CTRI ref/2022/08/044760, dated August 8, 2022) of 50 adult participants with DKA was conducted from August 2022 to June 2023. In the intervention arm, 25 participants received 0.25 IU/kg subcutaneous NPH insulin in 2 divided doses, along with a continuous insulin infusion from the time of randomization until the primary outcome was achieved. The control arm (n = 25) received the standard of care. The time to resolution of DKA was significantly shorter in the intervention arm [Intervention (I): 20 (16–32) hours vs. Standard (S): 38.5 (27–48) hours] in those with severe DKA (p = 0.01). Rebound hyperglycemia was delayed in the intervention arm [I: 7.9 ± 2.7 h vs. S: 5.9 ± 2.2 h]. There was no significant difference in the duration of hospital stay or frequency of hypoglycemia. However, the small sample size restricts the generalizability of the results and, larger studies are needed for confirmation of these findings.Trial registration: This trial was prospectively registered in the Clinical Trials Registry India (CTRI)-CTRI/2022/08/044760 on August 8, 2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07508-5.

Background and objectives

DKA is a severe life-threatening condition characterised by hyperglycemia, increased ketone body production and metabolic acidosis. The management of DKA is challenging because the patient requires hospitalisation, usually in an intensive care unit, and multipronged management of acidosis, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and the precipitating cause. The time for DKA resolution determines the duration of hospital stay. Treatment cost increases proportionately. To reduce the time to resolution of acidosis and to reduce hospital stay, a change in treatment regimen must be sought while ensuring patient safety. Previous studies, including a meta-analysis, have suggested the efficacy of basal glargine in reducing the time to closure of the anion gap, if given concomitantly with continuous insulin infusion (CII) [1–7]. We wanted to assess if NPH could be used in a similar manner. Neutral Protamine Hagedorn (NPH) insulin is an intermediate-acting insulin, used widely as basal insulin due to its easy availability and affordability. Its efficacy is comparable to that of Insulin analogues [8–10]. We planned this study with the primary objective to determine whether the time to resolution of DKA could be reduced by adding NPH insulin to CII. The secondary objectives were to compare the time to rebound hyperglycemia, frequency of hypoglycemia and duration of hospital stay between the early NPH insulin and control groups.

Methods

An open-label, randomized controlled trial (CTRI/2022/08/044760, dated August 8, 2022) of adult patients with diabetic ketoacidosis was conducted between August 2022 and June 2023. Sample size calculation was performed using OpenEpi 3.01 software. Anticipating a 3-hour (SD − 6) reduction in the time to resolution of DKA in the intervention arm compared to the control arm, with a power of 80% and an alpha error of 5%, 130 was the initial estimate. However, we decided to perform a pilot study of 50 adult DKA patients with 25 patients allocated to each arm, as no similar studies had been done earlier. The Institute’s Ethical Committee’s approval (IEC) (Ref no: Jawaharlal Institute of Postgraduate Medical Education and Research /IEC/2021/078) was obtained, and written informed consent was taken. Block randomisation with varying block sizes of 2, 4, and 6 was done using random allocation software, and allocation concealment was done via the Sequentially Numbered Opaque Sealed Envelope (SNOSE) technique. As the trial was conducted in an acute care setting, blinding of patients and treating clinicians was not feasible due to logistical and ethical considerations. However, randomisation was rigorously implemented to ensure balanced allocation of known and unknown confounders between the study arms. The study participants in the intervention arm received subcutaneous 0.25 mg/kg NPH insulin via two divided doses along with CII, within 4 h of randomisation, until the primary outcome was achieved. Outcomes were compared with those of the control arm, which received the standard of care. Patients older than 18 years with a diagnosis of DKA according to the ADA 2009 criteria were included in the study. Patients with a diagnosis of chronic liver disease or chronic kidney disease, those who were partially treated for DKA elsewhere, those on assisted ventilation and those on inotrope support were also excluded. All patients were randomised within 4 h of recruitment. All the participants received IV normal saline (0.9% NaCl) (15–20 ml/kg bodyweight in the first hour, followed by 250–500 ml/hour depending on the electrolytes, urine output and hydration status). If the serum potassium concentration was less than 3.3 mEq/L, 20–40 mEq of IV potassium was added to each liter of fluid, as per the standard care guidelines of DKA. Additional fluids (50–250 ml/hr) were given depending on the fluid status of the patients. Those in the standard arm received CII of Actrapid insulin at a dose of 0.1 U/kg infusion. In contrast, those in the intervention arm received 0.25 U/kg of NPH insulin in two divided doses in addition to CII until the resolution of DKA. Capillary blood glucose was monitored every 1–2 h, and the CII dose was adjusted to lower blood glucose at the rate of 50–70 mg/hour. Venous blood gas analysis (VBG) was performed once every 4–6 h. Once the random plasma glucose concentration reached < 250 mg%, the IV fluid was changed to 5% dextrose saline at 150–250 ml/hr, and the CII was reduced to 0.02–0.05 U/kg/hr to maintain a plasma glucose concentration between 150 and 200 mg/dl until DKA was resolved. After the DKA resolved, the treating physician managed the patient according to the blood sugar levels. Patients were followed up until discharge from the hospital.

DKA was diagnosed when the patient’s random plasma glucose level was more than 250 mg/dl, arterial pH was < 7.3, serum bicarbonate was less than 18 mEq/L, and there was moderate ketonuria. The classification of severity of DKA was based on the 2009 ADA criteria. DKA resolution was defined as a random plasma glucose concentration < 200 mg/dL, a venous pH ≥ 7.3 and a bicarbonate concentration greater than or equal to 18 mEq/L. [11–15] Rebound hyperglycemia was defined as a plasma glucose level > 200 mg/dL within 24 h of stopping IV Insulin Infusion. Hypoglycemia was defined as a plasma blood glucose concentration < 70 mg/dl. [11]

Infections were managed using antibiotics by the treating physician, in accordance with the institute’s antibiotic policy. The choice of empiric antibiotic was based on the clinical syndrome and further treatment was narrowed down based on the culture reports.

The data were compiled using Epicollect5 software and downloaded into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, after which the data were cleaned and coded. The collected data were analyzed using STATA V14. Continuous data were tested for normality of distribution by the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test and then summarized as the mean (SD) for normally distributed data and median (interquartile range IQR) for nonnormally distributed data. Categorical variables such as gender and hypoglycemia were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The non-normally distributed continuous data, such as time to resolution, were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and normally distributed data, such as rebound hyperglycemia, were compared between the two groups using the t-test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Ninety-three DKA patients who reported to the emergency medicine department between October 2021 and March 2023 were screened. Fifty were randomized to the intervention arm or the control arm (25 each). All were analyzed for DKA resolution. For four patients, the secondary outcome of length of hospital stay was not available (Fig. 1). The mean age of the participants was 38.2 ± 13 years, and the majority were males (56%). Thirty-two (64%) had type 1 diabetes (64%), and 16 (36%) had type 2 diabetes. The most common precipitating factor for DKA was default of medications (64%), followed by infection (30%). The detailed baseline patient characteristics of the intervention arm and standard arm are summarized in Table 1. In this study, 70% had severe, 28% moderate, and one had mild DKA. Fifteen had an infection precipitating DKA. These included urinary tract infection (60%), soft tissue infections like otitis media, diabetic foot and gluteal abscess (26%), gastrointestinal infection (7%) and community-acquired pneumonia (7%). The baseline characteristics were also similar between the two arms among the severe DKA patients (Supplementary Table 1).

All the outcomes were analyzed for a total of 50 participants (25 in each group), except for the duration of hospital stay, which was analyzed for 24 participants in the standard arm and 21 in the intervention arm (Tables 2 and 3). Four patients requested premature discharge against medical advice. One patient in the intervention arm underwent surgical thrombectomy for pulmonary embolism and died postoperatively. The time to resolution of DKA was shorter in the intervention arm [18 (14–24)] than in the standard arm [34 (12–44)], but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.15). However, in those with severe DKA, this difference in time to resolution of DKA was statistically significant [38.5 (27–48) hours] in the standard arm versus [20 (16–32) hours] in the intervention arm (p = 0.01). Rebound hyperglycemia was delayed in the intervention arm (7.9 ± 2.7 h) compared to the standard arm (5.9 ± 2.2 h), p = 0.005. There was no difference in the duration of hospital stay or the frequency of hypoglycemia between the two groups. The Kaplan-Meier (K-M) Survival Plot (Fig. 2) shows that the median time to resolution (in hours) was significantly shorter in patients with severe DKA who received subcutaneous NPH insulin plus CII (intervention arm) than in patients who received only IV insulin (control arm). Power analysis

Although the pilot study had a limited sample size, a post-hoc power analysis (G*Power 3.1.9.7) for the primary outcome, namely the time to rebound hyperglycemia, showed 80% power, considering an observed effect size of 0.812. This suggests sufficient statistical power to detect a large effect. Although a difference in time to rebound hyperglycemia was observed between groups, this secondary outcome was not the primary focus of the study, and the post-hoc power analysis should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

Current treatment guidelines advocate the use of rapid or short-acting insulin as IV infusion (CII) until the anion gap closes or DKA resolves, followed by the transition to subcutaneous injection of basal insulin plus regular insulin [11–16]. The CII is terminated 1–2 h after the administration of subcutaneous basal insulin. The concurrent administration of basal insulin, such as Glargine, within 1–2 h of initiating CII has been shown to reduce the time needed for the resolution of DKA. The primary objective of this study was to investigate whether NPH insulin can achieve similar results. The onset of action of NPH insulin is within 1–3 h, the peak occurs between 5 and 7 h, and the duration of action is 12–18 h after subcutaneous administration. Compared to insulin analogues, NPH is cheaper and readily available. Its efficacy in controlling hyperglycemia is similar, and it does not increase in the incidence of nocturnal hypoglycemia. [8–10]. This makes it a practical alternative in resource-limited settings. In the intervention arm, NPH 0.25 U/kg was administered in two divided doses, keeping in mind that the daily requirement is 0.1–0.2 U/kg in Type 2 DM and 0.4-1 U/kg in Type 1 DM, and higher doses are recommended for medical illnesses [17].

There are no studies using NPH in DKA that can be compared with our study. Hence, we have made a few observations from the studies using glargine. The pharmacokinetics of glargine are different from those of NPH, in that it provides a flat, peakless insulin profile with lesser risk of hypoglycemia when compared to the higher variability of the latter. This study is exploratory in nature with a small sample size compared to the studies using glargine, however it shows promising results. Our study outcome of faster resolution of DKA is similar to that of a study by Thammakosol K et al. from Thailand, in which insulin glargine given concomitantly with IV regular insulin reduced the time to resolution of DKA by approximately 3 h [1]. However, the outcome difference in our study was significant only for participants with severe DKA. The time to resolution of DKA was shorter by 18 h in the intervention arm. This group probably has more insulin deficiency when compared to the mild and moderate categories, and hence the addition of NPH insulin concomitantly with the CII resulted in faster resolution of DKA. However as the numbers with severe DKA are small and there is a potential risk of type 1 error due to multiple comparisons, these results should be interpreted with caution and warrant confirmation in larger adequately powered trials. There was no significant difference in those with mild and moderate DKA. The Thailand study did not compare participants based on the severity of DKA [1]. Other studies have shown similar results among children and adults who received subcutaneous glargine as an intervention [3–5].

The time to resolution of DKA in our study participants in the control arm was 34 h versus 18 h in the intervention arm (p = 0.15), which is longer than that in previous studies [1, 4–5]. This may be because of several factors, such as the time required to recover from the precipitating infection and the lack of ICU facilities for all those admitted with DKA due to the high patient load in the medical department of our Institute.

Since NPH is an intermediate-acting insulin and can have variability in its absorption, the secondary objective was to study the risk of rebound hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia in the study participants. In our study, all patients had rebound hyperglycemia; however, the time to occurrence of rebound hyperglycemia was longer in those with DKA in the intervention group (7.9 ± 2.7 h vs. 5.9 ± 2.2 h); this is not found in the studies using glargine [1]. The occurrence of rebound hyperglycemia increases the chances of recurrent ketosis and a delay in the interval of rebound hyperglycemia may improve patient outcomes, however this finding warrants further investigation in larger trials. The frequency of hypoglycemia was similar in both groups and was comparable to that reported in earlier studies. The intervention in this RCT did not increase the risk of hypoglycemia or rebound hyperglycemia in this study. Plasma glucose levels were controlled for a longer time in the intervention group before rebound hyperglycemia was recorded.

We also analyzed the difference in the length of hospital stay between the two groups and found that they were similar (6 vs. 7 days, p = 0.65). On comparing the duration of stay in the group with severe DKA, the intervention arm had a shorter stay, but the difference was not statistically significant. This was similar to the results reported by Doshi et al. where the glargine arm and the standard of care arms had similar length of stay(LOS) in hospital [5]. Thammakosol reported shorter LOS in the glargine group, likewise Lim et al. reported shorter LOS in the early basal insulin group [1, 6]. There could be several other factors affecting the length of stay including the severity of illness, need of ICU care, poor glycemic control, electrolyte abnormalities, presence of renal failure, urinary and respiratory tract infections etc. The overall length of hospital stay in our study was greater than that reported by Ata et al. (Median of 2 days) and by Shankar et al. in the pediatric population (3.7 days) [4, 18]. We did not have a discharge criteria for the trial participants. It was the physician’s decision to discharge them based on his/her clinical judgement. Those with infection-precipitating DKA were discharged after completion of the course of intravenous antibiotics, while those who were clinically stable were discharged earlier.

As this was a pilot study with a limited sample size, the findings, particularly the subgroup differences observed in severe DKA should be interpreted cautiously. The study was not powered to detect statistically significant differences in all outcomes, and the results are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Larger trials are needed to validate these preliminary observations.

Limitations

This pilot study is limited by its small sample size that is prone to more variations and lack of blinding. Taken together, the findings suggest that the addition of NPH insulin may result in faster resolution of DKA when administered concomitantly with regular insulin infusion, especially in those who have severe DKA. However, there may be variability in treatment protocols in different hospital settings and local factors like treatment resources and patient compliance have to be considered before applying these results. Our study had a small sample size of 50 patients, and hence, larger studies in different settings need to be performed in the future to confirm these findings. While the primary outcome (time to resolution of DKA) was based on objective biochemical parameters (e.g., random plasma glucose concentration, blood pH, serum bicarbonate), which limits the potential for subjective interpretation, we acknowledge that bias may still have occurred due to the open-label nature of the trial. We suggest three possible mitigation strategies for future trials. Firstly, employing blinded outcome adjudication, where a panel independently reviews anonymized biochemical data to determine endpoints [19]. Second strategy is to incorporate automated data capture for DKA resolution criteria through electronic health records or laboratory systems could further reduce the potential for subjective interpretation and enhance reproducibility. Third strategy is to adopt a double-dummy design to allow blinding despite differing routes or formulations of treatment. In this approach, all participants receive both active and placebo forms of each intervention, maintaining blinding among clinicians and patients alike. Though logistically more complex, the double-dummy approach has been shown to significantly reduce observer bias in comparative treatment trials, and may be applicable in acute metabolic trials with structured protocols, [20].

Conclusion

In this exploratory study, concomitant administration of NPH Insulin with continuous IV insulin infusion reduced the time to resolution of severe DKA.
