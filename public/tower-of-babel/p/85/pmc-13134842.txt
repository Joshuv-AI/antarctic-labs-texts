The Relationship between Sarcopenia and All-Cause and Cardiovascular Mortality Risk among Middle-Aged and Older Adults across Stages 0–3 of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Syndrome: Evidence from NHANES and CHARLS
Authors: Yan Chen, Yujun Liu, Shiqi Liu, Yafeng Mu, Zhihai Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSarcopenia has been proved to be associated with cardiovascular diseases (CVD), chronic kidney disease, and metabolic disorders, but the relationship between sarcopenia and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risk among middle-aged and older adults across stages 0–3 of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between sarcopenia and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risk among middle-aged and older adults across stages 0–3 of CKM syndrome based on Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2018 and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) 2011–2020.MethodsMultivariable Cox regression analysis was performed to analyze the association of sarcopenia with all-cause and CVD mortality. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was conducted to explore the non-linear relationship between body mass index (BMI)-adjusted muscle mass (appendicular skeletal muscle mass divided by BMI, ASMI) and all-cause and CVD mortality, and machine learning (ML) models were developed for mortality risk prediction. The CHARLS database was utilized as validation to enhance the stability of the results.ResultsOver an average follow-up of 5.11 years, NHANES recorded 85 all-cause deaths and 12 CVD deaths. After the full adjustment, sarcopenia was significantly associated with all-cause mortality (weighted hazard ratio [HR] = 3.428, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.484–7.915, p = 0.004) and CVD mortality (weighted HR = 1.070, 95% CI 1.009–1.570, p = 0.049). RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear negative relationship between ASMI and CVD mortality (p for nonlinear = 0.009). ML models demonstrated better predictive performance, with random forest (area under the curve = 0.766) achieving the highest accuracy. In the CHARLS cohort, sarcopenia significantly increases all-cause mortality (HR = 2.519, 95% CI 1.402–4.527, p < 0.001), and subgroup analysis reported consistent results with the main analysis.ConclusionSarcopenia was significantly associated with all-cause mortality and with CVD mortality in fully adjusted models, though the latter association was modest, and exhibits good predictive performance for mortality among middle-aged and older individuals within CKM stages 0–3.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome epitomizes the synergistic interplay of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), chronic kidney dysfunction, and metabolic disorders, where bidirectional pathological crosstalk accelerates multi-organ dysfunction [1]. Globally, CVDs remain the leading cause of mortality, with an estimated 523 million cases reported in 2021, doubling the burden observed in 1990 [2]. This escalation coincides with rising burdens of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and metabolic syndrome, prompting the American Heart Association (AHA) to formalize CKM syndrome in 2023 as a unified clinical entity [1]. The AHA staging framework (0–4) emphasizes early identification of high-risk individuals in stages 0–3 to mitigate progression to end-stage organ damage [3, 4].

Sarcopenia, recognized as an age-related decline in muscle mass, strength, and function, has emerged as a pivotal prognostic factor across chronic diseases, including heart failure, CKD, diabetes, obesity, and etc., thus increasing mortality and readmission rates [5, 6]. Defined by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) as the coexistence of low muscle mass and reduced muscle function, sarcopenia affects 10–27% of community-dwelling older adults, with prevalence exceeding 30–50% in populations with CKD or diabetes [7–9]. Beyond accelerating physical frailty, sarcopenia independently predicts all-cause mortality and mortality across chronic conditions including CVDs, CKD, diabetes, chronic lung diseases, and cancer [10–14]. Mechanistically, skeletal muscle functions as an endocrine organ, secreting myokines, such as irisin and myostatin, that modulate inflammation, glucose homeostasis, and energy metabolism [15]. Muscle atrophy exacerbates insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation – core drivers of CKM progression [16–19]. Critically, sarcopenia and CKM syndrome engage in bidirectional pathological crosstalk. For example, metabolic dysfunction in CKM activates ubiquitin-proteasome pathways, increasing muscle proteolysis, and CKD-driven uremic toxins inhibit muscle regeneration via p38 MAPK-induced satellite cell apoptosis [20, 21]. This creates a pathological loop: CKM components accelerate muscle catabolism, while sarcopenia worsens glycemic control and inflammation, propelling disease toward CKM. Yet, sarcopenia’s impact on mortality in early CKM (stages 0–3), where interventions could halt progression, remains unquantified. Therefore, this study leveraged NHANES and CHARLS cohorts to investigate whether sarcopenia independently predicts all-cause and CVD mortality in middle-aged/older adults with CKM stages 0–3, determine if muscle mass exhibits nonlinear effects on mortality risk, and establish ML models for early risk stratification.

Methods

Study Population

NHANES (accessible at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm) is a nationally representative cross-sectional study of the US civilian population conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), with samples approximately 5,000 individuals annually, ensuring representation across different ages, genders, and ethnic groups. Data from NHANES are released every 2 years since its inception in the 1960s and its transition to a continuous survey in 1999. CHARLS (accessible at https://charls.pku.edu.cn/en/), a nationally representative longitudinal survey conducted by Peking University to investigate the health, socioeconomic status, and aging dynamics of the Chinese population aged 45 and older, launched in 2011 and employs a multistage probability sampling design to ensure representativeness across 28 provinces, 150 counties, and 450 villages in China.

We selected data from NHANES 2011–2018 and CHARLS 2011–2020, with criteria of exclusion as follows: (1) participants under the age of 45 years; (2) participants missing data related to CKM syndrome; (3) participants in CKM syndrome stage 4; (4) participants missing data for sarcopenia; (5) participants missing survival status data. Definition of Sarcopenia

For NHANES, appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) is assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan, and sarcopenia is identified when body mass index (BMI)-adjusted ASM (ASMI) fell below sex-specific thresholds: <0.789 for males and <0.512 for females, following the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) [22–24]. For CHARLS, according to 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), sarcopenia is identified using muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical function [25]. ASM is obtained by an anthropometric formula [26]: ASM = 0.193 × weight (kg) + 0.107 × height (cm) − 4.157 × sex (male = 1/female = 2) − 0.037 × age − 2.631. Height-adjusted ASM (ASM/height2) below 7.0 kg/m2 (men) and 5.4 kg/m2 (women) indicate deficiency of muscle mass [27]. Muscle strength is reflected by hand grip strength measured by a calibrated dynamometer, with thresholds of <28 kg for men and <18 kg for women indicating decline of muscle strength [28]. Participants are required to perform maximal-effort grips with a 90° elbow flexion in seated/standing positions, completing two trials per hand, with peak values recorded. Physical function is assessed by either gait speed analysis (2.5-m walk) or chair rise timing [29]. Eligible participants aged ≥60 without mobility limitations complete two timed walks along a 2.5-meter course at natural pace, with average speed calculated from duplicate trials. The chair stand test requires five consecutive rises from a standardized 47-cm seat without arm use, timed from seated start to fifth full stand completion. Speed metrics (m/s) derive from vertical displacement divided by execution time. Gait speed <1 m/s and chair stand time >12 s were used as thresholds for low physical performance. Coexisting low muscle mass plus either subthreshold physical function or diminished grip strength is defined as sarcopenia [30].

Definition of CKM Syndrome across Stage 0–3

CKM syndrome, as defined by the 2023 AHA guidelines, is categorized into stages 0–4 to stratify individuals based on the presence and severity of interconnected cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic risk factors [31]. Stage 0 encompasses subjects free from CKM-related risk factors, maintaining optimal metabolic health with ethnicity-adjusted anthropometric thresholds: normal body mass index (BMI <25 kg/m2 for non-Asian populations; <23 kg/m2 for Asian populations), normal waist circumference (non-Asian female <88 cm and male <102 cm; Asian female <80 cm and male <90 cm), normoglycemia (fasting blood glucose <100 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c <5.7%), normal blood pressure <130/80 mm Hg, normal lipid levels (HDL-C ≥40 mg/dL for male and ≥50 mg/dL for female, triglycerides <150 mg/dL), preserved renal function (eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2), and no evidence of CVDs. Stage 1 represents excess adiposity or early glucose dysregulation, defined by elevated BMI (≥25 kg/m2 for non-Asian; ≥23 kg/m2 for Asian), or waist circumference exceeding ethnic-specific thresholds (non-Asian female ≥88 cm and male ≥102 cm; Asian female ≥80 cm and male ≥90 cm), or prediabetes (HbA1c 5.7–6.5% or fasting glucose 100–126 mg/dL). Stage 2 encompasses individuals with moderate-to-severe CKD (eGFR 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or self-reported CKD), or metabolic syndrome diagnosed by fulfilling more than 3 criteria as follows: abdominal obesity, systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥130/80 mm Hg, fasting triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, HDL-C <40 mg/dL for male or <50 mg/dL for female), or prediabetes. Stage 3 signifies subclinical CVD or very high cardiovascular risk, marked by a high Framingham CVD risk score (≥21.5% for female; ≥21.6% for male), or advanced CKD (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2). Stage 4 represents clinical overt CVD, including self-reported heart failure, coronary heart disease, or stroke.

Mortality Data Collection

All-cause and CVD mortality were selected as the primary and secondary endpoints, respectively. Follow-up commenced upon the initial interview from the baseline assessment and continued until participant death or the predefined study termination date – December 31, 2019, with the earlier event determining the observation period’s conclusion. Mortality data were sourced from NHANES public archives. To validate mortality status and causes, NHANES records were algorithmically cross-referenced with the National Death Index (NDI) through a validated statistical linkage protocol, ensuring high-fidelity event ascertainment. Underlying causes of death were systematically classified per the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), with CVD-related fatalities encompassing heart diseases or cerebrovascular disease (ICD-10 codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20–I51, and I60-I69). All-cause mortality included fatalities from any documented etiology. CVD mortality was not analyzed in CHARLS database. For CHARLS, mortality data were obtained through household reports and verified by local death certificates. Causes of death were not uniformly available, so only all-cause mortality was analyzed.

Covariates

According to previous studies, we adjusted possible confounders of demographic characteristics including sex, age, marital status (married/cohabitating with partners and other marital status), and educational level (below high school, high school, and college or above), health-related factors including BMI, waist circumstance, drinking status (non-current drinker and current drinker), smoking status (non-current smoker and current smoker), diabetes, and hypertension, and laboratory examinations including low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), creatinine, and HbA1c at baseline. Hypertension is defined as systolic blood pressure/diastolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg/90 mm Hg, self-reported history of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes is defined as fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥6.5%, self-reported history of diabetes, or current treatment with insulin and/or oral hypoglycemic agents.

Statistical Analysis

Stata 18.0 and R 4.4.1 were used for data cleansing and data analysis. For NHANES, due to the complex multistage sampling design, weight was introduced in the analysis. Continuous variables presented as weighted mean ± standard errors (SEs), and categorical variables presented as counts with percentages. Weighted T test and weighted Chi-square test were conducted to assess the difference in the characteristics between different groups. Cox regression models were constructed to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between sarcopenia and all-cause and CVD mortality. Three models were developed to conduct the analysis: model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, educational level, marital status, BMI, and waist circumstance; model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, marital status, educational level, BMI, waist circumstance, drinking status, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, LDL-c, HDL-c, HbA1c, and creatinine. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to estimate the nonlinear relationship between ASMI and all-cause and CVD mortality. ASMI was selected for nonlinear analysis because it provides a continuous measure of muscle mass adjusted for BMI, enabling more granular insight into dose-response relationships with mortality.

The dataset was randomly partitioned into training set and test set, including 70% and 30% of the total dataset, respectively. ABESS and Boruta algorithms were conducted to identify key variables, and variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity among them, with VIFs >5 indicating the existence of multicollinearity. ML models, including decision tree, random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector machines (SVM), neural network, logistic regression (LR), quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA), linear discriminant analysis, Naive Bayes (NB), and K-nearest neighbor, were developed. Models were trained using 10-fold cross-validation, and hyperparameters were optimized via grid search. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate performance. To enhance the reliability of our findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, we utilized CHARLS 2011–2020 to examine the relationship of sarcopenia with all-cause mortality among middle-aged and older adults within CKM stages 0–3. Second, we performed subgroup analysis to investigate the difference in the association between sarcopenia and mortality among different populations. Subgroup analyses were stratified by median age within each cohort (52 years in NHANES, 57 years in CHARLS) to ensure balanced comparisons, rather than by clinical definitions of middle vs. older age. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association of Sarcopenia and ASMI with All-Cause and CVD Mortality

Figure 2 presents the Kaplan-Meier curves depicting differences of survival probability between groups with different sarcopenia status. For both all-cause mortality and CVD mortality, participants with sarcopenia showed a lower survival probability (log-rank p = 0.0018 and p < 0.0001).

The KM curves illustrating the impact of sarcopenia on survival probability among middle-aged and older participants within CKM stage 0–3. a All-cause mortality. b CVD mortality.

We performed weighted Cox regression analysis by constructing three models to explore the independent relationship of sarcopenia with all-cause and CVD mortality risks, and the results are shown in Table 2. Significant positive relationship between sarcopenia and all-cause mortality was observed in the three models (model 1: weighted HR = 4.161, 95% CI 1.803–9.605, p < 0.001; model 2: weighted HR = 3.474, 95% CI 1.507–8.011, p = 0.003; model 3: weighted HR = 3.428, 95% CI 1.484–7.915, p = 0.004), as well as CVD mortality (model 1: weighted HR = 1.299, 95% CI 1.075–3.192, p = 0.003; model 2: weighted HR = 1.244, 95% CI 1.063–1.737, p = 0.044; model 3: weighted HR = 1.070, 95% CI 1.009–1.570, p = 0.049). We further investigated the relationship between ASMI and mortality among middle-aged and older participants within CKM stage 0–3. After adjustment in model 2 and model 3, a negative correlation of ASMI with all-cause mortality was observed (model 2: weighted HR = 0.029, 95% CI 0.002–0.429, p = 0.010; model 3: weighted HR = 0.029, 95% CI 0.002–0.531, p = 0.017), as well as CVD mortality (model 2: weighted HR = 0.002, 95% CI 0.000–0.828, p = 0.043; model 3: weighted HR = 0.001, 95% CI 0.000–0.012, p < 0.001).

Sensitivity Analysis

To enhance the stability of the results, we conducted sensitivity analysis. First, we utilized data from CHARLS 2011–2020 to assess the association of sarcopenia with all-cause mortality, and 8,563 participants were finally included in this study. Given that NHANES is a cross-sectional survey with mortality data ascertained through linkage rather than prospective follow-up, we utilized the prospective longitudinal CHARLS cohort as an external validation set to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the identified association between sarcopenia and mortality risk. As shown in online supplementary Table S3 and online supplementary Figure 1, results indicated that sarcopenia is positively related with all-cause mortality among participants within the CKM stage 0–3 in the three models (model 1: HR = 3.943, 95% CI 2.884–5.391, p < 0.001; model 2: HR = 2.560, 95% CI 1.671–3.921, p < 0.001; model 3: HR = 2.519, 95% CI 1.402–4.527, p < 0.001). In the RCS analysis, a significant nonlinear association of ASMI with all-cause mortality was observed in the RCS analysis (p for overall = 0.003, p for nonlinear = 0.009) (online suppl. Fig. 2). Crucially, when ASMI exceeds 0.711, the risk of all-cause mortality decreases, with an adjusted β of 0.006 (95% CI 0.000–0.112) and a p value less than 0.001 (online suppl. Table S4). However, when ASMI is below 0.711, the negative relationship between ASMI and all-cause mortality is unclear. The likelihood ratio test indicated that overall p value <0.001. Then, subgroup analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between sarcopenia and all-cause mortality among different populations stratified by age, gender, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, and hypertension, and the interactions were assessed. As shown in online supplementary Table S5, in CHARLS, results revealed a positive correlation of sarcopenia with all-cause mortality across all subgroups except for group aged <57, with no interaction observed. But hypertension significantly influenced the strength of the correlation between sarcopenia and all-cause mortality (interaction p value = 0.043), with participants without hypertension exhibiting a significantly elevated risk compared to those with hypertension. In NHANES, significant relationship was observed in different populations except for participants who are current drinking, aged <52, and without hypertension, with no interaction observed, indicating the three variables had no significant effect on the outcome.

Discussion

For NHANES, 3,724 middle-aged and older participants within CKM stage 0–3 were finally included for analysis, and the results indicated a significant positive correlation of sarcopenia with all-cause mortality, which was further validated in the analysis of CHARLS database. Linear relationship and nonlinear relationship of ASMI with all-cause mortality and CVD mortality were observed, respectively. We developed predictive models including sarcopenia using 10 ML algorithms to ensure the predictive accuracy, with 5 models exhibiting a AUC value exceeding 0.65, indicating good predictive performance. These finding provide a new light for the prediction of mortality among middle-aged and older participants within CKM stage 0–3 that early identification and treatment of sarcopenia is favorable for decreasing the risk of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality, supporting sarcopenia’s clinical utility as a prognostic marker.

CKM syndrome represents an integrative pathophysiological continuum where CVDs, CKDs, and metabolic disorders synergistically interact through shared mechanisms of chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neurohormonal dysregulation, prevalent among middle-aged and older adults. At its core, cell apoptosis, insulin resistance, systemic release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, especially IL-6 and TNF-α, and free fatty acids, induce endothelial damage, glomerular hyperfiltration, and glucose and lipid metabolism, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of multi-organ injury [32]. Unexpectedly, participants with sarcopenia in our NHANES cohort had lower prevalence of diabetes and hypertension. This may reflect survival bias or earlier detection of sarcopenia in individuals without overt metabolic disease. Alternatively, it may indicate that sarcopenia in earlier CKM stages is driven more by inflammatory and catabolic processes than by traditional cardiometabolic risk factors. This contrasts with some literature but aligns with studies suggesting sarcopenia can precede overt diabetes and hypertension in high-risk populations. The discordance between higher HbA1c and lower self-reported diabetes in the sarcopenia group may indicate underdiagnosis of diabetes or the presence of prediabetes, which is consistent with the metabolic dysregulation seen in early CKM stages.

In the context of CKM syndrome, sarcopenia may amplify cardiovascular, renal pathology and metabolic dysfunction through multiple pathways. Cardiovascularly, reduced muscle mass correlates with myocardial lipid infiltration and fibrosis due to downregulated PPAR-α/PGC-1α signaling and impaired fatty acid oxidation, compromising cardiac contractility and increasing heart failure risk [33–35]. Currently, myokine dysregulation, particularly elevated IL-6 from atrophied muscle, activates vascular endothelial NF-κB, increasing adhesion molecule expression (VCAM-1/ICAM-1) and promoting atherosclerotic plaque instability [36]. A cohort study based on CHARLS has showed a close relationship between sarcopenia and the incidence and progression of CVD, suggesting that sarcopenia not only increases the incidence of CVD but also worsens its severity, which may be mediated through several pathways including inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic abnormalities [37]. These factors contribute to both the development of sarcopenia and the progression of CVD [38]. Renally, the kidneys’ ability to filter and excrete waste products is compromised in patients with CKD, leading to the accumulation of uremic toxins in the bloodstream [39]. Sarcopenia exacerbates CKD progression via bidirectional toxicity. Impaired muscle mass reduces the body’s capacity to metabolize uremic toxins, such as indoxyl sulfate, allowing their accumulation to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in renal tubules – driving TGF-β-mediated fibrosis [40–43]. Simultaneously, CKD-induced metabolic acidosis accelerates ubiquitin-proteasome activation in muscle, increasing proteolysis and mitochondrial ROS production, creating a vicious cycle where uremic toxins directly inhibit myocyte differentiation via p38 MAPK/p53-dependent apoptosis [44]. Crucially, skeletal muscle regulates approximately 80% of insulin-mediated glucose uptake, and its atrophy disrupts GLUT4 translocation, inducing hyperglycemia and compensatory hyperinsulinemia. Preclinical evidence has showed that muscle-specific insulin receptor knockout induces systemic insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis, leading to inflammation, mitochondria dysfunction and increased apoptosis, which are the crucial mechanisms of metabolic syndrome and sarcopenia [45–47]. Studies have provided clinical evidence that individuals with sarcopenia are at higher risk for developing metabolic syndrome, which is characterized by obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia [48, 49]. Additionally, sarcopenia reduces skeletal muscle fatty acid β-oxidation, elevating circulating FFAs that deposit ectopically in the liver and heart, driving lipotoxicity and caspase-3-mediated apoptosis in cardiomyocytes [50, 51]. This bidirectional relationship underscores the potential of sarcopenia as a biomarker of multisystem decline in CKM.

An analysis of the weighted prevalence revealed a distinct pattern of sarcopenia distribution across CKM stages, with the highest prevalence in stage 2 (4.047%) and a marked decrease in stage 3 (0.449%). This counterintuitive finding invites a nuanced interpretation of the pathophysiological synergy between muscle health and cardiorenal-metabolic dysregulation within a cross-sectional study design. The elevated prevalence in stage 2 aligns with the intensification of interconnected catabolic drivers: insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation from excess adiposity are compounded by the onset of renal dysfunction in moderate CKD, where accumulating uremic toxins directly activate muscle proteolysis and inhibit regeneration. Concurrently, metabolic acidosis and heightened systemic inflammation exacerbate muscle catabolism [52]. The observed decline in prevalence in stage 3 is likely attributable to two principal factors intrinsic to studying advanced disease. First, severe survival bias: individuals progressing to the high-risk state of stage 3 with concurrent sarcopenia likely face exceptionally high short-term mortality, making them systematically underrepresented in a cross-sectional survey like NHANES. Second, diagnostic and clinical dynamics: the complex presentation of advanced CKM may confound the ASMI used to define sarcopenia, leading to potential under-ascertainment despite severe functional decline. Nevertheless, the mechanistic pathways linking CKM components to muscle loss intensify with disease progression. In stage 3, factors such as probable physical inactivity due to subclinical limitations, endothelial dysfunction impairing muscle perfusion, and elevated circulatory cytokines create an environment highly detrimental to muscle maintenance [53, 54]. This creates a bidirectional, self-perpetuating cycle: CKM components accelerate muscle loss, while sarcopenia worsens metabolic control and inflammation, propelling disease progression. Consequently, CKM stage 2 emerges as a critical and prevalent window for sarcopenia screening. Integrating sarcopenia assessment at this juncture into the CKM staging framework could enable early interventions aimed at preserving muscle mass, potentially disrupting this vicious cycle and mitigating the risk of progression to terminal stages.

The clinical implications of this work are profound. Sarcopenia assessment could be incorporated into routine CKM evaluations, enabling early identification of high-risk individuals. Interventions targeting muscle health, such as resistance training, protein supplementation, or pharmacotherapies-like selective androgen receptor modulators, may disrupt the death progression of CKM syndrome. Furthermore, machine learning (ML)-driven risk prediction models could optimize resource allocation in healthcare systems, prioritizing interventions for those at greatest risk.

However, the current study has several methodological limitations. First, muscle mass assessment methods differed substantially between cohorts: NHANES utilized gold-standard DXA scans, while CHARLS relied on anthropometric equations, potentially introducing bias of measurement. Second, temporal dynamics were unaccounted for – both sarcopenia diagnosis and CKM staging relied solely on baseline data. Given the progressive nature of muscle loss and metabolic dysfunction, this single-time assessment fails to capture critical transitions that may modulate mortality risk. Third, due to data availability constraints, the NHANES cohort exhibits a limited number of mortality events (particularly cardiovascular deaths), which may reduce statistical power and introduce type II error bias in Cox regression analyses. Fourth, we were unable to account for physical activity or cardiorespiratory fitness due to inconsistent measures across cohorts, which may confound the relationship between sarcopenia and mortality. Fifth, in the NHANES cohort, we defined sarcopenia based on DXA-measured low muscle mass alone to align with our aim of evaluating the specific role of muscle quantity. We recognize that incorporating measures of muscle strength or function would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the sarcopenia syndrome, and future studies could explore whether such composite definitions offer incremental prognostic value in the CKM population. Finally, therapeutic implications remain inferential. While sarcopenia predicted mortality, causal effects of muscle-targeted interventions, such as resistance training, on CKM outcomes cannot be established from observational data. Future trials are needed to test whether mitigating sarcopenia reduces mortality in stage-specific CKM cohorts.

In conclusion, sarcopenia independently predicts mortality among middle-aged and older adults within CKM stages 0–3 through interconnected cardiorenal-metabolic pathways. Its integration into risk stratification protocols, combined with ML-enhanced prognostics, offers a pragmatic approach for early intervention. As CKM syndrome prevalence escalates globally, these findings underscore the imperative of muscle health preservation as a modifiable determinant of clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Sarcopenia was significantly associated with all-cause mortality and with CVD mortality in fully adjusted models, though the latter association was modest, and exhibits good predictive performance for mortality among middle-aged and older individuals within CKM stages 0–3.
