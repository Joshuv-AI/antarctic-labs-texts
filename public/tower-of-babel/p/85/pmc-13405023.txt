Paediatric healthcare worker perspectives of visiting paediatric residents in Gaborone, Botswana
Authors: Felicity Mampe, Elizabeth Lowenthal, Alemayehu Gezmu, Tonya Arscott-Mills, Adelaide Barnes, Merrian Brooks, Dipesalema Joel, Mazvita Rankin, Andrew P Steenhoff, Morgan Congdon
Published: 2026

Global health electives are increasingly common yet host institution perspectives remain underexplored. We conducted a cross-sectional survey of paediatric healthcare workers at Princess Marina Hospital in Gaborone, Botswana regarding visiting US paediatric residents participating in a longstanding elective partnership. 52 healthcare workers responded. Most respondents perceived visiting residents as beneficial to patient care and the institutional mission with limited perceived clinical burden. Some respondents identified challenges related to orientation and workflow. These findings suggest that well-structured international paediatric electives may support mutually beneficial partnerships when host priorities and operational considerations remain central.

Introduction

Medical trainees from high-income countries have become increasingly interested in participating in rotations in low- and middle-income countries.1 Although global health electives benefit trainees, host institution perspectives remain underexplored. Our study sought to assess the perspectives of healthcare workers (HCWs) in Botswana regarding visiting paediatric resident physicians.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional survey study of paediatric HCWs who host physician trainees. The study was conducted at Princess Marina Hospital (PMH) in Gaborone, Botswana’s capital. Participants were surveyed about their perceptions of visiting residents from a large US residency programme that participates in an elective partnership established in 2009. Visiting residents complete a half-day pre-departure orientation including PMH and WHO clinical guidelines, though prior procedural exposure may vary by resident. The study was introduced during paediatric physician and nursing meetings before an electronic survey was distributed to eligible HCWs. Responses were collected anonymously to encourage candid feedback and minimise response bias. We developed and piloted the survey after a review of the literature related to international rotations for trainees in global health settings.2 Survey questions were designed to address perceived benefits, burdens and impacts on patient care, workflow and institutional mission. We calculated descriptive statistics and summarised the distribution of Likert-scale responses across the cohort. Open-ended comments were reviewed to contextualise survey findings.

Results

52 HCWs responded (46% overall response rate). Respondents represented a range of clinical roles, including paediatricians (20, 38%), paediatric physician trainees (19, 37%) and nurses (13, 25%). Greater than 90% of respondents reported that visiting residents were beneficial to PMH, the University of Botswana Department of Pediatrics, and their respective missions (figure 1). Most respondents reported positive impacts on patient care and clinical practice, though some noted additional time was needed for orientation and supervision due to unfamiliarity with diseases less common in high-income settings and procedures such as venous access and lumbar punctures. Most reported that residents have a neutral impact on resource utilisation (eg, gloves, sutures, laboratory testing), with a slightly larger number reporting more positive than negative impacts on resources. Most affirmed that visiting residents respected local team members (n=46, 88%), understood their role as visiting physicians (n=51, 98%), fulfilled professional responsibilities (n=48, 92%) and were receptive to feedback (n=44, 85%). More than 80% of respondents reported positive or slightly positive effects on patient care and their own clinical practice (figure 2).

Discussion

Our findings provide an important host perspective on visiting residents from the USA. Host HCWs largely perceived visiting residents as beneficial despite some logistical and resource burdens. Consistent with prior studies examining host institution perspectives on visiting trainees, our findings reveal a nuanced balance of benefits and burdens associated with hosting visiting residents.3–5 Host HCWs described workflow challenges, at times affecting clinical efficiency, a challenge similarly reported in studies examining ethical considerations of global health electives.6 Identifying ways to minimise these challenges, such as through enhanced pre-elective procedural training, could add further value in hosting visiting residents with minimal perceived clinical burden. These findings are already informing revisions to our existing pre-departure curriculum, including additional procedural training and greater emphasis on diseases commonly encountered in Botswana. The generalisability of our findings may be limited by the single-site design and focus on a single paediatric clinical elective. Lower nursing response rates may underrepresent perspectives from an important host stakeholder group. Despite these potential limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the perception of HCWs in Botswana regarding visiting paediatric residents. Overall, these findings suggest that well-structured international paediatric electives may support mutually beneficial partnerships when programmes prioritise robust orientation and strategies to minimise host burden.
