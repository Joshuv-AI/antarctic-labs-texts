Salivary genetic biomarkers of lung cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis of the diagnostic accuracy
Authors: Maryam Koopaie, Mahnaz Fatahzadeh, Sajad Kolahdooz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsidering the elevated mortality linked to lung cancer and the constraints of diagnostic and screening techniques, non-invasive biomarkers offer a promising alternative for early diagnosis. This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the diagnostic accuracy of salivary genetic biomarkers for lung cancer.MethodsWe searched for pertinent keywords in the Cochrane Library, Embase, LIVIVO, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar for relevant studies. A systematic review was performed, and data were extracted from the eligible studies. The methodological quality of the studies included in this review was assessed using the Quality Assessment tool for Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2). The split component synthesis method was used to determine sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios, and diagnostic odds ratios. Studies were classified into three categories based on the type of biomarkers used: the single salivary biomarker model, the multiple salivary biomarker model (using multiple salivary biomarkers), and the mixed biomarker model (combining salivary biomarkers with other data, such as blood biomarkers or demographic data). By synthesizing data from six studies, including single, multiple, and mixed biomarker models, the study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of these models.ResultsThe findings indicate that single-salivary biomarker models exhibit moderate diagnostic performance, with sensitivity and specificity of 0.72 and 0.73, respectively. However, multiple salivary biomarker models demonstrate improved diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity: 0.88, specificity: 0.75), supporting the use of multiple salivary biomarkers to increase accuracy and reduce false-positive results. Mixed models using a combination of salivary and blood biomarkers outperformed other models, achieving a diagnostic odds ratio of 115.66, with sensitivity at 0.92 and specificity at 0.91.ConclusionsOur findings show that the mixed biomarker model (model using salivary and blood genetic biomarkers) yields the highest diagnostic odds ratio and area under the curve, making it the most efficacious diagnostic approach among the models analyzed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03968-8.Keywords: Lung neoplasms, Lung cancer, Non-small cell lung cancer, MicroRNAs, MRNAs, Genetic markers, Mouth, Saliva, Diagnosis, Biomarker, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) is among the most prevalent cancer types and a primary cause of global mortality, resulting in millions of deaths annually [1]. Timely diagnosis of this malignancy is a challenge because, despite its aggressive nature and rapid progression, it often remains asymptomatic until advanced stages [2]. Since prognosis and survival are highly dependent on the stage at diagnosis, early detection has emerged as a critical factor for reducing mortality and improving treatment outcomes [3]. While efficacious in diagnosing LC, current diagnostic approaches, such as low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) and tissue biopsy, suffer significant drawbacks, including high costs, an invasive nature, and risk of radiation toxicity, making their widespread use in population-based screening programs impractical [4, 5]. Therefore, the utilization of saliva, a readily accessible biofluid for diagnostic purposes, has attracted significant interest among researchers. Saliva contains a variety of biomolecules, including DNA, RNA, proteins, and metabolites, which can provide valuable insights into the physiological and pathological processes in the body [6, 7]. Studies have shown that saliva contains biomarkers that can be utilized for non-invasive diagnosis of various malignancies, including LC [8, 9]. The unique properties of saliva render this biofluid a suitable medium for early detection and screening purposes [10–12]. Saliva can be easily collected without specialized tools, and the sampling process requires minimal training and expertise. Additionally, saliva storage and transport are simpler than those of blood or tissue samples, as they do not require advanced equipment and are less prone to coagulation and degradation [13]. Challenges pertinent to using saliva as a diagnostic sample include variability in collection methods, measurement techniques, and standard protocols, which could impact the accuracy and reliability of results [14, 15].

While systematic reviews and diagnostic meta-analysis have enhanced the evidence base for lung cancer biomarkers [16, 17], substantial deficiencies persist regarding salivary diagnostics in LC. Mohamed and colleagues systematically evaluated blood-based biomarkers but entirely omitted salivary analyses [18]. Similarly, Dolcini et al. restricted their analysis to blood-derived DNA methylation markers for risk stratification, neglecting diagnostic performance [19]. Although Skallevold et al. provided a qualitative overview of salivary biomarkers of LC, their review lacked quantitative synthesis and methodological rigour [20]. These limitations highlight three unaddressed imperatives: first, the dearth of meta-analytic assessments of salivary genetic biomarkers’ diagnostic accuracy; second, the unmeasured potential of integrated saliva-blood diagnostic models; and third, the urgent need for standardized protocols to mitigate methodological heterogeneity, identified by Skallevold et al. as a principal obstacle to advancement [20].

Recent research on salivary genetic biomarkers, such as circulating free DNA (cfDNA), messenger RNA (mRNA), microRNAs (miRNAs) [21], and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), has demonstrated that these molecules can serve as potential diagnostic markers for LC [21–24]. Researchers have identified specific miRNA profiles, such as miR-21 and miR-205, in the saliva of patients with LC, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity for this disease [25–27]. Investigators have shown that cfDNA originating from tumor cells in saliva and other bodily fluids contains unique genetic information that can aid cancer detection [20]. Genetic and epigenetic changes in cfDNA, such as methylation of tumor suppressor genes, have been observed in the saliva of patients with LC and could serve as potential biomarkers for screening [23]. Moreover, specific mutations in genes like EGFR, found in the saliva of patients with LC, can aid in the early detection and accurate diagnosis of the malignant disease [24, 28]. Furthermore, specific lncRNA profiles exhibit altered expression in the saliva of patients with LC, and these changes can serve as sensitive indicators for detecting the disease [29].

Several factors contribute to the inconsistency and non-generalizability of findings across salivary biomarker studies. These include differences in study design, sample processing, and detection methods. Variations in sample sizes affect statistical power [30, 31]. Control group variability, including high-risk individuals, can also impact results [32, 33]. Furthermore, geographic and ethnic differences in biomarker expression limit the universal applicability of findings [34, 35]. Methodological differences, such as inconsistent saliva collection methods (unstimulated vs. stimulated) [36, 37], storage conditions [38], and fractionation techniques (whole saliva vs. supernatant) [39, 40], influence biomarker stability. The primary research question of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to assess the diagnostic accuracy of salivary genetic biomarkers for LC and to evaluate how different biomarker models, including single, mixed, and multiple biomarker approaches, compare in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic performance. This review will advance non-invasive diagnosis methodologies, guiding future research.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses - Diagnostic Test Accuracy (PRISMA-DTA) framework [41] (Supplementary File 1) and the methodological guidelines provided in the Cochrane Handbook [42] were adhered to conduct this systematic review and meta-analysis. The details of this process have been recorded on the PROSPERO website (Registration number: CRD42024614024).

Design of the study and search strategy

The search strategy for the PubMed database was developed using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, focusing on two primary concepts: lung cancer and saliva. Relevant keywords were identified across several databases, including the Cochrane Library, Embase, LIVIVO, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. The search was conducted without limitations on language or publication date and was finalized in June 2024, with the most recent update in October 2024. The detailed search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary File 2 (Table S1). References identified through the search were imported into EndNote X 8.1 for efficient management. During this process, duplicate entries were detected and excluded from the dataset (Fig. 1, PRISMA flow diagram based on Page et al. [16]).

PRISMA flow diagram of the systematic review and meta-analysis

Selection and screening of studies

This systematic review and meta-analysis excluded in vitro and in vivo studies, review papers, meta-analyses, case reports, case series, editorials, and letters. A two-step screening approach was employed to identify studies that met the eligibility criteria. Two independent reviewers initially assessed the titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles. Any disagreements were resolved by consulting a third reviewer to reach a consensus. Individual reviewers then examined the full texts of potentially relevant studies, and only those considered relevant by both initial reviewers were included in the final review. The remaining discrepancies were resolved through thorough discussion among all authors to achieve consensus. The subsequent stages followed the same process, including data extraction, research quality assessment, and data analysis. Two independent reviewers performed these tasks separately, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and negotiation. This ensured all disagreements were addressed and all reviewers reached a consensus.

Eligibility and inclusion criteria

The eligible case-control and cohort studies included in this review focused on identifying and analyzing salivary genetic biomarkers in patients with LC using biopsy as the gold standard for diagnosis. Only studies that met the inclusion criteria and provided calculable data for true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) were incorporated into the meta-analysis.

Data extraction

Relevant data such as authors’ names, publication year, country, diagnostic biopsy procedure, type of control group, biomarker, method of biomarker measurement, the model used for LC detection, reference control gene, study phase (discovery or validation), patients’ status, lung cancer type and stage, and demographic information such as sex, age, and smoking status were extracted from each eligible study. Diagnostic accuracy metrics, including TP, FP, TN, and FN, were also recorded. When the confusion matrix was not directly available in the paper, the necessary data were obtained using digitizer software (GetData Graph Digitizer 2.24) to extract information from the source. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated using Youden’s index [43].

Research quality

We utilized the Quality Assessment Tool for Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) to evaluate the quality of the diagnostic accuracy studies included in this systematic review [44]. To ensure inter-rater reliability, two reviewers independently evaluated the quality of the studies and then discussed their assigned scores for eligible studies to reach a consensus.

Data analysis

The analyses were conducted using STATA (RRID: SCR_012763; Version 17.0), R software package (RRID: SCR_001905; Version 4.2.0), Meta-DiSc (Version 1.4, Madrid, Spain), MetaDTA: Diagnostic Test Accuracy Meta-Analysis (Version 2.1.1, available at https://crsu.shinyapps.io/MetaDTA/) [45], and GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5.0, RRID: SCR_002798). The characteristics of diagnostic tests and the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) were calculated using the split-component synthesis method, with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) included. Hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic (HSROC) curves were also generated to illustrate the balance between sensitivity and specificity. Furthermore, the I-squared (I²) statistic was used to assess the degree of heterogeneity among studies [46]. A random-effects meta-analysis was performed to synthesize study results and obtain overall estimates of diagnostic test characteristics, including the DOR. The AUC and the summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve were plotted to illustrate these findings. In this meta-analysis, we applied a likelihood matrix approach to combine results from individual studies, thereby summarizing the test’s ability to update the pre-test probability of disease. Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test was conducted on the included studies to evaluate potential publication bias. To account for the variety of studies included in this research and to minimize heterogeneity due to different diagnostic models for LC, we organized the studies into three categories based on the biomarkers used for LC detection: the single salivary biomarker model, where only one salivary biomarker is evaluated; the mixed biomarker model refers specifically to models that combine salivary biomarkers with other types of data, such as blood biomarkers or demographic information (e.g., age); and the multiple salivary biomarker model, where multiple salivary biomarkers are used simultaneously for diagnosis. In each model, subgroup meta-analyses were conducted based on study quality. For studies relying on the single salivary biomarker, subgroup meta-analyses were further stratified by biomarker type and study phase, with meta-regression analyses performed according to study quality and phase. Sensitivity analyses were conducted within each model as well.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 illustrates the process of study selection. The initial comprehensive database search identified 5217 studies. After removing duplicate records (n = 728) and unrelated studies (n = 1563) using EndNote X 8.1 software, 2926 studies remained. Screening these abstracts for relevance led to the exclusion of an additional 2698 papers, as they did not align with the research question of this systematic review and meta-analysis. After a more thorough review of the full text of the remaining 228 studies, 219 were excluded for irrelevance. Consequently, nine studies [23–29, 47, 48] were selected for inclusion in the systematic review, and six of these studies [23, 24, 26, 28, 47, 48] were deemed suitable for meta-analysis, as shown in Fig. 1.

A total of thirty study units were included, comprising 6601 subjects (1166 patients with LC and 1626 noncancerous controls, including 1565 healthy individuals and 61 benign pulmonary patients) (Supplementary File 3). Among the nine studies reviewed [23–29, 47, 48], six met the eligibility criteria for inclusion in the meta-analysis [23, 24, 26, 28, 47, 48]. The reasons for excluding three studies from the meta-analysis were as follows: insufficient evidence to use pathology as the diagnostic gold standard [25, 29], sample mix and non-standardized methodology [27], and lack of sufficient data to calculate diagnostic statistics [29]. In this systematic review, seven of the nine studies evaluated single biomarkers in saliva for the diagnosis of LC. Four studies utilized a combined model (saliva plus blood or saliva plus participant age), and two used models that included more than one salivary biomarker. Only two studies lacked information on the treatment status of patients, while six studies reported that patients had received no treatment. Patients in one study had not received anti-tumor treatment (Table 1).

In seven studies, the control group consisted entirely of healthy individuals; in one study, the control group included individuals with benign pulmonary diseases, while in another study, both healthy individuals and those with benign pulmonary conditions served as controls. Among these studies, 6 included a validation phase. Regarding biomarker types, three studies investigated mRNA, three examined miRNA, one focused on circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), one studied cfDNA, and one examined lncRNA. Three studies used quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR), two studies used the Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) method, two studies employed the Electric Field-Induced Release and Measurement (EFIRM) technique, one used Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR), and one study applied the Lateral Flow Assay (LFA) and Duplex-Specific Nuclease (DSN) techniques. In four studies, Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the control reference gene, and in three studies, U6 RNA was employed as the control reference gene.

Quality assessment

Studies identified as having a high risk of bias presented significant concerns regarding the reference standard (Fig. 2). These concerns may stem from a lack of clarity or a mismatch between the reference standard and commonly accepted diagnostic practices, which could result in reduced accuracy in the results. Issues with the reference standard were also reported in studies assessed as having an unclear applicability risk. This uncertainty may be due to a lack of alignment between methods and standards with clinical requirements, or limitations related to the target population. In contrast, studies categorized as having a low risk of bias demonstrated appropriate patient selection and used a valid index test. These studies adhered to clinical standards, making their findings more reliably generalizable to a broader population.

Assessment of the overall methodological quality of the included studies based on QUADAS-2

Similarly, studies assessed as having a low risk of applicability reflected patient selection, making them suitable for clinical applications. However, they paid less attention to other applicability aspects, such as the reference standard. The flow and timing were examined in studies categorized as having an unclear risk of bias. This may be due to inconsistencies in the timing of data collection or differences in study flow, which could contribute to variability in the quality and interpretation of results (Supplementary File 4).

Diagnostic value of the single salivary genetic biomarker

The overall sensitivity and specificity of the single salivary biomarker were 0.72 (95% CI: 0.66–0.78) and 0.73 (95% CI: 0.70–0.76), respectively. The combined positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and DOR were 2.71 (95% CI: 2.32–3.16), 0.38 (95% CI: 0.31–0.48), and 6.86 (95% CI: 4.82–9.76), respectively, with an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.75–0.82). SROC curve of the single salivary genetic biomarker in LC diagnosis is shown in Fig. 3.

Figure S1 of Supplementary File 5 evaluates publication bias using Deeks’ funnel plot method in the meta-analysis of the single salivary genetic biomarker for LC diagnosis. The funnel plot shows the relationship between the DOR and the inverse of the square root of the effective sample size for each included study unit. Based on this analysis, no evidence of publication bias was found in the data, indicating that the overall study results are likely to be meaningful.

The heterogeneity analysis yielded a chi-squared value of 21.74 with 13 degrees of freedom, resulting in a p-value of 0.059. This suggests moderate heterogeneity among the studies, as evidenced by an I-squared statistic of 40.2%, indicating that 40.2% of the variation in the DOR is attributable to heterogeneity across studies. Additionally, the estimated between-study variance (Tau-squared) is 0.17. The test for an odds ratio DOR of 1 produced a z-value of 10.69 with a highly significant p < 0.001, indicating strong evidence against the null hypothesis (DOR = 1) and suggesting a significant association in the overall analysis.

A Spearman’s correlation coefficient of 0.08 was observed, suggesting a possible threshold effect on diagnostic accuracy. The I2 heterogeneity of sensitivity, specificity, PLR, NLR, and DOR were 47.3% (p = 0.03), 60.8% (p < 0.05), 29.2%, 49.3%, and 40.2% (p = 0.06), respectively. In three studies, EGFR was investigated, while FGF19, FRS2, CCNI, and GREB1 were each examined in two studies (Figure S2 of Supplementary File 5). The heterogeneity test table indicates that specific biomarkers, such as FGF19 and CCNI, exhibit high heterogeneity across studies, suggesting that various factors may contribute to this variability. However, the significance test table reveals that some biomarkers, including EGFR, FRS2, and GREB1, differ significantly from DOR = 1, indicating that these biomarkers possess considerable diagnostic value.

Subgroup analysis of the single salivary genetic biomarker

Subgroup analysis was conducted considering the study phase (discovery and validation). The forest plot based on the phase of study revealed that the DOR of 4.55 (95% CI: 2.96–6.99) for the discovery phase and 10.34 (95% CI: 6.97–15.35) for the validation phase (Figure S3 of Supplementary File 5). The validation studies indicate a higher DOR and greater homogeneity, which may suggest higher accuracy in studies within this group. The significance test results showed that the DOR in both groups was significantly greater than 1 overall, indicating effective diagnostic value.

Multiple salivary biomarkers model

The overall sensitivity and specificity of the Multiple salivary genetic biomarkers were 0.88 (95% CI: 0.77–0.94) and 0.75 (95% CI: 0.66–0.83), respectively. The PLR, NLR, and DOR were 3.57 (95% CI: 2.35–5.43), 0.16 (95% CI: 0.08–0.34), and 21.86 (95% CI: 5.85–81.69), respectively, with an AUC of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.90).

The SROC curve for using multiple salivary genetic biomarkers in diagnosing LC is presented in Figure S4 of Supplementary File 5. Additionally, Fig. 5 shows a forest plot of these biomarkers for LC diagnosis, ordered by increasing DOR values. The assessment of publication bias using Deeks’ funnel plot method for the single salivary genetic biomarker in LC diagnosis revealed a statistically significant bias coefficient (p = 0.03).

Mixed biomarker model

The mixed biomarker model refers to model that combines salivary genetic biomarkers data with blood biomarkers or demographic data such as age. The overall sensitivity and specificity of the mixed biomarker model were 0.90 (95% CI: 0.84–0.94) and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.93), respectively. The PLR, NLR, and DOR were 8.98 (95% CI: 6.47–12.46), 0.11 (95% CI: 0.07–0.18), and 76.83 (95% CI: 34.16–172.80), respectively, with an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.92–0.96) (Supplementary File 5, Figure S5). Figure 6 presents a forest plot illustrating these biomarkers for LC diagnosis, arranged in order of increasing DOR values. The bias coefficient for publication bias was examined and found to be statistically insignificant (p = 0.40).

Forest plot meta-analysis of the mixed biomarker model in the diagnosis of LC

After conducting a sensitivity analysis and excluding the study of Kiseleva et al. [15] due to the model used (combination of salivary miRNA biomarker and age while other used combination of salivary and blood mRNA biomarker), the pooled DOR was recalculated to be 115.66 (95% CI: 64.24–208.24) (Supplementary File 5, Figure S6), with an AUC of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–0.98), sensitivity 0.92 (95% CI: 0.88–0.95), and specificity 0.91 (95% CI: 0.87–0.93) (Supplementary File 5, Figure S7). The bias coefficient for publication bias remained non-significant (p = 0.40).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this represents the first meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy specifically evaluating salivary biomarkers for LC detection, addressing a critical evidence gap in the liquid biopsy literature. While previous meta-analyses have assessed biomarkers in other biofluids [19, 49–51], none have comprehensively examined the diagnostic of saliva for LC. Although reviews have discussed salivary biomarkers in LC [11, 52], systematic reviews with meta-analyses provide the quantitative rigor essential for clinical translation. While Mohamed and colleagues addressed blood-based biomarkers (pooled AUC: 0.85) [49], our meta-analysis demonstrated robust pooled sensitivity (0.92), specificity (0.91), (AUC: 0.97), and diagnostic odds ratio (115.66), thereby establishing saliva as a clinically valuable, non-invasive alternative. Our methodology enables the estimation of sensitivity and specificity, a robust assessment of heterogeneity, and evidence-based guidance for guideline development, capabilities that are absent in traditional reviews.

This systematic review of diagnostic test accuracy included nine eligible studies, comprising 30 study units, 1166 patients with LC, and 1626 noncancerous controls, including 1565 healthy individuals and 61 patients with benign pulmonary conditions. The findings of this meta-analysis indicate that mixed biomarker model, particularly one integrating salivary and blood mRNA biomarkers, can significantly enhance the diagnostic accuracy for early LC detection. With the highest DOR and AUC, this model demonstrates superior diagnostic performance compared to other models evaluated, highlighting the potential of multi-source and multi-biomarker approaches. Previous studies have demonstrated that these approaches can effectively capture complex biological alterations associated with cancer in its early stages, thereby increasing the likelihood of accurate and timely cancer detection [53–55]. These findings are aligned with prior research emphasizing the diagnostic value of mRNA biomarkers, given their direct role in regulating gene expression, which provides essential insights into the presence and progression of malignancies [56]. Liu et al. documented analogous gains in their meta-analysis for oral cancer diagnosis, using a multiple-salivary biomarker model, which enhanced diagnostic accuracy [57].

The accessibility and non-invasiveness of saliva-based biomarkers make them attractive for early cancer diagnosis. Salivary sampling can be performed more easily and cost-effectively, potentially enhancing patient compliance [58]. The ease of repeated sampling and monitoring of biological changes over time is an inherent quality of saliva-based tests, which supports their role as valuable tools in early diagnosis. In contrast, a significant drawback in salivary diagnostics is the heterogeneity of methods used across different studies, which can impact results and interpretation. Although the present study, except for two studies [25, 26], is based on studies that provided sufficient and acceptable information on this matter (Table 1). It is recommended that future studies adopt standardized guidelines that specify the type of saliva collected (non-stimulated or stimulated), standardize fasting periods, and control collection timing (morning hours to minimize circadian fluctuations). Standardizing measurement protocols in future studies will help reduce heterogeneity and improve the clinical validity of saliva-based diagnostics [12].

The results indicate that models utilizing single salivary genetic biomarkers demonstrate lower diagnostic performance, with a DOR of 6.86. This finding highlights the limitations of relying on the single biomarker, suggesting that it may not fully capture the genetic and biological diversity of cancer cells or the complexity of the tumor microenvironment. In contrast, multi-biomarker models can detect a broader range of cancer-related biological changes, reducing the likelihood of false-positive or false-negative results. Recent studies also support the idea that combining genetic, protein, and mRNA biomarkers enhances diagnostic accuracy [59].

Since an increase in the case-to-control ratio may lead to a decrease in DOR and affect biomarker performance, this limitation highlights the need for future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts to validate biomarker efficacy. Additionally, the assessment of publication bias in single biomarkers revealed bias, emphasizing the importance of external validation to ensure the consistency of these findings. Furthermore, future studies employing machine learning-based approaches should carefully account for potential overlearning effects, ensuring that trials are representative and generalizable for real cases. This meta-analysis shows that combined salivary and blood-based genetic biomarker models offer high diagnostic accuracy for LC. These findings support their integration into targeted early-detection strategies. Standardized sampling protocols and portable platforms are critical for resource-limited settings. Future standardized, large-scale cohort studies are needed to confirm the clinical utility and cost-effectiveness of this approach. Furthermore, we propose that future research explore the potential of combining the mixed biomarker model with the multiple biomarker model, incorporating multiple salivary and blood biomarkers using AI-based models. The selection of these biomarkers can be based on the findings from the meta-analysis of the proposed models.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlight the promising role of salivary genetic biomarkers, especially when combined with blood-based biomarkers, for the early and accurate diagnosis of LC. Our findings show that a mixed biomarker model incorporating salivary and blood mRNA biomarkers yields the highest DOR and AUC, making it the most effective diagnostic approach among the analyzed models. This superior performance suggests that multi-source biomarker information provides a more comprehensive view of molecular alterations associated with LC, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and supporting timely intervention. These findings establish a foundation for future research and clinical applications, with the potential to transform LC early diagnosis as multi-source, multi-biomarker panels are further refined and validated across diverse populations.
