Subsequent Thromboembolic and Bleeding Events in Patients With Major Bleeding While on Factor Xa Inhibitors
Authors: Takahiro Suzuki, Masahiro Yasaka, Fumitaka Wada, Ryotaro Ide, Kota Suzuki, Shun Kohsaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManagement of patients after bleeding associated with Factor Xa inhibitors (FXa-I) remains challenging due to limited available data on unpredictable sequencing of bleeding and thromboembolic events.Methods and ResultsThis cohort study identified 5,349 patients with FXa-I-related major bleeding. During the 360-day follow-up, bleeding events were highest in the first 90 days (42.2 per 100 person-years), declining to 18.6 by days 271–360. Conversely, thromboembolic events showed a reverse pattern, increasing from 17.3 to 28.6.ConclusionsPatients surviving FXa-I-related bleeding experience a risk transition from bleeding to thromboembolic risk, suggesting the need for time-dependent management strategies.

Factor Xa inhibitors (FXa-I) are cornerstone anticoagulants for thromboembolism. However, major bleeding while on FXa-I remains life-threatening, with poorly understood long-term risk profiles. Clinicians face challenging decisions about anticoagulation resumption, needing to balance the risks of bleeding and thromboembolism. Though both risks evolve over time, temporal pattern data remain scarce. We aimed to evaluate the incidence of bleeding and thromboembolism in patients who survived major bleeding while on FXa-I.

Methods

This longitudinal study examined outcomes for patients who experienced major bleeding on FXa-I, using the Japanese hospital claims database (Medical Data Vision Co., Ltd. [MDV]) between 2010 and 2023. Major bleeding was defined as any bleeding diagnosis, identified by ICD-10 codes, requiring hospitalization. We included adults (≥18 years) hospitalized for bleeding while on FXa-I and discharged. Exclusion criteria comprised: bleeding hospitalization within 6 months prior, FXa-I initiation on admission, severe kidney disease, and pregnancy. We restricted our study to patients followed ≥360 days post-discharge or who died within 360 days. Primary outcomes included post-discharge thromboembolic events (acute coronary syndrome, stroke, venous sinus thrombosis, systemic embolism, venous thromboembolism (VTE), and acute limb ischemia) and bleeding events, identified using ICD-10 codes.

Event rates were calculated per 100 person-years with 95% confidence intervals from discharge until end of follow-up. We analyzed primary outcomes across 4×90-day intervals up to 360 days. Analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SAS viya version 3.5 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Among 8,011 patients hospitalized with FXa-I-related major bleeding (62.4% male, median age 80.0 years), the oral anticoagulants (OAC) being taken were rivaroxaban (24.1%), apixaban (34.2%), and edoxaban (41.7%), with 67.9% resuming anticoagulants. After excluding 569 in-hospital deaths, we focused on 5,349 patients who were discharged and followed for up to 360 days (totaling 12,513.4 person-years)

The incidence of post-discharge events showed temporal patterns. In the first 90 days, thromboembolic events occurred at a rate of 17.3 per 100 person-years (95% confidence interval: 15.2–19.7), while bleeding events were at a substantially higher rate of 42.2 per 100 person-years (38.8–45.8) (Figure). Thromboembolic events increased from 17.3 per 100 person-years in the first 90 days to 28.6 in days 271–360, primarily driven by stroke, the most frequent event, and VTE. Conversely, bleeding rates declined from 42.2 per 100 person-years in the first 90 days to 18.6 (16.2–21.2) in days 271–360. Discussion

Our findings revealed a dynamic temporal pattern in patients surviving major FXa-I bleeding events. Subsequent bleeding risk was highest in the first 90 days post-discharge, then declined, but conversely, thromboembolic event rates steadily increased during follow-up. This temporal pattern suggests that those vulnerable to bleeding may present with hemorrhagic complications after antithrombotic exposure.

Resuming anticoagulation after appropriate hemostatic intervals provides clinical benefit. A Danish study reported a 59% reduction in thromboembolism and 61% decrease in deaths among atrial fibrillation patients who resumed anticoagulation after gastrointestinal bleeding, despite slightly increased rebleeding risk.1 Similarly, restarting anticoagulation after intracranial hemorrhage reduced ischemic events without increasing hemorrhagic complications,2 supporting reinitiation of OAC.

Our analysis demonstrated a time-dependent reversal of risks following FXa-I-associated major bleeding. Therefore, risk assessment should be continuous, as changing CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores predict stroke risk.3 International guidelines recommend tailoring antithrombotic strategies through individualized assessment with annual reassessments.4 Our study results emphasize the need for longitudinal evaluation, as static tools cannot capture evolving risks from aging, comorbidities, and medication changes.

Study Limitations

Focusing on patients surviving index hospitalization may underestimate event rates, and reliance on the MDV database, with monthly event timing, limited clinical variables, and no cross-institutional linkage, may have introduced selection bias and limited generalizability. Furthermore, ICD-10-based identification could not distinguish bleeding severity, site concordance with the index event, or allow reliable comparison between patients who did and did not resume anticoagulation.

Conclusions

This large-scale cohort study revealed a critical temporal shift in risk patterns among FXa-I-associated major bleeding survivors. Early post-discharge periods show high subsequent bleeding risk, which transitions to increasing thromboembolic risk. This inverse relationship provides guidance for anticoagulation decisions in this population.
