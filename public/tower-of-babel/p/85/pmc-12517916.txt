Inflammatory Bowel Disease and Adverse Outcomes After Total Joint Arthroplasty
Authors: Mohammad-Taha Pahlevan-Fallahy, Farhad Shaker, Amir-Mohammad Asgari, Sadra Behrouzieh, Ronak Jalali, Sourena Sharifkashani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) is associated with systemic inflammation and which might influence surgical outcomes. As total joint arthroplasty (TJA) becomes increasingly common in this population as they age, this study aimed to evaluate whether IBD is associated with increased postoperative complications following total hip or knee arthroplasty.Methods:We performed a systematic review of studies including patients with and without IBD undergoing TJA. Databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase through February 24, 2025. Eligible studies included adults with a confirmed diagnosis of IBD who underwent TJA. Meta-analyses were conducted using random-effects models with 95% confidence intervals.Results:Fourteen studies comprising 2,651,318 patients (86,581 with IBD) met inclusion criteria. IBD was significantly associated with increased risks of PJI (OR: 1.47), hospital readmission (OR: 1.39), pneumonia (OR: 1.92), PTE (OR: 1.61), VTE (OR: 1.59), DVT (OR: 1.60), ileus (OR: 5.7), C. difficile infection (OR: 3.3), MI (OR: 2.12), CVA (OR: 2.90), UTI (OR: 2.31), sepsis (OR: 1.83), respiratory failure (OR: 2.17), revision surgery (OR: 1.38), periprosthetic fracture (OR: 1.82), wound (OR: 1.52) and implant complications (OR: 1.62). Length of hospital stay was marginally longer (SMD: 0.06). Mortality and non-home discharge did not differ significantly between groups.Conclusion:Patients with IBD undergoing TJA are at an elevated risk for some postoperative complications, especially infections, thromboembolic events, and revision surgeries. These findings support personalized management strategies in this population.Level of Evidence:Level IIIa. See Instructions for Authors for a complete description of levels of evidence.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has now emerged as one of the major global health burdens; it mainly comprises 2 major diseases, namely, Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). IBD cases are diagnosed between the ages of 20 and 40 years although there is a significant second peak of diagnoses for individuals older than 60 years. The site of the disease in the intestines exerts a profound impact on local physiological activities such as barrier integrity and, moreover, has implications on more distal organs such as bones and joints in both human beings and animal models1. Besides this, immunomodulatory therapies given for the treatment of IBD disrupt normal bone metabolism and predispose elderly patients to a considerable risk of osteoporosis, osteonecrosis, and fragility fractures. Nutritional deficiencies and bone loss are common in patients with IBD because of impaired micronutrient absorption and chronic intestinal inflammation, all of which have the potential to adversely affect surgical outcomes2,3. Recent series have reported conflicting results regarding the influence of IBD on postoperative recovery after joint arthroplasty4. This variability thus underlines the need for an evidence-based approach in combining cohort data and arriving at more concrete findings applicable to modern surgical practices5. Based on clinical significance and relevance to surgical or medical domains, we conducted this study to compare primary and secondary medical and surgical outcomes. Primary medical and surgical outcomes were (1) mortality, (2) readmission, and (3) periprosthetic joint infection (PJI). Secondary medical and surgical outcomes included (4) length of stay (LOS), (5) pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), (6) venous thromboembolism (VTE), (7) deep vein thrombosis (DVT), (8) Clostridioides difficile infection, (9) ileus, (10) respiratory failure, (11) transfusion, (12) myocardial infarction (MI), (13) cerebrovascular accident (CVA), (14) urinary tract infection (UTI), (15) emergency department (ED) visits, (16) nonhome discharge, (17) renal complications, (18) anemia, (19) revision surgery, (20) wound complications, and (21, 22, and 23) periprosthetic fracture, dislocation, and mechanical complications.

Materials and Methods

This study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025644677) and was conducted according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, the checklist of which can be seen in Supplementary Material 16.

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

We included cohort studies that included patients older than 18 years who underwent total knee or hip arthroplasty with UC or CD that had a minimal follow-up of 90 days. The exclusion criteria were case reports, conference abstracts, letters to the editor, commentaries, review studies, animal studies, and studies without full-text availability.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

A systematic search was conducted in 4 databases: Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase using a combination of MeSH terms and other keywords for IBD cases undergoing total arthroplasty to identify all relevant studies published since inception up until February 24, 2025, without any language restrictions. The complete search strategy can be found in Supplementary Material 1.

Study Selection Process

After deduplication, the titles and abstracts of all identified studies were first separately screened by 2 reviewers (AM.A. and F.S.). Then, the full texts of relevant articles were screened to determine their eligibility. Disagreements in every stage were resolved by consulting a third author (MT.PF.).

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Data extraction was performed by 2 independent authors (AM.A. and F.S.) using a predesigned excel spreadsheet. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the quality of the included studies7. Selection, comparability, and outcome were the main domains assessed by this scale, and a score of ≥7 is considered high quality.

Study End Points

PJI, mortality, and readmission rates were selected as primary outcomes because of their clinical significance and direct impact on patient survival, quality of life, and long-term prognosis. All other outcomes, namely, length of stay, PTE, VTE, DVT, C. difficile infection, ileus, respiratory failure, transfusion, MI, CVA, UTI, emergency department visits, nonhome discharge, renal complications, anemia, revision surgery, wound complications, periprosthetic fracture, dislocation, and mechanical complications, were considered secondary end points of our study.

Statistical Analysis

The extracted data were meta-analyzed using R (version 4.4.2), employing meta package; odds ratios (ORs) and standard mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for binary and continuous outcomes, respectively, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A random-effects model was used for meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneities, respectively. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the results. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test. All analyses were conducted at a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 419 records were identified through database searching. After deduplication, 244 were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 220 were excluded, leaving 24 full-text articles to be assessed for eligibility. On full-text review, 10 were excluded for various reasons. Ultimately, 14 studies were included in our study8-21. The main reasons for excluding full-text articles were their study populations (n = 4) and study types (n = 6). The PRISMA chart of the study selection process is presented in Supplementary Figure E1.

Summary of Included Studies

Fourteen articles were finally included in our study, with a total of 2,651,318 cases of TJA (850,160 TKAs, and 1,801,158). A total of 86,581 cases of IBD (53,720 UC, 19,197 CD, 35 cases of other types, and 13,629 of unspecified types) were included. A summary of included studies is provided in Table I.

Summary of Included Studies

Quality Assessment

Of the included studies, 12 studies had a quality score of 7, considering their quality to be “good,” and 2 had a score of 6, considering them of “fair” quality. A summary of quality assessment of included studies is provided in Supplementary Table E1.

Meta-Analysis: Primary Outcomes

Primary Medical Outcomes

Supplementary Table E2 provides a summary of results of this meta-analysis. Compared with non-IBD patients, the patients with IBD did not have a higher incidence of mortality (OR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.87-1.03; p = 0.214). Fig. 1-A shows a forest plot for mortality. The leave-one-out analysis was consistent with the main analysis (Figure E2); Figure E3 shows the funnel plot to this primary outcome. In the subgroup analysis, there was no significant difference regarding the type of IBD (Crohn's: OR, 1.41; 95% CI, 0.49-4.03; ulcerative colitis: OR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.08-2.42, P for interaction = 0.47). Similarly, no significance was observed when accounting for the type of arthroplasty (total knee: OR, 1.08; 95% CI, 0.40-2.89; total hip: OR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.87-1.03, p for interaction = 0.37). Figures E4 and E5 show a forest plot for each of the subgroup analyses, respectively.

Forest plot of mortality (Fig. 1-A), readmission (Fig. 1-B), PJI (Fig. 1-C), and length of stay (Fig. 1-D) in included studies. PJI = periprosthetic joint infection.

Moreover, patients with IBD had a higher incidence of hospital readmission compared with non-IBD patients (OR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.29-1.49; p < 0.0001). Fig. 1-B shows a forest plot for hospital readmission. The leave-one-out analysis was consistent with the main analysis (Figure E6); Figure E7 shows the funnel plot to this primary outcome. In the subgroup analysis, there was no significant difference in the type of IBD (Crohn's: OR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.28-1.67; ulcerative colitis: OR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.19-1.46, p for interaction = 0.28). Furthermore, the type of arthroplasty did not result in a significant difference between the 2 groups (total knee: OR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.35-1.47; total hip: OR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.14-1.70, p for interaction = 0.98). Figures E8 and E9 show a forest plot for each of the subgroup analyses, respectively.

Primary Surgical Outcomes

Another primary outcome of our study was PJI, and our analysis showed a higher incidence rate in patients with IBD compared with the non-IBD patients (OR, 1.47; 95% CI, 1.30-1.65; p < 0.0001). Fig. 1-C demonstrates a forest plot for this outcome. Moreover, the leave-one-out analysis showed that omission of no study changed the results (Figure E10); Figure E11 shows the funnel plot for PJI. Because of the insufficient number of studies, subgroup analysis was not performed for this primary outcome.

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary Medical Outcomes

IBD was associated with a slightly longer stay at hospital (SMD, 0.06; 95% CI, 0.01-0.10; P = 0.020) (Fig. 1-D). Besides, patients with IBD were significantly more prone to pneumonia (OR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.42-2.61; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2-A). Patients with IBD also had higher rates of pulmonary thromboembolic events compared with the non-IBD patients (OR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.25-2.09; p = 0.0003) (Fig. 2-B). In a similar manner, IBD was associated with significantly higher odds of venous thromboembolism (OR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.26-2.03; p = 0.0001) (Fig. 2-C). In addition, patients with IBD had higher rates of deep vein thrombosis events compared with the non-IBD patients (OR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.24-2.05; p = 0.0003) (Fig. 2-D). Moreover, patients with IBD were significantly more prone to C. difficile infection (OR, 3.3; 95% CI, 2.9-3.7; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2-E) and ileus (OR, 5.7; 95% CI, 4.9-6.7; p< 0.0001) (Fig. 2-F). Respiratory failure was also significantly higher in IBD patients (OR, 2.17; 95% CI, 1.53-3.07; P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2G). The need for blood transfusion was significantly different between the 2 groups (OR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.02-1.31; p = 0.0199) (Fig. 2-H). The occurrence of both myocardial infarction (OR, 2.12; 95% CI, 1.72-2.63; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3-A) and cerebrovascular accident (OR, 2.9; 95% CI, 2.3-3.7; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3-B) was significantly higher in patients with IBD. Patients with IBD also had higher rates of urinary tract infection (OR, 2.31; 95% CI, 1.84-2.89; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3-C) and sepsis (OR, 1.83; 95% CI, 1.19-2.82; p = 0.0065) (Fig. 3-D) compared with their non-IBD counterparts. Furthermore, patients with IBD experienced remarkably higher emergency department visits (OR, 1.4; 95% CI, 1.1-1.82; P = 0.0066) (Fig. 3-E). The odds of nonhome discharge for the 2 groups of patients were not significantly different (OR, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.96-1.2; p = 0.2191) (Fig. 3-F). In addition, the odds of renal complications were significantly raised in patients with IBD (OR, 2.0; 95% CI, 1.02-4.07; p = 0.0457) (Fig. 3-G); the odds of anemia were not, however, significantly different (OR, 1.72; 95% CI, 0.9-3.4; p = 0.115) (Fig. 3-H).

Secondary Surgical Outcomes

Considering surgical outcomes, there was a significantly higher need for revision surgery in patients with IBD (OR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.08-1.77; p = 0.011) (Fig. 4-A), in addition to significantly higher occurrences of periprosthetic fracture (OR, 1.82; 95% CI, 1.28-2.59; p = 0.0009) (Fig. 4-B). Patients with IBD also had higher rates of wound complications (OR, 1.52; 95% CI, 1.34-1.74; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4-C). There were an increased odds of implant complications in patients with IBD (OR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.29-2.05; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4-D). The differences in periprosthetic dislocation and prosthetic mechanical complications were not significantly increased (p = 0.055, and 0.078, respectively) (Figs. 4-E and 4-G).

Discussion

Our systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that patients with IBD had a higher risk of PJI, Readmission, in addition to a higher risk of PTE, VTE, pneumonia, LOS, DVT, transfusion, respiratory failure, MI, CVA, UTI, sepsis, ED visits, ileus, C. difficile infection, revision surgery, periprosthetic fractures, and wound complications, implants complications compared with their non-IBD counterparts.

Several ways have been discussed by which patients with IBD are put in a higher risk for infections, also found by our study22,23; IBD impairs the epithelial barrier of the lungs in addition to the intestines, which is associated with higher rates of pneumonia24. Along with gut microbiota, dysbiosis25 causes the enterogenous spread of bacteria into the bloodstream causing further complications26. Regarding PJI, several factors are suspected, which include intraoperative microbial contamination and hematogenous bacterial spread27. In several studies on IBD cases with PJI28-30, synovial fluid cultures had indicated intestinal flora such as Enterococcus faecalis and Escherichia coli which collectively led to the formation of the “Trojan horse” hypothesis. This hypothesis states that the bacteria which happen to overcome the deranged intestinal barrier are then contained inside the immune cells only to cause an infection elsewhere in the body8,31. Other factors affecting the higher rates of postoperative infections in patients with IBD are their immunocompromised status32 and long-term treatment with corticosteroids22. Recent advancements in IBD treatment have introduced various medical regimens, including aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunomodulators, biologics, and Janus kinase inhibitors33. Despite these options, few studies report the specific treatments patients with IBD receive before arthroplasty. A study by Oghenewoma et al. found that patients with untreated UC, as well as those on steroids, 5-aminosalicylic acid drugs, or immunomodulators, all had increased odds of adverse events after surgery, with risk increasing alongside treatment intensity17. As treatment details are a key confounding factor, future studies should adopt a standardized IBD therapy classification and make treatment reporting mandatory to improve data quality and comparability.

The higher rate of hospital readmissions in patients with IBD can also explain a part of the postoperative complications in patients with IBD, such as infections and thromboembolic events. As outpatient arthroplasty has become increasingly popular, patients with IBD may also benefit from it if they receive appropriate perioperative care34. Medically complex patients have been shown to have higher ED visits and admission rates in the first 90 days post-TJA35. These patients still benefit from TJA because of the increased quality of life if given the required care for their pre-existing condition. Our analysis showed that patients with IBD are prone to a higher risk of PTE after TJA. IBD activity per se leads to a hypercoagulable state through increased inflammatory cytokines36. A meta-analysis by Arvanitakis et al. reported a two-fold increased risk for VTE in patients with IBD compared with non–IBD-matched cohorts37. On the other hand, TJA counts as a major orthopaedic surgery which itself causes an inflammatory stress response and brings upon a higher risk for PTE38. Because glucocorticoids, commonly used in IBD treatment, are evidenced to induce a hypercoagulable state, alternative medications would better be considered for patients with IBD, such as immunomodulators and Tumor Necrosis Factor inhibitors39.

Limitations

All included studies were retrospective, introducing limitations such as unmeasured confounders, registry errors, and inconsistent end point definitions. Only a limited number of studies met inclusion criteria, preventing meta-regression and increasing heterogeneity risk.

Despite having a minimal follow-up duration of 90 days, there seemed to be considerable variations in the follow-up period for each outcome in studies. This might introduce a detection bias arising from differences in temporality.

The type of IBD treatment regimen, though important and highlighted in recent orthopaedic research, was not consistently reported in our included studies. This limited our ability to perform subgroup or meta-regression analyses. Future studies should collect detailed treatment data to enable more robust analysis of its impact on postoperative outcomes22.

Conclusion

To sum up, this study reported that patients with IBD are at higher risk of numerous postoperative complications after TJA. Surgeons are required to account for the activity status and the ongoing treatment regimen of IBD in the TJA candidates to reduce the incidence of unwanted and potentially life-threatening complications.
