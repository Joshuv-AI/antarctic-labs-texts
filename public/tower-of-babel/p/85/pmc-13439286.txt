Anatomy-driven dysphagia after technically successful peroral endoscopic myotomy in sigmoid-shape achalasia: short-term palliation with temporary fully covered self-expanding metal stents
Authors: Ioan Cristian Nedelcu, Octavian M. Mocanu, Daniel V. Balaban, Iulia C. Enache, Marina Balaban, Raluca S. Costache, Mariana Jinga, Carmen Fierbinteanu-Braticevici
Published: 2026

Since the first use of self-expanding metal stents (SEMS) for the palliation of malignant stenosis in 1990 by Domschke et al.,1 the indications for esophageal stenting (ES) have extended to encompass both benign strictures and esophageal fistulas. Although esophageal achalasia can be considered a benign esophageal stricture, SEMS have not been adopted as a feasible treatment for achalasia. Therapeutic interventions focus on peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) and laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM) as first-line therapies for achalasia, with pneumatic balloon dilation (PBD) as an alternative.2-4 The current guidelines recommend against the temporary use of an esophageal SEMS to treat dysphagia in patients with achalasia.2,5 Nonetheless, fully covered stents have been explored in this setting as a potentially safe and effective alternative for carefully selected patients.6

A 55-year-old woman presented with severe achalasia (Eckardt score, 7). High-resolution manometry (HRM) could not be performed because she suffered from a long-lasting disease with a sigmoid-shaped (S2) megaesophagus (Fig. 1), which prevented the passage of the HRM catheter. The treatment modalities were discussed with the patient, and POEM was selected using the posterior approach. The cardia was located 40 cm from the incisors, and submucosal tunneling was initiated 11 cm above the cardia. Both tunneling and myotomy were technically challenging because of the altered esophageal anatomy. The tunnel was extended 3 cm below the esophagogastric junction (EGJ), and this position was confirmed using the double scope technique; a slim (5.4 mm) scope was inserted into the esophagus alongside the main scope, which remained in the tunnel. The slim scope was advanced into the stomach and retroflexed. Transillumination through the gastric mucosa from the main scope was observed on the posterior wall of the stomach, confirming that the tunnel extended to the posterior gastric wall, approximately 3 cm below the EGJ (Fig. 2). The total myotomy length was approximately 11 cm (9 cm esophageal myotomy and 2 cm gastric extension). POEM was completed successfully without complications, and the postprocedural course was uneventful. The patient was discharged the day after undergoing a follow-up esophagogram, which showed reasonable passage of contrast into the stomach, and a clear liquid diet was initiated. Two weeks after POEM, the patient was readmitted with persistent vomiting and intolerance of oral intake. Upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy revealed a dilated sigmoid-shaped esophagus with abundant retained fluid, consistent with stasis. In contrast, a widely patent cardia with easy endoscopic passage supported effective myotomy. A contrast esophagogram showed complete retention of contrast in the esophagus 5 minutes after ingestion, despite apparently reduced luminal dilation (compared with the pre-POEM findings). This finding was unexpected because no muscle spasms were encountered endoscopically. We concluded that the esophageal anatomy was the cause (the double sigmoid shape, combined with complete lack of esophageal peristalsis, prevented contrast from passing into the stomach). After discussing possible therapeutic approaches with the patient, ES was chosen. A 16×120-mm fully covered SEMS (FC-SEMS) was successfully placed. The procedure was rapid and uneventful. The effect was immediate; the patient started liquid intake on the same day, with complete symptom remission. A subsequent esophagogram showed complete passage of the substance into the stomach (Fig. 3A). The patient was discharged and scheduled for a one-month follow-up with stent removal. Unfortunately, 2 weeks later, the patient was readmitted for melena. Her hemoglobin level was 8 g/dL (a decrease of 4 g/dL compared with that at discharge 2 weeks prior). Upper GI endoscopy revealed no active bleeding. While the stent was in place, it had partially migrated distally and impacted the greater curvature, causing mucosal ulcerations (a plausible bleeding source). The stent was removed; however, dysphagia recurred the following day. Contrast-enhanced esophagography showed complete esophageal stasis. Another FC-SEMS device was inserted across the EGJ. A 22×160 mm stent was placed, and oral intake was resumed.

Both implants were fully covered esophageal SEMS (non-valved). We avoided antireflux/valved designs to prevent any impedance to esophageal emptying in achalasia. To prevent migration, other methods such as endoscopic suturing and over-the-scope clip fixation, and other stent types, including “umbrella-type”, were considered but were not feasible. For the second type of stent, migration risk mitigation relied on its larger diameter and length. Post-placement fluoroscopy confirmed correct stent positioning across the EGJ. Because the patient remained asymptomatic with adequate oral intake, interim endoscopic imaging was not performed until the scheduled visit. The patient was counseled to return in case of any alarming symptoms. A follow-up visit was scheduled after one month, but the patient postponed it because she felt well. The patient returned 6 weeks after stent placement without any complaints. Her Eckardt score was 3. Moderate esophageal stasis was found on controlled endoscopy; however, there were no stents. The esophageal lumen was still dilated, and passage through the cardia was easy. An esophageal stent was found in the stomach (fully migrated). It was retrieved using a polypectomy snare. Repeated timed contrast esophagograms demonstrated prompt gastric passage (Fig. 3B) and minimal (less than 25%) esophageal stasis at 5 minutes (Fig 3C). The patient was discharged without any further intervention.

A tortuous (sigmoid) esophagus in patients with achalasia reflects long-standing disease and is typically accompanied by marked dilation. This altered anatomy culminates in megaesophagus, which is considered the end-stage (“terminal”) form of achalasia. In this setting, endoscopic palliation with pneumatic dilation or botulinum toxin injections (BTI) is considered ineffective.7 Both POEM and LHM can be technically challenging and are best performed in selected cases in experienced centers.4 The “father” of POEM, Prof. Inoue, in his first published paper on endoscopic myotomy (2010), listed sigmoid type 2 (S2) esophagus as an exclusion criterion for POEM.8 Surgical options in these situations include both LHM and esophagectomy. However, there is no consensus on the best approach. Some surgeons favor myotomy, whereas others consider resection the first-line treatment.9 With the widespread adoption of POEM, new data have emerged. Studies have shown good clinical outcomes with POEM in sigmoid-type achalasia in terms of long-lasting symptom relief and even morphological improvement.10

In our case, POEM was performed after the patient was informed about the technical challenges and risks of clinical failure. Although early imaging showed contrast passage through the cardia, symptoms recurred after the first two weeks. The discordance between technical success (reduced apparent dilation and a widely patent cardia) and clinical failure (symptoms and complete contrast retention) prompted the conclusion that dysphagia was probably attributable to structural distortion of the esophagus in the absence of peristalsis rather than to ongoing muscle contraction. However, different therapeutic approaches were still required. As the index myotomy was posterior, an anterior myotomy (either re-POEM or LHM) was considered; however, the patient was reluctant to undergo another general anesthesia or surgery. In addition, clinical failure after the first myotomy raised concerns about the potential success of another myotomy. Surgical esophagectomy is the least desirable option and is not readily feasible on short notice. Other endoscopic alternatives include BTI, PBD, and ES. BTI was rapidly ruled out because muscle contractions were not an issue. PBD is also unlikely to be effective and carries a significant risk of perforation due to recent POEM. While stenting after POEM is usually reserved for complications, we decided to use a temporary FC-SEMS to palliate dysphagia and potentially straighten the tortuous esophagus. We anticipated that by the time of stent removal (one month), the esophagus would have remodeled, and the recent myotomy, together with stent-induced dilation, would be functionally effective. The stent dimensions were carefully considered. Although larger stents should provide better outcomes, a wide stent poses concerns about perforation, given the recent POEM. Therefore, we opted for the narrowest available stent (16 mm diameter, 120 mm length). To prevent migration, the proximal flange was anchored using two through-the-scope clips. As stent removal was required earlier than anticipated, a second stent was required. Stent length was debated because a longer stent was less likely to migrate. A longer and wider SEMS (22×160 mm) was chosen. Stent dimensions were chosen pragmatically: the first SEMS (16×120 mm) prioritized safety after myotomy, whereas the second SEMS (22×160 mm) was selected to provide greater radial force and prevent early migration. No computed tomography-based luminal measurements were used, and selection was guided by endoscopic impression and contrast esophagography. Paradoxically, the longer and wider stent fully migrated into the stomach. The exact interval of optimal stent positioning before migration could not be determined, which limited the precise attribution of the remodeling effect to a defined in situ dwell time. Retrieval was challenging because the extraction thread was degraded by gastric acid. Snare capture at the mid-stent resulted in folding when pulled upward. Transit through the cardia also produced incidental dilation. The benefits appeared to last, as no further treatment was needed after the last procedure, and the calculated Eckardt score maintained a value of 3 on subsequent visits.

Taken together, our experience in this case suggests a pragmatic stepwise approach to treat sigmoid-shaped achalasia. Confirming EGJ patency after POEM and recognizing anatomy-driven dysphagia led to the consideration of temporary SEMS placement as a complementary therapy. This strategy offers short-term relief and potential long-term benefits after stent removal. Nevertheless, these findings warrant cautious use and further research.
