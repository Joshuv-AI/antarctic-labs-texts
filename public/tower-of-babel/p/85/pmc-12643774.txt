Network pharmacology and molecular docking analysis of Yishenqingzhuo oral liquid for chronic kidney disease
Authors: Aiping Zhao, Yuliang Qiu, Bishan Chen, Shangzheng Dai, Wenjie Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To examine the potential mechanisms of Yishenqingzhuo oral liquid (YSQZ) as a treatment for chronic kidney disease (CKD). The Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology and HERB databases were used to identify active ingredients and targets of YSQZ, and the GEO database was employed to screen CKD-related targets. Protein–protein interaction networks and enrichment analysis was conducted. Core targets and ingredients were identified from the protein–protein interaction network. Immune infiltration analysis was performed, and core targets and ingredients were validated via molecular docking. There were 97 active ingredients, 479 drug-predicted targets, 185 potential targets that were the same among the 479 drug-predicted targets and the 7193 CKD-related targets, 98 potential targets with interactions, and enrichment of 165 drug-related pathways. The topological analysis identified 29 core targets and 62 core ingredients. tumor protein 53, estrogen receptor 1, cyclin-dependent kinase 1, phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit delta, mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 , and mitogen-activated protein kinase 3 were the key targets, and quercetin, caffeic acid, aloe-emodin, kaempferol, and palmatine were the core ingredients. Immune infiltration analysis showed that memory B cells, naive B cells, and activated NK cells differed markedly between the CKD and normal groups. YSQZ is hypothesized to act on CKD through multiple ingredients targeting multiple genes and signaling pathways. However, these findings are based on 1 GEO dataset with a relatively small sample size. Further experimental and clinical validation is needed to confirm the proposed mechanisms.

1. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a prevalent and serious health problem that affects approximately 10% of the population worldwide.[1] The global burden of disease study estimated that CKD will be the fifth leading cause of death by 2040.[2] Currently, Western medical treatments for CKD focus on control of proteinuria, blood pressure, anemia, acid-base balance, calcium and phosphorus metabolism-related disorders, and lipid levels.[3] However, these conventional measures often fail to prevent multiple complications of CKD. Moreover, the combination of certain traditional Chinese medicines (TCMs) with Western medicines can provide better clinical outcomes for CKD patients than Western medicines alone. For example, a multicenter, prospective, nonrandomized, controlled study in China[4] confirmed that TCM combined with Western medicine delayed the need for dialysis in patients with CKD. Additionally, several randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses showed that use of TCM as an adjuvant to conventional medications may benefit patients with CKD.[5] TCM offers a holistic, multi-target approach, and advances in databases and computational methods have enabled systematic evaluation of complex herbal formulas.[6,7] The challenges such as data standardization and reproducibility in network pharmacology studies highlight the need for cautious interpretation and further validation.

The effectiveness of TCM in treating CKD is attributable to its holistic approach to diagnosis and treatment, which are considered prerequisites for assuring safety. TCM considers the pathogenesis of CKD to be characterized by patterns of “root vacuity” and “tip repletion.” Root vacuity refers to deficiencies in qi, blood, yin, and yang, and can be accompanied by complex patterns, such as dampness, turbidity, toxicity, and stasis. Thus, because CKD affects the kidneys and other internal organs, disease progression is associated with yin deficiency in the liver and kidneys and with dampness, turbidity, stasis, and toxins. Consequently, TCM treatment of CKD begins with nourishment of the liver and kidneys, and this can expel wind-damp, strengthen the bones and muscles, dissipate turbidity, and clear blood stasis.

Among many TCM prescriptions for CKD, Yishenqingzhuo Oral Liquid (YSQZ) was chosen because it is an empirical formula developed by Professor Shiwei Ruan, a nationally recognized TCM expert. It has been routinely applied as an in-hospital preparation at the Affiliated People’s Hospital of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine for the treatment of CKD, with remarkable clinical efficacy. Our previous clinical studies[8,9] have confirmed the safety of YSQZ when used to treat patients with CKD, and that this treatment also improved clinical symptoms and proteinuria. Additionally, YSQZ may also enhance renal function and exert antioxidant effects. Despite these promising results, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear.

YSQZ contains Semen Plantaginis, Radix Cyathulae, Radix et Rhizoma Rhei, Poria, Cortex Phellodendri, Herba Pyrolae chinensis, Cortex Moutan, Herba Taxilli, Rhizoma Dioscoreae, Fructus Corni, Rhizoma Anemarrhee, Radix Rehmanniae, and Herba Serissae. The Radix Rehmanniae can cool the blood and nourish the yin of the liver and kidneys, and serves as the “sovereign” of the formula. Rhizoma Dioscoreae can nourish the yin of the spleen, invigorate the kidneys, and nourish the essence. Fructus Corni tones the qi of the kidneys, adds essence, and serves as the “minister.” Poria can strengthen the spleen and relieve the dampness. The combination of Cortex Phellodendri, Cortex Moutan, and Rhizoma Anemarrhee can nourish the yin and eliminate fire, thereby restraining the warmth and astringency of Fructus Corni. Radix Cyathulae promotes blood circulation and removes stasis, and the combination of Radix et Rhizoma Rhei, Herba Serissae with Semen Plantaginis removes dampness. Similarly, Herba Taxilli and Herba Pyrolae chinensis can eliminate wind-dampness and strengthen the bones and muscles. All of these ingredients together are considered the “assistant,” and Radix Cyathulae is commonly used as a “courier.”

Therefore, to bridge the gap between its well-documented clinical effects and the lack of mechanistic understanding, we applied network pharmacology and molecular docking to characterize the active ingredients and molecular targets of YSQZ in CKD. Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis was performed to investigate its potential immunomodulatory effects. These findings may provide a theoretical basis for subsequent experimental and clinical research.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Active ingredients and predicted targets of YSQZ

The TCMs Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP; https://www.tcmsp-e.com › load_intro.php) is the most commonly used pharmacological database and analytical platform for network pharmacology studies of traditional herbal medicines.[10] Oral bioavailability (OB ≥30%) and drug-likeness (DL ≥0.18) were used as the screening criteria to identify the active ingredients and predicted target proteins. The target proteins retrieved from the UniProt database (http://www.uniprot.org/) were then matched to the corresponding gene names.[11]

Herbal medicines that were not listed in the TCMSP database were accessed in the HERB database (http://herb.ac.cn/), which provides the most comprehensive list of herbs and ingredients by integrating several herbal medicine databases.[12] The active ingredients were screened according to Lipinski rule of 5 (i.e., molecular weight ≤500 Da, H-bond donors ≤5, octanol-water partition coefficient ≤5, and H-bond acceptors ≤10).[13] Furthermore, the SwissADME database (http://www.swissadme.ch/) was screened following the principle of “GL absorption selects high, Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Ean, Megge together with at least 3 yes.”[14] Finally, the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was employed to obtain the SMILES strings, which were then imported into the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) to identify valid predicted targets (probability > 0).

2.2. Visualization of the predicted targets of active ingredients

The TCM names, active ingredients, and predicted targets were imported into Cytoscape version 3.10.1 for visualization. Then, different colors and shapes were used to distinguish the different TCMs, active ingredients (with duplicates distinguished separately), and predicted targets. The degree values were calculated, and the point sizes were adjusted accordingly.

2.3. CKD-related target collection

GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) is an open-source functional genomics database that can be used to search and download experimental and curated gene expression profiles.[15] This database was searched using microarray data for CKD with standard screening criteria (adj. P <.05, |log2(FC)| ≥1) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), i.e., CKD-related targets. Volcano plots were prepared using the Bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).[16] The GEO platform data files were downloaded, probe IDs were matched to gene names, and a heatmap was used to present the DEGs.

2.4. Identification of potential targets and protein–protein interaction networks

The potential targets were obtained by the intersection of predicted targets of the active ingredients of TCM with CKD-related targets, and presented as Venn diagrams using Venny version 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/). The potential interaction targets were also imported into the STRING PPI database (http://string-db.org/)[17] by selecting “Multiple Proteins” with a confidence interval > 0.900, and hiding the noninteracting nodes to obtain the corresponding PPI network. The network data were imported into Cytoscape for visualization. The degree value was calculated to determine the importance of nodes, and the size and color of points were adjusted accordingly. Moreover, the thickness and color of the lines were adjusted according to the combined score value. The transparency was also adjusted to refine the image.

2.5. Enrichment analysis

The enrichment analysis employed gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses in the Metascape (https://metascape.org/) database of gene annotation and analysis.[18] The potential interacting targets were analyzed in 4 approaches: KEGG Pathway, GO molecular functions (MF), GO biological processes (BP), and GO cellular components (CC). These results were visualized via the Bioinformatics platform. A Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate was to adjust p-values.

2.6. Identification of the key targets and core ingredients of the TCM

The PPI network data (section 2.4) were screened using standard criteria (greater than or equal to the medians of degree, closeness, and betweenness) to obtain the core targets. The core targets were then imported into Cytoscape to identify the core ingredients. The network was then regenerated using the core ingredients and the core targets to calculate the degree value. Further, the size of the points was adjusted according to the degree value, and the results were saved as a CSV file. Finally, the points were ordered according to the degree value and the amount of differential expression in the GEO dataset to determine the key target points, with presentation as a box plot.

2.7. CIBERSORT immune infiltration analysis

CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) estimates the abundance of cell types in a mixed population using gene expression data.[19] The research dataset for CKD was obtained from the GEO database and used to upload the microarray expression matrix on the CIBERSORTx website, and infiltration analysis of 22 types of immune cells was then performed. The results were downloaded and visualized on the Bioinformatics platform.

2.8. Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed for the key targets and core ingredients using Pymol and AutoDockTools version 1.5.7.[20,21] The 3D structures of macromolecular proteins (key targets) were downloaded from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Pymol was used to remove water and solvent molecules from the macromolecular proteins, and added as “perhydro” for receptors using AutoDockTools. The PubChem database was used to download the molecular structures of small proteins (core constituents), and the resulting SDF format was converted to the mol2 format by OpenBabel version 2.4.1. Further, “perhydro” was set as a ligand using AutoDockTools, followed by establishment of the docking box. Subsequently, autogrid4 was run to set the docking parameters and arithmetic methods, and autodock4 was run to save the results. The data for binding energy were plotted on a heatmap using the Bioinformatics platform, and the docking results were visualized using Pymol. The binding energy was used to evaluate the strength and stability of the ligand-protein interactions. A binding energy <0 kcal/mol indicates that the ligand can bind to the target protein under physiological conditions, while a binding energy less than −1.2 kcal/mol is considered to represent a good docking, indicating a stable and favorable interaction between the ligand and the protein.

3.2. Active ingredients and target visualization

We imported the resulting data (97 active ingredients and 479 predicted targets) into Cytoscape to obtain the TCM active ingredient-target network (Fig. 1). This network consisted of 589 nodes and 1473 edges. There were 13 TCMs in YSQZ, and these are denoted by purple-red circular nodes. The hexagonal nodes surrounding each TCM denote active ingredients without duplicates (89), the yellow inverted triangles denote the duplicates (8), the blue rounded rectangular nodes denote the predicted targets, and the black lines denote connections between nodes.

Network diagram of active ingredients and predicted targets.

3.3. CKD-related targets and DEGs

We selected GSE66494 as the study dataset in the GEO database. The discovery set contained 48 CKD samples and 5 control (normal) samples, and the validation set contained 5 CKD samples and 3 normal samples. We analyzed these data using GEO2R with conventional screening criteria (adj. P < .05, |log2(FC)| ≥1). The results showed there were 7193 DEGs relevant targets of CKD. We presented these results as a volcano plot, in which orange denotes up-regulated genes (5069) and blue denotes down-regulated genes (2124) (Fig. 2A). We also presented the top 20 up-regulated and down-regulated genes on a heatmap, in which red denotes increased expression and blue denotes decreased expression (Fig. 2B).

Volcano map and heatmap of differentially expressed genes in the GSE66494 dataset.

3.4. Potential targets and PPI network analysis

We then identified 185 potential targets that were the same among the 479 predicted drug targets and the 7193 CKD-related targets (Fig. 3). We also imported these data into the STRING database to develop a PPI network (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q697), and the results indicated 98 potential targets with interactions. Furthermore, we imported these results into Cytoscape to eliminate nodes with nonsignificant interactions. This led to the identification of 71 nodes and 184 edges (Fig. 4). In this plot, a point that is larger and darker denotes a more significant node, and a line that is thicker and darker denotes a higher interaction score.

Venn diagram of the relationship between the targets of active ingredients and CKD. CKD = chronic kidney disease.

PPI network diagram of potential targets of action. PPI = protein–protein interaction

3.5. Enrichment analysis

We then imported the 98 potential targets into Metascape for KEGG Pathway and GO enrichment analyses. The results show that 165 KEGG enriched pathways were potentially related to the YSQZ-based treatment of CKD (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q697). These pathways were primarily associated with lipids and atherosclerosis, Th17 cell differentiation, apoptosis, the cell cycle, PI3K-Akt signaling, HIF-1 signaling, autophagy, and mTOR signaling (Fig. 5A). Additionally, the GO analysis identified 1224 enriched genes, including 1037 in the BP category, 61 in the CC category, and 126 in the MF category (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q697). The BP-related genes were primarily involved in regulation of the MAPK cascade, protein serine/threonine kinase activity, lipid metabolism, cellular response to oxidative stress, regulation of the cell cycle, apoptotic signaling, and the inflammatory response. The CC-related genes were mainly associated with the transcription regulator complex, protein kinase complex, and serine/threonine protein kinase complex. The MF-related genes were associated with protein serine/threonine kinase activity, cytokine activity, and oxygen binding (Fig. 5B).

KEGG and GO analysis of interacting potential targets. GO = gene ontology, KEGG = Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes.

3.6. Key targets and core drug ingredients

We then performed a topological analysis of the PPI results (section 3.4) and identified 29 core targets (Table 2). Based on the active ingredient-predicted target network (section 3.2), we imported these 29 core targets into Cytoscape to determine the 62 active (core) ingredients. Analysis of the core ingredients and core targets led to a network that contained 91 nodes and 135 edges (Fig. 6). Based on degree values, the top 6 core targets were: tumor protein 53 (TP53), estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1), phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit delta (PIK3CD), mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAPK1), and mitogen-activated protein kinase 3 (MAPK3). These proteins also had significant differences in the GEO analysis, and we therefore considered them as the key targets (Fig. 7). Among the top 8 core ingredients of the TCM (quercetin, diincarvilone B, caffeic acid, 3-hydroxy-2,6,6-trimethylcyclohexene-1-carboxylic acid, aloe-emodin, kaempferol, anhydroicaritin, and palmatine), there is little known about the roles of diincarvilone B, 3-hydroxy-2,6,6-trimethylcyclohexene-1-carboxylic acid, and anhydroicaritin in CKD, and we therefore selected the other 5 core ingredients for subsequent analyses.

The results showed there was good binding of the core ingredients with the target proteins, as indicated by our analysis of the binding energy (free energy change, ΔG) (Table 5) and an associated heatmap (Fig. 9). We used Pymol to visualize the results of the molecular docking analysis (Fig. 10).

4. Discussion

TCM appears to have broad application for treatment of CKD, in that it can decrease symptoms and delay disease progression. However, there are concerns about safety of TCM due to its complex mixture of ingredients. Nevertheless, the holistic approach to diagnosis and treatment used by TCM helps to ensure its effectiveness and safety as a treatment for CKD. We employed network pharmacology analysis, and used analyses of a GEO dataset, molecular docking, immune infiltration, and other tools to develop a PPI network and investigate the underlying mechanism of YSQZ. Our results provide theoretical support for subsequent basic and clinical research on the use of YSQZ as a treatment for CKD.

The present study employed a comprehensive analysis of the PPI network and identified 6 key protein targets of YSQZ in the treatment of CKD: TP53, ESR1, CDK1, PIK3CD, MAPK1, and MAPK3. TP53 is a tumor suppressor gene that also plays a crucial role in kidney repair by regulating apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and autophagy.[22] A recent study[23] reported markedly elevated levels of TP53 and phosphorylated TP53 in the nucleus of the 5/6 nephrectomized rat model of CKD. After cytoplasmic TP53 enters the mitochondria, it can bind to antiapoptotic factors (Bcl-xL and Bcl-2) and attenuate their inhibitory effect on the pro-apoptotic factor Bax, leading to increased apoptosis. Moreover, another in vitro study of human proximal kidney tubular epithelial (HK-2) cells[24] reported a regulatory effect of TP53 in ochratoxin A (OTA)-mediated cell cycle blockage. Additional evidence suggests that diabetic kidney damage may be associated with signaling via TP53/miR-155-5p/sirt1 during autophagy.[25]

There is comparatively less known about the other 5 key protein targets. A previous study of ESR1 (one of the 2 nuclear estrogen receptors)[26] suggested it had a possible role in the pathology of type 2 diabetes and kidney disease. Additional evidence indicated that estrogen receptor modulators inhibited kidney fibrosis in a hypertensive nephrosclerosis model.[27] CDK1, a serine/threonine kinase, promotes the G2/M transition, and a prior study[28] found that hypoxia blocked this transition in kidney tubular epithelial cells, and led to increased fibrosis via the HIF-1α/TP53/CDK1 pathway. Activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway is linked to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition in hypoxia-induced kidney fibrosis.[29] The phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3Ks) include the regulatory and catalytic subunit p110δ, which is encoded by the PIK3CD gene.[30] A recent study[31] showed this subunit activated Akt, and thereby promoted the epithelial-mesenchymal transition and renal fibrosis. Proteins in the MAPK family, including MAPK1 and MAPK3, are crucial for the induction of kidney fibrosis.[32] There is also evidence that modulating the MAPK cascade inhibited inflammation and apoptosis in kidney cells.[33]

We identified 5 core ingredients of YSQZ: quercetin, caffeic acid, aloe-emodin, kaempferol, and palmatine. Quercetin, a flavonoid found in Semen Plantaginis and other herbs, has reno-protective effects due to its inhibition of apoptosis, fibrosis, and inflammation.[34] A previous study[35] showed that quercetin mitigated TGF-β1-induced fibrosis by inhibiting miR-21, and another study[36] found that quercetin reduced CKD by modulation of inflammation and oxidative stress. Caffeic acid, an antioxidant from Herba Serissae, reduced the levels of glucose and lipids in diabetic rats and led to improvements in renal histology.[37] It also alleviated diabetic nephropathy by modulating autophagy.[38] Aloe-emodin, which exists in Radix et Rhizoma Rhei, decreased kidney damage in a UUO rat model by enhancing TGF-β1-induced cell survival.[39] Kaempferol, which is present in Cortex Moutan, lowered blood pressure and fibrosis in hypertensive CKD rats by inhibiting the SHH pathway[40] and promoted autophagy via the AMPK/mTOR pathway.[41] Kaempferol also reduced injury of kidney tubules by inhibiting the MAPK pathway.[42] Palmatine, an alkaloid from Cortex Phellodendri, decreased gentamicin-induced nephrotoxicity by reducing oxidative stress[43] and mitigated renal inflammation and hyperuricemia-induced damage.[44]

The results of our KEGG and GO enrichment analyses showed that the major signaling pathways associated with the effect of YSQZ treatment of CKD were apoptosis, the cell cycle, PI3K-Akt signaling, autophagy, and mTOR signaling. The BPs were regulation of the MAPK cascade, protein serine/threonine kinase activity and cell cycle process, cellular response to oxidative stress, and apoptotic signaling; the CCs were transcription regulator complex and serine/threonine protein kinase; the related MFs included protein serine/threonine kinase, cytokine activity, and oxytocin activity. Collectively, these findings are consistent with the signaling pathways and mechanisms of action associated with the key protein targets and core ingredients of YSQZ that are described above.

A previous study of the early lymphocyte subpopulation in patients with CKD[45] reported that an increased number of NK cells and a decreased number of B cells were associated with a decreased estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and that patients with fewer NK cells and more B cells usually had better kidney function. NK cells are a crucial component of the innate immune system. A previous study[46] demonstrated that activation of human kidney tubulointerstitial NK cells led to release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the progression of CKD and kidney fibrosis. Another study[47] reported that the absolute count of naïve B cells had a positive correlation with the eGFR. The role of immune cell dysfunction and inflammation, especially the abnormal activation of B cells and NK cells, in CKD progression has been highlighted.[48] Chen et al.[47] described the distribution characteristics of circulating B cell subpopulations in CKD, which could offer further insights into the alterations observed in our analysis. These findings are consistent with our results regarding immune cell infiltration during CKD. However, whether YSQZ alleviates CKD by modulating these immune cells needs further validation.

Finally, our molecular docking analysis of the core YSQZ ingredients and the key protein targets showed there were high binding affinities. This supports our other findings, and suggests that YSQZ may decrease kidney damage by modulating 6 key target proteins.

This study has certain limitations. While our findings suggest potential mechanisms by which YSQZ may influence CKD progression, several alternative explanations should be considered. First, dataset noise may affect the accuracy of our results, as the GSE66494 dataset is derived from a single cohort and may contain inherent variability. Additionally, network topology bias could influence the identification of key targets in the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, as network-based methods are sensitive to the quality and completeness of the underlying data. Finally, there may be the pleiotropic effects of flavonoids such as quercetin, which is one of the core ingredients in YSQZ. Quercetin is known to target multiple proteins across various pathways, making it difficult to attribute specific effects solely to its interaction with individual targets. These factors highlight the need for further experimental validation to confirm the proposed mechanisms.

5. Conclusions

In summary, our results showed that YSQZ may ameliorate CKD due to the action of specific active ingredients (quercetin, caffeic acid, aloe-emodin, kaempferol, palmatine, and others) on specific target proteins (TP53, ESR1, CDK1, PIK3CD, MAPK1 and MAPK3) and signaling pathways (PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and AMPK/mTOR). At the physiological level, these molecular effects regulate oxidative stress, inflammation, cell cycle progression, autophagy, and apoptosis. However, the effect of YSQZ on NK cells, B cells, and other immune cells needs further investigation. Our results provide theoretical support for further basic and clinical studies of YSQZ as a treatment for CKD. However, further in vitro, in vivo, and clinical validations are needed to confirm these findings for clinical applications.
