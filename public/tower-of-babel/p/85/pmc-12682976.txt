Invasive thymoma extending into the right atrium
Authors: Jun Yamaguchi, Sachito Minegishi, Yusuke Inaba, Kohei Hashimoto, Hiroshi Kubota, Haruhiko Kondo
Published: 2025

An invasive thymoma occupying the right atrium.

Central Message

Invasive thymoma occupying the right atrium is a critical condition that necessitates surgical intervention for rescue. If the tumor is successfully resected, reasonable long-term outcomes may be expected.

Invasive thymoma is known to infiltrate the venous lumina; however, it is rare for the tumor to extend into the right atrium and cause heart failure. Surgical resection with cardiopulmonary bypass support is a life-saving choice in such cases.

Case Description

A 72-year-old male patient was transferred to our hospital from a referring hospital because of exertional dyspnea, loss of appetite, and presyncope for the past month. No symptoms of myasthenia gravis were observed. His medical history included nephrotic syndrome (creatinine, 1.6 mg/dL; prednisolone, 1 mg/day), steroid-induced diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. On admission, the patient's vital signs were as follows: blood pressure, 133/99 mm Hg; pulse, 82 bpm; respiratory rate, 16 breaths/minute; and oxygen saturation, 93% on room air. An enhanced computed tomography scan revealed a 15.0 × 6.0-cm mass with internal calcification in the anterior mediastinum extending into the brachiocephalic vein (BCV), the superior vena cava (SVC), and the right atrium (RA) just above the tricuspid valve, along with bilateral pleural effusion (Figure 1) and ascites. Both the SVC and the BCV were occluded. Venous return from the right upper body was rerouted via markedly developed cutaneous veins of the right trunk, while venous drainage from the left upper body occurred through the accessory hemiazygos vein into the RA (Video 1). No signs of metastasis or dissemination were detected.

Enhanced computed tomography scan showing a 15.0 × 6.0-cm mass in the anterior mediastinum growing through the brachiocephalic vein and occupying the right atrium. A, axial view (B) coronal view.

Echocardiography revealed a right atrial mass causing tricuspid valve obstruction (tricuspid flow velocity, 2.1 m/s; mean pressure gradient, 9.0 mm Hg) and reduced cardiac output (2.3 L/min). The tricuspid valve leaflet was not invaded (Video 2).

The patient was diagnosed with tricuspid valve obstruction and New York Heart Association class III congestive heart failure owing to the extension of the anterior mediastinal tumor into the RA. The intraluminal growth pattern and the patient's relatively stable condition despite the large tumor size suggested that tumor was an indolent tumor, most likely a thymoma. While further evaluation was being planned, including biopsy, pleural effusion worsened, and hemodynamic instability ensued 2 days after admission, prompting emergent surgical resection of the anterior mediastinal tumor to prevent sudden death from tricuspid valve obstruction. Given the imaging findings suggestive of thymoma and the emergent clinical situation, both biopsy and neoadjuvant therapy were omitted.

After the induction of general anesthesia, the patient was intubated via a single-lumen tube. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via the right femoral artery and vein, and a median sternotomy was performed. The extraluminal portion of the tumor was dissected. The mid-portion of the BCV was firmly adhered to the tumor and identified as the entry point for intraluminal invasion. The left BCV was mobilized and excised distal to the mass. The mid-portion of the BCV was resected along with the tumor. The distal SVC was transected and suture-closed at the proximal junction with the subclavian vein. The right upper lobe of the lung was partially resected owing to suspected tumor invasion. The RA was incised with the heart beating, followed by an incision into the proximal SVC. Although there was no direct invasion of the RA wall, the proximal SVC was transected above the azygos vein and the mid-portion of the SVC was resected with the tumor. Integrated findings from preoperative imaging and intraoperative observation revealed that the tumor had entered the venous lumen via the BCV and extended intraluminally through the SVC into the RA. The fragile intraluminal tumor collapsed during manipulation, and the fragments were retrieved as meticulously as possible. The RA wall was closed after the tumor removal. The left BCV and the RA were bypassed using a prosthetic graft with meticulous care to prevent kinking or twisting of the graft (Video 3). The total operation time was 4 hours and 35 minutes (cardiopulmonary bypass time, 2 hours 42 minutes). The patient was extubated the following day and then discharged to home on postoperative day 12 after completing rehabilitation aimed at facilitating daily life activities.

The pathologic diagnosis revealed a type AB thymoma (World Health Organization classification) with negative surgical margins. The tumor was classified as pathologically R1 owing to its collapse, which likely left cancerous cells in the mediastinum. Therefore, postoperative mediastinal radiation was administered. At 1 year postoperatively, although the prosthetic graft became occluded, the patient remained asymptomatic and free of recurrence.

Discussion

Although there are only few reports on the long-term prognosis of thymomas extending into the right atrium,1 the national data on thymic tumors (n = 1320) indicate a 5-year survival rate of 93% for Masaoka stage III or IV thymomas with R0 resection and 64% for R1/R2 resections.2 These data include thymic carcinoma and carcinoid tumors, suggesting that the prognosis for thymoma alone is likely even better. Furthermore, the available evidence suggests that postoperative radiation therapy improves overall survival in patients with positive surgical margins, supporting its use in our case, in which part of the tumor could exist in the mediastinum.3

To our knowledge, there have been no reports of sudden death caused directly by tricuspid valve obstruction due to thymoma. However, similar cases were reported in which a cardiac tumor associated with acute hemodynamic failure caused by tricuspid valve obstruction4 and a thymoma extending into the RA led to sudden death from intracardiac thrombosis following chemotherapy.5 These findings underscore the potential for intracardiac tumor extension to precipitate rapid clinical deterioration.

In this case, emergent tumor resection was performed to save the patient from imminent cardiac arrest due to RA extension of the invasive thymoma. Given the Masaoka stage III classification (TNM classification: T3N0M0, stage IIIA) and the subsequent administration of mediastinal irradiation after R1 resection, we believe that a favorable long-term prognosis could be achieved in this patient. Nevertheless, we will continue to closely monitor the patient for possible recurrence.

Reconstruction of venous circulation was considered unnecessary, given the extensive collateral development in the upper body. Nevertheless, the left BCV was reconstructed due to surgical feasibility. Anticoagulant therapy with warfarin (target prothrombin time-international normalized ratio (PT-INR), 1.6-2.6) was administered but was discontinued after the graft occlusion. Since the occlusion occurred 1 year postoperatively without complications, venous reconstruction ultimately might have been unnecessary in this case.

In conclusion, for patients with invasive thymoma extending into the RA, surgical resection under cardiopulmonary bypass support may contribute to prolonged survival.
