Comparative metabolomic profiling reveals distinct metabolic signatures in amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood between advanced and young maternal age pregnancies
Authors: Dianjie Li, Shilei Pan, Wei Cai, Mei Zhong, Yuxin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven the heightened risk of complications during pregnancy in women of advanced maternal age (AMA), it is crucial to understand the metabolites in amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood in this demographic. MethodsWe analyzed the metabolites in amniotic fluid from 60 women, divided into two groups: the AMA group (aged 35 or above, n = 29), and the control group (aged below 35, n = 31). We then conducted a follow-up analysis on the metabolites of umbilical cord blood from a sample of 19 women (9 from the AMA group, and 10 from the control group).ResultsIn total, we identified 96 differential metabolites in the amniotic fluid and 146 in the cord blood between the two groups. The significant changes in the metabolites of the amniotic fluid mainly involved sphingolipid metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and cholesterol metabolism. Conversely, the preliminary significant changes in cord blood metabolites were mainly linked to metabolism of arginine and proline, degradation of valine, leucine, and isoleucine, fatty acid metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, and the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids. Further analysis revealed a significant upregulation of lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and taurodeoxycholic acid in the amniotic fluid. In the cord blood, various forms of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), sphingomyelin (SM), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), LPC, and PC were found preliminarily to be either upregulated or downregulated.ConclusionOur results preliminarily showed that the metabolites of amniotic fluid and cord blood in AMA women differed significantly from the control group. These findings provide crucial insights for future research to explore the role of metabolomics in adverse pregnancy outcomes in AMA women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08118-6.

Introduction

In general, AMA is defined as a female age of 35 years or more at the time of a full-term birth [1]. Between 2010 and 2017, the average maternal age and the proportion of AMA births increased, which may be attributed to the relaxation of the one-child policy in China [2]. Similarly, the proportion of AMA births has been rising in the developed world [3]. The risks of perinatal death, fetal growth restriction, hypertensive disease, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), placenta previa, and placental abruption are significantly higher among AMA women compared to their younger counterparts [2, 4–6].

Over the last few decades, metabolomic analysis of biofluids or tissues has successfully been used to discriminate different phenotypic groups of subjects by extrapolating subtle metabolic peculiarities [7]. Metabolomics employs high-output identification, quantitation, and characterization of metabolites under 2000 Da, including lipids, amino acids, peptides, nucleic acids, organic acids, vitamins, thiols, and carbohydrates. These metabolites play a crucial role in various biological pathways related to physiological, pathological, and disease states [8].

Amniotic fluid is a crucial biological fluid whose metabolite transport is regulated by the placenta, permeable skin, fetal lung egress, and gastric fluid [9]. Its composition mirrors the mother’s interstitial fluid during the early stages of pregnancy, but after the 14th week, fetal urine starts forming, altering the biofluid composition significantly from the 20th week onwards. This fluid primarily contains urine and secretions from the fetus’s oronasal cavity, bronchi, and lung tissue, implying a high concentration of fetal metabolites. The amniotic fluid also absorbs metabolites from fetal plasma through the permeable skin of the fetus. The umbilical cord, serving as a bridge for material exchange between the mother and child, contains cord blood metabolites reflective of the fetus’s metabolic state. A correlation has been shown between umbilical cord blood metabolome and birth weight, particularly lysophosphatidylcholines, fatty acids, and phosphatidylcholines [10].

Recently, more and more research reported that follicular cell metabolism in AMA was different and effect the oocyte quality in assisted reproductive technology (ART) treatment [11–13]. He et al. found that plasma metabolites may be associated with the occurrence of gestational diabetes mellitus with maternal age between 35 and 40 years [14].Trohl et al. found that the insulin/IGF system, lipid metabolism, and stress defence were dysregulated in reproductive tissues of older rabbits [15]. Furthermore, the metabolic disease in newborns were also related to maternal age and blood markers [16].AMA even can change the levels of neurotransmitters and metabolites in the blood and the brain in offspring mice, showing persistent depressive and anxiety-like phenotypes in adulthood [17].These researches highlight the crucial influence of maternal age on embryo development and adverse pregnancy outcome. Understanding the metabolites in amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood in AMA women is crucial due to the risk of complications during pregnancy. In this study, we employed untargeted metabolomics to analyze these metabolites in AMA women and women of non-advanced maternal age. Our goal is to shed light on the potential pathogenic mechanisms leading to higher pregnancy complication risks in AMA women.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

We recruited a total of 60 healthy pregnant women undergoing antenatal care at Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University and Jiangmen Central Hospital. These women were divided into two groups based on their age at their expected delivery date: the AMA group (age ≥ 35 years, n = 29) and the control group (age < 35 years, n = 31). Inclusion criterion: Women with singleton pregnancies who had an indication for amniocentesis, including AMA, nuchal translucency > 2.5 mm and a positive result from maternal serum screening or non-invasive prenatal genetic testing (NIPT). Exclusion criteria: diabetes, hypertension, fetal growth restriction, placenta previa, placental abruption, abnormal fetal development, and pregnancy complicated with internal and surgical diseases. Amniotic fluid samples were obtained via amniocentesis.

Amniotic fluid was collected by a prenatal diagnosis specialist using ultrasound-guided amniocentesis between the 17th and 32nd weeks of gestation. Fetuses with chromosomal diseases were excluded. For umbilical cord blood collection, we followed these women until delivery. Of the 60 pregnant women, 19 (9 AMA, 10 control) delivered their newborns at Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University. Umbilical cord blood was collected via needle aspiration from the umbilical artery within 5 min of birth and prior to the delivery of the placenta.

The samples were then processed by centrifugation at 1200×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The serum was collected, divided into 100 µL aliquots, and frozen at − 80℃ for subsequent metabolomic assays. This study was approved by the Ethics Service Committee of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University (2021-KY-159-01).

Metabolites extraction

To extract metabolites, 100 µL of amniotic fluid was placed into EP tubes and mixed well with prechilled 80% methanol and 0.1% formic acid. After an ice incubation for 5 min, samples were centrifuged at 15,000 g at 4 °C for 20 min. A portion of the supernatant was then diluted to a final concentration of 53% methanol using LC-MS grade water. Samples were transferred to a fresh Eppendorf tube and re-centrifuged under the same conditions. Finally, the supernatant was injected into the LC-MS/MS system for analysis.

For serum samples, the freeze-dried umbilical cord blood (1mL) was thawed at room temperature and mixed by shaking. It was then re-suspended with prechilled 80% methanol and 0.1% formic acid and subjected to the same processing and analysis steps as the amniotic fluid samples, except that the final methanol concentration was adjusted to 60%.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

UHPLC-MS/MS analyses were conducted using a Vanquish UHPLC system (ThermoFisher, Germany) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive™ HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Germany) at Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The chromatographic column used in the experiment is Hypesil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) (Thermo Fisher, USA). The parameters for sample injection, column, flow rate, polarity mode, eluents, and solvent gradient were as specified. The Q Exactive™ HF mass spectrometer was operated in positive/negative polarity mode with a spray voltage of 3.2 kV, a capillary temperature of 320 °C, a sheath gas flow rate of 40 arb, and an auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 arb.

Data processing and metabolite identification

The raw data files generated by UHPLC-MS/MS were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD3.1, ThermoFisher) for peak alignment, peak picking, and quantitation for each metabolite, with parameters set as described. Peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity, and molecular formulas were predicted based on various ion peaks. Peaks were then matched with the mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList databases for accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. Statistical analyses were performed using R, Python, and CentOS software.

Data analysis

The annotated metabolites were cross-referenced with the KEGG, HMDB, and LIPIDMaps databases. Principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed using metaX software. We used univariate analysis (t-test) to calculate the statistical significance (p-value). Differential metabolites were identified based on specific criteria, and their roles in various metabolic pathways were studied using the KEGG database. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis of differential metabolites was performed as described, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Among the patients who provided amniotic fluid samples, the average age of the advanced maternal age (AMA) group (38.72 years) was significantly older than that of the control group (30.03 years). The AMA group had a higher gravidity, parity, and pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI). However, no significant differences were noted in the gestational age at the time of sample collection, fetal nuchal translucency (NT), and levels of leukocytes, neutrophil granulocytes, and hemoglobin in serum between the two groups (Table 1). For patients who provided umbilical cord blood samples, the average age of the AMA group (39.11 years) was significantly older than that of the control group (31.50 years). No significant differences were observed in pre-pregnancy BMI, gestational age at delivery, gender, weight and height of newborns, and the prevalence of Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) between the two groups (Table 2).

Metabolomic changes in the amniotic fluid of the AMA group

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) models were established to observe the overall distribution of samples and highlight potential differences in the metabolic profiles between the two groups. While PCA demonstrated the general metabolic differences among the samples within each group, PLS-DA helped identify potential differential metabolites that contribute to model clustering. The PCA and PLS-DA score plots indicated that the metabolites in the amniotic fluid of the AMA group differed from those in the control group (Figs. 1A, B, C, D). A combination of multivariate and univariate analyses led to the selection of 824 metabolites in the amniotic fluid (Supplementary Table S1), which included 96 differential metabolites. Out of the 96 differential metabolites, 41 metabolites were up-regulated, and 55 were down-regulated in the AMA group (Figs. 1G, H). The selection of these differential metabolites was based on the following criteria: variable importance in the projection > 1.0, fold change > 1.5 or fold change < 0.67, and p-value < 0.05 [18]. The q-value < 0.05 was used to define significance after FDR correction. Further KEGG enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites revealed associations with cholesterol metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, the longevity-regulating pathway, oocyte meiosis, sphingolipid metabolism, insulin resistance, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, ABC transporters, and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Figs. 1E, F). The down-regulated metabolite cAMP, identified in the AMA group, was associated with the aging pathway.

According to untargeted metabolomic analyses, the differential metabolites between the AMA and control groups were mainly molecules associated with lipid metabolism (Table 3). ACar 18:0 was the only lipid found to be down-regulated in the AMA group, while the remaining 11 lipids listed in Table 3 were up-regulated. The lipids up-regulated in the AMA group were primarily lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) and phosphatidylcholine (PC), which were related to sphingolipid metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and cholesterol metabolism.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluated the differential metabolites for their potential as biomarkers. The Area below the ROC Curve is called the Area Under Curve (AUC), which was used to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of biomarkers for predicting the occurrence of events. The sensitivity and specificity of each metabolite are determined by the optimal threshold of the ROC curve. The biomarker has no effect at all on predicting the occurrence of events and has no predictive value when the AUC = 0.5. The closer the AUC is to 1 when the 0.5 < AUC, the higher the accuracy of the prediction will be. The prediction accuracy is relatively low when the AUC value is between 0.5 and 0.7. The AUC value between 0.7 and 0.9 indicates moderate predictive value, whereas an AUC value above 0.9 signifies high predictive accuracy. An AUC value of 0.93 was observed for octadecanamine, suggesting its potential as an amniotic fluid biomarker for AMA (Figs. 2A). Similarly, LPC 15:0, sphinganine, bromfenac, and LPC 14:0 could also distinguish AMA from control in amniotic fluid with AUC values of 0.87, 0.87, 0.87, and 0.87, respectively (Figs. 2B, C, D, E).

Metabolomic changes in the ustmbilical cord blood of AMA patients

Metabolites in the umbilical cord blood were analyzed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA). These analyses revealed a significant difference in the distribution of metabolites between the two groups, with a clear separation of metabolites in the PLS-DA model (Figs. 3A, B, C, D). In total, 1212 metabolites were detected in the 19 umbilical cord blood samples (Supplementary Table S2). All metabolites were enriched across 35 pathways, with seven pathways — global and overview maps, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, digestive system, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, carbohydrate metabolism, and endocrine system — showing significant enrichment.

A total of 146 differential metabolites were selected preliminarily, including 69 up-regulated and 77 down-regulated metabolites (Figs. 3G, H). Unlike in amniotic fluid, the differential metabolites in the umbilical cord blood had to meet the following conditions: variable importance in the projection > 1.0, fold change > 1.2 or fold change < 0.833, and p-value < 0.05. Several KEGG pathways were significantly enriched among these differential metabolites, including metabolism of arginine, proline, valine, leucine, isoleucine, fatty acids, alanine, aspartate, glutamate, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Figs. 3E, F). Adenosine 5’-monophosphate, a metabolite down-regulated in AMA umbilical cord blood, was also associated with the aging pathway. Screening criteria (p < 0.05) identified four over-represented metabolic pathways with a high influence on AMA: mineral absorption, arginine and proline metabolism, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, and valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation. Nine metabolites increased preliminarily in abundance within these pathways in the AMA group, including L-Phenylalanine, L-Asparagine, 5-Aminovaleric acid, 4-Oxoproline, D-Proline, UDP-D-glucuronate, alpha-Ketoglutaric acid, 3-Methyl-2-oxobutanoic acid, and acetoacetate.

Lipid metabolism was also implicated in the differential metabolites observed in umbilical cord blood between the AMA and control groups (Table 4). There were preliminary 33 differential lipids, with 18 up-regulated and 15 down-regulated lipids in the AMA group. The up-regulated lipids were primarily categorized as sphingomyelin (SM), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and ACar. Conversely, the down-regulated lipids were mainly classified as phosphatidylglycerol (PG), PC, and SM. Differential metabolites with concentrations higher than the average value of the metabolomics data were analyzed using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. Thirty-one metabolites with an AUC value above 0.9 were preliminary found in the AMA group. Particularly, APK, quercetin and chlorogenic acid demonstrated potential as hypothesis-generating biomarkers, with AUC values of 0.98, 0.98, and 0.97, respectively, distinguishing AMA from the control group in umbilical cord blood (Figs. 2F, G, H).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the differences in metabolites within amniotic fluid and cord blood to identify metabolic changes in pregnant women with Advanced Maternal Age (AMA). To date, there has been scant metabolomic research focused on pregnant women with AMA. We observed that the metabolites in the amniotic fluid from the second trimester in the AMA group were distinct from those in the control group, as evidenced by PCA and PLS-DA score plots. It is widely recognized that women with AMA face a higher risk of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) compared to those with normal pregnancies. GDM adversely affects fetal, neonatal, and infant metabolic outcomes by impacting glucose metabolism and insulin balance.

In a related study, He et al. [14] examined the maternal plasma and urine of 20 GDM pregnant participants (≥ 35 years old) using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and identified dysregulated ABC transporters that might contribute to GDM development among AMA women. Similarly, we also discovered a down-regulation of inositol in the amniotic fluid of AMA, which correlated with ABC transporters. A separate study involving an AMA population showed an impairment in essential amino acid absorption by skeletal muscle after resistance exercise due to the lack of ABC transporter expression in older adults [19]. This same study reported reduced levels of specific fatty acids (stearic acid, arachidic acid, bishomogamma-linolenic acid) in the plasma of older GDM women [14].

Lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) is a derivative of phosphatidylcholine (PC). Wang et al. [20] observed a relationship between Body Mass Index (BMI) and LPC 14:0 in early pregnancy among 1008 women, suggesting that a higher BMI in early pregnancy could be associated with altered lipid metabolism, potentially contributing to an increased risk of GDM. Further metabolomic assessment found altered levels of LPC 18:1 were also associated with insulin resistance and weight changes [21]. Additionally, a cohort study proposed that LPC19:0, along with four other biomarkers, could predict preeclampsia in the mid-trimester using binary logistic regression analysis [22]. In our study, we found that several LPC, LPS, PC, and lysops were up-regulated in the amniotic fluid of AMA (Table 2), which could be associated with higher pregestational BMI in AMA.

Acylcarnitine (ACar), an acetyl derivative of L-carnitine, plays a critical role in the β-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids across the inner mitochondrial membrane [23]. Hence, ACar accumulation is typically seen as a response to dysregulated fatty acid oxidation. The levels of specific ACars in patients with obesity, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes have been found to differ from those in healthy participants [24]. Conversely, our study discovered that ACar 18:0 was down-regulated in the amniotic fluid of AMA (Table 2).

Bile acids are crucial for lipid metabolism and obesity. O’Neill et al. [25] discovered moderate increases in several primary bile acids in the amniotic fluid of GDM women, including glycochenodeoxycholate, chenodeoxycholate, and glycocholate. Several bile acids were also elevated in the amniotic fluid of preterm births, including glycocholate, taurocholate, taurochenodeoxycholate, taurodeoxycholate, and glycodeoxycholate [26]. We also noted increases in both taurochenodeoxycholic acid and taurodeoxycholic acid sodium salt in the amniotic fluid with AMA. This may be correlated with an increased risk of GDM and preterm birth in women with AMA. Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) are membrane surface markers of gram-negative bacteria and can induce inflammation. Both LPS 18:0 and LPS 16:0 were found to be up-regulated in the amniotic fluid of AMA. Since intrauterine inflammation can lead to preterm birth and miscarriage, the accumulation of LPS in AMA may be related to the occurrence of such complications.

Among the 65 metabolites detected, we found higher levels of glutamic acid and lower levels of glutamine, pyruvate, and inositol, as well as several amino acids, likely due to increased protein metabolism to support enhanced fetal growth. This was particularly evident in preterm deliveries, where there were higher energy demands [27]. D-(+)-Pyroglutamic acid and L-Pyroglutamic acid levels were also elevated in the amniotic fluid of AMA (Supplementary Table S1). Inositol also has an important physiological role in the placenta. The inositol derivatives (e.g.,inositolphosphoglycan IPG) are insulin mimetics and involved in the regulation of membrane fluidity, protein transport and transport processes, cytoskeleton assembly, intracellular calcium, lipid metabolism and gene expression [28, 29]. The decrease in placental inositol content in GDM may be partially mediated by the downregulation of glucose-induced placental inositol synthesis and input, increasing with the elevation of maternal blood glucose [30]. It is suggested that the changes of inositol in the placenta, umbilical cord blood and amniotic fluid may be associated with an increased risk of GDM in patients with AMA. Our findings suggest that metabolites in AMA are mainly enriched in cholesterol metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, sphingolipid metabolism, and steroid hormone biosynthesis. This implies that amniotic fluid in AMA could be highly correlated with lipid metabolism. The higher pregestational BMI of AMA may also play a role in lipid metabolism. However, some findings indicated that maternal age had no significant influence on the AF metabolic profile. Among the 178 metabolites successfully identified, only 2-hydroxybutyric acid, guanidinosuccinic acid, erythrose and putrescine were significantly different [31]. which may be due to the fact that maternal BMI may influence the metabolic profile of AF but this was not explored. Secondly, sample sizes were small for several subgroup analyses. In our future research, we will refine our study design to secure more persuasive results.

In this study, we noted significant differences in the metabolites of umbilical cord blood between the AMA and control groups, as illustrated by the PCA and PLS-DA score plots. Intriguingly, these differences were more numerous and distinct than those observed in the amniotic fluid. Two compounds, LPC 22:4 and LPC 19:0, were significantly up-regulated in the cord blood of the AMA group (Table 4). Patel et al. [32] discovered a positive correlation between LPC 16.1 and 18.0 and various factors such as neonatal birth weight, SSFT, subscapular and triceps SFTs, as well as infant growth and catch-up growth within the first six months of age. Yong-Ping et al. [33] also found a positive and independent correlation between male newborns’ serum LPC(20:3) and head circumference. This suggests that cord LPCs could play a significant role in the early growth velocities, providing a mechanistic insight into the early-life origins of obesity.

We also observed significant changes in several types of phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs) and phosphatidylcholines (PCs) in the AMA group. Maternal PC supplements have been shown to have a positive impact on the development of the human fetal brain [34]. Interestingly, a study found that all phospholipids (PEs and PCs) were positively associated with overweight or obesity among males and were also significantly correlated with attained height at their ages [35]. As crucial components of cell membranes, PEs and PCs play essential roles in maintaining the structures of the membranes. Consequently, the phospholipids (LPCs, PEs, and PCs) are likely to influence the height, weight, and even growth velocity of newborns.

Sphingolipids, a class of bioactive lipids, are key modulators of various physiological and pathological processes that include cell cycle, apoptosis, angiogenesis, stress, and inflammatory responses. Sphingomyelin (SM) is a major structural component of biological membranes and one of the end-points in the synthesis of sphingolipids [36]. SM(d18:1/16:0) has been found to be significantly up-regulated in cord serum of preeclampsia newborns [37]. We found a mixed pattern of increase and decrease in four kinds of SMs each in the AMA group (Table 4). Past studies have reported that abnormal sphingolipid metabolism can modify the permeability of intestinal cells, leading to inflammatory diseases [38, 39]. Nevertheless, the long-term effects of SM on newborns remain unclear.

We also observed that both PG (18:2/20:4) and PG (18:2/18:2) were down-regulated in the AMA group. That isomeric or closely related lipid species may appear as independent findings despite overlapping biological roles, and clustering or dimensionality reduction was not applied. PG is the second-largest lipid constituent of lung surfactant (LS) in almost all mammals [40], and its absence in the LS of foetal humans is an indicator of respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) [41]. Moreover, Lipidomic profiling revealed that the significant abnormalities of PG in amniotic fluid can disrupt fetal growth and lung development [42]. This led us to suspect that the down-regulated PG may be associated with an increased risk of pulmonary hypoplasia in AMA newborns.

Further, we noted a decrease in LysoPC(p-18:1/0:0) in cord blood across the SGA, AGA, and LGA groups [43]. Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), an extracellular lipid mediator, exerts multiple bioactivities through the activation of G protein-coupled receptors [44]. LPA plays a pivotal role in several aspects of pregnancy, including smooth muscle contraction, cell proliferation and differentiation, angiogenesis, and platelet aggregation [45]. An imbalance in placental LPA signaling may be implicated in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia [46]. Byeon et al. [47] observed a decrease of LPA 20:4 in SGA mothers, which could potentially compromise fetal growth and development. LPA 20:4 was also down-regulated in AMA cord blood, potentially be related to fetal growth and development. Notably, the weight of newborns in the AMA group was slightly lower than that in the control group (3.29 kg vs. 3.38 kg).

Several studies reported the association of distinctive cord plasma metabolite branched-chain amino acids with FGR, birthweight and newborn adiposity [48, 49]. Fetal proline in cord blood showed an independent association with GDM [50]. Exposure to GDM was associated with elevated cord blood serum ketone bodies (acetoacetate and 3-hydroxybutyrate) and citrate. Exposure to preeclampsia was associated with higher glutamine and glycerol [51]. We also observed an increase in various metabolites, including d-proline, 3-nitro-L-tyrosine, ornithine, N-carbamyl-L-glutamic acid, and citric acid, in the cord blood of AMA (Supplementary Table S2). However, we found no significant difference in the prevalence of GDM between the two groups (Table 2), leading us to question the association of these metabolites with GDM.

With the aging population, a variety of previously unrecognized risks of AMA associated pregnancy and parturition will be discovered. Maternal age remains an important factor with varying effects on maternal and fetal/infant health. However, the pathogenic mechanisms underlying the increased associated risks remain largely unknown. The related differential metabolites in amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood in our study may provide a potential to guidance for future research with larger cohorts, with the aim of identifying potential biomarkers for predicting the risk of GDM, PE or FGR in pregnant women with AMA. Nevertheless, there were still some limitations in our study. The pregestational BMI were higher in the AMA group, which might be related to the relatively low sample size. There were only three samples in the AMA group with BMI > 28. We attempted to exclude the three cases for stratified analysis. Out of the 89 differential metabolites, 40 metabolites were up-regulated, and 49 were down-regulated in the AMA group(Supplementary Table S3). There were 17 inconsistent differential metabolites were found in the two groups after excluding the three cases with BMI > 28, all of which were highlighted in the Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Table S3. However, there was no significant difference in the differential metabolites related to lipids. Some cases of cord blood were combined with GDM in the two groups. Subgroup analysis could not be conducted due to the small sample size. These factors can introduce bias in the observed associations. Findings likely confounded by maternal characteristics. This research was a retrospective study, lack of internal or external validation. The biomarker findings were exploratory, and future studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs are needed to validate the findings. We are looking forward that a model for the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes in AMA will be developed through the differential metabolites in subsequent studies. In the era of precision obstetrics, integrating omics-based biomarkers with AI-assisted predictive models holds great promise for enhancing early screening and individualized interventions in AMA pregnancies [52, 53]. By detecting relevant metabolites in maternal blood or amniotic fluid during pregnancy, it may become possible to identify high-reisk populations early and reduce the incidence of adverse outcomes through timely and targeted intervention.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified distinct metabolomic profiles in the amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood of AMA subjects, and comprehensively compared the lipidomic differences between AMA and controls. Our findings provide a foundation for future research to further investigate the role of amniotic fluid and cord blood metabolomics in relation to adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with AMA. The abnormal lipid metabolism may have a certain correlation with the increased risk of metabolic diseases in patients with AMA. Monitoring the lipid metabolism index of AMA during pregnancy may be beneficial. However, our study was limited by the sample size, and the identified metabolomic and lipidomic biomarkers still require verification through larger-scale studies and functional experiments.
