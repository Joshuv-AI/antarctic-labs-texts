Spatial distribution and determinants of health care access barriers among female Pakistan youths: spatial and multilevel regression analysis
Authors: Daniel Gashaneh Belay, Gizachew A. Tessema, Salima Meherali, Zohra S. Lassi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEnsuring universal access to high-quality healthcare services including services free from financial barriers, is a global priority. However, accessing healthcare services remains challenging for youth in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Pakistan. This study investigates the spatial distribution and determinants of healthcare access barriers among female youths in Pakistan.MethodsWe used the 2017/18 Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey (PDHS) dataset, including 2,924 female youth aged 15–24, to examine healthcare access barriers. These barriers were considered as an outcome variable and were regarded where a respondent reported encountering any of the following impediments to accessing healthcare services during illness: (i) difficulty obtaining permission to visit a health facility; (ii) financial constraints in meeting healthcare expenses or treatment costs; (iii) significant travel distance to access a health facility; and (iv) reluctance to visit health facilities alone. To analyse multi-level determinants and geographical disparities in healthcare access barriers among youths, we employed multilevel logistic regression and spatial analysis techniques, respectively. The analysis accounted for sample weights to ensure the robustness and representativeness of the findings.ResultsAlmost four-fifths (79.4%) of female youth in Pakistan (95% CI: 77.9 to 80.9) have reported having perceived barriers to accessing healthcare services. Barriers to accessing healthcare among female youth were associated with not having employment (AOR = 1.96; 95% CI; 1.39, 2.76), living in a household where the head was a male (AOR = 1.97; 95% CI; 1.40, 2.77), having low and middle household wealth status (low: AOR = 3.22; 95% CI; 2.18, 4.75; middle: AOR = 1.89; 95% CI; 1.36, 2.63), being unempowered (AOR = 2.08; 95% CI; 1.55, 2.80), and residing in areas with low community female youth literacy rates (AOR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.02, 2.15) and living in federally administered tribal areas (FATA), Balochistan, and Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK) regions.ConclusionA substantial proportion of female youth in Pakistan have major perceived barriers to accessing healthcare services. Several individual- and community-level factors were associated with perceived barriers to accessing healthcare services among female youth. Significant geographic variation was observed in the perceived barriers to healthcare services among Pakistan’s female youth. Policymakers had better take measures to improve access to healthcare services through the formulation of youth-responsive policies and strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13204-8.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines youth as individuals aged 15–24 years [1]. This age marks a critical transitional period from childhood to adulthood, characterised by significant biological, social, and psychological changes, presenting both risks and opportunities for their future well-being [2]. This is a time when individuals become independent, form new relationships, develop social skills, and acquire behaviours that will shape the rest of their lives [3]. Young people are agents of change and a key asset and resources, who can positively contribute to their families, communities and country’s life [4]. However, during this transition period, young people may face a range of health risks. These include exposure to harmful products, devastating mental health issues (including suicide), risks of violence, and sexual and reproductive health issues [3]. Thus, young people should have equitable access to health services that meet their needs [5].

Achieving universal health coverage (UHC) has emerged as a paramount priority for the global community [6]. The WHO defines UHC as ensuring that all individuals have access to comprehensive, quality health services they require, regardless of their financial circumstances, and at the right time and place [7]. However, approximately 400 million people worldwide lack access to essential healthcare services [8]. Despite constituting around 24% of the global population in 2021, adolescents and youth aged 10 to 24 often encounter unique barriers, associated with their age, that hinder their access to quality healthcare [6]. Health interventions frequently overlook the distinct biological, social, and emotional needs associated with these life stages [6]. Moreover, many adolescents and youth perceive public healthcare services as unsuitable for them due to perceived or real concerns regarding respect, privacy and confidentiality, and the imposition of moral values on healthcare providers [6]. Qualitative literature across many countries consistently indicated that young people often report suboptimal experiences when accessing healthcare compared to other demographic groups [9–11]. For example, a study in Mozambique showed that young adults usually have numerous erroneous opinions about contraception, leading to inconsistent use and vague perceptions of risk-taking [11]. A study also showed that social stigma, embarrassment, confidentiality concerns, distrust of unknown persons, and limited knowledge are the main factors preventing this age group people from seeking professional help for their mental health problems [12]. Furthermore, despite the diverse health service needs, young women encounter more significant barriers to accessing healthcare compared to young men, leading to overall poorer health outcomes and increased morbidity and mortality rates [13, 14]. Various factors contribute to these disparities, including limited decision-making autonomy [15, 16], and restrictions on independent travel [15]. Consequently, teenage pregnancy and early childbirth represent a significant cause of mortality among 15–19-year-old girls globally [17]. A study in Kenya and Zambia also showed that HIV prevalence in women was six times and three times higher than in men among sexually active 15–19 year-olds and 20–24 year-olds respectively [14].

Pakistan has a huge reservoir of young people, with 64% of the population below the age of 29 and 30% between 15 and 29 years [18]. Pakistan’s economy is among the top performers in South Asia registering a growth rate of over 5.2% in 2017 [19]. However, according to the Global Gender Gap Report 2018, Pakistan ranks among the lowest gender parity scores for health and survival, globally [20, 21]. Gender disparities persist across various domains in Pakistan, including healthcare, education, income opportunities, employment, and participation in decision-making processes [16]. The patriarchal framework of society, which operates at all levels places women in a more vulnerable position than men [22]. The low autonomy of women is largely influenced by cultural and religious beliefs [20, 23, 24]. Spatial inequalities within the country further exacerbate this disparity. Studies showed regional disparities in reproductive healthcare services in the country [25, 26]. The Northeast of the country, particularly northern Punjab and northeastern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) regions has better health services coverage due to their centrality in terms of urbanization and infrastructure [25]. In contrast, the Baluchistan region has been identified as significantly more deprived or disadvantaged in terms of health and well-being, as it is predominantly rural, with scattered populations spread across vast, underdeveloped terrains [26].

Hence, this study aimed to: (i) determine the magnitude and identify the determinants of healthcare access barriers among female youth in Pakistan, (ii) explore its spatial distribution, and (iii) assess the association between barriers to accessing healthcare and the uptake of reproductive health services among female youth in Pakistan. This is crucial for informing targeted interventions and guiding policy decisions.

Methods

Data source and setting

The Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey (PDHS) Individual Records (IR) datasets utilised in this study were extracted from the 2017/18 PDHS [27]. Pakistan is composed of five provinces (Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa [KPK], Balochistan [BC], Gilgit-Baltistan [GB]) along with federally administered areas [28]. According to the Population Census of 2017, Pakistan’s total population stands at 207 million [29], with females constituting 48.6% [29]. The survey estimates are representative of the four provinces of Punjab, Sindh, KPK, and Balochistan; as well as for two regions: Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK) and Gilgit Baltistan (GB); along with Islamabad Capital Territory (ICT); and federally administered tribal areas (FATA) [27].

Participants and sample size

Female youth aged 15 to 24 years who were either permanent residents of the selected households or visitors who stayed in the households the night before the survey were eligible to be interviewed for PDHS [27]. Of all women aged 15–49 at the time of the survey (n = 15,068), 2,948 were female youth participants. Two of these youth had missing data about healthcare access barriers and were excluded from further analysis. Finally, 2,946 samples (2,924 weighted) were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Sampling technique

Details of the sampling procedure were published elsewhere [27]. In brief, a stratified two-stage cluster sampling method was used for data collection. The stratification process involved dividing each of the eight regions into urban and rural areas, resulting in a total of 16 sampling strata. At the first stage of sampling, sample points (clusters) were selected, each comprising enumeration blocks (EBs), with a probability proportional to the size of the household residing at the time of the census. Initially, 580 clusters were selected, but 19 clusters had to be excluded due to security concerns during the fieldwork [27]. Consequently, the study finally included 2,946 female youth sampled from 541 clusters.

In the second stage of sampling, households were systematically sampled, with a fixed number of 28 households per cluster selected using an equal probability systematic selection process [27]. However, due to non-proportional sample allocation between regions, sample weighting was applied during the analysis. This weighting was conducted separately for other provinces and AJK and GB to ensure that the results were representative at the national level [27].

Outcome variable

The outcome variable for this study was derived from women’s’ reports of perceived barriers to healthcare access. Healthcare access barrier was deemed when women reported ‘major problems’, labelled as ‘major barriers’ in this study, to at least one of the following four maternal healthcare access-related questions when they sought medical advice or treatment: (i) getting permission to visit a health facility; (ii) getting money to cover healthcare expenses or treatment; (iii) travel distance to reach a health facility; and (iv) not wanting to visit facilities alone.

Therefore, these barriers were combined into a single outcome variable. If a woman responded with a “major barrier” to one or more of the four questions mentioned above, she was considered to have perceived barriers to healthcare access.

Independent variables

Independent variables considered in the study include individual and community-level factors. Individual-level risk factors included socio-demographic characteristics such as the women’s age, education status, marital status, employment, family size, and wealth index. Wealth status was derived using Principal Components Analysis (PCA) and categorised as lowest, low, middle, high, and highest [27]. Behavioural characteristics, such as media exposure (frequency of watching TV, listening to the radio, and reading a newspaper or magazine), were also considered. At the community level, determinants such as place of residence, administrative region, and community-level female youth literacy level were included. These variables were considered based on previously published literature and the availability of information in the dataset (Supplementary Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Determinants of barriers to accessing healthcare

The statistical analysis involved conducting multilevel binary logistic regression. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in the bivariable analysis were considered eligible for the multivariable analysis. Associations between outcome and independent variables were assessed using a generalised linear mixed (GLM) logistic regression model, incorporating both random and fixed-effect analyses into the linear predictor, and their strength was presented using adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Four models were developed: Model 1 (null model) only included the cluster level using random intercepts; Model 2 included individual-level variables plus random intercept; Model 3 included community-level variables plus random intercept; and Model 4 included both individual- and community-level variables plus random intercept.

The measure of variation or random effects was estimated by the median odds ratio (MOR), intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), and Proportional Change in Variance (PCV). Deviance was utilised for model comparison, with the model exhibiting the lowest deviance considered the best-fit model (Table 1).

Spatial analysis of barriers to accessing healthcare

For the spatial analysis, we used a similar outcome variable derived from the mixed model analyses. The Global Moran’s I statistic was used to assess spatial autocorrelation [30]. The Global Moran’s I value ranges from − 1 to + 1, where a value ≤ -0.8 indicates very strong negative spatial autocorrelation, while values ≥ 0.8 indicate very strong positive spatial autocorrelation [30, 31]. For spatial interpolation, a spherical semivariogram ordinary kriging technique was used to predict the barriers to accessing healthcare barriers among youth in Pakistan for unsampled areas based on sampled clusters. The default of the 12 nearest input sample points was used to perform interpolation. The proportion of female youth facing barriers to accessing healthcare in each cluster served as input for spatial prediction. Kuldorff’s SaTScan version 9.6 software was utilised to fit Bernoulli-based modelled spatial scan statistics to identify the locations of clusters or spatial windows where barriers to accessing healthcare exist [32]. The scanning window traverses the study area, with female youth facing barriers to accessing healthcare designated as cases and those not facing barriers designated as non-cases to fit the Bernoulli model.

Barriers to accessing healthcare and reproductive health services uptake

While confirming the precise timing of maternal and reproductive health services uptake and incorporating them into our marginal GLM model presents challenges, a chi-square analysis was conducted to assess the association between perceived barriers to healthcare access and the uptake of reproductive health services (including contraceptive utilisation, place of delivery, recommended ANC visit, and early ANC visit follow-up) among female youth in Pakistan.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total weighted sample of 2,924 female youth were included in this study. Three-fourth of (77.2%) these youth were aged between 20 and 24 years, with a median age of 21 years (interquartile range (IQR) = 3 years). More than two-fifths (42.8%) of youth had no formal education. Additionally, the majority of these youth resided in rural areas (71.7%) and hailed from the province of Punjab (42.6%) (Table 2).

Perceived barriers to accessing healthcare among female youth in Pakistan

In this study, about 79.4% (95% CI: 77.9 to 80.9) of female youth in Pakistan reported encountering at least one barrier to accessing healthcare services. Among the types of barriers identified, the most prevalent was the reluctance to visit health facilities alone and travel distance to reach a health facility, reported by 74.9% and 51.1% of female youth respectively. Additionally, more than one-fourth (26.4%) of female youth faced difficulties in obtaining permission to visit healthcare facilities (Fig. 2).

Determinants of barriers to accessing healthcare among female youth in Pakistan

The final model results showed that not having employment, being in a household where the head is a male, having low and middle household wealth status, being an unempowered woman, residing in areas with low community female youth literacy rates, and living in Punjab, KPK, Balochistan, AJK and FATA regions are all significantly associated with increased odds of facing barriers to accessing healthcare (Table 2).

In the study, female youth without employment have nearly twice the odds of facing barriers to accessing healthcare compared to those with employment (AOR = 1.96; 95% CI; 1.39, 2.76). Similarly, if the head of household is male, the odds of facing barriers to accessing healthcare are approximately two times higher (AOR = 1.97; 95% CI; 1.40, 2.77). Regarding household wealth status, female youths from households with low and middle wealth status have three- and two-times greater odds of facing barriers to accessing healthcare (low: AOR = 3.22; 95% CI; 2.18, 4.75; middle AOR = 1.89; 95% CI; 1.36, 2.63), respectively. Furthermore, unempowered female youth and those from communities with low literacy clusters have two- and 1.5-times higher odds of encountering barriers to accessing healthcare compared to empowered youths and those from communities with high female youth literacy clusters (unempowered: AOR = 2.08; 95% CI; 1.55, 2.80; communities with low female youth literacy clusters: AOR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.02, 2.15), respectively. Moreover, the odds of having barriers to accessing healthcare are higher among female youths from Punjab, KPK, Balochistan, AJK and FATA regions compared to Sindh region (Table 3).

Spatial analysis of barriers to accessing health care among female youth in Pakistan

Spatial autocorrelation and distribution

The Global Moran’s Index spatial autocorrelation was applied to assess the spatial clustering of barriers to accessing healthcare among female youth in Pakistan. The standardised spatial weights are applied, with each weight being divided by the sum of the weights of all neighbouring features. Spatial standardisation is recommended whenever the distribution of features is expected to be potentially biased due to the sampling design. The analysis revealed significant positive spatial autocorrelation of barriers to accessing healthcare among female youth across regions in the country. This indicates clustering, with less than 1% likelihood that this clustered pattern could be the result of random chance, as evidenced by a Global Moran’s Index value of 0.15 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Moreover, barriers to accessing healthcare among female youth were more prevalent in the FATA (99%), Balochistan (88.7%), and AJK (85.1%) regions (Figs. 4 and 5).

Hotspot analysis and significant windows of barriers to accessing health care among female youth in Pakistan

The Getis-Ord Gi* hotspot analysis showed that the FATA region, southern parts of Punjab, Southern part of KPK and Southeastern parts of Balochistan are estimated with 90% or more confidence to be hotspot areas with barriers to accessing healthcare among female youth. However, ICT, Southern Sindh, GB and AJK regions are estimated with 90% or more confidence to be cold spot areas (Fig. 6).

Furthermore, Bernoulli-based modelled spatial scan statistics identified 115 clusters found in a primary window, indicating barriers to healthcare access. This primary window was centred at 31.567465 N and 69.752357 E, with a radius of 316.42 km. These clusters were distributed across all FATA provinces, the southern part of KPK, the northern part of Balochistan, and the eastern parts of Punjab province. Female youth residing in the primary window were 1.2 times more likely to have barriers to accessing health care than those residing outside the window regions (RR = 1.21, P-value < 0.001) (Table 4; Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the determinants and spatial distribution of healthcare access barriers among female youths in Pakistan. Despite efforts by the WHO and other stakeholders to achieve universal health coverage for the global community [7], nearly four-fifths (79.4%) of young females in Pakistan reported facing major barriers to accessing healthcare services. Among these, reluctance to visit health facilities alone and the travel distance to reach a health facility were the most frequently mentioned barriers. Several individual level factors such as unemployment, being in a household where the head is a male, having low and middle household wealth status, being an unempowered woman and community-level factors such as residing in areas with low community female youth literacy rates and living in some geographic areas such Punjab, KPK, Balochistan, AJK and FATA regions were associated with increased odds of facing barriers to accessing healthcare.

The magnitude of female youth having major barriers to accessing healthcare services in this study is higher than in a study conducted in Ethiopia with the same age group (61.3%) [33], and Benin (60.4%) [34], Tanzania (65.0%) [35] and Bangladesh (66.3%) [36] among reproductive women (15–49 years old). This might be due to socio-cultural differences among countries which may affect health-seeking behaviors. This was reflected when financial barriers were the primary barrier (46.3%) to accessing health care among female youth in Ethiopia [33], rather than the fourth barrier (34.1%) in Pakistan in this study. Meanwhile, unwillingness to visit health facilities alone (74.9%) was predominant in Pakistan as compared to compared to being the least significant barrier in Ethiopia [32]. This could be attributed to the patriarchal framework of society, which operates at all levels to place women in a more vulnerable position than men [22] and may not be confident to visit the healthcare facilities alone. According to the Global Gender Gap Report 2018, Pakistan only outperformed Yemen globally [20]. The other factors might also be due to differences in health service availability across study areas. A study showed that the important factors influencing women’s access to primary health care (PHC) services in Pakistan were the convenience of PHC locations and distance [37].

This study showed that female youths from households with low and middle wealth status have greater odds of facing barriers to accessing healthcare. This is in line with studies done in Tanzania [35], Ethiopia [33], and Sub-Saharan Africa [38]. The possible reason might be that financial difficulty in covering health care expenses is one of the barriers to accessing health care, and youths from households with better wealth status may experience reduced challenges in accessing health care [33, 38]. Despite the government of Pakistan having launched the social health protection initiative ‘Sehat Sahulat (health convenience) Program [39, 40], a large proportion of the population in Pakistan is not yet covered [40]. Hence, they lack the necessary financial protection while accessing health services. Therefore, direct costs, such as payments for drugs and services, as well as indirect costs such as transportation expenses and lost wages due to unpaid working hours may negatively impact the accessibility of health services [34].

Our results showed that unempowered female youth and those whose head of household is male faced higher odds of barriers to accessing healthcare. This is consistent with a study in Myanmar, which found that women with low and moderate levels of empowerment are more likely to face barriers to accessing health care compared to those with high empowerment [41]. This finding is further supported by previous studies [42–44]. This may be because Pakistan has been characterised as having low autonomy or low empowerment for women, largely influenced by cultural and religious beliefs [20, 23, 24]. Despite women participating in domestic decisions reported in Pakistan [23, 45], women empowerment in this study was calculated from all three decision-making (own health care, large household purchases, and visiting family or relatives) and disagreed with all reasons justifying wife-beating.

Furthermore, female youth from communities with low literacy clusters have two times higher odds of encountering barriers to accessing healthcare compared to those from communities with high female youth literacy clusters. This is supported by studies in Ethiopia [33], and Benin [34], in which youths with no formal education and primary education had higher odds of encountering barriers to accessing healthcare compared to youths with higher educational status. This is because literacy rate is an outcome indicator to evaluate educational attainment. Community literacy was calculated based on the proportion of female youth in the community who can both read and write with understanding a short simple statement about their everyday life [46, 47]. As a result, they may have better awareness and exhibit increased health-seeking behaviour [33]. Literate women imply that they can seek and use information for the betterment of the health, and education of their household members [46, 47]. Literate women are also empowered to play a meaningful role in the communities [46]. Moreover, it might be due to education being the prominent factor of higher employment opportunities, earning an individual, or community economic growth that may in turn increase accessibility for healthcare services [33, 34].

In this study, female youth without employment have nearly two times greater odds of facing barriers to accessing healthcare compared to those who had employment. This is supported by a study which states that people in disadvantaged social positions are more likely to perceive barriers to accessing healthcare [48, 49]. The cost of health care is one of the major barriers to accessing health care that young people commonly experience [50]. Young people may believe that they cannot access a service without payment or without their parents finding out [50]. If young people are without employment, they may have difficulty meeting health care costs and other indirect expenses such as transport. According to PKDHS 2017-18 report a significantly smaller percentage of currently married women (19%) than men (98%) were employed during the past 12 months [51]. Therefore, the government had better subsidise health expenditures for those who are without employment.

Moreover, the odds of having barriers to accessing healthcare are higher among female youths from Punjab, KPK, Balochistan, AJK and FATA regions compared to Sindh region. The spatial distribution results also indicate that the FATA, Balochistan, and AJK regions exhibit the highest prevalence of barriers to accessing healthcare among female youth. This is supported by a study in Ethiopia which showed that barriers to healthcare access among female youths varied greatly across regions [33]. A study in Pakistan also showed that spatial inequalities concerning access to health and health-related social indicators persist across different regions and geographical areas within the country [26]. This study states that based on the community health index score, the Baluchistan region has been identified as significantly more deprived or disadvantaged in terms of health and well-being than the Punjab [26]. This could be because the variations in healthcare programs across regions might contribute to these disparities. For example, UNICEF [52] launched an effective adolescent empowerment pilot programme in Sindh in 2017 [4], but not in other regions. Moreover, the low barriers in Sindh might be due to the presence of Pakistan’s largest city and economic hub in that province. Conversely, rural and agrarian regions, such as parts of Balochistan, face infrastructural limitations, including fewer healthcare facilities and challenges related to cultural norms that discourage healthcare-seeking behaviours, especially for women. Differences in socioeconomic status and cultural practices among regions may have also contributed to these variations.

Generally, this study is significant to Pakistan since it focuses on the youth population, which makes up nearly one-third of the total population [18]. However, there are several limitations to consider. First, as we used secondary data, not all proximal predictors of healthcare access barriers could be included, potentially leaving out key determinants. Second, the study relied on nationally aggregated data, which may not fully capture regional and residential differences in social and cultural norms that influence healthcare-seeking behaviours. Lastly, the urban-rural divide, particularly in terms of access to healthcare facilities and differences in cultural practices, may have influenced the study’s results, warranting caution in generalizing findings.

Conclusions

Most of the female youth in Pakistan have reported having major barriers to accessing healthcare services. Barriers to accessing healthcare were associated with female youth not having employment, being in a household where the head is a male, having low and middle household wealth status, being unempowered, and residing in areas with low community female youth literacy rates. Significant geographic variation was observed in the barriers to healthcare services among Pakistan’s female youth.

Policy implication

The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for targeted policy interventions to improve healthcare access for female youth in Pakistan. Policymakers should prioritise the following actions:

Enhance women empowerment: Implement educational and vocational training programs to empower female youth, enabling them to independently visit health facilities and increasing their employment opportunities and financial independence. Empowerment programs should also focus on increasing community awareness about the importance of female autonomy in health-related decisions.Increase healthcare accessibility: Develop and implement policies that reduce travel distance to healthcare facilities by increasing the number of health facilities and introducing mobile health clinics to reach hot spot areas such as FATA, Balochistan and AJK.
