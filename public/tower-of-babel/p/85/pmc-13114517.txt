Addressing Low E/S and N/P Ratio Challenges in Li–S Batteries with a Multifunctional Interlayer
Authors: Cinthya Paulina, Donghyeok Son, Huiwon Jang, Hyunmin Park, Junhee Woo, Su Yeong Kim, Dongsoo Kim, Jihan Kim, Jinwoo Lee, Steve Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are promising next‐generation energy storage systems due to their high energy density and low cost of sulfur. However, their commercialization is hindered by the shuttle effect of soluble polysulfides and the poor rate capability, which are further exacerbated under practical conditions of low negative/positive (N/P) ratio and electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio necessary for high‐energy and cost‐effective Li–S batteries. To address these challenges, we report the fabrication of a large‐area (9.5 cm × 7.5 cm), ultrathin (90 nm), lightweight (0.05 mg cm−2), and conductive PFSA/CNT/PEDOT:TCB (PCP) composite interlayer via the solution shearing technique coupled with Marangoni‐flow‐assisted film transfer, enabling uniform film formation and facile transfer of the interlayer onto a polypropylene (PP) separator. In addition to providing efficient electronic pathways, the PCP interlayer improves ionic conductivity and mitigates polysulfide shuttling through electrostatic repulsion and physical size exclusion, as validated by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. These combined functionalities allow the PCP interlayer to overcome key challenges in Li–S batteries, leading to a 3 × 5 cm2 pouch‐type cell that delivers a high initial discharge capacity of 1086 mAh g−1 over 68 cycles under practical conditions of E/S = 5 µL mg−1 and N/P ratio = 1.3.Keywords: lithium‐sulfur batteries, Marangoni‐flow‐assisted film transfer, multifunctional composite interlayer, shuttle effect inhibition, solution shearing technique

Introduction

The rapid growth in electric vehicles and portable electronics has created an urgent demand for energy storage technologies that deliver higher energy density at lower costs [1]. Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are among the most promising candidates to meet this need, offering a high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1 due to the high capacities of sulfur (1675 mAh g−1) and lithium metal (3860 mAh g−1) as electrodes [2, 3]. Additionally, sulfur contributes to the cost‐effectiveness of Li–S batteries as it is abundant in nature and serves as a byproduct of the petroleum industry [4]. Despite their remarkable potential, the practical application of Li–S batteries remains hindered mainly by two major intrinsic challenges: (1) the shuttle effect, where soluble lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) migrate between electrodes, leading to the irreversible loss of active sulfur and severe capacity decay [5], and (2) poor rate capability, arising from the intrinsically insulating nature of sulfur (5 × 10−30 S cm−1) [6] and its discharge product, Li2S (≈ 10−9 S cm−1) [7].

Addressing these issues becomes even more challenging under the stringent conditions required for high‐energy and cost‐efficient Li–S batteries. Manthiram and colleagues proposed the “five 5s” as the critical metrics to achieve the high‐energy‐density goal of Li–S batteries, three of which include electrolyte‐to‐sulfur (E/S) ratio <5 µL mg−1, negative‐to‐positive (N/P) ratio <5, and sulfur loading >5 mg cm−2 in pouch cells [8]. Under these practical conditions, however, the intrinsic challenges of Li–S batteries are further exacerbated, making the realization of practical Li–S batteries more challenging. Under low E/S ratio conditions, the high concentration gradient of LiPSs intensifies the shuttle effect, while the increased electrolyte viscosity reduces ionic conductivity, degrading the rate performance [9, 10]. In low N/P ratio systems, the lack of excess lithium makes it challenging to compensate for the loss of active lithium caused by the shuttle effect, significantly shortening battery lifespan [11, 12, 13].

Among various strategies proposed to mitigate these issues, the use of interlayers has gained considerable attention due to its simplicity and effectiveness in enhancing Li–S battery performance [14, 15]. Interlayers typically function as physical or chemical barriers to suppress polysulfide shuttling [16, 17]. When functionalized with conductive materials or catalysts, however, they go beyond this conventional role by simultaneously addressing multiple challenges, such as poor electrical/ionic conductivity and lithium anode degradation [18, 19, 20, 21]. Moreover, compared to the complex and costly processes required for electrode structure modifications, the fabrication of interlayers is relatively more straightforward and scalable [22]. Despite these advantages, most studies on interlayers have been conducted under flooded electrolyte and excess lithium conditions (Table S1), which fail to address the challenges posed by practical operating parameters [8]. Furthermore, the inclusion of an interlayer introduces an additional component to the battery system, which can decrease energy density and elongate the diffusion pathways for Li+ [23]. Therefore, developing thin and lightweight interlayers with minimal energy‐density penalty, together with a scalable and facile fabrication process, is critical for advancing the commercial viability of Li–S batteries.

Herein, we report the development of a functional interlayer designed to address key limitations of Li–S batteries under the practical conditions of low E/S and low N/P ratios (Figure 1). The composite interlayer consists of short‐side‐chain perfluorosulfonic acid (SSC‐PFSA, specifically Aquivion D72‐25BS), multi‐walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), and poly(3,4‐ethylene dioxythiophene):polystyrene‐sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS), coupled with 1‐ethyl‐3‐methyl‐imidazolium tetracyanoborate (EMIM TCB) ionic liquid (IL) through counter‐ion exchange. The resulting PFSA/CNT/PEDOT:TCB (PCP) interlayer exhibits superior performance in suppressing the shuttle effect via both physical and chemical mechanisms. The nanometer‐scale pore size selectively permits the passage of Li+ while effectively confining LiPSs, while the sulfonic acid groups in SSC‐PFSA repel soluble LiPSs via electrostatic interactions. The PCP interlayer also improves rate capability through the incorporation of conductive materials, such as MWCNTs and PEDOT:TCB, which significantly accelerates sulfur redox kinetics. Using a novel Marangoni‐flow‐assisted film transfer method combined with a scalable solution shearing technique, we successfully fabricated an ultrathin (90 nm), lightweight (0.05 mg cm−2), and uniform interlayer over a large area (up to 9.5 cm × 7.5 cm) on a polypropylene (PP) separator. As a result, the Li–S batteries assembled with the PCP interlayer demonstrated an excellent performance under the practical conditions of E/S ratio = 5 µL mg−1 and N/P ratio = 1.3 in a pouch‐type cell, delivering a high initial discharge capacity of 1086 mAh g−1 over 68 cycles. This performance can be attributed to the interlayer's outstanding ability to suppress the shuttle effect and accelerate sulfur conversion reaction kinetics. These results under practical battery conditions underscore the critical role of scalable and controllable fabrication methods in optimizing the effectiveness of functional interlayers in Li–S batteries.

Insertion of the PCP interlayer alleviates the shuttle effect, reactivates dead sulfur species, and enhances Li+ transport, resulting in high‐performance Li–S batteries under practical conditions of low E/S and N/P ratios.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication and Characterizations of The PCP Interlayer

The fabrication of the PCP interlayer follows a three‐step process: solution shearing of the composite ink, ionic liquid (IL) treatment, and Marangoni‐flow‐assisted film transfer. First, the PCP ink composed of PFSA, MWCNTs, and PEDOT:PSS was prepared. Notably, this specific combination was found to be critical from a fabrication perspective, as control experiments excluding individual components showed that only the full PCP composition enabled stable slurry formation and successful film transfer (Figure S1). The prepared ink was then coated onto a heated glass substrate via solution shearing, forming a uniform interlayer film (Figure 2a). This method enables precise control over film thickness by adjusting key parameters, such as the concentration of the ink solution, the volume of the solution applied, substrate temperature, blade speed, and the gap between the substrate and the blade [24, 25]. Subsequently, the coated film underwent an IL treatment, wherein EMIM TCB IL was solution‐sheared onto the preformed film to induce counter‐ion exchange with PEDOT:PSS (Figure 2b). During this process, bulky and insulating PSS− within the film were replaced with TCB−, leading to the formation of PEDOT:TCB. This structural modification enhances electrical conductivity by promoting highly ordered structures and reducing the π‐π stacking distance [26]. As the final step, the PCP interlayer was transferred from the glass substrate to the PP separator using a novel Marangoni‐flow‐assisted film transfer method developed in this study. Given the mechanical fragility of ultrathin films, it is prone to wrinkling and damage during the transfer process. This technique leverages Marangoni flow, a phenomenon of surface flow driven by surface tension gradients, to facilitate spontaneous film spreading [27]. By utilizing the surface tension gradients between the ethanol‐wet film on the PP separator and the surrounding water, this method ensures rapid, uniform, and defect‐free film transfer (Figure 2c). This fabrication method enables large‐area printing and smooth film transfer, yielding interlayers with the size of up to 9.5 cm × 7.5 cm (Figure S2), which is essential for practical Li–S battery applications.

To minimize the impact of gravimetric/volumetric energy density loss and the extended Li+ diffusion pathways associated with interlayers, optimizing both their thickness and weight is crucial [28, 29]. As shown in Figure S3, varying the shearing speeds and ink concentration led to different film thicknesses. The thinnest film was achieved with a 1:1 water dilution of the PCP ink at a shearing speed of 1.0 mm s−1, the speed that belongs to the transition region between the evaporation and the Landau‐Levich regime. These results highlight the scalability of the solution shearing technique, enabling precise thickness control through parameter adjustments. With a thickness of only 90 nm, the PCP interlayer achieved an ultralow mass loading of 0.05 mg cm−2, significantly outperforming other reported interlayers fabricated via conventional methods such as slurry coating or vacuum filtration (Figure S4 and Table S2). Notably, the vacuum‐filtrated PCP films lacked precise thickness control, whereas slurry‐coated films suffer from non‐uniformity (Figure S5), leading to inferior cycling performance compared with the solution‐sheared PCP film (Figure S6). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) surface images and atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis further confirmed that the solution‐sheared PCP interlayer formed a highly uniform coating on the PP separator, exhibiting a surface roughness lower than that of the pristine PP separator (Figure 2d).

The successful film transfer was further confirmed by SEM cross‐sectional imaging and energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Figure 2e), showing uniform adhesion of the PCP interlayer to the PP separator, while high‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) and the corresponding elemental analysis (Figure S7) verified the successful incorporation of the interlayer components. The chemical stability of the PCP interlayer in the electrolyte was assessed through an electrolyte aging test (Figure S8). After immersion in the electrolyte for 40 days, no visible degradation or detachment of the film was observed. This result indicates that the film is stable in electrolyte environments, which is essential for ensuring long‐term functionality during battery cycling. Moreover, mechanical flexibility was evaluated via the bending/recovery test (Figure S9). The PCP interlayer exhibited excellent flexibility and mechanical resilience, fully recovering its original structure after bending. These characteristics are critical for maintaining structural integrity during battery assembly and cycling.

The counter‐ion exchange mechanism between PEDOT:PSS and EMIM TCB is illustrated in Figure 2f and further validated by X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The N 1s XPS spectra of PEDOT:PSS and the PCP film (Figure 2g) revealed the emergence of an N 1s peak after IL treatment, attributed to the positively charged nitrogen in EMIM+ binding to the negatively charged SO3 − groups of PSS− [30]. Meanwhile, the S 2p XPS spectra of PEDOT:PSS and PCP films (Figure 2h) showed a shift towards lower binding energy following IL treatment, indicating that the cationic PEDOT+ originally bound to PSS− is replaced by EMIM+ [26, 30]. The presence of EMIM+ PSS− interactions confirmed the successful exchange process, inferring the formation of PEDOT:TCB in the composite. Additionally, the N 1s and S 2p XPS spectra of CNT/PEDOT:PSS (CP) before and after IL treatment are presented in Figure S10 for reference. The electrical conductivity of the PCP film before and after IL treatment was evaluated using four‐point probe measurements. The results show that the conductivity of the PCP film increased from 4.4 to 31.4 S cm−1 upon IL treatment (Figure 2i), underscoring the effectiveness of counter‐ion exchange in enhancing electrical conductivity, which can be attributed to the improved π–π stacking [26].

Shuttle Effect Inhibition by The PCP Interlayer

The effectiveness of the PCP interlayer in mitigating the shuttle effect was assessed through H‐cell tests and shuttle current measurements. As shown in Figure 3a, the H‐cell with only a PP separator exhibited a pronounced color change after 24 h, turning from colorless to dark yellow, whereas the H‐cell with the PCP interlayer showed only a slight change to pale yellow, indicating significantly reduced polysulfide migration. This visual observation was further quantified using ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy. According to the Beer‐Lambert law, the peak intensities of the UV–Vis spectra are directly proportional to the relative concentrations of the sample solutions, which, in this case, were collected from the H‐cell after 24 h. As shown in Figure 3b, the solution from the H‐cell with the PCP interlayer exhibited a lower peak intensity, indicating a lower polysulfide concentration compared with the solution from the H‐cell with only a PP separator. Consistently, shuttle current measurements further verified the superior shuttle effect suppression by the PCP interlayer. The Li–S cell with the PCP interlayer exhibited a significantly lower shuttle current (0.0628 mA cm−2) than the cell with only a PP separator (0.127 mA cm−2) (Figure 3c). To gain a deeper insight into the mechanism behind this suppression behavior, zeta potential measurements (Figure S11 and Table S3) were performed on different interlayer slurry compositions to evaluate their surface charge characteristics. The results show that the addition of PFSA significantly increased the negative surface potential, confirming the formation of a negatively charged interface capable of repelling polysulfide anions. These findings highlight the PCP interlayer‘s superior ability to mitigate the shuttle effect, which can be attributed to the sulfonic acid groups of PFSA that electrostatically repel polysulfide anions.

As shown by the SEM surface images of the PP separator and the PCP interlayer in Figure 2d previously, the pores of the PP separator were uniformly covered by the PCP interlayer. This suggests that the PCP interlayer can function as a physical barrier to polysulfide migration. To verify this hypothesis, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to analyze the functionality of the PCP interlayer. The interlayer was modeled using PEDOTs, PSSs, PFSAs, and CNTs mixed according to their experimental mass ratio (Figure S12) and subsequently equilibrated. Root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD) analysis of interlayer atoms showed convergence after approximately 1 ns, confirming that the interlayer attained structural stability following equilibration (Figure S13). Owing to its amorphous composite nature, the PCP interlayer exhibited a heterogeneous and dynamically fluctuating pore structure rather than a single well‐defined pore size (Figure S14). Pore size distribution (PSD) analysis revealed a broad distribution of intrinsic free‐volume elements, with the pore population predominantly concentrated in the small‐size regime. In particular, the interlayer composite exhibited a pore‐limiting diameter (PLD) of 1.87 Å, corresponding to the minimum geometric constriction that governs molecular passage through the pore network, rather than representing a characteristic or average pore size of the interlayer. The consistency of the PLD value was further supported by MD simulations (Figure S15). This geometric bottleneck is significantly smaller than the characteristic sizes of polysulfide species, including (8.1 Å), S4− (11.1 Å), and S6− (13.8 Å) [31], indicating that the interlayer structure is geometrically unfavorable for polysulfide penetration. To further elucidate the ionic transport environment within the interlayer, an additional geometric descriptor, the largest cavity diameter (LCD), was analyzed. The LCD reached 6.4 Å, reflecting the size of the largest pore cavity accommodated within the interlayer. This descriptor (Figure 3d) indicates the presence of larger pore cavities beyond the geometric bottleneck, which allow ion transport through the interlayer once the narrow constriction is traversed, even for solvated Li+ species. Collectively, these structural characteristics suggest that the PCP interlayer effectively suppresses polysulfide diffusion while remaining compatible with Li+ transport across the interlayer.

Further penetration MD simulations demonstrate the effectiveness of the functional interlayer by illustrating the dynamics of Li+ and LiPSs migration. Initially, Li+ and LiPSs, along with the solvents, were positioned on one side of the interlayer. The system was then observed to determine whether these molecules could penetrate the interlayer after a 5 ns simulation time. As depicted in Figure 3e, the results indicate that only Li+ species penetrated the interlayer, while the LiPSs remained blocked even after sufficient simulation time. This phenomenon was consistently observed across three simulation sets of the same system, each featuring different composite configurations (Figure S16), enhancing the reliability of the simulation results. These findings elucidate the mechanism by which the functional interlayer effectively prevents the shuttle effect by selectively blocking the migration of LiPSs toward the anode while allowing Li+ to pass through. This computational evidence reinforces the role of the PCP interlayer as a dual‐function shuttle inhibitor, effectively suppressing polysulfide crossover via both electrostatic repulsion and physical size exclusion.

By inhibiting the shuttle effect, the PCP interlayer also plays a critical role in preserving the structural integrity of the lithium anode by preventing detrimental side reactions with dissolved polysulfides. As shown in Figure 3f,g, the lithium anode in the Li–S cell with the PCP interlayer exhibited a more compact and uniform morphology, with large granular features and minimal porosity, compared to the anode in the cell without the PCP interlayer after both 1 and 200 cycles. To analyze the changes in Li deposition morphology, XPS analysis was conducted on the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer formed on cycled lithium in Li–S cells. The results show that, in the absence of the PCP interlayer, the cycled Li anode exhibited a significantly higher sulfur atomic ratio at all etch times than that with the PCP interlayer, due to the severe shuttle effect (Figure S17). In particular, compared with the SEI layer on cycled Li anode with the PCP interlayer, the SEI layer formed without the PCP interlayer exhibited a more pronounced Li2S peak (159.8 eV) [32], originating from the reductive decomposition of LiPSs, than the SO3 2− peak (166.8 eV) [33] from salt decomposition. Since the Li2S‐rich SEI layer is chemically unstable against LiPSs, continuous corrosion between Li metal and LiPSs occurs, leading to the formation of a nonuniform SEI layer [34]. This, in turn, induces an uneven Li+ flux, making uniform lithium deposition challenging. Therefore, the formation of a LiPSs‐stable SEI layer with a lower Li2S content, enabled by the strong shuttle‐suppression capability of the PCP interlayer, promotes a more uniform Li+ flux, facilitating uniform and compact lithium deposition. These results underscore the PCP interlayer's protective role in stabilizing the lithium anode, effectively reducing side reactions between lithium and soluble polysulfides.

Kinetics Evaluation of Li–S Batteries With The PCP Interlayer

Several analyses were conducted to evaluate the electrochemical effects of the PCP interlayer in Li–S batteries. Distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis was employed to provide a rigorous kinetic assessment by deconvoluting impedance responses (Figure S18 and Table S4) into electrochemical processes with distinct characteristic time constants. As shown in Figure 4a,b, the Li–S cell with the PCP interlayer exhibited a pronounced reduction in the intermediate‐τ region (log τ ≈ −4), corresponding to charge‐transfer processes at the positive electrode [35]. Quantitatively, the charge‐transfer resistance decreased from 31.5 Ω for the PP separator to 8.7 Ω for the PCP interlayer, representing a 72.4% reduction, which directly evidences intrinsically accelerated sulfur redox kinetics.

Plots of specific peak current vs. the square root of the scan rate for the (d) cathodic peak 1, (e) cathodic peak 2, (f) anodic peak.

In addition, the high‐τ region (log τ > 0), associated with bulk Li+ diffusion, showed a dramatic decrease in resistance from 22.9 Ω (PP) to 1.7 Ω (PCP) [35], indicating substantially enhanced ionic transport across the cell. These quantitative reductions in both charge‐transfer and Li+ diffusion resistances demonstrate that the performance improvement induced by the PCP interlayer originates from kinetic enhancement. The Li+ conductivity of the PCP interlayer was further evaluated using stainless‐steel (SS) symmetric cells, showing an ionic conductivity of 7.55 mS cm−1, which is significantly higher than that of the cell without the interlayer (2.52 mS cm−1) (Figure S18 and Table S4). This result suggests that the abundant lithiophilic sulfonic groups in PFSA significantly enhance Li+ transport within the cell. To further investigate the effect of interlayer thickness on the electrochemical performances of Li–S battery cells, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on both full Li–S cells and SS symmetric cells with thicker PCP interlayers (750 nm and 1300 nm). The corresponding spectra, shown in Figure S19, indicate that increasing the interlayer thickness led to an increase in both charge‐transfer resistance (Rct) and solution resistance (Rs) values, likely due to the extended Li+ transport pathways. These findings emphasize the importance of an ultrathin interlayer for practical Li–S battery applications.

The sulfur conversion reaction kinetics in Li–S batteries with the PCP interlayer were further evaluated through cyclic voltammetry (CV). As shown in Figure 4c, the CV curve at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1 of the battery with the PCP interlayer exhibited a higher current and lower overpotential than the battery with only a PP separator, indicating that the interlayer enhances both electrical and ionic conductivity, thereby improving reaction kinetics. For comparison, CV tests conducted with other cathode compositions (Figure S20) also showed reduced overpotentials and slightly increased peak currents upon incorporating the PCP interlayer, confirming that the kinetic enhancement is consistently observed regardless of cathode configuration. CV measurements were also conducted at various scan rates (0.1 to 1.0 mV s−1) for both Li–S coin cells with and without the PCP interlayer (Figure S21). Further analysis of the Li+ diffusion coefficients at each CV peak using the Randles–Sevcik equation reveals that Li–S cells with the PCP interlayer demonstrated superior Li+ diffusion compared to those without the interlayer across all analyzed peaks (Figure 4d–f). This enhanced ionic conductivity can be attributed to the high‐density lithiophilic sulfonic acid groups in PFSA, which promote Li+ transport, as well as the improved electrolyte wettability of the PCP interlayer. The latter is evidenced by the lower contact angle observed for the PCP interlayer compared to the PP separator (Figure S22). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the PCP interlayer significantly enhances both the electrical and ionic transport properties in Li–S batteries, thereby improving the overall electrochemical performances.

Performance Evaluation of Li–S Batteries With The PCP Interlayer Under Practical Conditions

The electrochemical performances of Li–S coin cells with and without the PCP interlayer were evaluated through rate capability and galvanostatic cycling tests. As shown in Figure 5a, the cell with the PCP interlayer consistently delivered higher discharge capacities across all C‐rates, with the performance gap widening at higher C‐rates (1.0 C and 2.0 C). This improvement is attributed to the interlayer's high electrical conductivity, which accelerates reaction kinetics. The effect becomes more pronounced at higher C‐rates, where faster charge and discharge cycles are required. Galvanostatic cycling at a high C‐rate of 1.0 C, following precycling at 0.1 C for three cycles, demonstrated that the cell with the PCP interlayer achieved a high initial discharge capacity of 933.43 mAh g− 1 and maintained stable performance for up to 500 cycles. In contrast, the cell with only a PP separator exhibited a lower initial discharge capacity of 838.94 mAh g− 1 and reached the end of its cycle life after 344 cycles (Figure 5b). The corresponding voltage profiles at the 300th cycle are shown in Figure S23. Furthermore, a long‐term cycling test at a lower C‐rate of 0.1 C was conducted to further evaluate the long‐term performance of the Li–S cells with the PCP interlayer under various conditions (Figure S24). This enhanced performance is mainly attributed to the PCP interlayer's ability to mitigate the shuttle effect and improve both electrical and ionic conductivity. The improved electrical conductivity facilitates the reactivation of dead sulfur species, as evidenced by the reactivation test (Figure S25), showing a higher recovered capacity and lower overpotential compared with the PP separator. Meanwhile, the enhanced ionic conductivity promotes more efficient Li+ transport, further contributing to stable long‐term cycling.

To further assess the interlayer's viability in practical Li–S batteries, galvanostatic cycling tests were conducted under stringent conditions: lean electrolyte (5 µL mg−1), low N/P ratio (1.7), and high sulfur loading (6 mg cm−2). As shown in Figure 5c, under lean electrolyte conditions, where limited electrolyte availability exacerbates the shuttle effect and hinders ion transport, the cell with the PCP interlayer exhibited significantly higher discharge capacity and extended cycling stability than the cell with only a PP separator. The charge–discharge voltage profiles at selected cycles are shown in Figure S26. This improvement can be attributed to the sulfonic acid groups in PFSA, which mitigate the shuttle effect and facilitate Li+ transport even in highly viscous electrolyte environments.

Under low N/P ratio conditions, where coin cells were assembled with higher sulfur‐loading cathodes (6–7 mg cm−2) and thin lithium anodes (50 µm), the impact of the PCP interlayer was further evident. Initially, both cells exhibited comparable discharge capacities. Over repeated cycles, however, the cell with the PP separator suffered severe capacity degradation, retaining only 470.45 mAh g−1 after 45 cycles (Figure 5d). In contrast, the cell incorporating the PCP interlayer maintained superior cycling stability, retaining a discharge capacity of 678.59 mAh g−1 at the same cycle number. Voltage profiles corresponding to the cycling tests under low N/P conditions are provided in Figure S27. This stability is due to the interlayer's ability to suppress polysulfide migration and minimize side reactions between dissolved LiPSs and Li+, thereby preserving active Li+ availability for sulfur redox reactions. As a result, the coin cell with the PCP interlayer achieved both high discharge capacity and extended cycle life, even under conditions with limited excess lithium.

To further validate the PCP interlayer's scalability, 3 × 5 cm2 pouch‐type Li–S batteries were fabricated and cycled under practical conditions (E/S ratio = 5 µL mg−1 and N/P ratio = 1.3). As shown in Figure 5e, the pouch cell with the PCP interlayer delivered a high initial discharge capacity of 1086 mAh g−1 over 68 cycles, outperforming the cell with only a PP separator in both capacity and cycling stability. The corresponding voltage profiles (Figure 5f) reveal that after 30 cycles, the pouch cell with the PCP interlayer exhibited a higher discharge capacity and lower overpotential, further underscoring its improved electrochemical performance. When compared to previously reported Li–S pouch cells under comparable or even milder conditions (Figures S28,S29 and Table S5), our system demonstrated superior stability, showing the lowest capacity decay rate of approximately 0.6% per cycle despite operating under practical parameters of lower E/S ratio and thinner lithium metal anode. In addition, Figure S30 demonstrates the practical feasibility of the pouch cell with the PCP interlayer, as it successfully powered an array of LED lights. These results highlight the PCP interlayer's ability to enhance Li–S battery performance under practical operating conditions, reinforcing its potential to enable high‐energy‐density Li–S batteries for commercial applications.

Conclusions

We developed an ultrathin (90 nm) and lightweight (0.05 mg cm−2) PCP functional interlayer for practical Li–S batteries via a solution shearing technique combined with Marangoni‐flow‐assisted film transfer. This approach enables the fabrication of large‐area films (9.5 cm × 7.5 cm) with uniform thickness and stable adhesion to PP separators. Our findings demonstrate that the PCP interlayer effectively integrates key properties essential for mitigating the shuttle effect and enhancing reaction kinetics, both of which are critical for achieving high‐performance, practical Li–S batteries. Experimental characterization and computational analyses confirm that the PCP interlayer offers the following properties: (i) effective shuttle effect inhibition via electrostatic interactions and size‐selective pores; (ii) enhanced ionic conductivity facilitated by high‐density sulfonic acid groups in PFSA, which promote efficient Li+ transport; and (iii) improved electrical conductivity through the incorporation of MWCNTs and PEDOT:TCB (via counter‐ion exchange), leading to improved sulfur utilization. With these properties, the pouch‐type Li–S battery assembled with the PCP interlayer exhibited excellent performance even under stringent practical conditions (E/S ratio = 5 µL mg−1 and N/P ratio = 1.3), achieving a high initial discharge capacity of 1086 mAh g−1 over 68 cycles. These results demonstrate that, beyond material compositions, innovative fabrication methods also play a decisive role in enabling functional interlayers to operate effectively under practical battery conditions. Overall, this work provides practical insight into the role of interlayer engineering in advancing high‐energy Li–S batteries.

Experimental Section/Methods

Chemicals

The multiwalled carbon nanotube (MWCNT, MR‐99) was purchased from Carbon Nanomaterial Technology Co. The poly(3,4‐ethylene dioxythiophene) polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) solution (Clevios PH1000) was purchased from Heraeus (Germany). Carbon black (Super P) was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. LA132 binder and 250 µm lithium metal were purchased from MTIKorea. 50 µm lithium metal was purchased from Wellcos Corporation (South Korea). Aquivion D72‐25BS, 1‐ethyl‐3‐methylimidazolium tetracyanoborate (EMIM TCB), N‐methyl‐2 ‐pyrrolidone (NMP), poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), 1,3‐dioxolane (DOL), 1,2‐dimethoxyethane (DME), lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI), lithium nitrate (LiNO3), and lithium sulfide (Li2S) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. EMIM TCB was purified before use by using a centrifuge, while other materials were used without further purification.

Preparation of the PCP Interlayer

The overall fabrication process of PFSA/CNT/PEDOT:TCB (PCP) interlayer can be categorized into three steps: solution shearing, ionic liquid (IL) treatment, and Marangoni‐flow‐assisted film transfer. First, the ink to be utilized in the solution shearing process was prepared. PEDOT:PSS solution and 0.5 wt.% MWCNT were homogenously mixed using tip sonication for 2 h. Aquivion D72‐25BS, which is a type of PFSA with an equivalent weight of 720 g mol−1, and distilled water were added to the MWCNT/PEDOT:PSS mixture in a 1:7:6 volume ratio and then magnetically stirred at 500 rpm for 3 h. The resulting solution was used as an ink to form a uniform film on a glass substrate via solution shearing at 1.0 mm s−1 shearing speed and 20° blade angle under 80°C. The fabricated film on the glass substrate was then heated on a hot plate at 120°C for 10 min to evaporate any remaining solvent. In preparation for IL treatment, EMIM TCB IL solution was prepared by mixing 30 mg of EMIM TCB with 1 mL of isopropyl alcohol. The film was treated with the IL solution to induce counter‐ion exchange between PEDOT:PSS and EMIM TCB via solution shearing at 2.0 mm s−1 shearing speed and 20° blade angle under 50°C. To remove any unwanted residues, the IL‐treated film was rinsed with ethyl alcohol and then air‐dried. As the final step of the fabrication process, the film on the glass substrate is transferred to the commercial polypropylene (PP) separator via Marangoni‐flow‐assisted film transfer.

Preparation of Sulfur Cathode

A sulfur/carbon composite was prepared at a 7:3 weight ratio via melt diffusion for 2 h at 155°C. MWCNTs were used for all the cathodes in the rate capability and galvanostatic cycling test, while SP was used for the cathode in CV measurements. For the MWCNT cathodes, the sulfur/MWCNTs were mixed with SP and LA132 binder at a 7:2:1 weight ratio and dispersed in distilled water. For the SP cathodes, the sulfur/SP carbon composite was mixed with a PVDF binder at a 9:1 weight ratio and dispersed in NMP solvent. The prepared cathode slurry was coated onto Al foil using a bar coating method and dried in an oven at 60°C for 8 h. Finally, a 12 mm sulfur cathode, suitable for the CR 2032 coin cell experimentation, was punched out. For pouch cell assembly, 3 × 5 cm2 S/MWCNT/LA132 cathodes (63:27:10 wt.%) with sulfur loading of 4.7 mg cm−2 were fabricated using the same methods as the MWCNT cathode.

H‐Cell Test Experiment and UV–Vis Spectroscopy

The Li2S6 solution used for the H‐cell test was prepared by mixing lithium sulfide (Li2S) and sulfur in a 1:5 molar ratio in a solvent mixture consisting of 1,3‐dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2‐dimethoxyethane (DME) in a 1:1 volume ratio, followed by stirring overnight at 60°C inside an argon‐filled glove box. The 5 mM Li2S6 solution and a 1:1 DOL/DME solution mixture were prepared. One side of the H‐cell was filled with the Li2S6 solution, the interlayer film was inserted between the cells with the PCP side facing the Li2S6 solution, and another side of the H‐cell was filled with the DOL/DME solution mixture. The color changes of the DOL/DME side were observed over 24 h and recorded in real time to assess the ability of the PCP interlayer to inhibit polysulfide shuttling. After 24 h, samples were taken and diluted 1/16 times for measurement using UV–Vis spectroscopy (PerkinElmer Lambda 1050) to quantify the relative amount of LiPSs that diffused through the interlayer.

Characterizations

The morphology of the PCP interlayer was characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a Hitachi SU8230 model and atomic force microscopy (AFM) using an AFM WORKSHOP PS‐2010. To further analyze the elemental distribution of the PCP interlayer, energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping was performed on the Hitachi SU8230 (with EDS analysis). High‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) imaging was performed using an FEI Titan double Cs‐corrected TEM. The counter‐ion exchange between PEDOT:PSS and EMIM TCB was verified through X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), using a Kratos Axis‐Supra model. The electrical conductivity of the films before and after IL treatment was measured using the four‐point probe technique with a Keithley 2400 and a four‐point probe head from MS TECH. Contact angle measurements were conducted with a contact angle analyzer (SEO Phoenix). The amount of sulfur in the sulfur/carbon composite was quantified using high‐resolution thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) on a Netzsch TG209 F1 Libra.

Cell Assembly and Electrochemical Measurements

A CR2032 coin‐type cell consisting of a lithium metal anode, sulfur cathode, electrolyte, and polypropylene separator (with and without an interlayer) was assembled inside an argon‐filled glove box. An electrolyte consisting of 1.0 M LiTFSI and 2.0 wt.% LiNO3 in DOL/DME (1:1, v/v) was used, and Celgard 2400 was used as the separator. The coin cells with low‐loading sulfur cathodes were assembled with an electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio of 20, while those with lean electrolyte conditions and low N/P ratios were assembled with E/S ratios of 5 and 10, respectively. For the pouch‐type cell, 40 µm‐thick lithium metal anode was used, and the E/S ratio was set to be 5. The assembled battery was then cycled using the WonATech battery cycler. Using the same type of battery cycler, the shuttle current measurement test was conducted following the conditions of precycling at 0.1 C for 3 cycles, discharging to 2.38 V, and stabilizing the current at 2.38 V for 15 000 s. A WonATech ZIVE MP2 instrument was used to perform electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). EIS analysis was performed to measure charge‐transfer resistance (Rct) by applying a 10 mV sine wave in the frequency range of 100 kHz to 100 mHz to full battery cells. To measure the Li+ conductivity, EIS analysis was conducted on stainless‐steel (SS) symmetric cells. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were performed in a voltage range of 1.7–2.8 V (vs. Li/Li+) at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. To obtain the plot of peak current vs. the square root of scan rate, CV measurements were conducted at various scan rates (0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0 mV s−1). From the resulting CV plots, the Li+ diffusion coefficient can be obtained from the Randles–Sevcik Equation [36],

where Ip is the peak current, n is the number of electrons per reaction species, a is the active electrode area, D is the Li+ diffusion coefficient, ΔCo is the Li+ concentration change, and v is the scan rate (V s−1). According to this equation, the Li+ diffusion coefficient is proportional to the slope of the plot between the peak current and the square root of the scan rate. Therefore, the Li+ diffusion coefficient can be estimated from these plots.

Reactivation Test Procedure

Li–S battery cells with either a PP separator or the PCP interlayer were first cycled for 30 cycles. After cycling, the cells were disassembled, and the separators or interlayers were collected. Without any washing procedure, each separator/interlayer was reassembled into a new cell containing fresh lithium metal (reference electrode), stainless steel (counter electrode), and a fresh electrolyte. The cells were then cycled to evaluate the recoverable capacity originating from sulfur species remaining on the separator or interlayer. The reactivation ratio of dead sulfur was determined by comparing the recovered capacity values of the cells containing the PP separator and the PCP interlayer.

Computational Methods

The PCP interlayer was composed of PEDOTs, PSSs, PFSAs, and CNTs, mixed according to their experimental mass ratios (Table S6). The parameters for the interlayer components were derived using Dreiding [37] and Gaestier charges [38]. The system was equilibrated using an NPT ensemble at 600 K and 1 atm for up to 0.5 ns, followed by cooling from 600 to 300 K over 0.2 ns. It was then maintained at 300 K for an additional 0.5 ns under NVT to ensure a stable configuration and density. This sequential equilibration was designed to achieve thermal and mechanical equilibrium of the PCP interlayer before further analysis. The equilibrated interlayer was then combined with a solvent mixture consisting of DOL, DME, TFSI−, Li+, and polysulfides (S2−, S4−, S6−). The parameters for DOL and DME were sourced from the GAFF forcefield [39], and RESP charges were applied [40]. Parameters for Li+, TFSI−, and polysulfides were meticulously derived from DFT‐based calculations modeled to each molecule from previous studies [41]. Adjustments were also made to ion charges to account for the polarizability of the system [42, 43, 44, 45]. The dynamics of Li+ and polysulfide migration through the PCP interlayer were then monitored at 300 K in an NVT ensemble for 5 ns, offering detailed insights into the mobility within the system. The temperature control was maintained using the Nose–Hoover thermostat and barostat throughout the simulation. The entire molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was conducted in LAMMPS with a timestep of 1 fs. Nonbonded interactions were calculated using the 12–6 Lennard–Jones potential, and cross‐interactions were handled according to Lorentz–Berthelot mixing rules with a cutoff distance of 12.5 Å. Coulombic interactions were evaluated using Ewald summation. The pore size distribution of the separator was calculated using Zeo++ software. To ensure the robustness and repeatability of our results, all systems were simulated three times, taking into account the amorphous nature of the system.
