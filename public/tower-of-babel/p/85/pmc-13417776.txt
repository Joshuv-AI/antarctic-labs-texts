A Collaborative, Interinstitutional Program to Improve the Care of Patients With Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension
Authors: Bryan A Kelly, Vikas Aggarwal, Prachi P Agarwal, Samuel Allen, Rana Awdish, Wael Berjaoui, Domingo J Franco-Palacios, Reda E Girgis, Gillian Grafton, Jonathan W Haft, Brandon Hooks, Reem Ismail, Sheila Krishnan, Scott Visovatti, Vallerie V McLaughlin, Victor M Moles
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), a subcategory of pulmonary hypertension and chronic sequela of acute pulmonary embolism, is often underdiagnosed due to nonspecific symptoms. Pulmonary endarterectomy remains the optimal, potentially curative therapy; however, determination of operability is based on multiple factors that may be relatively unique to each patient and largely based on physician expertise. Patients with CTEPH should be referred to CTEPH centers for comprehensive confirmatory diagnostics and operability assessments by multidisciplinary teams. Because CTEPH center experience and expertise are key to improving clinical outcomes, challenges arise when they are not available or easily accessible to patients. This article describes the Michigan Multi-Center CTEPH Collaboration, a multidisciplinary, interinstitutional collaboration program developed by health care centers in Michigan. Pulmonary hypertension and pulmonary embolism teams from centers across the state share expertise and clinical opinions in a monthly virtual conference. This collaborative approach tackles the two main challenges in the treatment of CTEPH head on: accurate diagnosis and patient access to CTEPH centers. Our collaboration model can be used as a best practice across the nation to benefit patients, multidisciplinary teams, and CTEPH centers.Key Words: Balloon Pulmonary Angioplasty, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, CTEPH, multidisciplinary team model, Pulmonary Endarterectomy, pulmonary hypertension

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a subcategory of pulmonary hypertension (PH) and chronic sequela of acute pulmonary embolism (PE). The disease is caused by obstruction of the pulmonary vasculature by organized thromboembolic material, ineffective fibrinolysis at the macrovascular level (comprising proximal and distal obstructive disease compartments),1 and a microvascular response characterized by pulmonary vasculopathy, deficient angiogenesis, and inflammation.2,3 The prevalence of CTEPH is estimated to be 26 to 38 cases per 1 million adults; however, the number of patients diagnosed with CTEPH is growing, likely because of increased understanding and recognition of the disease.1 Although awareness and understanding of CTEPH are growing,4 there is often still a delay in diagnosis because of nonspecific symptoms in the early stages of disease.5 A 2014 registry study reported a median time of 14 months from onset of symptoms to diagnosis.6 To improve prognosis, early suspicion of CTEPH should be considered in all patients with unexplained PH,7 and all patients should be monitored for chronic sequelae after acute PE.8,9

Current guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of PH have been previously reported.1 In brief, patients suspected to have CTEPH should be referred to an experienced CTEPH center for a comprehensive diagnostic workup, including CT pulmonary angiography, invasive pulmonary artery angiography, and right heart catheterization (Fig 1).1,10 Accurate diagnosis or exclusion of CTEPH at this stage is imperative because it is the only form of PH that is potentially curable with pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA), the treatment of choice for patients who are surgical candidates.11

Patients with confirmed CTEPH must first undergo an operability assessment for PEA candidacy at a CTEPH center by a multidisciplinary team to ensure they receive the most appropriate care.1 This is important because operability assessments remain variable and subjective, with the experience of the surgeons and the CTEPH team appearing to be the most important predictors of outcomes. The recommended first-line treatment for CTEPH is lifelong therapeutic anticoagulation, and for patients with inoperable CTEPH, medical therapy, balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA), or a combination of both are treatment options.1,10 Dependent on the assessment outcome, some patients will benefit from a multimodal approach that can combine PEA, medical therapy, and BPA (Fig 1).1,10,12

This article will discuss the statewide collaboration of interdisciplinary care teams in Michigan, streamlining and centralizing the CTEPH multidisciplinary patient management process. In our region, this collaboration allows the timely referral of patients to specialized centers with the necessary resources and expertise to facilitate early diagnosis and the highest level of patient care.

Improving Clinical Outcomes With Expert Centers

The 2022 European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society1 guidelines for the treatment of PH and a 2024 scientific statement from the American Heart Association10 outlined the requirements for expert PH and CTEPH centers. Expert PH centers offer accurate diagnosis and improved outcomes due to the expertise and experience of managing a high volume of cases.1,6,13 Criteria for designating CTEPH centers of excellence have been proposed and suggest that patient-centered criteria may serve as the foundation for designation, resulting in meaningful clinical outcomes.14 Data from the international prospective CTEPH registry demonstrated that low-volume CTEPH centers (performing 0 to 10 PEAs per year) reported significantly higher proportions of inoperable patients than high-volume CTEPH centers (performing > 50 PEAs per year), suggesting that even after patients have gained access to a PH center, varying CTEPH expertise may influence the decision to operate.6 Data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database also suggest that low-volume centers (< 16 circulatory arrest procedures over 7 years) have worse operability outcomes, whereas patients at high-volume centers (> 100 circulatory arrest procedures over 7 years) require fewer transfusions, shorter duration of intensive care, and lower operative mortality.15

Geographic Landscape of Expert Centers in the United States

In the United States, there are currently 74 Pulmonary Hypertension Association (PHA)-accredited comprehensive care PH centers, primarily located in the Eastern half of the country.16 Many patients with suspected CTEPH are faced with challenges when PH centers are not available in their home state, they are unable to travel due to severe illness, or social determinants of health make access to care in these centers very challenging. It has also been suggested that there may be an inadequate awareness of specialty center services among providers practicing further away from referral centers.17 Lack of accessibility to early specialized care may significantly impact patients’ PH-related morbidity and mortality. A study investigating age-adjusted mortality rate for patients with PH across the United States found that many states without access to tertiary PH care centers (eg, Vermont, Wyoming, Idaho, Montana) had a higher age-adjusted mortality rate.18 In addition to this, few PH centers offer comprehensive CTEPH care and surgical capabilities, meaning that even if a patient can access a PH center, they may not specialize in CTEPH.

Statewide CTEPH Collaboration in Michigan

Because of the complexity of diagnosing and treating CTEPH, health care organizations in Michigan have developed a multidisciplinary, interinstitutional collaboration program to allow PH and PE teams from centers across the state to share expertise and clinical opinions. Operationally, this program can be likened to tumor boards in cancer care, but on a multi-institutional level. Similar to tumor board meetings, the Michigan Multi-Center CTEPH Collaboration is a multidisciplinary meeting that discusses cases on a monthly basis with the goal of supporting clinical decision-making and patient care coordination, ultimately resulting in improved patient outcomes.19 Given the limited number of disease specialists within the multiple disciplines of PH care, multi-institutional involvement is helpful to share experience and perspective on individualized treatment approaches.

The state of Michigan has three PHA-accredited comprehensive care centers, which serve as regional referral centers: University of Michigan, Henry Ford Health, and Corewell Health West. Both Henry Ford Health and University of Michigan are high-volume expert PH centers providing the PH population with diagnosis, medical treatment, and comanagement of CTEPH with PEA and BPA; heart and lung transplantation; a pulmonary embolism response team (PERT); and post-PE programs. These centers serve South, East, West, and Northeast Michigan. Corewell Health West is a PHA-accredited referral center that provides advanced PH care, a large PERT activation program, a post-PE clinic, and heart and lung transplantation. The state also has regional health care facilities that serve as important referral sources to the PHA-accredited centers for diagnosis and operability/treatment assessments. Corewell Health East and Ascension Health are regional health care facilities dedicated to the early diagnosis and medical treatment of patients with CTEPH, serving South and West Michigan and East and South Michigan, respectively. There are also other regional health care facilities across the state serving as important referral sources.

Our interinstitutional collaborative group attends a monthly statewide virtual multidisciplinary conference for assessing operability and developing individualized plans for patients with CTEPH, as outlined in the core requirements of an expert PH center.1 Referring physicians are invited to join the conference, where their patients can be presented, facilitating a discussion of each patient’s unique characteristics. PH specialists from different medical fields are also involved in the conferences, including those from cardiothoracic surgery, pulmonology, cardiology, interventional cardiology, cardiothoracic radiology, and interventional radiology, as well as registered Nurse Care Navigators (liaisons that advocates for and facilitate the best experience for patients). Partnering centers in Michigan are invited to attend these monthly conferences, enabling challenging cases to be presented and discussed to receive a real-time, multidisciplinary opinion and consensus recommendation. This ultimately allows patients to stay with their referring teams throughout their diagnosis and treatment while still receiving expert regional care and close collaboration with the CTEPH center in postprocedural surveillance care (Fig 2).

Any patient suspected of having CTEPH through relevant testing can be referred for discussion at the conference. There are purposefully no inclusion/exclusion criteria that would prompt evaluation; the aim is for physicians to be confident that their patients are receiving the most appropriate care. The conference is held via password-protected video call using end-to-end encryption to ensure secure and private communications. To further conform with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, patients must sign a medical record release form. A brief description of the clinical history and comorbidities are presented, along with a review of the CT images by an experienced thoracic radiologist. Pulmonary angiograms and ventilation perfusion and hemodynamic data are reviewed by an interventional cardiologist/interventional radiologist with BPA experience and a cardiac surgeon with PEA experience. Pulmonologists are included in discussions to determine the relative contribution of CTEPH to patient symptoms and to assess the potential benefit of the procedure. A discussion is then held about surgical, percutaneous, and medical management, and a treatment plan is determined. Multimodality treatment is discussed in specific cases. Notes from the discussion are documented in the patient’s electronic medical record, and the referring provider communicates the next steps with the patient. Case Examples

Case 1

An illustrative case example, subsequently presented, shows the impact of this program on patient care.

A female in her mid-70s with CTEPH underwent PEA in the early 2000s and was subsequently treated for residual PH with dual oral medical therapy (riociguat and macitentan). The patient presented back to her physician at Corewell Health West with worsening dyspnea, and an echocardiogram showed progression of right ventricular dilation and dysfunction. The physician submitted the case for discussion at the monthly multidisciplinary conference (Michigan Multi-Center CTEPH Collaboration). Medical comorbidities, including persistent atrial fibrillation, were discussed, and determined unlikely to be the main driver of the patient’s symptoms. An echocardiogram showed a moderately dilated and dysfunctional right ventricle, and a right heart catheterization showed precapillary PH with mildly reduced cardiac index. A pulmonary angiogram showed distal thromboembolic disease, and a CT scan of the chest ruled out significant parenchymal lung pathology. The patient was diagnosed with residual PH after PEA with distal, nonsurgically accessible disease. Because this patient was referred to the Michigan Multi-Center CTEPH Collaboration, after careful assessment for risk of complications, a treatment plan was put in place for the patient to undergo BPA at the University of Michigan. The patient underwent six BPA sessions in 2022 and was monitored clinically at the University of Michigan and Corewell Health West during these sessions. The patient improved clinically, with World Health Organization functional class improving from II to I, and pulmonary vascular resistance and cardiac index normalized. The patient was referred back to Corewell Health West for continued long-term follow-up and continues to do well. The total time from referral to completion of treatment was approximately 6 months.

Before the Michigan Multi-Center CTEPH Collaboration was in place, this patient’s journey would have been longer and more resource intensive, as is frequently the case for patients who live far away from CTEPH centers. A patient would have needed to be seen at the University of Michigan, then diagnostic testing would be ordered and performed, and eventually a treatment plan carried out. As a result of the collaboration program, referring colleagues were able to seek a second opinion, leading to timely initiation of appropriate treatment. In addition, patient anxiety and hardship were minimized due to expert care and lack of travel.

Case 2

An illustrative case example, subsequently presented, shows how cases can be streamlined when the collaborative program is used.

A female in her late 30s with a history of pulmonary embolism and possible interstitial lung disease presented to the University of Michigan after an 18-month workup for occluded right pulmonary artery by CT angiogram. Her care occurred out of state, and her caring physician contacted the CTEPH center via email. When the case was reviewed, it was thought the patient had predominately unilateral chronic thromboembolic disease with parenchymal lung changes suggestive of chronic infraction. The patient had a full diagnostic workup for pulmonary vascular disease which showed predominately right-sided CTEPH with proximal occlusion of the right pulmonary artery. Because of the unilateral nature of the disease, malignancy was ruled out. The patient underwent PEA after around 24 months of workups, with successful removal of chronic thromboembolic disease and improvement of hemodynamics postoperatively (mean pulmonary artery pressure 50 to 37 mm Hg and pulmonary vascular resistance 7.7 to 4.1 Wood units). This case illustrates a patient who had a long workup for CTEPH leading to a successful treatment option. Her case could have been streamlined if a collaborative, multidisciplinary, interinstitutional program had been available in the state this patient resided.

Benefits of the Statewide CTEPH Collaboration Program

This statewide, multidisciplinary, interinstitutional CTEPH collaborative program has benefits for both the patients and PH centers. Patients receive the highest quality of care from CTEPH experts while continuing to receive local care until intervention is needed. Virtual clinic evaluation can also be provided for patients geographically remote from University of Michigan and Henry Ford Health to initiate the consultation and evaluation process. The streamlined process allows for early access to expert centers for timely diagnosis and treatment, which is thought to be associated with improved outcomes. Smaller PH centers receive ongoing CTEPH education through the monthly conferences and reassurance regarding diagnosis and treatment decisions. This is important to note because the physician community continually strives to improve access and expand specialty services for the treatment of CTEPH. Additionally, the monthly conferences promote the establishment of intercenter relationships, enabling patient cases to be discussed between conference calls and allowing easy access to expertise. For CTEPH expert centers, this approach allows maintenance of high-volume status and continued success in treating patients with CTEPH.

As a result of the monthly conferences, 35 unique patients underwent comprehensive, multidisciplinary evaluation for CTEPH in 2022 in Michigan. Of these 35 patients, 12 (34%) were recommended medical therapy, 13 (37%) were recommended PEA, and 10 (29%) were recommended BPA. Those who required an intervention other than medical therapy waited on average 103 days for their procedure. It is important to highlight that many restrictions were still present due to the COVID-19 pandemic that might have impacted the time for testing or treatment.

Lessons Learned and Reproducibility

The main driver of the Michigan Multi-Center CTEPH Collaboration program is the desire of physicians and PH specialists to provide the highest level of patient care. Although the benefits of the program have been extensive, including increased access to world-class care and continued education, there are some limitations that may need to be overcome to promote expansion to other states. Logistically, it has proven challenging to coordinate a time each month when all team members are available to join the conference. This often leads to program-related work being completed outside of usual working hours. However, this virtual model was beneficial to the continued growth of the program throughout the COVID-19 pandemic.

Another potential logistical issue is that coordination of care is not reimbursable in terms of relative value units (the measure of value used in the US Medicare reimbursement formula), including preparation of patient referral packets, attending conferences, and reviewing complex patient cases. Additionally, the sharing of patient information between institutions may lead to patient confidentiality issues. However, to maintain patient confidentiality, patients must consent to releasing their medical records, and cases are deidentified prior to discussion.

When using this program as a best practice to expand to other states, it is also important to consider that the levels of PH and CTEPH expertise in Michigan are high, particularly in Southeast Michigan. Other states that lack regional PH centers may encounter problems in initially building a team of PH experts with whom to collaborate. Additionally, some states may have more residents living in rural counties, or more residents with a higher burden of unfavorable social determinants of health, which may affect their ability to be seen even at regional centers if they are available. However, because the program allows for as much remote consultation and planning as possible, once a team is built, it can foster more integration and better patient care overall.

Despite these challenges, the Michigan Multi-Center CTEPH Collaboration program is proof that multidisciplinary and multi-institutional collaboration can be achieved. Although coordinating a specific time to meet may prove challenging, team members eventually come to a consensus, especially after realizing the patient impact and educational benefits of this endeavor. Telehealth has become a critical ally in our project because it has allowed referral centers to establish relationships with patients and for the coordination of testing and procedures before having to drive long distances, in addition to facilitating and building meaningful patient-provider relationships.

Conclusions

Two of the main challenges in the treatment of CTEPH are accurate diagnosis and patient access to expert centers to enable treatment to start in a timely manner. This statewide collaborative approach can be used as a best practice model to benefit patients and multidisciplinary teams, to support CTEPH centers in succeeding to facilitate the early diagnosis of patients, and to enhance the management of patients with CTEPH via access to an expert multidisciplinary team regardless of their location.
