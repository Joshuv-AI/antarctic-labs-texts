Identification of the ZmATG18 subfamily genes in maize and the role of ZmATG18a in drought stress
Authors: Ziming Wang, Feiyan Luo, Cheng Meng, Jihao Si, Wenzhi Zhang, Jiahao Sun, Zhe Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutophagy represents an ancient cytoprotective program that sustains cell viability under metabolic or environmental stresses via targeted recycling of cellular components. However, the contribution of ATG18s in maize is ambiguous.ResultsThis work aims to identify and characterize the maize ZmATG18 subfamily genes at the whole-genome level and a total of eight ZmATG18 subfamily genes were identified. Gene duplication analysis showed that segmental duplication events were involved in the ZmATG18 gene evolution process. Promoter analysis suggested that ZmATG18 genes contained numerous stress and hormone-related elements. The phylogenetic relationships, conserved motifs and gene structure analysis revealed that ZmATG18 subfamily members were highly comparable to their counterparts in Arabidopsis, rice and tomato. The expression profiles of ZmATG18 gene family during development revealed that ZmATG18 genes exhibited distinct tissue-specific expression patterns at different developmental stages in maize. Furthermore, the role of ZmATG18a was analyzed under drought stress. Subcellular localization showed that ZmATG18a was present in both the cytoplasm and nucleus. ZmATG18a plays a positive role in drought stress since the stop-of-gain EMS mutant of ZmATG18a was more sensitive to drought stress whereas the Mutator mutant which showed higher ATG18a expression was less sensitive to drought stress.ConclusionsIn this study, we identified the ZmATG18 subfamily members in B73 inbred line for their physicochemical properties, gene structures, conserved motifs, phylogenetic analysis, expression profile analysis. Finally, ZmATG18a was demonstrated to play a positive role in drought stress. This study provides insights into the diversity of ZmATG18 subfamily genes and their potential role in drought stress. More importantly, ZmATG18a was identified to be potential target for genetic engineering to improve drought stress tolerance of maize. Introduction

Plants usually exploit two degradation pathways for unwanted or aberrant intracellular components during growth, development or under various stresses. One is the ubiquitin-proteasomal system, which mediates the targeted degradation of short-lived proteins after performing their normal function generally [1], another is autophagy. Autophagy is a vital, evolutionarily conserved process for cytoplasmic constituents such as proteins and organelle materials for degradation within vacuoles in eukaryotes from yeast to animal during evolution [2]. During autophagy, unwanted cellular components are encapsulated by a newly formed double-layer isolation membrane. Then a double-membrane structure called an autophagosome is generated, which subsequently merges with lysosomes (in animals) or vacuoles (in yeast and plants) to deliver its cargo for degradation and nutrient recycling [3–6]. First studied in the budding yeast, Saccharomuces cerevisiae, subsequently in plants and animals, over 40 autophagy-related (ATG) proteins have been identified [4]. ATG-encoded proteins orchestrate key stages of autophagy, including its induction, autophagosome biogenesis (from nucleation to maturation), and the fusion of autophagosomes with vacuoles. These proteins mainly assemble into four complexes: (1) the ATG1 kinase complex is activated in response to upstream nutrient availability signals within cellular metabolic pathways; (2) the ATG9 cycling system is a transmembrane complex including ATG2/9/18, it likely supplies lipid membranes to the phagophore; (3) the PtdIns3K complex is composed of a class III PtdIns3K, Vps34; a serine/threonine kinase, Vps15; Vps30/Atg6; and Atg14, it plays a role in vesicle nucleation, and (4) the ATG8/ATG12 complex drives the capture of cellular material, promotes autophagosome growth and closure, and enables its fusion with the vacuole [3, 5].

Numerous studies indicate that autophagy plays a vital role in plant abiotic stresses such as drought. Under normal conditions, a low basal level of autophagy occurs whereas it is induced in response to drought stress. In Arabidopsis, atg5, atg7 mutants are less tolerant to drought [7]. In agricultural crops like tomato and wheat, drought triggers autophagy activation, accompanied by elevated expression of autophagy-related (ATG) genes and increased autophagosome formation. For example, suppression of autophagy components (ATG8d and ATG18h in tomato and ATG6 in wheat) significantly enhances drought sensitivity in these crops [8–10]. In addition, some proteins have been reported to regulate the autophagic process responding to drought stress. In tomato, the heat shock factor HsfA1a acts as a key modulator of drought tolerance by transcriptionally activating ATG10 and ATG18f expression, thereby initiating autophagic processes [9]. Similarly, Medicago truncatula employs a protein degradation strategy through MtCAS31-ATG8 interaction, which facilitates autophagic removal of the drought sensitivity factor MtPIP2;7, consequently improving drought resilience [11]. AtCOST1 is a significant regulator controlling the balance between growth and drought via the direct regulation of autophagy [12].

Although there is an increasing body of evidence about the function of autophagy in other plant species, there is limited research about its function in maize, especially in drought stress. Only recently, Xiang reported that ZmNBR1, an autophagy receptor, can facilitate the autophagic degradation of ZmBRI1a and improve drought tolerance in maize [13]. In this research, we focus on maize ATG18 subfamily. As stated above, ATG18 belongs to the ATG9 cycling system, which supplies lipid membranes to the phagophore. In Arabidopsis, there are eight ATG18 genes in total. Due to its high expression level, ATG18a is the most studied. AtATG18a is involved in resistance against biotrophic and necrotrophic pathogens as well as abiotic stresses such as nutrient starvation, oxidative, drought, salt and ER stress [14–20]. Similarly, MdATG18a plays a positive role not only in resistance to Diplocarpon mali infection but also in abiotic stresses such as nitrogen deficiency, drought, heat, alkaline stress as well [21–23]. However, the function of ATG18a in maize is rare. In this study, we identified the ZmATG18 subfamily in maize and utilized ATG18a mutants to explore its function in drought stress. The results showed that ZmATG18a conferred drought tolerance in maize, which may be a potential target for the molecular breeding of drought tolerant maize.

Results

Identification of ZmATG18 subfamily members in maize

Following comprehensive screening and systematic validation of the maize (Zea mays B73 V4) genome, we identified ten putative ZmATG18 subfamily members designated ZmATG18a through ZmATG18j. Notably, ZmATG18i and ZmATG18j were not included in the subsequent analysis based on two criteria: 1) undetectable expression levels across all the examined tissues (Fig. S1); 2) absence of conserved WD40 domain that characterize ATG18 proteins in Arabidopsis, rice, tomato, and other maize homologs (Fig.S4). Detailed molecular characterization of the ZmATG18 subfamily is presented in Table 1, including chromosomal position, CDS, and genome length of the ZmATG18 subfamily genes. The molecular weight (MW) ranged from 35,665.32 (ZmATG18e) to 113,266.06 (ZmATG18h), corresponding to 328–3,180 amino acid residues. Isoelectric points (pI) exhibited a bimodal distribution, with ZmATG18c showing the highest pI (8.29) and ZmATG18h the lowest (5.84). Subcellular localization predictions revealed compartment-specific distribution patterns: ZmATG18a/c in nucleus, ZmATG18b/d in cytoplasm, ZmATG18e in chloroplasts, ZmATG18f/g in mitochondria, and ZmATG18h in endoplasmic reticulum.

Genetic structures and conserved motifs analysis of the ZmATG18 subfamily genes

To investigate the evolutionary characteristics and functional diversification of ZmATG18 proteins in maize, we performed a comprehensive analysis of conserved motifs and gene structures. Using the MEME suite, ten characteristic conserved motifs were identified across all ZmATG18 family members (Table 2). The motif distribution analysis revealed distinct patterns among paralogs: ZmATG18e contained only four motifs, while ZmATG18a-d each possessed eight motifs, ZmATG18f and ZmATG18h shared five motifs, and ZmATG18g exhibited six motifs. Notably, motifs 1 and 2 were universally conserved across all ZmATG18 members, with motif 3 additionally present in all members except ZmATG18e, suggesting their fundamental role in protein function. Phylogenetic subfamily-specific motif patterns emerged through our analysis. Motif 6 was exclusively identified in ZmATG18a-d, while motifs 7 and 10 appeared unique to ZmATG18f-h, indicating that these motifs may contribute to functional specialization. Comparative analysis revealed remarkable conservation of motif organization between maize ZmATG18 members and their orthologs in Arabidopsis, rice, and tomato, indicating potential functional conservation across these species. Gene structure analysis demonstrated substantial variation in exon–intron architecture among ZmATG18 genes, with exon numbers ranging from 4 to 10 (average = 6) (Fig. 2). This structural diversity correlated with phylogenetic relationships, as evidenced by three highly conserved gene pairs: ZmATG18a/b, ZmATG18c/d, and ZmATG18g/h. These paralog pairs showed not only sequence similarity but also conserved motif composition and similar exon–intron organization, suggesting recent duplication events followed by functional conservation.

Colinearity and duplication of ZmATG18 subfamily genes

Given the paleotetraploid nature of maize, we performed duplication pattern analysis on ZmATG18 genes. Duplicated genes, known as paralogs, arise from gene duplication, a key mechanism driving gene family expansion and functional diversification. They share a common ancestral origin but evolve independently after duplication. Tandem duplications create a new gene copy within 200 kb chromosomal region, while segmental duplications correspond to large DNA segments (1–200 kb) with high sequence identity, sometimes even on different chromosomes [24, 25]. Following the established criterion for tandem/segmental duplication [26], collinearity analysis of the eight ZmATG18 members revealed three segmentally duplicated gene pairs: ZmATG18a/ZmATG18b, ZmATG18c/ZmATG18d, and ZmATG18g/ZmATG18h. Notably, these paralogous pairs reside on distinct chromosomes, consistent with segmental duplication characteristics (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, evolutionary selection pressure analysis demonstrated Ka/Ks ratios < 1 for all duplicated pairs (Table 3), suggesting strong purifying selection acting on ZmATG18 genes during maize evolution.

Ka/Ks analysis

The maize ATG18 subfamily (n = 8) exhibits identical member numbers to Arabidopsis (n = 8) but exceeds rice (n = 6). To further elucidate the evolutionary relationship in plants, we conducted comparative synteny analysis among maize, rice, and Arabidopsis using MCScanX (Fig. 3B). Strikingly divergent collinear patterns emerged: only four orthologous pairs were identified between maize and Arabidopsis, compared to nine between maize and rice. This discrepancy implies closer evolutionary kinship between maize and rice. Furthermore, several conserved one-to-one orthologous relationships were observed, including ZmATG18e/AtATG18b, ZmATG18f/AtATG18f, ZmATG18f/OsATG18e, ZmATG18e/OsATG18a, and ZmATG18h/OsATG18f. These evolutionarily conserved loci likely represent ancestral ATG18 lineages that facilitated subfamily expansion across plant speciation events.

Cis-acting regulatory element analysis

To elucidate the transcriptional regulation of ZmATG18s, we extracted the promoter sequence 2000 bp upstream of the translation start site of the ZmATG18s gene and analyzed cis-acting elements using the PlantCARE database. We found multiple stress responsive and hormone related cis-acting elements in the ZmATG18s promoter region (Fig. 4, Table 4), such as hormone related ABRE, as well as stress responsive cis-acting elements MYB, MYC, and DRE. MYB is the most abundant element and is present in all ZmATG18s gene promoter regions. These components indicate that ZmATG18s can be subjected to stress and hormone induction, playing an important role in stress response. Since numerous cis-acting elements such as ABRE and DRE are generally involved in drought stress [27–30], it is likely that ZmATG18s contribute to plant drought tolerance.

Analysis of expression profiles of maize ATG18 genes in different tissues

To elucidate the spatial and temporal expression profiles of ATG18 gene family members in maize, we conducted systematic transcriptomic profiling using a comprehensive RNA-seq dataset encompassing a broad range of tissues spanning multiple developmental stages (endosperm, seed, silk, shoot tip, tassel, pericarp, leaf, internode, root) (Fig. 5, Table 5). Quantitative analysis revealed strikingly differential expression patterns among different members: ZmATG18a, ZmATG18d and ZmATG18g demonstrated broad transcriptional activity across the most of the examined tissues. Notably, ZmATG18f and ZmATG18h showed elevated transcript levels in vegetative and reproductive organs (tassel, leaf, internode, root), whereas ZmATG18c displayed tissue-specific enrichment in tassel, pericarp and root. Intriguingly, ZmATG18b and ZmATG18e maintained basal expression levels across the majority of tissues. This pronounced functional diversification among the eight ATG18 homologs suggests their potential involvement in distinct regulatory networks governing maize growth and development.

Response of maize ATG18 genes to ABA treatment

Since most of ATG18 genes contain ABRE element, we would like to examine whether they respond to ABA treatment. To this end, we utilized RNA-Seq data generated by the maize biology and breeding group in our college, which examined the transcriptomic profiles of the elite inbred line KA105—developed within the group—at the seedling stage under ABA stress in both leaf and root tissues [31]. The data were obtained to analyze their expression under ABA treatment. As shown in Fig.S2, many of these genes showed up regulation at almost all time points after ABA treatment in the root, and reached their peak at 12 h such as ZmATG18a (1.45-fold), ZmATG18b (5.46-fold), ZmATG18c (2.32-fold), ZmATG18e (1.63-fold), ZmATG18f (2.52-fold), ZmATG18g (2.00-fold), ZmATG18h (3.34-fold), respectively.

Transcript levels of ZmATG18a in response to PEG treatment in maize

Among the genes which responded to ABA treatment, we focused particularly on ZmATG18a due to the extensive functional characterization of its homologs in other species such as Arabidopsis, tomato and apple. In order to further examine whether ATG18a responded to drought stress, plants were transferred to hydroponic systems containing either 20% PEG-6000 for drought simulation or Hoagland’s nutrient solution for control treatments following the establishment of three fully expanded leaves. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that leaf ZmATG18a expression elevated 3.25-fold 4 h and 2.63-fold 8 h after drought stress and then declined. Root ZmATG18a elevated 3.40-fold 8 h after drought stress and then declined (Fig. 6). Combined with the promoter analysis, ABA and PEG treatment, we speculate that ZmATG18a may be involved in drought stress response.

Subcellular localization of ZmATG18a

Subcellular localization of maize ATG18a was investigated by constructing a ZmATG18a-GFP fusion protein and expressed it transiently in tobacco epidermal cells. In tobacco, the green fluorescent signals were present throughout the cells and there are numerous punctuate structures in the cytoplasm (Fig.S3). To verify the autophagosomal nature of these structures, we co-expressed it with the mCherry-ATG8a which is an autophagosome marker in tobacco epidermal cell. The fluorescent signals from ZmATG18a-GFP and mCherry-ATG8a showed significant spatial overlap in cytoplasmic compartments (Fig. 7), indicating potential association of ATG18a-containing structures with autophagosomes.

ZmATG18a confers drought tolerance in maize

To establish genetic evidence for ZmATG18a function, we screened maize mutant stocks and isolated two independent mutants. The zmatg18a-1 allele was identified through screening the Maize EMS Mutant Database (MEMD), derived from ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis of B73 inbred lines. Molecular characterization revealed a C → T transition in exon 1 (EMS4-1c34b3), introducing a premature termination codon (TGA) that truncates the encoded protein. In parallel, we isolated the zmatg18a-2 allele from the UniformMu resource, a W22 inbred population containing sequence-indexed Mutator (Mu) transposon insertions. The UFMu08477 line carries a Mu element (mu1031514) inserted in the 5′-UTR of ZmATG18a (Fig. 8).

A Schematic diagram of zmatg18a-1 and zmatg18a-2 mutation sites. B Identification of zmatg18a-1 mutant. C Identification of zmatg18a-2 mutant, M is Marker, 0 is wild-type, 1 is heterozygous, and 2 is homozygous mutant. D Change in expression of ZmATG18a in zmatg18a-2

Under well-watered control conditions, no significant phenotypic differences were observed between mutant and WT. However, after 14 days of water withholding, zmatg18a-1 showed much more wilting and necrosis whereas zmatg18a-2 displayed less wilting and necrosis compared to their corresponding control, respectively. Since zmatg18a-2 mutant carries a Mu insertion in the 5′-UTR of ZmATG18a, we wondered whether it affected ZmATG18a expression. As shown in Fig. 8D, ZmATG18a expression was significantly higher in zmatg18a-2 mutant than its WT counterpart on day 3 and day 6 after water withholding. These data demonstrated that ZmATG18a played a positive role in maize drought tolerance.

To further characterize the physiological responses of the mutants, we measured electrolyte leakage (EL), relative water content (RWC), and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels under both control and drought conditions. Under control conditions, no significant differences were observed between the mutants and WT. However, under drought stress, zmatg18a-1 exhibited significantly lower RWC and higher EL and MDA levels compared to WT-1, while zmatg18a-2 showed the opposite trend (Fig. 9). These findings indicate that ZmATG18a contributes to maintaining cellular integrity and membrane stability under drought stress.

In order to examine whether ZmATG18a-mediated drought stress was related to ROS homeostasis, DAB staining was used to assess H2O2 level under drought stress. As shown in Fig. 10, a higher abundance of H2O2 was accumulated in the zmatg18a-1 after drought treatment and vice versa in zmatg18a-2 (Fig. 10). The ROS scavenging-related genes were analyzed in WT and zmatg18a-1 after drought treatment. Some genes showed down-regulation in the zmatg18a-1 mutant compared to WT under drought stress (Fig.S5). Accordingly, we measured the ROS-scavenging enzymes-POD and SOD activity after drought stress. Under normal conditions, there was no apparent difference between WT-1 and zmatg18a-1. However, under drought stress, there was significantly decreased POD and SOD level in the mutant, indicating that there is weaker ability to remove the ROS in the zmatg18a-1. In contrast, zmatg18a-2 exhibited the opposite trend. The result showed that ROS homeostasis played a vital role in ZmATG18a-mediated drought stress.

Phylogenetic analysis, gene structure and motif patterns

The phylogenetic analysis of ATG18 protein sequences was performed using an unrooted neighbor-joining (NJ) method implemented in MEGA X software, with bootstrap validation based on 1000 replicates. Genomic data including promoter regions, coding sequences (CDS), and corresponding amino acid sequences were retrieved from the Ensembl Plants database (https://ensembl.gramene.org/Zea_mays/Info/Index) [39]. For conserved motif analysis, the MEME Suite online platform (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) was employed to identify evolutionary preserved protein motifs [40]. Cis-acting regulatory elements within ZmATG18 promoters were systematically predicted using the PlantCARE web server (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [41]. Gene structure visualization and conserved domain analysis were conducted using TBtools software [42].

Chromosomal distribution and gene duplication of ZmATG18 subfamily genes

The GFF files of maize (Zea mays), rice (Oryza sativa), and Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) were retrieved from the Phytozome database and subsequently analyzed to map collinearity patterns of ZmATG18 subfamily genes in maize using TBtools software [42]. For cross-species genome comparison, we employed the One-Step MCScanX-Super Fast pipeline to systematically identify and quantify all potential homologous gene pairs among the three plant species. To investigate evolutionary selection pressures on duplicated genes, we conducted Ka/Ks ratio analysis through DnaSP6 software, specifically examining the nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitution rates in paralogous gene pairs.

Expression profile analysis of maize ATG18 subfamily genes

Gene expression profiles of ZmATG18 genes across nine maize tissues (endosperm, internode, pericarp, seed, silks, tassel, shoot tip, leaf, and root) were analyzed using standardized RNA-seq data obtained from the Stelpflug et al. study (PRJNA171684) [43]. The tissue-specific expression data, originally presented as Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (RPKM), were retrieved from MaizeGDB (http://www.maizegdb.org.) and subsequently processed through log2 transformation to normalize expression values. For comparative visualization of gene expression patterns across developmental stages and tissues, the normalized data were subjected to hierarchical clustering analysis and graphically represented as a heatmap using TBtools software [42].

Plant materials

The ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutant EMS4-1c34b3 was ordered from the Maize EMS induced Mutant Database (MEMD; http://www.elabcaas.cn/memd/) [44] and the genetic background is inbred line B73. Another mutant UFMu-08477 was requested from the Maize Genetics Cooperation Stock Center (http://maizecoop.cropsci.uiuc.edu/) and the genetic background is inbred line W22 [45]. Both mutants were selfed three times, at last homozygous zmatg18a mutant plants and homozygous ZmATG18a wild-type plants were isolated from heterozygous zmatg18a mutant plants. Maize inbred lines B73, zmatg18a mutants and their corresponding control plants were germinated and grown in the growth room with 16 h light and 8 h dark at 26℃.

Drought treatments of plants

Twenty homozygous mutants and their corresponding wild-type (WT) control seeds were cultivated in the same pots. Water was withheld when they reached the two-leaf stage, and drought stress was maintained for 14 days, after which the plants were re-watered. Survival rates were assessed five days post re-watering. Above-ground tissues were harvested at day 12 following the initiation of water withholding for subsequent analyses, including the evaluation of physiological parameters and ROS-related experiments including ROS-scavenging-related gene expression analysis and SOD, POD activity assay.

Histochemical staining of H2O2

Hydrogen peroxide accumulation in leaf tissues was visualized through 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining according to established protocols [46]. Fresh leaf specimens were subjected to vacuum infiltration with DAB staining solution (1 mg/mL, pH 3.8) for 2 h, followed by 12–16 h of incubation at 30°C under dark conditions. The stained leaves were subsequently preserved in 10% (v/v) glycerol solution and imaged using a Nikon SMZ25 stereo microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital imaging system.

Physiological parameter (RWC, EL, MDA and antioxidant enzyme activity) measurement

Relative water content (RWC) and electrolyte leakage (EL) were determined following the methodology outlined by Pant et al. [47]. Lipid peroxidation levels were quantified through malondialdehyde (MDA) measurement using a commercial assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). This colorimetric method relies on the specific reaction between MDA and thiobarbituric acid (TBA), forming a red adduct with maximum absorbance at 532 nm. The interference from non-specific absorbance at 600 nm was subtracted for accurate MDA quantification [48]. Antioxidant enzyme activities were determined using established spectrophotometric methods as described in previous studies [49].

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from plant tissues using the Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA), with RNA integrity and concentration verified by spectrophotometric analysis (NanoDrop Technologies). Then, cDNA was synthesized using the FastKing RT Kit (Tiangen, China). Quantitative PCR amplifications were conducted on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with SYBR Green-based SuperReal PreMix Plus chemistry (Tiangen, China). The housekeeping gene ZmEF-1α (GenBank accession: XM_008666358.2) served as an internal control for data normalization. All primer sequences are detailed in Table 7. Experimental design included three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates. The 2−ΔΔCt method was applied for the relative gene expression caculation.

Discussion

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved intracellular degradation pathway in eukaryotes, crucial for cellular survival and remodeling during developmental processes and environmental stress adaptation. While extensively characterized in plant model systems, the functional elucidation of autophagy-related components in maize (Zea mays L.) remains comparatively understudied. In this study, we conducted systematic bioinformatics analysis of ZmATG18 subfamily members, including comprehensive investigations of their phylogenetic relationships, structural features, tissue-specific expression profiles, and promoter cis-elements. Sequence analyses revealed that ZmATG18 proteins contain conserved WD40-repeat domains, known to facilitate scaffold-mediated protein–protein interactions and protein complex assembly. Furthermore, we successfully cloned ZmATG18a from the maize B73 inbred line and employed two different kinds of mutants to gain more insight into its mechanistic role in drought stress responses. Our overall data clearly demonstrated that ZmATG18a played a positive role in maize drought stress.

Genome-wide analysis of the maize (Zea mays L.) genome via MaizeGDB (https://maizegdb.org/) identified 10 annotated ZmATG18 homologs. However, ZmATG18i and ZmATG18j were excluded from further investigation due to their lack of canonical WD40 domain and absence of detectable transcriptional activity under normal condition. It is probable that ZmATG18i and ZmATG18j are pseudogenes derived from retrotransposon-mediated duplication events within the ZmATG18 subfamily. Phylogenetic analysis showed that ZmATG18s had significant evolutionary conservation with their counterparts in Arabidopsis, rice and tomato. Notably, ZmATG18a, ZmATG18b, ZmATG18c, and ZmATG18d formed an orthologous group with AtATG18a, exhibiting conserved motif architectures indicative of functional conservation. Collinearity analysis indicated that ZmATG18a and ZmATG18b, ZmATG18c and ZmATG18d as well as ZmATG18g and ZmATG18h had gene duplications, which is consistent with the phylogenetic analysis.

ZmATG18 subfamily genes exhibited varying levels of expression in the different tissues (endosperm, seed, silks, shoot tip, tassel, pericarp, leaf, internode and root) of maize plants (Fig. 5). Both ZmATG18b and ZmATG18e had low expression in all the examined tissues whereas ZmATG18a and ZmATG18d displayed constitutively high transcript abundance in all the tissues. ZmATG18c, ZmATG18f, ZmATG18g and ZmATG18h had relatively higher expression in the most tissues. Thus, these genes are proposed to exhibit diverse functional contributions to the growth and development of distinct organs and tissues in maize. Autophagy has been reported to play different roles in plant growth and development. For instance, autophagy has been reported to partcipate in the regulation of root development. Arabidopsis ARK2 and PUB9 coordinately promoted auxin accumulation and lateral root development through autophagy [50, 51]. In Arabidopsis, zeatin-mediated root-structure can be regulated by ATG8 [52]. Autophagy can regulate starch, protein synthesis, seed size, thus affecting seed yield and quality [53–55]. Reproductive development is similarly autophagy-dependent, as demonstrated by male sterility in rice atg7/atg9 mutants [56] and SlATG8f-mediated acceleration of fruit ripening in tomato [57]. Since autophagy plays varying roles in plant growth and development, it is meaningful to examine the role of ZmATG18 subfamily in maize growth and development.

The promoters of ZmATG18 subfamily genes harbor several cis-acting elements associated with hormone and stress. Notably, the drought-associated ABRE and DRE motifs were ubiquitously present across all ZmATG18 subfamily members, with ZmATG18a, ZmATG18b, ZmATG18g, and ZmATG18h exhibiting both elements concurrently [58, 59]. Particularly, ZmATG18a promoter contained four ABRE and one DRE element. Transcriptional profiling demonstrated ABA-inducible expression of most ZmATG18 subfamily members in maize root tissues, with the exception of ZmATG18d and ZmATG18e (Fig.S2). Thus, it is very likely that ZmATG18s may play significant roles in drought stress. Since ATG18a has been reported to play vital roles in drought stress in Arabidopsis, tomato and apple, we are more interested in the role of ZmATG18a in drought stress. It is interesting that ZmATG18a responded to PEG treatment in both leaf and root. Thus, ZmATG18a mutants were obtained to determine the biological function of ZmATG18a. zmatg18a-1 is a stop of gain mutant whereas zmatg18a-2 has a transposon inserted in the 5’-UTR and ZmATG18a showed higher expression in zmatg18a-2 under drought stress. Several indicators were used to determine the capacity for water retention and the extent of tolerance to drought stress. RWC was significantly higher in the WT than in the zmatg18a-1 mutant and vice versa in the zmatg18a-2 mutant. MDA concentrations and electrolyte leakage were significantly higher in zmatg18a-1 than its corresponding WT and vice versa in the zmatg18a-2 when exposed to drought conditions. All the physiological parameters indicated that ZmATG18a played a positive role in maize drought stress.

Drought stress induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction, which triggers oxidative damage to cellular proteins and promotes their misfolding/aggregation. Autophagy serves as a critical protein quality control mechanism by selectively degrading these damaged aggregates, thereby mitigating stress-induced proteotoxicity. In apple, overexpression of MdATG5, MdATG8i and MdATG18a has been shown to enhance autophagic flux and coordinately upregulate antioxidant defense systems, conferring improved drought tolerance [49, 60, 61]. Notably, SOD and POD are two pivotal antioxidant enzymes which can eliminate O2− and H2O2, thus reducing cellular ROS burden. Previous study has reported that autophagy participates in ROS scavenging. In order to examine whether ROS is important for ZmATG18a-mediated drought tolerance, the SOD, POD and ROS level were determined. The result showed that ZmATG18a was positively associated with the levels of SOD and POD, thus lower ROS accumulated in zmatg18a-1 and higher ROS accumulated in zmatg18a-2 compared to their WT counterparts under drought stress (Fig. 6). Thus, we speculate that the altered antioxidant enzyme activities may be an effective strategy of ZmATG18a to improve drought tolerance.

Conclusion

In this study, a total of 8 ZmATG18 genes were identified from the maize B73 genome and bioinformatics information was provided including genetic structures and conserved motifs, collinearity and duplication analysis, cis-acting regulatory element analysis, expression profile analysis. Furthermore, ZmATG18a was identified to positively regulate drought tolerance in maize and could be a potential target for maize drought breeding.
