Clinical Features and Prognosis of SEPTIN9 ‐Related Hereditary Neuralgic Amyotrophy
Authors: Julian Theuriet, Isabelle Quadrio, Frédéric Fer, Pauline Monin, Adrien Bohic, Alice Gravier‐Dumonceau, Vianney Poinsignon, Emilien Delmont, Emmanuelle Salort‐Campana, Shahram Attarian, Sylvain Nollet, Philippe Petiot, Solène Ronsin, Stéphane Peysson, Pierre Lozeron, Françoise Bouhour, Philippe Corcia, Antoine Pegat, Tanya Stojkovic

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNeuralgic amyotrophy (NA) causes acute episodes of neuropathic pain in the upper limbs, followed by weakness and atrophy. NA can be idiopathic (INA) or hereditary (HNA). The SEPTIN9 gene has been linked to HNA. This study aimed to characterize the clinical and prognostic features of patients with SEPTIN9‐related HNA.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study included all adult patients diagnosed with SEPTIN9‐related HNA in France from January 2012–June 2025. INA patients were included as controls.ResultsTwelve patients with SEPTIN9‐related HNA and 25 with INA were included. A family history of NA (75%) and dysmorphic features (50%) were reported exclusively in the SEPTIN9 group. The median age at neurological episode was significantly lower in the SEPTIN9 group (26.0 years) than in the INA group (38.5 years; p < 0.01). Multiple episodes were more frequent in the SEPTIN9 group (75%) than in the INA group (24%; p < 0.01). Distal upper‐limb nerves were more frequently affected in SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes than in INA episodes. Sensory symptoms were significantly more frequent in SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes (57%) than in INA episodes (22%; p < 0.01). A higher proportion of SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients had a modified Rankin Scale score ≥ 2 (42%) compared with INA patients (16%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.12).ConclusionsWhile not completely sensitive or specific, certain features may prompt clinicians to suspect a SEPTIN9‐related form when assessing a patient with NA: young age, dysmorphic features, a family history of NA, repeated episodes, sensory symptoms, and distal nerve involvement affecting the upper limbs.Keywords: genetic, neuralgic amyotrophy, Parsonage–Turner syndrome, SEPT9, SEPTIN9

Introduction

Neuralgic amyotrophy (NA) is a clinical syndrome characterized by acute episodes of neuropathic pain in the upper limbs, followed by motor weakness and atrophy, typically occurring within two weeks after the painful event [1, 2]. NA can be classified as idiopathic (INA), which is thought to have an autoimmune origin, or hereditary (HNA), with HNA exhibiting autosomal dominant inheritance [1, 3]. In 2005, the SEPTIN9 gene was linked to families affected by HNA [4]. SEPTIN9 is a member of a conserved family of filament‐forming GTPases and is highly expressed in Schwann cells of the peripheral nervous system [5]. Its structure includes a proline‐rich region in the N‐terminal domain, followed by a polybasic region and a GTP‐binding domain in the C‐terminal region [5, 6]. SEPTIN9 is involved in several cellular functions, including cytokinesis, cell trafficking, exocytosis, cell polarity, and motility [7].

The SEPTIN9 gene produces at least seven different mRNA transcripts encoding six different polypeptides [6]. However, only SEPTIN9_v1, v2, v3, v5, and v6 (NM_001113491, NM_001113493, NM_006640, NM_001113492, and NM_001113494) contain known HNA mutations [6]. Three missense variants have initially been reported:‐134G> C, c.262C> T (p.R88W), and c.278C> T (p.S93F; RefSeq NM_006640) [4]. The‐134G> C variant is located in the 5‐UTR region of the SEPTIN9_v3 transcript, whereas the other two are present in an exon shared by five SEPTIN9 transcripts (SEPTIN9_v1, SEPTIN9_v2, SEPTIN9_v3, SEPTIN9_v5, and SEPTIN9_v6) and in the 5‐UTR region of the SEPTIN9_v4 transcript [4, 8]. Additionally, several SEPTIN9 duplications have been associated with HNA, all of them including a 645‐bp conserved exon where the R88W and S93F variants are located [6, 9]. In a previous cohort of HNA patients, SEPTIN9 defects were identified in 30/55 patients (55%) [9].

The pathomechanisms underlying HNA remain largely unclear. However, previous studies have suggested that under hypoxic conditions, the p.(R88W) variant affects the translation level of the SEPTIN9_v4 transcript [10]. The p.(R88W) and p.(S93F) variants appear to disrupt the colocalization of SEPTIN9 with SEPT4 and SEPT11, alter the microtubule bundling and neurite outgrowth, and perturb the regulation of SEPTIN9‐containing filaments by Rho/Rhotekin signaling [5, 11].

Patients with SEPTIN9‐related HNA typically experience recurrent attacks beginning in childhood [4, 12, 13]. They may also present with dysmorphic features such as hypotelorism, blepharophimosis, thin and down‐slanting eyebrows, epicanthal folds, deep‐set eyes, microstomia, dysplastic ears, and short stature [4, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. However, the clinical features of HNA were predominantly studied before the discovery of the involvement of the SEPTIN9 gene, and therefore not specifically in SEPTIN9‐mutated patients, who account for only half of HNA patients [1, 9, 19, 20]. While earlier research on SEPTIN9‐related HNA has largely emphasized genetic analysis, there has been limited investigation into the neurological symptoms and prognosis experienced by these patients [3, 7, 21].

Here, we describe the clinical features of a group of patients with SEPTIN9‐related HNA, compare key characteristics and attack patterns with those of INA patients, and offer an assessment of their prognosis.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective, observational, multicenter study included all adult patients diagnosed with SEPTIN9‐related HNA in the two French reference genetic laboratories (Unité Fonctionnelle de Neurogénétique Moléculaire, Groupement Est, Hospices Civils de Lyon; Service de Médecine Génomique et Pharmacogénomique, Hôpital Kremlin‐Bicêtre, Assistance Publique des Hôpitaux de Paris) from January 2012 through June 2025. Twenty‐five INA patients diagnosed in the neuromuscular reference center of the Hospices Civils de Lyon over the same period were included as controls. All clinical data were collected anonymously from medical records.

Clinical, Radiological, and Electrophysiological Data

The demographic data collected included sex, age at inclusion, age at each neurologic episode, and the presence of a family history of NA. Clinical data of interest included the presence of dysmorphic features, the number of neurologic episodes, the presence of pain, motor weakness and sensory symptoms (hypoesthesia, paresthesia, dysesthesia) at each episode and at last visit, the laterality of the symptoms, the presence of a trigger for neurologic episodes, the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score and the upper limb part of the Overall Neuropathy Limitations Scale (ONLS) at last visit, and the treatments received by the patients. Data from electrodiagnostic (EDX) studies, including nerve conduction studies (NCS) and needle EMG, and from plexus MRI were collected. Peripheral nerve involvement was defined by two trained neurophysiologists (J.T. and A.P.) based on clinical and EDX findings and was classified as root, brachial plexus trunks (upper, middle, or lower), brachial plexus cords (posterior, anteromedial, or anterolateral), or peripheral nerves.

Genetic Analyses

Genomic DNA from all patients was tested using next‐generation sequencing (NGS) with a custom‐designed gene panel for hereditary peripheral neuropathy, including the SEPTIN9 gene (NM_006640). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq550 (San Diego, CA, USA). The presence of single nucleotide variation was detected using an in‐house Nextflow pipeline on NGS data and then confirmed using Sanger sequencing [21]. Copy‐number variation was first estimated using the in‐house DeCoVa pipeline applied to the NGS data, and then confirmed using a gold‐standard multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification method (SALSA MLPA Probemix P‐307‐SEPTIN9, MRC‐Holland, The Netherlands) [22].

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables are presented as median [interquartile range] and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. The median differences with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported as the effect size for continuous variables. Categorical variables are presented as n/N (%) and compared using Fisher's exact test or Pearson's Chi‐squared test, as appropriate. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI are reported as the effect size for categorical variables. When complete separation occurred (0% or 100% prevalence in one group), OR could not be estimated. When multiple comparisons were made within the same table, no correction for multiple testing was applied given the exploratory nature of the analysis. The overall distribution of nerve territories involved in neurologic episodes was compared between the SEPTIN9 group and the INA group using Fisher's exact test with Monte Carlo simulation (10,000 replicates), as many expected cell frequencies were below 5. Standardized residuals from Chi‐square analysis were calculated as an exploratory tool to identify which specific territories contributed most to the observed difference (absolute residual > 2 considered meaningful). Individual Fisher's exact tests were then performed for territories identified as major contributors. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics Statement

All patients who underwent genetic testing provided written informed consent. All procedures involving human participants in this study were carried out in accordance with the ethical standards of the Hospices Civils de Lyon (HCL ethics approval #25–5003) and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Results

Patients' Demographic and Clinical Characteristics at Inclusion

Twelve patients with SEPTIN9‐related HNA belonging to 10 families and 25 patients with INA were included in the study. Among the SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients, six (50%) carried the p.(R88W) variant and six (50%) had duplications. The duplications involved exon 2 in four patients, and exons 1 to 11 in two patients from the same family (RefSeq NM_006640). SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients were more frequently female (8/12, 67%) compared to INA patients (7/25, 28%; p = 0.04; Table 1). A family history of NA (9/12, 75%) and dysmorphic features (6/12, 50%) were reported exclusively in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients (Table 1). Dysmorphic features included hypotelorism (6/12, 50%), deep‐set eyes (2/12, 17%), and microstomia, dysmorphic ears, epicanthal folds, or short stature (1/12, 8% each). Age at inclusion did not significantly differ between groups (p = 0.30; Table 1). A higher proportion of patients experienced at least two NA episodes in the SEPTIN9 group (9/12, 75%) compared with the INA group (6/25, 24%; p < 0.01). Among the six INA patients with multiple episodes, three tested negative for SEPTIN9 mutations. In SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients, the median diagnostic delay between first symptoms and genetic diagnosis was 18.0 years [IQR 4.8–23.0]. In 7/12 of these patients, the first symptoms occurred before the initial publication (2005) [4]. A misdiagnosis was initially made in 7/12 (58%) of the SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients: INA in three cases, and Lewis‐Sumner syndrome, radiculopathy, hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsy (HNPP), and thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS), respectively, in the remaining four. The median delay between the initial diagnosis and the accurate genetic diagnosis was 4 years [IQR 1.0–12.8]. The diagnosis was revised following a recurrence in six patients and after the identification of a family history in one patient.

Clinical Characteristics of Neurologic Episodes

A total of 27 neurologic episodes were observed in the 12 SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients and a total of 32 in the 25 INA patients. The median age at neurologic episodes was significantly lower in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients (26.0 years [IQR 17.0–39.0]) compared with INA patients (38.5 years [IQR 29.5–50.0]; p < 0.01; Table 2). SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients were significantly younger at the first episode compared with INA patients (Table 2). The first episode occurred before the age of 18 in 5/12 SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients (42%), and in none of the INA patients. The distribution of neurologic episodes per patient by age is shown in Figure 1. In SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients, episodes occurred between 3 and 59 years of age, whereas in INA patients they ranged from 18 to 80 years. Triggers were reported in both groups without a significant difference (Table 2). They are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. The median delay between trigger and neurologic episode was 1 week [IQR 1.0–2.0]. Pain was the first symptom in all but one NA episode, which was in a SEPTIN9‐related HNA patient. Symptoms involved only the upper limbs in all episodes and were bilateral in 5/24 SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes (21%) and 4/32 INA episodes (13%; Table 2). Motor weakness occurred in 24/27 episodes (89%) in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients and in all INA episodes, without a statistically significant difference (p = 0.09). In SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes, the motor weakness was classified as proximal in 4/18 episodes (22%), proximo‐distal in 7/18 (39%), and distal in 7/18 (39%). By contrast, in INA episodes, the motor weakness was classified as proximal in 18/31 episodes (58%), proximo‐distal in 6/31 (19%), and distal in 7/31 (23%). Sensory symptoms were significantly more frequent in SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes (12/21, 57%) than in INA episodes (7/32, 22%; p < 0.01; Table 2).

Paraclinical Characteristics of Neurologic Episodes

Regarding NCS, sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) and compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes were significantly more frequently low in SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes (11/13, 85% and 13/13, 100%, respectively) compared with INA episodes (8/27, 30% and 15/27, 56%, respectively; p < 0.01; Table 2). All NCS abnormalities were classified as axonal, without demyelinating features. On needle EMG, abnormal spontaneous activity was less frequent in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients (6/12, 50%) than in INA patients (23/27, 85%; p = 0.04; Table 2). Signs of chronic axonal loss with reinnervation, defined by long‐duration large motor unit action potentials (MUAPs) and reduced recruitment, were observed in most patients, with no difference between groups (Table 2). Multiple territory involvement occurred more often in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients (10/19, 53%) than in INA patients (11/32, 34%), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.20; Table 2). The distribution of territories involved in NA episodes in HNA and INA patients is summarized in Table 3. The distribution of nerve territories involved differed significantly between SEPTIN9‐related and INA episodes (p = 0.003). Individual comparisons identified radial nerve (47% vs. 10%, p = 0.005) and median nerve (32% vs. 3%, p = 0.009) as the main contributors to this difference, both being significantly more frequent in SEPTIN9‐related episodes. The median delay between symptoms and the EDX study was 12.0 weeks [8.0–12.0] in SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes and 8.0 weeks [4.0–24.0] in the INA episodes.

Treatment of Neurologic Episodes

Immunomodulatory treatments were administered in 5/22 SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes (23%) and in 8/31 INA episodes (26%; p = 0.80; Table 2). In 3/5 SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes, the genetic diagnosis had not yet been made at the time of immunomodulatory treatment. In the SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients, treatments included intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIg) in two episodes and oral corticosteroids in three. IVIg were considered ineffective in both cases, involving two different patients, whereas corticosteroids were judged ineffective in an episode in one patient but effective on motor weakness and pain in two episodes in another patient. Sequelae were observed after most NA episodes in both groups (Table 2).

Prognosis of SEPTIN9 ‐Related HNA Patients

The median follow‐up duration was significantly longer in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients (21.0 years [IQR 6.5–24.5]) compared with INA patients (2.0 years [IQR 1.0–4.0]; p < 0.001; Table 4). However, the median age at last visit did not significantly differ between SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients (39.0 years [28.0–57.0]) and INA patients (46.0 years [37.0–53.0]; p = 0.40; Table 4). At the last visit, sensory symptoms were significantly more frequent in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients (8/12, 67%) than in INA patients (4/25, 16%; p < 0.01; Table 4). In contrast, at the last follow‐up, no differences were observed regarding motor weakness, pain, or scapular winging between groups (Table 4). A higher proportion of SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients had an mRS score ≥ 2 (5/12, 42%) and an upper limb ONLS score ≥ 2 (8/12, 67%) compared with INA patients (4/25, 16% and 9/25, 36%, respectively), although these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.12 and p = 0.08, respectively; Table 4). One patient in the SEPTIN9 group (8%) and five patients in the INA group (19%) were considered asymptomatic at last follow‐up. Two SEPTIN9‐related HNA families, with data available for two affected members, were included in this study. In the first family, the mother was asymptomatic at the last visit after a single episode of wrist and finger extension weakness, while her son had distal motor weakness in the right upper limb and proximal motor weakness in the left upper limb. In the second family, the mother had mild symptoms at the last visit, consisting of scapular winging, while her daughter exhibited proximal upper‐limb weakness. No significant differences were found when comparing the clinical and prognostic characteristics of SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients with the p.(R88W) variant versus those with duplications (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This study reports 12 patients with a genetic form of NA linked to SEPTIN9 mutations or duplications. The genetic diagnostic delay in these patients was considerable (median of 18 years). The delay may have resulted from symptoms starting before SEPTIN9 was identified, as well as challenges in accurate diagnosis.

Although SEPTIN9‐related HNA exhibits clinical similarities to INA—such as sudden, severe pain leading to muscle weakness—the present study identified several epidemiological and clinical differences between SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients and those with INA at the group level. These distinctions should prompt clinicians to consider a SEPTIN9‐related form of NA. As previously described, SEPTIN9‐related NA episodes may occur in young patients [2, 12, 13, 15, 17, 23]. In our study, NA episodes in childhood were observed only in patients with SEPTIN9‐related HNA, and the median age at first symptoms was 20.5 years. In contrast, the age range of NA episodes partially overlapped between SEPTIN9‐related HNA and INA, and SEPTIN9‐related episodes may also occur in adulthood, even up to the fifth decade. Moreover, the first episode may occur in adulthood. Thus, clinicians should not exclude this diagnosis in adult patients. A positive family history of NA was observed only in SEPTIN9‐related HNA and should guide clinicians toward this diagnosis, as in other genetic diseases [24]. Dysmorphic features, such as hypotelorism and deep‐set eyes, can be a key clinical finding for the genetic diagnosis and were observed only in SEPTIN9‐related forms of NA. However, they were present in only half of our SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients, which appears lower than previously reported [4, 12, 13, 14, 15]. We cannot exclude the possibility that these features were overlooked by the treating clinicians, as on the one hand they may be subtle in some patients and, on the other hand, difficult to recognize. In this context, the use of artificial intelligence could be useful in helping clinicians detect facial dysmorphisms [25, 26]. A female predominance was observed in the SEPTIN9‐related HNA group in our study. However, this was not observed in previous reports and, given the autosomal dominant inheritance, a 1:1 sex ratio would be expected [13, 15, 23, 27]. This finding may therefore be explained by the small sample size. Young age at disease onset, a family history of NA, and dysmorphic features were also associated with HNA prior to the discovery of the involvement of the SEPTIN9 gene [1, 2, 19, 28].

The characteristics of NA episodes may serve as valuable indicators for clinicians to evaluate the appropriateness of genetic testing. While recurrences can occur in INA patients, they were observed more frequently in SEPTIN9‐related HNA cases, consistent with previous reports on HNA irrespective of SEPTIN9 status [1]. Moreover, recurrence led to a corrected diagnosis in most patients who had initially been misdiagnosed. The authors therefore suggest testing for SEPTIN9 mutations in any patient with a second episode of NA. The following two findings have not been previously reported. First, sensory symptoms were more frequent in SEPTIN9‐related HNA compared with INA, whereas motor symptoms are classically predominant in INA [3]. Thus, the presence of sensory symptoms may be a key feature suggesting a SEPTIN9‐related form. Second, the pattern of nerve involvement also appeared distinct: unlike INA, in which proximal motor nerves are frequently affected, distal nerves such as the radial and median nerves were commonly involved in SEPTIN9‐related HNA episodes.

Triggers were identified in approximately half of the episodes in SEPTIN9‐related HNA, and their nature was heterogeneous, including vaccinations, surgery, sports and trauma, infections, and childbirth, as observed in INA from our study and previous ones [3]. A previous report suggested that immunomodulatory treatment might prevent episodes in patients undergoing planned surgery [29]. Further studies are needed to determine the best approach to vaccination in these patients regarding the risk of NA episodes.

The EDX study findings may suggest a SEPTIN9‐related form of HNA, although they do not constitute definitive diagnostic criteria at the individual patient level. On NCS, SNAP amplitudes were more frequently low in patients with the genetic form of the disease, correlating with the higher prevalence of sensory symptoms; CMAP amplitudes were also more frequently reduced. These differences in CMAP and SNAP amplitudes may also reflect the involvement of distal nerves, which are those typically assessed in routine studies. Abnormal spontaneous activities were less frequent in SEPTIN9‐related HNA compared with INA. The longer median delay between symptom onset and EDX in INA patients may explain this. This delay may also have contributed to the higher prevalence of reduced CMAP and SNAP amplitudes. Regarding plexus MRI, only limited data were available; abnormalities in genetic cases were similar to those in idiopathic forms, including T2 nerve hyperintensity, gadolinium enhancement, and abnormal muscle signals [3]. No hourglass sign was observed in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients, possibly due to a more distal nerve involvement compared with INA, which is often beyond the field of view of the MRI.

Residual symptoms were present in both HNA and INA patients. However, prognosis was worse in SEPTIN9‐related cases, as observed in HNA patients independently of SEPTIN9 status [1, 28], although the differences in scores were not statistically significant. This could be related to the distal predominance of nerve involvement and motor weakness, which may strongly affect daily activities, as reported in patients with CMT disease [30]. The higher prevalence of sensory symptoms in SEPTIN9‐related episodes may also contribute to the poorer prognosis, since sensory involvement has been shown to be disabling in CMT patients with only mild weakness [31]. Furthermore, the higher recurrence rate could lead to cumulative deficits in the upper limbs. As previously observed, intra‐familial variability was noted, with some relatives showing severe weakness at the last follow‐up, while others had mild disease or were asymptomatic [12, 15, 23]. Associated with this intra‐familial variability, an incomplete penetrance has also been previously reported and complicates the diagnosis [4, 9]. The follow‐up was longer in SEPTIN9‐related HNA than in INA, potentially reflecting the higher recurrence rate and the fact that genetic identification may have prompted more systematic monitoring after NA episodes.

Only a few NA episodes in SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients were treated. A long diagnostic delay for neurologic episodes, suggested by the several‐week interval between symptom onset and EDX, may partly explain this. Our data do not allow firm conclusions regarding the efficacy of immunomodulatory treatment. However, among the small number of treated episodes, corticosteroids were more often reported as effective compared with IVIg. By contrast, a previous report of a child with the p.(R88W) variant suggested that IVIg may be useful: even though a recurrence occurred, it was considered less severe [16].

Our study has several limitations. The limited sample size restricts the generalizability of the findings and may have reduced the statistical power to identify significant differences. The retrospective design may have led to missing data, particularly in the SEPTIN9 group, where long follow‐up was associated with missing early records. Additionally, SEPTIN9 genetic testing was not systematically performed in INA patients. Notably, three of the six patients with recurrent episodes had not undergone testing. Given that recurrence is a hallmark of SEPTIN9‐related HNA, we cannot exclude the possibility that some of these patients may have undiagnosed genetic forms, even in the absence of a family history of NA. This further underscores the importance of systematic SEPTIN9 genetic testing in patients with recurrent NA episodes.

In conclusion, several clinical features may prompt clinicians to consider a SEPTIN9‐related form when evaluating a patient with NA: young age, dysmorphic features, family history of NA, recurrences of NA episodes, sensory symptoms, and distal nerve involvement in the upper limbs. It should be acknowledged that these features lack sensitivity and specificity and serve primarily as indicators to inform the decision‐making process regarding genetic testing. Finally, the prognosis of SEPTIN9‐related HNA patients appears to be worse than that of INA patients.
