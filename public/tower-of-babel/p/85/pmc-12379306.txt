Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease characterized by chronic inflammation and immune-mediated damage affecting multiple organ systems [1, 2]. While most commonly presenting in adults, it can commence in childhood, with children often experiencing a more severe disease course [2]. Initial symptoms can be extremely variable given the ability of SLE to involve any organ system [3]. The most common presenting symptom is the classic facial malar rash. Other frequent presentations include arthralgias, arthritis, fatigue, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and cytopenias [2, 4, 5]. Up to half of patients have renal involvement at presentation. The microvascular inflammation and vascular damage of SLE can lead to systemic hypertension, arterial and venous thrombosis, and systemic vasculitis [6, 7]. Abdominal symptoms and gastrointestinal vasculitis are much less common presentations of SLE [2]. Given the significant variation in presenting symptoms, the diagnosis of SLE can be challenging.

Forty percent of children with SLE have antiphospholipid antibodies, although thrombotic events occur in less than half of these [2]. Antiphospholipid syndrome (APL) is an autoimmune disorder that leads to arterial and venous thrombosis [8]. It is characterized by elevated antiphospholipid antibodies and is often associated with other autoimmune conditions such as SLE. We present a case of a 12-year-old female diagnosed with a splenic infarction leading to a new diagnosis of SLE with associated APL.

Case presentation

The patient was a 12-year-old female who presented to a community hospital for recurrent and intermittent fevers, nausea, decreased oral intake, night sweats, body aches, and easy bruising. These symptoms had been occurring for approximately six weeks prior to presentation. She also reported left sided abdominal and flank pain for two months. Her initial exam showed normal vital signs and a temperature of 37.9 Celsius. She was alert and non-toxic appearing with no lymphadenopathy, normal heart and breath sounds, tenderness to the left upper abdomen, and no rashes.

Her workup revealed microcytic anemia and thrombocytopenia (Table 1). Her creatinine was normal, but she did have proteinuria with an elevated urine protein to creatinine ratio of 5.3 (Table 2). A chest radiograph and respiratory viral panel were unremarkable. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen was obtained for abdominal pain and showed a splenic infarction measuring up to 2.6 cm (Fig. 1). The splenic artery was patent, with no evidence of active extravasation. She was transferred to the nearby children’s hospital for further care and work-up. The initial differential of her various symptoms with splenic infarction included oncologic versus rheumatologic causes. While admitted, the patient underwent a lymph node and bone marrow biopsy which were negative for acute leukemia. She required two packed red blood cell transfusions for decreasing hemoglobin counts. She had two platelet transfusions due to platelet counts less than 50,000 prior to biopsy procedures. Her rheumatologic workup showed multiple abnormalities, and the patient was diagnosed with SLE and APL (Table 3). A renal biopsy revealed class three lupus nephritis and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (Fig. 2).

Her hospital course was complicated by intubation and mechanical ventilation for respiratory distress associated with a hemoglobin transfusion. There was concern that the patient had developed transfusion-related acute lung injury. She then went into acute renal failure requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. She then developed cardiogenic shock with severely diminished ventricular function, pleural effusions, and mitral regurgitation on echocardiography. She was diagnosed with myocarditis with an elevated troponin level and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showing elevated T2 values consistent with myocardial inflammation. The myocarditis was believed to be multifactorial including from renal failure, fluid overload, SLE, and vasculitis. At this time, she met criteria for polyserositis.

The patient was started on aspirin for APL and TMA. Other anticoagulation was not given as the patient developed a large perinephric hematoma after biopsy and a hemothorax after thoracentesis for her pleural effusion. Initially, the patient was treated with methylprednisolone one gram daily, then transitioned to oral prednisone 30 mg (mg) twice daily. Hydroxychloroquine 200 mg daily and mycophenolate mofetil 750 mg twice daily were given. For nephritis with TMA, the patient had three rounds of plasmapheresis. Given continued and severe symptoms, the patient was given a one-time cyclophosphamide 650 mg infusion.

She gradually recovered and was able to be transferred to a lower level of care. She was discharged on prednisone 30 mg twice daily, hydroxychloroquine 200 mg daily, and plan for monthly cyclophosphamide infusions. Due to instability in her outpatient social situation, the patient was lost to follow-up for a short time and missed a dose of cyclophosphamide. She continued her medications, but self-weaned her prednisone to 10 mg daily then off after five months. Upon follow-up six months after her hospital discharge, the patient had no significant symptoms including no rashes, joint pain or swelling, mouth sores, abdominal pain or fever. Her urine and blood tests showed no proteinuria and normal levels of hemoglobin, platelets and complements. She had only very mildly elevated double stranded DNA levels. Her rheumatologist continued her hydroxychloroquine and monthly cyclophosphamide injections. As of one year after her hospital admission, the patient was still stable on the above treatment regimen.

Discussion and conclusions

Splenic infarction is less of a distinct entity, but rather a symptom or syndrome of an underlying disorder and can be found incidentally. Common causes of splenic infarction include trauma, hypercoagulable states, infection, hematologic and oncologic issues, rheumatologic disease, and post-surgical complications [9–12]. Infections include malaria, Epstein-Barr virus, mononucleosis, tuberculosis, typhoid fever, and typhus. Hematologic issues include sickle cell disease or trait, spherocytosis and beta-thalassemia. Oncologic etiologies include polycythemia vera, chronic myeloid leukemia, acute myeloid leukemia and lymphoma. Rheumatologic diseases include SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, Wegener’s granulomatosis and antiphospholipid syndrome [13, 14]. Antiphospholipid syndrome is almost exclusively observed in younger patients [15]. Splenic infarction can occur after a gastrectomy or gastric endoscopy with cyanoacrylate injections for variceal bleeding. Older patients are more likely to have an etiology of atrial fibrillation or atherosclerosis [15]. Up to 30% of patients have no identifiable risk factors [16].

The most common symptoms of a splenic infarct are left upper quadrant abdominal pain, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and elevated white blood cell count [17]. Up to one-third of patients have no abdominal pain, and 20% of patients may be completely asymptomatic [9, 11]. CT of the abdomen is the most common and reliable method of diagnosis [16, 17]. Patients with splenic infarction are more likely to have longer hospital stays and higher mortality, possibly related to underlying etiologies and comorbidities [12].

This case serves as a reminder that if a splenic infarction is diagnosed, the provider should consider hospitalization of the patient to evaluate and treat serious etiologies. Similar to how the diagnosis of uveitis is often a manifestation of a broader issue, splenic infarction can be a signal of serious systemic disease.
