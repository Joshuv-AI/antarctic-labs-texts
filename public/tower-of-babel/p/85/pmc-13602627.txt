Beyond biomedical control: governance, social protection, and system gaps in tuberculosis control in Kazakhstan — a qualitative study of decision-makers
Authors: Nadira Aitambayeva, Altyn Aringazina, Laila Nazarova, Nazerke Narymbayeva, Shnara Svetlanova, Arzu Mamutova, Nelli Amandikova, Tatyana Popova, Gulnaz Kairatova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health challenge globally and in Kazakhstan, despite progress in reducing incidence. Increasing evidence highlights the role of social and structural determinants in shaping TB risk, disease progression, and treatment outcomes. However, limited research has explored how decision-makers conceptualize these factors and identify system-level gaps in TB control. This study aimed to examine regional decision-makers’ perspectives on TB burden, social protection, health system capacity, and governance in Kazakhstan.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 12 decision-makers from six regions of Kazakhstan, including representatives of regional health authorities and local governance structures. Participants were purposively sampled to ensure diversity in institutional roles and geographic context. Interviews were conducted between August and December 2020, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using inductive codebook thematic analysis supported by ATLAS.ti software. The study followed COREQ guidelines.ResultsSix major themes were identified. Participants consistently framed TB as a condition closely linked to structural inequality rather than solely a biomedical issue. Risk was perceived as concentrated among socially and geographically vulnerable populations, particularly in rural areas with limited access to care. A key finding was the identification of a “biomedical–social gap,” whereby strong diagnostic and treatment capacity was perceived as insufficiently connected to social protection, outreach, and governance mechanisms. Participants attributed this gap to fragmented social assistance, uneven support for transport, food, and income-related needs, workforce and infrastructure constraints, inequities in rural access, and insufficient institutionalization of NGO involvement. Sustaining political commitment and non-stigmatizing public awareness was also identified as important for long-term TB control.ConclusionsRegional decision-makers perceived TB control in Kazakhstan as constrained by fragmented linkages between biomedical services, social protection, NGO outreach, rural access support, and intersectoral governance mechanisms. Strengthening patient-centered social support and coordination across clinical, social, and civil society sectors may help reduce perceived fragmentation and support more equitable TB control.

Introduction

Adopted in 2014, the WHO End TB Strategy seeks to end the global Tuberculosis (TB) epidemic by 2035 through major reductions in disease burden and mortality, alongside protection from catastrophic costs [1]. The End TB Strategy achieved 28% decline in TB incidence from 2000 to 2023 [1]. Nevertheless, TB remains one of the most significant infectious causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide and continues to pose a substantial public health challenge. According to the 2024 Global TB Report of the World Health Organization, TB continues to affect over 10 million individuals annually, with global incidence increasing since 2021 [2]. In addition, estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study indicate that TB accounted for approximately 43.6 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost in 2023 (95% CI: 35.3–53.6 million), underscoring its persistent global impact [3].

In Kazakhstan, TB has represented a significant public health burden, shaped by social vulnerability, geographic disparities in disease distribution, and a notably high prevalence of multidrug‑resistant forms of TB [4, 5]. National electronic health data indicate that between 2014 and 2019, TB incidence in Kazakhstan declined from 227 to 15.2 per 100 000 population, while all-cause mortality increased from 8.4 to 15.2 per 100 000 over the same period [6]. However, this overall decline in incidence has occurred alongside a persistently high burden of drug resistance. Drug susceptibility testing among newly diagnosed TB cases revealed resistance to at least one anti-TB drug in 55.6% of clinical isolates [7]. Among these resistant cases, multidrug-resistant TB, characterized by resistance to rifampicin and isoniazid, was identified in 52.3%, highlighting the continued transmission and clinical complexity of drug-resistant TB in the country [7].

Although national TB indicators have improved over the past decade, important disparities remain across population groups. Recent evidence indicates that the burden of TB in Kazakhstan is predominantly borne by the working-age population, with substantial implications for economic productivity and household stability [8, 9]. Moreover, measures of disease burden, including DALYs, years of life lost (YLL), and years lived with disability (YLD), have been consistently higher among males than females, highlighting persistent gender-related inequities in TB morbidity and mortality [9].

Despite advances in TB care, important gaps persist that threaten the sustainability and equity of disease control and hinder the country’s progress toward achieving the goal of reducing TB death by 95% by 2035 [10]. These gaps are not limited to clinical service delivery. International and regional evidence indicates that TB risk and outcomes are closely linked to material deprivation, including undernutrition, poor housing conditions, income insecurity, overcrowding, and limited access to basic resources [11]. In Kazakhstan, this social-determinants perspective is particularly relevant because TB burden remains concentrated among working-age adults and is consistently higher among males, while rural and remote populations may face additional barriers related to distance, transport, and access to primary care [8, 9]. Thus, although global evidence on social determinants should not be assumed to apply uniformly to all Kazakhstan settings, it provides an important framework for interpreting why biomedical progress alone may be insufficient to eliminate persistent TB inequities.

Understanding how these social and material conditions are recognized by decision-makers is critical for informing future TB strategies. In particular, regional decision-makers are positioned to identify whether material deprivation is addressed through existing social support, outreach, and governance mechanisms, or whether gaps between biomedical care and social protection continue to undermine patient-centered TB control [12, 13]. However, qualitative evidence remains limited on how regional-level decision-makers in Kazakhstan conceptualize TB burden, identify priority risk groups, assess the adequacy of social support and health services, and evaluate the roles of non-governmental organizations and public awareness initiatives in TB control. Capturing these perspectives is essential to ensure that national TB policies are responsive to on-the-ground realities and aligned with equity-oriented approaches. While extensive research has examined patient-level barriers to TB care, significantly less is known about how decision-makers conceptualize system-level constraints and trade-offs in TB control, particularly in middle-income countries undergoing health system transition.

This qualitative perspective is needed because quantitative epidemiological indicators can describe TB burden and its associations with social disadvantage, but they do not explain how those determinants are interpreted, prioritized, or translated into policy and implementation decisions at regional level. Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore regional decision-makers’ perspectives on TB in Kazakhstan, including its perceived severity and public health relevance, population groups at highest risk, adequacy of social support for patients, organization and effectiveness of TB services, the role of non-governmental organizations, and the effectiveness of public information and awareness strategies. By examining these interrelated domains, this study seeks to inform more integrated, patient-centered, and equitable approaches to TB control in Kazakhstan.

Methods

Context and setting

As of 2020, TB services in Kazakhstan were delivered through a centrally governed, predominantly public healthcare system under the oversight of the Ministry of Health and regional health departments. Service provision was based on the regulatory framework outlined in the national sanitary rules, which mandated that TB prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up be delivered through a tiered system involving both specialized TB organizations (dispensaries, hospitals) and general primary healthcare facilities [14]. TB care followed standardized national clinical protocols, but delivery remained largely vertical and institution-based. Specialized anti-TB dispensaries were responsible for case detection, hospitalization, and management of complex cases, including multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), which was handled almost exclusively in designated TB hospitals. This centralization often limited accessibility for patients in remote or rural areas, where only basic TB services were available.

To capture diverse perspectives on TB-related challenges, this qualitative study was conducted across multiple regions of Kazakhstan with varying epidemiological profiles, healthcare infrastructure, and levels of remoteness. Study sites were purposively selected to reflect differences in TB burden, population density, and access to health and social services, thereby representing the complexity of TB control within the national context.

Study design

A qualitative study design was employed to explore how decision-makers perceive TB as a social and health system issue and to understand systemic barriers and facilitators influencing TB prevention and care. The study was grounded in an interpretive paradigm, aiming to elicit shared meanings, experiences, and interpretations held by individuals involved in TB governance, policy development, and program implementation.

Rather than focusing on patient experiences directly, the study examined TB through the perspectives of actors responsible for planning, coordinating, and overseeing TB-related services and social support mechanisms at regional and local levels.

Participants and sampling

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants with diverse institutional roles, professional backgrounds, and geographic representation. Eligible participants included senior and mid-level decision-makers involved in TB-related health policy, social policy, service coordination, or oversight, including representatives of regional health authorities, deputy governors responsible for health and social sectors, and deputies of regional maslikhats (local government branch).

The initial sampling plan aimed to include 18 respondents, with three decision-makers from each selected regional entity, to capture variation in institutional perspectives and geographic context. Potential participants were identified through regional health administration structures, local governance bodies, and professional networks related to TB control and public health governance. Eligible individuals were approached by the research team or trained interviewers through formal institutional communication and/or direct invitation. The invitation included information about the study aim, voluntary participation, confidentiality, expected interview duration, and the right to decline or withdraw without consequence.

Of the 18 planned interviews, 12 were completed. Six planned interviews were not completed because some invited respondents were unavailable, could not be scheduled within the data collection period, or did not participate after invitation. No replacement participants with equivalent institutional authority were available within the study period. The final sample therefore included 12 decision-makers from the selected six regions. All participants had a minimum of six months of professional experience related to TB governance, health system management, or social support programs. Researchers had no prior professional or personal relationships with participants. At the time of recruitment, participants were informed that the study aimed to generate evidence to improve TB control policies and intersectoral coordination in Kazakhstan. Participants were informed about the aims of the study, the researchers’ roles, and the voluntary nature of participation prior to the interviews.

Although the final sample was smaller than initially planned, the completed interviews provided representation across key decision-making levels and regional contexts. During data collection and analysis, the research team monitored whether new interviews generated substantially new codes or themes. Recruitment was discontinued after the completed interviews showed code redundancy and thematic completeness in relation to the study objectives. Therefore, the sample was considered sufficient for qualitative thematic analysis, while not intended to provide statistically generalizable national estimates.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews conducted between August 2020 and December 2020. All interviews were conducted face-to-face by trained researchers with experience in qualitative public health research. The data collection period followed the first nationwide lockdown in Kazakhstan, which was implemented in March 2020 in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, and preceded the second lockdown, which took place in July 2021 [15]. All interviews were carried out in accordance with prevailing COVID-19 safety regulations, including the use of personal protective equipment, physical distancing where feasible, and adherence to institutional infection prevention and control measures. Interviews were carried out in Russian or Kazakh, depending on participant preference, and were audio-recorded with informed consent. Written consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Scientific Center of Phthisiopulmonology of the Republic of Kazakhstan (Local Ethics Committee protocol No. 54, 21 July 2020).

Although data collection took place during the COVID-19 pandemic, the study was designed to capture enduring structural and policy-level determinants of TB control rather than pandemic-specific operational disruptions.

A semi-structured interview guide was used to ensure consistency across interviews while allowing flexibility to explore emerging topics. The semi-structured interview guide covered the following thematic domains: (1) perceived severity and relevance of TB at regional and national levels; (2) perceived population groups at highest risk of TB; (3) availability and adequacy of social support for TB patients and areas for improvement; (4) organization and effectiveness of TB services, including workforce and infrastructure; (6) role of non-governmental organizations (NGOs); and (7) effectiveness of public information and awareness strategies. Probing questions were used to clarify and deepen responses.

Only the interviewer and participant were present during interviews to ensure confidentiality and encourage open discussion. Field notes were taken immediately after each interview to capture contextual observations and preliminary analytical reflections. Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min. Data saturation was assessed iteratively during data collection, and recruitment was discontinued when no new themes emerged.

Although interviews were relatively focused in duration, the interview guide was structured around predefined domains directly aligned with the study objectives, and probing questions were used to clarify responses and explore examples of governance, social support, service organization, NGO involvement, and public awareness.

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in the original interview language, either Russian or Kazakh. Transcripts were anonymized before analysis and retained in the original language during initial coding to preserve participants’ wording, contextual meaning, and locally specific terminology. English translation was used primarily at the stage of manuscript preparation, particularly for theme descriptions and illustrative quotations.

Research team and reflexivity

The interviews were conducted by staff of the Center for the Study of Public Opinion (CIOM), with which a formal contract was concluded between CIOM and KMU “KSPH” for the purpose of conducting the interviews. The staff possessed the necessary education, professional skills, and experience in qualitative interviewing. The interviewers were trained in sociological research and public health and had practical experience in conducting interviews and working with respondents.

At the time of the study, N. Aitambayeva, A. Aringazina, and L. Nazarova served as coordinators of the interview process. The development and formulation of the interview questions were carried out by A. Aringazina, N. Aitambayeva, and L.Nazarova.

The researchers did not hold direct supervisory or administrative roles over participants, and no prior personal or professional relationships existed before recruitment. Participants were informed about the researchers’ academic roles and the purpose of the study prior to the interviews.

To minimize potential bias, reflexivity was maintained throughout the study through regular team discussions, memo-writing, and critical reflection on how researchers’ backgrounds, assumptions, and professional perspectives could influence data interpretation.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Data collection continued until thematic saturation was assessed through code redundancy and thematic completeness. Saturation was understood as thematic sufficiency in relation to the study aim rather than as statistical representativeness. During data collection and analysis, the research team assessed whether additional interviews produced new codes, new dimensions of existing themes, or substantially different interpretations of TB governance, social support, and health system capacity. After 12 interviews, the dataset showed code redundancy and thematic completeness across the main domains of the interview guide. However, the sample was still considered small because it included a limited number of elite decision-makers, did not include all regions of Kazakhstan, and did not directly capture the perspectives of patients, frontline clinicians, NGO representatives, or social protection workers. Participants were re-contacted when clarification or elaboration of specific issues was required.

To preserve meaning across languages, translation was conducted as an interpretive and quality-controlled process rather than as a purely literal conversion. Initial coding and theme development were conducted using the original Russian and Kazakh transcripts. Selected quotations and theme summaries were then translated into English by bilingual members of the research team familiar with public health and TB-related terminology. Translated excerpts were checked against the original transcripts to ensure conceptual equivalence, preservation of tone, and accurate representation of context. Where wording had no direct English equivalent or where meaning was potentially ambiguous, the research team discussed alternative translations and selected the wording that best preserved the participant’s intended meaning.

To enhance credibility, data triangulation was applied by comparing perspectives across participants from different regions and institutional roles. Relevant policy documents, national TB strategies, and regulatory materials were reviewed to contextualize and validate interview findings. Participants were offered the opportunity to review and confirm the accuracy of their interview transcripts, thereby supporting ethical rigor and participant empowerment. Transcripts were returned to participants for verification where feasible, and minor clarifications were incorporated into the final dataset.

Researcher reflexivity was maintained through memo-writing and regular team discussions that examined how researchers’ professional backgrounds, assumptions, and institutional perspectives could shape coding decisions, theme development, and interpretation.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using an inductive codebook thematic analysis approach, informed by the main stages of thematic analysis described by Braun and Clarke [16]. This approach was selected because the study involved multiple analysts, systematic coding across a defined dataset, and iterative development of a shared coding framework while retaining an interpretive orientation to participants’ accounts.

The analytical process began with familiarization, during which researchers repeatedly read the original-language transcripts and reviewed audio recordings where needed to understand participants’ accounts in context. Initial analytic reflections were documented in memos, including observations about recurrent concepts, contradictions, institutional framing, and possible links between individual statements and broader health system structures.

Initial coding was conducted inductively using ATLAS.ti software (version 26) [17]. Two researchers first coded a subset of transcripts independently to identify preliminary codes and compare interpretations. This step was not used to calculate intercoder reliability or to establish a single “correct” coding of the data. Rather, it was used to broaden analytic sensitivity, discuss alternative interpretations, and develop a shared codebook that could be applied consistently across the dataset.

The preliminary codebook was then refined iteratively as additional transcripts were analyzed. New codes were added where necessary, existing codes were modified to better reflect the data, and code definitions were clarified through team discussions. Coding remained responsive to participants’ accounts and was not constrained by a predefined framework. Codes captured both explicit content, such as rural access, social assistance, workforce shortages, and NGO involvement, and more interpretive meanings, such as perceived fragmentation, responsibility shifting, and tensions between biomedical capacity and social vulnerability.

Codes were organized into candidate themes by examining relationships and patterns across participants, institutional roles, and regional contexts. Theme development focused on moving beyond descriptive categories toward broader interpretive patterns. For example, codes related to food assistance, transport subsidies, housing instability, and treatment adherence were synthesized into a theme concerning the misalignment between biomedical TB care and social protection systems. Similarly, accounts of rural access, workforce shortages, and delayed diagnosis were interpreted as reflecting spatial and organizational inequities in service delivery.

Candidate themes were reviewed for internal coherence, conceptual distinctiveness, and grounding in the original data. Differences in interpretation among researchers were discussed to refine codes, clarify theme boundaries, and strengthen analytic coherence; these discussions were not treated as a measure of coding reliability. Final themes were defined through written analytic summaries, and illustrative quotations were selected to reflect both the empirical basis of each theme and variation in perspectives across respondents. Final English-language theme descriptions and quotations were reviewed against the original Russian and Kazakh transcripts to ensure conceptual and contextual accuracy.

The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [18]. Respondents are identified using anonymized codes (R1–R12) indicating interview sequence.

Participants occupied senior or mid-level positions and were directly involved in TB-related policy, service coordination, or oversight functions. The sample provided perspectives across regions with varying epidemiological profiles, geographic accessibility, and health system capacity. Overview of identified themes

Six interrelated themes were identified through thematic analysis. These themes represent interpretive patterns developed across decision-makers’ accounts rather than descriptive summaries of isolated interview topics. In the sections below, direct quotations are used to illustrate participants’ perceptions, while the accompanying analytic text presents the researchers’ interpretation of how these perceptions reflect broader governance, social protection, and health system challenges in TB control.

TB as a manifestation of structural inequality

Participants consistently framed TB not merely as an infectious disease, but as a manifestation of broader structural and social inequities. Participants emphasized that TB reflects intersecting vulnerabilities, including poverty, housing instability, unemployment, and comorbid conditions.

While acknowledging improvements in epidemiological indicators, decision-makers highlighted a disconnect between declining incidence and persistent systemic vulnerability. Participants described TB as remaining embedded in social disadvantage, rather than one that can be fully addressed through biomedical interventions alone.

“Although we see a clear decline in incidence over the past five years, TB still contributes substantially to mortality. This disease cannot be considered solved; it requires constant attention at both medical and social levels.” (R11).

“Although we see a clear decline in incidence over the past five years, TB still contributes substantially to mortality. This disease cannot be considered solved; it requires constant attention at both medical and social levels.” (R11).

TB was repeatedly described as a “mirror of social inequality,” reflecting broader structural disadvantages rather than failures of medical technology alone.

“TB is not just a medical issue. It is a mirror of social inequality — poverty, poor housing, and unemployment all come together here.” (R3).

“TB is not just a medical issue. It is a mirror of social inequality — poverty, poor housing, and unemployment all come together here.” (R3).

Several participants also noted that declining incidence may paradoxically reduce political attention, potentially weakening sustained investment in TB control. This issue is further reflected in Theme 6, which addresses political commitment and public awareness.

Concentration of risk within socially and geographically marginalized populations

Although participants acknowledged that TB can affect any individual, they consistently described risk as concentrated among socially and medically vulnerable populations. These included low-income groups, migrants, individuals experiencing homelessness, persons with chronic conditions, and populations in detention settings.

Importantly, respondents challenged prevailing narratives that restrict TB to “marginal” populations, instead emphasizing the dynamic nature of vulnerability, where structural barriers such as delayed care-seeking, poor living conditions, and limited access to services contribute to disease progression.

Geographic inequality was interpreted as an important dimension of vulnerability because seven out of twelve participants repeatedly linked rural residence with delayed diagnosis and limited access to care. Rural and remote populations were repeatedly identified as experiencing systematic delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation, driven by distance to facilities, limited transportation, and constrained primary care access.

“There is a common misconception that only prisoners or alcohol-dependent individuals get TB. In reality, anyone with a weakened immune system or chronic conditions may be affected.” (R11).

“There is a common misconception that only prisoners or alcohol-dependent individuals get TB. In reality, anyone with a weakened immune system or chronic conditions may be affected.” (R11).

Several respondents highlighted rural residence and geographic isolation as compounding factors, contributing to late diagnosis and more advanced disease at presentation.

“In rural areas, people often seek care very late. Health facilities are far away, transport is irregular, and symptoms are tolerated until the disease becomes severe.” (R6).

“In rural areas, people often seek care very late. Health facilities are far away, transport is irregular, and symptoms are tolerated until the disease becomes severe.” (R6).

“Villages are especially vulnerable. Patients delay visits because reaching a district center takes several hours, and many cannot leave work or family responsibilities.” (R8).

“Villages are especially vulnerable. Patients delay visits because reaching a district center takes several hours, and many cannot leave work or family responsibilities.” (R8).

“We often detect TB at an advanced stage in rural residents, not because of lack of treatment, but because of delayed diagnosis and limited access to primary healthcare.” (R9).

“We often detect TB at an advanced stage in rural residents, not because of lack of treatment, but because of delayed diagnosis and limited access to primary healthcare.” (R9).

Taken together, these accounts suggest that participants understood TB risk as shaped by the interaction between social vulnerability and spatial access to care, rather than by individual-level factors alone.

The policy–practice gap in social protection systems

We interpreted a recurrent pattern across interviews as a perceived misalignment between biomedical TB care and social protection mechanisms, hereafter conceptualized as a policy–practice gap. While participants consistently emphasized that diagnostic and treatment services are free and technologically advanced, they highlighted that social support systems remain fragmented, inconsistent, and insufficiently individualized.

Existing forms of assistance—including financial transfers, food support, and transport subsidies—were described as unevenly implemented across regions and often insufficient to address patients’ complex needs. Housing instability, lack of income, and administrative barriers were identified as critical determinants of treatment adherence.

“We have modern diagnostics and effective treatment, everything is free, but if a patient has no housing, no income, and no food, treatment adherence becomes impossible. Medicine alone cannot solve this.” (R1 ).

“We have modern diagnostics and effective treatment, everything is free, but if a patient has no housing, no income, and no food, treatment adherence becomes impossible. Medicine alone cannot solve this.” (R1 ).

Respondents repeatedly stressed that social support must be individualized and needs-based, rather than standardized or symbolic.

“Simply giving money or food is not enough. There must be assessment of needs, supervision, and continuous support, otherwise assistance does not reach its purpose.” (R10).

“Simply giving money or food is not enough. There must be assessment of needs, supervision, and continuous support, otherwise assistance does not reach its purpose.” (R10).

Participants emphasized that standardized forms of support are inadequate in the context of prolonged TB treatment, particularly for MDR-TB, and called for needs-based, continuous, and supervised social support systems.

Health system duality: strong biomedical capacity alongside structural vulnerabilities

Decision-makers described Kazakhstan’s TB system as characterized by a dual structure, combining strong biomedical capacity with persistent systemic weaknesses. On the one hand, participants highlighted significant progress in diagnostics, treatment availability, and alignment with international standards. On the other hand, they identified critical constraints related to workforce shortages, infrastructure disparities, and gaps in service accessibility.

Workforce challenges were particularly salient, with respondents reporting shortages of specialized TB professionals and limited interest among younger clinicians in entering the field.

“The main challenge is human resources. There is a shortage of phthisiatricians, and young specialists are reluctant to enter this field.” (R2).

“The main challenge is human resources. There is a shortage of phthisiatricians, and young specialists are reluctant to enter this field.” (R2).

In addition, certain populations—such as undocumented migrants—were described as excluded from formal financing mechanisms, despite having comparable healthcare needs.

“Undocumented migrants and people without identification are not included in financing models, although they require the same level of care.” (R7).

“Undocumented migrants and people without identification are not included in financing models, although they require the same level of care.” (R7).

This duality reflects a broader pattern in which biomedical advancements have outpaced health system and governance adaptations, limiting the effective translation of technical progress into equitable outcomes.

Non-governmental organizations as bridging actors in fragmented systems

Participants consistently identified non-governmental organizations (NGOs) as important intermediaries bridging gaps between healthcare services and social support systems. Participants emphasized the role of NGOs in reaching marginalized populations, supporting treatment adherence, facilitating access to documentation, and addressing stigma.

“Medical institutions cure TB, but NGOs ensure that patients do not relapse by supporting them socially after discharge.” (R5).

“Medical institutions cure TB, but NGOs ensure that patients do not relapse by supporting them socially after discharge.” (R5).

NGOs were described as operating at the intersection of healthcare, social protection, and community engagement, often compensating for limitations in formal systems. However, participants noted that NGO involvement remains insufficiently institutionalized and dependent on project-based funding.

“Without NGOs, many socially marginalized patients would never complete diagnostic procedures.” (R2).

“Without NGOs, many socially marginalized patients would never complete diagnostic procedures.” (R2).

Respondents repeatedly called for expanded NGO funding and formalized intersectoral collaboration.

Sustaining political commitment and public awareness

Participants emphasized the importance of maintaining TB as a policy priority and public health concern, particularly in the context of declining incidence and competing health system demands. Public awareness strategies were described as most effective when delivered through digital platforms, community leaders, and non-stigmatizing messaging.

“Information must be simple, non-stigmatizing, and delivered through trusted voices, including bloggers and community leaders.” (R12).

“Information must be simple, non-stigmatizing, and delivered through trusted voices, including bloggers and community leaders.” (R12).

At the governance level, respondents highlighted the need for continuous political oversight and intersectoral coordination, warning that reduced attention may undermine long-term progress.

“Without regular parliamentary oversight, TB risks losing priority compared to other diseases.” (R4).

“Without regular parliamentary oversight, TB risks losing priority compared to other diseases.” (R4).

Some participants also described how competing health system priorities during the COVID-19 pandemic contributed to reduced attention to TB detection and continuity of preventive services.

“During the COVID-19 pandemic, TB began to be overlooked. As a result, mortality increased due to delayed detection and interruption of preventive services.” (R6).

“During the COVID-19 pandemic, TB began to be overlooked. As a result, mortality increased due to delayed detection and interruption of preventive services.” (R6).

Cross-theme synthesis: the biomedical–social gap in TB control

Across the six themes, we interpreted participants’ accounts as pointing to a consistent pattern: Kazakhstan’s TB control system demonstrates strong biomedical capacity but insufficient integration with social protection and governance mechanisms. This structural misalignment—conceptualized here as the biomedical–social gap—limits the effectiveness of TB programs (Fig. 1). Participants frequently emphasized the availability of diagnostics and treatment, but also described fragmented social assistance, uneven support for vulnerable patients, rural access barriers, workforce constraints, and insufficiently institutionalized NGO involvement. This pattern suggests that technical capacity for TB diagnosis and treatment was perceived as stronger than the social protection and intersectoral governance arrangements needed to sustain adherence, reduce delays in diagnosis, and address inequities across populations and regions. This interpretation is grounded in participant accounts but should be understood as an analytic synthesis rather than a definitive system-wide evaluation.

Discussion

This study provides qualitative insight into how decision-makers conceptualize tuberculosis control within a transitioning health system, suggesting a perceived misalignment between biomedical capacity and social protection mechanisms. While Kazakhstan has achieved measurable progress in TB diagnosis, treatment, and infrastructure development, findings indicate that these advances are not fully translated into equitable health outcomes due to persistent structural and governance constraints.

The central empirical finding was the perceived mismatch between biomedical TB care and social protection mechanisms. This finding should not be interpreted as introducing a new theory, as the importance of social determinants, social protection, and patient-centered care in TB control is already well established in the international literature. Rather, the contribution of this study is to show how regional decision-makers in Kazakhstan described this mismatch in practice: free diagnostics and treatment were perceived as insufficient when patients also faced unstable housing, food insecurity, lack of income, transport barriers, or administrative exclusion. In this sense, the “biomedical–social gap” is used as a concise analytic label for a recurring pattern in the interviews, linking participant accounts to existing TB social determinants and health system integration literature.

This finding aligns with broader frameworks in public health that emphasize the role of social determinants and health system integration in infectious disease control. Building sustainable social determinants of health infrastructures in public health institutions that extend across health sectors and non-health systems may enhance progress toward reducing morbidity, mortality, and health disparities [19, 20]. Findings from international research consistently demonstrate that enhanced investment in social protection mechanisms is associated with reductions in tuberculosis burden [21]. However, the present study extends this literature by demonstrating how biomedical-social gap is recognized at the policy and implementation levels, highlighting the need for coordinated reform across sectors.

Consistent with international evidence, participants emphasized that TB outcomes are strongly influenced by social conditions, including housing stability, income security, and access to basic resources [22, 23]. However, this study highlights that existing social protection mechanisms in Kazakhstan remain fragmented and insufficiently responsive to patient needs, particularly in the context of long-term TB treatment. These findings suggest that strengthening TB control requires a shift from standardized support models toward integrated, needs-based social protection systems, embedded within TB care pathways. Evidence from other settings supports this approach; for example, a study conducted in South Korea demonstrated that the integration of a vulnerability assessment tool into TB care improved treatment outcomes by enabling more targeted and responsive support for high-risk patients [24]. Such approaches are consistent with the WHO End TB Strategy, which emphasizes patient-centered care and social support as essential components of disease control [2].

The study further identifies geographic inequality as a structural driver of delayed diagnosis and disease progression, particularly in rural and remote areas. Despite significant investments in infrastructure and healthcare modernization, decision-makers reported persistent barriers related to transportation, workforce distribution, and service accessibility. These findings underscore that infrastructure expansion alone is insufficient without parallel investments in operational capacity, workforce distribution, and referral systems, reinforcing the importance of equity-oriented health system design. Overlapping inequalities in disease susceptibility, disease progression and access to effective treatment amplify the risk of tuberculosis transmission and progression across households and communities [25].

The role of NGOs emerged as a central component of TB control, particularly in addressing gaps in social support and reaching marginalized populations. Participants described NGOs as uniquely positioned to operate across sectors and provide individualized support that is not fully captured within formal health systems. However, the reliance on NGOs also reflects underlying system fragmentation. Institutionalizing NGO involvement through sustainable financing and formal integration into national TB strategies may enhance continuity of care and improve treatment outcomes. However, to date, there are no comprehensive regulatory packages formally approving and governing the work of NGOs [26].

These findings are relevant to ongoing TB reforms in Kazakhstan, including updated Sanitary Rules for TB Prevention and Control and the National Project on the Modernization of Rural Healthcare, both of which aim to strengthen community-based care, risk-oriented prevention, and rural service capacity [14, 27–29]. However, the persistence of perceived gaps in social support, rural access, workforce capacity, and intersectoral coordination suggests that reform implementation should be assessed not only by infrastructure expansion, but also by its ability to reduce fragmentation across TB care, social protection, and community-based support.

Policy implications: toward an integrated TB control model

Taken together, the findings suggest that effective TB control in Kazakhstan requires a transition from a predominantly biomedical model to an integrated, multi-sectoral approach. Based on the perspectives of decision-makers, a three-pillar framework for strengthening TB control can be proposed:

Sustained biomedical capacity, including diagnostics, treatment, and workforce development.Integrated social protection systems, tailored to patient needs and embedded within care pathways.Strengthened governance and intersectoral coordination, including formalized roles for NGOs.

Operationalizing this framework may help close the biomedical–social gap identified in this study and improve both equity and effectiveness in TB control.

Although this qualitative study was not designed to produce statistically generalizable findings, the results may have transferability to comparable health system contexts. The perceived mismatch between biomedical TB capacity, social protection, rural access, NGO involvement, and intersectoral governance may be relevant to other Central Asian and post-Soviet health systems that share features such as historically vertical TB services, centralized public-sector governance, rural access barriers, and continuing challenges related to multidrug-resistant TB and social vulnerability. However, transferability should be assessed cautiously, taking into account differences in health financing, decentralization, civil society capacity, migration patterns, and the organization of social protection systems. Future comparative qualitative studies across Central Asia and other post-Soviet settings could help determine whether similar governance and social protection gaps are observed beyond Kazakhstan.

This study provides policy-relevant insights from decision-makers involved in TB governance, but several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size was relatively small, and the final number of completed interviews was lower than initially planned. Although thematic saturation was reached, the findings should be interpreted as an in-depth qualitative account of perceived governance and implementation challenges rather than as a statistically generalizable assessment of Kazakhstan’s TB system. Non-participation among some planned respondents may have limited the range of perspectives captured, particularly if unavailable respondents differed in institutional role, regional experience, or views on TB governance. Therefore, saturation should be interpreted as adequacy for identifying the main themes within this purposively sampled decision-maker dataset, not as evidence that all relevant national, regional, or stakeholder perspectives were captured. Second, the focus on decision-makers introduces the possibility of elite respondent bias and social desirability bias. Participants were reflecting on systems, policies, and implementation processes that they may help oversee or influence, which may have led them to emphasize formal achievements, understate implementation weaknesses, or present institutional responses in a more favorable way. To reduce this risk, interviews were conducted confidentially, participants were anonymized, and the analysis compared accounts across respondents, regions, and institutional roles. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted as decision-makers’ perceptions and the researchers’ thematic interpretation of those perceptions, rather than as a definitive evaluation of the TB system or as a substitute for patient, clinician, NGO, or social protection sector perspectives. Third, the focus on decision-makers introduces potential elite respondent and social desirability bias, as participants were reflecting on systems they may help oversee. Fourth, the study did not directly include patients, frontline clinicians, NGO representatives, or social protection actors; therefore, findings related to patient support and NGO roles reflect decision-makers’ perceptions rather than the full range of stakeholder experiences. Finally, although the study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, it was not designed to systematically assess pandemic-related disruptions, which may nevertheless have shaped participant perceptions.

Conclusion

This qualitative study found that regional decision-makers perceived TB control in Kazakhstan as having strong biomedical capacity, particularly in diagnosis and treatment, but as being constrained by insufficient integration with social protection, community-based support, and intersectoral governance mechanisms. Participants described persistent challenges related to social vulnerability, rural access, workforce capacity, fragmented social assistance, and insufficiently institutionalized NGO involvement. Strengthening patient-centered social support, improving coordination between clinical and social services, and formalizing collaboration with civil society actors may help reduce perceived fragmentation and support more equitable TB control in Kazakhstan and comparable middle-income settings.
