Clinical cardiac phenotype beyond ejection fraction: a missing dimension in prognostication of VA-ECMO outcomes in cardiogenic shock
Authors: Sajid Kadir, Stephen Tran
Published: 2026

Matters arising from Shin J, Kang E-M, Lee S-H, et al. Prognostic implication of venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation in acute myocardial infarction-related cardiogenic shock. J Intensive Care 13, 38 (2025).

Shin et al. [1] present valuable real-world data from 667 patients with cardiogenic shock (CS) treated with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO), demonstrating that acute myocardial infarction-related CS (AMI-CS) was associated with lower in-hospital mortality compared with non-AMI-CS (58.6% vs. 69.7%; OR 0.46, 95% CI 0.29–0.75). The authors identified seven independent predictors of mortality, including severe left ventricular (LV) dysfunction defined as LV ejection fraction (LVEF) < 30%. While these findings contribute meaningfully to the evolving understanding of VA-ECMO outcomes, we believe the study’s reliance on LVEF as the sole echocardiographic variable, combined with the absence of cardiac rhythm data, represents a significant limitation that warrants further consideration.

The authors themselves highlight a paradox that, in our view, underscores this concern. Patients who died within 24 h of VA-ECMO initiation had substantially higher LVEFs (46%) than those who died after 24 h (19%) or survived to discharge (23%). Shin et al. attribute this finding to the higher proportion of mixed or secondary CS in the early death group. A complementary, hypothesis-generating consideration is that LVEF alone may be insufficient to describe cardiac dysfunction in heterogeneous CS populations. In future studies, a broader clinical cardiac phenotype incorporating rhythm status, diastolic function, and right ventricular (RV) performance may improve risk stratification beyond LVEF alone.

The concept of a clinical cardiac phenotype extends beyond isolated systolic function to encompass the constellation of cardiac abnormalities that collectively determine hemodynamic reserve and prognosis [2, 3]. In CS and related VA-ECMO-supported populations, several echocardiographic and rhythm parameters may provide prognostic information not captured by LVEF alone.

First, the complete absence of rhythm data in this study is a notable omission. Atrial fibrillation (AFib) is highly prevalent in both AMI-CS and heart failure-related CS populations, with reported rates of 20–45% depending on the cohort and shock severity [4, 5]. The presence of AFib in CS independently predicts adverse outcomes, including failed VA-ECMO weaning, increased thromboembolic complications, and mortality [6, 7]. AFib compromises hemodynamic recovery during VA-ECMO through loss of atrial contribution to ventricular filling, irregular R-R intervals impairing VA-ECMO flow optimization, and heightened thrombotic risk [6]. In Shin et al.’s non-AMI-CS group, where acute-on-chronic heart failure with established cardiomyopathies comprised 31.3% of patients, the prevalence of AFib was likely substantial. We note, however, that AFib in the ultra-acute phase is typically a target of immediate intervention, including direct current cardioversion and antiarrhythmic therapy, which may partly explain its underrepresentation in registry data of this kind. Because rhythm status was not captured in the original registry, we frame its potential prognostic contribution as an important direction for future investigation rather than a confounder demonstrably driving the reported mortality difference.

Second, diastolic dysfunction parameters, including E/e’ ratio and left atrial volume index, provide prognostic information that is independent of LVEF in CS [8, 9]. Patients with preserved LVEF but severe diastolic dysfunction represent a phenotype with elevated filling pressures, pulmonary congestion, and poor hemodynamic reserve that is invisible to an LVEF-based assessment. An elevated E/e’ ratio or advanced diastolic dysfunction grade in this subgroup could have identified patients with restrictive physiology whose prognosis was driven by filling pressure abnormalities rather than systolic impairment.

Third, RV function assessment, including tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), RV fractional area change, and indices of RV-pulmonary artery coupling, has emerged as a critical determinant of VA-ECMO outcomes [10, 11]. VA-ECMO increases LV afterload and may unmask or worsen RV dysfunction through altered biventricular loading and ventricular interdependence, a hemodynamic interaction that LVEF alone cannot predict [12]. The differential rates of LV unloading between the AMI-CS (2.3%) and non-AMI-CS (7.2%) groups in Shin et al.’s study suggest that RV and LV loading conditions differed substantially between groups, but no RV functional data were reported. This point is particularly relevant because VA-ECMO retrograde flow increases LV afterload and may expose limitations in RV reserve through ventricular interdependence. In this setting, RV-pulmonary artery coupling may provide insight into recovery potential that is not captured by any single LV metric alone. Coupling-based assessment should therefore be evaluated prospectively as part of future VA-ECMO phenotyping studies.

The authors acknowledge that “additional studies focusing on the heterogeneity of non-AMI are needed to develop more accurate prognostic models.” Additional echocardiographic data could have helped describe cardiac mechanics more completely in this heterogeneous group. A prospective cardiac phenotyping approach integrating LVEF with diastolic function parameters, RV metrics, and rhythm status may enable more granular sub-classification of the non-AMI-CS group beyond the current etiological categories of acute-on-chronic HF-CS, de novo HF-CS, and secondary/mixed CS.

We note that the timing of echocardiographic assessment was not standardized, with some data collected pre-ECMO and others within 24 h post-initiation. This introduces particular concern for LVEF measurements obtained after VA-ECMO initiation, as LVEF is acutely affected by the altered loading conditions imposed by VA-ECMO itself, limiting its interpretation as a baseline prognostic marker [12]. Rhythm status is less directly load-dependent, whereas diastolic and RV indices may also vary with ECMO flow, unloading strategies, ventilation, and vasoactive support. These variables may therefore be most informative when captured prospectively at standardized pre-ECMO and post-cannulation timepoints.

We acknowledge several caveats to the phenotyping approach we propose. Much of the supporting prognostic evidence for diastolic and RV parameters derives from general CS populations rather than from VA-ECMO-supported cohorts specifically, and parameters such as E/e’ and TAPSE are themselves difficult to interpret under VA-ECMO because of altered loading, circuit flow, and concurrent mechanical ventilation. These interpretive challenges are compounded when patients receive additional mechanical circulatory support, such as an intra-aortic balloon pump or a microaxial flow pump, whose left ventricular unloading effects further alter loading-dependent echocardiographic indices. Of note, during the ultra-acute phase these indices fluctuate substantially as flow settings, pump speed, and vasoactive dosing are titrated, which complicates the extraction of any single static prognostic marker. We therefore advance comprehensive cardiac phenotyping as a hypothesis-generating direction for prospective study rather than a parameter set readily abstracted from existing registry data.

In summary, we commend Shin et al. for their important contribution to the real-world evidence on VA-ECMO in CS. Their findings highlight the limitations of relying on LVEF alone to characterize cardiac dysfunction in heterogeneous VA-ECMO-supported CS populations. Future prognostic models may be strengthened by prospective clinical cardiac phenotyping that extends beyond LVEF to include rhythm characterization, diastolic function, and RV functional assessment, particularly in the heterogeneous non-AMI-CS population where LVEF alone appears insufficient for risk stratification.
