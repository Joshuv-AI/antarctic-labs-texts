Leaf development is associated with coupled metabolome-fungi trajectories in the phyllosphere of Panax ginseng
Authors: Xiaohang Yang, Qi Wang, Penglan Wang, Jiaju Li, Lina Wang, Xingbo Bian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeaf development underpins physiological performance and secondary metabolite accumulation, yet its influence on phyllosphere fungal communities in perennial medicinal plants such as ginseng (Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer) remains poorly understood. Understanding how developmental stage affects leaf chemistry and microbial assembly is important for linking plant metabolism with aboveground microbial ecology.ResultsWe analyzed ginseng leaves across four developmental stages (S1–S4) using physiological measurements, ginsenoside profiling, untargeted metabolomics, ITS amplicon sequencing, fungal association networks, and metabolite-fungus correlations. Leaf physiological traits and ginsenoside content peaked at stage S2, representing a developmental optimum. Metabolomics revealed early enrichment of sugar phosphates, nucleotide metabolites, and membrane lipids, followed by late accumulation of raffinose-family oligosaccharides, phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, oxylipin-related lipids, and brassinosteroid-linked metabolites. Fungal richness and evenness increased from S1 to S4, with communities shifting from Ascomycota-dominated at early stages to Basidiomycota-enriched on senescing leaves. Fungal association networks showed stage-related increases in network size and changes in topological organization. Integrative analyses indicated that stage-specific metabolic changes were closely associated with fungal community succession, with amino acid, carbohydrate, lipid, flavonoid, and terpenoid pathways potentially linked to stage-specific fungal association patterns.ConclusionsGinseng leaf development was accompanied by coordinated physiological and metabolic reprogramming that was associated with shifts in phyllosphere fungal composition and inferred association networks. These findings highlight leaf developmental stage as an important context for aboveground microbial dynamics and provide a framework for linking plant metabolism with microbial ecology in perennial crops, while emphasizing that causal relationships between specific metabolites and fungal taxa require further experimental validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09388-8.

Introduction

Ginseng (Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer) is a perennial medicinal plant with a long history of use in East Asia for its diverse pharmacological activities [1, 2]. While the root has traditionally received the most attention due to its high economic and medicinal value, the leaf also represents an essential organ that contributes substantially to photosynthesis, primary metabolism, and secondary metabolite production [3]. Moreover, ginseng leaves are increasingly recognized as alternative sources of bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, and ginsenosides, which are involved in plant defense and are also valued for their beneficial effects on human health [4]. Traditionally prepared as teas and soups, ginseng leaves have more recently been developed into functional foods and supplements due to their combined flavor and health benefits. Despite their importance, the dynamic metabolic transitions occurring in ginseng leaves during development remain largely unexplored. Recent studies have begun to address this gap. For instance, an integrative metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis revealed that light intensity regulates ginsenoside biosynthesis in ginseng leaves, highlighting the developmental regulation of secondary metabolism [5]. Similarly, a study on metabolic dynamics across different ages demonstrated that leaf metabolites undergo substantial reprogramming in parallel with root metabolism [6].

Leaf development is accompanied by profound biochemical reprogramming [7]. Young leaves are typically enriched in primary metabolites, supporting growth and energy supply, whereas mature leaves accumulate a wide range of secondary metabolites, including flavonoids and terpenoids, that play key roles in stress adaptation and quality formation [8, 9]. These metabolic adjustments are not only central to the physiology of the plant but also affect its ecological interactions. Recent studies have revealed diverse metabolic and ecological trajectories along the leaf developmental continuum. Previous studies have revealed that leaf development is accompanied by tissue- and stage-specific metabolic reprogramming, including dynamic changes in secondary metabolites such as flavonoids [10]. Transcriptomic analyses further indicate that mature leaves undergo extensive reorganization of carbon metabolism and other physiological processes [11]. Moreover, leaf development has been shown to coincide with dynamic shifts in microbial communities, which are strongly influenced by changing nutrient profiles such as sugars and nitrogen [12]. For medicinal plants such as ginseng, understanding such stage-specific metabolic patterns in leaves is crucial for clarifying the mechanisms underlying active compound biosynthesis and for optimizing cultivation practices aimed at enhancing product quality. However, systematic studies linking developmental stage, metabolism, and leaf-associated microbial dynamics in medicinal plants remain scarce, particularly in ginseng.

In parallel, the leaf-associated microbiota, primarily consisting of fungal communities, plays a vital role in plant health through multiple mechanisms, such as modulation of host immunity, suppression of pathogens, and regulation of metabolic activities [13, 14]. In many crops and model plants, fungal communities on plant surfaces and within tissues have been shown to vary with developmental stages, environmental conditions, and metabolite production. For example, in Arabidopsis, host genetic factors were found to significantly shape the phyllosphere fungal composition, highlighting the strong link between host developmental programs and microbial community assembly [15]. Similarly, fungal communities in the phyllosphere have been observed to shift dynamically in response to environmental fluctuations and host physiology, with microbe-microbe interactions also playing a key role in determining microbial colonization success and functional outcomes [16, 17]. However, in ginseng, the composition and dynamics of leaf-associated fungal communities across different developmental stages remain poorly characterized, and little is known about their potential interactions with leaf metabolites. This gap in knowledge underscores the need for further investigation into the role of fungal communities in ginseng leaf development and their interactions with plant metabolism.

Recent advances in multi-omics technologies provide powerful approaches for linking plant developmental processes with associated microbial communities [18, 19]. Metabolomics enables comprehensive profiling of stage-dependent biochemical changes, whereas high-throughput sequencing provides detailed information on microbial diversity and community structure. Integrating these approaches can help clarify how developmental reprogramming of leaf metabolism contributes to the formation of distinct phyllosphere microhabitats [20]. In this study, we investigated physiological traits, ginsenoside profiles, untargeted metabolomes, and phyllosphere fungal communities of ginseng leaves across four developmental stages, from leaf expansion to late senescence. Our objectives were to characterize stage-specific physiological and metabolic transitions, reveal developmental shifts in fungal community composition and association networks, and explore metabolite–fungus correlations linked to leaf chemical changes. This work provides stage-resolved insight into the coupling between ginseng leaf development, senescence-associated metabolic reprogramming, and phyllosphere fungal assembly, thereby contributing to a broader understanding of plant metabolism–microbiome interactions in perennial medicinal crops.

Materials and methods

Plant material selection and sample collection

Two-year-old ginseng seedlings (cultivar “Da Ma Ya”) were obtained from a commercial farm in Fusong County, Jilin Province, northeastern China (42°29′N, 127°18′E). Fusong County is located in the Changbai Mountain region and is one of the major traditional ginseng-producing areas in China. The region is characterized by a humid mountainous climate, with a mean annual temperature of approximately 4 °C and mean annual precipitation of approximately 800 mm; rainfall is mainly concentrated in summer. The seedlings were carefully screened to ensure uniform size, intact buds, and robust growth, thereby minimizing variation among individuals. Forest soil (dark brown soil) collected from a natural birch forest in the same region was used as the growth substrate. The soil was manually cleaned to remove branches, weeds, insects, and stones before use, and no fertilizers or chemical amendments were applied during the experiment.

The pot experiment was established in April 2025 following previously described protocols with minor modifications [21, 22]. Sixty healthy seedlings were transplanted into polypropylene pots (17 cm height, 20 cm diameter) filled with the prepared soil, with two seedlings per pot, resulting in 30 pots in total. Plants were irrigated as needed to maintain soil moisture, and weeds were removed manually. Greenhouse conditions were maintained at 25 ± 2 °C with a relative humidity of 60 ± 5% and a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle. Illumination was provided by cool white fluorescent lamps (32 W) at an intensity of approximately 9500 lx. Plant growth was monitored regularly to ensure consistent health status.

Leaf samples were collected at four representative developmental stages, designated as S1 to S4: the expanding stage (10–15 days after leaf expansion, DAE), the fully expanded stage (45–50 DAE), the early senescence stage (75–80 DAE), and the late senescence stage (105–110 DAE). The developmental stages were identified based on key morphological characteristics, including leaf area stabilization, coloration changes, and SPAD measurements. At each stage, leaves were harvested from multiple plants across different pots to ensure representative sampling and to minimize individual variation. Six independent biological replicates were collected per stage. Sampling was consistently conducted at 18:00–19:00 to minimize potential diurnal effects. This fixed evening window was selected to standardize sampling relative to the controlled light/dark cycle and to reduce short-term variation in leaf metabolism and phyllosphere fungal communities among developmental stages. Immediately after collection, each leaf sample was divided into two portions: one portion was frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for metabolomic analysis and fungal community profiling (ITS sequencing), while the other portion was reserved for targeted quantification of ginsenosides.

Chlorophyll content and photosynthetic parameters

SPAD (Soil and Plant Analyzer Development) values were periodically recorded using a handheld chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502, Konica Minolta, Japan). For chlorophyll quantification, fresh leaf tissues were extracted in 80% acetone, and the absorbance of the extracts was measured at 663 and 645 nm with a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) (LICHTENTHALER and WELLBURN 1983). Chlorophyll a and b contents were calculated and expressed as mg·g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW).

Leaf gas exchange parameters, including net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), and transpiration rate (Tr), were measured with a portable photosynthesis system (LI-6400XT, LI-COR Biosciences, USA). Measurements were performed between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. under natural light conditions. For each treatment, ten fully expanded functional leaves were randomly selected, and the average of these measurements was recorded as one biological replicate. Each treatment group included three biological replicates.

Determination of bioactive compounds

The concentrations of major ginsenosides in ginseng leaf tissues were determined using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole-Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-Exactive Orbitrap/MS, Thermo Scientific, USA). The procedure followed the guidelines of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition) with minor modifications, as well as previously published protocols [23, 24].

Metabolomics analysis

Approximately 50 mg of frozen ginseng leaf tissue was weighed into 2 mL sterile centrifuge tubes. Each tube was supplemented with 400 µL of extraction solvent (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v) and a 6 mm stainless steel bead. Samples were homogenized at − 10 °C for 6 min at 50 Hz using a high-throughput tissue grinder, incubated at − 20 °C for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were transferred to LC-MS/MS vials for analysis. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal aliquots from each extract.

Metabolomic profiling was performed using an UHPLC-Triple TOF 6600 system (AB SCIEX, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (95% water and 5% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (47.5% acetonitrile, 47.5% isopropanol, and 5% water containing 0.1% formic acid). The flow rate was 0.40 mL min− 1, the injection volume was 10 µL, and the column temperature was maintained at 45 °C. The gradient elution program is provided in Table S1. Mass spectrometric data were acquired in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. The main ion source parameters were as follows: Ion Source Gas 2, 50 psi; Curtain Gas, 35 psi; source temperature, 500 °C; IonSpray Voltage Floating, + 5500 V in positive mode and − 4500 V in negative mode; declustering potential, 80 V; collision energy, 40 ± 20 eV; and interface heater, on.

Raw LC-MS/MS data were processed using Progenesis QI v3.0 (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak detection, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment, resulting in a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity. Only features with a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) ≥ 3 were retained as reliable signals. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching MS and MS/MS data against public databases, including HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/) and METLIN (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), as well as an in-house database. Candidate metabolites were assigned based on precursor ion m/z, possible adducts, isotope patterns, and molecular formula prediction, using a mass error threshold of < 10 ppm. MS/MS spectra were further evaluated using an improved weighted cosine similarity score, and only candidates meeting the predefined criteria were retained for annotation (initial candidate score > 30; score > 35 for matches supported by experimental reference spectra; score > 40 for matches based on theoretical spectra). Relative quantification was based on integrated peak areas and normalized to the total peak area. Before statistical analysis, metabolites with > 20% missing values within a group were removed, remaining missing values were imputed using the minimum value for each metabolite, sum normalization was applied, and metabolites with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% were excluded. The reported entries were therefore described as LC-MS/MS-detected metabolite features and, depending on annotation confidence, were assigned to putatively annotated metabolites, broader compound classes, or remained unclassified when no confident assignment could be achieved.

The processed data matrix was uploaded to the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) for statistical and bioinformatics analyses [25]. Data were normalized using Pareto scaling, and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied to assess metabolic differences among leaf developmental stages. Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were defined as those with variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.0 and p < 0.05. DAMs with database-supported annotations were further subjected to KEGG-based pathway enrichment analysis to elucidate major metabolic transitions during leaf development.

To provide an additional exploratory view of metabolite dynamics across leaf development, time-series profile analysis was performed using the Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM) algorithm. Independently, metabolite features were also subjected to a stage-peak grouping analysis. For this analysis, metabolite abundances were normalized using MaxMinScale, and the mean normalized abundance within each developmental stage was calculated. Metabolite features were then assigned to the developmental stage at which their mean normalized abundance reached a maximum, and the resulting stage-associated feature sets were further summarized by pathway enrichment analysis.

DNA extraction and MiSeq high-throughput sequencing

The phyllosphere microbiota was collected from ginseng leaves using a washing and filtration protocol. Briefly, the entire leaf sample was placed in a sterile 50 mL centrifuge tube containing 25 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.0) with 0.02% Tween-20. The tube was shaken at 200 rpm for 30 min to dislodge the epiphytic fungi. The resulting microbial suspension was then carefully aspirated and vacuum-filtered through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone (PES) membrane. The membrane, with trapped fungi, was used for subsequent DNA extraction.

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the membrane using the DNeasy PowerPlant Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and concentration were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop UV-Vis spectrophotometer (ND-2000c, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Fungal communities were profiled by amplifying the ITS1 region with primers ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′) (Chen et al. 2022). The primer pair included adapter and barcode sequences. Amplicons were purified, pooled in equimolar concentrations, and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 300 bp) on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) at Majorbio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Bioinformatic analysis for sequencing data

Paired-end reads generated from the MiSeq platform were merged using FLASH (v1.2.11) and assigned to individual samples based on unique barcodes. Quality filtering and chimera removal were performed in QIIME (v1.9.1), and high-quality sequences (> 97% similarity) were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using UPARSE (v7.1). Representative OTU sequences were taxonomically classified with the RDP Classifier (v2.11) against the UNITE 8.0 database for fungi. After quality filtering, the number of reads per sample ranged from 50,221 to 83,301. To standardize sequencing effort across samples, the fungal ITS dataset was rarefied to 50,152 reads per sample prior to downstream diversity analyses. Rarefaction curves based on observed OTU richness (Sobs) were generated to evaluate whether sequencing depth was sufficient for community characterization and are shown in Figure S1. A summary of sequencing depth for each sample is provided in Table S2.

Alpha diversity indices, including Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson, were calculated to assess microbial richness and diversity. Beta diversity was evaluated using hierarchical clustering analysis and principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances in R (v4.1.2), and differences in fungal community composition among developmental stages were further assessed by analysis of similarities (ANOSIM). As a complementary analysis to ANOSIM, PERMDISP was performed based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix using the vegan package in R. Within-stage community dispersion was measured as the distance from each sample to its group centroid, and significance was assessed using 9,999 permutations. Biomarker taxa were identified by linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), with an LDA score threshold of 4.0.

Fungal association network construction, analysis, and visualization

Fungal association networks were constructed at the genus level to characterize stage-dependent association patterns of phyllosphere fungal communities across the four developmental stages (S1-S4). To reduce the influence of rare taxa, genera were filtered separately for each stage, and only those with a mean relative abundance ≥ 0.01% and detected in at least 50% of samples were retained.

Stage-specific association networks were inferred using SparCC, which reduces spurious correlations in compositional sequencing data [26]. Correlation matrices were calculated in R (v4.1.2) using the sparcc() function in the SpiecEasi package, with 20 outer iterations, 10 inner iterations, and a convergence threshold of 0.1. Pairwise associations with |r| > 0.8 were retained to construct undirected weighted networks. Positive edges indicated positive correlations, whereas negative edges indicated negative correlations among genera.

Network topology was analyzed using the igraph package in R. The calculated parameters included the numbers of nodes and edges, the proportions of positive and negative edges, average degree, network density, mean edge weight, global and average clustering coefficients, average path length, and network diameter. Modules were identified using the Louvain algorithm. Small-world properties were evaluated by comparison with 100 random networks of the same size, and networks with σ > 1 were considered small-world. Natural connectivity was calculated as a graph-theoretical indicator of network robustness based on the eigenvalue spectrum of the adjacency matrix for each stage-specific fungal association network [27]. Higher natural connectivity values indicate greater redundancy of alternative paths and stronger network robustness.

Node roles were classified according to the Zi-Pi framework, where Zi reflects within-module connectivity and Pi reflects among-module connectivity [28]. Using thresholds of Zi = 2.5 and Pi = 0.62, nodes were categorized as peripherals, connectors, module hubs, or network hubs, with the latter three considered key nodes. Additional node-level metrics, including betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, eigenvector centrality, PageRank, and K-core value, were also calculated. Networks were visualized with nodes colored by phylum and edges colored by positive or negative association.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to evaluate differences among developmental stages for physiological traits, targeted metabolite data, and the relative abundances of fungal taxa, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Multivariate and community-level analyses for metabolomic and fungal datasets, including OPLS-DA, PCoA, ANOSIM, LEfSe, and SparCC-based association network analysis, are described in the relevant subsections above. Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R (v4.1.2, R Core Team).

Results

Leaf photosynthetic traits and ginsenoside profiles across developmental stages

Across the S1-S4 developmental continuum, ginseng leaves exhibited a clear physiological trajectory (Fig. 1A). SPAD and chlorophyll (a and b) contents increased from S1 to a maximum at S2 and then declined markedly at S3 and S4. Gas-exchange traits showed a consistent pattern: Pn peaked at S2 and decreased thereafter, with the lowest values at S4; Gs and Tr remained relatively high from S1 to S3 but dropped sharply at S4, whereas Ci displayed the opposite trend, reaching its minimum at S2 and increasing progressively toward S4. Together, these results indicate that fully expanded leaves (S2) have the highest photosynthetic capacity, which is substantially impaired during late senescence (S4).

Ginsenoside profiling further revealed stage-dependent remodeling of secondary metabolism (Fig. 1B). PCA based on eight major ginsenosides separated samples by developmental stage (PC1 + PC2 = 84.12%), forming an ordered progression from S1 to S4. Most ginsenosides (Rg1, Re, Rb1, Rc, Rb2, and Rb3) accumulated maximally at S2 and declined thereafter, whereas Rg2 remained relatively stable among stages. In contrast, Rd showed delayed accumulation, peaking at S3 before decreasing at S4. Thus, photosynthetic traits and ginsenoside composition undergo coordinated, but metabolite-specific, changes across leaf development.

Global metabolomic reprogramming of ginseng leaves during development

Untargeted LC-MS detected > 3,700 metabolite features across the samples, which were subsequently annotated at different levels based on database matching (Fig. 2A). Among these features, lipids and lipid-like molecules constituted the largest fraction (36.42%), followed by unclassified features (18.55%), organic acids and derivatives (12.11%), organic oxygen compounds (9.38%), organoheterocyclic compounds (8.20%), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (6.08%), and benzenoids (4.48%). Overall, the detected feature set was dominated by lipid-related features, with substantial representation of organic acids and aromatic compound-associated categories.

PCA of the full metabolite dataset showed clear stage-dependent separation (PC1 = 58.30%, PC2 = 23.70%), with samples following an ordered S1-S4 trajectory and QC samples clustering tightly, supporting pronounced and reproducible metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 2B). Supervised OPLS-DA analysis further supported the stage-dependent separation observed in PCA (Figure S2). Pairwise comparisons between consecutive stages identified 1,441 DAMs (S2 vs. S1), 1,563 DAMs (S3 vs. S2), and 1,755 DAMs (S4 vs. S3), indicating progressively intensified remodeling toward senescence, particularly during the S3-S4 transition (Fig. 2C).

KEGG enrichment analysis combined with DA-score summary revealed stage-specific pathway shifts (Fig. 2D-F). S2 vs. S1 changes were dominated by lipid metabolism (glycerophospholipid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid), nucleotide metabolism (purine/pyrimidine), hormone signaling, oxidative phosphorylation, brassinosteroid biosynthesis, flavone/flavonol biosynthesis, and cutin/suberine/wax biosynthesis. S3 vs. S2 highlighted sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism, nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism, galactose and pyruvate metabolism, biosynthesis of cofactors, phenylalanine metabolism, and multiple secondary metabolic pathways (including isoflavonoid and alkaloid biosynthesis). S4 vs. S3 was characterized by strong shifts in carbohydrate/energy metabolism (glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, starch/sucrose, galactose), amino acid and nucleotide pathways, amino sugar/nucleotide sugar metabolism, glycerolipid/glycerophospholipid metabolism, hormone signaling, and tropane/piperidine/pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis. Collectively, leaf development is accompanied by extensive, ordered reconfiguration of primary and secondary metabolism.

Time-series analysis of metabolite trajectories reveals distinct developmental modules

STEM time-series clustering identified two significant temporal profiles across S1–S4 (Figure S3A). Profile 9 comprised metabolites that increased monotonically toward senescence, whereas profile 0 consisted of metabolites that decreased continuously along development. Enrichment of the increasing module (profile 9) implicated pathways related to lipid and hormone metabolism (linoleic acid, glycerophospholipid metabolism, brassinosteroid biosynthesis), amino acid metabolism (including branched-chain amino acids and glycine/serine/threonine), carbohydrate metabolism (starch/sucrose, galactose, pyruvate), multiple secondary metabolic routes (e.g., flavonoid biosynthesis), and stress/resource management pathways (autophagy and ABC transporters) (Figure S3B). In contrast, the decreasing module (profile 0) was enriched in pathways associated with primary carbon assimilation and energy metabolism (carbon fixation, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, fructose/mannose metabolism), together with antioxidant- and nucleotide-related processes (ascorbate/aldarate metabolism; purine and pyrimidine metabolism) (Figure S3C). These two dominant profiles indicate a structured transition from early-stage primary metabolism toward late-stage accumulation of lipids, hormone-related metabolites, and specialized metabolic products.

Time-series differential analysis further defined stage-specific metabolic signatures (Figure S4). S1-enriched metabolites mapped mainly to linoleic acid/α-linolenic acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism. S2-specific metabolites were enriched in glycerophospholipid metabolism, purine metabolism, biosynthesis of cofactors, and starch/sucrose metabolism. S3 featured glycine/serine/threonine metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, one-carbon pool by folate, and glycerophospholipid metabolism. S4 was dominated by galactose metabolism, brassinosteroid biosynthesis, ABC transporters, and additional glycerophospholipid species. Together, these analyses identify coherent modules and stage-specific pathways underlying developmental metabolic transitions.

Pathway-level visualization highlights coordinated reprogramming of primary and secondary metabolism

To obtain a pathway-level view of the metabolic transitions underlying ginseng leaf development, significantly changed metabolites were mapped onto representative KEGG pathways and their relative abundances at S1-S4 were visualized as scaled heatmaps (Fig. 3). In amino acid metabolism, numerous intermediates in the serine/glycine and aromatic amino acid branches (tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine pathways) showed pronounced stage-dependent shifts (Fig. 3A). Several upstream amino acids and related organic acids gradually increased from S1 to S4, whereas some downstream phenolic acids and amines exhibited more complex, stage-specific patterns, consistent with a progressive redirection of nitrogen and carbon skeletons from proteinogenic amino acids toward phenylpropanoid- and alkaloid-related intermediates.

Carbohydrate metabolism displayed a clear redistribution of carbon from sugar phosphates toward disaccharides and organic acids along the developmental gradient (Fig. 3B). Hexose phosphates such as glucose 6-phosphate, fructose 6-phosphate, and sucrose 6-phosphate were relatively enriched at early stages, whereas sucrose, trehalose, and several galactose-derived sugars accumulated preferentially at later stages, particularly at S4. In parallel, intermediates connecting to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, such as malic acid and succinic acid, also showed marked temporal changes, indicating a coordinated adjustment of glycolysis, sucrose cycling, and organic acid metabolism as leaves transition from expansion to senescence.

Lipid metabolism was extensively remodeled as well (Fig. 3C). Multiple glycerophospholipids and related lysophospholipids exhibited distinct temporal profiles, with many species tending to increase from S2 to S4, while certain early intermediates such as dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glycerol 3-phosphate decreased over time. Several fatty acids and their derivatives also showed stage-specific enrichment, highlighting dynamic adjustments in membrane lipid composition and fatty-acid-derived signaling molecules during leaf development.

In secondary metabolism, flavonoid-related pathways showed strong temporal differentiation of individual compounds (Fig. 4). Upstream phenylpropanoid acids and selected flavonoid aglycones and glycosides displayed divergent patterns, with some metabolites (e.g., specific flavanones, flavonols, and their glycosides) being most abundant at intermediate stages, whereas others, including certain phenolic acids and anthocyanin-related compounds such as cyanidin, were preferentially accumulated at later stages (Fig. 4A). Terpenoid-related intermediates also exhibited distinct developmental profiles, indicating a reconfiguration of terpenoid flux along the leaf lifespan (Fig. 4B). In the plant hormone panel, trans-zeatin and brassinolide showed an overall increasing trend from S1 to S4, whereas jasmonic acid was relatively high at S1 and declined thereafter, suggesting a shift from jasmonate-associated early responses toward cytokinin- and brassinosteroid-linked signaling in later stages (Fig. 4C). Together, these pathway maps provide an intuitive overview of how central carbon, nitrogen, lipid, and specialized metabolism are coordinately reprogrammed during ginseng leaf development.

Leaf developmental stage is associated with shifts in phyllosphere fungal diversity and community structure

Leaf development was associated with pronounced changes in the phyllosphere fungal community (Fig. 5). Alpha diversity increased with leaf age: Chao1 richness and Shannon diversity were low and similar at S1-S2, increased at S3, and reached the highest levels at S4, whereas Simpson index declined toward S4, indicating increased evenness and reduced dominance in late-stage communities (Fig. 5A).

Beta diversity analyses showed robust stage-dependent separation. Hierarchical clustering grouped samples primarily by developmental stage, and PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distances separated S1-S4 along the first two axes (PC1 = 42.51%, PC2 = 18.17%), with significance supported by ANOSIM (Fig. 5B). PERMDISP analysis further showed that within-stage fungal community dispersion differed significantly among developmental stages (F (3, 20) = 18.62, p < 0.001; Figure S5; Table S3). The mean distance to the group centroid was highest in S2 (0.283), followed by S1 (0.244) and S3 (0.201), and was lowest in S4 (0.143; Table S4). Pairwise comparisons further supported significant differences in dispersion among several developmental stages (Table S5). This pattern indicates that early and fully expanded leaves showed greater within-stage fungal community heterogeneity, whereas late-senescing leaves harbored more compositionally convergent fungal communities. Therefore, the observed beta-diversity patterns should be interpreted as reflecting both stage-related shifts in fungal community structure and developmental changes in within-stage community heterogeneity. Taxonomically, Basidiomycota and Ascomycota dominated across stages, but their relative proportions shifted: Ascomycota was highest at S2, whereas Basidiomycota became increasingly dominant at S3-S4; unclassified fungal phyla decreased toward later stages (Fig. 5C). Genus-level profiling further revealed ordered compositional turnover along development (Fig. 5D).

Stage-specific fungal taxa and fungal association network patterns across leaf development

Consistent with community-level trends, dominant taxa exhibited clear stage-related patterns (Fig. 6A, B). Ascomycota peaked at S2 and decreased at S3, whereas Basidiomycota showed the opposite pattern, reaching pronounced enrichment at S3 stage. At the genus level, Pseudogymnoascus was specifically enriched at S2, while Cutaneotrichosporon was more abundant at S1 stage and declined thereafter. Additional genera showed distinct trajectories: Irpex and Schizophyllum peaked at S3; Peniophora increased progressively from S1 to S4 stage; Cladosporium increased toward S4 stage; and Chaetomium decreased sharply after S1 stage, whereas Alternaria remained relatively stable across stages (Fig. 6B). LEfSe further identified stage-discriminatory biomarkers across multiple taxonomic ranks, supporting distinct fungal signatures at each stage (Fig. 6C).

SparCC-based fungal association networks revealed clear stage-dependent differences in fungal correlation structure across leaf development (Fig. 7A; Table S6). Network size increased progressively from S1 to S4 stage. The S1 stage network was the smallest, comprising 81 nodes and 248 edges, whereas the S2 and S3 stage networks expanded to 106 nodes/541 edges and 204 nodes/1627 edges, respectively. The S4 stage network showed the largest size and complexity, with 347 nodes and 2698 edges. Thus, both the number of taxa involved in the network and the number of inferred associations increased markedly along leaf development.

Across the four stages, positive edges accounted for 51%-57% of total links (56.85% in S1 stage and approximately 52% in S2-S4 stages), indicating that positive and negative associations were relatively balanced overall. Network density was comparable among S1-S3 stages (0.077–0.097) but was lowest in S4 stage (0.045), suggesting that although the S4 network contained the greatest numbers of nodes and edges, it was structurally more diffuse. The average degree increased from 6.12 in S1 to 15.95 in S3 stage and then decreased slightly to 15.55 in S4 stage, indicating an overall increase in the number of associations per genus as network size expanded.

Louvain community detection divided the four networks into 6–8 modules, with modularity values ranging from 0.352 to 0.489. Modularity was relatively higher in S1 and S4 stage (0.489 and 0.454, respectively), suggesting more clearly partitioned network organization at these stages. All four networks exhibited small-world properties, with σ values > 1 (S1, 5.13; S2, 2.93; S3, 3.52; S4, 6.01). Notably, S4 showed the highest σ value, indicating the most pronounced small-world characteristics. Natural connectivity increased steadily from 7.02 in S1 to 23.09 in S4 stage, suggesting greater robustness in the larger late-stage networks.

Zi-Pi analysis identified 145 key nodes in total, including 5 in S1, 25 in S2, 54 in S3, and 61 in S4 stage. Peniophora (Basidiomycota) was identified as a network hub in S2, S3, and S4 stage, with particularly high degree values in S3 and S4 stage (143 and 208, respectively), indicating that it was the most consistently central taxon across stages (Fig. 7B-E). Coprinellus acted as a network hub or module hub in S3 and S4 stage, whereas Cladosporium (Ascomycota) was identified as a network hub in S4 stage. In contrast, only five connector nodes were detected in S1 stage, and the most connected taxon, Cutaneotrichosporon (degree = 26), reached only connector status, suggesting a comparatively weak concentration of central taxa at this early stage. At the phylum level, Basidiomycota and Ascomycota dominated all four networks. In addition, one genus each from Chytridiomycota, Fungi_phy_Incertae_sedis, and Mortierellomycota was present in the S1 network, whereas the S2-S4 networks were composed almost exclusively of taxa from the two dominant phyla.

Coordinated metabolome-fungi patterns and pathway-resolved metabolite-fungus associations

Procrustes analysis revealed significant concordance between the overall metabolome and the phyllosphere fungal community across S1-S4 stages (M2 = 0.0748, p = 0.001), supporting coordinated chemical and fungi shifts along development (Fig. 8A). The low M2 value indicates a close fit between the two ordinations, whereas the significant P value indicates that this concordance was unlikely to occur by chance under the permutation test. The metabolite-fungus correlation network displayed a hub-like architecture, suggesting that a subset of genera showed broad statistical associations with metabolic variation (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

Ginseng leaf development is characterized by a coordinated transition in physiology and specialized metabolism, with a clear functional optimum at the fully expanded stage. The peak SPAD and chlorophyll contents, maximal Pn, and minimal Ci at S2 indicate that mature leaves act as the dominant photosynthetic “source” tissues, whereas senescing leaves increasingly experience both stomatal and non-stomatal limitations to CO2 assimilation associated with chloroplast degradation and broader metabolic reprogramming [29]. Consistent with this physiological trajectory, most major ginsenosides (Rg1, Re, Rb1, Rc, Rb2, and Rb3) reached their maxima at S2 and declined afterward, suggesting that active primary metabolism is closely linked to triterpenoid accumulation in ginseng leaves [5]. Similar stage-dependent coupling between photosynthesis and specialized metabolite accumulation has been reported in other species, where fully expanded leaves show enhanced flux through terpene and flavonoid pathways relative to young or senescing tissues [30]. The decline of most ginsenosides during senescence likely reflects reduced biosynthetic capacity and/or enhanced turnover, with potential redistribution to storage organs remaining a plausible but untested possibility in the present study [6]. Notably, ginsenoside Rd displayed a delayed peak at S3, which is consistent with its position as a downstream protopanaxadiol-type product and with reports that senescence or stress can promote structural conversion within the ginsenoside pool, potentially via hydrolysis/deglycosylation of Rb-group members [31–33]. Such compositional remodeling may fine-tune defense during a stage when leaves remain metabolically active yet become increasingly vulnerable to pathogen pressure [34]. Biologically, these patterns suggest that leaf development in ginseng is not simply a process of gradual decline, but includes a distinct functional window in which carbon assimilation, specialized metabolite production, and potential defensive capacity are coordinated. Together, these patterns identify a developmental window around S2 in which photosynthetic performance and pharmaceutically relevant ginsenoside accumulation coincide, suggesting potential practical value for stage-informed leaf harvesting and utilization [6].

Beyond ginsenosides, the metabolome-wide results depict leaf development as a sequence of structured metabolic states rather than a linear decline. The dominance of lipids and lipid-like molecules highlights the central role of membrane/storage lipids and lipid-derived signaling in leaf function [35, 36]. From S1 (expanding stage) to S2 (fully expanded stage), the strong involvement of glycerophospholipid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid metabolism suggests active membrane remodeling and engagement of oxylipin-related processes as leaves expand and acquire full photosynthetic competence [37, 38]. In parallel, the enrichment of brassinosteroid biosynthesis, hormone signaling, and cutin/suberine/wax biosynthesis is consistent with coordinated hormonal regulation, surface barrier formation, and structural maturation in functional source leaves [39–41]. In the comparison between S2 (fully expanded stage) and S3 (early senescence stage), enrichment in sphingolipid metabolism, nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism, and cofactor biosynthesis, is consistent with a transition from growth-oriented anabolism toward redox regulation and stress responsiveness, in which sphingolipid-derived signaling is linked to programmed cell death and defense and nicotinamide-related pathways are associated with NAD(P) turnover and energy/redox metabolism [42–45]. Concurrent enrichment of phenylalanine metabolism, isoflavonoid biosynthesis, and alkaloid pathways suggests that early senescence is accompanied by active investment in phenolic and nitrogen-containing metabolites with roles in antioxidant protection, UV buffering, and biotic defense [46, 47]. The strongest remodeling occurred from S3 (early senescence stage) to S4 (late senescence stage), where carbohydrate/energy pathways and multiple amino acid and nucleotide routes shifted prominently, consistent with intensified catabolism and nutrient recycling typical of late senescence [3, 48, 49]. Enrichment of glycerolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism at this stage likely reflects accelerated turnover of membranes as cellular structures—particularly chloroplasts—are dismantled, while sustained hormone-associated changes and alkaloid-related signals indicate continued regulatory involvement even as biosynthetic capacity declines [28, 50, 51]. Taken together, these metabolic transitions suggest that leaf development progressively changes the nutritional and signaling landscape of the phyllosphere, thereby generating chemically distinct environments that may be associated with different fungal assemblages at different stages. Importantly, these developmental shifts provide a plausible chemical context for the observed changes in phyllosphere community composition and inferred association patterns [52, 53].

Time-series modeling provides complementary support for this staged metabolic choreography. The emergence of only two significant STEM profiles—a monotonically decreasing and a monotonically increasing module—indicates that a large fraction of differential metabolites follow coherent developmental trajectories rather than idiosyncratic fluctuations [54]. The decreasing module, enriched in carbon fixation, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, starch/sucrose metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism, is consistent with progressively down-regulated primary assimilation and central energy/nucleotide turnover as leaves age [55]. This aligns with the classic source-sink transition framework in which young/expanding leaves function as strong sinks prioritizing biomass construction before shifting to source status and eventual senescence [56, 57]. Conversely, the increasing module was enriched in lipid, amino acid, hormone, and diverse secondary pathways, consistent with rising importance of membrane remodeling, hormone-mediated regulation, and specialized metabolite pools during maturation and senescence. Oxylipin-related pathways and brassinosteroid biosynthesis contribute to both developmental control and stress signaling, while flavonoids and related phenolics support photoprotection and defense [56, 57]. The co-enrichment of autophagy and ABC transporter pathways further suggests coordinated activation of recycling and transport systems to manage resource redistribution and defense compound trafficking during late stages [58, 59]. Stage-specific high-level metabolite signatures refine this view: S3 was linked to glycine/serine/threonine metabolism, sphingolipids, and one-carbon pool by folate, whereas S4 highlighted galactose metabolism (including raffinose-family related shifts), brassinosteroid biosynthesis, ABC transporters, and membrane lipid remodeling [60, 61]. From an ecological perspective, such temporally structured metabolic environments may alter substrate availability on the leaf surface and the chemical context through which fungi experience host developmental status, providing an ecological background for the stage-specific assemblages observed [62].

In parallel, the phyllosphere fungi showed pronounced successional dynamics that were associated with leaf developmental stage. The increase in richness and Shannon diversity (and decreased Simpson index) toward S4 indicates that older leaves harbor more diverse and even fungal communities [63, 64], consistent with broader evidence that prolonged exposure to airborne inocula and increasing microhabitat heterogeneity promote taxon coexistence on older leaf surfaces [65]. The clear separation among stages in clustering and ordination supports developmental stage as an important factor associated with fungal community structure [66]. The contrasting dynamics of Ascomycota (peaking at S2 stage) and Basidiomycota (enriched at S3-S4 stages) are consistent with distinct stage associations and substrate-use tendencies; the enrichment of Basidiomycota at later stages is consistent with increased representation of taxa associated with litter/wood-linked substrates and senescing microenvironments [67]. The developmental shift in leaf chemistry, from sugar phosphates and nucleotide-related metabolites toward sucrose/RFOs, phenylpropanoid/flavonoid derivatives, and oxylipin-related lipids, provides a plausible substrate and chemical context for these community changes, with phenolic enrichment potentially favoring, or at least coinciding with, taxa tolerant of phenolic-rich environments [65]. Hormone reconfiguration across stages may further influence immunity and cuticular traits, indirectly shaping microbial assembly [68, 69]. These patterns suggest that leaf age in ginseng is an ecologically meaningful axis along which the phyllosphere fungal community is progressively reorganized. In practical terms, different developmental stages may differ not only in leaf physiology and chemistry, but also in their associated fungal taxa and potential functions relevant to leaf health, senescence processes, and crop quality. Collectively, these patterns support a coupled trajectory of metabolic and microbial change and may inform future stage-aware studies of leaf quality and phyllosphere-associated functions in ginseng cultivation [52, 70].

Stage-discriminatory taxa and fungal association network reorganization further indicate stage-associated restructuring of the phyllosphere fungi. The opposing Ascomycota/Basidiomycota dynamics and the stage specificity of multiple genera are consistent with possible stage-associated environmental filtering across development, where early leaves represent a relatively selective habitat and later stages provide more heterogeneous microhabitats and substrates [63, 65, 71]. Stage-associated genera (e.g., Cutaneotrichosporon at S1 stage and Pseudogymnoascus at S2 stage) may reflect early colonizers adapted to the initial phyllosphere environment and fluctuating surface conditions, whereas late-stage increases in taxa such as Peniophora and the S3 stage peaks of Irpex and Schizophyllum are consistent with saprotrophic lifestyles and complex substrate utilization typical of senescing tissues [72–75]. These dominant genera may have different ecological implications in the ginseng phyllosphere. Cutaneotrichosporon is a yeast-like basidiomycetous genus, and phyllosphere yeasts are generally considered well adapted to nutrient-limited and fluctuating leaf surface environments [76]. Its enrichment at S1 may therefore reflect adaptation to the young leaf microhabitat, where available carbon sources and surface conditions differ from those of older leaves. Pseudogymnoascus, which was enriched at S2, includes fungi frequently associated with cool environments, soil, and plant-derived substrates; its occurrence may be related to the cool ecological background of the Changbai Mountain ginseng-growing region and to substrate availability on fully expanded leaves [77]. In contrast, the late-stage enrichment of Peniophora, Irpex, and Schizophyllum is consistent with the saprotrophic potential of many basidiomycetous fungi and may be associated with the increased availability of complex plant-derived polymers during leaf senescence [78]. The increase in Cladosporium toward S4 may also reflect the accumulation of broadly tolerant fungi capable of persisting under changing leaf surface conditions [79]. However, these functional interpretations are based on taxonomic identity and previously reported ecological traits, and the precise roles of these fungi in the ginseng phyllosphere require further validation through isolation and functional assays. The apparent stability of Alternaria across stages suggests persistence of broadly tolerant residents or consistent dispersal input [80]. LEfSe-based biomarkers across multiple ranks further indicate lineage-level restructuring rather than a uniform drift process [81]. Network topology showed clear stage-dependent reorganization from S1 to S4 stage. Under the SparCC-based framework, network size increased markedly across development, with progressively more genera incorporated into the association networks and substantially more inferred links among them, indicating that later leaf stages were associated with broader fungal association structure. However, the late-stage networks should not be interpreted simply as denser, because network density was lowest in S4 despite its much larger size. Instead, the S4 stage network appeared more expansive but also structurally more diffuse. Likewise, modularity did not decline monotonically across development; relatively higher modularity in S1 and S4 stage suggested more clearly partitioned organization at the earliest and latest stages. Natural connectivity increased steadily from S1 to S4 stage, indicating greater robustness of the larger late-stage networks. Together, these topological features suggest that leaf senescence is associated not merely with taxonomic turnover, but with broader reorganization of fungal community structure, including the emergence of more extensive and potentially more resilient association architectures. The recurrent central positions of taxa such as Peniophora, together with the late-stage prominence of Coprinellus and Cladosporium, further suggest that a subset of fungi may be important for organizing late-stage association patterns, although these roles remain inferential within the present correlation-based framework. These results suggest that leaf development is associated not only with the scale of fungal associations, but also with their topological organization. Nevertheless, as with other correlation-based approaches, SparCC networks represent inferred associations rather than direct biotic relationships and may still be influenced by shared environmental filtering or indirect effects [82, 83]. Therefore, these networks should be viewed as hypothesis-generating frameworks that help identify candidate taxa and modules for further experimental validation.

Finally, the observed metabolome-fungi concordance and pathway-resolved associations provide an integrative view of stage-dependent biochemical changes across development. Significant Procrustes concordance supports coordinated variation between developmental metabolic transitions and fungal succession, consistent with the idea that shifts in carbon/nitrogen metabolites, membrane-related lipids, and specialized metabolites are associated with stage-specific biochemical environments linked to fungal colonization patterns [84, 85]. Hub-like taxa in the correlation network may serve as sensitive responders to the changing chemical milieu or as taxa consistently associated with major metabolic modules [86]. Pathway-resolved heatmaps further suggest that amino acid pools, sugars/sugar phosphates, and lipid remodeling processes covary with fungal dynamics, while correlations extending to flavonoid- and terpenoid-associated metabolites imply potential links between defense chemistry, host signaling state, and fungal succession [87–89]. Importantly, this integrative pattern indicates that leaf development is associated with coordinated changes in host function, chemical phenotype, and fungal community organization, rather than with isolated shifts in any single layer. The pathway-structured association patterns and the prioritized genus-metabolite pairs summarized here can guide follow-up tests using isolate-based assays or controlled substrate and synthetic-community experiments [82, 90].

Although this study provides stage-resolved evidence for coordinated changes in ginseng leaf metabolism and phyllosphere fungal communities, several points should be interpreted with caution. The multi-omics design used here was observational, and the metabolite–fungus relationships identified by Procrustes analysis, correlation heatmaps, and SparCC-based networks should therefore be viewed as statistical associations rather than direct evidence of causal interactions. These associations may reflect shared responses to leaf developmental stage, changes in the leaf surface microenvironment, or indirect interactions among fungal taxa. In addition, ITS amplicon sequencing based on OTU clustering provides a useful overview of community-level fungal succession, but it has limited resolution for distinguishing closely related taxa and does not directly reveal functional potential. The greenhouse experiment reduced environmental heterogeneity and allowed clearer comparison among developmental stages, but field-grown ginseng may experience more complex climatic, edaphic, and management-related influences. Future work integrating ASV- or metagenome-based profiling with fungal isolation, metabolite supplementation, and synthetic-community experiments will be important for testing whether specific leaf metabolites directly affect the colonization, persistence, or turnover of key phyllosphere fungi.

Conclusion

This study shows that ginseng leaf development is accompanied by coordinated physiological, metabolic, and phyllosphere fungal changes (Fig. 8C). Across the S1–S4 developmental gradient, fully expanded leaves represented a short physiological optimum, with peak photosynthetic performance and ginsenoside accumulation. Metabolomic profiling further revealed a transition from growth-associated primary metabolism in young leaves to enhanced carbon remobilization, membrane lipid remodeling, and defense-related specialized metabolism during senescence. In parallel, phyllosphere fungal communities followed a clear successional trajectory, shifting from relatively simple, Ascomycota-enriched assemblages at early stages to more diverse, even, and Basidiomycota-enriched communities on senescing leaves. Fungal association network analysis and metabolome–fungi integration indicated that developmental changes in leaf chemistry were closely associated with fungal community structure and inferred association patterns. Together, these findings support a developmental-metabolic perspective on phyllosphere dynamics in ginseng and highlight leaf developmental stage as an important factor associated with plant physiology, specialized metabolism, and aboveground microbial assembly. Further experimental validation will be required to determine whether specific metabolite classes directly regulate fungal colonization, persistence, or turnover.
