Disease-related complications and risk factors in hemoglobin H disease in a Thai multicenter registry
Authors: Nattiya Teawtrakul, Duantida Songdej, Chattree Hantaweepant, Adisak Tantiworawit, Sasinee Hantrakool, Pornpun Sripornsawan, Pacharapan Surapolchai, Supanun Lauhasurayotin, Kitti Torcharus, Pranee Sutcharitchan, Noppacharn Uaprasert, Krissana Panrong, Napat Laoaroon, Patcharee Komvilaisak, Panachai Silpsamrit, Arunotai Meekaewkunchorn, Bunchoo Pongtanakul, Pimlak Charoenkwan, Red Blood Cell Disorders Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHemoglobin H (Hb H) disease is a prevalent hereditary hemolytic anemia worldwide. Clinical severity varies by genotype, particularly between deletional and non-deletional forms, as well as the presence of co-inherited Hb E. However, disease-related complications remain underreported. We analyzed a Thai multicenter registry of 1,298 patients with Hb H disease to evaluate disease-related complications and risk factors. Deletional Hb H disease accounted for 55.9% of cases, whereas 44.1% had non-deletional disease. Hb E co-inheritance was present in 253 patients (19.5%). Patients with non-deletional Hb H disease and co-inherited Hb E had lower hemoglobin, higher ferritin levels, and greater transfusion requirements. Gallstones, pulmonary hypertension, extramedullary hematopoiesis, osteoporosis, and fractures were more frequent in non-deletional Hb H disease. After adjustment, increasing age (AOR 1.5; 95% CI 1.3–1.6; p<0.001), non-deletional Hb H disease (AOR 2.3; 95% CI 1.5–3.5; p<0.001), and transfusion dependency (AOR 2.7; 95% CI 1.4–5.3; p=0.003) were independently associated with overall complications. Splenectomy showed a marginal association (AOR 2.0; 95% CI 0.9–4.2; p=0.06). Gallstones were independently associated with non-deletional Hb H disease, splenectomy, and higher ferritin levels. Hyperuricemia was independently associated with increasing age and higher hemoglobin levels. Introduction

Alpha-thalassemia is one of the most common inherited hemoglobin disorders worldwide, characterized by reduced production of alpha-globin chains. This leads to a range of clinical phenotypes, from silent carriers to hemoglobin H (Hb H) disease, and in its most severe form, Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis. The severity of the disease depends on the number and type of alpha-globin gene deletions or mutations1. In Thailand, it is estimated that 20–30% of the general population are carriers of alpha-thalassemia, with Hb H disease being particularly prevalent and clinically significant2,3. In many cases, individuals with Hb H disease also co-inherit hemoglobin E (Hb E), a common beta-globin variant in Southeast Asia. The combination of Hb H disease and Hb E often results in a more severe clinical presentation, including worsening anemia, which may require frequent blood transfusions, as well as splenomegaly and other organ complications4–6. While non-deletional Hb H disease is consistently associated with more severe anemia and higher transfusion requirements6,14, evidence regarding the additional impact of Hb E co-inheritance derives mainly from recent multicenter Thai data12.

Although Hb H disease has traditionally been considered less severe than beta-thalassemia, emerging evidence suggests that individuals with this disorder can experience a range of significant disease-related morbidities. These include complications such as pulmonary hypertension, extramedullary hematopoiesis (EMH), gallstones, osteoporosis, hyperuricemia, and thromboembolic events7–11. Contributing factors to these complications include chronic anemia, iron overload, splenectomy, and repeated transfusions. Recent studies have also highlighted that individuals with non-deletional Hb H disease, particularly those with co-inherited Hb E, tend to experience greater clinical severity and lower survival rates than those with deletional Hb H disease12,13. However, much of the existing literature on Hb H disease has focused on pediatric populations or single-center studies, with limited large-scale, real-world data on the clinical course and complications of the disease. This study aims to address these gaps by analyzing data from a nationwide registry of Thai patients with Hb H disease. The primary objective is to assess the prevalence and predictors of disease-associated complications, with a focus on differences between deletional and non-deletional Hb H disease, as well as the impact of co-inherited Hb E.

Results

A total of 1,298 patients with Hb H disease were enrolled in the alpha-thalassemia registry, comprising 722 females (55.6%). Deletional Hb H disease was more common than non-deletional Hb H disease (726 [55.9%] vs. 572 [44.1%], respectively). Patients were stratified into four predefined subgroups. Group 1 comprised patients with deletional Hb H disease without co-inherited Hb E (n = 631, 48.6%), among whom 205 (32.5%) underwent molecular confirmation. Group 2 included deletional Hb H disease with co-inherited Hb E (n = 95, 7.3%), with molecular testing performed in 49 patients (51.6%). Group 3 consisted of non-deletional Hb H disease without Hb E (n = 414, 31.9%); 246 patients (59.4%) had molecular confirmation. Group 4 included non-deletional Hb H disease with co-inherited Hb E (n = 158, 12.2%), and 101 patients (63.9%) underwent molecular testing.

Median age varied significantly among groups, being highest in Group 1 (22 years, IQR 11-54) and lowest in Groups 2 and 3 (14 years, IQR 8-23 and 8-26, respectively, p < 0.001). The median hemoglobin level was lowest in Group 4 (8.0 g/dL; IQR 8–9, p < 0.001). Median serum ferritin levels were highest in Group 4 (532 ng/mL, IQR 324-762, p < 0.001). Splenectomy was significantly more common in non-deletional Hb H disease (8% in Group 3 and 9.5% in Group 4) compared to the deletional groups (0.8% in Group 1 and 3.1% in Group 2, p < 0.001). Transfusion dependency was notably higher in non-deletional Hb H disease, particularly in Group 4, with 71 patients (44.9%) classified as transfusion-dependent compared to 14 patients (2.2%) in Group 1 and 8 patients (8.4%) in Group 2 (p < 0.001). Disease-related complications were more frequent among non-deletional Hb H disease, notably in Group 4 (31.6%, p < 0.001). (Table 1)

Disease-related complications

The prevalence of disease-related complications varied across genotype groups. Gallstones were the most frequently observed complication, occurring more often in patients with non-deletional Hb H disease, regardless of Hb E status (57 cases), than in those with deletional Hb H disease (22 cases) (p < 0.001). Hyperuricemia was the second most common complication and was observed at similar rates across groups. Pulmonary hypertension, osteoporosis, and EMH were also more frequent in the non-deletional groups, particularly among patients with co-inherited Hb E (Group 4) (p < 0.001). Cardiovascular complications, bacterial infections, thrombosis, gout, and leg ulcers were uncommon and did not differ significantly between deletional and non-deletional Hb H disease (Table 2). In univariable analyses, Hb E co-inheritance was not significantly associated with any disease-related complication (unadjusted OR 1.2; 95% CI 0.8–1.6, p = 0.3) or hyperuricemia (unadjusted OR 0.6; 95% CI 0.2–1.3, p = 0.2), but was significantly associated with gallstones (unadjusted OR 2.0; 95% CI 1.2–3.2, p = 0.006). The univariable results for variables assessed in each outcome-specific model are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

Factors associated with the development of disease-related complications

Multivariable analysis identified increasing age, non-deletional Hb H disease, and transfusion dependency as independent factors associated with any disease-related complication. Each 10-year increase in age was associated with a 1.5-fold higher risk (AOR 1.5; 95% CI 1.3–1.6, p < 0.001). Patients with non-deletional Hb H disease had more than twice the odds of complications compared to those with deletional disease (AOR 2.3; 95% CI 1.5–3.5, p < 0.001). Transfusion dependency was also strongly associated with complications (AOR 2.7; 95% CI 1.4–5.3, p = 0.003). Splenectomy showed a marginal association after adjustment (AOR 2.0; 95% CI 0.9–4.2, p = 0.06), whereas female sex and serum ferritin were not independently associated with the outcome. Hb E co-inheritance and mean hemoglobin level were not retained in this multivariable model as they did not meet the prespecified univariable selection threshold (Supplementary Table S1). (Table 3)

Factors associated with the development of gallstones

In the multivariable analysis of gallstone formation, non-deletional Hb H disease remained independently associated with increased risk (AOR 2.8; 95% CI 1.3–5.7, p = 0.006). Splenectomy was also a strong predictor (AOR 3.8; 95% CI 1.4–10.2, p = 0.007), and higher serum ferritin, per 500 ng/mL increment, was independently associated with gallstones (AOR 2.0; 95% CI 1.1–3.7, p = 0.01). All variables that met the prespecified univariable selection threshold were included in this outcome-specific model, including age, female sex, non-deletional Hb H disease, Hb E co-inheritance, splenectomy, transfusion dependency, hemoglobin level, and serum ferritin. After adjustment, Hb E co-inheritance (AOR 0.6; 95% CI 0.2–1.3, p = 0.2), female sex, age, transfusion dependency, and hemoglobin level were not independently associated with gallstones. (Table 4)

Discussion

This large registry provides a comprehensive overview of the clinical spectrum and complication burden in patients with Hb H disease, emphasizing significant differences between deletional and non-deletional forms. Our findings demonstrate that patients with non-deletional Hb H disease, particularly those co-inheriting Hb E, exhibited significantly lower hemoglobin levels, higher ferritin levels, and increased rates of transfusion dependency and splenectomy compared to patients with deletional Hb H disease. These results, consistent with previous studies, underscore the typically more severe clinical course associated with non-deletional forms of the disease6,12–14.

Disease-related complications were more prevalent in patients with non-deletional Hb H disease, with a notably higher incidence of gallstones, EMH, osteoporosis, and pulmonary hypertension. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which have shown that most disease-related complications occur more frequently in patients with non-deletional Hb H disease compared to those with deletional Hb H disease7,13,15,16. The exclusive presence of EMH in the non-deletional group, particularly among individuals co-inheriting Hb E, suggests that ineffective erythropoiesis in these more severe phenotypes may contribute to this complication. Supporting this observation, a study on biomarkers of ineffective erythropoiesis demonstrated significantly higher levels of phosphatidylserine-exposed red blood cells (PS-exposed RBCs) and other related markers in patients with non-deletional Hb H disease compared to those with deletional forms17. Conversely, complications such as cardiovascular disease, thrombosis, and infections were uncommon and showed no significant differences between the two forms of Hb H disease. These findings may be explained by the relatively young median age of the cohort compared to the typical age at onset for these complications. A previous longitudinal cohort study indicated that the median age at the onset of cardiovascular complications in patients with Hb H/Hb H-Constant Spring disease with the Hb E mutation and in those with Hb H/Hb H-Constant Spring disease was 56 years and 59 years, respectively13.

In the adjusted analyses, increasing age, non-deletional genotype, and transfusion dependency were independent predictors of overall complications, while splenectomy showed a marginal association. For gallstones specifically, non-deletional Hb H disease, splenectomy, and higher ferritin level remained independently associated with risk after adjustment for other significant univariable factors. Hb E co-inheritance and female sex were associated with gallstones in univariable analyses, but these associations were no longer evident in the multivariable model. These findings suggest that the higher observed gallstone burden among patients with Hb E co-inheritance may be partly explained by related disease-severity features, including non-deletional genotype, splenectomy, transfusion exposure, lower hemoglobin level, and iron burden, rather than by Hb E co-inheritance alone. The results also support the concept that different complications in Hb H disease may arise through partly distinct mechanisms.

Gallstones, the most frequent complications, were strongly associated with both splenectomy and elevated ferritin levels, consistent with prior prospective study indicating that splenectomy increases the risk of gallstones8. These results support the role of chronic hemolysis and iron overload in the development of cholelithiasis. Iron-related hepatic complications are also clinically relevant in adult patients with Hb H disease, particularly those with non-deletional forms, supporting continued monitoring for iron-related organ injury18. Hyperuricemia, the second most common complication in this cohort, was prevalent, although symptomatic gout was rare, and only a small proportion of patients required treatment. While data regarding the prevalence of hyperuricemia in alpha-thalassemia is more limited in comparison to beta-thalassemia, the contributing factors are similar, including ineffective erythropoiesis, chronic hemolysis, iron overload, and reduced renal function19,20. In the present cohort, hyperuricemia was found to be associated with increasing age and higher hemoglobin levels, which may reflect both increased cell turnover and the renal excretion burden associated with more active hematopoiesis9,21.

Although genotype-based risk stratification may inform surveillance strategies, the retrospective design of this study does not allow conclusions regarding the impact of earlier intervention on long-term morbidity. As with all observational registry-based analyses, causal inferences cannot be established.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective data collection may underestimate subclinical complications and does not permit detailed evaluation of disease progression over time. In addition, molecular confirmation was performed in only a subset of patients; therefore, rare non-deletional variants without distinct electrophoretic patterns may have been under-recognized. Although all variables meeting the prespecified univariable selection threshold were included in the corresponding multivariable models, the numbers of patients with Hb E co-inheritance and the numbers of events for some complication-specific outcomes were limited. Accordingly, adjusted estimates for Hb E co-inheritance and other variables in the gallstone and hyperuricemia models should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when clinical severity markers overlap. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether genotype-directed monitoring or earlier therapeutic intervention can modify long-term outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this large multicenter registry provides valuable real-world insight into the burden and spectrum of disease-related complications in patients with Hb H disease.

In conclusion, this nationwide registry highlights a clinically meaningful burden of complications in Hb H disease, particularly among patients with non-deletional forms. Increasing age, non-deletional Hb H disease, and transfusion dependency were independent predictors of overall complications, with splenectomy showing a marginal association. For gallstones, non-deletional Hb H disease, splenectomy, and higher ferritin level were independent predictors. These findings emphasize the importance of genotype-informed risk assessment and individualized management strategies to reduce disease-related morbidity in patients with Hb H disease.

Methods

This study employed data from a nationwide, web-based registry specifically designed for patients with Hb H disease in Thailand. The registry was established and overseen by the Red Blood Cell Disorders and Aplastic Anemia Committee, with additional support from the Thai Society of Hematology. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of each participating center prior to patient enrollment at that center, in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Boards, as the study posed no more than minimal risk to participants. Participants were recruited from 14 hematology centers across nine major university-affiliated hospitals, encompassing both pediatric (nine units) and adult (five units) hematology divisions. The hospitals included were Ramathibodi Hospital, Siriraj Hospital, Chulalongkorn Hospital, and Phramongkutklao Hospital in Bangkok; Srinagarind Hospital in Khon Kaen; Songklanagarind Hospital in Songkhla; Chiang Mai University Hospital in Chiang Mai; Thammasat University Hospital in Pathum Thani; and the Queen Sirikit National Institute of Child Health in Bangkok. Patient enrollment occurred between December 2022 and December 2023.

Eligibility criteria required patients to be diagnosed with Hb H disease, with or without co-inheritance of the Hb E mutation. Both newly diagnosed and previously diagnosed patients under follow-up were eligible. The diagnosis of Hb H disease was primarily based on hemoglobin analysis using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) or capillary electrophoresis at each participating center. The presence of Hb H, with or without detectable Hb Bart’s, together with compatible red blood cell indices and clinical features of chronic hemolytic anemia, was considered diagnostic. Co-inherited Hb E was identified by its characteristic peak on hemoglobin analysis. Molecular testing was performed when electrophoretic findings were unclear or when additional confirmation was clinically indicated. However, molecular analysis was not performed in all patients. Therefore, rare non-deletional α-thalassemia variants that do not produce distinct electrophoretic patterns may have been under-recognized, introducing the possibility of limited misclassification. Nonetheless, given that Hb H–Constant Spring is the predominant non-deletional form in Thailand, the overall impact on genotype classification is likely modest.

Thalassemia information

Medical histories of thalassemia and red blood cell (RBC) transfusions were collected, including age (defined as age at registry enrollment), age at first diagnosis, age at first transfusion, history of splenectomy, RBC transfusion frequency, and transfusion-related complications. Transfusion-dependent thalassemia was defined as patients requiring seven or more RBC transfusions per year, with the remaining classified as non-transfusion-dependent.

Disease-related complications

Disease-related co-morbidities included cardiovascular complications, pulmonary hypertension, extramedullary hematopoiesis, osteoporosis, fractures, bacterial infections, gallstones, thrombosis, and leg ulcers, as previously defined in Thai transfusion-dependent thalassemia studies.22,23 Hyperuricemia was defined as serum uric acid levels above the normal range. Gout was diagnosed based on the criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology24.

Laboratory investigations

The diagnosis of Hb H disease was confirmed through hemoglobin analysis and/or DNA analysis. Laboratory data included mean pre-transfusion hemoglobin levels over the previous six months and mean serum ferritin levels from the past year.

Data collection and management were conducted using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform25, hosted by the Faculty of Medicine, Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate disease-related complications in Thai patients diagnosed with Hb H disease.

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on their distribution.

Baseline characteristics across the four genotype groups were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate. The primary outcome was the presence of at least one disease-related complication. Univariable logistic regression analyses were first performed to explore potential associations between clinical variables and three outcomes: overall disease-related complications, gallstones, and hyperuricemia. The univariable results for variables assessed in each outcome-specific model are provided in Supplementary Tables S1-S3. For multivariable logistic regression, variables were selected based on the prespecified univariable screening threshold (p-value < 0.10), biological plausibility, and model stability. Variables meeting this threshold were entered into the corresponding outcome-specific multivariable model. Hb E co-inheritance was evaluated because its potential impact was one of the study objectives; it was included in the gallstone model because it met the univariable selection criterion, but it was not forced into the models for overall complications or hyperuricemia, for which the univariable associations were not statistically significant. Age was analyzed per 10-year increment and serum ferritin per 500 ng/mL increment to improve interpretability. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
