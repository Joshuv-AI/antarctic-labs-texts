Successful Bortezomib–Lenalidomide–Dexamethasone Therapy in a 72‐Year‐Old Patient With Sporadic Late‐Onset Nemaline Myopathy Associated With Monoclonal Gammopathy of Muscle Significance

Case Report

Sporadic late‐onset nemaline myopathy associated with monoclonal gammopathy of muscle significance (SLONM‐MGMS) is a rare disorder that may be fatal if untreated [1]. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) and autologous peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (auto‐PBSCT) are currently considered to be first‐ and second‐line therapies, respectively, for this condition and are often initiated in a stepwise manner [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, IVIg is not effective for every patient, and the safety of auto‐PBSCT remains uncertain in some patients, such as those aged over 70 years and those with a performance status (PS) greater than 2 (assessed using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scale [5]) or with multiple comorbidities.

In multiple myeloma, with the development of novel agents, various standard chemotherapy regimens such as bortezomib–lenalidomide–dexamethasone (VRd) have been established [6]. In previous reports, chemotherapy has been used as a second‐line treatment after auto‐PBSCT or as an alternative option in patients younger than 70 years with severe comorbidities [7, 8].

The patient was a 72‐year‐old male without relevant past or family history of myopathy. At the age of 68 years, he gradually developed progressive limb atrophy, dysphagia, difficulty lifting his arms above his head and gait disturbance characterized by difficulty firmly planting his feet when walking downhill, and some difficulty arising from a seated position. His medical history included hypertrophic cardiomyopathy diagnosed 1 year before presentation. Physical examination revealed marked lumbar lordosis, a positive Gowers sign, and asymmetric proximal muscle weakness (shoulder abduction: MRC [Medical Research Council manual muscle testing scale; right/left] 4/3, elbow flexion: MRC 3/3, hip flexion: MRC 3/3, knee extension: MRC 4/2). Distal muscle strength was normal in all limbs. Sensory and motor nerve conduction studies were normal in all limbs. Needle electromyography revealed fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves at rest. Motor unit action potentials were of large amplitude and long duration, with early recruitment. Blood examination showed normal serum creatine kinase (116 U/L), and inflammatory markers were within normal limits (C‐reactive protein 0.58 mg/dL, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 26 mm/h). A myositis‐associated antibody panel (anti‐Jo‐1, PL‐7, PL‐12, EJ, KS, OJ, Zo, Ha, SRP, Mi‐2, TIF1‐γ, TIF1‐α, TIF1‐β, NXP‐2, SAE, SMN, U1‐RNP_70, U1‐RNP_A, U1‐RNP_C, U2‐RNP, Ku, PM‐Scl100, PM‐Scl75, RuvBL1/2, Ki, SS‐A/Ro52, SS‐A/Ro60, SS‐B antibodies) was negative, except for an isolated elevation of anti–cN1A antibody. Serum immunofixation electrophoresis demonstrated an IgG‐κ monoclonal gammopathy. Magnetic resonance imaging showed asymmetric muscle atrophy and fatty degeneration, most prominent in the sternocleidomastoid, infraspinatus, subscapularis, paraspinal, thigh adductor and biceps femoris muscles. A biopsy of the left vastus lateralis muscle demonstrated features of SLONM (Figure 1). The patient received a single course of intravenous immunoglobulin at a dose of 0.4 g/kg per day for 5 days, without objective improvement of muscle strength. The patient did not demonstrate hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, anemia or bone pathology. Bone marrow examination revealed no evidence of plasma cell neoplasia (plasma cells 1.6%), arguing against multiple myeloma. On this basis, the diagnosis of SLONM‐MGMS was established. His spirometry showed decreased to a forced vital capacity of 85% of predicted, compared to a value of 98% 1 year prior.

Pathology of the left vastus medialis muscle biopsy. (A) Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed muscle fiber size variation and increased internalized nuclei. (B) Modified Gömöri trichrome staining showed numerous nemaline rods. Some atrophic fibers were filled with “sand‐like” nemaline rods.

The patient was considered a poor candidate for auto‐PBSCT because of advanced age, a PS of 3, and coexisting hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. After approval by the institutional ethics and research committees (2025004CL), the patient received VRd therapy (bortezomib 1.3 mg/m2/dose, lenalidomide 25 mg/day, dexamethasone 40 mg/dose) for a total of three treatment cycles, each administered at 4 week intervals. His muscle weakness and posture improved markedly, with complete recovery of strength in the upper limbs (Figure 2). Moreover, the M‐protein became undetectable after the third course, and VRd therapy was discontinued. The main adverse event was distal limb dysesthesias, consistent with chemotherapy‐induced peripheral neuropathy. His neurological examination improved gradually, with preserved proximal muscle strength and no new deficits for 10 months after treatment.

Summary of clinical course and treatments. Asymmetric proximal weakness gradually progressed over several years, followed by substantial improvement after initiation of the VRd regimen, in parallel with the disappearance of the M‐protein. Discussion

In chronic myopathies, such as inclusion body myositis (IBM), large‐amplitude motor unit potentials can also be observed, which may have contributed to the EMG findings in this case [9]. Although anti–cN1A antibodies are frequently associated with IBM, the clinical phenotype and muscle pathology in this patient were not consistent with IBM, and the antibody finding was interpreted as not clinically relevant [9].

In oncology, PS is widely used as an overall measure of a patient's physiological reserve and tolerance for intensive therapies. In SLONM‐MGMS, however, severe axial and proximal weakness can outwardly worsen PS scores, leading to a potential overestimation of treatment‐related risk. At the same time, the genuine risks of transplantation in older patients must be acknowledged. Moreover, in other B cell–mediated neuromuscular disorders, such as AL amyloidosis and POEMS (polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal protein, and skin changes) syndrome, treatment largely consists of modified anti–multiple myeloma regimens tailored to the individual patient's clinical profile [10]. Optimization of anti–SLONM‐MGMS treatment, including daratumumab aided therapy, also warrants further investigation. This case highlights an alternative therapeutic strategy that may offer a viable option for selected patients in an increasingly aging global population.
