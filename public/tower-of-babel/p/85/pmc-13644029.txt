CD133 structural instability as a gateway to medulloblastoma vulnerability

AbstractBackgroundMedulloblastoma (MB) is the most common malignant paediatric brain tumor. Among distinct MB subtypes, Group 3 represents one of the most aggressive subtypes, with high relapse rate, frequent metastasis, and mortality. These malignant traits are largely driven by MB stem cells (MBSCs), a subpopulation with enhanced self-renewal capacity and resistance to standard therapies, making them prime targets for innovative treatment strategies.MethodsGroup 3 MB human cell lines were treated with 0.3 µM Brefeldin A (BFA), a potent inducer of endoplasmic reticulum stress. Proteomic analyses via nano-LC-MS/MS were employed to investigate structural alterations in CD133, a key cell surface and stemness-associated receptor. Background

Medulloblastoma (MB) is the most frequent pediatric malignant brain tumor and a leading cause of cancer-related death in children. MB arises in the cerebellum and has been molecularly divided into 4-principal molecular subgroups: WNT, SHH, Group 3 (G3), and Group 4 (G4) [1], further divided into more subtypes following large scale methylation and transcriptome profiling [2] (Cavalli et al., 2017). Aggressive multimodal therapies, specifically surgical resection followed by radiotherapy and systemic chemotherapy, still represent the first-line treatment for MB [3]. However, patient outcomes vary widely, with the G3MB generally associated with the poorest prognosis and a higher likelihood of recurrence. G3MB is indeed recognized as the deadliest subtype with the 5-year overall survival (OS) spanning from the 66.2% and 55.8% for the G3α and G3β subtypes, respectively, and the 41.9% for the G3γ, the subtype with the least favourable prognosis among all the G3 subtypes [2], although its biological background remains poorly understood.

G3MB is characterized by a significant proportion of undifferentiated and progenitor-like cells known as MB stem cells (MBSCs), which are key players in tumor biology because of their significant self-renewal ability and resistance to conventional therapies, contributing to the high relapse rate seen in G3MB [4–7].

Additionally, MBSCs are implicated in two major challenges in the treatment of G3MB: post-surgical residual disease and leptomeningeal dissemination (LMD), making of particular interest the selective targeting of this cells’ subpopulation [7].

To date, therapeutic approaches targeting MBSCs are poor and ineffective [8], largely because cancer stem cells (CSCs) adopt several mechanisms to maintain homeostasis and adapt to adverse environments. One of these mechanisms is the activation of a stress-adaptation pathway, the Unfolded Protein Response (UPR), downstream the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress initiation [9, 10].

However, the UPR overactivation may lead to a loss of cells’ viability, leading to the hypothesis that ER stress-inducing drugs can be used as powerful tools in anti-cancer therapies [11]. In this study, we selected a well-known ER stress inducer, Brefeldin A (BFA) [12, 13], to gain insight into the molecular mechanisms through which the ER stress exacerbation may impair MBSCs stemness properties. Noteworthy, BFA has already been studied for its anticancer [14–16] and anti-stemness [17, 18] ability in cancer of diverse origin; however, to date, no data have been reported in the context of G3MB.

Leveraging mass spectrometry, we provide the first evidence of a novel structural alteration in CD133, a critical marker of stemness and with tumor-promoting potential. This alteration impacts not only its localization on the cell surface and its release via extracellular vesicles (EVs) but also the activation of downstream signaling pathways. However, the clinical translation of BFA, which initially revealed these effects, has been hindered by its unfavorable physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties. To overcome these limitations and enhance clinical applicability, we employed a novel bioinformatic approach that developed an algorithm to identify, among others, Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) approved compounds capable of mimicking BFA’s mechanism of action (MoA). These candidates exhibit significantly improved pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles, offering a promising avenue to harness BFA-like anticancer effects for MB treatment—potentially opening new therapeutic perspectives and renewed hope for affected children.

Methods

Cell culture and treatments

G3MB cells (CHLA-01, D283-Med and D341-Med) were routinely grown accordingly to the recommended conditions (ATCC, VA, USA), in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. For stem cells enrichment, cells were cultured in a selective medium containing DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 0.6% glucose, 25 µg/mL insulin, 60 mg/mL N-acetyl-L-cysteine, 20 ng/mL EGF, 20 ng/mL basic FGF, penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), 2 mg/mL heparin (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA) and B27 supplement without vitamin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 72 h. Then, 500.000 cells were seeded onto 35-mm diameter dishes and the day after cells were treated with BFA (Sellekchem, MA, USA; stock solution in DMSO) at 0.3 µM as final concentration, following previous studies [15, 19]. An equal volume of DMSO was used as vehicle control.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Cells were lysed by using RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 1 × protease and 1 × phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein content was quantified using Pierce™ BCA protein assay kits (Thermo Fisher). Protein lysates were subjected to electrophoresis on 4–12% NuPage Bis–Tris gels (Life-Technologies). The gels were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes in Tris–glycine buffer. The membranes were blocked in PBS-0.1% Tween 20 solution containing 5% milk, probed with antibodies and visualized by chemiluminescence (Chemidoc™ Imaging System, BioRad). Primary antibodies are the following: CD133 (Abcam, ab19898, 1:1000), 1:1000), #2772, 1:1000), p-AKT ser473 (Cell Signaling, #4060, 1:1000), Sox2 (Genetex, GTX627404, 1:500), #10628D), Tubulin (Abcam, ab6046, 1:1000), Actin (Cell Signaling, #8457, 1:1000), GAPDH (Cell Signaling #3683); AKT (Cell Signaling, 9272, 1:1000), p-MTOR (Cell Signaling, 5536P, 1:1000), m-TOR (Cell Signaling, 2983P, 1:1000), Hsp70 (Abcam, #ab37455, 1:500), Calnexin (Merck, #AB2301, 1:500); Sil 1 (Sigma #HPA011949, 1:1000), Calreticulin (Stressgen,#SPA-606, 1:1000) and MIA-3 (Sigma #HPA055922, 1:1000).

Bright field microscopy

Bright field images were acquired using an inverted optical microscope Motic AE-2000 (10X/0,25 NA) (Motic Europe, BCN, ES).

Clonogenic assay

G3MBSCs were treated with BFA or DMSO for 48 h. After that, cells were disaggregated to single cell (Accutase solution, Sigma-Aldrich), resuspended in medium without drug/vehicle and plated at low density (102 cells/well) into 96-well plates. Oncospheres were counted after 10–14 days.

Immunofluorescence

After treatment, 120.000 cells were collected and spotted onto a poly-L-lysine coated glass and fixed for 10 min with paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma-Aldrich). For membrane staining the Image-iT LIVE Plasma Membrane and Nuclear Labeling Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, I34406) was employed, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Then, cell membranes were permeabilized with a solution of 0.1% Triton-X 100 for 10 min at room temperature (RT) followed by incubation with blocking solution (5% donkey serum in phosphate buffered saline, PBS). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight (O/N) at 4 °C in blocking solution and the day after stained with the proper Alexa Fluor secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with 2-(4-amidinophenyl)−1 H-indole-6-carboxamidine (DAPI). The primary antibody employed was the following: CD133 (Abcam, #ab19898,1:100).

Images were acquired using a FV1200 MPE laser scanning (Olympus, JA) with a UPlanSAPO 60 ×/0.75 NA and a UPlanSAPO 40x/0.30 NA oil immersion objectives. Final acquisitions were processed by using ImageJ software.

N-glycosydase F (PNGaseF), endoglycosidase H (EndoH) and alpha 2–3,6,8 neuraminidase enzymatic processing

Removal of the N-linked was carried out on 20 µg cell extract with PNGase F (New England Biolabs Inc., NEB, MA, USA) and Endo H (NEB). Removal of sialic acid residues was performed by using alpha 2–3,6,8 Neuraminidase (NEB), according to the manufacturer’s specification. After enzymatic treatment, samples were boiled with NuPage LDS sample buffer (Invitrogen) and NuPage Reducing Agent (Invitrogen), resolved on NuPage Bis–Tris gels and immunoblotted for CD133 protein.

Datasets and in-silico analysis

Gene expression data were retrieved using the R2 genomics platform (https://hgserver1.amc.nl/cgi-bin/r2/main.cgi), including the Cavalli (GSE85217; n = 763) and Williamson (E-MTAB-10767; n = 331) primary MB datasets.

Data were downloaded using the data grabber function for each subgroup (Cavalli dataset: 70 samples for WNT, 223 for SHH, 144 for G3 and, 326 for G4; Williamson dataset: 31 samples for WNT, 67 for SHH, 63 for G3, and 147 for G4). CD133 expression levels were log2-transformed. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As CD133 expression did not follow a normal distribution, differences across subgroups were evaluated using a non-parametric test (Kruskal-Wallis test), followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Additionally, survival analysis was performed using the R2 genomics platform on both datasets. Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical differences in OS among G3MB patients were assessed computing p-values of the log-rank test.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and 1D-electrophoresis

CD133 protein was immunoprecipitated from 500 µg of cell lysates in RIPA buffer. IP was carried out using Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10003D), according to manufacturer’s specifications. Briefly, 2.5 µg of anti-CD133 (Abcam, #ab284389) antibody was incubated with Dynabeads for 1 h in rotation at RT, then 500 µg of cell lysate was incubated in gentle rotation at 4 °C O/N. After washing with a solution of 0.02% Tween 20 in PBS, the elution of the target antigen was conducted by denaturation, following manufacturer’s instructions.

40 µg of immunoprecipitated proteins (untreated controls and treated with BFA and PNGase F) were loaded onto 12% acrylamide resolving gel and subjected to 1D-electrophoresis, as previously reported [20]. Each gel lane was cut in 16 bands, washed twice with wash buffer (25 mM NH4HCO3 in 50% acetonitrile, ACN), reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT, 45 min, 56 °C) and alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide (30 min at RT in the dark). After two washes with the washing buffer, protein bands were completely dried in a centrivap vacuum centrifuge. Then the dried pieces of gel were rehydrated for 30 min at 4 °C in a porcine trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) solution (3 ng/µL in 100 mM NH4HCO3) and incubated O/N at 37 °C. The reaction was quenched by adding 10% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). The solutions of extracted peptides were stored at −20 °C until analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

Immunoprecipitated proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and CD133 was detected by western blotting. Briefly, immunoprecipitated protein samples were separated by 10% acrylamide gel and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were blocked with TBS containing 0.02% Tween 20 and 3% of non-fat dry milk and incubated for 2 h at RT with the primary anti-CD133 antibody (Abcam, #ab19898, Fig. 3a) diluted 1:1000 or the primary anti-CD133 antibody, recognizing the N-terminal end of the protein (Biorbyt, Cat. #orb766797, Fig. 3c). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Enzo Life Sciences, Inc., NY, USA) was used as secondary antisera at a 1:10.000 dilution. Immunoblots were developed using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, the chemiluminescent images were acquired using LAS 4010 (GE HealthCare, IL, USA), and the immunoreactive specific bands were quantified using ImageQuant L software.

Cell lysates were also treated with PNGase to confirm that the CD133 isoform detected after BFA treatment was not the deglycosylated one. (b) The MS analysis revealed that the lower molecular weight band of CD133 corresponds to an isoform lacking the N-terminal region, which corresponds to the extracellular domain (pink) cartoon description. (c) CD133 was immunoprecipitated after 24 h with/without 0.3 µM BFA and detected by using a primary antibody recognizing the N-terminal end of the protein. Loading control: IgG

MS analysis of gel bands

Each band was analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a UltiMate3000 RSLCnano (Thermo Fisher Scientific) chromatographic system coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer, operating in positive ionization mode, equipped with a nanoESI source (EASY-Spray NG) (ThermoFisher Scientific). Peptides were loaded on a PepMap100 C18 pre-column cartridge (5 μm particle size, 100 Å pore size, 300 μm i.d. × 5 mm length, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and subsequently separated on an EASY-Spray PepMap RSLC C18 column (2 μm particle size, 100 Å pore size, 75 μm i.d. × 15 cm length, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min and a temperature of 35 °C.

EVs isolation by ultrafiltration and characterization by human tetraspanin assay

After BFA treatment, cell culture media were collected and EVs were isolated by ultrafiltration with Amicon Ultracel-100 K tubes (Sigma Aldrich), following manufacturer’s specifications. The collected supernatant was subjected to differential centrifugations at 4 °C, as follows: 300g for 5 min; 500g for 5 min; 1200g for 20 min; and 3900g for 30 min. The final supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm filter. The filtrate was then concentrated by using Amicon Ultracel-100 K tubes, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fraction obtained was diluted 1:37 (vol/vol) with PBS.

The resulting preparation was resuspended in NuPage LDS sample buffer and NuPage Reducing Agent and analyzed by Western blotting.

Isolated EVs were characterized by employing single-particle interferometric reflectance imaging sensor (SP-IRIS) technology (to measure size and count particles) combined with a microscope (to detect fluorescently labelled antibodies), provided by Leprechaun platform. EVs preparation was diluted (1:100) in an incubation solution and then spotted onto capture chips provided in the Leprechaun Exosome Human Tetraspanin Kit (Leprechaun, Unchained Labs, Brighton, MA, USA). CD63, CD81, CD9, and Mouse IgG capture antibodies were by default coated onto chip to capture EVs. Incubation with fluorescently labelled detection antibodies and subsequent washing steps were conducted following manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the chips were dried for 30 min at RT and scanned. Results represent the mean of triplicate spots, representative of technical replicates within the same analyzed sample. Fluorescent intensity (arbitrary units, a.u.) cut-off values for detection antibodies were defined using the isotype control (IgG) spots. Data were analyzed with Leprechaun Analyzer software (Unchained Labs, CA, USA) with sizing thresholds set to 35–200 nm diameter.

Shotgun proteomics of total cell lysate

Total protein content was measured through the BCA assay, following manufacturer’s instructions. A volume corresponding to 50 µg of proteins was loaded onto a Nanosep 10-kDa-cutoff filter (Pall Corporation, MI, USA) and digested according to our protocol [21]. Briefly, the sample was washed twice with 200 µL urea buffer (8 M urea, 100 mM Tris pH 8.5 in milliQ water) to remove salts and detergents. Proteins were reduced by adding 100 µL of DTT solution (8 mM DTT in urea buffer) and alkylated, and 100 µL of Iodoacetamide (IAA) solution (50 mM IAA in urea buffer). The urea buffer was exchanged with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, before adding trypsin to a ratio of 1:50 (enzyme: proteins). The digestion was incubated overnight at 37 °C, and the mixture of peptides was collected by centrifugation, acidified with 10% TFA and stored at −20 °C until analysis. Each digested protein sample was analyzed in technical triplicate by LC-MS/MS using the same system as above. The chromatographic gradient was as follows: from 95% eluent A (0.1% Formic Acid (FA) in water) to 35% eluent B (99.9% ACN, 0.1% FA) in 45 min and total LC run of 65 min. Precursor (MS1) survey scans were recorded in the Orbitrap, at resolving powers of 120 K (at m/z 200). Data-dependent MS/MS (MS2) analysis was performed in top speed mode with a 3 s cycle time, during which most abundant multiple-charged (2+–7+) precursor ions detected within the range of 375–1500 m/z were selected for activation in order of abundance and detected in ion trap at rapid scan rate. Quadrupole isolation with a 1.6 m/z isolation window was used, and dynamic exclusion was enabled for 60 s after a single scan. Automatic gain control targets were 4.0 × 105 for MS1 and 2.0 × 103 for MS2, with 50 and 300 ms maximum injection times, respectively. For MS2, the signal intensity threshold was 5.0 × 103, and the option “Injection Ions for All Available Parallelizable Time” was set. High-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was performed using 30% normalized collision energy.

Bioinformatics pipeline

Raw data were processed using PEAKS studio Xpro (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Ontario, Canada) using the ‘correct precursor only’ option. Gel bands raw data were processed and spectra were matched against the UniProt SwissProt database restricted to Homo sapiens taxonomy, to which a list of common contaminants was appended (20,619 entries). False discovery rate (FDR) was set to 0.1% at the peptide-level. The post-translational modification (PTM) profile was set as follows: carbamidomethylation of cysteines (ΔMass: 57.02), and possible oxidation of methionine (ΔMass: 15.99). Non-specific cleavage was allowed to one end of the peptides, with a maximum of 2 missed cleavages and 2 PTMs per peptide, and trypsin enzyme specificity. The highest error mass tolerances for precursors and fragments were set at 15 ppm and 0.5 Da, respectively. The total cell lysates raw data were processed using PEAKS studio v.13 using the same parameters as above for the database search, and setting the protein FDR to 1.0%. Subsequently, an identification directed quantification (LFQ) was performed to detect differentially expressed proteins. Parameters for LFQ were set as follows: Mass Error Tolerance: 15 ppm; Retention Time Shift Tolerance: Auto; Feature intensity ³ 5,000. Samples were allotted in 4 groups (D283_CTRL, D283_BrefA, D341_CTRL and D341_BrefA) and the significance of the differences was assessed by ANOVA test with a protein-level FDR (adjusted p-value) threshold of 0.1%, a fold change ≥ 2, and at least one peptide identified. The intensities were normalized on the Total Ion Current (TIC). The protein list with protein name and expression values was manually curated to remove keratins and matches from the contaminant database, and to keep only the proteins identified with at least 2 peptides and showing a similar modulation trend in both cell lines.

Bioinformatic analysis was performed through the BigOmics Playground platform (https://bigomics.ch/) version v3.3.4.9001-master240426. The significantly altered proteins (as determined by the LFQ module of PEAKS Studio) were uploaded to the platform selecting proteomics type of analysis and “homo sapiens” species. The Analysis methods were set as follows: Enrichment methods: fgsea, fisher, gsva - Gene tests: ttest, trend.limma ‐ Extra analysis: WGCNA, cell type deconvolution, drug connectivity, wordcloud. The drug connectivity module provides a score and associate p-value: if the score is positive the drug mimics the gene signature (may reinforce the condition) if the score is negative the drug reverses the gene signature (potential therapeutic candidate). For the present study, drugs with positive score from the Drug Connectivity analysis, could replace BFA and potentially be tested as novel therapeutic agent.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± S.D or as fold increase vs. untreated cells and are representative of at least three independent experiments. Unpaired t-test was performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

BFA modifies tumor sphere morphology and affects stemness markers expression

According to the CSC hypothesis, effective treatments for cancer should target CSCs [22, 23] thus, to explore the effects of BFA on the tumor-sphere forming and stem-like properties capacity, three G3MB cell lines (D283, D341 and CHLA) were grown in serum-free stem cell permissive medium [24, 25] for 72 h and treated with 0.3 µM BFA for 24 h [15, 19]. BFA induced a marked alteration in tumor sphere morphology, from a typical compact, spherical architecture to a fragmented, amorphous structure (18), thereby compromising the tumor sphere integrity (Fig. 1a). To validate the impairment of stem-like features, we evaluated the effect of BFA on clonogenic ability and protein expression levels of two widely used stem cell markers, SOX2 and CD133 (Prominin-1) [26, 27]. BFA treatment induced a significant reduction in oncospheres formation and in SOX2 levels, and a shift of the CD133 expected band from 133 to 110 kDa, suggesting the induction of a PTM (Fig. 1b, c and d).

BFA treatment modifies tumor sphere morphology and clonogenicity and affects stemness markers expression (a) D283, D341 and CHLA were treated for 24 h with 0.3 µM BFA and the tumor sphere morphology was evaluated. Bars indicate 200 μm. (b) Clonogenic assay (expressed as percentage of oncospheres) of D283, D341 and CHLA cells performed after BFA treatment. (c) SOX2 and CD133 protein levels were evaluated. (d) Densitometric analyses of SOX2 and CD133 lower band (110 kDa) are shown for each cell line. Data represent means ± S.D., from at least three independent experiments. * p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01 vs. DMSO. Loading control = Tubulin

These results underline that BFA attenuates the stem-like behaviour of G3MBSCs and induces a PTM of CD133.

BFA treatment induces the cleavage of the N-terminal portion of CD133

The reduction in cellular clonogenicity (Fig. 1a) together with the appearance of a lower MW isoform of CD133 (Fig. 1c) suggests a possible impairment of CD133 functionality, as a consequence of BFA exposure. Thus, with the aim to dissect the role of CD133 in G3MB, we interrogated the Williamson (331 samples) and the Cavalli (763 samples) datasets to assess CD133 expression and its association with overall survival (OS) in MB patients. In the Williamson dataset, reported in Fig. 2a, a Kruskal-Wallis (p < 0.0001) followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test (p-adjusted < 0.0001) was performed, showing a statistically significant difference in CD133 expression between G3MB and G4MB patients (Fig. 2a). Moreover, high expression of CD133 was negatively associated with OS in G3MB patients (Log-rank test with Bonferroni correction, adjusted p < 0.05 for Williamson dataset Fig. 2b). As stated above, we also analysed the Cavalli dataset, in which the expression of CD133 showed a statistically significant difference across all the molecular subgroups and G3MB (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.0001 and Dunn’s post-hoc test, p-adjusted < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. 1a), while the OS showed a worst trend in G3MB patients, although not reaching a statistically significant difference (p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1b), probably due to the small size of the high-expression CD133 group, which may affect the reliability of survival estimates.

Williamson dataset was interrogated for CD133 expression, its association with OS in G3MB patients and its glycosylation and sialylation profile was evaluated after enzymatic digestion in G3MBSCs. (a) CD133 expression among different subgroups. **** p < 0.001 G4MB vs. G3MB (b) CD133 expression is correlated with the OS probability of G3MB patients, p-value = 0.036. (c, d) CD133 was analyzed on G3MB cells treated with 0.3 µM BFA for 24 h in the absence/presence of EndoH and/or PNGase (c) or α-2,3-neuraminidase (d). Loading control: GAPDH

CD133 is a heavily glycosylated cell surface protein, and previous studies have demonstrated that its glycosylation and sialylation status may play a critical role in CSCs maintenance [28, 29]. To determine whether BFA acted on CD133 glycosylated and/or sialylated residues in MBSCs, we treated G3MBSCs with EndoH and/or PNGase enzymes to assess glycosylation and with α−2,3,6,8-neuraminidase to evaluate sialylation. These enzymatic digestions revealed that the MW of CD133 following BFA exposure did not correspond to those observed after EndoH, PNGase, and α−2,3,6,8-neuraminidase digestions (Fig. 2c and d). These findings effectively exclude the possibility that BFA-mediated changes are due to altered glycosylation or sialylation patterns.

To gain insight into the changes induced by BFA on the molecular structure of CD133, we isolated the protein by IP in the absence and presence of BFA and characterized its biochemical features using MS. In detail, we used 1D-PAGE to separate immunoprecipitated proteins, and, after separation, 16 gel bands per lane were cut and analyzed by MS. Specifically, we focused on the band corresponding to the canonical mass of CD133 and to the adjacent band at lower mass (Fig. 3a). As reported in Fig. 3b, no peptides were identified for the N-terminal region of CD133 in BFA treated samples, highlighting that the isoform observed at 110 kDa is the protein lacking the N-terminal end. To validate this finding, we performed immunoblotting using an antibody specifically targeting the CD133 N-terminus (Fig. 3c). In BFA-treated cells, we observed a single CD133 band with markedly reduced intensity compared to untreated cells, the conclusion that the different migratory profile of CD133 specifically results from the loss of the N-terminal region, as indicated by MS.

Collectively, these results highlight for the first time that CD133 expression is negatively correlated with OS in G3MB patients, further establishing it as a marker of high-grade, aggressive tumors [27, 30], and that BFA treatment triggers the cleavage of the CD133 N-terminal domain, suggesting a possible functional alteration of this surface receptor.

Shotgun proteomics of BFA-treated G3MBSCs reveals unexpected biological effects

To further investigate the biological impact of BFA on MBSCs, we performed shotgun label-free proteomic profiling to identify deregulated molecular pathways. G3MBSCs (D283 and D341cell lines) were processed using the FASP protocol, followed by trypsin digestion, as detailed in the Materials and Methods. With a peptide-level FDR of 0.5%, approximately 3,000 proteins were identified per technical replicate. LFQ analysis revealed 1,657 protein groups differentially modulated between BFA-treated and control cells. To validate the reliability of the proteomic data, we confirmed the expression changes of three randomly selected proteins via western blot (Supplementary Fig. 2). Following data cleaning, a refined list of 823 protein entries was obtained (Supplementary file 1) and submitted to the BigOmics platform for bioinformatic analysis.

The hierarchical clustering performed at the gene set level with a single-sample enrichment value computed from the gene expression data using GSVA, returned 4 significance modules from both cell lines. Specifically, we focused on the Hallmark Gene Sets, since they summarize and represent specific well-defined biological states or processes. The first module (S1) consists of hallmarks activated after BFA treatment and includes apoptosis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and protein secretion; while the second module (S2) consists of hallmarks inhibited by the treatment and includes PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, MYC targets and mTORC1 signaling (Fig. 4a and b).

Of note, one of the most significantly inhibited signaling pathway, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, is known as a key oncogenic pathway whose hyperactivation is described in several cancers, among which MB [31]. In addition, CD133 has been shown to promote the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, particularly in CSCs and progenitor cells [32], being therefore very interesting the study of their connection in our context.

With this in mind, we evaluated the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling in the presence and/or absence of BFA exposure both in D823 and D341 cell line. AKT phosphorylation (on serine 473), together with the phosphorylation of the downstream effector mTOR (on serine 2448) were significantly down-regulated following BFA treatment (Fig. 4c and d), suggesting that the structural modification of CD133 may have a role in the impairment of this signaling pathway activation. Additionally, we evaluated the phosphorylation of S62-cMYC (pS62-cMYC), marker of cell proliferation, and of the cleaved form of PARP (cPARP), a valuable apoptotic marker, demonstrating a significantly reduction of pS62-cMYC accompanied by a significant increase of cPARP. These findings are consistent with the evidence reported in proteomics analysis, which shows that BFA treatment determines an increase in the apoptosis signalling as one of the pathways most consistently modulated following treatment.

BFA-induced CD133 structural alteration impairs membrane exposition and EVs-release

The impairment of the activation of the CD133 downstream related pathways (Fig. 4c and d), suggests that the cleavage of the CD133 N-terminal portion influenced its biological activity. Since the N-terminal part of a protein could affect its membrane localization, we evaluated CD133 cellular localization by using a dye that specifically labels the plasma membrane. As shown in Fig. 5a, we observed a reduction in the co-staining of CD133 and the plasma membrane, suggesting the inability of BFA-treated cells to bring this receptor onto the cell surface and confirming the above-mentioned hypothesis. In addition, since CD133 is associated with EVs and found in extracellular body fluids, under both healthy and pathological conditions [33–36], we wondered if the observed structural modification could also determine the impairment of its extracellular release. In detail, cell culture supernatants were collected 24 h after BFA exposure and EVs were isolated and characterized by means of the human tetraspanin assay and SP-IRIS-based Leprechaun platform. The size particles distribution (Supplementary Fig. 3) together with the three human tetraspanins CD9, CD63 and CD81 confirmed the successful isolation of EVs from cell culture media (Fig. 5b), satisfying the requirements for characterization of EVs, on the basis of the MISEV2023 guidelines [37], and the diminished total amount of particles/mL, in accordance with other studies, reporting the inhibition of vesiculation induced by BFA treatment [38] (Supplementary Fig. 2). Noteworthy, the EVs-enriched fraction only contained the CD133 highest MW canonical isoform and the total amount of EVs-released CD133 was significantly lower in BFA-treated than in untreated cells, as demonstrated in Fig. 5C.

Overall, these data suggest that BFA is also able to reduce CD133 functionality as evidenced by its decreased localization at the cell surface and reduced extracellular release.

An innovative bioinformatic approach exploring BFA-MoA reveals new possible avenues for MB treatment

Considering the observed alterations at the proteome level, specifically the suppression of proliferation markers (e.g., PCNA clamp factor, histones, ribosomal proteins), the activation of ER stress and secretory pathway regulators (e.g., SEC24, HspA5, syntaxin) and the altered RNA metabolism and splicing, our data confirm that BFA is triggering ER-Golgi trafficking disruption, UPR, and possible stress-induced dormancy or differentiation.

We therefore aimed at querying for drugs inducing similar or opposite expression signatures and therefore we used the Drug Connectivity submodule of the Omics Playground platform, a computational tool designed to identify potential therapeutic drugs that could either mimic or reverse a given gene expression signature derived from omics data. The platform compares the gene expression profile to reference drug signatures, such as those from the Connectivity Map (CMap) or LINCS L1000 dataset [39] to find compounds that produce a similar protein signature. A similarity score is calculated for each drug, often based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic or a GSEA-like enrichment method.

As shown in Table 1, several classes of compounds that disrupt pro-tumorigenic signaling pathways alters similar protein patterns to BFA. By analysing these compounds based on their normalized enrichment score (NES), key inhibitors were identified, including topoisomerase inhibitors (i.e., etoposide, topotecan), cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitors (i.e., CGP-60474), and MEK inhibitors (i.e., PD-0325901) among the most positively correlated with BFA, indicating their potential as therapeutic candidates. Notably, many of these are already in clinical use for various cancers, including the same tumor type (clinicaltrials.gov). Additionally, BFA shows the highest NES, validating the reliability of the analysis. Tunicamycin, an ER stress inducer, also ranked highly, further supporting the robustness of the findings. With the aim to further corroborate the in-silico analysis, we selected and tested Tunicamycin, Etoposide and CGP-60,474 determining IC50 (Fig. 6a) and thereby evaluating their ability in reducing the clonogenic properties of the medullospheres. As reported in Fig. 6b, all the tested compounds were able to significantly reduce the colony-formation ability of both D283 and D341 MBSCs. Of note, homoharringtonine, a protein synthesis inhibitor used in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), was the most negatively correlated FDA-approved compound, suggesting a distinct MoA from BFA [40].

Discussion

Despite significant advances in molecular classification, MB patients still suffer from poor clinical outcomes [1], with G3MB, especially the G3MBγ subtype, exhibiting the most unfavorable prognosis and the highest rates of relapse [2, 41]. A major therapeutic challenge is posed by MBSCs, a subpopulation that drives tumorigenesis and therapy resistance, and is particularly enriched in G3MB. Therefore, the identification of novel molecular targets within the pathways sustaining MBSC aggressiveness remains an urgent clinical necessity.

BFA is a fungal-derived macrocyclic lactone, historically used to disrupt the Golgi apparatus and indirectly induce ER stress. Beyond its utility in studying Golgi dynamics, BFA has demonstrated antiproliferative and antitumor activity in various cancers [14–16]. In this study, BFA treatment of G3MBSCs markedly altered tumor sphere morphology and disrupted stem cell marker expression. Similar detrimental effects on tumor spheres have been reported in other solid tumors [17, 18], further supporting its impact on stem-like properties. Here, this effect was confirmed by the downregulation of the widely used stemness markers SOX-2 and CD133. Interestingly, the reduced expression of CD133 at the expected MW was accompanied by the appearance of a lower-mass band, suggesting a modulation at post-translational level. CD133 is a membrane glycoprotein composed of an N-terminal extracellular domain, two extracellular loops, and an intracytoplasmic C-terminal domain. Notably, CD133 is subject to complex regulatory layers, mainly related to PTMs [42]. In particular, CD133 N-glycans are known to carry sialic acid residues that regulate protein stability in neural stem cells and glioma-initiating cells [43], and its glycosylation status plays a key role in maintaining stemness [44]. Thus, in this study, we examined the glycosylation and sialylation status of CD133 following BFA treatment. Surprisingly, no significant loss of N-linked glycosylation or sialylation was observed. To further investigate the molecular changes induced by BFA, we performed an in-depth analysis, which, to our knowledge, led for the first time to the identification of a novel CD133 isoform lacking the N-terminal region, providing a structural explanation for the observed shift in MW. Consequently, we explored the biological significance and a functional implication of this N-terminal truncation. Previous studies have shown that the N-terminal region of CD133 mediates interactions with gangliosides, such as monosialotetrahexosylganglioside (GM1), facilitating protein–ganglioside binding and contributing to cell-to-cell communication [45, 46].

The loss of this domain in our model may account for the disruption of tumor sphere integrity, likely due to impaired CD133-mediated intercellular interactions. Further analysis revealed reduced co-localization of CD133 with a plasma membrane dye, indicating decreased membrane localization of the protein. We hypothesize that this mislocalization could affect CD133-associated intracellular signaling. In line with this, we observed decreased phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR, suggesting impaired activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. However, since CD133 has been previously involved in AKT pathway regulation [32], these findings may mirror a compromised CD133 function rather than a direct independent effect of BFA on this signalling cascade.

This signaling attenuation correlates with the observed loss of tumor sphere maintenance and is consistent with the known sensitivity of MBSCs to AKT inhibition, which promotes their differentiation [47]. In addition, the N-terminal region of CD133 contains two highly conserved residues (proline 37 and tyrosine 41) which define the GM1-binding domain in the microvilli of epithelial cells. Mutations in this region have been shown to cause microvilli to display vesicle-like protrusions, suggesting impaired membrane vesicle budding [48]. Similarly, following BFA treatment, we observed a reduction in CD133 expression within EVs. Notably, the N-terminally truncated CD133 isoform was completely absent from the EV fraction, likely due to its exclusion by the vesicle biogenesis machinery. This finding is of remarkable biological significance, as CD133-positive EVs are involved in fundamental biological processes, such as cell differentiation, tumor progression, and intercellular communication [42].

In summary, the BFA-induced loss of the CD133 N-terminal domain not only impairs its proper surface localization but also disrupts intracellular signaling and vesicles-mediated intercellular communication. Notably, current research is increasingly focused on developing strategies to selectively target CD133 + cells with the aim to hit CSCs more selectively [49–52]. Our findings provide mechanistic support for these therapeutic strategies, especially the development of antibody-drug conjugates [51] since BFA could be encapsulated within these vectors to directly disable the oncogenic activity of the CD133 receptor within the primary CD133-expressing cell subpopulation, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity while maximizing anti-stemness efficacy.

To further elucidate BFA-regulated signaling pathways, we employed a shotgun label-free proteomics approach. As detailed in the results section, BFA exposure led to downregulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 pathway and MYC target genes. Notably, MYC amplification, that characterizes G3 and G4 MBs, is a key driver of tumor aggressiveness [41, 53]; thus, the down-regulation of the MYC-regulated gene sets further reinforces the detrimental impact of BFA on SCs fitness. Moreover, analysis of the proteomic data using the UMAP-based gene set representation and Kinase Enrichment Analysis revealed the activation of the Protein Kinase A catalytic subunit gamma (PRKACG) and the inhibition of Casein Kinase II alpha (CSNK2A1). PRKACG, a component of the Protein Kinase A (PKA) complex, plays a central role in regulating diverse signaling cascades, while Casein Kinase II (CK2) is a constitutively active serine/threonine kinase involved in the regulation of over 300 substrates, including transcription factors and DNA repair proteins [54]. The combined effect of PKA activation and CK2 inhibition in this oncogenic context appears to be synergistic, with potential tumor suppressive net total effect. Overall, these findings indicate that BFA treatment perturbs key molecular pathways critical for MBSCs survival, tumor progression, and cellular stress resistance.

Despite its promising anticancer activity, the clinical translation of BFA has been hindered by its significant systemic toxicity and poor pharmacokinetic profile. To address these limitations, various strategies have been explored, including the synthesis of novel BFA derivatives and the utilization of nanocarriers to enhance stability and bioavailability [46–48]. Here, we propose an alternative approach to repurpose BFA as an anticancer molecule by leveraging its effects on MBSCs. In detail, we employed an innovative bioinformatic tool (integrated into the BigOmics platform) to identify compounds that share a similar MoA but possess superior pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamics properties, making them more suitable for clinical applications.

Topoisomerase, HDAC, CDK, and MEK inhibitors emerged as the compounds most strongly correlated with BFA, highlighting their potential therapeutic relevance.

Notably, topoisomerase inhibitors, such as topotecan and irinotecan, have already been proposed in molecularly guided treatment strategies for MB, particularly in combination regimens [55].

Furthermore, topoisomerase studies support this rationale, as elevated Topoisomerase II expression has been associated with increased sensitivity to etoposide in both MB cells lines and patient-derived samples [56].

Among CDK inhibitors, CGP-60,474 showed a strong positive correlation with the MoA of BFA. This compound exhibits potent inhibitory activity against multiple CDKs and PKC, and has previously demonstrated significant anti-proliferative effects against lung adenocarcinoma cells [57]. In our system, CGP-60,474 displayed high potency, with IC₅₀ values in the nanomolar range, and significantly impaired sphere-forming capacity in MBSCs, suggesting that CDK-dependent pathways may contribute to the regulation of CD133-associated stem-like phenotypes. CDKs regulate transcriptional and cell-cycle programs required for the maintenance of stem-like tumor cell states. Notably, pharmacological CDK inhibitors have been shown to target CD133-positive glioma stem-like populations and to impair their proliferative and sphere-forming capacity [58]. In addition, CD133-positive tumor cells are closely linked to cell-cycle regulatory networks, further supporting the possibility that CDK-dependent pathways contribute to their maintenance [59]. Nevertheless, the translational relevance of the identified as well as the of the tested compounds should be interpreted in light of their blood-brain barrier permeability, which remains a crucial consideration when assessing their translational applicability in MB.

Although its efficacy in MB models has not yet been explored, the therapeutic potential of CDK inhibition in MB is well established, with agents such as palbociclib and dinaciclib that have been shown to significantly suppress tumor growth in vitro and in vivo [60, 61].

Among the MEK inhibitors identified in our analysis, PD-0325901 (Mirdametinib) emerged as a particularly promising candidate. This selective MEK1/2 inhibitor suppresses MAPK signaling by preventing ERK phosphorylation. Its antitumor activity has been reported in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, where it sensitizes cells to PI3K/mTOR pathway inhibitors [62]. Notably, in a G4MB model, PD-0325901 showed synergistic activity when combined with BMI1 inhibition, leading to increased apoptotic response and extended survival in xenograft-bearing mice [63].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides novel insights into the MoA of BFA in MBSCs. In our model, BFA impairs stem-like phenotype and reduces tumor-sphere integrity as well as the activation of oncogenic signaling pathways. Moreover, BFA disrupts CD133 structure and induces a decrease in its cell surface localization and EVs release (Fig. 7). These findings reveal a novel pharmacological mechanism and provide a strong rationale for the repurposing of next-generation compounds that mimic BFA-mediated effects. Although preliminary, these results offer promising new avenues for the development of targeted, more effective, and less toxic MB therapies, warranting further in vivo validation and evaluation of central nervous system delivery.
