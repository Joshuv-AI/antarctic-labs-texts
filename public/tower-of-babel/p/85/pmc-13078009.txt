Integrating community engagement and involvement in surgical care processes in low-resource settings: a case study from Pakistan
Authors: Almas Fasih Khattak, Syeda Shamal, Sarah Afridi, Hamza Gohar, Tom Bashford, Laura Hobbs, Yashma Sherwan, Khushbakht Shah, Charlotte Whiffin, Tariq Khan, Peter J. A. Hutchinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity engagement and involvement (CEI) is highly recognized as an important factor in providing ethical, effective, and equitable healthcare and medical research. However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the implementation of the CEI has been limited, especially in surgical systems. In collaboration with the UK’s National Institute of Health and Care Research (NIHR) Global Health Research Group on Acquired Brain and Spine Injury (ABSI) group at the University of Cambridge, this study describes the development, implementation, and initial outcomes of a formal CEI initiative at Northwest General Hospital and Research Centre (NWGH & RC) in Pakistan. The primary purpose of this initiative is to actively engage patients, caregivers, and community members in the development, administration, and management of surgical care, focusing on conditions such as traumatic brain injury (TBI), spina bifida, and burns.MethodsParticipants were recruited via telephone using records from the hospital system, outpatient services, and community outreach events. The prominent features of the CEI initiative were patient-led podcasts, the co-creation of educational materials, involvement in the review of research ethics, and participation in public awareness and advocacy efforts. These initiatives led to key institutional transformations, such as the integration of CEI principles into Institutional Review Board (IRB) applications. The activities repositioned participants from passive recipients of care to active healthcare advocates and research partners.ResultsThis CEI model is now recognized as a framework that can be used both locally and internationally and can be reproduced and extended in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Adapting to local cultures, utilizing multilingual resources, and maintaining engagement through informal feedback channels are the primary elements that contributed to the success of these practices. A Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats (SWOT) analysis revealed opportunities for growth and skill enhancement, alongside challenges such as limited resources and the potential for participant dropout.ConclusionThe results suggest high potential to enhance the relevance of research, the responsiveness of healthcare systems, and community engagement in surgical care within resource-constrained global environments, if the CEI is carefully managed to fit cultural frameworks and integrated into existing structures. Despite this, future work to assess the long-term clinical outcomes and policy-level impacts of CEI in surgical care is required.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00856-x.

Introduction

Community engagement and involvement (CEI) has become a key part of providing equitable and effective healthcare delivery, as well as ethical medical research [1]. It ensures that services meet the needs, values, and lived experiences of patients, caregivers, health professionals, and communities [2]. CEI makes healthcare interventions more ethical, culturally appropriate, and long-lasting by involving all stakeholders from the design phase through implementation [3]. International guidelines, such as the UNICEF Minimum Quality Standards for Community Engagement [4] and the National Institutes of Health Research (NIHR) CEI Guidance [5], highlight the significant role that the CEI plays in strengthening health systems and outcomes of research around the world [6].

Although the CEI is becoming increasingly common in high-income countries, it is still not widely used in health systems in low- or middle-income countries (LMICs) [7], especially in regard to improving global surgery by addressing inequities in access to timely, safe, affordable, and quality surgical care [8]. In LMICs, where there is a profound surgical need and where constraints at the system level are high, the CEI is a promising way to overcome barriers that have been prevalent for a long time, including stigma, low health literacy, gender differences, and lack of infrastructure [9].

It is particularly important to involve patients and communities in the planning and delivery of surgical care, especially for high-burden diseases that disproportionately affect vulnerable populations worldwide, leading to long-term disability if not treated [10]. Conditions such as spina bifida, traumatic brain injury (TBI), and burns have a significant impact on public health. Yet, they are often not given enough attention in global health plans. However, these conditions do not represent the highest surgical volumes; they may have upstream drivers that are easily modifiable with community buy-in. Moreover, our patient and caregiver advocates are mainly from these conditions, and are very actively participating in and advocating for them. That’s why we formalized our CEI around these conditions; however, the framework, designed to be adaptable and transferable, applies to all surgical as well as medical conditions. We urgently need community-informed plans and actions that address prevention, early diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, and social reintegration. This approach is especially needed for time-critical pathologies, such as TBI and burns, where the community response can have an enormous impact on the health system’s ability to manage the condition effectively [11].

In 2023, our team at Northwest General Hospital and Research Centre (NWGH) established a CEI initiative in collaboration with the Acquired Brain and Spinal Injury (ABSI) group of the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) at the University of Cambridge, aiming to integrate the voices of patients and the community on the global surgery and research continuum [12]. Through this collaboration, the burn care was included deliberately due to similar trajectories of long-term recovery, psychosocial impact, and community-level determinants of outcomes. Although strong CEI frameworks have been developed in high-income countries, not enough has been explored on how to contextually and ethically adapt and implement them in LMICs [13].

This study presents a CEI model that has been adapted for use in surgery in a resource-poor setting. It lays out the action plan, explores barriers and enablers that are specific to the local context, and presents early outcomes. We aim to focus on the design, governance, and operationalization of CEI within surgical systems, using selected conditions as tracer examples to illustrate implementation, to strengthen the evidence base for the use of the CEI in LMICs, and provide a patient-centered, scalable approach to improve global surgical outcomes through meaningful involvement of patients and the community.

Methods

Setting

This was a descriptive case study documenting the development, implementation, and early outcomes of a structured ongoing CEI program in surgical care within a low-resource setting in Pakistan. The CEI program was launched in June 2023 at NWGH in Peshawar, Pakistan, to engage patients, the community, and caregivers meaningfully in healthcare delivery, research, and advocacy. The initiative was launched in alignment with global CEI frameworks, the local context, and ground realities in Pakistan, aiming to actively involve patients, caregivers, and communities within the surgical and neurosurgical domains, with a focus on planning, delivering, and assessing healthcare services and research.

The CEI activities were tailored to ensure cultural relevance, inclusivity, and sustainability. The program was approved by the NWGH Institutional Review Board (approval number: IRB&EC/2023-GH/0254). The study design and reporting were guided by the GRIPP2 Short Form (Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public) to ensure transparency in documentation of patient and community engagement and involvement [14].

Participant recruitment

The recruitment of participants took place over six months from July 2023 to December 2023, employing different strategies to guarantee inclusivity and diversity. There were no strict eligibility criteria except willingness to participate or share personalized lived experiences. To reduce biases and ensure accessibility for all participants, no prior experience in healthcare research or formal education was required.

Participants were recruited via telephone using records from the Hospital Management Information System (HMIS). Our team carefully contacted patients and families who had previously sought surgical or neurosurgical care for diseases. During these calls, their interest in participating in the program was evaluated following an explanation of the initiative. Participants were also included through clinic-based recruitment, surgical outpatient departments, and community outreach through awareness seminars and flyers.

We contacted a total of 68 potential participants; 40 showed interest, 23 signed up, and 14 remained actively involved. Figure 1 shows the recruitment process and outcomes across the three channels of recruitment. Given the small sample size (n = 14), the analysis focused on describing implementation processes and practical lessons rather than identifying themes or making generalizable claims. Potential participants were invited to a launch meeting to discuss our CEI program action plan and affirm their willingness to attend quarterly meetings and engage in CEI activities, including patient and caregiver consultations. The final selection was made collaboratively by prospective members and the coordination team to ensure alignment with the program objectives and participants’ expectations. Participant withdrawal occurred during the transition from initial orientation to sustained participation and was mainly due to long travel distances, availability, concurrent commitments, and the time constraint required for sustained participation.

After the participant recruitment, CEI activities were implemented subsequently and are ongoing.

Types of CEI activities

Large-scale public awareness campaigns

The CEI participants actively engaged in ongoing public awareness seminars within NWGH as active patient and community advocates and as invited speakers on the most common conditions, including prevention of TBI, folate fortification to prevent spina bifida, advocacy for road traffic safety and helmet use, and burn prevention in vulnerable populations.

Patient-led podcasts and video testimonies

These were the prominent features of the CEI program used to amplify patients’ and caregivers’ voices and their lived experiences in dealing with the rising burden of surgical conditions. By serving as advocacy and education tools, these podcasts and videos supported the program’s purpose of destigmatization and public engagement.

Codesign of health and research information materials

The CEI participants gave their direct input to codesign patient and community education pamphlets, informed consent documents, research opportunity brochures, etc. They helped in revising those for clarity, cultural appropriateness, and readability, particularly for people with low literacy.

Research activities

The CEI participants also actively participated in research activities, not only as contributors but also as coinvestigators. They helped in conducting peer interviews for qualitative research activities and provided advice on research governance in terms of studies being ethically sound and contextually appropriate. The authors also provided support to interpret findings from the lived experiences of the research participants.

Program coordination and oversight

A CEI coordination team was established to supervise the initiative; the team consisted of three coordinators (SS, SK, and HG), the CEI lead (AFK), and the program head (TK). In consultation with the institutional Community Advisory Board (CAB), the team defined the following goals:

Planning and processing of CEI activities

Preparation and process

Every CEI activity was carefully organized to create a warm and inclusive environment. Preparations involved choosing suitable venues and sending official invitations before the planned activities. All participants received complimentary medical consultations, refreshments, and travel support as a token of appreciation. All essential materials were prepared in both English and Urdu, including the participant information sheet, consent form, educational pamphlets, and feedback forms. Each session followed a structured agenda designed to facilitate improved patient and caregiver participation in the activities. Each CEI activity began with an orientation conducted by the coordinating team. Three coordinators engaged with each participant individually for 15–20 min to elucidate the activity’s aims, understand expectations, and resolve any queries or concerns. At the end of each activity, participants recorded their experience through feedback forms and informal interviews. With their informed consent, select participants were encouraged to record video testimonials shared on institutional social media platforms to support public advocacy initiatives and engage a larger audience.

The experiences, perceptions, and contextual factors were descriptively summarized using patients’ and community members’ feedback and reflections. SWOT analysis was used as a structured reflective tool to summarize implementation facilitators, barriers, and lessons learned.

Results

Program outcomes and community impact

The CEI program has generated a series of outcomes at the institutional, community, and national levels. One of the key impacts has been improving patients’ and the community’s understanding of health systems and their capacity for research literacy. Some of the program members have progressed from passive attendees to active contributors in ongoing research studies, taking part as coresearchers in qualitative interviews and helping in data interpretation.

Patient and community members’ perspectives

Pre- and post-session feedback was routinely gathered from participants after every CEI activity. Feedback was collected through simple survey forms to rate participants’ expectations, motivation, satisfaction, and interest in continuing their participation, and via informal interviews for qualitative insights.

All participants reported high expectations for the CEI program, with a motivation level of either “high” or “extremely high,” and rated the activities as “very satisfying” or “satisfying.” Similarly, most of the participants expressed being either “interested” or “very interested” in participating in future CEI activities.

Informal interviews were routinely conducted with the CEI participants to explore their insights and perceptions about the CEI program. Formal thematic analysis was not performed; however, informal feedback indicated that the participants appreciated the initiative’s transparency and recognized the value of their participation. 93% of the participants expressed that their involvement made them feel valued and that their voices were heard in patient- and community-centered initiatives and research activities.

The participants appreciated being engaged in public awareness events to obtain the opportunity to contribute meaningfully to the cause of CEI. Based on the relevance to the CEI processes, the participant quotes were purposely selected to clearly convey their opinions to support descriptive reporting of the CEI implementation process.

As one participant expressed,When I was first invited, I was not sure why I was going and what my role would be, but I am happy that I participated and was able to contribute to the program. I exactly know how I could help.

Almost all participants mentioned that the CEI program was very helpful and expressed that they would be willing to participate in future activities and events; one participant, however, was unable to remain involved due to work commitments and frequent travel, rather than discontent with the program.

At the institutional level

The CEI component has been incorporated into the research governance framework, formally requiring researchers to include CEI plans when submitting new study protocols to the IRB. Components of the CEI model have also been integrated into external research grant proposals, including those submitted to international funding agencies.

Participants’ feedback through the CEI process has been instrumental in shaping service delivery models in hospitals. Specific changes were made after the members’ input, including the revision of outpatient educational materials and the start of structured patient and family education sessions. Moreover, the CEI program has encouraged community-led public awareness seminars and campaigns, wherein CEI members, together with institutional support, have collaboratively designed and carried out large-scale public awareness sessions on road traffic safety and prevention of TBI, spina bifida, and burns. These sessions were supported by the co-creation of culturally appropriate educational video messages shared on social media.

One of the most important innovations emerging from the CEI initiative was the development of patient-centered podcasts. In these podcasts, patients, caregivers, and community members shared their lived experiences with TBI, spina bifida, burns, and other preventable surgical conditions. These podcasts serve as powerful and effective education and advocacy tools to amplify patient voices within broader community discourse and help in destigmatization. Some of the CEI participants have become active patient advocates, representing patient groups and the community in large-scale public awareness seminars, events, and campaigns. Their stories have significantly contributed to not only raising awareness but also inspiring others in the community to become more actively involved in health education, advocacy, and research.

At the national and global levels

The success of the CEI model at NWGH has garnered significant acclaim at both the national and international levels. Our CEI team has been invited to present our implementation strategies, lessons learned, and insights to help disseminate our best practices at several local, regional, and national-level platforms, including academic symposia, professional networks, and institutional capacity-building workshops focused on CEI. Our team has also been invited to and presented at international webinars to showcase our activities and implementation framework, showing the increasing recognition of our CEI program as a replicable and scalable model for integrating the CEI into surgical settings in LMICs. These invitations have recognized the hospital as a leader in community-engaged global surgery research and health system strengthening in Pakistan and beyond.

CEI team reflections

Team reflections provided contextual insights into implementation considerations, challenges, and process-level observations to support the descriptive reporting of CEI activities. One of the key drivers of the program’s success was its well-defined engagement process, which effectively addressed the needs and expectations of the participants. One of the team members remarked:Having a clear framework made it easier for the CEI team and the participants. We both knew what we were getting into and what to expect from the program.

Although time-consuming, the team found the structured process to be significantly effective. From the perspective of the CEI program lead, the initiative was more than just a project milestone; it represented a significant shift in patient, family, and larger community engagement and involvement within surgical care and research. The program consistently incorporated cultural contextualization, structured engagement, and patient empowerment throughout the program’s development and implementation. The CEI lead reflected:This program educated us that when we empower patients, families, and communities and give them a voice and genuinely listen, they do not just participate; they lead, generate new ideas, and emerge as powerful advocates for improving health systems.

All these outcomes show that institutional CEI programs, when tailored to local contexts, not only improve the quality of research and healthcare delivery but also help bring about a greater cultural shift toward putting patients first, working with communities, and making health systems more sustainable in resource-limited settings.

Discussion

This study documents one of the first examples of a structured CEI model integrated within surgical care in a low-resource setting. The participatory framework and contextual adaptation of the initiative at NWGH, Peshawar, Pakistan, have shown how a CEI program, traditionally well developed in high-resource countries, can be successfully implemented to meaningfully engage patients, caregivers, and the community to influence public awareness, care delivery, community advocacy, and research governance in resource-constrained environments.

CEI in healthcare is now considered an integral part of systems strengthening and global health research [16], particularly to promote equitable research partnerships and decolonize global health [17]. Global frameworks for CEI, including UNICEF’s Minimum Quality Standards for Community Engagement [4] and the National Health Research Program (NIHR) [5], recommend involving patients and communities in all stages of the healthcare delivery continuum and research frameworks [6]. However, the integration of these frameworks in low-resource settings, especially in global surgery domains, is still limited in LMICs [18].

Institutionalization of CEI within governance processes

Our initiative is innovative, having integrated CEI components into institutional governance, including active and regular participation of patients and families in awareness and advocacy activities, inclusive research participation, and the requirement by IRB for CEI plans to be formally embedded into research study applications [19]. Although the CEI is frequently utilized in high-income countries for research funding, its adaptation is not well supported or utilized within LMIC settings for meaningful participation by communities [20]. However, our CEI institutionalization mirrors the findings of other studies [21] showing that formal CEI integration in healthcare governance not only improves patient and caregiver participation [22] but also helps them advance from consultative to coproduction roles in research and awareness activities [23].

Impact on service delivery and advocacy within low-resource settings

The most important outcome was the transition of participants from passive engagement to active health advocates and coresearchers, which resonates with similar findings in other settings. Studies have also shown that CEI initiatives that go beyond symbolic participation cultivate ownership [24], deeper trust, improved research literacy, and shared decision-making, ultimately improving overall health outcomes [25]. Our initiative demonstrated how the participants actively contributed to public awareness seminars on largely overlooked global surgical conditions, including road safety, folate fortification to prevent spina bifida, TBI care, and prevention of burns. Patient-led podcasts, video testimonies, and codesigns of research materials align with established community participation frameworks [26], especially in low-resource settings [27], and their use amplifies digital health advocacy strategies to bridge the information gap and reduce stigma around health conditions [28, 29], and global surgery initiatives, especially in LMICs [30]. CEI initiatives such as ours are increasingly endorsed by current global surgery frameworks advocating for patient- and community-centered initiatives [10]. The impact of the intervention was manifested through the revision of patient and family education materials, educatory pamphlets, and brochures in local languages, assisting in improving healthcare quality and responsiveness, as evidenced in the literature [31]. Culturally contextualized public awareness campaigns led by community members fortified the desired impact, corresponding to effective community engagement approaches endorsed by the WHO and UNICEF [32].

Participant reflections and motivation

The positive feedback from the CEI participants showed clarity, trust, and strong motivation, satisfaction, and willingness for continued participation, corroborating the literature’s emphasis on mutual respect, power sharing, and tailored communication as the core facilitators of engagement among communities and institutions [33], participants’ suggestions, such as regular communication through a WhatsApp group and co-creation of future activities, show how cost-effective, easy-to-use tools help in sustaining CEI in low-resource settings [34].

Opportunities, threats, and limitations

Several opportunities were identified to explore avenues, including continuous feedback mechanisms, the expansion of multilingual resources, and the integration of codesigned workshops, to foster participatory, iterative, and linguistically accessible frameworks to fortify the effectiveness of participant involvement. Participants showed exemplary capacity, which can be translated into future initiatives focused on relevant health advocacy and literacy to empower participants to assume leadership roles, leading to sustained patient and community engagement.

Along with these opportunities, a few threats were also observed, including resource constraints, stigma, competing obligations of the participants, and lack of recognition, with the potential for participant disengagement from the program. Another barrier identified was the presumed institutional resistance that might hinder the successful integration of CEI within the policy and governance structure for sustained impact.

The CEI model has certain limitations, including selection bias and limited diversity and representativeness due to small sample size, difficulty accessing rural populations, voluntary participation, and focus on selected tracer conditions. The time-consuming workarounds include the translation of material and verbal consent processes. The absence of formal thematic analysis and a long-term evaluation framework constrained the robustness of the findings, while reliance on self-reported feedback and team reflections may have introduced social desirability bias. Moreover, long-term clinical outcomes and policy-level impacts of CEI integration were also not assessed. These limitations highlight the need for future work incorporating broader participation, structured analytical approaches, and longitudinal evaluation to assess adaptability across additional surgical domains in low-resource settings. Regardless, the team is committed to addressing these barriers in the future with dedicated funding and stakeholder support for a durable CEI program at NWGH.

Implications for health systems and global surgery

Our CEI initiative demonstrates how a replicable and scalable CEI model in a local surgical context can contribute to bridging the gap between patients, the community, and the healthcare system in a meaningful way, making global surgery more responsive, community-centered, and inclusive. This aligns with the global consensus and the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery [10] calling for local ownership, equity, and culturally grounded interventions, and ensuring that patients and communities are at the heart of healthcare service delivery and research.

This CEI initiative offers a practical model in low-resource settings to strengthen health systems by demonstrating the ability to cultivate a cultural shift toward meaningful patient and community partnerships. To ensure sustainability, continued adaptation, monitoring, and evaluation will be required to ensure alignment with changing community needs and the health system itself.

Conclusion

This initiative shows that community engagement and participation can be meaningfully incorporated into surgical care and research in settings with limited resources. CEI can effectively transcend tokenistic involvement to successful partnerships and coproduction of activities with patients, families, and communities by integrating CEI within the governance, education, and advocacy frameworks of tertiary care hospitals in LMICs. The CEI model at NWGH shows that when we genuinely involve patients and communities through culturally relevant public participation, collaboratively designed materials, and participatory research, they bring transformative change to healthcare delivery and research. This model provides a replicable and scalable pathway for other similar settings to integrate community voices in surgical systems through patient-led advocacy and research protocols. Moving ahead, the keys to long-term impacts will be ongoing commitment, policy-level endorsement, and continued reflection with CEI members. CEI must remain at the center of global surgery as it continues to evolve, not as an optional add-on but as an essential standard for building stronger, ethical, responsive, and equitable health systems.
