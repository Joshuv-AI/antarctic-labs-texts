Association of Race and Ethnicity and Social Factors With Postpartum Primary Care or Cardiology Follow‐Up Visits Among Individuals With Preeclampsia
Authors: Malamo E Countouris, Kayle S Shapero, Gretchen Swabe, Alisse Hauspurg, Esa M Davis, Jared W Magnani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia is associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and death. Primary care or cardiology follow‐up, in complement to routine postpartum obstetric care, provides an essential opportunity to address cardiovascular risk. Prior studies investigating racial differences in the recommended postpartum follow‐up have incompletely assessed the influence of social factors. We hypothesized that racial and ethnic differences in follow‐up with a primary care provider or cardiologist would be modified by income and education.Methods and ResultsWe identified adult individuals with preeclampsia (September 2014 to September 2019) in a national administrative database. We compared occurrence of a postpartum visit with a primary care provider or cardiologist within 1 year after delivery by race and ethnicity using multivariable logistic regression models. We examined whether education or income modified the association between race and ethnicity and the likelihood of follow‐up. Of 18 050 individuals with preeclampsia (aged 31.8±5.7 years), Black individuals (11.7%) had lower odds of primary care provider or cardiology follow‐up within 1 year after delivery compared with White individuals (adjusted odds ratio, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.70–0.85]) as did Hispanic individuals (14.8%; adjusted odds ratio, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.73–0.87]). Black and Hispanic individuals with higher educational attainment were more likely to have follow‐up than those with lower educational attainment (P for interaction=0.033) as did those in higher income brackets (P for interaction=0.006).ConclusionsWe identified racial and ethnic differences in primary care or cardiology follow‐up in the year postpartum among individuals diagnosed with preeclampsia, a disparity that may be modified by social factors. Enhanced system‐level interventions are needed to reduce barriers to follow‐up care.Keywords: follow‐up, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preeclampsia, primary care, racial disparitiesSubject Categories: Pregnancy, Women, Race and Ethnicity, Preeclampsia

Preeclampsia, defined by elevated blood pressure and proteinuria or other end‐organ dysfunction, occurs in 5% to 8% of pregnancies and affects up to 15% of pregnant individuals. 1 , 2 Preeclampsia carries a higher risk of peripartum comorbidities including severely elevated blood pressures, heart failure, and stroke, which can contribute to postpartum maternal death. 3 Because of preeclampsia's strong association with hypertension and cardiovascular disease in the years after pregnancy, 4 obstetric and cardiovascular professional society guidelines uniformly recommend that individuals with preeclampsia have regular lifelong follow‐up to optimize their cardiovascular health and prevent adverse cardiovascular outcomes. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 The International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy recommends that postpartum follow‐up with a general practitioner or obstetrician occur at 6 to 12 weeks, 6 months, 1 year, and then annually beyond 1 year with a general practitioner 5 , 9 to screen and manage cardiovascular risk factors. Despite this recommendation, only about 60% of individuals report being counseled on cardiovascular risk after pregnancy at their 4‐ to 6‐week postpartum visit. 10

For those patients with pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia the longitudinal cardiovascular care required to mitigate adverse cardiovascular outcomes requires a health care team with collaboration among clinicians. 11 A health care team composed of obstetricians, primary care providers (PCPs), and cardiologists can improve quality and reduce health care gaps for postpartum individuals. Thus, in addition to follow‐up with an obstetrician, it is essential for individuals with preeclampsia to have postpartum follow‐up with a primary care or cardiology clinician who can provide longitudinal blood pressure management, cardiovascular risk factor modification, and heart‐healthy lifestyle counseling in complement to the typical 6‐week postpartum obstetrician visit.

Among those with preeclampsia, Black individuals have higher postpartum blood pressure than White referents, accompanied by higher risk of serious morbidity including stroke, pulmonary edema, and heart failure. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Similarly, Hispanic individuals with preeclampsia have higher risk of serious morbidity and death than White referents. 15 , 16 Of those with preeclampsia, Black individuals, those in the poorest quartile of median income, and those with public insurance, had higher postpartum 30‐day readmission rates when compared with their respective reference groups. 13 Prior studies have demonstrated that Black and Hispanic individuals with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy have lower rates of follow‐up compared with White individuals but have inconsistently accounted for the impact comorbidities may have on these results. 16 , 17 Furthermore, lower educational attainment has been associated with decreased postpartum follow‐up rates, 18 , 19 , 20 and additional social factors such as access to social resources, health literacy, inequities in delivery of care, and access to prenatal care may also limit postpartum care. Notably, structural factors, such as structural racism and racial bias in health care, may also impact postpartum care. There has been little assessment of how social factors impact racial and ethnic disparities in follow‐up, particularly with nonobstetric providers.

In this study, we investigated racial and ethnic differences in primary care or cardiology postpartum office visits among individuals with preeclampsia and aimed to determine if these differences were influenced by comorbidities or socioeconomic factors. We hypothesized that Black and Hispanic individuals with recent preeclampsia would have lower rates of 1‐year PCP or cardiology follow‐up and that this finding would be modified by the key social factors of educational attainment and income.

METHODS

Study Population

We used Optum's deidentified Clinformatics Data Mart Database (Eden Prairie, MN; www.optum.com, hereafter Clinformatics), which is a geographically diverse claims database representing over 68 million individuals with commercial and Medicare Advantage insurance coverage in the United States who are similar to the insured population. 21 The Clinformatics database includes inpatient, outpatient, emergency department, pharmacy, and laboratory health claims. Medical claims include International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision and Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9/10‐CM) diagnosis and procedure codes, Current Procedural Terminology version 4 procedure codes, Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System procedure codes, and site‐of‐service codes. These data are commercially licensed, and interested parties may obtain them directly from the vendor. As per licensing agreement, the authors do not have the permission or authority to share these data.

We selected individuals ≥18 years old with all deliveries (not limited to live births) defined by Current Procedural Terminology codes and a preeclampsia diagnosis no more than 6 months prior to delivery as defined by ICD‐9/ICD‐10 diagnosis codes from 9/2014 to 9/2019. Table S1 provides a comprehensive list of ICD‐9/ICD‐10 and Current Procedural Terminology codes used in the analysis to determine delivery date and preeclampsia diagnosis as done in prior studies. 16 , 22 We then followed individuals prospectively for 1 year and concluded follow‐up in March 2020 to accommodate for the COVID‐19 pandemic. We excluded those with noncontinuous insurance enrollment for 1 year after delivery or delivery <12 months from March 2020, male sex, and missing or unknown race or income. The University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board deemed this study as exempt from human subject review as it uses previously collected, deidentified data. The need to obtain consent was waived for this study as participant data were deidentified.

Demographic Variables

Collection of sociodemographic variables by Clinformatics has been discussed previously. 23 , 24 Age, sex, race, and ethnicity are included in the Clinformatics claims data. Specifically, race and ethnicity are ascertained by public records and imputed from commercial software that uses validated algorithms incorporating racial and ethnic neighborhood composition from the US Census, residential zip code, and first and last name. 25 Race and ethnicity are reported as one of the following: Asian, Black, Hispanic, White, or unknown.

Derivation of household income by Clinformatics® has been described previously. 23 Briefly, annual household income was estimated and validated from surveys of US households using a comprehensive set of variables that encompass ZIP+4 (a highly specific geographic locator), Internal Revenue Service data, address‐level home value, aggregated credit, and short‐term loans. Annual household income was categorized as <$40 000, $40 000 to $49 999, $50 000 to $59 999, $60 000 to $74 999, $75 000 to $99 999, and ≥ $100 000. Education level was derived from Census data at the ZIP+4 level. Educational attainment was categorized as less than high school diploma, high school diploma, less than bachelor's degree, or bachelor's degree or greater. We combined less than high school and high school diploma into a single group due to small sample sizes in the less than high school group. Clinformatics also provided information on number of adults in the household. Insurance type was classified as either Medicare or commercial insurance.

Comorbidities (gestational hypertension, congestive heart failure, chronic hypertension, diabetes, and obesity) were assessed on the basis of presence of claims containing corresponding diagnosis codes at any time before delivery. Table S1 describe the diagnosis codes for variables of the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, which includes >30 conditions with different organ system involvement. 26 Antihypertensive medication claims were defined by receipt of the antihypertensive medications listed in Table S2 within 6 months of delivery.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was occurrence of follow‐up with either a PCP or cardiologist by 1 year postpartum using previously described methods to identify visits 19 with Current Procedural Terminology codes listed in Table S1. A composite outcome variable with either PCP or cardiology follow‐up (or both) was chosen to identify care with a nonobstetric provider. PCP included internal medicine or family practice physician, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant. We also assessed differences in timing of first follow‐up visit with PCP or cardiology in weeks after delivery by race and ethnicity. As a secondary outcome, we compared follow‐up with an obstetrics provider by race and ethnicity.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic variables were compared across racial and ethnic groups using ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables (age) and using the Kruskal–Wallis test for nonnormally distributed continuous variables (enrollment time in months postpartum and number of Elixhauser comorbidities). A χ2 test was used to compare categorical variables across race and ethnicity groups.

We compared the proportion of individuals with cardiology or PCP follow‐up at 1 year by race and ethnicity using a χ2 test among individuals with preeclampsia. For adjusted analyses, we compared differences in follow‐up by race and ethnicity among individuals with preeclampsia using multivariable logistic regression models. Model 1 adjusted for age; model 2 adjusted for model 1 plus Elixhauser Comorbidity Index; model 3 adjusted for model 2 plus income, education, insurance type, and number of adults per household. We examined the effect modification of education and income separately on follow‐up by race and ethnicity by the inclusion of 2‐way interactions between race and ethnicity categories and education, and also between race and ethnicity categories and income. As sensitivity analyses, we excluded individuals with hypertension predating pregnancy, as determined by a diagnosis of hypertension before the start of the second trimester of pregnancy (defined as 180 days before the date of delivery).

Additionally, we were interested in investigating racial and ethnic differences in the timing of follow‐up in the year after delivery. We further analyzed differences in timing of follow‐up across race and ethnicity with survival models. Follow‐up was censored at 1 year after delivery. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to assess differences in time to follow‐up by race and ethnicity. Cox proportional hazard models estimated differences in follow‐up timing by race and ethnicity adjusting for age, comorbidities (Elixhauser score), income, education, and insurance type. We tested the proportional hazards assumption within the models using time‐dependent variables and found no violations of the assumption. We also performed a visual inspection and looked for intersecting lines using Kaplan–Meier curves. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A 2‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered significant.

We observed that educational attainment modified the association between race and ethnicity with primary care and cardiology follow‐up among individuals with preeclampsia (P for interaction=0.033). Higher educational attainment reduced the racial and ethnic differences in primary care or cardiology follow‐up within 1 year (Figure 3). In contrast, Black and Hispanic individuals with lower educational attainment (defined by less than or equal to high school diploma or some college) had lower odds of follow‐up than White individuals of the same education (Figure 3). Similarly, we observed that income modified the association between race and ethnicity, with follow‐up among individuals with preeclampsia (P for interaction=0.006). Higher income (≥$50 000 annually) generally reduced the racial and ethnic differences in primary care or cardiology follow‐up (Figure 4). However, Hispanic individuals earning >$100 000 per year were less likely to have primary care or cardiology follow‐up compared with White individuals in the same income bracket.

DISCUSSION

In this study of individuals with preeclampsia, we found that Black and Hispanic individuals were less likely than White individuals to have a postpartum visit with a PCP or cardiologist in adjusted analyses up to 1 year postpartum. These differences in follow‐up were modified by educational attainment and income, such that Black and Hispanic individuals with at least a college education had similar likelihood of follow‐up compared with White individuals as did those in higher income brackets. For individuals with preeclampsia, follow‐up with a PCP or cardiologist provides a dedicated time in the postpartum period to address blood pressure management and optimize cardiovascular health as well as establish a relationship for longitudinal care. Importantly, identification of racial and ethnic disparities in longitudinal follow‐up and the influence of social factors even among insured individuals can help guide targeted interventions to address inequities in care.

In a similar study using the Clinformatics database, Lewey et al found that both Black race and Hispanic ethnicity as well as lower education attainment and geographic region were associated with lower odds of follow‐up with an individual's women's health provider or PCP among individuals with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. 16 This study was limited in its assessment of the effect of the interaction between race and ethnicity with education attainment on follow‐up and did not incorporate follow‐up with cardiology. Our study includes a more contemporary cohort of patients with deliveries from 2014 to 2019 as compared with 2005 to 2014 in the prior study. An advantage of a more contemporary cohort is the use of revised definitions of preeclampsia 27 , 28 and modified practice patterns in the postpartum period for individuals with preeclampsia. Notably, even with these changes, our study shows that racial and ethnic disparities have persisted over time.

Racial and ethnic disparities in maternal health have multifactorial pathogeneses, which include (1) structural racism, which has yielded generational income inequality, educational, and social disadvantage for Black and Brown communities; (2) systemic barriers that limit health care access (eg, limited clinic hours, lack of virtual visit options); (3) provider biases and limited organizational attention toward health literacy; and (4) individual‐level factors such as resources embedded in one's social network. 29 Racism is a well‐documented barrier to accessing and receiving care for Black individuals, particularly during the postpartum period. 30 Racism also fuels distrust in health care providers and undermines adherence to medical care. 31

In our study, racial and ethnic disparities in follow‐up were modified by educational attainment, with Black and Hispanic individuals with at least a college education having similar likelihood of follow‐up to White individuals. Our findings are consistent with prior studies suggesting that the association of educational attainment with cardiovascular health differs by race and ethnicity. 32 A prior analysis demonstrated that racial differences in obtaining postpartum blood pressure measures were attenuated in adjusted analyses accounting for social factors (including race and insurance status), 33 further underscoring the relevance of social determinants to inequities.

Education impacts access to health care and having higher education reduces the likelihood of disparities. The modification on postpartum follow‐up by educational attainment suggests that there are barriers encountered by individuals of Black race or Hispanic ethnicity, particularly those with less educational attainment. Such barriers occur at the societal, health care system, and individual levels. Language barriers impact health literacy and may play a role in lower follow‐up for those of lower educational attainment in Black and Hispanic groups. Disparities in follow‐up by race and ethnicity, of any educational attainment, may be due to unmeasured social determinants of health such as structural racism in the health care system. 34 Structural racism has been shown to be a deterrent to individuals seeking health care, particularly for Black individuals in the peripartum or postpartum periods. 30 , 31 However, common barriers to attending a postpartum follow‐up visit encountered by many individuals regardless of race, have included adjusting to a new baby, child care, return to work within 6 weeks, and insufficient transportation. 35 Individuals with higher parity have been shown to have lower show rates and postpartum blood pressure visits. 36 Since all individuals in this data set have insurance coverage, our findings of differences in follow‐up by income bracket may be a proxy for job type and job‐related flexibility that impacts the ability to attend postpartum care visits. However, more granular details on household size, caretaking duties, and job responsibilities are needed to be able to assess this level of influence. Requirements for having an order for a subspecialty referral may also contribute to increased barriers in accessing valuable subspecialty cardiology care.

By showing disparities in postpartum primary care or cardiology visits, we identified a missed opportunity for blood pressure management and cardiovascular health modification, especially for individuals who may have higher postpartum morbidity and death. Black individuals may be at the highest risk for persistent postpartum blood pressure elevation; Black individuals with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy have a slower decrease in blood pressure in the postpartum period and are more likely to remain hypertensive at 6 weeks after delivery, a difference not explained by area deprivation index. 37 , 38 In line with these prior findings showing Black individuals have higher postpartum blood pressures, our analyses demonstrate that Black individuals are more likely to have chronic hypertension and to have an antihypertensive medication claim within 1 year after delivery. When we looked at postpartum follow‐up divided by provider type (separating PCP and cardiologist), Black individuals were more likely to have a follow‐up visit with a cardiologist than White individuals (7.2% versus 4.9%), a difference that may be explained by a higher prevalence of comorbidities.

Achieving better follow‐up rates with primary care or cardiology in the “fourth trimester” after a preeclamptic delivery would provide an opportunity to improve blood pressure control and address cardiovascular disease prevention. We identify Black and Hispanic individuals, particularly those with less than a college‐level education and those at lower‐income brackets, as potential groups who may benefit the most from multipronged public health interventions. Public health interventions could help reduce barriers and improve inequities in postpartum follow‐up by targeting structural changes to the health care system, social and societal factors, and individual education. Implementation of standardized care protocols in the postpartum period that minimize barriers to attendance, such as using virtual visits, may help reduce racial disparities. 39 Standardized remote blood pressure monitoring approaches have promise to reduce racial disparities in postpartum blood pressure ascertainment. 40 Similar remote monitoring interventions warrant study to assess if they could also improve postpartum follow‐up with primary care or cardiology. Importantly, for those who are publicly insured, Medicaid coverage ends in many states 60 days after delivery, limiting access to care and potentially worsening these inequities in long‐term cardiovascular health. Providing continuous health care insurance coverage for up to 1 year postpartum would help reduce inequities in care.

Strengths of this study include analyses using the Clinformatics national claims database, which allowed for comparison of a large, geographically and racially diverse population, allowing us to generalize our results to the insured population of US adults. Studying a contemporary cohort increases the likelihood of accurately capturing individuals with a preeclampsia diagnosis.

This study has important limitations worth noting. First, there is the potential for misclassification of demographic variables (race, income, and education) and misclassification of follow‐up status. Misclassification of race is likely to be nondifferential, hence may bias our results toward the null. In the Clinformatics database, race and ethnicity are combined into one variable that does not differentiate individuals who have >1 race or ethnicity. Misclassification of follow‐up status may have resulted in not having identified all follow‐up visits, for example, those who had a visit without an insurance claim, like receiving care at a free health clinic. Variables of income and education were assessed by area level as a proxy for individual level income and education and thus may not have accurately classified socioeconomic status for individuals. Second, by definition, for inclusion in the Clinformatics database, we studied only commercially insured patients and those covered by Medicare Advantage. The generalizability of our results is limited to insured individuals and may not apply to uninsured populations, individuals receiving Medicaid, or the general US population. Because we excluded individuals with fluctuating insurance status, we may have, by design, excluded individuals who were more socially disadvantaged and thus at higher risk, and this may have induced a biased sample. The Clinformatics database does not have any survey or patient satisfaction information. Finally, we cannot exclude residual confounding due to lack of additional granular data on clinical history, such as parity, and unmeasured social variables and social determinants of health, such as marital status, number of dependents, caretaker load, employment status, and distance to care.

CONCLUSIONS

Among individuals with preeclampsia, Black and Hispanic individuals had decreased odds of follow‐up with a PCP or cardiologist in the 1 year postpartum. This disparity was attenuated in those individuals with higher educational attainment and higher income. Interventions that aim to improve follow‐up care for Black and Hispanic individuals, particularly those with lower education or lower income, may provide opportunities to enhance blood pressure management and cardiovascular disease prevention to reduce disparities in cardiovascular health.
