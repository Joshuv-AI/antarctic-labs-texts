Registries and surveillance models used to screen neonates for congenital anomalies in LMIC settings: a scoping review
Authors: Mahnoor Nawab, Hameeda Arif Arain, Maryam Sherwani, Katrine Aronsen, Aneeta Channar, Hakon Angell Bolkan, Heather Melanie R Ames, Lubna Samad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo map and characterise congenital anomalies (CAs) registries and surveillance systems implemented in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), and to examine their reported effectiveness, challenges and potential for replication.DesignScoping review conducted in accordance with Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.Data sourcesPubMed, Epistemonikos, Global Index Medicus, EMBASE, PakMediNet, CINAHL (EBSCO), and Google Scholar were searched from database inception to April 2025.Eligibility criteriaStudies describing registries, surveillance systems or screening programmes for externally visible CAs in neonates or stillbirths implemented in LMIC settings were included.Data extraction and synthesisData were extracted on publication characteristics, registry design, surveillance methods, screening personnel, diagnostic approaches and anomaly types. Findings were synthesised descriptively and mapped across WHO regions.ResultsOf 12 328 records identified, 39 studies met the inclusion criteria. Most studies were hospital-based (79%) and regional in scope (74%). Prospective surveillance was used in 79% of studies, with physical examination as the primary screening method. Doctors conducted screening in 41% of studies, while 13% employed mixed cadres including nurses, midwives and health workers. Nine sustained national or subnational registries and two multinational surveillance networks were identified. Neurological, musculoskeletal and cardiovascular anomalies were most frequently reported.ConclusionsCAs surveillance in LMICs is predominantly hospital-based, with limited community-level coverage. Sustainable registries demonstrate feasibility when integrated into existing health systems, supported by trained frontline workers and focused on pragmatic datasets. Future efforts should prioritise scalability, community integration and linkage to care pathways.Keywords: Scoping Review, Mass Screening, Population Surveillance

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Congenital anomalies (CAs) registries and surveillance systems are well established in high-income countries but remain limited in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to resource and system constraints. However, some successful registries in LMICs have demonstrated their feasibility and highlighted the need for a more detailed examination of their strategies and outcomes.

Introduction

Congenital anomalies (CAs) are structural and functional defects that develop during intrauterine life and can be diagnosed in utero, at birth or later in life. An estimated 6% of babies are born with CA globally every year, and approximately 94% of these anomalies occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1,4 Globally, CAs are responsible for 25.3–38.8 million disability-adjusted life-years and caused an estimated 549 000 deaths in 2019.14,7

Efforts to reduce infant mortality through systematic vaccinations, early identification and increasingly successful management of infectious illnesses have increased the proportionate contribution of birth defects to the childhood burden of disease. The aetiology of birth defects remains largely unknown8 and antenatal care may not be available in many LMIC settings4; thus, surgical correction is invariably required to treat a large proportion of structural birth defects. This puts an additional strain on already fragile health systems in low-resource settings.

Birth defect surveillance systems were established in response to the thalidomide tragedy in the 1950s9 to put in place systems for monitoring the frequency of birth defects, identifying geographic clusters and conducting research on risk factors. To facilitate data sharing among different countries, two international consortia dedicated to birth defect monitoring were created. In 1974, the International Clearinghouse for Birth Defects Monitoring Systems was established, which has since evolved into the International Clearinghouse for Birth Defects Surveillance and Research (ICBDSR),9 and in 1979, the European Congenital Anomalies and Twins10 consortium was formed. Since then, six international birth defects collaborations have been established.11

Most of these registries or systematic surveillance for CAs are based in high-income countries (HICs), where systems for data collection and linkage from antenatal to perinatal and postnatal periods are better established. This allows epidemiological and outcome data on different types of anomalies to be collected and in many settings, for preventive and curative interventions to be planned and instituted. Implementing similar models in most LMICs is not feasible due to population dynamics, resource limitations and health system weaknesses. Despite these challenges, some registries have successfully been established in resource-limited settings and we aim to review them in detail to understand the strategies adopted, challenges faced and potential for replicability.

The WHO South East Asia Regional Office has established a Newborn and Birth Defects Database12; however, there is no similar database for the Eastern Mediterranean Region. At a country level, Iran has piloted a birth registry programme in Tabriz (Tabriz Registry of Congenital Anomalies (TRoCA)), which has helped in determining the incidence, geographical comparisons, treatment and service provision for selected anomalies and created a validated tool for doctors for case detection and diagnosis of birth defects.13 Recent studies have cited the lack of high-quality data as a major impediment to timely diagnosis and management, as well as to understanding neonatal outcomes in South Asia.12

The purpose of this scoping review was to systematically explore the literature for congenital anomalies registries (CARs), surveillance systems, facility and community-based screening programmes implemented in various LMIC settings. It aimed to identify, characterise and map the current knowledge on these initiatives, offering an analysis of CAR models and neonatal surveillance systems, and to develop an understanding of the challenges and facilitators for establishing CARs in LMICs. Key areas of focus encompassed surveillance methods, geographical scope, training and tools used, personnel involved, data management systems, scalability and long-term sustainability.

Materials and methods

In this scoping review, we included articles focusing on CARs in LMICs, as defined by the World Bank Criteria. We specifically considered registries that involved screening of neonates and stillbirths for externally visible CAs through physical examination. A protocol for this review was developed in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) protocol template for scoping reviews (online supplemental material). It was registered prospectively with Open Science Framework (OSF) on 16 February 2023, as an open-ended registration (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/YUCFW).

Eligibility criteria

The first review encompassed all surveillance models reported in the literature from the inception of the database to December 2022. A second, updated search covered publications from January 2023 to April 2025. These included single-centre to national-level surveillance, community or facility-based programmes, conducted by both medical and non-medical personnel. Both current and past operational registries, regardless of the duration of surveillance, were considered. Our focus was on externally visible anomalies, as classified by the WHO,14 that were present at birth or identified during the neonatal period; studies covering a broader age range were included only if newborns were part of the study population. Since the focus was on anomalies identified on physical examination, any registries that solely relied on tests or interventions to establish diagnosis were excluded. For multi-country collaborative registries, we required at least 50% of enrolled participants and/or countries to be classified as LMICs. We included articles with authors from both HICs and LMICs, as long as the primary implementation site was an LMIC setting.

Only articles published in English were included since most LMIC countries use English as the language for scientific communication and the authors are fluent in the language. Studies published in languages other than English were identified during the search process. Abstracts of non-English articles were translated using Google Scholar and screened for eligibility. Full-text articles were included only if an English version was available, as full-text translation was not feasible. A list of potentially relevant non-English publications identified during screening has been provided in an appendix to support future research.

Information sources and search

Eight bibliographic databases were searched to secure a comprehensive review of literature from LMICs with a focus on a broad range of global health topics: PubMed; Epistomonikos; Global Index Medicus; EMBASE PakMediNet; Google Scholar; CINAHL; EBSCO; EMBASE. The search strategies were drafted by an experienced Health Sciences Librarian (KA) and further refined through team discussion. All search results were exported into EndNote X9 and merged. The final search strategy for Medline can be found in online supplemental appendix A.

Study selection

Duplicates were removed using SR accelerator (https://sr-accelerator.com/#/). De-duplicated records were uploaded into Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai), a web-based tool, to facilitate a two-stage screening process. Two independent reviewers screened all abstracts. Disagreements were resolved through consensus, with a third reviewer resolving any outstanding conflicts. Full-text versions of selected articles were retrieved and a similar process of independent screening was carried out. A tool was developed on Microsoft Word for literature review based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria that were developed to guide objective screening of full-text articles (table 1).

Data variables

Data charting

A data-charting form was developed on Microsoft Excel to extract information on predetermined variables. After a pretest on five studies, data extraction was conducted by one reviewer and double-checked by a second reviewer. The two reviewers discussed the results and updated the form in an iterative process. Any inconsistencies or disagreements were addressed and resolved through discussion or the involvement of a third reviewer to ensure consistency. The characteristics studied were grouped into three categories: that is, publication, registry and surveillance characteristics. The final stage involved collating, summarising and reporting on the results of the collected data.

Data items and synthesis of results

The LMICs identified to have published papers were grouped into WHO regions. For each study, the years of active surveillance were recorded, with data reported in chronological order within each region. Additional information was charted on the geographic scope of implementation (regional vs national) and setting of implementation (hospital-based vs community-based). Publication and registry characteristics of each identified study were summarised. Surveillance characteristics of each study were extracted with a focus on screening personnel, screening methods used and the total number of individuals screened. The number and types of anomalies were also noted. Detailed publication, registry and surveillance characteristics are presented in online supplemental tables S1 and S2. Operational registries and their linked networks were recorded and summarised (table 2). Results

Selection of sources of evidence

A total of 12 328 initial abstracts were deduplicated. After deduplication, 6602 articles were included in the initial review process. The deduplicated citations were identified through comprehensive searches across electronic databases and by reviewing references in relevant articles. Subsequently, 6511 citations were excluded based on a thorough evaluation of their titles and abstracts, resulting in 92 full-text articles selected for further examination for eligibility. From these, articles were excluded for the following reasons: 39 did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria, 8 articles were published in languages other than English and 6 full-text papers could not be retrieved. The remaining 39 articles met the eligibility criteria and were considered suitable for inclusion in this scoping review. Registry and publication characteristics

A total of 39 studies in the time period from 1989 to 2025 met the inclusion criteria for this scoping review. The studies had one or more broad focus areas as follows: 19 studies (49%) prominently featured the terms ‘surveillance’ or ‘registry’ in their titles, and 17 studies (43%) were directly affiliated with registries or participated in surveillance networks.

These 39 studies have been grouped in supplementary 1 and 2 based on the WHO geographical regions. The largest number of studies was from the Region of the Americas (n=11), followed by the Eastern Mediterranean region (n=7), which was mainly from Iran. Similarly, of the seven studies reported from the South East Asian region, most were from India.

The scope of implementation of most studies was regional (N=29) (74%), and 10 (26%) were national registries or part of multi-country surveillance networks. The majority of studies were hospital-based (N=31) (79%), with a few extending additionally to community-based surveillance (N=7) (18%).

Since half of the papers included in this scoping review were incidence or prevalence studies of limited duration, they provided snapshots instead of ongoing surveillance data. Notably, nine national and subnational registries, and two multi-country surveillance networks, have been sustained in LMICs, including the National Registry of Congenital Anomalies of Argentina (RENAC), TRoCA, Thailand National Birth Defects Registry, The Philippine Birth Defects Surveillance, Ukrainian-American Birth Defects Prevention Programme (UABDP), Bogotá Congenital Anomalies Surveillance Programme (BCMSP), Programme for the Prevention and Surveillance of Birth Defects and Rare Diseases of Bogotá, Cali Congenital Anomalies Surveillance Programme (CCMSP), ReLAMC and ECLAMC.

Five registries stood out based on their sustainability, scale and/or publication volumes; their varying approaches are highlighted. Two linked networks, ECLAMC and ReLAMC, promote surveillance and research for CAs in Latin America. The ECLAMC42 started in 1967 as a voluntary network focused on the clinical and epidemiological investigation of aetiology of CAs, with the key objective of prevention of CAs through research. Since their creation, population-based national or regional congenital anomaly registries have been set up in parallel in several Latin American countries. In 2016, ReLAMC16 was created to fill this gap as a transnational network of Latin American national or regional registries, inclusive of ECLAMC. ReLAMC provides an open-access database for CAs in Latin America, providing a shared platform of regional registries to track variations in incidence, investigate aetiology, improve classification and coding of anomalies, and collectively advocate for prevention strategies. In contrast, RENAC1927,29 originated in Argentina as a hospital-based pilot study in 2009 with the goal of becoming a sustainable and scalable national system. Initially, it encompassed high-volume public maternity hospitals. In 2013, RENAC expanded to include non-public maternity hospitals; currently, 134 public and 7 non-public hospitals are included in this registry. The most successful Asian experience has been TRoCA,13 31 34 initiated as a pilot project in 2000 in Tabriz, Iran, to investigate the epidemiology of CAs. Building on the data, framework and foundational structure provided by TRoCA, a nationwide registry of CAs was established in 2012 and sustained to date by the government.

Surveillance characteristics

The 39 papers in this review included 31 papers with prospective data collection (79%), 6 were retrospective (15%), 2 studies included both prospective and retrospective data (5%).19 46 Surveillance characteristics from three papers each from Iran (TRoCA) and Argentina (RENAC) are combined and presented. In all instances, the primary method employed for identifying CAs was physical examination. Only nine of these studies supplemented their diagnosis with additional diagnostic tests (23%). While all studies incorporated live births in their inclusion criteria, stillbirths were additionally reviewed in 17 studies (43%), and 4 studies included spontaneous abortions.15 20 25 35 In terms of the type of anomalies included, 22 studies encompassed both major and minor anomalies (56%), while 16 studies focused on major anomalies alone (41%). Two studies exclusively collected epidemiological data related to orofacial clefts,21 41 while one study solely focused on clubfoot.52 The International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision coding system was used for the classification of anomalies in most papers, with additional parameters added in some instances.

The screening of newborns was conducted solely by doctors in 16 registries (41%), whereas in 5 registries a combination of doctors, nurses, midwives and healthcare workers (13%) was responsible for newborn examination. In four studies from Uganda,20 33 Guatemala18 and Vietnam,30 screening was exclusively carried out by trained healthcare workers or midwives. In nine studies (23%), photographs were taken when an anomaly was identified to assist in the diagnostic process. In four instances, follow-up calls were done to monitor outcomes and detect delayed presentation of anomalies (10%).17 25 26 39

Of the reported anomalies, neurological anomalies were the most common, followed by musculoskeletal and cardiovascular anomalies. These trends were consistently observed across the studies included in this scoping review.

Discussion

This scoping review evaluates CAs surveillance systems and registries instituted in LMICs. The registry and surveillance characteristics are explored and summarised, providing valuable insights into the feasibility of screening and reporting CAs in resource-limited settings.

Surveillance programmes for CAs rely on frontline healthcare providers to examine newborns.20 51 Midwives, birth attendants, doctors and nurses, who are in contact with the newborn at, or soon after, birth have a unique opportunity to examine the baby, identify anomalies early and provide required referral and counselling to families. Many LMICs face critical shortages of health workforce53; in such settings, the value of non-medical workforce for frontline care becomes increasingly important. A task-sharing approach using lay health workers for CAs screening has been demonstrated to be comparable to screening conducted by doctors and nurses,51 encouraging the incorporation of CAs screening as a component of training curricula for health workers providing maternal and newborn care.

A majority of CARs established in LMICs focus on hospital-based surveillance, as evidenced by this scoping review, where 25 of the 33 registries were primarily hospital-based. This approach proves effective in countries like Argentina and Colombia,19 where the vast majority of births take place in hospitals. However, this hospital-centric model is not suitable for those LMICs where a significant proportion of births occur at home or in non-hospital settings.54 The reliance on hospital-based registries in such countries inevitably leads to under-reporting of CAs. More concerningly, the lowest socioeconomic strata in LMICs tend to opt for home births, and the association of CAs with lower socioeconomic status is well established; therefore, focusing on hospital births alone may lead to a significant under-reporting of anomalies in LMIC settings and subsequent marginalisation of these underserved populations in designing service delivery approaches. In such situations, community-based registries or integration of newborn screening with existing points of maternal and childcare in the health system is needed as part of a comprehensive strategy to record and address CAs.

While the burden of CAs is undeniably substantial, allocating financial resources to set up and maintain CARs is a complex decision, particularly for resource-constrained LMICs. The paradox is that while community-based surveillance is required to meet many LMIC needs, this is even more expensive to sustain as compared with hospital-based registries. Thus, most policymakers in LMICs, understandably given the competing priorities, do not commit to comprehensive national-level surveillance for CAs. Of the 33 registries established in LMICs between 1989 and 2022, only nine national and regional-level registries are currently functional, including: RENAC, TRoCA, Thailand National Birth Defects Registry, The Philippine Birth Defects Surveillance, UABDP, BCMSP, CCMSP, ReLAMC and ECLAMC. These challenges underscore the need for pragmatic approaches to funding allocation in developing impactful CARs, with an end-goal of better care and improved outcomes for affected newborns rather than to sustain epidemiologically rich registries alone. An essential focus on providing preventive interventions, counselling and referral support, in addition to identifying and recording anomalies, will make a stronger case for establishing and sustaining surveillance for CAs in resource-constrained settings. Redirecting resources to develop region-specific care pathways and investing in children’s care, especially surgical interventions at the district level, can significantly reduce long-term morbidity and mortality from CAs. This approach supports parents and ultimately eases the burden on the healthcare system.

Another strategy that might help ensure the sustainability of CARs is to focus on a minimum dataset for routine evaluations. This approach was successfully adopted by TRoCA,31 in which limited, essential data variables are recorded, thus increasing the likelihood of key information being collected as well as providing an opportunity for additional information to be recorded for subsets of affected children as and when needed. A notable example of a broader system-level effort is India’s Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) programme, which, although its studies did not meet our eligibility criteria, goes beyond routine screening to support early identification, referral and management of nine prioritised congenital defects. The programme conducts large-scale screening at birth, during early home visits and across educational settings by both physician and non-physician personnel.

A Guatemalan study annexed the CAR data component to the existing Maternal Newborn Health Registry, thus reducing time and effort input.18 By focusing on a minimum dataset and avoiding duplication in data collection, we can not only alleviate the burden on frontline health providers but also establish a robust foundation for ongoing research, counselling and targeted regional interventions. Additionally, integrated service delivery platforms, like RBSK, can complement these efforts by linking screening with management, thereby ensuring continuity of care.

The evolving health patterns in emerging economies result in a decreasing contribution of infectious disease and a proportionate increase in the effect of CAs on neonatal mortality rates.17 Therefore, despite funding limitations, it is imperative to recognise the long-term benefits of investing in CARs and care systems as part of a comprehensive public health strategy. As demonstrated by Colombia and Iran, well-run CARs become essential instruments for public health campaigns and policy-making targeted at lowering the incidence of CAs. In order to keep these registries up to date, government organisations and health ministries become essential partners. An Indian maternal cohort study also made a significant contribution by emphasising the registry’s critical significance in predicting future health requirements, particularly with regard to comprehensive prenatal care and paediatric surgery services. This highlights the key role of registries in the development of public health policies and healthcare system architecture. Thus, registries need to be at the forefront of promoting policy change in LMICs since they are essential for resource allocation, prioritising interventions and making well-informed decisions.

Strengths and limitations

The scoping review was conceptualised and conducted in close adherence to the guidelines published by the JBI. A team approach was adopted with two reviewers conducting screening independently, with arbitration by a third reviewer where needed. A comprehensive literature search was conducted, and an attempt to screen a wide range of relevant databases and grey literature was made. However, this search was restricted to English-language literature, thus potentially excluding perspectives in other languages. The intent for this to be an exhaustive review was balanced against feasibility and time constraints. Every attempt was made to include published grey literature in this review; however, it is possible that national-level policies and similar publications may not be accessible online. This review was limited to LMICs with established CAs surveillance or registry systems. Consequently, LMICs that may lack formal surveillance or registry infrastructure were not included in the analysis despite a high burden of CAs. Despite these limitations, we recognise that this is one of the few published reviews on CAs surveillance approaches and among the first to focus on this topic in LMIC settings.

Conclusions

In summary, this scoping review includes 39 studies spanning from 1989 to April 2025 on CAs surveillance in LMICs. The registries were predominantly hospital-based, with regional and national implementations. Notable registries like RENAC (multinational) and TRoCA (regional) showcase successful and scalable models. Doctors were the primary screening personnel, using prospective and retrospective approaches with physical examination as the primary identification method.

The study also emphasised the crucial role of frontline healthcare workers, advocating for their training to enhance early anomaly detection. It sheds light on the predominance of hospital-based registries in LMICs and proposes alternative strategies, such as community-based approaches, to ensure comprehensive data representation. Overall, the findings stress the importance of sustained commitment, diversified approaches and strategic funding to effectively address the challenges associated with CAs surveillance on a global scale.
