Using Multi-Omic Signatures in the Understanding of Inflammation and Psychosis: Can Methylation-Derived White Blood Cell Proportions Guide Early Intervention?
Authors: Scott Richard Clark, Victoria Kiriaki Arnet, Magdalene C. Jawahar, Catherine Toben, K. Oliver Schubert, Azmeraw T. Amare
Published: 2025

In a first-of-its-kind study, Barker et al. (1) describe the relationships between white cell proportions derived from DNA methylation profiles, methylation probes, and differentially expressed genes in a sample from the STEP (Staged Treatment in Early Psychosis) Study. STEP, a sequential multiple assignment randomized clinical trial, evaluated a complex sequential intervention to improve outcomes in youth at ultra-high-risk (UHR) for psychosis. In this multisite study, 342 UHR patients completed: 1) 6 weeks of supported problem solving (SPS); 2) 20 weeks of cognitive behavioral case management (CBCM) versus SPS; or 3) 26 weeks of CBCM with fluoxetine versus CBCM with placebo with the option to switch to ω-3 fatty acids or low-dose antipsychotic medication if required. Participants who remitted at any stage were randomized to SPS or monitoring for up to 12 months. Consistent with recent UHR literature, the 12-month transition rate to full-blown psychosis was modest at 13.5%, with low rates of remission of UHR symptoms (27% at any stage) and high rates of subsequent relapse into the UHR state over 12 months (37.7%–65.1% depending on stage and group). There was no significant effect of study interventions on symptoms, function, or transition. Overall, the negative findings of the main study highlight the challenge of implementing complex interventions in real-world clinical environments.

The links between inflammatory response and psychotic illness are complex, subsuming multiple integrated and overlapping processes at the levels of immune activation, neuronal signaling, synapse organization, and brain connectivity that are developmental, acute, and chronic in nature (2). Studies are confounded by cross-sectional sampling of emergent and fluctuating measures, gene-environment interactions, and epigenetic effects that are difficult to adequately adjust for in analyses. In this context, the combination of multi-omics with detailed clinical phenotyping and longitudinal follow-up suggests that in the future, researchers will be able to connect the dots between genotype, exposure, molecular mechanisms, and heterogenous clinical expression of inflammation and psychosis risk. While -omic science has developed rapidly, genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic domains individually only explain limited variance in complex mental health outcomes and are not yet clinically translatable (3). The emerging field of multi-omics seeks to integrate this information, building more comprehensive understanding of molecular networks that are affected by inheritance, environmental exposure, and biological regulation and their association with complex illnesses and behavioral phenotypes.

The early innate immune response is mediated by specific white cell subsets including neutrophils, macrophages, natural killer cells, eosinophils, basophils, and mast cells, while B and T lymphocytes and dendritic cells mediate targeted secondary defenses. Neutrophil counts are consistently elevated in patients with first-episode psychosis and schizophrenia compared with controls, while studies of lymphocytes show lower levels or no difference. However, the ratio between neutrophils and lymphocytes (NLR) is consistently elevated in psychosis regardless of phase or severity, while antipsychotic treatment may normalize NLR (4). There is substantial genetic correlation between schizophrenia and white cell counts, which was estimated at 32% to 59% across 383 specific shared genetic loci involving both innate and adaptive immune mechanisms, with the correlation being strongest for lymphocytes (5). Surprisingly, there is only 1 previous study of NLR in a high-risk for psychosis group. Mekori-Domachevsky et al. (6), in a small sample of patients with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22q11.2DS), found that NLR was higher in people with psychotic and high-inflammation non-psychotic 22q11.2DS than in controls and was positively associated with negative symptoms and poorer function. Overall, there have been few studies of inflammation in the UHR syndrome in general; however, evidence suggests altered cytokine levels, with elevated interleukin (IL)-6 and low IL-1β in comparison to controls, consistent with low-grade chronic inflammation (7).

In this study, Barker et al. (1) analyzed gene expression and methylation profiles in a subsample of 90 STEP trial UHR participants, 31 of whom achieved UHR state remission after steps 1 or 2 of the trial, and 60 non-remitters across trial arms. They used methylation data to derive white cell-type proportions before analyzing the relationships between gene expression/methylation and remission while considering cell-type proportions. Adjusted for demographic and clinical covariates including childhood trauma, symptoms of depression (Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale), and the presence of comorbid psychiatric diagnosis, baseline lymphocyte cell proportions (odds ratio [OR] 0.23, 95% CI 0.07–0.62, p = 9.2 × 10−3) and NLR (OR 2.9, 95% CI, 1.37–7.46, p = .012) were significantly associated with subsequent remission status. These findings suggest that lymphocyte proportions were significantly lower in future remitters than in non-remitters; this was particularly true for natural killer cells after adjusting for clinical covariates (OR 0.16, 95% CI, 0.03–0.57, p = .012). In comparison, a recent meta-analysis of studies of children and youth up to 18 years of age, which considered a range of subthreshold psychosis criteria and excluded UHR individuals, found higher total white blood cell count, NLR, tumor necrosis factor, C-reactive protein, and IL-6 in participants with full-threshold psychosis but not subthreshold psychotic symptoms compared with controls (8). In the largest meta-analysis reported to date of immune cells in adult patients with schizophrenia, leucocyte, granulocyte, neutrophil granulocyte, monocyte, and B lymphocyte counts were all elevated compared with controls, as were the neutrophil/lymphocyte, monocyte/lymphocyte, and platelet/lymphocyte ratios (4). Together, these analyses confirm that psychosis is associated with a peripheral pro-inflammatory state but suggest this association may be dependent on symptom severity early in illness.

As Barker et al. (1) point out, their study of UHR remission status is not directly comparable to a case-control analysis, and the lack of absolute cell counts makes interpretation challenging. Common confounders that modify inflammation including body mass index, smoking, chronic inflammatory conditions, anti-inflammatory drug use, and timing of sample collection are also missing from their analysis (7). Furthermore, there are no proteomic data on cytokines or C-reactive protein to further map peripheral inflammatory status at the time of sampling. Cell counts or proportions alone are likely insufficient to describe the complexity of inflammatory status in psychosis; instead, immune cell function, blood-brain barrier permeability, and central inflammation, including activation of the microglial system, may be more relevant to brain function (9). To explain heterogeneity in results, some authors have suggested that psychosis can be stratified into high and low inflammatory groups. In contrast, the emerging concept of inflamm-aging proposes that chronic activation of the innate immune system leads to an increase in neutrophils and secondary mediators such as cytokines and auto-antibodies but also dysfunction in both innate and adaptive cell subsets (9). Inflamm-aging has been linked to cognitive decline, poor physical health, and early mortality in psychosis and is thought to arise from genomic risk and environmental exposures, including infection. Finally, the psychosis prodrome as defined in the UHR state has a range of heterogenous outcomes including persistence, resolution, and fluctuation of attenuated psychotic symptoms; mood disorder; and comorbidity with autism spectrum and personality disorders that may confound analysis of relationships between psychotic symptoms and inflammation (10). Different basal patterns of immune activation and responsiveness may be predictive of affective over primary psychotic trajectory of illness; however, more studies are needed (11). Given the complexity of these findings and the variation across study designs, there is a need for robust, well-controlled studies with longitudinal data that consider the limitations outlined above.

In this underpowered study, Barker et al. (1) reported no statistically significant association between UHR remission status and gene expression or methylation profiles. However, correction for cell proportions removed potentially spurious -omic associations with remission status. Accounting for the cellular heterogeneity in complex tissues such as whole blood has been recognized as essential in epigenome-wide association studies for more than a decade, with clear evidence that shifts in immune cell proportions can confound observed differences in biomarkers such as DNA methylation (12). This is particularly relevant in whole blood, where inflammation, from stressors, and comorbid diseases can alter white cell composition and the dynamic immune landscape. While designing studies to incorporate gold-standard methods to directly measure and adjust for cellular heterogeneity is ideal, it is not always feasible, especially in retrospective analyses. However, various computational and experimental tools that can estimate and adjust for cell-type heterogeneity can be considered where appropriate, including single-cell epigenetic approaches, with reporting of both adjusted and unadjusted results for more balanced interpretability (12).

Although the study is confounded by its complex trial design, Barker et al. (1) provide evidence that baseline immune cell profiles, lymphocytes, and NLR are associated with remission in youth at UHR of psychosis. Their findings also underscore the importance of cell-type adjustment in psychiatric -omics research. Sequential clinical trials such as STEP with small subgroups are not ideal for discovery based-omics due to their limited power and variability in exposure between arms. Future longitudinal studies should rigorously control for the full range of confounders of inflammatory status. While there is emerging evidence for anti-inflammatory treatments in psychosis, much more evidence is required to consider the potential for methylation-derived white blood cell proportions to guide early intervention (13).
