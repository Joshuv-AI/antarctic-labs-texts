Protocol for analyzing arginase I expression in tumor-associated myeloid-derived suppressor cells from murine colon cancer using flow cytometry
Authors: Amin Zakeri, Keman Zhang, Li Lily Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryArginase1 (ARG1) is a metabolic enzyme that is highly expressed in tumor-associated myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and causes the dysfunction of tumor-reactive T cells. Here, we present a protocol for detecting ARG1 expression in tumor MDSCs from a murine model of colon cancer using flow cytometry. We describe steps for tumor tissue processing, antibody staining, and data acquisition. We then detail procedures for identifying MDSC subsets and detecting ARG1 expression using a precise gating strategy.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Zhang et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Cell isolation, Flow Cytometry, Immunology, Model Organisms, Antibody

Materials and equipment

Liberase stock

To prepare 5 mg/mL Liberase stock solution, add 1 mL Mili-Q water to the bottle containing Liberase powder (5 mg). Dissolve the powder by gently shaking at 4°C for 20 min. Sterilize the solution by passing through 0.2 μM filter. Aliquot 500 μL per tube and store at −80°C for up to one year.

DNase I stock

To prepare 20 mg/mL DNase I stock solution, add 50 mL IMDM media to DNase I powder (1 g). Dissolve the powder by gently shaking at 4°C for 20 min. Sterilize the solution by passing through 0.2 μM filter. Aliquot as 500 μL per tube and store at −80°C for up to one year.

Step-by-step method details

MC38 colon cancer cell culture, harvest, and inoculation in mice

Timing: 90 min

Here we describe the steps of culturing and harvesting MC38 colon cancer cells. MC38 cells have been validated as mycoplasma-free and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen. The handling of cells, buffers, culture media, and other reagents are performed in BSL-II biosafety cabinets.

1.Prepare cell culture of MC38 colon cancer cells.a.Prewarm a bottle of complete RPMI media at 37°C in a water bath.b.Take one vial of MC38 cells out of the liquid nitrogen tank and place it in the 37°C water bath.c.Swirl the tube gently to warm it up for 1–2 min. Observe the cells and stop when the frozen cells are 80% thawed. Spray the vial with 70% ethanol to sterilize the external.d.Carefully open the vial and transfer the cells to a 15 mL conical tube preloaded with 4 mL complete RPMI media. Invert the tube two times to mix the cells.e.Use a pipette to take out 10 μL aliquot and mix with trypan blue for cell counting and viability check. Only proceed if cells are more than 95% viable.f.Centrifuge MC38 cells at 500 × g for 5 min at 4°C, aspirate off the supernatant and resuspend the cells with 5 mL complete RPMI media by gently pipetting up and down several times.g.Transfer 2 × 106 MC38 cells into a cell culture dish (150 mm diameter), add 25 mL complete RPMI media and culture cells in an incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2 for three days.h.On day 3, examine cells under a microscope to verify that cells have reached 70% confluence due to exponential growth.

2.Harvest MC38 colon cancer cells for mice inoculation.a.Take the dish to the cell culture hood and remove the media by aspiration. Add 20 mL DPBS to wash the cells and aspirate off the DPBS.b.Add 4 mL DPBS supplemented with 2 mM EDTA and 2 mL Accutase to the dish and place the dish in the incubator for 3 min to detach the cells.c.Add 8 mL of the complete RPMI to deactivate the Accutase and use a 10 mL pipette to harvest cells to a 15 mL conical tube. Then centrifuge cells at 500 × g at 4°C for 5 min.d.Remove supernatant by aspiration and resuspend cells in 10 mL ice-cold 1× HBSS. Take 10 μL aliquot for cell count and viability check.e.Centrifuge cells again at 500 × g at 4°C for 5 min.f.Resuspend cells in ice-cold 1×HBSS at the density of 2 × 106/mL. Filter the cell suspension through a 30 μM cell strainer to remove cell aggregates and place cells in a 2 mL tube on ice.g.For mice inoculation, load an insulin syringe with cells and inject 100,000 cells in 50 μL by intradermal injection on the lower flank of the mice. Both male and female mice that are of C57BL/6 background and 6–8 weeks of age are used for experiments.h.Tumors grew for about 2 weeks before being harvested for flow cytometry analysis.

Harvest murine tumor tissues and process them into single-cell suspensions

Timing: 2–3 h

Here, we describe how to harvest MC38 colon tumor tissues from tumor-bearing mice and process them to obtain single-cell suspensions that are suitable for antibody staining. For human endometrial tumor tissues, we use the same DNase/Liberase enzymatic digestion and tissue dissociation protocols to obtain single-cell suspensions for antibody staining. The exact enzymes and processing procedures may need to be optimized for different tumor and tissue types.

3.Harvest tissues from mice.a.Euthanize mice using CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation, or according to your institutional approval protocols.b.Lay mice on its back and use sterile forceps and scissors. To expose the tumors, cut both skin and abdominal muscles.c.Remove the tumor-draining inguinal lymph node that is typically located near tumors growing on the lower flank. Remove excessive fat and connective tissues surrounding the tumor.CRITICAL: Before harvesting tumor tissues, carefully identify and remove the inguinal lymph node that drains the flank tumor mass. This is a critical step that prevents contaminating the tumor samples with lymph node cells.d.Collect the tumor tissues by using a sterile scalpel and put the tissues in an Eppendorf tube (5 mL size) containing 0.5–1 mL of the Tissue Digestion Buffer that contains Liberase and DNase I in serum-free RPMI medium. Leave the tissues on ice until further processing.Note: We recommend using a scalpel to dissociate the tumor tissue from the attached skin.Note: We typically harvest at least 8–10 individual tumors for each experimental group, such as WT mice vs mutant mice. Thus, we prepare at least 30 mL of Tissue Digestion Buffer for the experiment.e.Use a pair of sterile surgical scissors to repeatedly cut the tissues inside the tube and dissociate the large tumor tissues into smaller chunks (∼ 2 mm in diameter).Note: Rinse scissors with 70% ethanol to clean and sterilize between samples. If processing many samples, we recommend keeping all tissues on ice to prevent tissues from warming up.

Expected outcomes

For both murine and human tumor tissues, the enzymatic digestion and mechanical dissociation steps will yield single-cell suspensions that contain tumor cells, stromal cells, and immune cells. We expect that most solid tumors contain abundant amounts of MDSCs, which can be identified based on the surface protein markers as shown in Figure 1. For MC38 tumors, from 1 million total dissociated cells (mixed populations of tumors and lymphocytes and stromal cells), we could obtain >150,000 viable CD45+ TILs, among which about 40%–50% of cells were CD11b+ myeloid cells. For human endometrial tumors that were analyzed in this study, we found that the abundance of MDSCs was variable, which is likely the result of patient-specific immune responses.

Both M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs express ARG1, which can be detected using flow cytometry as described in this protocol. Variable expression levels of ARG1 may be detected in MDSCs from tumors with distinct immune microenvironments. One example is that in our studies, VISTA-deficient MDSCs expressed lower levels of ARG1 and showed defective T-cell suppressive function.1

Limitations

This protocol describes the steps for processing the endometrial tumor tissues. The enzymatic digestion and mechanical dissociation steps will generate single-cell suspensions with good overall viability in immune cells. However, poor cell viability or low yield may occur if using highly necrotic tumor tissues or due to prolonged processing time. The markers for identifying tumor-associated MDSC subsets, including M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs, are based on the current consensus in the literature. Future studies may identify additional markers to stratify additional MDSC subsets. ARG1 is highly expressed not only in MDSCs, but also in macrophages and some dendritic cells, which is not the focus of this protocol. Flow cytometry-based detection of ARG1 is sensitive and quantitative, but intracellular antibody staining may generate non-specific background signals and require alternative approaches, such as Western blotting, to validate results.

Problem 2

No signal or dim signal, or high level of background and nonspecific staining for intracellular protein markers.

Potential solution

Aside from following the troubleshooting steps in problem 1, one reason for the lack of staining may be insufficient permeabilization (Referring to “antibody staining” steps 6c). To improve permeabilization, it is critical to make the Permeabilization Buffer fresh, apply 200 μL permeabilization buffer to cells, and allow 20 min for permeabilization. It is also critical to titrate the antibodies using a positive control sample.

Problem 3

Low viability of tumor-associated immune cells.

Potential solution

This problem may occur due to prolonged processing time with many samples (Referring to “harvest murine tumor tissues and process them into single-cell suspensions”). Strategies to solve this problem include:

•Reduce the processing time by doing extensive preparations in advance.

•Team up with a partner during tumor dissociation steps.

•Keep cells at 4°C at all times, except during enzymatic digestion.

Problem 4

Insufficient recovery of tumor-associated immune cells.

Potential solution

CD45+ tumor-infiltrating immune cells are a minority population within the tumor tissues and their abundance varies greatly depending upon tumor types In the untreated murine MCD38 tumors, less than 10% of cells are CD45+ immune cells. Referring to “harvest murine tumor tissues and process them into single-cell suspensions”, step 4: For some tumor types, the enzymatic digestion may be extended to 40 min to release immune cells. Using a shaker during digestion will further improve tissue dissociation. If a rare immune cell subset is the desired study target, we recommend using a Percoll gradient to enrich lymphocytes.

Problem 5

Cell aggregation and clumping during flow cytometry analysis.

Potential solution

Aggregated cells are not permissive for flow cytometry analysis. Referring to “antibody staining”, step 6: To reduce cell clumping, we suggest.

•Resuspend cells in a sufficient amount of FACS buffer, for example, 300–500 μL for 1 million cells.
