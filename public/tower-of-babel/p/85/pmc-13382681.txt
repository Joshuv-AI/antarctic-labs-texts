[EMIm]NO3‑Modified PVDF–HFP Ionic Gel Electrolyte Membranes for Improved Lithium-Ion Transport and Interfacial Stability
Authors: Ruifeng Wang, Huangxin Cheng, Jiangnan Li, Gengzhe Shen, Yu Li, Jingmei Lyu, Zhengjian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gel polymer electrolytes typically suffer from low ionic conductivity, limited oxidative stability, and thermal runaway. Nitrate additives can be beneficial, but they are limited by poor solubility. Herein, sandwich-structured ionic liquid gel electrolyte membranes were fabricated by incorporating a polyethylene (PE) interlayer between two poly­(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF–HFP) layers loaded with LiTFSI and 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium nitrate ([EMIm]­NO3). The PFENE3 membrane prepared with 22.9 wt % [EMIm]­NO3 shows optimal overall performance. Specifically, the PE scaffold provides robust mechanical strength (36.7 MPa). The incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3 induces multiple synergistic effects: (i) increasing ionic conductivity to 5.03 × 10–4 S cm–1 at 25 °C by suppressing PVDF–HFP crystallinity; (ii) achieving a high Li+ ion transference number (0.449) by confining other charge carriers within the TFSI-[EMIm]+-NO3 hydrogen-bonding network; and (iii) the sacrificial decomposition of NO3 to form robust protective interphases on both the anode and cathode. Consequently, Li/PFENE3/Li symmetric cells demonstrated stable cycling for over 600 h at 0.3 mA cm–2. Furthermore, the Li/PFENE3/NCM622 full cell delivered an initial capacity of 170.9 mAh g–1 at 0.1 C and 159.3 mAh g–1 at 0.5 C, with 78% retention after 100 cycles, significantly outperforming [EMIm]­NO3-free systems. These findings provide a viable strategy for developing high-performance and safe Li-metal batteries.

Introduction

Liquid electrolytes, widely used in lithium-ion batteries, pose significant safety and performance challenges, such as flammability, leakage, thermal instability and lithium dendrite growth, which can lead to short circuits and thermal runaway. Solid-state electrolytes are considered a promising solution to the aforementioned challenges, but still face many technical bottlenecks in practical applications. For example, oxide electrolytes possess high chemical stability and mechanical strength but suffer from brittleness and poor interfacial contact with electrodes. Sulfide electrolytes offer excellent ionic conductivity and good interfacial compatibility, yet they are highly sensitive to moisture and prone to interfacial degradation. Polymer electrolytes are flexible and have good contact with electrodes, but demonstrate low ionic conductivity. Gel polymer electrolytes (GPEs) effectively bridge liquid and solid systems by embedding a liquid electrolyte in a solid polymer matrix. This combines liquid-like ionic conductivity with solid-like mechanical stability and leak resistance. GPEs mitigate metal dendrite growth through the homogenization of ion flux and the stabilization of interfaces, and also allow the integration of functional additives such as flame retardants to improve safety and performance.

Poly­(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF–HFP) is a promising semicrystalline fluoropolymer matrix for GPEs. Its copolymer structure balances mechanical support from VDF with enhanced flexibility and low crystallinity from HFP. Key properties include a high dielectric constant (∼8.4), which aids lithium salt dissociation, good thermal stability, and strong hydrophobicity and mechanical flexibility. These properties render PVDF–HFP-based GPEs particularly suitable for lithium metal batteries, offering enhanced safety, wide electrochemical windows (>4.3 V), and suppression of lithium dendrite growth through physical blocking and more uniform ion distribution. Nonetheless, PVDF–HFP still confronts several formidable challenges, including inherently low ionic conductivity (∼10–5 S cm–1), insufficient mechanical strength to robustly inhibit dendrite growth, high interfacial resistance between electrode and electrolyte, and inadequate long-term stability with high-voltage cathodes. , Future research should focus on multiscale structural design (e.g., continuous ion-conductive paths), solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) engineering (e.g., SEI forming agent LiNO3), exploring novel composite fillers (e.g., deep eutectic solvent for improving ionic conductivity to 10–3 S cm–1), to enhance performance for practical high-energy battery applications.

Nitrate (NO3 –)-based additives play a crucial role in lithium–metal batteries by modulating SEI composition and stabilizing the lithium anode. Mechanistically, NO3 – reduction facilitates an inorganic-rich SEI containing Li3N, LiN4O X , and Li2O, which improves ionic conductivity, promotes uniform lithium deposition/stripping, and suppresses dendrite growth. , Meantime, NO3 – also modifies Li+ solvation structures, mitigates solvent decomposition, and enhances interfacial kinetics. , The most commonly used NO3 – additive is LiNO3, but its solubility is limited (e.g., ∼0.012 M in carbonate electrolytes). Strategies to improve the solubility include cosolvents (e.g., tetraglyme), multiple salt components, solubility promoters (e.g., CsF), and encapsulation in polymers.

Ionic liquids (ILs) offer distinctive electrolyte properties due to their modular cationic and anionic structures, enabling tunable physicochemical properties such as nonflammability, wide electrochemical stability, and unique solvation behavior. When incorporated into polymer matrices to form IL-based gel polymer electrolytes (IL-GPEs), these systems create a hybrid that merges the benefits of both liquid and solid-state electrolytes. , This design not only delivers high ionic conductivity and excellent electrode wettability, but also significantly suppresses the risks of leakage, volatility and flammability of electrolytes, therefore enhancing the overall battery safety. Moreover, the unique solvation environment of ILs can favorably modulate the lithium-ion flux and interfacial kinetics, contributing to more reversible lithium plating/stripping. ,

In this study, to address the key challenges of GPEs, such as insufficient ionic conductivity, poor interfacial stability, and risk of thermal runaway, the ionic liquid 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium nitrate ([EMIm]­NO3) was integrated into a three-layer GPE, which consisted of PVDF–HFP/polyethylene (PE)/PVDF–HFP. The prepared GPEs exhibit a set of advantages: reduced PVDF–HFP crystallinity alongside high ionic conductivity, improved lithium-ion transference number, enhanced oxidation stability, robust mechanical strength, along with elevated ability to suppress lithium dendrite growth. These properties collectively contributed to significantly enhanced performance in both Li/Li symmetric cell and Li/NCM622 full-cell configurations. These results offer insight into a viable strategy for developing high-performance and safe lithium metal batteries.

Experimental Section

Materials

The chemicals and materials primarily include LiNi0.6Co0.2Mn0.2O2 (NCM622, Canrd), polyvinylidenefluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene pellets (PVDF–HFP, MW = 400,000, Sigma-Aldrich), polyethylene membrane (PE, 10 μm, 41% porosity, Canrd), lithium bis­(trifluoromethane)­sulfonimide (LiTFSI) (99.9%, DoDoChem), Super P carbon black (TimCal), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium nitrate ([EMIm]­NO3, ≥98% (HPLC), Aladdin), N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP, ≥99.9%, Aladdin), Li metal anode (20 μm in thick, Canrd), coin-type cells (CR2032, Canrd), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF powder, MW = 600,000, Arkema), aluminum foil collector (20 μm in thick, Canrd).

Preparation of Gel Electrolyte Membranes

First, 0.60 g of LiTFSI and a certain amount (0, 0.12, 0.24, 0.36, or 0.48 g) of [EMIm]­NO3 were dissolved in 5.0 g of NMP, where the molar ratio of LiTFSI to [EMIm]­NO3 is 3.02, 1.51, 1.01 or 0.75, respectively (excluding the 0 g case). Then 0.60 g of PVDF–HFP was added and vigorously stirred at 60 °C for 4 h to form a slurry. The slurry was cast onto the PE membrane support (fixed on glass plate) using a scraper with a 100 μm gap, and after natural drying, another identical layer was applied to the opposite side of the membrane. The double-sided coated membranes were dried at 80 °C overnight, and then cut into discs with a diameter of 19 mm. The discs were further dried at 100 °C under vacuum for 24 h before transferring into a glovebox (H2O <0.01 ppm, O2 <0.01 ppm) for cell assembly. The residual NMP in the gel electrolyte was extracted using ethanol and determined on an Agilent GC–MS 7820A-5977E equipped with a flame ionization detector, as listed in Table . Based on the amount of [EMIm]­NO3 incorporated (w/w), the obtained gel electrolyte membranes were designated as PFE (0 g, 0%), PFENE1 (0.12 g, 9.0%), PFENE2 (0.24 g, 16.5%), PFENE3 (0.36 g, 22.9%), and PFENE4 (0.48 g, 28.4%). Battery Assembly

A cathode slurry was prepared by mixing 0.80 g of NCM622, 0.10 g of PVDF and 0.10 g of Super-P in 6.0 mL NMP and defoaming using a planetary centrifugal mixer. The homogeneous cathode slurry was cast onto Al foil with an average mass loading of ∼3.0 mg cm–2. After vacuum-drying at 80 °C for 24 h, the cathode sheet was punched into 12 mm diameter discs, which were transferred into the glovebox. Both Li–Li symmetric cells and Li-NCM622 batteries with gel electrolyte separators were assembled into CR2032-coin cells in the glovebox with H2O/O2 <0.01 ppm.

Property Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging and elemental energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were carried out on a Sigma 500 (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) at an operating voltage of 10 kV. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a D8 Advance (Bruker, Germany) X-ray diffractometer with a Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were measured using a Bruker Tensor 27 infrared spectrophotometer equipped with attenuated total reflectance (ATR) technique. Thermogravimetry (TG), derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves were collected using a simultaneous thermal analyzer (TG 209 F3 Tarsus, Germany) under N2 atmosphere and a heating rate of 10 °C min–1. The tensile stress–strain behavior was determined using a Labthink C610 M universal tensile testing machine with samples of 50 × 5 mm2 (length × width) at a crosshead speed of 20 mm min–1. The thickness of the membranes was measured by a LDFT-A12 film thickness gauge with 0.1 μM resolution. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was recorded on a Thermo VG Scientific (Sigma Probe, Mg Kα source). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) characterization was performed at 300 keV using a Tecnai F30 microscope equipped with an Elsa 698.STP cooling system.

Porosity and electrolyte uptake are also key parameters for GPEs. However, in this work, the active liquid components (e.g., [EMIm]­NO3) were already embedded within the membrane matrix by direct casting, rendering the conventional immersion methods inapplicable for this purpose. Therefore, a deconvolution strategy was developed by decoupling the mass and volume contributions to the polymer matrix, the trapped native solution, and the absorbed probe liquid. Experimentally, a pristine PE film with 16 mm diameter was weighed (W PE), and its skeletal volume (V PE) was calculated using its intrinsic density (0.96 g cm–3). A freshly prepared GPE film of identical diameter was weighed to obtain its total mass (W GPE). The native liquid components were then removed by elution with anhydrous ethanol and subsequent vacuum drying. The residual dry mass consists of PE (WPE) and PVDF–HFP (WPH), allowing the mass of the native liquid to be determined as W L = W GPE – W PE – W PH. The skeletal volume of PVDF–HFP (V PH) was calculated from W PH and its density (1.78 g cm–3). The total volume of the native liquid (V L) was quantified from W L and the content ratios and densities of [EMIm]­NO3 (1.29 g cm–3), LiTFSI (2.02 g cm–3), and residual NMP (1.03 g cm–3). Finally, a fresh GPE film (16 mm diameter) was immersed in dibutyl phthalate (DBP) for 2 h. DBP was deliberately selected as a moderately polar and nonswelling probe liquid to preserve the PVDF–HFP skeleton and avoid component exchange with the native liquid species. After removing the surface-attached liquid, the film was reweighed. The mass of absorbed DBP (W DBP) was obtained by subtracting (W GPE), and its volume (V DBP) was calculated using its density (ρDBP: 1.05 g cm–3).

Porosity (P%) and electrolyte uptake (U) were calculated as follows (for the meaning of the symbols in the equations, please refer to the preceding context): 1 P(%)=(VL+VDBP)/(VPE+VPH+VL+VDBP)×100% 2 U=ρDBP(VL+VDBP)/(WPE+WPH)×100%

Electrochemical Measurements

The ionic conductivity (δ, S m–1) of the prepared gel electrolyte membranes was calculated by the following equation: 3 δ=L/RS where L, R, and S are the membrane thickness (m), bulk ohmic resistance (Ω), and electrolyte-electrode contact area (m2), respectively. The bulk ohmic resistance was measured by electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) of stainless-steel symmetric cells within a frequency range of 0.01 Hz–1 MHz under amplitude of 10 mV on a CHI760E electrochemical workstation.

Li+ ion transference number (tLi+) was measured by Bruce–Vincent method and calculated by the following equation: 4 tLi+=Iss(ΔV−I0R0)/I0(ΔV−IssRss) where I 0, I ss, R 0 and R ss are the initial current, steady-state current, initial interfacial resistance and steady-state interfacial resistance, respectively. Chronoamperometry and EIS measurements of Li/Li symmetric cells were conducted using a CHI760E electrochemical workstation under a voltage of 10 mV.

Galvanostatic charge and discharge measurements were performed on a battery testing system (L5 and CT2001A, China). Li/Li symmetric cells were cycled with 4-h charge/discharge period at 0.3 mA cm–2. Li/NCM622 batteries were operated within a voltage range of from 2.8 to 4.3 V at different C-rates (0.1–2 C). All the electrochemical tests were carried out at 30 °C. Three cells were tested for a single electrochemical experiment.

Results and Discussion

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the gel electrolyte membranes were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping, as typically shown in Figure . As seen in Figures a–b, lots of micropores are present on the surfaces of PFE and PFENE3, induced by the phase separation during solvent evaporation. The difference is that PFENE3 features slightly smaller and more uniform pores than PFE, most probably due to the presence of the nonvolatile [EMIm]­NO3 in PFENE3. Figures c–d depict the EDS mappings of two elements in PFENE3, where the N element belongs to [EMIm]­NO3 and LiTFSI and the F element comes from PVDF–HFP and LiTFSI. Evidently, [EMIm]­NO3 and LiTFSI are uniformly dispersed across the PVDF–HFP cross-linked network. Although TFSI– has weaker coordination ability than NO3 –, it forms distinct hydrogen bonds with the hydrogen atoms on the imidazolium ring. This promotes the mutual integration of [EMIm]­NO3 and LiTFSI to form a distinct hydrogen-bonding network. Figures e–f show the cross-sectional morphologies of PFE and PFENE3, which confirm their laminar sandwich structures. Furthermore, PFENE3 (27.8 μm) is thicker than PFE (22.2 μm). The excess thickness of PFENE3 (vs PFE) can be attributed to the retention of the nonvolatile [EMIm]­NO3 in film during the solvent evaporation. The cross-sectional EDS mappings in Figures g–h also demonstrate the sandwich structure and the close contact between layers.

The XRD patterns of the gel membranes are shown in Figure a. For PFE, the diffraction peaks located at 18.5° and 26.6° are assigned to the (100)/(020) and (021) planes of the α-crystalline phase of PVDF–HFP, respectively, while the broad peak centered at 20.0° is a composite feature resulting from the contributions of both the α-phase (110) and the β-phase (110) reflections. The additional (sharp) peaks at 21.5°, 44.1° and 50.1° in the XRD pattern of PFE represent the presence of LiTFSI crystalline in the binary polymeric gel electrolytes (LiTFSI/PVDF–HFP) with high salt concentrations (>40 wt %). By comparison, the incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3 significantly reduces the α-phase content while displaying a negligible effect on the β-phase. Notably, in the PFENE3 membrane (22.9 wt % of [EMIm]­NO3 content), the α-phase (021) reflection at 26.6° is almost completely eliminated. Suppressing the α-phase is essential for increasing polymer chain flexibility, thereby promoting high ionic conductivity of Li-ion electrolytes. While the stabilization of the β-phase is sought after for the advanced functional materials, owing to its superior ferroelectric, piezoelectric and pyroelectric properties. Meantime, the attenuation of the LiTFSI peaks in the ternary gel electrolytes (LiTFSI/PVDF–HFP/[EMIm]­NO3) indicates that [EMIm]­NO3 acts as an effective solvent. This interaction improves the dispersion of LiTFSI within the polymer matrix, which is crucial for enabling high-concentration salt gel electrolytes. This phenomenon is most evident in the PFENE3 composition.

XRD patterns (a) and FT-IR spectra (b) of the gel electrolyte membranes.

The FT-IR spectra of the samples are shown in Figure b. For the pristine PFE, the characteristic absorption bands of PVDF–HFP matrix could be clearly identified. Specifically, the peaks assigned to the nonpolar α-phase were observed at 614, 765, 795, and 1406 cm–1; the electroactive β-phase was evident from a single peak at 835 cm–1; and the amorphous phase was identified by the peaks at 876 and 1173 cm–1. The incorporation of LiTFSI was confirmed by the specific peaks belonging to the TFSI– anion. Specifically, the doublet located at 1349/1325 cm–1 is attributed to the asymmetric SO2 vibration, while the peak at 741 cm–1 is characteristic of the weakly coordinated TFSI– anion (νs S–N–S). Moreover, the characteristic bands of NMP in the FT-IR spectrum of PFE are either absent or significantly shifted (for example, a shift of the amide carbonyl peak from 1670 to 1648 cm–1). The substantial changes observed in the infrared absorption bands of NMP suggest that NMP is solvated by Li+ ions as Li+[NMP] x rather than existing as a free solvent, which is also proven by the coordination-induced peak at 570 cm–1. The relatively strong coordination of NMP with Li+ ions is the direct reason for its high retention in PFE after drying. A comparison of the IR spectra of PFE and PFENE3 reveals that the incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3 leads to several distinct changes to the electrolyte membrane. First, the characteristic peak of imidazolium (1574 cm–1) becomes clearly visible. Second, a sharp decrease in the intensity of the NMP peak at 1648 cm–1 reveals that the NO3 – anion of [EMIm]­NO3 outcompetes NMP in coordinating with Li+ ions, making NMP more volatile during drying and thus almost negligible in the membranes. This result agrees well with the residual NMP contents measured by GC–MS, as presented in Table . In the absence of competition from NO3 –, the NMP content in PFE was as high as 6.1 wt %, close to literature values. After the addition of [EMIm]­NO3, the NMP content decreased rapidly to only 0.85 wt % for PFENE3. Unlike abundant free solvent, which decomposes on the Li anode and compromises interfacial stability, a small amount of bound solvent may contribute to Li+ transport. Third, the subtle spectral shifts of PVDF–HFP upon incorporating [EMIm]­NO3 imply it slightly modulates the polymer microenvironment, by augmenting the amorphous phase to improve ion transport while maintaining stable backbone integrity and mechanical properties.

Porosity and electrolyte uptake are critical parameters for GPEs. However, in this work, since the active liquid components (such as [EMIm]­NO3 had already been integrated into the membrane matrix as intrinsic plasticizers via a direct casting method. Consequently, conventional direct immersion methods were inapplicable for determining porosity and uptake. To overcome this, an indirect determination strategy was developed, which mathematically decouples the mass and volume contributions of the polymer matrix, the native ionic liquid/salt solution, and the absorbed n-butanol.

Figure a presents the thermogravimetric (TG) plots of the membrane samples. The TG results demonstrate that PFENE3 exhibits the highest thermal stability, with decomposition temperatures at 1% and 5% mass loss measured at 206 and 267 °C, respectively, which are superior to those of the other samples: 176/233 °C for PFE, 119/244 °C for PFENE1, 131/244 °C for PFENE2, and 108/258 °C for PFENE4. The enhanced thermal resistance of PFENE3 is likely attributable to its unique internal microstructure, where the ternary components (LiTFSI/PVDF–HFP/[EMIm]­NO3) are integrated and mutually reinforced, as indicated by the above XRD analysis. Overall, the incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3 enhances the thermal stability of the membranes at elevated temperatures. A notable observation is the significantly larger weight drop for the PFE sample around 200 °C compared to the other samples (see the inset in Figure a). This can be related to the residual NMP in PFE, which is consistent with the above FT-IR results. Without the competitive coordination of [EMIm]­NO3, NMP is free to coordinate strongly with LiTFSI, allowing it to remain trapped within the membrane matrix during drying. Finally, there is a distinct inflection point at around 315 °C for all the [EMIm]­NO3-containing samples, which likely corresponds to the decomposition of [EMIm]­NO3 itself. This phenomenon is also reflected in the DTG curves (Figure b), manifested as a small peak near 315 °C.

TG plots (a), DTG curves (b), DSC traces (c), and tensile stress–strain curves (d) of the prepared electrolyte membranes.

Figure c shows the DSC traces of the prepared membranes. As observed, the broad and weak melting peaks of PVDF–HFP indicate its low crystallinity within the membranes. The incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3 significantly depresses the melting point of PVDF–HFP. This depression culminates in PFENE3, reaching a minimum melting point temperature at 105 °C. This phenomenon is credited to the plasticizing effect of [EMIm]­NO3. The ionic liquid molecules penetrate into the polymer matrix, diluting and shielding the interchain interactions and facilitating polymer segmental motion, and therefore lowering the polymer crystallinity. The plasticization-induced reduction in crystallinity is beneficial not only for having a low melting point and a wide process temperature range, but also for achieving high ionic conductivity. In contrast, PFENE4 reverses the above trend, exhibiting a higher PVDF–HFP melting point than PFENE3. A plausible explanation is that the excess NO3 – anions from the additional [EMIm]­NO3 interact with Li+ ions to form less soluble LiNO3 salt.

Figure d illustrates the tensile stress–strain curves of the membranes. As seen, the pristine PE membrane shows the highest tensile strength of 180.4 MPa, with an elongation at break of 36.1%. Following the deposition of the PVDF–HFP/LiTFSI composite material to both sides of the PE layer (forming the PFE membrane), the tensile strength significantly drops to 78.6 MPa (elongation at break: 38.9%). This reduction is primarily attributed to the membrane composition: in the total 22.2 μm thickness of PFE, the robust PE layer accounts for only 10 μm, while the remaining 12.2 μm is the delicate PVDF–HFP/LiTFSI composite. The incorporation of the [EMIm]­NO3 solvent further compromises the mechanical integrity of the membrane, because the penetration of [EMIm]­NO3 into the polymer network not only thickens the membrane (e.g., 27.8 μm for PFENE3), but also weakens the interchain cohesion. As a result, the tensile strength and elongation at break of PFENE3 are only 36.7 MPa and 22.4%, respectively. Nonetheless, the tensile strength of 36.7 MPa for PFENE3 is still superior to that of most PVDF–HFP-based electrolyte membranes, and exceeds the 20 MPa threshold commonly considered sufficient for dendrite suppression. ,

Porosity and electrolyte uptake are crucial properties for GPEs. Given that the prepared GPEs inherently contain liquid components, a deconvolution strategy was employed to determine these properties using eqs and . As presented in Table , the porosity and DBP uptake of the baseline PFE are 45.3% and 63.1%, respectively, which monotonically increase upon the incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3, culminating at 55.2% and 92.5% for PFENE4. Probably, [EMIm]­NO3 outcompetes NMP during drying and suppresses the rigid α-phase crystallinity of PVDF–HFP, thereby enlarging the amorphous domains to lock more liquid channels and leading to apparent thickness swelling (from 22.2 to 28.9 μm). Theoretically, such concurrent increments in porosity and electrolyte uptake pave the way for multidimensional, low-tortuosity ion-transport highways, which can facilitate rapid Li+-ion migration within the gelled polymer network.

By comparison, the incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3 significantly enhances the conductivity. This effect is due to the ability of [EMIm]­NO3 to expand the amorphous regions and reduce crystallinity, thereby creating more ion transport pathways. Notably, a higher [EMIm]­NO3 content leads to greater conductivity, with PFENE4 achieving a maximum of 6.07 × 10–4 S cm–1. The temperature dependence of the ionic conductivity for the prepared GPEs was determined and is presented in Figure b, which well obeys the Arrhenius behavior. To evaluate the impact of the PE interlayer on conductivity, a control membrane (denoted as PFNE3) was prepared. This membrane is identical to PFENE3 except for the absence of the PE layer. It had a thickness of 17.6 μm and achieved an ionic conductivity of 18.6 × 10–4 S cm–1. Compared to the PE-containing membrane, PFNE3 exhibits significantly higher conductivity, confirming that the PE layer severely inhibits ion transport. This suppression is primarily attributed to the physical barrier effect resulting from the low porosity (41%) of the PE interlayer. Furthermore, the increased overall thickness introduced by the PE interlayer further impedes efficient ion migration.

Since Li+ ions are the actual charge carriers for Li-ion battery operation within the overall ionic conductivity, determining the Li-ion transference number (tLi+) is critically important. Figures c–d display two typical chronoamperometry curves of Li/membrane/Li cells, and the insets show the Nyquist plots of the cells before and after the polarization. Accordingly, the initial current (I 0), steady-state current (I ss), initial interfacial resistance (R 0) and steady-state interfacial resistance (R ss) of these cells were extracted and used to calculate the tLi+ values by Bruce–Vincent method (eq ), as compiled in Table . The obtained tLi+ values range from 0.289 to 0.449, which are higher than those of the conventional liquid electrolytes (<0.3). By comparison, incorporating [EMIm]­NO3 was shown to effectively boost the tLi+ values of the gel membranes, with the tLi+ value for PFENE3 notably reaching up to 0.449. This improvement can be attributed to the plasticizing effect of [EMIm]­NO3 and TFSI–-[EMIm]+-NO3 – hydrogen-bonding network. The plasticization reduces polymer crystallinity, enhances segmental mobility, and thus provides more efficient pathways for Li+ migration. The confinement of hydrogen-bonded charge carriers enables Li+ ions to dominate charge transport. , Additionally, hopping transport mediated by Li+–anion interactions has also been demonstrated to enhance the Li+ transference numbers. A high tLi+ value is critical for achieving high-performance Li-ion batteries.

In comparison with recently reported IL-based GPEs ,− (Table ), PFENE3 exhibits competitive performance in terms of mechanical strength, conductivity, Li+ transference number, and electrochemical stability. Notably, due to the PE interlayer, its tensile strength (36.7 MPa) is far superior to that of other IL-GPEs (<4.78 MPa), and surpasses the 20 MPa benchmark typically regarded as adequate for inhibiting dendrite growth. ,

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted on Li/membrane/SS cells to evaluate the electrochemical stability of the membranes, as shown in Figure e. The cell with the pristine PFE membrane shows a steady potential window of 4.21 V (vs Li/Li+). This stability is significantly enhanced by the incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3, with the window progressively growing to 4.52 V for PFENE1, 4.67 V for PFENE2, and finally reaching 4.70 V for both PFENE3 and PFENE4. This observation aligns with the reported role of NO3 – in decomposing to form a protective electrode layer, thereby enhancing the interfacial stability. Additionally, [EMIm]­NO3 integrates more effectively into the membrane than the conventional additive LiNO3, which substantially increases the NO3 – concentration and is more conducive to enhance the battery’s operating voltage and cycling stability under identical voltage conditions.

As depicted in Figure , all the symmetric Li–Li cells with different electrolyte membranes show good long-term cycling performance. Compared to the PFE-based cell, the cell with PFENE1 exhibits more stable charge–discharge voltage plateaus, smaller overpotentials, and a longer cycling lifespan. This is because ionic liquids in electrolytes improve the Li/electrolyte interfacial stability by facilitating the in situ formation of a robust SEI layer, which ensures the uniform Li plating/stripping and inhibits dendritic growth. Among the PFENE series, PFENE3 demonstrates the best cycling performance. This result can be associated with the superior structural properties of PFENE3, where the optimal incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3 reduces the membrane crystallinity and creates more Li+ conduction pathways, yielding higher ionic conductivity and Li+ transference number, than others.

The electrochemical performances of the electrolyte membranes were further evaluated by employing them as both electrolyte and separator in Li/NCM622 full cells, as illustrated in Figure . To avoid significant anisotropic lattice changes and microstrain in NCM622 under high-voltage conditions, a cutoff voltage of 4.3 V was specifically chosen. Figure a presents the rate capability of the cells at various C-rates. At 0.1 C, the initial discharge capacity of the PFENE4-based cell reaches 172.0 mAh g–1, which is a bit higher than those of PFENE3 (170.9 mAh g–1), PFENE2 (169.0 mAh g–1) and PFENE1 (168.5 mAh g–1), and significantly exceeds that of PFE (163.6 mAh g–1). As the C-rate increases, the capacities of all the cells decrease; however, the capacities of the PFENE-based cells are always higher than that of the PFE-based cell. When the C-rate was gradually reduced from 2 C back to 0.1 C, the capacities of the PFENE-based cells recovered almost well, whereas the PFE-based cell exhibited a notable capacity drop. This indicates that incorporating [EMIm]­NO3 enables easy accessibility and efficient utilization of Li in electrodes, as evidenced by the superior rate performance of PFENE3 and PFENE4. Figures b–c illustrate the charge/discharge curves of the cells with PFE and PFENE3 at various C-rates. At low C-rates (0.1–0.5 C), the charge/discharge curves show relatively flat voltage plateaus near 3.6 V (delivering a power density of ∼306 W kg–1 at 0.5 C), which give way to steep slopes at high C-rates (1–2 C). This phenomenon is primarily attributable to the lower conductivity of the polymer electrolytes, which increases the cell’s internal resistance at high current density.

Figure d displays the cycling performance of the Li/NCM622 cells at 0.5 C. After the initial cycle, the cells consistently maintained a high Coulombic efficiency (>99%), which is a prerequisite for long-term cycling life. The PFENE3-based cell delivered an initial discharge capacity of 159.3 mAh g–1 (corresponding to an energy density of ∼573.5 Wh kg–1), with a capacity retention of 78% after 100 cycles (SD of 2.5% for three replicates). This retention rate was the highest among all the tested cells, surpassing the other PFENE series (69–73%) and significantly exceeding that of PFE (49%). Similar results were also evidenced in the charge/discharge curves shown in Figures e–f. As seen, the PFENE3-based cell shows well-defined charge/discharge plateaus after 100 cycles, while for the PFE-based cell, the plateaus have almost disappeared by just 50 cycles. As evidenced by the charge/discharge curves, the voltage gap between charging and discharging widens significantly with increasing C-rate. Based on the median voltage difference, the PFE-based cell shows an overpotential of 0.42 V at 2 C, substantially higher than the 0.29 V observed for PFENE3. Undoubtedly, and consistent with the foregoing discussion, this superior performance can be attributed to the favorable structure and properties of PFENE3.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed before and after cycling (Figure ) to evaluate the electrode kinetics. Before cycling (Figures a–b), PFE-based cell shows the highest interfacial resistance (R CEI = 95.4 Ω) and charge transfer resistance (R CT = 52.2 Ω). The incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3 (PFENE1–4) significantly reduces these resistance values, with higher content generally leading to lower impedance and PFENE4 yielding the lowest R CEI (38.0 Ω) and R CT (34.9 Ω). This observation suggests that [EMIm]­NO3 enhances the initial electrolyte-electrode compatibility and facilitates faster charge transfer. Upon cycling (Figures c–f), both R CEI and R CT for all the cells decrease drastically due to electrode activation and the gradual formation of stable interphase. Notably, after 10 cycles, PFENE3-based cell maintains R CEI and R CT as low as 9.5 and 4.1 Ω, respectively. PFENE1–4 cells show similar impedance values and trends, all lower than those of the PFE-based cell. This behavior can be correlated with the sacrificial decomposition of NO3 –, which rapidly facilitates the in situ formation of robust protective interphases on both the anode and cathode during the initial cycle. These interphases subsequently stabilize and provide continuous protection, thereby enabling stable long-term cycling of symmetric Li–Li cells (Figure ) and Li/NCM622 full cells (Figure d).

The SEM and TEM images shown in Figure reveal that the GPEs containing [EMIm]­NO3 promote the formation of protective electrode–electrolyte interphases in Li/NCM622 cells during cycling. Relative to the basal PFE, which leads to a coarse and porous Li deposition layer (Figure a), PFENE1 and PFENE3 yield a smoother and flatter deposition morphology composed of larger nodule-like particles (Figures b,c), indicating effective dendrite growth suppression. Meanwhile, the TEM results show that PFENE1 and PFENE3 (Figures e,f) promote a thinner and more uniform cathode–electrolyte interphase (CEI) on the NCM622 particles than the thicker and uneven CEI layer observed with PFE (Figure d). Among them, PFENE3 yields the thinnest and most uniform CEI. The robust interfaces on both the anode and cathode mitigate electrolyte decomposition, preserve the electrodes’ structural integrity, and ultimately contribute to improved cycling performance.

The surface chemistry of the Li anode and NCM622 cathode after 10 cycles was analyzed by XPS, as shown in Figure . On the Li anode (Figures a–c), the N 1s spectrum for PFENE3 reveals a new prominent peak at ∼398.2 eV (Li3N), along with significantly increased LiF content in the F 1s spectrum, relative to the PFE baseline. These findings provide compelling evidence that [EMIm]­NO3 directly participates in the electrochemical decomposition and subsequent SEI formation. The enrichment of the ionically conductive Li3N and mechanically robust LiF facilitates a more stable, high-performance SEI, which is instrumental in suppressing Li dendrite growth. On the NCM622 cathode (Figures d–f), the emergence of −NO3 and LiN x O y species in the N 1s spectrum for PFENE3, which is absent in the PFE baseline, confirms the involvement of [EMIm]­NO3 in CEI formation. The higher LiF concentration further indicates a dense, inorganic-rich CEI for PFENE3 for effective cathode protection. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the direct participation of [EMIm]­NO3 in interphase formation yields inorganic-rich SEI/CEI, thereby enhancing the cycling stability of the Li/NCM622 system.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a three-layer GPE membrane by sandwiching a PE scaffold between two PVDF–HFP layers loaded with LiTFSI and [EMIm]­NO3. The optimized PFENE3 membrane containing 22.9 wt % [EMIm]­NO3 achieves a tensile strength of 36.7 MPa, a conductivity of 5.03 × 10–4 S cm–1 at 25 °C, a tLi+ value of 0.449, and an oxidation-stability voltage of 4.70 V. These improvements arise from three synergistic effects induced by the incorporation of [EMIm]­NO3: (i) suppressing the crystallinity of PVDF–HFP; (ii) forming TFSI–-[EMIm]+-NO3 – hydrogen-bonding network for high tLi+ value; and (iii) the sacrificial decomposition of NO3 – to form inorganic-rich and robust SEI and CEI interphases. Consequently, the Li/PFENE3/Li symmetric cell cycles stably for over 600 h, and the Li/PFENE3/NCM622 full cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 170.9 mAh g–1 at 0.1 C and 159.3 mAh g–1 at 0.5 C with a capacity retention of 78% after 100 cycles. This work offers a viable strategy for simultaneously addressing the mechanical, conductive, and interfacial challenges of GPEs for high-performance lithium metal batteries.
