Real‐World Impact of Amiodarone on Apixaban Population Pharmacokinetics in Hospitalized Patients
Authors: Samantha Kolowrat, Chazmyn Riley, Kevin Lam, Lynda Thomson, Douglas F Stickle, Walter K Kraft
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite common coadministration in nonvalvular atrial fibrillation (NVAF), there is minimal evidence regarding the impact of concomitant amiodarone use on apixaban pharmacokinetics (PK). We described the population PK of apixaban (2.5 and 5 mg twice daily) in 106 hospitalized patients with and without concomitant amiodarone administration at steady state. Apixaban PK was reasonably described by a one‐compartment model with first‐order absorption and linear elimination. Aside from the receipt of amiodarone, age was retained as a statistically significant covariate on apparent clearance. Model‐based simulations depicted concomitant amiodarone use increasing AUCT for both 2.5 mg (1.58, 90% CI [1.28, 1.87]) and 5 mg (1.12, 90% CI [0.91, 1.34]) dosing groups based on geometric mean ratios relative to the apixaban only groups. Similarly, C max was also increased for both 2.5 mg (1.48, 90% CI [1.21, 1.75]) and 5 mg (1.09, 90% CI [0.92, 1.27]) dose groups. Lastly, concomitant amiodarone increased C min for both 2.5 mg (1.68, 90% CI [1.35, 2]) and 5 mg (1.11, 90% CI [0.85, 1.37]) dose groups. Overall differences in apixaban exposures do not appear to be clinically significant across both dosing regimens, but amiodarone coadministration was found to decrease apixaban clearance by 33% (ranging from 12% to 48%). The results support the current practice of no empiric dose adjustment for apixaban when concurrently administered with amiodarone.Keywords: amiodarone, apixaban, drug–drug interaction, non‐valvular atrial fibrillation, pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Apixaban is indicated to reduce the risk of cerebrovascular events and systemic embolism in patients with a history of nonvalvular atrial fibrillation (NVAF) [1]. Metabolism of apixaban primarily occurs through CYP3A4. It is also a substrate of transporter P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) [1, 2]. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors can impair apixaban metabolism while P‐gp and BCRP inhibitors may prevent efflux, increasing apixaban exposures and risk of bleeding [3, 4, 5, 6]. As such, the U.S. prescription label of apixaban cautions its combined use with strong CYP3A4 and P‐gp inhibitors and recommends apixaban dose reduction by 50% or avoid coadministration [1]. Amiodarone is often co‐administered with apixaban for NVAF [7]. It is a weak inhibitor of CYP3A4; however, its minor metabolite N‐monodesethylamiodarone (MDEA) exhibits moderate time‐dependent inhibition of CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 [4]. It was surmised that MDEA cytochrome inhibition combined with the parent amiodarone inhibition of P‐gp may result in increased apixaban exposure. Current data is mixed regarding the effects of amiodarone on apixaban pharmacokinetics (PK), with some reports of more major bleeding [8, 9, 10], while other studies have not identified increased risk of bleeding or stroke [11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

Due to the long half‐life of amiodarone (~50 days) and its side effects, a traditional crossover drug interaction study in healthy volunteers is neither ethical nor feasible. However, additional data to evaluate the potential of an interaction between apixaban and amiodarone in a real‐world patient cohort would be of value. This study aimed to describe the population PK of apixaban in hospitalized patients with a history of NVAF with and without a stable dose of amiodarone 200 mg. The secondary objective was to evaluate potential covariates such as age, renal function, and body mass index on apixaban PK. We hypothesized that the addition of amiodarone would increase apixaban plasma concentration, with this effect being amplified in patients with renal dysfunction.

Methods

This was a single‐site, retrospective, observational PK clinical trial conducted at Thomas Jefferson University Hospital. Potential patients were screened from electronic medical records from December 2023–July 2024. Eligible patients were hospitalized adults (≥ 18 years old) with NVAF on stable doses of apixaban 2.5 mg or 5 mg twice daily with or without stable doses of 200 mg amiodarone taken for at least 30 days prior to admission (Figure 1). Exclusion criteria included concurrent prescription of a strong CYP3A4 or P‐gp inducer or inhibitor during inpatient admission, weight ≥ 120 kg or apixaban prescribed for venous thromboembolism. Patients meeting eligibility criteria were evaluated for available salvage samples. Briefly, these were plasma samples drawn for clinical care that met the discard threshold defined as at least 5 days since blood draw. Three‐hundred microliters (300 μL) of plasma were aliquoted and stored at −80°C until batch analysis. Apixaban was measured by a liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) assay [16, 17, 18, 19]. No formal power analysis was conducted. Sample information and corresponding patient information were drawn from bulk transfer from electronic medical records. Although samples were drawn as per standard care, sampling times were relative to the time of first apixaban dose. For parameters not in the electronic medical records or easily accessed, data were hand extracted to a REDcap database. This study was approved by the Thomas Jefferson University Institutional Review Board on December 6, 2024 (IRB number iRISID‐2023‐2228). Informed consent was not required as the samples were discarded and no longer utilized for clinical care, and there was no direct interaction with patients.

Population Pharmacokinetic Analysis

R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) was used for data assembly. Monolix version 2024R1 (Lixoft, Antony, France) was utilized to perform population PK analysis via the stochastic approximation expectation maximization method. Any plasma apixaban measurement that was below the limit of quantification (BLQ) of 5 ng/mL was censored in the dataset. Several patients temporarily discontinued apixaban due to scheduled procedures or had separate hospitalizations where the elapsed time since the last apixaban dose was beyond five half‐lives (i.e., 60 h) [1]. Therefore, only the encounter with the most available samples as documented in the electronic medical records was utilized for these patients in the analysis. All patients were assumed to be at steady state with apixaban and amiodarone at the time of the first dose based on standard of care.

Structural Model

Various multicompartment models were explored with first‐order absorption and linear elimination without lag time assuming a log‐normal distribution for all PK parameters. Interindividual variability (IIV) was assessed for each PK parameter. Residual variability models (e.g., additive, proportional, or combined proportional and additive error) were explored and compared.

Covariate Model

After identifying the structural model, concomitant amiodarone was added as a binary variable (i.e., 0 for no and 1 for yes). Additional covariates were then added one by one, which were guided by the change in the corrected Bayesian information criteria (ΔBICc) values, visual inspection of diagnostic plots (observed vs. individual and population predictions, residuals vs. time and predictions, distributions of individual weighted residuals and normalized prediction distribution errors, and random effects vs. covariates), and Pearson's correlation and Wald tests [20]. Model Selection and Simulations

Sycomore version 2024R1 (Lixoft, Antony, France) was used for model management and as an aid to final model selection. In general, the threshold for retaining one model over another was based on at least 2 in the change in BICc. The final model was graphically evaluated using dose‐normalized visual predictive check plots (VPC) generated in Monolix and plotted with R. Additionally, that model was used to compare PK simulations over time against a previously published model with approved dosing regimens [21]. Lastly, the final model was used to calculate individual median area under the curve for a given dosing interval (AUCT), maximum concentration (C max), and minimum concentration (C min) stratified by receipt of amiodarone and dose with Simulx. 90% confidence intervals (CI) were computed from those individual exposures using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1000 replicates in R. Additionally, geometric mean ratios (GMRs) and their 90% CI were constructed assuming unequal variances between the two groups for each dose level relative to the apixaban only groups. The exposure metrics were evaluated by visual inspection of box plots for the current cohort compared to those for 1000 simulated individuals randomly sampled from individual covariate parameters.

Results

Dataset

A total of 106 patients and 360 samples (seven of which were BLQ) were included in the analysis. The demographics of included patients are shown in Table 1. While the number of samples in each group was comparable, there were fewer individual patients in the concomitant amiodarone group. The amiodarone group was slightly younger and had more males than females. There was a median of five samples per patient with the minimum and maximum being 1 and 19, respectively. Thirty‐one patients were taking at least one concomitant medication other than amiodarone known to be mild/moderate inducers or inhibitors of either CYP3A4 or P‐gp. Thirteen patients were only taking CYP3A4 inhibitors, one patient was taking a P‐gp inducer, four patients were taking at least one CYP3A4 and P‐gp inhibitor, two patients were taking at least one CYP3A4 inhibitor and one P‐gp inducer, and 11 patients were taking at least one CYP3A4 inducer and inhibitor as well as one P‐gp inducer and inhibitor.

Model Development and Evaluation

A one‐compartment model with first‐order absorption without lag time and linear elimination best described the current data. Two‐compartment models did not allow PK parameter estimation with adequate precision despite removing IIV on each PK parameter. More specifically, the estimated peripheral volume of distribution and intercompartmental clearance were not supported by the current data. Thus, a one‐compartment model was selected for further model building. Similarly, there was suboptimal precision around the estimation of the IIV on K a (absorption rate constant). Removing IIV on K a decreased BICc by 7.37 units, which was further improved by fixing K a to 0.82 (ΔBICc = −9.48) [22]. After adding amiodarone, age was identified as significant covariates on CL IIV. All other covariates had weak associations or were not statistically significant for inclusion. Moreover, concomitant medications other than amiodarone were not considered given the number of individuals per category (Table 1). The addition of age (normalized to a median of 77) on CL resulted in a better model (ΔBICc = −9.72), but adding eGFR (normalized to a median of 48) did not (ΔBICc = −0.83). Thus, amiodarone and age on CL IIV with a combined proportional and additive error structure was deemed to be the final model. The PK parameters for the base and final models are listed in Table 2. Additionally, the diagnostic plots assessing goodness‐of‐fit including dose normalized VPC stratified by receipt of amiodarone are provided in the Figures S1–S4.

Model‐Based Simulations

Prior to computing summary exposures, a PK simulation was done with the current model and compared to a similar study where different covariate values were sampled and the standard deviations of random effects were set to zero for 1000 simulated individuals [21]. Figure S5 illustrates higher predictions for the current model versus the published model. This is not expected to impact the assessment of amiodarone coadministration given the markedly reduced creatinine clearance seen with the current cohort. Nonetheless, 104 patients were included in the calculation of simulated exposure metrics. Two patients were removed as they had an apixaban dose reduction during their hospitalization. The simulated exposure metrics are presented in Figure 2 and Table 3. Upon visual inspection of the box plots, similar performance was noted between the two simulations for n = 104 and n = 1000 simulated individuals. Concomitant amiodarone resulted in approximately a 1.5‐fold increase in the median AUCT, C max, and C min for the 2.5 mg dosing group. These correspond to a GMR of 1.58 (90% CI [1.28, 1.87]), 1.48 (90% CI [1.21, 1.75]), and 1.68 (90% CI [1.35, 2]) for AUCT, C max, and C min, respectively. In contrast, a 1.2‐fold increase was observed in the median C max and C min as well as a 1.4‐fold increase in AUCT for those taking apixaban 5 mg and amiodarone. These correspond to a GMR of 1.12 (90% CI [0.91, 1.34]), 1.09 (90% CI [0.92, 1.27]), and 1.11 (90% CI [0.85, 1.37]) for AUCT, C max, and C min, respectively.

Discussion

While similar studies had smaller sample sizes and did not assess the variability in apixaban PK [23, 24], the findings described align with our study. The utility of a salvage sampling method was also showcased in not only minimizing additional research sampling but also circumventing the need for a prospective trial involving drugs with long half‐lives or increased bleeding risk.

The estimated V is within the range established by prior studies (~25–53 L) [22, 25, 26], but CL was much lower which may be due to age‐related changes in hepatic and renal function. The majority of the cohort consisted of elderly patients, who are known to have decreased hepatic activity and renal elimination due to smaller organ weights and perfusion [27, 28]. Although not added in the current model, this was found during the model building process where eGFR was significantly associated with CL. The final model detected a decrease of approximately 33% (ranging from 12% to 48%) in apixaban CL with concomitant amiodarone administration, which may be reflective of inhibitory effects on both CYP3A4 and P‐gp mediated clearance.

Current findings on C min are within the ranges reported by similar studies that utilized population PK modeling and/or a real‐world patient cohort. More specifically, current findings on C min are within the predicted ranges for patients with either stage 3 chronic kidney disease or an estimated creatinine clearance of 37 mL/min based on the Cockcroft‐Gault equation [21, 22]. Of note, Gaspar et al. ascertained the effects of CYP3A4/5 and P‐gp phenotypes instead of concomitant amiodarone administration. Similar inferences could be made for C max based on preexisting data although limited. The moderate P‐gp and CYP 3A4 inhibitor diltiazem increases apixaban C max 1.4‐fold and AUC 1.3‐fold, while inhibitors verapamil and dronedarone do not have a suggested dose adjustment for apixaban with concomitant amiodarone administration [1, 29, 30]. This is consistent with other publications and supports the lack of a need for apixaban dose alteration when co‐administered with amiodarone [2, 12, 23]. There are a few limitations with this work. Covariate data pulled from the electronic medical record only reflected the first reported value per encounter, which does not necessarily reflect the actual time course of those covariates throughout hospitalizations. Additionally, apixaban was temporarily held for several patients due to reasons including nonadherence and procedures. As several patients had multiple encounters as documented in the electronic medical record, only the encounter with the highest number of available samples was considered. Additionally, neither metabolizer phenotypes nor CYP/P‐gp genotypes were reported for each patient which may contribute to the overall variability in the data as seen here. Plasma apixaban samples were drawn at times driven by clinical care, which were not necessarily done at fixed times after dosing. Additionally, the number of samples available for retrieval could not be controlled. Together, these may have also contributed to the differences when compared to published apixaban pop PK models. Lastly, the effect of amiodarone dose nor its metabolites on apixaban PK were not assessed.

This study did not examine clinical outcomes of bleeding or embolization, as it was not powered to capture distinctions for such low frequency events over the short time period examined. However, the results are supported by a well‐established linear relationship between apixaban PK and anti‐Xa levels at therapeutic concentrations [2]. A 1.1‐ to 1.7‐fold increase in apixaban exposures would be expected to result in equivalent increases in anti‐Xa levels. Given that the current cohort was older and the extent of the apixaban elevations was not necessarily maintained between dose levels, the results support current guidance of no empiric dose reductions for patients on apixaban with concomitant amiodarone.
