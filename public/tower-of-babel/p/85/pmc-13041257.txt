Quantitative perspectives of patients and healthcare professionals to inform the co-design of an mHealth application for knee osteoarthritis management
Authors: Karmen Erjavec, Sabina Krsnik, Petra Kotnik, Mohsen Hussein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a leading cause of disability worldwide, and limited access to physiotherapy presents major barriers to evidence-based management. Mobile health (mHealth) applications can help address these challenges by supporting self-management, rehabilitation, and patient education. However, most existing applications lack robust evidence and demonstrate low adherence. Co-design approaches involving both patients and healthcare professionals may improve usability, engagement, and clinical integration. This study quantitatively examined user perspectives to inform the subsequent co-design and development of an mHealth application for KOA management; the application itself was not developed or evaluated in this study.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in Slovenia using a structured questionnaire, developed from previous studies and piloted with experts, to assess functional preferences, aesthetic preferences, perceived barriers, and perceived benefits of an mHealth application. Responses were rated on 5- or 10-point Likert scales and analysed using descriptive statistics and independent-sample t-tests.ResultsA total of 164 patients with symptomatic KOA and 136 healthcare professionals completed the survey. Both groups identified exercise videos, goal setting and progress tracking, written communication with a physiotherapist, and educational content as essential app components, with no significant differences in functional preferences (p > 0.05). However, patients placed greater importance on aesthetic features, including visual clarity (M = 4.59 vs. 4.12, p = 0.001), layout organisation (M = 4.41 vs. 4.09, p = 0.039), and design appeal (M = 4.60 vs. 4.32, p = 0.035). Healthcare professionals reported greater perceived barriers, particularly limited technical literacy and poor internet connectivity, while patients rated motivational and practical benefits, autonomy, convenience, and information accuracy significantly higher (all p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe findings highlight the critical importance of participatory co-design approaches that systematically integrate clinical expertise and patient experience in developing digital rehabilitation tools. Although patients and healthcare professionals demonstrated convergence in their evaluation of core functional requirements, their differing perspectives on aesthetic qualities, perceived barriers, and perceived benefits highlight the need to balance user engagement with clinical robustness. These results provide an empirical foundation for designing user-centred and clinically integrated mHealth applications for KOA rehabilitation and contribute to the growing evidence base guiding digital health innovation, implementation, and policy for musculoskeletal rehabilitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03410-7.Keywords: knee osteoarthritis, mHealth, Co-design, Digital health, Usability, Patient empowerment, Rehabilitation, Mobile applications

Background

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a prevalent chronic musculoskeletal disorder that significantly impairs mobility, functional independence, and quality of life, while imposing an increasing global healthcare and economic burden [1–5]. Evidence-based guidelines emphasise exercise therapy, patient education, and self-management as the foundations of KOA care [6–9]. However, implementation of these recommendations in clinical practice remains limited due to restricted access to physiotherapy, long waiting times, and poor adherence to prescribed regimens.

Mobile health (mHealth) technologies, defined by the World Health Organization as medical and public health practices supported by mobile devices [10], offer promising opportunities to bridge these gaps. By enabling remote delivery of evidence-based exercise, education, and behavioural support, mHealth applications can improve access to care, support self-management, and increase patient engagement [11–13]. Despite their potential, most OA-related applications demonstrate low clinical integration and poor long-term adherence. Systematic reviews indicate that while these applications often provide exercise guidance and educational content, few are developed using participatory, user-centred methods or are based on behavioural theory [14–17].

These challenges are increasingly recognised as a global health systems issue, reflecting the widening gap between rehabilitation needs and workforce capacity in ageing populations [9, 11–18]. Similar patterns are evident across regions, where demand for physiotherapy and long-term rehabilitation services exceeds available resources, resulting in delays in evidence-based care delivery. The problem is particularly pronounced in Central and Eastern Europe, where access to physiotherapy is further limited by workforce shortages, regional disparities, and prolonged waiting times [18]. In Slovenia, the number of physiotherapists remains below the EU average, with estimates indicating that at least twice as many professionals are needed to meet population needs. Limited rehabilitation capacity and the lack of established digital frameworks further exacerbate inequities in access to continuous, evidence-based care.

Theoretical frameworks are essential in guiding digital health design by clarifying how technological, behavioural, and contextual factors influence outcomes. Harrison et al. [19] emphasise that theory-informed co-design improves conceptual coherence and implementation fidelity by integrating behavioural and experiential perspectives throughout development. In KOA management, these approaches can improve usability, reduce cognitive load, and enhance adherence by aligning user needs with mechanisms that support sustained engagement.

Given persistent issues with usability and adherence, user involvement in app design is essential to ensure that functional requirements, aesthetic preferences, and perceived barriers are adequately addressed [14, 20]. Prior research indicates that while patients welcome digital self-management tools, healthcare professionals may express scepticism, highlighting the need for shared accountability and clinical reliability [21–25]. Co-design, defined as a participatory approach that actively involves end users and stakeholders, addresses these challenges by ensuring that the resulting solutions are relevant, acceptable and effective [26, 27].

Currently, the evidence base for digital and blended care in OA remains limited. Although recent studies have explored digital integration into physiotherapy practice [28, 29], few have quantitatively compared patient and healthcare professional perspectives. Such understanding is essential for developing clinically robust and user-centred digital rehabilitation tools. Therefore, this study aimed to quantitatively examine user perspectives on functional and aesthetic preferences, perceived barriers, and perceived benefits of app-based KOA management among patients and healthcare professionals, to inform the co-design of a future mHealth application.

Based on previous theoretical and empirical evidence indicating differences between patients and healthcare professionals in their evaluation of digital health technologies, the following hypotheses were formulated to guide the analysis.

H1

There are no statistically significant differences between healthcare professionals and patients in their functional preferences for mHealth applications for KOA management.

H2

Patients assign higher importance to aesthetic preferences than healthcare professionals.

H3

Healthcare professionals report higher perceived barriers to mHealth adoption than patients.

H4

Patients report higher perceived benefits of mHealth applications than healthcare professionals.

This study represents the initial quantitative phase of a broader participatory co-design project aimed at developing and evaluating an evidence-based mHealth application for KOA rehabilitation.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study used a cross-sectional observational design to quantitatively examine user perspectives on mHealth applications for KOA management. Data were collected from April to June 2024. The study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guideline to ensure methodological transparency and completeness.

Participants and recruitment

Given the exploratory and descriptive nature of this study, a pragmatic approach to sample size determination was adopted, aiming for sufficient heterogeneity to identify patterns across user groups.

A purposive sample of patients with symptomatic KOA and healthcare professionals was included. KOA patients were recruited by their attending orthopaedic specialists and physiotherapists during routine outpatient consultations. Before recruitment, each patient received oral and written information about the study objectives, procedures, voluntary participation, and data confidentiality. Patients were given sufficient time to ask questions and decide whether to participate. Only those who provided written informed consent were included. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, a clinical diagnosis of symptomatic KOA classified as Kellgren–Lawrence grade 1–3, and the ability to complete the questionnaire independently. Exclusion criteria were defined to align the sample with the intended use case: an mHealth app supporting ambulatory, guideline-based exercise and education for non-surgical KOA management. Wheelchair dependence and severe comorbidities were excluded because they limit safe engagement in ambulatory exercise and would require tailored interventions beyond the scope of this study. Cognitive impairment and insufficient language or digital literacy were excluded to ensure informed consent and reliable completion of self-report measures.

Healthcare professionals were recruited through institutions implementing the clinical KOA care pathway described by van den Bogaart et al. [30]. This pathway standardises the diagnostic, referral, and treatment processes for patients with knee or hip osteoarthritis in primary care and rehabilitation, ensuring the participation of professionals directly involved in evidence-based KOA management. In this study, it served as a structured framework to identify and contact professionals involved in KOA management across various stages of care. Invitations were sent via institutional email addresses obtained from publicly available registries.

The patient questionnaire was administered in paper form after the clinical examination, with an average completion time of approximately 15 min. The healthcare professional survey was conducted online using the 1 ka platform (University of Ljubljana, Slovenia), a secure and GDPR-compliant academic survey environment, and required approximately 10 min to complete.

Participation in both groups was voluntary, and all participants received detailed information about the study objectives, confidentiality, and data protection before completing the survey. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the National Committee on Medical Ethics of the Republic of Slovenia (No. 0120–471/2023-2711-4) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Survey instrument

The survey instrument was developed using validated items from previous studies on digital rehabilitation and mHealth usability [22, 30, 31]. It was refined through expert consultation with five academics and five healthcare professionals. The questionnaire also included items on participants’ preferred digital platforms for accessing mHealth interventions (mobile phone, tablet, or computer) as part of the assessment of functional preferences for KOA self-management applications. The academics comprised three professors of physiotherapy and kinesiology, one medical informatics expert, and one public health researcher specialising in digital health and patient engagement. The healthcare professionals included two orthopaedic specialists, two physiotherapists, and one nurse specialist. All expert panel members had at least 10 years of professional or research experience in KOA management, rehabilitation, or digital health. Individual interviews were conducted to assess the clarity, relevance, and completeness of the questionnaire items before pilot testing. The survey instrument was piloted with 25 participants (patients and professionals) to ensure clarity, content validity, and cultural relevance. Following expert interviews and pilot testing, minor revisions were made to improve the clarity and usability of the questionnaire. The wording of several items was simplified to accommodate participants with varying levels of health literacy, two response scales were standardised to a 5-point Likert format, and one redundant demographic question was removed. No conceptual changes were required, and the internal consistency across the questionnaire sections was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.85). This level of internal consistency was comparable to previously validated usability measures in mHealth research, particularly the mHealth App Usability Questionnaire (MAUQ) developed and validated by Zhou et al. [31], which has been widely used to assess perceived usability and engagement in digital health applications. The questionnaire assessed four key dimensions of user perspectives relevant to mHealth app co-design: (1) functional preferences (e.g. desired app features, preferred platforms, optimal exercise duration); (2) aesthetic preferences (e.g. visual clarity, layout, content organisation); (3) perceived barriers (e.g. limited technical literacy, communication challenges, poor internet connectivity); and (4) perceived benefits (e.g. motivation, autonomy, convenience, information accuracy). The questionnaire also included items on participants’ preferred digital platforms for accessing mHealth interventions (mobile phone, tablet, or computer) as part of the assessment of functional preferences for KOA self-management apps, since platform choice is an important determinant of usability, accessibility, and long-term adherence in digital health interventions.

The final questionnaire consisted of four thematic sections assessing user perspectives on mHealth applications for KOA management. Each section contained multiple items rated on either a 5-point or 10-point Likert-type scale. Table 1 summarises the focus, number, and type of items in each section. Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics. Independent-sample t-tests were used to compare responses between patients and healthcare professionals. The assumptions of the independent-sample t-test, including normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test), were assessed before analysis. The Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed that all continuous variables followed a normal distribution (p > 0.05), allowing for parametric statistical comparisons between patients and healthcare professionals. The assumptions for independent-sample t-tests, including normality and homogeneity of variances, were met, as indicated by non-significant results of the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests (p > 0.05).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 250 patients with symptomatic KOA were invited to participate, of whom 180 consented (response rate: 72%), and 164 completed the questionnaire in full (completion rate: 91%). Among healthcare professionals, 200 were invited via institutional email, 150 consented (response rate: 75%), and 136 completed the survey (completion rate: 91%). Table 2 presents the sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants, including 164 patients with symptomatic KOA and 136 healthcare professionals. Most respondents in both groups were female (70.6% of healthcare professionals and 67.1% of patients). Healthcare professionals were predominantly younger (47.1% aged 21–30 years), while patients were mainly older adults aged 51–70 years, consistent with the epidemiological profile of KOA. Educational attainment also differed substantially between groups: most patients had completed secondary education (65.9%), whereas healthcare professionals primarily held tertiary qualifications, including bachelor’s (29.4%), master’s/specialist (20.6%), and doctoral degrees (11.8%). Among healthcare professionals, physicians (29.4%) and administrators (29.4%) were the most represented occupational groups, followed by nurses and physiotherapists (each 11.8%).

Functional preferences

The following sections present comparative results between the two groups regarding functional and aesthetic preferences, perceived barriers, and perceived benefits related to mHealth applications.

Both healthcare professionals (82.7%) and patients (53.7%) reported a preference for using mobile phones to access KOA management applications. Regarding computer use, 40.2% of patients preferred computers only, whereas 14.7% of health professionals did (p = 0.001).

The two groups agree that an appropriate time length for exercise guided by an online application is between 16 and 30 min, with 64.7% of healthcare professionals and 48.8% of patients in favour of this range. (p = 0.001).

No statistically significant differences were found between healthcare professionals and patients in their functional preferences for mHealth applications for knee osteoarthritis management (p > 0.05). Both groups identified exercise videos, goal setting and progress tracking, written communication with a physiotherapist, and educational content as the most important functional components of a KOA mHealth application (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study quantitatively examined how patients with KOA and healthcare professionals perceive the functional and aesthetic features, barriers, and benefits of mHealth applications for KOA management, providing evidence to guide the co-design of clinically integrated digital rehabilitation tools. Taken together, these results provide a multidimensional understanding of how patients and professionals conceptualise digital rehabilitation support, informing future participatory development processes.

Consistent with Hypothesis 1, no statistically significant differences were observed between healthcare professionals and patients in their functional preferences for mHealth applications. Both groups identified exercise videos, goal setting and progress tracking, written communication with a physiotherapist, and educational content as the most important components. This finding aligns with previous research emphasising these features as central to user engagement and adherence in mHealth interventions [16, 20, 30]. The convergence between groups supports the theoretical premise that digital rehabilitation tools must integrate core behavioural and educational mechanisms to facilitate adherence, autonomy, and continuity of care [21, 32]. It also confirms that both user groups prioritise features that operationalise evidence-based clinical recommendations in digital form. These findings provide empirical support for the first stage of co-design, in which consensus on functional requirements represents a precondition for technical development and usability testing. This convergence can be explained by the shared understanding among patients and healthcare professionals of the essential components required for effective digital rehabilitation. Both groups appear to recognise that functional features—such as exercise demonstrations, progress monitoring, communication with physiotherapists, and educational content—represent standard expectations that directly support evidence-based practice and patient self-management. As these elements have become well established in contemporary mHealth design, differences between user groups are likely minimal. Consequently, future co-design efforts should build on this functional consensus and focus on optimising usability, personalisation, and sustained engagement rather than redefining core functionalities.

In contrast, aesthetic dimensions revealed statistically significant differences, with patients consistently assigning greater importance to visual clarity, layout organisation, and overall design appeal, as predicted by Hypothesis 2. This result is consistent with prior findings that patients’ sustained engagement depends strongly on perceived attractiveness, intuitive navigation, and perceived effort required for use [14, 20, 30]. It aligns with hedonic–utilitarian design theory, which posits that perceived enjoyment and aesthetic appeal enhance engagement beyond utilitarian functionality. According to usability theory and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), visual appeal and perceived ease of use directly influence behavioural intention and adherence [31]. Healthcare professionals, on the other hand, valued informational accuracy and functional reliability more than aesthetic design, reflecting their professional responsibility for clinical quality. These findings highlight the need for co-design processes that balance visual engagement with informational precision, ensuring that digital interventions meet both patient motivation and clinical standards of reliability. The difference in aesthetic preferences likely stems from the distinct ways patients and healthcare professionals interact with mHealth applications. Patients rely on visual appeal, clarity, and intuitive design to sustain motivation and engagement, as these elements reduce cognitive effort and enhance perceived usability [31]. In contrast, healthcare professionals primarily view such applications as clinical tools, prioritising functionality, reliability, and data accuracy over aesthetic appeal. This divergence reflects differing user goals: patients prioritise engagement and support, whereas professionals emphasise clinical efficiency and data validity.

As hypothesised, healthcare professionals reported significantly higher perceived barriers than patients. These results mirror those of Weber et al. [29] and Granja et al. [33], who found that professional caution stems from accountability for patient safety, data protection, and workflow integration. Conversely, patients primarily perceived barriers related to clarity of exercise presentation and communication with physiotherapists, suggesting that practical usability issues outweigh technical or ethical concerns from their perspective. Addressing these discrepancies requires targeted strategies, such as professional digital literacy training, standardised data security protocols, and clear communication channels between users and providers. These interventions could enhance professional trust and facilitate large-scale adoption of mHealth solutions in rehabilitation contexts.

Patients rated motivation, autonomy, convenience, and accuracy of information significantly higher than healthcare professionals, confirming the fourth hypothesis. This is consistent with previous evidence indicating that patients perceive digital interventions as empowering tools that promote independence and flexible engagement in care [16, 21, 34]. Healthcare professionals, however, tended to regard these applications as adjuncts to traditional therapy, reflecting a cautious approach toward the clinical replacement of in-person care [22, 35]. The divergence between perceived empowerment and professional control illustrates the dual role of mHealth technologies: while patients see them as means of self-determination, professionals view them as instruments for structured monitoring. Co-design must therefore bridge these perspectives by embedding behavioural support features, −−such as feedback loops and personalised goal-setting—within clinically supervised frameworks.

This study also contributes to the field of medical informatics and decision making by providing quantitative evidence to inform data-driven design decisions in digital rehabilitation. The results guide developers, healthcare organisations, and policymakers in prioritising features and facilitating the effective integration of mHealth tools into rehabilitation pathways. By identifying both converging and diverging perspectives, the study offers a foundation for more inclusive digital health strategies aligned with international calls for evidence-based and equitable digital transformation in healthcare [20, 33, 36–38].

Collectively, these findings support the theoretical principle that successful digital rehabilitation depends on aligning user motivation with clinical oversight through participatory design. The convergence in functional expectations suggests that co-design efforts can efficiently focus on interface usability and integration into clinical workflows. Meanwhile, the divergence in aesthetic preferences and perceived barriers underscores the need for iterative prototyping involving both patients and healthcare professionals to balance engagement with reliability. From a policy perspective, these results highlight the relevance of national digital health strategies, such as the WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025 [37], which advocate inclusive co-design and human-centred development to ensure equitable access and sustainability. These insights may guide clinicians and developers in prioritising user interface testing, accessibility features, and digital literacy interventions to maximise adoption.

This study did not include the design, prototyping, or evaluation of an operational application. Instead, the findings provide an empirical foundation for subsequent research phases, which will focus on the co-creation and usability testing of a prototype mHealth application for KOA management, to be reported separately.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in Slovenia, where limited physiotherapy availability and digital infrastructure may influence user perceptions and restrict generalisability. Second, the cross-sectional design does not capture temporal changes in preferences or adoption behaviour. Third, self-reported data may be influenced by social desirability bias. Future research should include longitudinal and mixed-methods designs to explore behavioural determinants of mHealth engagement, as well as iterative co-design workshops and prototype testing to assess usability, clinical outcomes, and cost-effectiveness. Comparative studies across healthcare systems could further identify contextual factors affecting adoption and scalability. Finally, although the study identified preferences and perceptions, it did not directly assess behavioural outcomes or actual usage patterns, which should be addressed in future research.

Conclusion

This study confirms that while patients and healthcare professionals share similar views on essential functional features of mHealth applications for KOA management, they differ in aesthetic preferences, perceived barriers, and perceived benefits. These findings underscore the necessity of participatory, theory-informed co-design approaches that integrate both user groups’ perspectives. Incorporating these insights into app development will help ensure that future mHealth interventions are not only technically robust and clinically relevant but also engaging and empowering for patients. By integrating these insights, future co-designed mHealth applications can better align patient empowerment with clinical safety, contributing to scalable and sustainable digital rehabilitation ecosystems. This research provides foundational empirical evidence to inform the future design of an mHealth application for KOA management; the application has not yet been developed or empirically evaluated. These insights also have policy implications for digital health implementation, supporting evidence-based integration of mHealth tools into rehabilitation pathways and national digital health strategies.
