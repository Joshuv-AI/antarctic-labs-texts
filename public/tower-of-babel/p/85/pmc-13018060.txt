Surgical simulation of minimally invasive bronchial sleeve resection and reconstruction using interactive 3-dimensional airway models
Authors: Yoshifumi Hirata, Kohei Hashimoto, Takashi Nozawa, Keisei Tachibana, Ryota Tanaka, Kenichi Yokoyama, Tomonari Kinoshita, Takashi Ohtsuka, Haruhiko Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBronchial sleeve resection is a lung-sparing procedure that avoids pneumonectomy and reduces postoperative mortality, but it remains technically demanding. Introduction

Bronchial sleeve resection is a procedure that is typically employed to avoid pneumonectomy while completely resecting lung cancer and reducing post operative mortality [1]. However, this procedure is relatively rare, high-risk compared to standard anatomical pulmonary resection, and technically challenging. Previously, we have created an interactive real-size 3-dimentional (3D) tracheobronchial model designed from computed tomography data that reproduces the texture of actual surgical procedures. We also confirmed that training with this model shortened the anastomosis time and improved technical scores in thoracic trainees compared to clinical training alone [2]. There is growing evidence of the feasibility and safety of bronchial sleeve resection in the minimally invasive setting including video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) [3] and robotic surgery [4]. This relatively new procedure adds further technical complexity related to limited working space and maneuverability, and thus a high-quality lab-training is desired for preparation. The purpose of this study is to develop high fidelity dry lab for minimally invasive bronchial sleeve resection and reconstruction.

Materials and methods

Creation of the chest module

In designing the chest wall module, we aimed to create a device that could accommodate various minimally invasive surgical approaches, including multiportal and uniportal VATS, and different port placements in robotic surgery. The chest wall module was designed with 25 holes to support a variety of port locations (Fig. 1). The holes are 30 mm in diameter, and a removable 8 mm rubber plug can be attached to sustain the variety of sizes of the port (Fig. 2). It functions as a utility port when the rubber is removed. The case can be rotated to allow approaches in the left or right lateral decubitus position, and the multi-slit rubber edges act as a suture holder.

Chest wall module with video-assisted thoracic and robotic surgery port settings. Various sizes of ports can be inserted

Simulations of a sleeve resection

The thoracoscopic and robotic approaches were reproduced and a 3D bronchial model was used to perform the bronchial anastomosis for right upper lobe sleeve resection (Fig. 3). The 3D bronchial model was manufactured using 3D printing technology provided by an external company (Cross Medical, Kyoto, Japan). For the thoracoscopic approach (3-port approach), two 7 mm ports were inserted and used as the surgeon’s left hand and scope. The 30 mm utility port, which was created by removing the rubber plug, was used for the surgeon’s right hand (dominant hand). A continuous suture with double-armed needles (4 − 0 Maxon Medtronic, Ireland) was used for anastomosis. The da Vinci or Xi system (Intuitive Surgery, USA) was used for the robotic approach. Continuous suturing with absorbable barbed sutures (3 − 0 Stratafix, Ethicon Endosurgery, USA) was used for anastomosis. In both approaches, anastomosis was started at the posterior wall, followed by anastomosis at the anterior wall.

A view of the simulation with video-assisted thoracic and robotic surgery from the outside

Evaluation of the training model

The training model was evaluated with a 5-point Likert scale, (surgical exposure, manipulation, and usefulness as training) after four board-certified thoracic surgeons performed simulations with both approaches. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Review Board (No. R05-020) in 2023, and consent was waived because of its retrospective nature.

Results

The training could be repeated both in thoracoscopic as well as in robotic-assisted procedures (supplemental video 1, video 2). Multiportal and uniportal VATS resection and reconstruction were also reproduced. In each case, the anastomosis was performed without any problems. There were no problems associated with the chest wall module, such as port interference or port slippage/displacement.

The evaluation of the training model by the four thoracic surgeons was generally acceptable (VATS: median score [range] on a 5-point Likert scale: surgical exposure 3.5 [2–5], manipulation 2.5 [2–5], usefulness as training 4.0 [4–5]. Robotic-assisted surgery: surgical exposure 4.5 [4–5], manipulation 4.0 [4–5], usefulness as training 5.0 [4–5].

Regarding cost considerations, each 3D bronchial model costs approximately EUR 500; however, a single model can be used for multiple different procedures. Moreover, material is durable enough allowing over ten anastomoses in each cut, allowing repeated training and reducing the effective cost per session.

Discussion

In this study, we have successfully created a training model for minimally invasive bronchial sleeve resection, including VATS and robotic approaches. To our knowledge, this is the first report in the literature of such a training model. Operative mortality after sleeve lobectomy is approximately 5–10 times higher than that of regular lobectomy for lung cancer [5]. The acquisition of a robust technique of bronchial reconstruction with an adequate training system is therefore very important for all thoracic surgeons. On the other hand, not every institution can provide sufficient case volume for trainees because of the rarity of this type of operation. Minimally invasive surgery increases the complexity of needle handling and suture movement, making training even more challenging. Simulations using animals and cadavers are becoming popular as surgical training methods, but these training methods have many issues, such as cost, anatomical differences from human, and animal ethics [6, 7]. Here, high quality dry lab-based simulations could play an important role. Training using our system allows for repeated simulations regardless of location, which is thought to be particularly beneficial for learning new techniques.

This study has several limitations. First, the absence of the lung (or other structures) may have made the simulation easier than the actual surgery. Second, long-term success of bronchial anastomosis requires control of bronchial arterial supply and anastomotic tension. Therefore, users should be aware that this system is highly focused on anastomosis technique, and trainees need to learn such knowledge and techniques from actual clinical training, and textbooks or videos. Third, improved performance in actual surgery with our training system is not yet proven. A prospective study is needed to further verify the effectiveness of training in clinical settings.

Conclusions

We have successfully developed a training system for bronchial sleeve resection in the setting of minimally invasive surgery, including thoracoscopy and robotic surgery. It is designed to help thoracic surgeons prepare for the growing demand for this type of procedure and ultimately improve surgical outcomes.
