Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Patients With Symptomatic, Severe Aortic Regurgitation: 1-Year Outcomes of the J-Valve Transfemoral Early Feasibility Study
Authors: Dean J. Kereiakes, Azeem Latib, Santiago Garcia, David G. Rizik, Peter Fail, Ron Waksman, Sameer A. Gafoor, Sachin Goel, Vijay Iyer, David J. Cohen, João L. Cavalcante, Rebecca T. Hahn, Yunyi Li, Tsuyoshi Kaneko, Michael J. Reardon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinically significant native aortic regurgitation (AR) increases with age. Surgical aortic valve replacement has been the only curative guideline-recommended therapy, while transcatheter options remain limited by anatomic constraints.MethodsThe J-Valve early feasibility study (NCT06034028) evaluated the safety and effectiveness of transfemoral J-Valve transcatheter heart valve implantation in patients with symptomatic, severe native AR. The prospective single-arm study enrolled 25 patients with symptomatic, severe, native AR deemed high risk for surgical aortic valve replacement at 8 centers. Clinical and echocardiographic assessments were performed at baseline, 30 days, 6 months, and 1 year with independent core laboratory and clinical events committee adjudication. The primary effectiveness end point was a non-hierarchical composite of all-cause mortality or disabling stroke at 30 days.ResultsMean age was 80.6 ± 4.3 years; 76% were male; 100% were New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II/III; mean Society of Thoracic Surgeons-Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) score was 5.6 ± 4.5%. Successful implantation occurred in 92% (23/25). The primary end point occurred in 2 patients (8.0%). All-cause mortality was 4.0% at 30 days and 1 year, with no cardiovascular-related deaths. Two strokes (1 disabling) (8.2%) and 2 new pacemaker implantations (10%) occurred within 30 days. At 1 year, none/trace AR was present in 95.5% (21/22) and mild AR in 4.5% (1/22), with a mean gradient of 5.5 ± 1.7 mm Hg, and an effective orifice area of 2.48 ± 0.53 cm2. Reverse left ventricular (LV) remodeling was evident at 30 days with sustained reductions in LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volume indices to 1 year. NYHA class improved in 72.7% (16/22) of patients, with 95.5% (21/22) in NYHA class I/II at 1 year.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates preliminary safety and effectiveness of the J-Valve in high-risk patients with severe AR, with favorable 1-year outcomes and stable hemodynamic performance.

Introduction

Clinically significant native aortic valve regurgitation (AR) increases with age and is present in 3% to 4% of individuals ≥65 years old.1 The pressure and volume overload of AR leads to left ventricular (LV) remodeling, and by the time heart failure symptoms develop, myocardial dysfunction, LV dilatation, and subsequent myocardial fibrosis are frequently present.2 While medical therapy may provide symptomatic benefit or delay disease progression in selected patients with chronic AR, surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) remains the standard of care for patients who exhibit heart failure symptoms or those who developed LV dysfunction/chamber enlargement, with specific thresholds for intervention defined by echocardiography and/or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.3 Nonetheless, the majority of patients with symptomatic, severe AR do not receive timely SAVR because of advanced age, LV dysfunction, comorbidities, and the risk of surgery.4 Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) using transcatheter heart valves (THVs) designed for treatment of aortic valve stenosis has been limited by anatomic factors including dilatation of the aortic root, larger annular dimensions, and lack of sufficient leaflet calcification to assure adequate valve fixation. “Off-label” THV use for AR has been associated with an increased incidence of valve migration, embolization, perivalvular regurgitation, and the requirement for repeat intervention.5 In this context, TAVR without a dedicated THV for AR has received a level 2B indication for patients with “symptomatic, severe AR who are ineligible for surgery, if the anatomy (valve calcification and annular size) is suitable.”3,6 More recently, the US FDA has approved the first dedicated THV (Trilogy, JenaValve) for treatment of high surgical risk patients with symptomatic, severe AR. In parallel, additional dedicated transcatheter devices for AR are under clinical investigation, including the transfemoral J-Valve system (Edwards Lifesciences). Herein, we present the results of the J-Valve Transfemoral Early Feasibility Study (EFS) with complete outcomes through 1-year follow-up.

Methods

Study design and patients

The J-Valve Transfemoral EFS (NCT06034028) is a prospective, single-arm, multicenter study enrolling patients with symptomatic, severe, native AR deemed high risk for surgery by the local heart team. Surgical risk was determined using a prespecified, integrated approach in accordance with the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines for the management of patients with valvular heart disease.3 Assessment of high surgical risk incorporated objective measures including STS-PROM, along with frailty indices, major organ system compromise, and procedure-specific factors and is detailed in Section E of the Supplement. Final determination of “high surgical risk” was made by the multidisciplinary heart team based on this comprehensive evaluation.

Patients were enrolled at 8 sites in the United States. The trial protocol was approved by each local institutional review board or ethics committee, and all patients provided written informed consent for the study. Eligible patients had New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class II or higher symptoms and severe, native AR, defined per the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines7,8 using an integrative echocardiographic approach based on multiple parameters of regurgitation severity, and confirmed by an independent echocardiography core laboratory. All patients were required to have anatomy suitable for transfemoral access. Patients with ascending aortic diameters >5.0 cm, annular perimeter <57 mm or >104 mm, bicuspid aortic valves, mixed aortic valve disease, severe mitral regurgitation, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <25%, or coronary artery disease requiring revascularization were excluded. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in Section D of the Supplement.

An independent case review board assessed all patients for eligibility. Sites reported adverse events and an independent clinical events committee adjudicated prespecified end points and key clinical events according to Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3)9 criteria. An independent data and safety monitoring board reviewed all safety data. Echocardiographic data were analyzed by an independent core laboratory, and computed tomography (CT) imaging was evaluated by a dedicated CT core laboratory.

Study device and procedures

The J-Valve THV consists of a self-expanding, low-profile nitinol frame, bovine pericardial leaflets, and an alignment feature consisting of 3 nitinol anchor rings designed to locate and position the valve within the native aortic valve sinuses. The delivery system is steerable and designed for transfemoral access. Valve sizing was guided by multidetector CT, with annular dimensions assessed during systole (approximately 10%-20% or 30%-40% of the cardiac cycle) to capture maximal annular size and minimize undersizing. Given that annular dimensions are typically larger in systole than diastole, systolic measurements were prioritized, particularly in minimally calcified valves, to reduce the risk of paravalvular leak. All procedures were performed under general anesthesia by experienced TAVR operators in hybrid operating rooms or cardiac catheterization laboratories. Device implantation was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for use following completion of sponsor-provided device training. Intraprocedural anticoagulation was achieved with heparin (target activated clotting time >250 seconds). Postprocedural therapy followed 2020 ACC/AHA guidelines,3 with recommended lifelong single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or a P2Y12 inhibitor). Dual antiplatelet therapy was used in patients undergoing prior percutaneous coronary intervention per stent instructions. Oral anticoagulation was continued when indicated; if discontinued, single antiplatelet therapy was initiated.

Echocardiographic assessments

All echocardiographic measurements were performed at an independent core laboratory with the use of standard hemodynamic measures as per American Society of Echocardiography guidelines.8,10 Transthoracic echocardiography was the primary imaging modality used to characterize baseline cardiac structure, valve hemodynamics, and AR severity, with the exception of select anatomic measurements obtained by CT angiography as described below. All postimplant imaging follow-up assessments were performed by echocardiography at prespecified timepoints.

Left ventricular chamber size and remodeling were assessed using standard linear and volumetric measures, including left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic diameters, end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes with corresponding indexed values, left ventricular mass, and left ventricular mass index. Transvalvular and paravalvular AR were evaluated according to VARC-3 criteria,9 after valve implantation at each follow-up visit.

Hemodynamic assessment of the implanted THV included measurements of mean gradient, stroke volume and indexed stroke volume, effective orifice area and indexed effective orifice area, effective stroke volume and indexed effective stroke volume, and effective cardiac output and indexed effective cardiac output. Effective forward flow measures were derived to characterize the true effective forward flow and valve performance after intervention. Effective stroke volume was calculated as the left ventricular outflow tract Doppler-derived stroke volume minus the AR volume. Effective cardiac output was calculated as effective stroke volume (in mL) multiplied by heart rate and divided by 1000.

Computed tomographic assessments

Baseline cardiac CT angiography was performed and analyzed by an independent CT core laboratory for procedural planning and anatomical characterization in accordance with contemporary expert consensus recommendations.11 CT imaging was used solely for baseline assessment of aortic root and annular geometry and was not used to define baseline hemodynamic or AR severity. CT-derived measurements reported at baseline included annular perimeter, annular area, and aortic root angle.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary end point was a nonhierarchical composite of all-cause death or disabling stroke within 30 days of the procedure. Additional end points included changes in NYHA functional class, Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire overall summary (KCCQ-OS) score (scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health status), hemodynamic valve performance, and clinical outcomes. Clinical, health status, and echocardiographic outcomes were evaluated at 30 days, 6 months, and 1 year.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means ± standard deviation, as appropriate. Paired comparisons between baseline and follow-up timepoints were made using the Wilcoxon signed rank test to provide a robust assessment for small sample sizes. Time-to-event variables are summarized with the use of Kaplan-Meier estimates and 95% Greenwood’s confidence interval. No imputation was performed for missing follow-up data, except for partial dates in safety data, which were imputed using the most conservative approach available. Unless otherwise specified, the analyses were performed on the full analysis set. A 2-sided P value of < .05 was considered statistically significant. No multiplicity adjustments were performed. All statistical analyses were done using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Between October 16, 2023, and July 17, 2024, 25 patients were enrolled, with 100% follow-up compliance throughout 1 year (Supplemental Figure S1). In total, 45 patients were screened (20 screen failures, including 30% (6/20) due to anatomic exclusion). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 80.6 ± 4.3 years, and 76.0% of patients were male. All patients were symptomatic, with 60.0% in NYHA class II and 40.0% in class III, and a mean KCCQ-OS score of 61.8 ± 20.0 at baseline. The mean STS-PROM score was 5.6 ± 4.5%, with 8 patients (32.0%) having an STS-PROM ≥8%, and all were designated high surgical risk based on site heart team assessment. Comorbidities were common and included hypertension (96.0%), congestive heart failure (76.0%), renal insufficiency (40.0%), and conduction defects (56.0%). Baseline echocardiographic and CT characteristics as assessed by the core laboratories are shown in Table 1. All patients had moderate to severe (56.0%) or severe (44.0%) native AR. Mean LVEF was 52.6 ± 8.2%, with evidence of left ventricular dilation and remodeling with mean left ventricular end-systolic volume index (LVESVi) of 46.9 ± 14.1 mL/m2, mean left ventricular end-diastolic volume index (LVEDVi) of 97.7 + 19.3 mL/m2, and mean left ventricular mass index (LVMi) of 127.2 ± 35.2 g/m2.

Baseline regurgitant volume was 46.5 ± 13.3 mL and regurgitant fraction was 46.2 ± 5.2%. Annular dimensions were large, with a mean annular perimeter of 87.0 ± 8.9 mm, and 40.0% of patients had annular perimeters >90 mm. Annular calcification was absent or mild in all patients. Mean aortic root angle was 53.4 ± 10.2 degrees, with 24% of patients having a root angle >60 degrees.

Mean procedure duration and fluoroscopy time were 79.2 ± 75.3 minutes and 22.4 ± 14.6 minutes, respectively. Preballoon valvuloplasty was performed in 1 patient (4.0%), and no patients underwent post-TAVR balloon dilation. Among patients who received a valve, larger valve sizes predominated, with 65.2% of patients receiving 31-mm or 34-mm valves. The majority of procedures were performed using a 22F sheath (76.0%).

Intraprocedural complications occurred in 3 patients (12.0%). VARC-3 type 3 bleeding was observed in 2 patients (8.0%)—the patient with the aborted procedure as well as the patient requiring conversion to SAVR. A major vascular complication was observed in the aborted case. No cardiac structural complications were observed.

Through 1 year, 2 patients (8.2%) experienced ischemic stroke, both within 30 days, including 1 of which was disabling. One stroke occurred on day 0 following the procedure; the patient presented with dizziness, slurred speech, and balance difficulties. Imaging demonstrated multiple small recent lacunar infarcts, and the event was adjudicated as disabling (modified Rankin score of 2 at 30 days). The second, nondisabling stroke occurred on day 12 and was determined to be embolic in nature, attributed to underlying severe carotid artery stenosis and not related to the study device or procedure. The patient demonstrated complete functional recovery with a modified Rankin score of 0 at 30 days.

New permanent pacemaker implantation was required in 2 patients (10.0%) within 30 days, with both events adjudicated as device related. By 1 year, the KM estimate of new pacemaker implantation increased to 20.6%, reflecting 2 additional pacemakers that occurred after 30 days, which were adjudicated as not device related. Three VARC-3 type 3 bleeding events occurred in 2 total patients (8.0%) through 1 year. Two major bleeding events occurred within 30 days, both intraprocedural as previously described, and 1 additional bleeding event occurred (syncope and intracranial hemorrhage adjudicated as not device related) between 30 days and 1 year in the patient who required conversion to SAVR.

Vascular complications occurred in 12.2% of patients within 30 days, with no additional events observed through 1 year. These included 2 minor events and 1 major event, which occurred in the aborted case. No myocardial infarction or cardiac structural complications were observed. Acute kidney injury occurred in 1 patient and was limited to stage 1 severity.

Similarly, measures of effective forward flow improved following the procedure and were sustained through 1 year. At 30 days, effective stroke volume index increased from 28.5 ± 5.7 mL/m2 at baseline to 41.4 ± 9.8 mL/m2 (P < .001), and effective cardiac index increased from 1.8 ± 0.4 L/min/m2 to 2.8 ± 0.6 L/min/m2 (P < .001). Functional and health status outcomes are shown in Figure 1. Mean KCCQ-OS scores increased from 64.2 ± 18.7 at baseline to 77.7 ± 19.4 at 30 days and to 83.2 ± 14.5 at 1-year follow-up (mean change 19.0 ± 22.0, P < .001), and NYHA functional class improved at all follow-up time points (P < .001). At 1 year, 72.7% (16/22) of patients had improved NYHA functional class, with 95.5% of patients reporting NYHA class I/II symptoms, and only 1 patient (4.5%) remaining in class III.

Discussion

For patients with symptomatic, severe native AR and high surgical risk, the J-Valve Transfemoral EFS with 1-year follow-up provides several important observations. First, procedural success with the J-Valve was high, with successful implantation achieved in 92% of patients. Second, both 30-day and 1-year clinical outcomes were promising. Through 1 year, there were no cardiovascular-related deaths, 1 noncardiovascular death, 2 ischemic strokes (8.2%, 1 disabling), and 2 new pacemaker implants (10%) occurring within 30 days. Third, J-Valve hemodynamics and function through 1 year were excellent. Fourth, significant and sustained improvements in functional and health status outcomes were observed through 1 year following J-Valve implantation. Fifth, LV reverse remodeling occurred early (by 30 days) and was sustained through 1 year.

Although limited by study size, the J-Valve EFS study population appears to be representative of previously reported high surgical risk populations with symptomatic severe AR, except for a lower proportion of female subjects.12,13 Of note, the single case of aborted valve delivery and major procedural vascular complication occurred in a woman with extreme iliofemoral tortuosity. All subjects enrolled in this EFS were designated high risk for SAVR by the local heart team in accordance with study inclusion criteria. Within this complex and high surgical risk population, procedural and clinical outcomes through 30 days and 1 year were favorable.

The salutary clinical outcomes following J-Valve implantation are consistent with the echocardiographic and hemodynamic assessments to 1 year. At 1 year, none to trace residual total AR was present in 96% of patients, with stable postprocedural mean transvalvular gradients and mean effective orifice areas compared with the immediate postprocedural findings. Concurrently, both early (30-day) and late (1-year) measures of health status (KCCQ-OS) and functional status (NYHA class) demonstrated significant and sustained improvement through 1 year. The early and sustained improvement in valve function, hemodynamics, and clinical measures correspond temporally with objective evidence for LV reverse remodeling, which was significant by 30 days and sustained to 1 year. These findings appear to be similar to those observed in the expanded ALIGN-AR trial for regulatory approval of the JenaValve and reflect the immediate and sustained benefit provided by a competent aortic valve.13 Following J-Valve implantation, significant reductions in LVESVi, LVEDVi, and LVMi were observed at 30 days, 6 months, and 1 year. Further, reverse LV remodeling was accompanied by increases in effective forward stroke volume index and cardiac output index (P < .001 for all time points to 1 year).

Finally, although not a typical study “outcome,” this study demonstrates the importance of an adequate-size matrix and flexible valve delivery system for treatment of patients with severe AR. In the J-Valve EFS, mean aortic annular diameter (87.0 ± 8.9 mm) and annular area (589.6 ± 121.7 mm2) were considerably larger than those observed in the expanded ALIGN-AR trial (80.1 ± 6.9 mm and 495 ± 87.7 mm2, respectively), which studied the Trilogy THV (JenaValve, Inc).13 Notably, 40% (10/25) of patients enrolled in the J-Valve EFS had annular perimeters >90 mm, exceeding the upper limit for treatment with the Trilogy valve. This difference is explained by the availability of larger valve sizes (28, 31, and 34 mm), as well as the expanded diameter of anchor rings versus the Trilogy THV (27 mm maximum valve size and locators, respectively). The J-Valve system is also able to treat patients with aortic root angulation >70 degrees—a contraindication to the Trilogy THV. One patient in this EFS had an aortic angulation of >70 degrees, and more severe degrees of angulation have been previously described.14 Finally, the J-Valve features a lower valve frame height (maximum 25 mm versus 36 mm for Trilogy THV) and incorporates sinus “cut-outs” to facilitate coronary access. Collectively, these findings highlight the unique anatomical challenges associated with treating severe native AR and underscore the need for continued innovation in THV design and development.

Limitations

The limited number of patients enrolled in an EFS study, together with participation by a small number of enrolling centers and operators (8 total centers), many of whom were implanting the device for the first time, limits the generalization and extrapolation of these results to “real world” practice and does not provide sufficient evidence to support device approval, which is the goal of the ongoing pivotal JOURNEY (J-Valve to Treat AR via Transcatheter Therapy) trial (NCT06455787).15 Further, the basis of an EFS classically provides the platform for device iteration with increasing operator experience.

Additionally, a procedural failure occurred in the setting of marked vascular tortuosity, which resulted in difficulty releasing the anchor rings. This was likely related to increased tension within the delivery system associated with the complex vascular anatomy. This case highlights a potential limitation of the technology in patients with excessive tortuosity along the delivery pathway and underscores the importance of patient selection and anatomic assessment.

Finally, follow-up duration in this EFS is currently limited to 1 year; however, follow-up through 5 years is planned and will be important for understanding the durability of the J-Valve platform as well as outcomes in the context of lifetime management for patients with valvular heart disease.

Conclusion

One-year outcomes from the J-Valve Transfemoral EFS provide preliminary evidence for the safety and effectiveness of this disease-specific transcatheter heart valve in patients with severe, symptomatic native AR. Clinical, echocardiographic, hemodynamic, and functional outcome measures to 1 year were favorable. Importantly, J-Valve effectively treated a population of patients with larger aortic annuli who were excluded from the ALIGN-AR trial and are not candidates for any commercially approved AR-specific THV platform. More definitive evidence for safety, effectiveness, and utility of J-Valve awaits the results of the larger JOURNEY IDE trial for regulatory approval.
