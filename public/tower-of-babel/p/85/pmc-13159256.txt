Synergistic emission reduction mechanism of command-and-control and market-based incentives: an empirical study based on core firms in supply chains
Authors: Liping Wang, Ru Li, Chaoya Wang, Chuang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effectiveness of carbon emission reductions by core firms in high-carbon industries is a crucial link for achieving China’s “Dual Carbon” goals. Based on provincial panel data from 2011 to 2021 and data from 526 supply chain firms in high-carbon industries, this study employs the Difference-in-Differences method, linear regression, and mediating effect models to systematically examine the mechanisms and effects of command-and-control, market-based incentive, and public participation environmental regulations on the carbon emissions of supply chain firms. The findings indicate that: (1) Both command-and-control and market-based incentive environmental regulations can significantly reduce corporate carbon emissions and carbon intensity; the direct emission reduction effect of public participation regulations is not yet significant. (2) Mechanism tests reveal that green technology innovation plays a key mediating role in the carbon reduction process driven by the two types of formal regulations. (3) Heterogeneity analysis shows that the policy effects are more pronounced in regions with a higher degree of marketization and greater intensity of environmental regulation. This study provides a mechanistic explanation for understanding how firms in high-carbon industries can resolve the dilemma of emission reduction responsibilities through green innovation under regulatory pressure, and offers empirical evidence for differentially combining environmental regulation tools to facilitate low-carbon transformation of supply chains.Keywords: Environmental regulations, Green technology innovation, Supply chain carbon reduction, Government intervention, Marketization level

Introduction

Global climate change is posing unprecedented and severe challenges to the sustainable development of human society, with excessive greenhouse gas emissions widely recognized by the scientific community as a primary driver of this crisis [1, 2]. Against the backdrop of this global issue, China, as the world’s largest developing country and a major economy, is actively assuming its responsibility as a major power, demonstrating a firm resolve to address climate change. The strategic goals of “achieving carbon peaking before 2030 and carbon neutrality before 2060” proposed by China not only reflect the national will to promote a comprehensive green transition of the economy and society but also constitute a significant contribution to global climate governance. To achieve this ambitious goal, China is continuously improving its environmental governance system, striving to propel the green and low-carbon transformation of high-carbon industries by comprehensively utilizing diversified environmental regulation instruments such as command-and-control, market-based incentives, and public participation. Traditionally, industrial point sources have been regarded as the absolute primary emitters and the core focus of regulation. However, with the deepening of research and the advancement of practice, the overall carbon footprint of supply chains has increasingly become a focal point of attention [3]. The supply chain networks of high-carbon industries are intricate and interwoven, with their carbon emissions permeating every stage from raw material extraction, intermediate goods production, and product manufacturing to transportation, storage, and final consumption [4]. This implies that emission reduction policies focusing solely on the production end may yield limited effectiveness and could even lead to “carbon leakage.” Therefore, examining the sources of carbon emissions from a panoramic supply chain perspective and evaluating the carbon reduction effects of various environmental regulation tools across the entire supply chain are of paramount practical significance for the precise and efficient implementation of the “Dual Carbon” goals.

Extensive academic discussions on the emission reduction effects of environmental regulations have yielded a rich body of theoretical work, providing a solid foundation for this study. Early research, often based on the “Porter Hypothesis,” explored how environmental regulations could enhance corporate competitiveness and indirectly promote emission reduction by incentivizing technological innovation. As theory evolved, some scholars found that environmental regulations might increase firms’ operational costs, thereby crowding out resources for technological innovation or prompting firms to relocate production to regions with laxer regulations—the “Pollution Haven” effect—thus potentially inhibiting carbon reduction at the supply chain level. Although theoretical perspectives on the effectiveness of environmental regulations remain contested, empirical research has, to some extent, provided support for their policy efficacy. At the empirical level, a substantial body of literature confirms that command-and-control regulations can directly reduce pollutant emissions in the short term. For instance, studies based on data from China’s industrial sector show that stringent emission standards and mandatory technology introduction policies can significantly reduce the emission intensity of pollutants such as SO₂ and CO₂ [5]. Furthermore, research indicates that command-and-control instruments can effectively induce invention patents with higher innovative content and have a stronger promoting effect on China’s overall environmental efficiency [6]. Meanwhile, market-based incentive tools are highly favored for their cost-effectiveness and flexibility, with a general consensus in research that they can guide firms to autonomously choose optimal emission reduction paths through price signals [7]. For example, China’s pilot carbon emission trading policies have significantly reduced carbon emission levels in the pilot regions and have promoted energy efficiency improvements and investments in clean technology [8]. Additionally, with the deepening of the concept of social governance, the potential constraining role of public participation regulations, as an informal regulatory force, has gradually been revealed. Multiple empirical studies show that environmental information disclosure and public complaint mechanisms can effectively encourage enterprises to strengthen environmental management and comply with environmental regulations [9], and regions with high public environmental concern are often associated with lower incidences of illegal emissions.

Although the emission reduction effects of different types of environmental regulations have received considerable attention, current research still exhibits notable limitations in several key aspects. Firstly, when examining environmental regulations, existing studies often tend to treat them as a whole or focus on a single policy instrument, lacking a sufficient systematic comparison and comprehensive assessment of different regulatory tools—such as command-and-control, market-based incentives, and public participation—within a unified analytical framework. This limitation hinders a deeper understanding of the potential interactions, relative advantages, and applicable contexts among various policy instruments, and, on the other hand, makes it difficult to design more synergistic and efficient policy mixes tailored to different situations in practice. Secondly, the current literature predominantly focuses on direct emissions at the firm level or specific production stages, or relies on macro-aggregated data at the regional level. Research examining the impact mechanisms of environmental regulations on carbon emissions from a whole-chain supply chain perspective remains relatively limited. As the carbon footprint of core supply chain firms can more comprehensively and authentically reflect the climate impact of economic activities, the absence of this perspective may, to some extent, affect the comprehensive assessment and precise decision-making regarding emission reduction policy effectiveness. Based on this, from a policy synergy perspective, this paper systematically investigates the effect differences and synergistic mechanisms of different types of environmental regulation tools on the carbon emissions of core supply chain firms, and conducts a comprehensive assessment of their emission reduction effectiveness. The aim is to provide empirical references for constructing precise and efficient supply chain emission reduction policy mixes, thereby promoting the green transformation of supply chains and the achievement of the “Dual Carbon” goals. Specifically, this paper aims to address the following core questions: (1) Are there significant differences in the effects of different types of environmental regulation tools on carbon emission reduction for core supply chain firms? (2) What is the impact mechanism of environmental regulations on carbon emission reduction for core supply chain firms? (3) What are the key factors influencing the efficacy of environmental regulations in driving carbon emission reduction for core supply chain firms?

The potential marginal contributions of this study are mainly reflected in the following three aspects: (1) In terms of research perspective, this paper, within a unified analytical framework, systematically examines the synergistic mechanisms between command-and-control and market-based incentive environmental regulation tools, deepening the theoretical understanding of how the “constraint-compensation” dual-drive model promotes carbon emission reduction in core firms within high-carbon industries. Compared to research focusing on a single policy or macro-level effects, this paper provides a more integrated analytical perspective for understanding how emission reduction responsibilities are transmitted from core firms to the supply chain by elucidating the complementary logic of the two types of regulations in setting baselines and optimizing pathways. (2) Regarding research scale and subject, this study effectively bridges the macro policy environment with micro-level corporate behavior and explicitly focuses the analysis on core firms within the supply chains of high-carbon industries. This focus not only addresses the practical constraint of data availability but also enhances the relevance and application value of the policy assessment. (3) Methodologically, this paper employs a combination of the Difference-in-Differences (DID) model, mediation effect tests, and heterogeneity analysis, constructing a multi-layered and progressively advanced empirical testing system. By treating the “New Environmental Protection Law” and the “Carbon Trading Pilot” as quasi-natural experiments, it identifies the net effects of the policy treatments; subsequently, it reveals the core transmission pathway through a mediation model of green technology innovation; and further examines the heterogeneity of policy effects under contextual factors such as regional marketization levels and regulatory intensity. Literature review

The impact of environmental regulation on carbon emission reduction

As a core policy instrument for addressing the externality of carbon emissions, environmental regulation is, in essence, a public governance process through which the government imposes environmental constraints on market entities using diverse policy tools to balance environmental protection and economic development goals [10]. The American scholar Alfred E. Kahn first defined the core connotation of “regulation” in 1970, viewing it as a public governance act that intervenes in market competition through administrative orders to maintain a balance between economic and social performance. As a significant branch of the regulatory system, environmental regulation can be categorized into three types based on policy attributes: command-and-control, market-based incentives, and public participation [11, 12].

Academic debates on the carbon emission reduction effects of environmental regulation have formed three representative perspectives. The first is the “compliance cost effect” viewpoint, which posits that environmental regulations, especially command-and-control types, increase corporate compliance costs, squeeze R&D investment, and may even lead firms to expand production to spread costs, ultimately resulting in increased carbon emissions. This conclusion has found partial validation in empirical studies of heavily polluting or high-carbon industries like steel and apparel [13, 14]. The second is the “forced emission reduction effect” theory. Based on the Porter Hypothesis, scholars argue that well-designed environmental regulations can stimulate corporate green technology innovation and industrial structure upgrading, generating an innovation compensation effect. Its mechanism exhibits type heterogeneity: command-and-control regulations promote corporate green, low-carbon transition and technological progress through mandatory standards [15, 16]; market-based incentive regulations foster pollution control technology advancement via tools like carbon pricing and emission trading [17, 18]; and public participation regulations achieve emission reduction by promoting regional green technology innovation [19, 20]. Furthermore, formal and informal environmental regulations complement each other through institutional constraints and reputation mechanisms, respectively. The third is the “nonlinear relationship” hypothesis. Numerous empirical studies confirm an inverted U-shaped relationship between environmental regulation and carbon emissions. As regulatory intensity increases from weak to strong, its effect shifts from “compliance cost” to “forced emission reduction.” This relationship is moderated by threshold variables such as economic development level, technological innovation capability, and industrial structure [21]. It is noteworthy that existing research has clarified the heterogeneity in the carbon reduction effects of different regulatory tools: both command-and-control and market-based incentive regulations exhibit a significant inverted U-shaped characteristic, while public participation regulations demonstrate a sustained emission reduction effect [22]. Moreover, the carbon reduction effect of environmental regulations also exhibits regional spatial spillover effects; in particular, command-and-control regulations may have a U-shaped impact on carbon emissions in neighboring regions [23].

Supply chain carbon reduction

As a dynamic and highly interconnected network system formed by multiple entities through win-win collaboration, the interconnectedness and interdependence among stakeholders in a supply chain determine that corporate synergy harbors significant potential for carbon emission reduction [24, 25]. The construction and development of low-carbon supply chains are not only a core component of supply chain synergy theory but also a critical pathway supporting the achievement of current sustainable development goals. Academia has produced a relatively rich body of research findings on supply chain carbon reduction, which can be broadly divided into three core research streams: low-carbon supply chain operation management strategies, low-carbon supply chain finance, and low-carbon supply chain carbon regulation. Research in the first two areas has established relatively mature analytical frameworks, providing an important theoretical foundation for subsequent studies.

In the study of low-carbon supply chain operation management strategies, scholars primarily conduct in-depth explorations from three dimensions: logistics system optimization, cost control, and the identification of influencing factors. As a foundational aspect of supply chain carbon reduction, logistics system optimization research focuses on the low-carbon transformation of the entire logistics chain, specifically encompassing logistics network layout optimization [26, 27], the establishment of low-carbon-oriented logistics supplier selection criteria, low-carbon choices for transportation modes and routes [28], dynamic management mechanisms for low-carbon inventory [29], low-carbon supply chain resilience [30], and exploring the carbon reduction potential of reverse logistics systems [31, 32]. These studies provide concrete operational pathways for carbon reduction in the logistics segment. Cost control strategies focus on the issue of interest coordination during the achievement of carbon reduction goals, centering on two key topics: cost-sharing and target allocation. Research in this area often takes manufacturers or retailers as the dominant entities, emphasizing the balance of profits across all supply chain tiers within cost control. Regarding carbon emission reduction cost-sharing, contract design is a core research direction. For instance, Liu et al., [33] pointed out that in a market environment where consumers have a preference for low-carbon products, carbon emission reduction cost-sharing contracts can effectively promote synergistic supply chain development. Zhang and Yu, [34] further confirmed that such contracts can achieve Pareto improvement for both manufacturers and retailers, enhancing overall supply chain efficiency and profit levels at each stage. As another important dimension of cost control, carbon emission reduction target allocation was examined by Huang et al., [35], who explored government-led carbon reduction target allocation decisions under different supply chain power structures, clarifying the mechanism through which power structures influence allocation schemes and social welfare. Additionally, research on the influencing factors of supply chain carbon reduction has gradually become a hotspot in recent years. Scholars commonly focus on variables such as supply chain power structure [36], emission reduction fairness [37], and consumer low-carbon preference [38], providing empirical support for the precise formulation of emission reduction strategies.

As a crucial financial support system for carbon emission reduction, research on low-carbon supply chain finance primarily focuses on three major aspects: financing models, influencing factors, and efficiency evaluation [39]. As the core element, low-carbon supply chain financing aims to provide targeted financial support to capital-constrained firms, alleviate the financial pressure of emission reduction investments, and enhance supply chain resilience and flexibility. It encompasses diverse forms such as accounts receivable financing, sales financing, advance payment financing, inventory financing, and purchase order financing. Related research concentrates on optimizing the selection of financing models, including the comparison and suitability analysis of pathways like trade credit, asset securitization, peer-to-peer (P2P) lending, bank loans, and carbon emission rights pledge financing [40–42]. Research on the factors influencing financing strategies unfolds around dual market and policy dimensions. At the market level, factors include consumer low-carbon preference, transaction fairness, and market competition intensity. For example, Tang et al., [43] found that transaction fairness issues can alter financing equilibrium and harm manufacturer profits, emphasizing that considering fairness is key to achieving supply chain win-win outcomes. Xia et al., [44] study showed that market competition intensity and consumer low-carbon preference significantly impact firms’ equilibrium decisions and profits, with intense competition not only hindering manufacturer carbon reduction but also negatively affecting their profits. At the policy level, the focus is primarily on the influence of environmental policies such as carbon quotas, carbon trading, carbon taxes, carbon emission rights pledge financing, and emission reduction cost subsidies [45–47]. Regarding the evaluation of financing efficiency, existing research frequently employs methods such as grey relational analysis, factor analysis, and data envelopment analysis [48], providing quantitative tools for assessing the effectiveness of financing schemes.

The impact of environmental regulation on carbon emission reduction in core supply chain firms

As an important policy tool for governments to coordinate environmental protection and economic development, environmental regulation imposes environmental constraints on market entities through diverse approaches such as command-and-control, market-based incentives, and public participation. Its impact in the fields of carbon emissions and supply chain emission reduction has become a research focus [49]. Existing research indicates significant differences in the pathways and effects of different environmental regulation tools on carbon emissions: command-and-control regulations reduce emissions by promoting corporate green transition and technological progress [50], market-based incentive regulations foster green economic development by leveraging advancements in pollution control technology [51, 52], while public participation regulations achieve regional emission reduction by driving green technology innovation [19]. These regulatory tools not only play important roles at the national, regional, and industry levels (e.g., apparel, tourism, steel industry) [14], but their influence also extends to all stages of supply chain operations. In the supply chain context, environmental regulations profoundly influence corporate decisions and member collaboration through policies such as carbon taxes, emission trading, and emission reduction subsidies, thereby promoting carbon reduction. Research often employs game-theoretic methods to analyze the behaviors of multiple agents like governments, firms, and the public under competitive or cooperative conditions, focusing on operational decisions such as pricing, production, inventory, sales, and closed-loop supply chain design [53–55], and further explores key issues like emission reduction investment, strategy selection, and the effectiveness of green supply chain management. Practice shows that a single policy tool often struggles to achieve ideal emission reduction outcomes, while the synergistic use of command-and-control and market-based incentive regulations can significantly enhance the effectiveness of green supply chain management [56, 57]. Especially when considering factors like supply chain power structure, consumer preferences, and regional heterogeneity, policy synergy and institutional fit are crucial for constructing an efficient emission reduction system [21].

Literature review and analysis

According to the existing literature, the academic community has achieved abundant research results on the carbon emission reduction effect of environmental regulation, the realization path of carbon emission reduction of supply chain enterprises, and the impact of environmental regulation on carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises, which provides important theoretical support for understanding the macro policy logic and micro practical mechanism of low-carbon transformation. However, the existing research still has some deficiencies. First, although the importance of policy synergy has been noted in environmental regulation research, most studies focus on the evaluation of synergy effects at the macro level, lacking the deconstruction of the micro mechanism of “constraint-compensation” synergy between command-and-control and market-incentive regulation, especially without clarifying their dynamic interaction paths and adaptive conditions in the supply chain context. Second, most studies on carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises focus on strategy optimization of a single link or a single entity, with insufficient discussion on the transmission mechanism of how environmental regulation, as an external core driving force, runs through upstream and downstream enterprises in the supply chain and promotes multi-agent collaborative emission reduction, and limited attention to the emission reduction dilemmas and support paths of small and medium-sized enterprises under policy pressure. Third, most cross-field research focuses on the independent effect of a single regulatory tool, lacking systematic empirical testing of the collaborative emission reduction effect of different tool combinations in the entire supply chain, which also provides a clear research gap and innovation direction for this study focusing on the synergy path of command-and-control constraints and market-incentive compensation.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

As a core policy instrument for governments to address environmental negative externalities, environmental regulation has a profound theoretical foundation for its role in promoting carbon emission reduction among supply chain enterprises.

First, based on the theory of new institutional economics, regulatory policies reshape the incentive structure of market entities by altering the rules of the game. Command-and-control environmental regulations establish legally binding hard constraints, clearly defining the compliance boundaries for corporate environmental behaviors and substantially increasing the costs of non-compliance [58]. From the perspective of institutional theory, such rigid constraints essentially reduce institutional uncertainties both within and outside the supply chain by setting unified emission reduction standards and compliance baselines. This lays a stable institutional foundation for the coordinated operation of subsequent policy instruments, and avoids the free-riding problem in emission reduction caused by ambiguous standards and unbalanced competition under a single market mechanism. To evade risks such as fines and production suspension for rectification, core supply chain enterprises are compelled to incorporate carbon emission reduction into their core strategic decisions, and transmit emission reduction pressures to upstream and downstream partners through contracts, performance requirements, and other means, thereby driving carbon emission reduction across the entire supply chain network [59].

Second, the Porter Hypothesis provides a dynamic perspective, arguing that well-designed environmental regulations can stimulate the innovation offset effect. Market-incentivized regulations (e.g., carbon trading, environmental taxes) internalize carbon emissions as either production costs or assets of enterprises [60, 61], offering economic incentives for flexible emission reduction. This aligns with the core logic of “incentive-based regulation” in regulatory economics: by optimizing resource allocation through price signals, it compensates for the lack of flexibility and innovation incentives inherent in command-and-control regulations. Consequently, supply chain enterprises can still minimize emission reduction costs through independent choices under rigid constraints, enhancing the efficiency of policy implementation and corporate acceptance. To minimize compliance costs or maximize returns on carbon assets, supply chain enterprises proactively optimize production processes, energy structures, and even supply chain collaboration models, seeking the lowest-cost emission reduction pathways and thus achieving overall carbon emission reduction.

Furthermore, stakeholder theory posits that the survival and development of enterprises depend on their responsiveness to the expectations of various stakeholders. Public-participatory regulations amplify the green preferences and pressure signals from stakeholders such as consumers, communities, and environmental organizations [62], directly impacting corporate reputation and brand value. To maintain their social legitimacy and market competitiveness, core supply chain enterprises have strong incentives to strengthen environmental management and prioritize cooperation with low-carbon suppliers, thereby advancing carbon emission reduction [63].

In summary, despite their divergent mechanisms of action, the three types of environmental regulations can directly or indirectly promote carbon emission reduction among supply chain enterprises through cost, innovation, and legitimacy channels. Therefore, this paper proposes:

H1

Environmental regulation has a significant positive effect on carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises.

Although environmental regulations are generally expected to promote emission reduction, significant differences exist among different types of regulatory tools in terms of policy rigidity, mechanism of action, and effective conditions, and their net effects await empirical testing, which constitutes the focus of in-depth discussion in this study.

Characterized by mandatory and direct attributes, command-and-control regulations derive their theoretical effectiveness from the theory of public enforcement. From the perspective of transaction cost theory, standardized emission reduction requirements and explicit penalty mechanisms reduce government supervision costs and information asymmetry costs among supply chain enterprises, ensuring the rapid implementation of emission reduction targets. However, excessively stringent standards may squeeze the space for enterprises’ independent decision-making and trigger high adaptive transaction costs [64]. This theory holds that clear, strict, and strongly enforced deterrent rules can effectively constrain corporate behaviors and ensure the rapid achievement of emission reduction goals. Nevertheless, its drawbacks lie in the potential inhibition of enterprises’ autonomy and innovation space, as well as the generation of high administrative supervision costs.

The theoretical underpinnings of market-incentivized regulations are Pigouvian tax theory and the Coase theorem. Guiding resource allocation toward low-carbon sectors through price signals, such regulations are renowned for their flexibility and economic efficiency [65]. Their core advantage lies in reducing the technical transaction costs of corporate emission reduction through economic incentives and stimulating the motivation for independent innovation. However, in scenarios where the market is underdeveloped and the carbon pricing mechanism is irrational, they are prone to triggering vicious competition due to the divergence in enterprises’ willingness to reduce emissions, thereby increasing the transaction costs of market coordination [66]. These regulations allow enterprises to independently select cost-optimal emission reduction approaches, which theoretically can stimulate stronger innovation motivation. Yet, their effectiveness is highly dependent on a sound market mechanism and a rational carbon pricing mechanism; in regions with underdeveloped markets and weak data supervision capabilities, their effects may be significantly compromised [67].

Public-participatory regulations are rooted in the theory of information asymmetry and the theory of corporate social responsibility, exerting their functions through social supervision and corporate reputation mechanisms [68]. While they can effectively complement the deficiencies of formal regulations, their effects are indirect and lagging, and they are heavily reliant on social capital conditions such as public environmental awareness, media freedom, and the quality of information disclosure [69].

At China’s current stage of development, the first two types of “hard regulations” may still serve as the main drivers of emission reduction, whereas the “soft constraint” effect of public participation may not have been fully manifested, or it may need to be combined with the former two to exert greater effectiveness. Therefore, the emission reduction effects of the three types of regulations are not homogeneous, and their relative effectiveness is a proposition to be verified by empirical evidence. Based on the above theoretical differentiations, this paper proposes:

H2

Command-and-control and market-incentivized environmental regulations have a significant positive effect on carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises, whereas the direct emission reduction effect of public-participatory regulations may be insignificant.

The key to whether environmental regulation can effectively drive emission reduction lies in its ability to trigger fundamental changes in enterprises’ technologies and behaviors. Green technological innovation plays a crucial mediating role in this process, a conclusion that integrates multiple theoretical perspectives.

First, the induced technological innovation theory clearly states that relative changes in factor prices are the key driving force guiding the direction of technological innovation. Environmental regulations, especially market-incentivized tools, alter the relative price ratios of capital, labor, energy and environmental factors by increasing the price or cost of carbon emissions, thereby directing enterprises’ R&D investment and innovation activities toward energy-saving and carbon-reduction technology fields, and ultimately achieving emission reduction through technological progress [70].

Second, the dynamic version of the Porter Hypothesis further strengthens this logic, arguing that strict and well-designed environmental regulations can not only offset compliance costs but even generate net benefits through the innovation offset effect. Both the forward-looking standards set by command-and-control regulations and the economic value created by market-based regulations provide strong market and policy pull for enterprises to carry out green process innovation and product innovation, driving them to develop and apply more efficient low-carbon technologies [71].

Third, the resource-based view provides support from the perspective of building enterprises’ internal capabilities. Faced with regulatory pressure, enterprises regard green technological innovation as a strategic resource for gaining future competitive advantages. By developing green patents and technologies, enterprises can not only meet compliance requirements but also shape their green brand image and seize emerging markets, thus gaining a favorable position in supply chain competition [72].

It is worth emphasizing that the joint driving effect of command-and-control and market-incentivized regulations on green technological innovation is essentially based on the logic of institutional complementarity and regulatory coordination: command-and-control regulations, by setting mandatory emission reduction standards, define clear application scenarios and market demand for green technological innovation, reducing the market uncertainty of innovation outcomes; market-incentivized regulations, through economic tools such as carbon pricing and subsidies, compensate for the high input and high-risk costs of green technological innovation, accelerating the commercialization of innovative technologies. Together, the two synergistically construct an innovation-driven mechanism featuring demand pull and cost compensation.

Therefore, the transmission path through which environmental regulation promotes carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises by stimulating green technological innovation has a solid theoretical foundation. Based on this, this paper proposes:

H3

Green technological innovation plays a mediating role in the process by which environmental regulation promotes carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises.

(3)Mediating Variable: Green Technological Innovation. This paper uses the green patent application data of supply chain enterprises as the mediating variable. To comprehensively reflect the outcomes of green technological innovation of supply chain enterprises while fully considering data availability, the number of intellectual property rights is selected as the indicator for evaluating the technological innovation capability of supply chain enterprises. Specifically, the enterprise’s technological innovation value is measured by the sum of “existing patents plus patents pending application”. The reasons are twofold: First, green patents are a direct manifestation of green technological innovation outputs and possess quantifiable characteristics. They allow for the classification of technologies based on patent data, which can reflect the differences in the value connotations of innovation activities [73]. Second, the patent application process is relatively lengthy, so using green patent application data to reflect the outputs of enterprises’ green technological innovation activities offers higher timeliness. Green patents include two categories: green invention patents and green utility model patents.

Sample selection and data sources

This paper selects data of 526 upstream and downstream enterprises in the supply chains of high-carbon industries from 2011 to 2021 as the research sample. The variable measurement covers 30 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities directly under the Central Government of China (Tibet Autonomous Region, Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan regions are excluded due to data unavailability). The carbon emission data in this paper are mainly integrated from the China Sectoral Carbon Emission Inventory (1997–2019) and provincial carbon emission inventories released by the China Emission Accounts and Datasets (CEADs), as well as the publicly disclosed energy consumption and carbon emission data of listed enterprises from the China Stock Market & Accounting Research Database (CSMAR). Other firm-level data are sourced from corporate annual reports, CNINFO, East Money Information, China Energy Yearbook, China Environmental Yearbook, China Science and Technology Yearbook, and CSMAR, among other databases. In terms of data processing, this paper mainly adopts interpolation and forecasting methods to supplement partially missing data. Further analysis

Although the previous chapter preliminarily verified the statistical correlation between the three types of environmental regulations and carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises, as well as the transmission mechanism of green technological innovation, through multiple regression and mediating effect analysis, the benchmark regression results may be disturbed by endogeneity issues, making it difficult to establish a rigorous causal relationship. To more effectively identify the net policy effect of environmental regulations and enhance the reliability of the research conclusions, this chapter adopts the Difference-in-Differences (DID) method—a more rigorous causal identification strategy. Specifically, we select the revision and implementation of the new Environmental Protection Law as a typical representative of command-and-control regulations, and the launch of the carbon emission trading pilot policy as a natural experiment for market-incentivized regulations. By constructing treatment and control groups, this study accurately evaluates the causal effects of these two core environmental regulatory tools on carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises.

In summary, the following conclusions can be drawn. First, command-and-control environmental regulation tools outperform market-incentivized ones in terms of emission reduction effectiveness at both provincial and national regional levels. Command-and-control environmental regulations contribute to the formation of “pollution havens” and facilitate the spillover of polluting industries. While market-incentivized environmental regulations also exert a significant positive effect on emission reduction in the process of pollution control, their effects are subject to a certain time lag, and a longer observation period is required to verify their sustainability and stability. Against the backdrop of continuously increasing environmental regulation intensity, the concentration of local polluting industries will be further enhanced. This is because pollution control imposes stringent requirements on the overall technology, capital, and equipment of the supply chain, entailing relatively high costs. For small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the supply chain that lack clean technology capabilities, if the costs of environmental governance far exceed the benefits, they will conduct a cost-benefit trade-off analysis, which in turn can achieve a relatively high level of environmental regulation effectiveness under such circumstances.

Second, the emission reduction effect of public-participatory environmental regulation is relatively insignificant. Despite the continuous improvement of environmental protection laws, regulations, and law enforcement efforts in China, several issues remain unresolved. First, some environmental management departments turn a blind eye to environmental problems. Second, the current environmental laws impose relatively lenient penalties on polluters. Third, the existing litigation system restricts the effective participation of the public, resulting in situations where some citizens are reluctant to initiate lawsuits even when they witness environmentally destructive behaviors. Therefore, establishing and improving the environmental public interest litigation mechanism plays a positive and pivotal role in intensifying environmental protection efforts in accordance with the law, punishing environmental offenders, and providing legal and institutional safeguards for the public.

Finally, the emission reduction effects of market-incentivized environmental regulations exhibit regional heterogeneity. Their impact magnitude and intensity in western China are far higher than those in eastern China, while they exert an opposite effect in central China. This is because the industrial restructuring and upgrading in central China are still in the early stage, and the maturity of energy-saving and clean technologies within the supply chain remains relatively low, leading to insufficient innovation offset effects. It is undeniable that no single environmental regulation tool is a panacea; each has its own advantages and inherent limitations.

Overall evaluation of the impact of environmental policies on supply chain emission reduction

As a methodology evolved from natural experiments, the difference-in-differences (DID) method requires the timing of policy implementation to be random in its application, with only one round of experimental intervention. Otherwise, it will lead to biases in policy evaluation. However, the DID method also has certain limitations; for instance, the selection and grouping of samples are often subject to the subjective factors of researchers.

Statistical analysis based on panel data in this chapter reveals that China’s carbon trading policy and the new Environmental Protection Law have generally achieved favorable outcomes, yet the policy effects exhibit significant regional heterogeneity. In pilot regions, the implementation of the carbon trading policy has not only improved energy utilization efficiency but also effectively curbed carbon emissions of core supply chain enterprises. Policy effectiveness varies across different regions: the higher the level of marketization and the intensity of environmental regulation, the more pronounced the policy effects will be. Vertically, the carbon trading policy in pilot regions has promoted overall low-carbon innovation within the supply chain, with an enhancement effect ranging from 5% to 10%, without generating crowding-out effects. Relevant academic research focusing on the field of carbon emission reduction technology investment by core supply chain enterprises also indicates that the carbon trading policy may lead to output reduction, where the carbon emission reduction effect is a direct result of decreased output, rather than the policy exerting a positive impact on enterprises’ investment in emission reduction technologies.

The empirical results presented above demonstrate that environmental regulation has a negative impact on carbon emissions of supply chain enterprises, indicating a significant negative correlation between the two variables: a 1% increase in the intensity of environmental regulation leads to an approximate 0.5% decrease in carbon emissions. This suggests that the environmental policies implemented in China at the current stage have achieved remarkable effectiveness. To attain carbon emission reduction targets, it is imperative to steadily advance industrial structure transformation. Against the backdrop of China’s continuous urbanization, the level of industrial development has been rising, industrial output value has expanded consistently, carbon emissions from supply chain enterprises have kept growing, and the greenhouse effect has become increasingly severe. Drawing on the research of numerous scholars and the regional distribution of carbon emissions, it can be concluded that carbon emission reduction by core supply chain enterprises should also focus on two fundamental effects: the industrial structure effect and the energy intensity effect. In response to these two aspects, China has issued a series of policies, continuously increasing investment in assets for supply chain environmental pollution control. Market-incentivized environmental regulation tools have been applied in a more flexible and extensive manner, playing an important role in promoting carbon emission reduction. Under the combined influence of the new Environmental Protection Law and the carbon trading policy, the carbon emission reduction effect of environmental regulation in high-emission regions is relatively weak. The underlying reason is that more economically developed regions are characterized by more frequent industrial and commercial activities, denser population, and consequently higher corresponding carbon emissions. Promoting the transformation and upgrading of industrial structure will help increase the proportion of emerging industries, thereby reducing carbon emissions from supply chain enterprises. At the current stage, the carbon emission per unit of GDP of China’s three major industries has been showing a downward trend year by year. In particular, the continuous optimization and improvement of the secondary industry structure have exerted a positive role in promoting carbon emission reduction by core supply chain enterprises. According to the regression model, in the process of China’s industrial structure transformation and upgrading, environmental regulation has generated a significant forcing effect. In addition, energy prices do not have a significant impact on the overall carbon emissions of the supply chain, indicating no significant correlation between the two. At present, China’s energy intensity has maintained a downward trend, meaning that the demand for energy consumption has gradually decreased, which has in turn led to a reduction in carbon emissions from supply chain enterprises. Regarding the intensity of environmental regulation, the key observation is whether it has a significant effect on reducing carbon emissions from supply chain enterprises—that is, the stronger the intensity of environmental policies, the better the emission reduction effect, which is also the policy outcome pursued by China.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Conclusions

According to the Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), global carbon emissions must be reduced by 43% by 2030 to achieve the temperature control target. China’s self-determined contribution goals of “reaching peak carbon dioxide emissions by 2030 and achieving carbon neutrality by 2060” constitute a positive response to this global consensus. Whether environmental regulations can effectively drive carbon emission reduction by core supply chain enterprises is not only an urgent theoretical proposition to clarify against the backdrop of global climate governance, but also a crucial practical issue in China’s process of fulfilling its “dual carbon” goals. However, existing studies have not yet systematically distinguished the implementation effects and mechanisms of action of different policy types. Against this backdrop, this paper adopts China’s provincial panel data from 2011 to 2021 and micro-data of 526 supply chain enterprises in high-carbon industries, combining the difference-in-differences (DID) model, linear regression model and mediating effect model. It classifies environmental regulations into three categories: command-and-control, market-incentivized and public-participatory, and systematically examines the impacts of different environmental regulations on carbon emission reduction of core supply chain enterprises and their inherent mechanisms. The research findings indicate that both command-and-control and market-incentivized environmental regulations can significantly reduce the carbon emissions and carbon emission intensity of supply chain enterprises. From the perspective of economic significance, the marginal emission reduction effect of command-and-control regulations is approximately 1.88 times that of market-incentivized regulations, suggesting that under the current policy framework, administrative regulatory tools still play a dominant role in promoting carbon emission reduction by core supply chain enterprises. This conclusion is first verified by the linear regression model, and then further confirmed by the DID model, which takes the implementation of the new Environmental Protection Law as a representative of command-and-control regulations and the launch of carbon emission trading pilot policies as a representative of market-incentivized regulations. Moreover, the conclusion remains valid after a series of robustness tests. Further mechanism analysis reveals that green technological innovation plays a key mediating role in the process of environmental regulations driving carbon emission reduction. These two types of formal regulations not only force enterprises to undergo green transformation through the cost constraint effect, but also promote technological upgrading and diffusion through the innovation offset effect, thereby significantly increasing the number of green patent applications filed by enterprises.

Policy recommendations

Based on the empirical findings of this study, the following actionable policy recommendations are proposed to effectively drive carbon emission reduction among core supply chain enterprises:

Construct a three-stage hybrid regulatory framework. Adopt a phased strategy featuring “command-and-control as the foundation, market-based incentives as the core driver, and public participation as a supplement”. In the initial phase, establish emission reduction baselines by setting carbon emission quota standards, and formulate supporting transitional policies for technological transformation. In the intermediate phase, develop a dynamic quota adjustment mechanism that allows enterprises with excess emission reductions to reserve quotas for trading or offsetting, thereby balancing short-term compliance obligations and long-term innovation incentives. In the mature phase, introduce a blockchain-based carbon footprint tracing platform to achieve synergistic effects among regulatory tools.

Research limitations and prospects

Although this paper provides new empirical evidence for understanding the regional heterogeneity of environmental regulations and their policy combinations, it still has certain limitations that warrant further exploration in future research. For instance, this study is mainly based on provincial panel data and information at the level of core enterprises. While it can effectively identify the net policy effects and mediating paths, it is insufficient in capturing the dynamic transmission mechanisms of regulatory tools across the entire supply chain network, enterprises’ long-term strategic adjustment behaviors, and the practical application effects of green technologies. Future research may consider integrating micro-behavioral data of industrial enterprises, real-time environmental monitoring big data, or supply chain transaction records to construct a more granular dynamic analysis framework. This will help reveal the spillover effects and adaptation conditions of policies in multi-level networks, thereby further enhancing the timeliness and policy guidance value of the research.
