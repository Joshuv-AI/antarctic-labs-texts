Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) Recurrence and Death After Viral Clearance in an HCV-Viremic Donor to HCV-Negative Kidney Recipient
Authors: Shengliang He, Tomohiro Tanaka, Sung-Hoon Kim, David Thomsen, Daniel A Katz, Christie P Thomas, Prerna Rastogi, Hassan Aziz, Alan I Reed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent direct-acting antiviral (DAA) regimens have achieved nearly universal viral eradication in hepatitis C virus (HCV) nucleic acid test (NAT)-positive donor to HCV-negative recipient (HCV NAT+/–) transplantation. This case study presents an early HCV recurrence after initial viral clearance with DAA therapy in an HCV NAT+/– kidney recipient that then rapidly progressed to acute liver failure and the death of the transplanted individual. A 52-year-old man with type 2 diabetes underwent a deceased donor HCV NAT+/– kidney transplant. Postoperatively, the allograft function gradually improved without necessitating dialysis. HCV NAT at postoperative day (POD) #3 revealed a viral load of 5 log IU/mL. A 12-week course of sofosbuvir/velpatasvir was completed, and HCV NAT was negative by POD#94. His liver enzymes were significantly elevated at POD#170. Repeat HCV NAT showed viral load at >8 log IU/mL. Sofosbuvir/velpatasvir was reinitiated with a plan to transition to sofosbuvir/velpatasvir/voxilaprevir. On POD#183, the patient was admitted for diabetic ketoacidosis and acute liver failure. His condition rapidly deteriorated to multi-organ failure, and he died on POD#188. Fatal recurrence is rare in HCV NAT+/– kidney transplant recipients after completion of DAA regimen. HCV recurrence should be considered with abnormal liver chemistries in the absence of alternative explanations.Index Words: Hepatitis C viremic organ transplantation, kidney transplantation, hepatitis C virus recurrence

Introduction

Direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapies have transformed the landscape of solid organ transplantation, permitting kidney transplants from hepatitis C virus (HCV) nucleic acid test (NAT)-positive donors to HCV-negative recipients (HCV NAT+/–).1 Meta-analyses show that current DAA regimens achieve nearly universal viral eradication after HCV NAT+/– kidney transplantation.2 This case study presents an early HCV recurrence after initial viral clearance in an HCV NAT+/– kidney recipient who rapidly progressed to acute liver failure and death.

Case Presentation

A 52-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease underwent kidney transplantation from a donation after circulatory death donor. The recipient’s past medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus and coronary artery disease. His kidney disease was presumably secondary to longstanding diabetes. Preoperative hemoglobin A1c was 7.1%. His cardiac history included coronary angioplasty with 3-stent placements. Before transplant, the patient successfully participated in cardiac rehabilitation. His ejection fraction was 51% on transthoracic echocardiogram, and stress echocardiogram was negative for ischemia. He did not have a history of any liver disease.

The donor was a 60-year-old man who died of anoxia after an accident. The duration of cardiac arrest and downtime was unknown, but cardiopulmonary resuscitation was administered for 26 minutes. His medical history included HCV, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, schizophrenia, and hypothyroidism. He had been diagnosed with HCV approximately 20 years prior, with no documented history of treatment. The donor’s Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) was 97% at the time of organ procurement. The kidney was preserved using hypothermic machine perfusion. Donor kidney biopsy revealed 4% glomerulosclerosis, <5% interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, and no evidence of fibrin thrombi (Fig 1A).

On the day of surgery, the recipient’s HCV antibody and viral load were both undetectable (Fig 2). He was immune to hepatitis B virus, and human immunodeficiency virus testing was negative. FibroTest showed no evidence of fibrosis. His calculated panel reactive antibody was 23%, but he had no known sensitizing events. The final flow crossmatch was negative, and donor-specific antibodies were absent. The patient received antithymocyte globulin and methylprednisolone as induction therapy. Maintenance immunosuppression consisted of tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone.

The allograft functioned immediately without the need for postoperative dialysis but remained suboptimal (Fig 2). His posttransplant course was complicated by impaired wound healing and poor glucose control, resulting in multiple episodes of diabetic ketoacidosis, and urosepsis, several requiring hospitalizations. Additionally, on postoperative day (POD) #27, he developed atrial fibrillation and suffered a non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction. His cardiac function continued to decline, with his ejection fraction dropping to 20% by POD#51. His kidney function worsened, and he required intermittent hemodialysis. His first biopsy, performed 1 month posttransplant, revealed significant acute tubular injury, mild to moderate interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, and no evidence of rejection (Fig 1B-D). A second biopsy performed another month later demonstrated thrombotic microangiopathy and mild interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (Fig 1E and F). The patient experienced multiple episodes of elevated tacrolimus trough levels between the 2 biopsies, raising concern for drug-induced thrombotic microangiopathy in the context of supportive histopathologic findings. After excluding other potential etiologies, the thrombotic microangiopathy was attributed to tacrolimus use, and the immunosuppressive regimen was modified accordingly by substituting tacrolimus with sirolimus, in alignment with our institutional protocol.

His HCV NAT at POD#3 revealed a viral load of 5 log IU/mL (genotype 1) with normal liver function tests. Sofosbuvir/velpatasvir was initiated on POD#12. The patient completed a 12-week course, but with sporadic interruption during multiple hospitalizations noted above. HCV NAT was undetectable on POD#94, and he was considered to have achieved end-of-treatment response. There was no clinical or laboratory evidence of cryoglobulinemia or paraproteinemia that might interfere with the HCV NAT results.

On POD#170, he was admitted with 1 week history of nausea, vomiting, and generalized weakness. Laboratory results were significant with glucose 417 mg/dL, creatinine 5.26 mg/dL, alkaline phosphatase 361 U/L, alanine transaminase 225 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 168 U/L, and bilirubin 1.5 mg/dL. HCV NAT showed viral load >8 log IU/mL (genotype 1). Sofosbuvir/velpatasvir was restarted with plans to switch to sofosbuvir/velpatasvir/voxilaprevir once it became available. HCV resistant testing was not performed as it would not change our treatment plan. The patient was discharged on POD#173 but on POD#183, the patient was readmitted with diabetic ketoacidosis and acute liver failure. His clinical condition rapidly deteriorated, with worsening liver function tests (alkaline phosphatase 375 U/L, alanine transaminase 109 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 225 U/L, and bilirubin 5.3 mg/dL) suggesting fibrosing cholestatic hepatitis. He developed multi-organ failure, and the family elected for compassionate care. He died on POD#189.

Discussion

The introduction of DAA therapy has significantly improved outcomes in HCV NAT+ kidney transplantation.3 Multiple open-label trials have reported sustained virologic response rate of 100% at 12 weeks and excellent graft function well beyond 1 year posttransplant, without an elevated risk of rejection or development of de novo donor-specific antibodies compared with HCV NAT− kidneys.4, 5, 6, 7 Furthermore, studies of the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network database have shown no significant difference in 3-year and 5-year allograft survival, in the DAA era, between recipients of HCV+ kidneys and those receiving HCV– kidneys.8,9 Cure rates for transmitted HCV following a full course of first-line DAA therapy exceed 95%, with near-universal viral eradication after second- or third-line treatments.2,10 Fibrosing cholestatic hepatitis has been reported among HCV NAT+/– kidney recipients who did not have immediate access to DAA treatment posttransplant.11,12 However, those cases were rare and usually treatable after prompt initiation of DAA. Fatal HCV recurrence after successful completion of DAA in HCV NAT+/– kidney transplantation has not been reported.

Our institutional protocol for HCV+/– kidney transplant includes monitoring the patient’s liver chemistries from POD#1 and HCV NAT beginning on POD#3. Pan-genotypic DAA therapy is started once viremia is confirmed. The patient achieved end-of-treatment response after a 12-week course of DAA therapy. His liver function tests were all within normal limits during that period (Fig 2). HCV recurrence was confirmed approximately 10 weeks after the completion of DAA. Given the consistency of the pre- and posttreatment HCV genotype and the apparent absence of high-risk behaviors in the transplant recipient, the reoccurrence of viremia was more likely a relapse rather than reinfection. Our patient had a high pretreatment HCV viral load level, which is a negative predictor even in the DAA era. He did not have any prior history of bariatric surgery and was not on any antacid medication posttransplant. Resistance testing was not performed as it would not change our treatment plan (genotype 1). The patient faced some barriers to medication adherence posttransplant, completing the 12-week course of sofosbuvir/velpatasvir approximately 1 week later than scheduled. Although medication adherence is critical to treatment success, the SIMPLIFY study demonstrated that interruptions in HCV treatment of <7 days are unlikely to affect the sustained virologic response of 100% at 12 weeks.13 The patient’s multiple admissions for diabetic ketoacidosis and urosepsis, along with suboptimal kidney function and immunosuppressive drug levels, may also have increased the risk of HCV recurrence. His best creatinine level was 3.16 mg/dL (estimated glomerular filtration rate 23 mL/min/1.73 m2) 1 month posttransplant. Hence, the choice of a high KDPI, donation after circulatory death, NAT+ kidney to implant may have led to suboptimal kidney function. This kidney was transplanted before the KDPI policy change; the KDPI of the donor was 97%. HCV positivity was a risk factor for adverse allograft outcome in the pre-DAA era, but the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network’s recent policy change excluding HCV from KDPI calculation would have only reduced this donor’s KDPI to 85%.14

The diagnosis of fibrosing cholestatic hepatitis was made clinically, based on the presence of transaminitis, cholestasis with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia without biliary obstruction, marked HCV viremia, and the lack of alternative explanations, as with other cases reported in the literature.11,12 A biopsy was not performed due to his critical condition. Recipients and donors in earlier HCV NAT+/– studies were carefully selected. It is possible that contemporary recipients of an HCV NAT+ kidney may not experience the same favorable outcomes if donor or recipient characteristics are not optimized. This may be the cause of the unfavorable outcome in this case. The potential benefits of accepting an HCV NAT+ kidney, such as reduced waiting time and improved quality of life compared to remaining on dialysis, needs to be carefully weighed against the risk of altered immune responses that could facilitate the emergence of other viral infections (such as cytomegalovirus or polyomavirus) or of developing acute liver failure.

Conclusions

Fatal HCV transmission has not been reported, to our best knowledge, in HCV NAT+/– kidney transplant recipients in the DAA era. Elevated liver function tests after viral clearance should still prompt concern for recurrence. Careful patient and graft selection, along with vigilant monitoring for HCV recurrence, remain essential components in the management of HCV NAT+/– kidney transplantation.
