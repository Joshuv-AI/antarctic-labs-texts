Validation of the Bengali version of the Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS) pain scale: A tool for differentiating neuropathic and nociceptive pain
Authors: Shima Rani Sarkar, Md. Shamsudduha Tauhid, Salma Ahsan Khanam, Rafsana Rouf, Jheelam Biswas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pain management is crucial in treating chronic conditions, and screening for neuropathic pain is important due to its distinct management compared to nociceptive pain. Simple screening questionnaires play a key role in patient management and clinical research. This study aims to translate and adapt the English version of the Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS) pain scale into Bengali, followed by standard validation. The study was conducted in 2 phases. Four independent translators performed forward and backward translations of the English LANSS pain scale into Bengali. The Bengali version was finalized after expert committee reviews and cognitive debriefing. The final validation was carried out among 191 patients with chronic pain. In the last phase, the validity (construct, and discriminant validity) and reliability (interclass item-wise correlation coefficient) of the translated tool were evaluated. The Bengali version demonstrated a high reliability (α = 0.85 and rSB = 0.92). Exploratory factor and confirmatory factor analysis revealed a three-factor structure. ROC analysis showed the highest sensitivity (94.6%) and specificity (93.4%) at a cutoff value of 9.5. Additionally, the agreement between the clinical assessments and the Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale diagnosis indicated almost perfect agreement (κ = 0.84). The Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale exhibits high reliability and validity for assessing neuropathic pain in Bengali-speaking populations.

1. Introduction

Chronic pain, defined as pain lasting 3 months or more, is a complex and distressing condition and a major cause of disability among adults worldwide.[1,2] From a pathophysiological perspective, pain is classified as either nociceptive or neuropathic.[3] Among these, neuropathic pain is likely a major contributor to the global burden of chronic pain.[2] This type of pain results from direct damage to or disease of the somatosensory nervous system.[4] The prevalence of neuropathic pain varies depending on the method of identification, but studies have found that it affects approximately 1 in 10 adults over the age of 30.[5]

Pain management is essential in treating many chronic conditions. Traditional methods focused on addressing pain intensity, whereas recent approaches consider the underlying pathophysiological characteristics of pain to guide treatment choices.[6] Screening for neuropathic pain is important because its management differs from that of nociceptive pain[7] Neuropathic pain components significantly affect patients’ mood, function, and quality of life, highlighting the need to differentiate between neuropathic and non-neuropathic pain to choose the most appropriate intervention.[8] In clinical practice, diagnosis of neuropathic pain primarily relies on sensory signs and patient-reported symptoms.[8,9] Simple screening questionnaires are useful in both patient management and clinical research.[10]

Many screening tools have been developed to diagnose and assess neuropathic pain. Some of the most commonly used scales include the Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS), Self-Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs, douleur neuropathique 4, and painDETECT questionnaires.[9] Each tool has its strengths and weaknesses, and none offer complete diagnostic accuracy.[5] However, the LANSS has been recognized as one of the simplest and most useful tools for differentiating between neuropathic and nociceptive pain.[9] It contains 7 items: 5 for determining pain characteristics and 2 for clinical examinations. This tool is nearly consistent with clinical diagnoses, demonstrating a sensitivity of 83% and specificity of 87%.[11] The original English version of LANSS pain scale has been translated into numerous languages including Italian, Persian, Malayalam, Turkish, Spanish, Korean, Brazilian Portuguese, Greek, Pashto, Urdu, Arabic, Swedish, Portuguese, Chinese-Mandarin and Nepalese. All of these versions reported high reliability.[6–8,10,12–23]

There is limited information about the prevalence of neuropathic pain among Bangladeshi adults. However, one study reported that 63.9% of Bangladeshi patients suffered from neuropathy-related pain.[24] Additionally, no questionnaire, including the LANSS, is available in Bengali to assess neuropathic pain. As a result, the diagnosis of neuropathic pain currently relies on clinicians’ opinions, diagnostic tests, and imaging studies. While these methods are considered the gold standard in many studies, they are often costly and sometimes unavailable in lower-middle-income countries like Bangladesh.[9,24] Therefore, a simple screening tool could provide clinicians with a quick and affordable method to distinguish between neuropathic and nociceptive pain, aiding in the development of appropriate management plans.

This study aims to translate and culturally adapt the LANSS pain scale into Bengali while evaluating its reliability and validity among Bengali-speaking populations. Adapting and validating the LANSS in Bengali could help identify neuropathic pain more effectively and improve its management and treatment outcomes.

2. Methods

2.1. Instrument

The LANSS, developed by Bennett in 2001, is a clinical tool used to identify and evaluate neuropathic pain.[11] It consists of 5 questions assessing pain characteristics and 2 brief clinical examinations to test sensory abnormalities – allodynia and altered pain thresholds. The tool helps differentiate neuropathic pain from other types of pain, aiding in diagnosis and treatment planning. Each question is answered with a “yes” or “no” response and scored according to the scale. The total score ranges from 0 to 24. A score of ≥12 indicates that neuropathic mechanisms are likely contributing to the patient’s pain.

2.2. Translation and linguistic validation phase

2.2.1. Translation

The translation from English to Bengali followed the guidelines outlined by Beaton et al (2002).[25] Forward translation was independently conducted by 2 bilingual translators: one from a medical background (T1) who was familiar with the concept of the tool, and another from a non-medical background (T2) who was not informed about the tool’s objectives. The 2 forward translations were then reviewed and synthesized by a third, unbiased individual to produce a single reconciled version (Ts). This version was subsequently back-translated into English by 2 independent translators with non-medical backgrounds: BT1, who was aware of the conceptual basis of the tool, and BT2, who was not. An expert committee, comprising 5 pain specialists, 4 members from the research team, an English language expert, and a Bengali language expert, was formed to review and compare all translations with the original scale. After thorough evaluation and consensus among the committee members, the final Bengali version was prepared for pilot testing (Fig. 1).

2.2.2. Cognitive debriefing

Ten patients with chronic pain (duration ≥ 3 months) participated in cognitive debriefing. The study’s objective was explained to each participant, and they were questioned both before and after completing the questionnaire to identify any difficult or confusing terms. The researchers tested various wordings and explanations for the challenging terms until suitable replacements were found. The expert group then reviewed and approved the final version of the updated questionnaire, concluding that no further pilot testing was needed (Fig. 1).

2.3. The final validation phase

2.3.1. Setting and sample criteria

This phase of the study was conducted at Sher-E-Bangla Medical College Hospital from November 2024 to February 2025. Patients with chronic pain lasting ≥3 months, who either attended the outpatient clinic or were admitted to the inpatient ward of the internal medicine department during the study period, were invited to participate. Participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older, reported a pain intensity of ≥3 on a 10-point numeric rating scale, and had at least a primary level of education (grade 5 or higher). Patients were excluded if they had mixed pain, fibromyalgia, depression, substance abuse (alcohol or drugs), visual impairments, or were unable to understand or complete the questionnaire.

2.3.2. Sample size and sampling technique

Two separate studies reported that, for factor analysis, the required sample-to-item ratio is only 2:1 or higher.[26,27] Additionally, to detect a significant internal consistency, a minimum sample size of 60 to 63 is required.[27] Initially, we aimed for a sample-to-item ratio of 10:1 to determine the minimum sample size. Since there were 7 items in total, we considered 70 samples for each pain group. However, due to enthusiastic participation from our patients, the final sample size reached 191 (98 samples in each group), resulting in a sample-to-item ratio of 13.2:1. Sampling was done by consecutive sampling method.

2.3.3. Assessment of pain

Each patient was assessed independently by 2 pain specialists who reviewed their medical history, conducted clinical exams, and performed relevant tests to determine the type of pain. Neuropathic pain was identified through clinical findings and laboratory tests, including the detection of abnormal sensory perception, neuronal tissue damage in computed tomography scans or magnetic resonance imagings, and abnormal findings in nerve conduction studies. Nociceptive pain was diagnosed based on clinical and/or laboratory evidence of non-neuronal tissue damage and/or normal results from electrophysiological studies. Since there is no officially recognized “gold standard” pain scale for differentiating between these 2 types of pain, clinical and/or laboratory evidence was used as the “gold standard” to evaluate the effectiveness of the Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale in identifying neuropathic pain. The patient was then referred to a member of the research team who was blinded to the clinical diagnosis of pain. This investigator administered the Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale to the patient, reading each question aloud, word by word, without any modifications, and recorded the patient’s responses. Additionally, the investigator conducted 2 brief examinations (items 6 and 7) to determine the type of pain according to the LANSS pain scale (Fig. 2).

Pain assessment procedure.

2.3.4. Statistical analysis

Face validity was assessed during the standard translation process. Content validity was also evaluated through translation, back-translation, and expert committee review. Construct validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with Principal Component Analysis and Varimax rotation. Items with factor loadings below 0.30 were excluded.[28] To validate the factor structure derived from EFA, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was subsequently performed. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach alpha (α), with a cutoff of ≥0.50 considered acceptable.[29] Split-half reliability was assessed using the Spearman–Brown and Guttman coefficients. Convergent validity was examined by comparing patient-reported LANSS scores with clinician diagnosis using Cohen Kappa (κ) and the point-biserial correlation coefficient. Discriminant validity, including sensitivity and specificity, was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and ROC analysis. Comparisons of socio-demographic variables between patients with both pain types were made using independent t-tests and Fisher-exact test. A 2-sided P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 26.

2.4. Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the research (Approval no: SBMC/IRB/2024/2157; Date: September 12, 2024) was granted by the Ethical Review Committee of Sher-E-Bangla Medical College Hospital. Before participating in the study, all eligible individuals were asked to provide written informed consent. All data was kept anonymous, and no information or images used in the study could identify the participants.

3. Result

3.1. Linguistic validation

3.1.1. Face and content validity

The process of face validity was also built into the translation and adaptation method. The members of the expert committee and the naive respondents (uninformed translators) both agreed that the purpose of the tool was clear and it should differentiate between neuropathic and nociceptive pain. No significant language discrepancies were identified during the translation process. However, during the expert committee review, one minor issue was noted. The exact synonym for the term “Allodynia” does not exist in Bengali. Therefore, we opted for explanatory phrases rather than direct word-for-word translations. For “Allodynia”, we used “increased sensation of pain to non-painful stimuli.” As signs of neuropathic pain (Allodynia and Altered pin-prick threshold) were assessed by health professionals who are usually well-versed in English, we retained the original English terms alongside the Bengali translations.

3.1.2. Cognitive debriefing

During cognitive debriefing, all patients fully understood 6 items, while 8 patients (80%) required clarification for item 2. Since most people in Bangladesh have brown skin tones, the term “looking more red or pink” did not elicit accurate responses. Consequently, we rephrased it as “change in skin color such as more darkish hue or reddish looking.” However, the phrase is somewhat vague. To obtain more accurate results, the investigators conducted a clinical observation of skin color changes.

3.2. Final validity and reliability assessment

This stage included a total of 191 patients (141 men and 50 women) with a mean age of 53.3 ± 13.8 years. Among them, 93 patients had nociceptive pain, and 98 patients had neuropathic pain. A significant association was found between the type of pain and gender, primary diagnosis, and the site of the pain. Male patients (86.7%) were more likely to suffer from neuropathic pain. Neuropathic pain was significantly higher among patients with diabetic neuropathy (45.9%) and myelopathy (33.7%), while nociceptive pain was more common among cancer patients (72%). Patients with neuropathic pain predominantly experienced pain in their lower limbs and palms and soles, whereas nociceptive pain was most commonly reported in the back (31.2%) and abdomen (29%) (Table 1).

3.2.1. Construct validity

EFA using principal component analysis with varimax rotation was conducted to evaluate the construct validity of the final version. Based on eigenvalues, the Bengali version revealed a three-factor structure. Items 1 and 4 loaded onto Factor 1, representing patient-reported pain characteristics, while Items 3 and 5 loaded onto Factor 2, reflecting patient-observed symptoms of neuropathic pain. Together, Factors 1 and 2 aligned with the symptom domain of neuropathic pain as defined in the original LANSS scale. Item 2, 6, 7 was loaded into Factor 3, representing signs of neuropathic pain. Notably, Item 2 (autonomic symptoms) showed loading in Component 3, which represented clinical signs. All items under each factor demonstrated adequate factor loadings, ranging from 0.6 to 0.9 (Table 2).

CFA was subsequently performed to validate this three-factor model. The model specification included Item 1 (0.68), Item 3 (0.71), and Item 2 (0.15) on Factor 1; Item 4 (0.71) and Item 5 (0.92) on Factor 2; and Item 6 (0.97) and Item 7 (0.99) on Factor 3. Closer inspection of factor loadings revealed that Item 2 demonstrated a very weak loading (0.15) on its intended factor, suggesting it may not adequately represent the latent construct and may have contributed to the elevated root mean square error of approximation (Fig. 3).

3.2.2. Convergent validity

κ was conducted to assess agreement between patient-reported LANSS scores and clinician diagnosis of neuropathic pain. There was almost perfect agreement, κ = 0.84 (standard error = 0.03), Z = 11.75, P < .001. Also point-biserial correlation coefficient was used to assess the convergent validity between the LANSS total score and clinician diagnosis of neuropathic pain. Results showed a strong positive correlation, R = 0.88, P < .001, suggesting high convergent validity between the 2 measures.

3.2.3. Discriminant validity

To determine sensitivity and specificity, we compared the Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale with clinical assessments, considering the latter as the “gold standard.” The ROC curve for the Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale showed an area under the curve of 0.98, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.97–1.00 (P < .001) (Fig. 4). Using the original version’s cutoff value of ≥12, the sensitivity was 87.4%, and the specificity was 86.2% (Youden index = 0.862). However, the Bengali version achieved the highest sensitivity (94.6%) and specificity (93.4%) at a cutoff value of 9.5 (Youden index = 0.934) (Table 3).

3.3. Reliability analysis

3.3.1. Internal consistency

The Bengali version of LANSS pain scale demonstrated high internal consistency, with a α score of 0.85. Additionally, the α values for individual items ranged from 0.7 to 0.8, further indicating the high reliability of each question (Table 4).

3.3.2. Split-half reliability

It was assessed using the Spearman–Brown and Guttman coefficients. The correlation between the 2 halves was R = 0.857. The Spearman–Brown coefficient (rSB) was 0.92 (equal length), indicating excellent internal consistency. The Guttman Split-Half coefficient was 0.75, further supporting the reliability of the scale.

4. Discussion

This study assessed the validity and reliability of the Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale and found it to be highly reliable (α = 0.85, and rSB = 0.92) as a neuropathic pain assessment tool. This reliability is comparable to that of the original study conducted by Bennett (2001).[11] Additionally, the internal consistency of the Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale exceeds that of the Persian (0.64), Spanish (0.68), Brazilian Portuguese (0.67), Greek (0.67), Italian (0.76), and Portuguese (0.78) versions, as well as the Arabic (0.79) and Pashto (0.76) versions. It is also consistent with the values reported for the Turkish (0.84), Urdu (0.82), and Korean (0.81) versions.[6,8,10,12–22] Furthermore, each item in the questionnaire demonstrated internal consistency ranging from 0.7 to 0.8, which is comparable to the Korean version, indicating good internal consistency for individual items[14]

Both EFA and CFA of the Bengali version demonstrated a three-factor structure for observed symptoms of neuropathic pain (Items 3, 5), signs of neuropathic pain (Items 2, 6, 7), and pain characteristics (Items 1, 4). We did not find any previous validation studies exploring this three-factor model of this tool. However, these factors reflect the original structure of the LANSS questionnaire, where the items were categorized under 2 headings – symptoms and signs of neuropathic pain.[11]

Interestingly, in the Bengali version, Item 2 (autonomic symptoms) demonstrated a weak loading on its originally intended factor, “symptoms of neuropathic pain,” in the CFA. However, in the EFA, it loaded strongly onto the factor representing “signs of neuropathic pain.” This contrasts with the original classification, where it was grouped under the symptom domain.[11] This finding suggests partial structural consistency with the original version and indicates cultural and linguistic variations in symptom perception. Item 2 of the LANSS pain scale describes skin vasomotor changes (such as changes in skin color) as patient-reported autonomic symptoms of neuropathy. However, one study reported that physicians sometimes also notice this symptom, suggesting that it can be considered a clinical sign as well.[30] Additionally, due to the natural skin tone of Bengali population, some individuals may have difficulty noticing subtle skin color changes in the painful area. This factor was also considered during the linguistic validation. The original scale was developed for a white-skinned population, where skin vasomotor changes (such as changes in color to red or pink) are more prominent.[11] However, in the South Asian region, the natural skin tone ranges from dark to dark brown, so such color changes may not be as noticeable or could be misinterpreted by patients. Therefore, we followed the approach from the Korean version, where the description of skin color changes was modified to suit their population.[14] We rephrased it as “changes in the skin color,” with examples such as “more darkish hue or reddish looking,” to better align with the understanding of our population.

Convergent validity analysis demonstrated an almost perfect agreement between clinical diagnosis and assessments based on the LANSS pain scale, indicating a strong association between clinician-based evaluation and patient-reported outcomes.[31,32] Furthermore, the strong positive correlation between clinical diagnosis and LANSS scores reinforces the scale’s reliability and utility as a valid screening tool for identifying neuropathic pain.

Regarding discriminative validity, the area under the curve of the Bengali version was 0.986, considering the clinical diagnosis as the “gold standard.” Additionally, ROC curve analysis showed that the sensitivity and specificity of the Bengali version were 87.4% and 86.2%, respectively, using the original version’s cutoff value of ≥12. This is comparable to the original scale’s sensitivity (83%) and specificity (87%).[11] The Bengali version also exhibits sensitivity and specificity similar to other translated versions, including Arabic (88.12%, 76.19%), Malayalam (89.58%, 84.91%), Brazilian Portuguese (89%, 74%), Korean (72.6%, 98%), Turkish (89.9%, 94.2%), and Greek (82.76%, 95.24%).[7,10,12,14–16,19]

However, the Bengali version demonstrated the highest sensitivity (94.6%) and specificity (93.4%) at a cutoff point of 9.5 (Youden index = 0.934). These findings suggest that the Bengali version of the LANSS Pain Scale is highly effective in identifying neuropathic pain, with optimal sensitivity and specificity at a lower cutoff point (9.5) than the original version’s threshold (≥12).[11] This indicates that a slightly lower cutoff may enhance diagnostic accuracy in the Bengali-speaking population. The comparison with the Korean version, which demonstrated the highest sensitivity and specificity at an even lower cutoff of 7, and the Greek and Portuguese versions, which had higher cutoff values of 10.5 and 14, respectively, suggests that linguistic and cultural factors may influence the optimal cutoff values across different populations.[14,16,21]

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to translate and validate the Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale in a clinical setting. However, this study has some limitations. Firstly, the sample size was small, and the study was conducted in a single center, which limits its generalizability. Secondly, as measurements were taken at a single time point, test-retest reliability could not be assessed. Additionally, since the LANSS scale is a self-reported instrument rated solely by the patient, inter-rater reliability could not be evaluated.

5. Conclusion

The Bengali version of the LANSS pain scale exhibits high reliability and validity for assessing neuropathic pain in Bengali-speaking populations. With excellent sensitivity, specificity, and near-perfect concordance with clinical assessments, this translated tool provides an effective and reliable method for diagnosing neuropathic pain, enhancing both clinical practice and research in chronic pain management.
