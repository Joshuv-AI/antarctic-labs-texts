Prevalence of sexually transmitted infections among persons aged 15–24 in the Republic of Korea: A retrospective population-based descriptive study
Authors: Jinhee Seo, Minji Han, Sangrak Bae, Sooyoun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDespite rising sexually transmitted infection (STI) incidence globally, evidence on the burden among adolescents remains limited. This study examined trends in the prevalence and medical costs of STIs among Korean youth aged 15–24 from 2010 to 2023, with a focus on age- and gender-specific disparities.Materials and MethodsUsing national health insurance claims data from the HIRA (Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service), we identified STI cases by ICD-10 (10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases) codes and calculated annual age-standardized and age-specific prevalence rates based on census data. Sex-stratified analyses were performed, and medical expenditures were estimated and converted to US dollars.ResultsOver 2010–2023, STI prevalence among Korean youth rose by 22.9%, peaking in 2019. Rates were consistently higher in the 20–24 group than in the 15–19 group, with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), genital warts, and mycoplasma showing four- to eight-fold differences. Chlamydia (21.8%) and gonorrhea (18.5%) accounted for the largest share of youth cases. While HIV and nongonococcal urethritis were predominantly male, mycoplasma, chlamydia, and genital herpes were more common in females. Total medical costs reached US$121.9 million, with 77.5% incurred by males, largely driven by nongonococcal urethritis.ConclusionsSTI prevalence among Korean youth has risen steadily despite a shrinking youth population, with substantial gender and age disparities in both disease patterns and medical costs. These findings highlight the urgent need for comprehensive, youth-centered sexual health education and improved access to confidential sexual and reproductive health services in Korea.Keywords: Adolescent, Health expenditures, Prevalence, Sexually transmitted infections, South Korea

INTRODUCTION

Adolescence and young adulthood are transitional life stages characterized by exploration of identity, social relationships, and sexuality. During this period, the risk of acquiring sexually transmitted infections (STIs) is particularly high due to both biological susceptibility and behavioral vulnerability [1]. Globally, STIs remain a major public health challenge among youth, contributing to adverse reproductive health outcomes such as infertility, pelvic inflammatory disease, chronic pain, and increased risk of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) acquisition [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that more than one million STIs are acquired worldwide every day, with a disproportionate burden falling on individuals under the age of 25 [2].

In Korea, patterns of sexual behavior among adolescents and young adults have undergone rapid changes over the past two decades, including earlier initiation of sexual activity, wider use of digital platforms for dating, and inconsistent condom use [3]. Despite these shifts, youth-targeted sexual health policies and services remain underdeveloped. Sex education in schools often emphasizes abstinence or biological facts, while access to confidential, youth-friendly sexual health services is limited. As a result, many young people face barriers to timely diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of STIs, increasing the risk of long-term complications and continued transmission [4].

Previous research on STIs in Korea has largely focused on the adult population, leaving a critical knowledge gap regarding the specific burden among youth [5,6,7,8]. National-level surveillance has not sufficiently addressed age- and gender-specific patterns in STI prevalence, nor the associated medical and economic costs. Understanding these trends is essential for guiding targeted prevention strategies and optimizing the allocation of limited healthcare resources.

The present study aims to fill this gap by examining national claims data from 2010 to 2023 to assess the prevalence and medical costs of STIs among individuals aged 15–24 in Korea. By analyzing trends across age and gender, this study provides evidence to inform more effective, youth-centered public health interventions that can reduce the growing burden of STIs and protect the sexual and reproductive health of future generations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Data source

We used data from the Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service (HIRA) big data system spanning 2010–2023. They were used for their comprehensive coverage of national health insurance claims, enabling reliable identification of STI cases and associated costs. Population denominators were derived from the resident registration central population dataset provided by the Korean National Statistical Office.

2. Study population and case definition

This study is a retrospective population-based descriptive study. We investigated individuals aged 15–24, following the “youth” definition of the WHO and ILO (International Labour Organization). Individuals who were diagnosed with STIs were included according to ICD-10 (10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases) codes in the HIRA database (Supplementary Table 1). The STIs analyzed were chlamydia, gonorrhea, genital herpes, mycoplasma infection, genital warts, syphilis, trichomoniasis, HIV, pubic lice, scabies, and nongonococcal urethritis (NGU). Patients were included if STI codes appeared as either a primary or secondary diagnosis during the observation period (2010–2023). As defined in the HIRA open data specification, duplicate encounters for the same diagnosis within a calendar year are excluded from the annual patient count. Accordingly, each individual is counted once per diagnosis per year, yielding patient-based prevalence estimates in this study. The total number of cases reflects the sum of all individuals diagnosed with an STI each year from 2010 to 2023.

3. Statistical analysis

Annual age-standardized prevalence was calculated as the number of identified patients per 100,000 population, adjusted for changes in age distribution using the direct standardization method. Age-specific prevalence rates were first calculated for two age groups (15–19 and 20–24 years), and then standardized using the 2010 Korean population as the reference. Age-standardized prevalence was estimated separately for men, women, and the total population. Population data for each corresponding year (2010–2023) were obtained from the National Statistical Office.

Age-specific prevalence was assessed. Age groups were divided into two groups of 15–19 and 20–24. The prevalence was calculated by comparing the number of cases in each group to the population size of that age group for each year. The highest and lowest prevalence rates by age group were also identified. To examine the diseases especially susceptible to adolescents, percentage share of 15 to 24 years old among all STI patients were calculated annually. Gender differences were also measured. Ratio of average prevalence of male and female were calculated. Diseases showing high gender differences were shown.

Medical costs of patients with STIs were calculated using the ‘total amount of medical care benefit cost’ variable from the HIRA database. This variable represents the confirmed total medical expenditure, including consultation, diagnostic tests, imaging and radiotherapy, medication and dispensing, injection and anesthesia, and procedure and surgery. Patients with zero treatment cost were excluded, as such cases typically represent non-treatment visits (e.g., administrative purposes, non-reimbursable services, or canceled claims) rather than actual medical care. Costs were converted to US dollars using the exchange rate on July 15, 2025 (1 KRW=0.00072 USD), based on IMF (International Monetary Fund) data at the time of writing. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp.), with the assistance of Excel Microsoft 365 (Microsoft Corp.) and R version 4.4.1.

4. Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University (approval number: E2504/003-004). The requirement for informed consent was waived as the study used publicly available, de-identified data.

RESULTS

1. Annual number of patients and prevalence in youth (age 15–24)

Total STI patients increased from 719,068 in 2010 to 855,841 in 2023, marking a 19.0% increase over the 13-year period. This corresponds to an average annual increase of approximately 10,521 patients. During the same period, however, the total adolescent population declined by 21.9%, from 6,647,144 to 5,190,934.

From 2010 to 2023, the age-standardized prevalence of STIs showed a continuous upward trend (Fig. 1A). In 2010, the prevalence was 1,221 per 100,000 population and gradually increased, peaking at approximately 1,686 per 100,000 in 2019. Although a slight decline was observed thereafter, the rate remained above 1,500 per 100,000 through 2023, indicating a substantial long-term increase. Over the 13-year period, the prevalence increased by an average of 21.5 per 100,000 population annually, resulting in a total increase of 22.9%. During 2010–2023, NGU showed the highest average prevalence among STIs.

In 2023, of the total 855,841 patients, 68.7% (588,030) were male and 31.3% (267,811) were female. Among males, STI patients increased by 22.9%, from 478,396 in 2010 to 588,030 in 2023. The prevalence also rose from 1,515 to 1,975 per 100,000, showing a consistent upward trend with a 30.4% overall increase, except for a slight decline between 2021 and 2023. The prevalence peaked in 2021, reaching 2,134 per 100,000. On average, NGU had the highest prevalence from 2010 to 2023 (Fig. 1B).

Among females, the number of patients slightly increased by 11.3%, from 240,672 to 267,811. Prevalence also increased from 891 to 981 per 100,000, with a peak in 2017 (1,262 per 100,000) followed by a moderate downward trend. The overall increase over the 13-year period was 10.1%. Genital herpes exhibited the highest average prevalence of all STIs between 2010 and 2023 (Fig. 1C).

4. Sex differences in STI burden: prevalence ratios and disease patterns

Analysis of average STI prevalence by sex revealed notable differences across specific diseases. Certain infections were significantly more common in males (Table 2). HIV showed the most pronounced disparity, with a male-to-female prevalence ratio of 19.71, indicating that men were nearly 20 times more likely to be diagnosed with HIV than women. NGU also exhibited a strong male predominance, with a ratio of 7.64, followed by pediculosis (3.11), gonorrhoeae (1.99), and scabies (1.56).

5. STI-related medical cost

From 2010 to 2023, the total estimated medical cost associated with STIs among individuals aged 15–24 reached approximately US$121.9 million. Of this total, US$94.5 million (77.5%) was incurred by males and US$27.8 million (22.8%) by females, indicating a substantial gender difference in cost burden. Overall, the total medical cost increased steadily over the study period, reaching a peak around 2020 before showing a slight decline thereafter (Fig. 4A).

When broken down by infection type, NGU accounted for the largest portion of overall expenditures, with a cumulative cost of approximately US$5.59 million, of which 92.4% was incurred by males and 7.6% by females. This was followed by HIV-related costs, which totaled around US$2.22 million, with 93.8% attributed to males and 6.2% to females.

Among males, NGU was the leading cause of STI-related medical expenditure, amounting to about US$5.17 million, which represents 60.7% of the total male STI-related cost (Fig. 4B). Among females, genital warts accounted for the highest cumulative expenditure at approximately US$0.53 million, contributing 24.2% of the total STI-related cost for females (Fig. 4C).

DISCUSSION

This study provides a comprehensive overview of trends and characteristics in STI prevalence and economic burden among Korean youth (aged 15–24) from 2010 to 2023. Despite a declining youth population, STI rates rose steadily, indicating a true increase in transmission rather than demographic shifts. This rise appears linked to behavioral and technological changes—earlier sexual debut, more casual or multiple partnerships, inconsistent condom use, and widespread adoption of dating apps. For instance, a Korean survey reported that 43.7% of young adults used condoms intermittently and 15% rarely did [9,10].

STI prevalence has shown a declining trend from 2020 to 2023 across all groups (total, male, and female), which appears to have been influenced by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Several studies have reported decreases in non–COVID-19 diseases during the epidemic [11,12]. Reduced interpersonal contact due to social distancing is likely a major contributing factor, as it is directly related to the transmission of STIs. In particular, among youths aged 15–24, school closures and the shift to remote learning limited opportunities to meet sexual partners and engage in risky sexual behaviors.

The burden of STIs was higher in males than in females: in 2023, of the total patients, 68.7% were male and 31.3% were female. Cases rose 22.9% among males but only 11.3% among females, and males accounted for 77.5% of total STI-related healthcare costs. Biological susceptibility, symptom-driven care seeking, and testing practices likely contributed to this discrepancy. This trend contrasts with the widely accepted notion that females are generally more susceptible to STIs than males. This greater susceptibility is largely attributable to female urogenital anatomy: the vaginal mucosa has a much larger surface area and remains exposed for a longer duration, thereby facilitating pathogen transmission [13]. The divergent trend may be explained by the specific circumstances of adolescence. Compared with females, adolescent males tend to initiate sexual activity at an earlier age [14], and report a higher prevalence of having ever had sexual intercourse, as well as a greater proportion with multiple sexual partners (58% vs. 41.4%) [15].

Most common STIs were NGU in males and genital herpes in females. NGU is well recognized as the most common STI among males [16]. Its primary cause is Chlamydia trachomatis, which represents the most prevalent bacterial STI worldwide [2]. Genital herpes is also known as one of the most common STIs in female [13].

The largest sex disparities were observed in HIV (male-dominant; 20-fold) and mycoplasma infections (female-dominant; 16-fold).

• Males showed distinctly higher prevalence of HIV, NGU, and pediculosis. HIV disproportionately affects MSM (men who have sex with men)—now approximately 60% of new HIV cases in Korea, particularly among youth—with anal intercourse carrying a significantly higher transmission risk [17]. NGU often involves clear symptoms—such as burning, discharge, and pruritus—leading to diagnosis in ~90% of male cases, while many female infections remain undetected [18]. Pubic lice, the cause of pediculosis, preferentially inhabit coarse hair [19]. The broader distribution of coarse pubic hair in males may help explain their higher prevalence of infestation.• Females showed distinctly higher prevalence of mycoplasma, chlamydia, and genital herpes. Although mycoplasma and chlamydia infections occur in both sexes, male cases are most often diagnosed under the category of NGU. Consequently, the prevalence of mycoplasma and chlamydia infections tends to appear higher in females. In both infections, females are more likely than males to have asymptomatic infections [20,21]. These often remain undiagnosed and untreated, allowing infections to persist for longer periods. As a result, population-based surveys tend to show higher prevalence in females. Genital herpes is primarily caused by HSV-2, which is transmitted more efficiently from males to females (11%–17%) than from females to males (3%–4%) [22]. This imbalance may contribute to the observed sex difference in prevalence.

• Males showed distinctly higher prevalence of HIV, NGU, and pediculosis. HIV disproportionately affects MSM (men who have sex with men)—now approximately 60% of new HIV cases in Korea, particularly among youth—with anal intercourse carrying a significantly higher transmission risk [17]. NGU often involves clear symptoms—such as burning, discharge, and pruritus—leading to diagnosis in ~90% of male cases, while many female infections remain undetected [18]. Pubic lice, the cause of pediculosis, preferentially inhabit coarse hair [19]. The broader distribution of coarse pubic hair in males may help explain their higher prevalence of infestation.

• Females showed distinctly higher prevalence of mycoplasma, chlamydia, and genital herpes. Although mycoplasma and chlamydia infections occur in both sexes, male cases are most often diagnosed under the category of NGU. Consequently, the prevalence of mycoplasma and chlamydia infections tends to appear higher in females. In both infections, females are more likely than males to have asymptomatic infections [20,21]. These often remain undiagnosed and untreated, allowing infections to persist for longer periods. As a result, population-based surveys tend to show higher prevalence in females. Genital herpes is primarily caused by HSV-2, which is transmitted more efficiently from males to females (11%–17%) than from females to males (3%–4%) [22]. This imbalance may contribute to the observed sex difference in prevalence.

Age-stratified analysis revealed that youth aged 20–24 had significantly higher STI prevalence than those aged 15–19, particularly for HIV, genital warts, and mycoplasma, which demonstrated 4- to 8-fold differences. This likely reflects greater sexual activity, diminished parental supervision, and lower healthcare engagement among older youth. Consequently, early intervention for adolescents aged 15–19 is crucial to establish safer sexual practices.

Certain infections disproportionately affected 15 to 24 years old: although they represented only 9.9% of all STI cases in 2023, they accounted for 21.8% of chlamydia, 18.5% of gonorrhea, and 13.6% of mycoplasma cases. Biologically, adolescent females are more susceptible to these infections due to cervical ectopy—exposure of sensitive columnar epithelium in the immature cervix—which increases vulnerability to C. trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Mycoplasma genitalium infections [23,24].

NGU was the leading driver of medical costs (US $48.7 million), followed by HIV-related care (US $35.3 million). NGU often involves dysuria and discharge, necessitating antibiotics, follow-up, and repeat treatments in persistent cases [25]. The high incidence and recurrence—particularly among sexually active men—result in substantial healthcare utilization [18].

Despite expanded STI surveillance and school-based sex education, our findings suggest that existing efforts are insufficiently youth-targeted. Structural barriers—such as limited access to youth-friendly clinics and stigma surrounding sexual health—continue to undermine prevention.

Sex education should be more comprehensive, with greater emphasis on STIs. In Korea, the limited annual hours allocated to sex education are primarily devoted to topics such as sexual violence, prostitution prevention, and drug abuse, leaving little room for STI-related content. As a result, many students enter adulthood without adequate knowledge of what STIs are or how to prevent them. The higher prevalence of STIs among individuals aged 20–24 compared with those aged 15–19 may, in part, reflect this gap. Beyond STIs, current public school curricula also lack sufficient coverage of essential topics such as consent, contraception, genital biology, and gender sensitivity. A renewed curriculum with expanded instructional time is therefore needed to provide truly comprehensive sex education. Evidence from other countries also demonstrates the effectiveness of comprehensive sex education. For example, the programs in the United States [26] and the Netherlands [27,28] linked to delayed sexual initiation, higher contraceptive use, and lower rates of adolescent pregnancy and STIs.

Greater investment in adolescent-centered care—such as confidential clinics, peer-led outreach, and age-tailored surveillance—will be critical to reversing STI trends and protecting long-term reproductive health.

This study has several limitations inherent to the use of national insurance claims data. First, the prevalence estimates may be underestimated because asymptomatic or untested individuals were not captured in the claims records, which may also reflect differences in healthcare access across populations. Second, diagnostic miscoding of STIs may have occurred. Since HIRA claims data are based on finalized medical billing records, it could include some suspected or provisional diagnoses, potentially leading to minor misclassification bias. Despite these limitations, the use of comprehensive nationwide data allows for robust estimation of patient-based STI prevalence trends over time.

CONCLUSIONS

This study highlights the growing burden of STIs among Korean youth aged 15–24. STI prevalence and related medical costs have steadily increased over the past decade, with notable age and gender disparities. The findings emphasize the need for more effective, youth-centered interventions—such as comprehensive sex education and improved access to sexual health services—to reduce infection rates and protect adolescent well-being.
