Rational emotive behavior therapy for preventing smoking initiation among school children: Addressing peer influence and social susceptibility
Authors: Sari Wardani Simarmata, Azizah Batubara, Dony Darma Sagita, Erwita Ika Violina, Akhmad Syaikhu, Juliana Simangunsong
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We are responding to the results of a recent study on secondhand smoke (SHS) exposure among schoolchildren in Jeddah during the COVID-19 pandemic. The study found that while most children did not smoke, 14.1% had tried smoking, 3.4% were active smokers, and 4.7% stated they would likely smoke if offered a cigarette by a close friend.1 These findings underscore children's vulnerability to social influences in initiating smoking, a serious issue that needs to be addressed systematically through counseling-based approaches. One relevant approach is rational emotive behavioral therapy (REBT), a psychological intervention that emphasizes eliminating and restructuring irrational thoughts that drive risky behavior.2 In the context of smoking, children often harbor irrational beliefs such as, “I have to smoke to be accepted by my friends” or “Smoking makes me look more mature.” These thoughts contribute to their vulnerability to social pressures. Through REBT, counselors can help children replace these beliefs with rational thoughts such as, “Social acceptance is not dependent on smoking” or “Smoking is detrimental to my health and future”.3

Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of REBT in reducing smoking tendencies in adolescents. Counseling programs significantly reduced weekly cigarette consumption among male experimental smokers.4 For example, in a quasi-experimental study, REBT-based group counseling was found to effectively reduce smoking behavior in junior high school students.5 Carillo et, al also showed that REBT-based group counseling services successfully reduced smoking behavior in junior high school students, with more than 75% of participants decreasing the number of cigarettes they smoked. This is consistent with international research showing that cognitive-behavioral approaches, including REBT, can increase adolescents’ resistance to peer pressure.6

However, global studies indicate that exposure to SHS and peer influence are key factors that increase children's future smoking intentions.7 Therefore, the REBT approach, which focuses on cognitive reconstruction, not only helps children reject peer pressure but also increases their self-awareness of the dangers of smoking. Furthermore, REBT can be integrated with school guidance and counseling programs, public health campaigns, and family support. In the school context, school counselors or guidance counselors can conduct small group REBT sessions, use pressure techniques to challenge irrational beliefs, role play smoking refusal, and assign homework to strengthen rational beliefs.8,9 At the family level, parents need to be equipped with skills to recognize signs of vulnerability in children and strengthen assertive communication.

Therefore, implementing REBT as part of a prevention strategy can be a practical and evidence-based solution to reduce smoking initiation rates in children. This approach aligns with research findings highlighting the critical importance of protecting children from social influences that encourage smoking behavior. We recommend that REBT-based counseling programs be integrated into school guidance and counseling services and strengthened through integrated education and public health policies.
