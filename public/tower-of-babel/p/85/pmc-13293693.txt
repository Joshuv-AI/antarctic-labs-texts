In vitro analysis of the anticancer and antimicrobial potentials and phytochemical profiles of leaf extracts from five Vachellia species
Authors: Garland Kgosi More, Chimdi Mang Kalu, Yvan Anderson Ngandjui Tchangoue, Memory Tekere, Titus Alfred Makudali Msagat, Ebere Lovelyn Udeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prevalence of antimicrobial resistance and cancer is on the rise, and these ailments create difficulties in therapeutic management and patient compliance. Hence, this necessitates the discovery of novel, less toxic, bioactive compounds from natural sources. This study aimed to investigate the phytochemical composition of leaf extracts from Vachellia karroo, Vachellia kosiensis, Vachellia Farnesiana, Vachellia sieberiana, and Vachellia xanthophloea using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) and Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS), as well as their cytotoxic activity and antimicrobial properties. Cytotoxicity was assessed in human breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7) and colorectal carcinoma (HCT116) cell lines, as well as in normal human lung fibroblast cells (MRC5), to determine selectivity. Antimicrobial activity was evaluated against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans. The FTIR analysis indicated the presence of functional groups representing phenols, carboxylic groups, hydroxy groups, alcohols and benzene rings. GC–MS was performed on five species of Vachellia and confirmed 51 volatile compounds, including phytol, germanicol, and supraene. Among all the extracts, V. karroo and V. xanthophloea presented the most cytotoxic activity with IC₅₀ = 9.61 ± 1.00 and 9.82 ± 0.96 μg/mL against the MCF-7 cells and 3.51 ± 1.05 and 10.20 ± 0.96 μg/mL against the HCT116 cells. In addition, all the extracts show lower cytotoxicity to MRC-5 normal cells (IC₅₀ > 20 μg/mL), thus indicating good selectivity with camptothecin used as a positive control. V. karroo showed cytotoxic activity with IC₅₀ values of 9.61 ± 1.00 and 3.51 ± 1.05 μg/mL against MCF-7 and HCT116 cells, while camptothecin exhibited IC₅₀ values of 5.52 ± 0.70, 6.52 ± 0.76 and 4.60 ± 0.56 μg/mL against MRC-5, MCF-7 and HCT116 cells. The extracts showed IC₅₀ values of >20 μg/mL against MRC-5 cells, suggesting non-toxicity. As mentioned earlier, the extracts also showed a great range of antibacterial activity against S. aureus, E. coli and C. albicans. V. karroo showed minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 1.25, 2.50 and 5.00 mg/mL against S. aureus, E. coli and C. albicans, respectively and V. kosiensis with MIC values of 0.31, 1.25 and > 10.00, respectively. This study revealed the presence of several diverse and significant phytochemicals in the leaves. The bioactivities and low toxicity towards normal cells are indicative of the potential for cancer and associated bacterial co-infection treatment.

Introduction

Cancer and infectious diseases are still among the biggest global health challenges with considerable morbidity, death and socio-economic effects worldwide. The rising burden of malignancies and the increasing rates of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) have also burdened the healthcare systems, highlighting the vital need for new therapeutic solutions (Ahmed et al., 2024). Cancer is characterized by unregulated cell division, genetic instability and metastatic spread, leading to progressive tissue damage and organ failure. Lung and colorectal cancers are the two leading malignancies worldwide, while breast cancer is the third most common malignancy worldwide, with increasing incidence rates in developed and emerging countries (Xiong et al., 2025). Breast cancer is increasing in developed and developing countries. Invasive cancers such as prostate, colorectal, lung cancers, melanoma and non-Hodgkin lymphoma are major contributors to cancer-related mortality and the public health burden in South Africa (Pizzato et al., 2024). Despite the major advances in chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy and targeted therapies, the treatment of cancer is still hampered by the development of drug resistance, toxicity, tumour heterogeneity and low selectivity towards malignant cells.

To make matters worse, the complex interaction between microbial infections and cancer growth is becoming increasingly apparent. Opportunistic infections are most common in cancer patients due to immunological dysregulation caused by the disease and therapy (Yusuf et al., 2023). Evidence of microbial co-infection and superinfection is rising. These can contribute to worse survival in cancer patients (Kubeček et al., 2021; Ntim et al., 2025). Certain pathogenic bacteria have been found to play a direct role in the development of cancer and tumour progression. There is a significant link between Helicobacter pylori infection and gastric adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, Escherichia coli is implicated in colorectal cancer, stemming from both genotoxic and inflammatory processes. Microorganisms resembling biofilms are proposed to contribute to cancer by triggering chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and evasion of the immune system (Montanari et al., 2025). The study on cancer patients co-infected with COVID-19 found a significant prevalence of clinical Streptococcus pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus, with increased mortality (Gudiol et al., 2021). Likewise, Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2023) showed that Salmonella infection has been associated with carcinogenesis in colorectal cancer when it modifies the acetylation of numerous key regulatory proteins, such as the cell division cycle 42 (CDC42), hence leading to activation of oncogenic signalling pathways. Together, this information demonstrates the interplay of microbial infection with cancer and cancer biology and thus points to the need for developing targeted therapies and integrated clinical care and the dependence of therapeutic approaches on tumour and pathogenic microorganisms in cancer.

In cancer and infectious disorders, natural compounds of medicinal plants are irreplaceable. Since time immemorial, plants have been a source of structurally diverse bioactive molecules that possess exceptional pharmacological properties and have made a considerable contribution to the current drug discovery and development (Davis and Choisy, 2024). Phytochemicals exhibiting significant biological activity can be used for cytotoxic activity and antibacterial objectives, and there is a great deal of them. The phytochemicals are flavonoids, terpenoids, tannins, lignans, alkaloids, phenolic acids and essential oils (Riaz et al., 2018). These secondary metabolites have a wide range of pharmacological actions such as antibacterial, antifungal, antiprotozoal, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, immunomodulatory, neuroprotective and cytotoxic effects (Batiha et al., 2020). Importantly, many cytotoxic phytochemicals induce anticancer effects through the induction of apoptosis, interference with mitochondrial integrity, modulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibition of cell proliferation, and interference with critical signalling cascades involved in cell survival and metastasis (Al-Oqail et al., 2021). The medicinal plants are also preferred due to their relative safety, chemical diversity, easy availability and long history of ethnopharmacological use, making them possible candidates for the discovery of novel therapeutic agents against multidrug-resistant pathogens and refractory cancers (van Wyk and Prinsloo, 2020).

The species of the genus Vachellia have been explored scientifically in an increasing way due to their rich phytochemical makeup and wide variety of pharmacological effects. Our group has previously demonstrated significant antioxidant potential of five Vachellia species (V. karroo, V. kosiensis, V. sieberiana, V. tortilis and V. xanthophloea) when tested using DPPH radical scavenging, ABTS+ radical cation decolourization, and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assays (More et al., 2021a). The metabolomic profiling was performed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy and ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-qTOF-MS). Several physiologically important chemicals were identified, such as epigallocatechin, methyl gallate and quercetin. These chemicals are well known for their antioxidant, antiproliferative and antibacterial activities and suggest that Vachellia species may have enormous therapeutic potential against cancer and infectious diseases.

In addition to the antioxidant activities, several species of Vachellia have shown considerable antifungal, cytotoxic and anti-inflammatory effects in previous studies. For example, V. karroo and V. xanthophloea showed high inhibition activity against many Candida species, including C. glabrata, C. auris, C. tropicalis and C. parapsilosis. Notably, V. kosiensis showed promising antifungal efficacy against C. tropicalis and C. parapsilosis (More et al., 2021b). These findings prompted the present work to evaluate the antibacterial and cytotoxic properties of five selected Vachellia species against clinically important pathogens and cancer cell lines. The antimicrobial study was carried out on the bacteria Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli and the fungus Candida albicans, because of their proven role in inflammation-mediated carcinogenesis, immunological regulation, therapeutic problems and long-term stay in cancer patients. The cytotoxicity activity was further tested on human breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7) and human colorectal carcinoma (HCT-116) cell lines, and the cytotoxicity on normal human fibroblast cells (MRC-5) was evaluated to know the selectivity and therapeutic importance of the extract.

To understand the mechanisms of the observed biological activities, the induction of apoptosis and cell cycle changes in cancer cells was also investigated computationally. Cytotoxicity and antibacterial activity were determined by colourimetric MTT assay and serial dilution-based minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay using resazurin, respectively. The advanced phytochemical characterisation was achieved using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy coupled with attenuated total reflectance (FTIR-ATR) and comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC × GC–MS). Moreover, in silico molecular studies were also performed to predict the binding efficacy and likely interactions of selected phytochemicals against some important molecular targets linked with cancer progression. The positive control was camptothecin, a quinoline alkaloid isolated from Camptotheca acuminata, known for its cytotoxic effects on MCF-7 and HCT-116 cell lines and its clinically validated mechanism of action, which involves DNA topoisomerase I inhibition, DNA damage, and apoptosis activation (Pristov and Ghannoum, 2019).

The present work is important because it unites in one pharmacological base cytotoxic and antibacterial studies. Although there is a wealth of studies on the therapeutic potential of medicinal plants, few comparative studies have been conducted to explore the correlation between the phytochemical profile, antibacterial activity, and anticancer activity of Vachellia species. The synthesis of plant-derived drugs with dual biofunctional activities may be of tremendous clinical utility, considering the vulnerability of cancer patients to opportunistic microbial infections (Zheng et al., 2022). Such multimodal therapy might also lessen the microbial issues and tackle the tumour growth simultaneously. Thus, this study provides a substantial background for future bioassay-guided isolation, mechanistic and translational studies for the development of novel phytotherapeutic compounds against cancer and antibiotic resistance.

Materials and methods

Plant material collection and preparation

Five Vachellia species, namely V. sieberiana, V. farnesiana, V. karroo, V. kosiensis and V. xanthophloea, were collected into paper bags from the campus of the University of Pretoria and immediately taken to the laboratory to be dried at room temperature. Plant materials were identified, and voucher specimens were deposited in the H. G. W. J. Schweickerdt Herbarium (PRU), University of Pretoria.

Extraction method for bioassays

The leaves were dried at ambient temperature and ground into a fine powder using a Cutting Mill (FRITSCH PULVERISETTE 19 Universal, Idar-Oberstein, Germany). A total of fifty grams (50 g) of the powdered plant material was extracted with 500 mL of 80% ethanol-water mixture. The samples underwent centrifugation at 4000 rpm and were filtered through the Buchi® filtration system (Sigma-Aldrich®, Darmstadt, Germany). To concentrate the extracts, a Stuart® rotary evaporator (IKA®, Munich, Germany) was employed. Additionally, the extracted samples were freeze-dried until completely dry. The extracts were diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for bioassays. The final concentration of DMSO used in the assays was 0.01%.

Extraction method for GC–MS analysis

A small quantity (12 mg) of the dried raw material from each of the five Vachellia species was extracted with 0.9 mL of methanol, vortexed thoroughly, and filtered through a 0.5 μm syringe filter. The filtrate was transferred into autosampler vials for analysis.

In vitro cytotoxicity assays

The MTT (3- [4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2, 5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) cell proliferation assay involves the conversion of MTT into formazan crystals by living cells. The reduction of MTT to formazan by mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase enzyme activity is the hallmark of measuring cell viability or its death. The assay was conducted following a slightly modified version of the method described by (Asong et al., 2019). The MCF-7, HCT116 and MRC5 cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in 5.0% CO2 saturation to allow attachment before treatment. The cells were treated with various gradients of extract concentrations from 1, 10, 25, 50, 100 μg/mL, while camptothecin was used as a positive control with a concentration of 1, 10, 25, 50, 100 μg/mL. The 96-well plates were incubated for 48 h, after which MTT (5 mg/mL) was added, and the plates were incubated for a further hour. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a solubilizing agent to dissolve the formazan, and the plates were spectrophotometrically measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermofisher Scientific). The percentage of cell viability was calculated using the formula below.%Cell viability=AtAb×100where At and Ac represent the absorbance of the extract-treated cell and untreated control cells, respectively.

Microorganisms and culture media

A total of three microorganisms were chosen for this study. These strains, obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), were sourced from Thermofisher Scientific in Johannesburg, South Africa. The study included two bacterial strains: the Gram-negative Escherichia coli (ATCC 8739), the Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 11632), and Candida albicans (ATCC 10231) for the in vitro evaluation of antibacterial activities.

MIC microplate dilution assay

The MIC values of the extracts were assessed using the microtitre plate dilution method (Asong et al., 2019). Bacterial strains were revived by streaking them onto nutrient agar petri dishes and incubating at 37 °C for 24 h. A single positive colony of each bacterium was inoculated into sterile 10 mL nutrient broth and incubated at 37 °C while shaking for 24 h. The overnight cultures were diluted in a 1:100 ratio. To each well of a 96-well microtiter plate, sterile distilled water (100 μL) was added. 100 μL of the plant extract dissolved in 5% DMSO (50 mg/mL) was serially diluted two-fold with distilled water. Streptomycin served was used as a positive control. To each well of the microtiter plate, 100 μL of each bacterial strain was added to achieve an approximate final inoculum equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard solution. Nutrient broth, untreated bacteria, 5% DMS0 were used as negative controls. The plates were wrapped in parafilm and incubated overnight at 37 °C, after which 50 μL (0.2 mg/mL) of resazurin sodium salt was added to every well. The microtiter plates were then incubated at 37 °C for another hour to observe growth. The resazurin antibacterial assay is a rapid, colorimetric method to assess the viability of bacteria and determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of antimicrobial agents, based on the reduction of resazurin (blue) to resorufin (pink) by metabolically active bacterial cells (Apu et al., 2013). The experiments were conducted in triplicate.

FTIR-ATR spectroscopic analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectra were obtained using a Nicolet iS5 FTIR spectrometer, manufactured by Thermo Scientific in Waltham, MA, USA. The analysis utilized an iD7 ATR (Attenuated Total Reflectance) module equipped with a ZnSe diamond crystal to ensure accurate readings. Before measuring each sample, a background reference was recorded. The dried extract powders were analysed, with each spectrum resulting from 16 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1. Data acquisition was performed in transmittance mode using the OMNIC 9.2.86 software.

GC-HRTOF-MS analysis

The analysis was performed using a Pegasus GC-HRTOF-MS (gas chromatography high-resolution time-of-flight) system (LECO Corporation, St. Joseph, MI, USA), coupled to an Agilent 8890 A gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) and equipped with a Gerstel MPS multipurpose autosampler (Gerstel Inc., Germany). The system was calibrated before analysis. Separation was achieved on an Rxi®-5 ms capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.25 μl film thickness; Restek, USA) under constant flow mode, with helium as the carrier gas at 1 mL/min. Samples (1 μL) were injected in splitless mode. The inlet and transfer line temperatures were maintained at 250 °C. The oven program was set as follows: initial temperature 70 °C (held 1 min), ramped to 120 °C at 10 °C/min (held 2 min), then increased to 300 °C at 10 °C/min (held 3 min). Mass spectrometric detection was performed with electron ionisation at 70 eV. Data were acquired in high-resolution mode at 13 spectra/s, with an m/z range of 30–700.

Collection and identification of compounds

The data collected from the GC-HRTOF-MS was formatted and processed on the LECO Chroma TOF-HRT software. Peak detection and deconvolution were performed automatically by the software, and the resulting mass spectra were matched against the NIST, Mainlib, and Replib metabolomics libraries. Metabolites were considered positively identified when their similarity value (SV) exceeded 800/1000 (≥80%).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the cytotoxicity results are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data from the experiments were analysed using GraphPad Prism Version 8.0 Statistical Software and Origin Pro 2024 (OriginPro Corporation, New York, NY, USA). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the student's t-test to calculate the statistical significance.

Results

In vitro cytotoxicity results

To assess the effects of plant extracts and the FDA-approved anticancer drug, camptothecin, on cell proliferation, the MTT cell viability assay was conducted on MRC-5, MCF-7, and HCT-116 cells, which were then treated with varying concentrations of the Vachellia extracts and camptothecin for 48 h. The cytotoxicity of crude extracts can be categorised based on their IC50 values into four distinct classes: very active, potent extract with IC50 ≤ 20 μg/mL. Moderately active extract with IC50 > 20–100 μg/mL, weak active extract with IC50 > 100–1000 μg/mL. Non-Cytotoxic Extract Concentration (NCEC), this includes extracts that have an IC50 value exceeding 1000 μg/mL, suggesting they do not exhibit significant cytotoxic effects (Atjanasuppat et al., 2009). The results demonstrated that the extracts showed potent to moderate inhibition of cell proliferation, with this inhibitory effect observed to be dose-dependent (Fig. 1a-c) and the IC50 values, which indicate the concentration required to inhibit 50% of the cells, are presented in Table 1. The extract of V. xanthophloea demonstrated notable cytotoxicity with IC50 values of 9.82 ± 0.96 μg/mL and 10.20 ± 0.96 μg/mL against MCF-7 and HCT116 cells, with less toxicity to normal MRC-5 cells, exhibiting an IC50 of 19.04 ± 1.22 μg/mL. Following the activity of V. xanthophloea, is V. karoo, which exhibited lower toxicity towards the MRC-5 cells, with an IC50 of 21.52 ± 1.30 μg/mL. However, it displayed significant toxicity towards MCF-7 and HCT-116 cells, with IC50 values of 13.51 ± 1.05 μg/mL and 9.61 ± 1.00 μg/mL, respectively. On the other hand, V. farnesiana (IC50 = 16.08 ± 1.16 μg/mL) and V. sieberiana (IC50 = 18.25 ± 1.22 μg/mL) showed good inhibitory activity against HCT116 cells. V. kosiensis demonstrated moderate inhibitory effects on cancer cells, MCF-7 and HCT116, showing IC50 values of 24.00 ± 1.32 and 32.20 ± 1.50 μg/mL, though not toxic to MRC5 fibroblast cells (IC50 = 36.25 ± 1.55 μg/mL). Furthermore, V. sieberiana exhibited strong activity against both MCF-7 and HCT-116, with IC50 values of 13.32 ± 1.08 μg/mL and 13.43 ± 1.09 μg/mL, respectively. Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial properties of Vachellia species were evaluated against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans using the broth dilution method, with resazurin serving as an indicator of viable microorganisms. Fig. 2 shows antimicrobial tests, where the resazurin turns pink as an indication of microbial growth and remains blue, signifying microbial growth inhibition. (Chakansin et al., 2022). The results, illustrated in Table 2, indicate that V. kosiensis exhibited the strongest inhibitory effects on S. aureus and E. coli, showing MICs of 0.31 mg/mL and 1.25 mg/mL, respectively. However, C. albicans demonstrated resistance to V. kosiensis, with an MIC greater than 10.00 mg/mL. Additionally, V. karoo and V. xanthophloea were identified as effective inhibitors of S. aureus, both displaying an MIC of 1.25 mg/mL, while also serving as moderate inhibitors of E. coli with an MIC of 2.50 mg/mL. To provide comparative data, streptomycin and streptomycin (positive controls) were utilized. Streptomycin, a broad-spectrum fluoroquinolone antibiotic, proved highly effective against all the tested microorganisms with an MIC below 0.02 mg/mL. Meanwhile, streptomycin, an aminoglycoside antibiotic, showed significant inhibition of S. aureus, E. coli, and C. albicans, presenting MICs of 0.31 mg/mL, 0.15 mg/mL, and 0.31 mg/mL, respectively. FTIR-ATR spectroscopic analysis

The different functional groups present in five species of Vachellia (V. karoo, V. kosiensis, V. farnesiana, V. sieberiana, and V. xanthophloea) were detected using FTIR spectral analysis, which was based on the peak values in the region of infrared radiation. According to Saxena and Rathore (Rathore et al., 2007), FTIR separates the functional groups of the compound based on peak ratios obtained through the infrared radiation. Fig. 3 and Table 3 provide a summary of the FTIR spectral analysis of the ethanol-water extracts and the peak values/functional groups, respectively, obtained from five Vachellia species. The spectral analysis detected functional groups in six different frequency ranges (Table 3) with variations in the transmittance for the different species of Vachellia (Fig. 3). The identified spectra showed six major peaks at 3293.34 cm−1, 2920.66 cm−1, 2168.08 cm−1, 1601.11 cm−1, 1200.95 cm−1, and 1068.85 cm−1. Since ethanol and water were used to extract the sample, there could be an ethanol and water band in the spectrum (Fig. 3). The characteristic ethanol band would be 3293.34 cm-1, which shows O—H group stretching vibration or O—H wagging in phenolic compounds (Branches et al., 2024) for all the species. This suggests the presence of phenolic compounds, which have been reported to have antioxidant properties (More et al., 2021a). The transmittance value of the species occurred in the order V. karoo > V. kosiensis > V. farnesiana > V. xanthopholoea > V. sieberiana (Fig. 3). The variations in the transmittance value could indicate varying abundance of the phenolic compounds present in the five different species. Nine various functional groups belonging to five different compounds were identified (Table 3). The band occurring at 3293.34 cm−1 indicates that alkaloids may be present in all five species (with variations in their abundance based on transmittance), which could be due to the N—H stretch (Kalaivani and Anilkumar, 2018). Comparative chromatogram evaluation revealed qualitative and quantitative differences among the studied species, which point to species-dependent metabolic specialisation. Several metabolites were discovered only in specific species or were present at dramatically different relative abundances, suggesting that these phytochemical changes may contribute to the observed variances in biological activity.

Metabolites from multiple pharmacologically important chemical families, including alkanes, alkenes, aldehydes, ketones, fatty acids, phenolic compounds, indoles, diterpenes, and triterpenoids. Phytol was present in every species examined, except for V. farnesiana, among the discovered chemicals. Phytol is a well-known diterpene alcohol having extensive biological properties such as antibacterial, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and cytotoxicity actions (Konieczynski et al., 2022; Almeida-Bezerra et al., 2024). The presence of this compound in V. karroo, V. kosiensis, V. sieberiana and V. xanthophloea may therefore partly explain the antibacterial and cytotoxic actions reported for these species. Interestingly, V. farnesiana showed the most potent cytotoxic activity among the studied extracts in the absence of phytol, which could imply that other bioactive ingredients might be responsible for its prominent cytotoxic properties.

Discussion

There is limited literature reporting the cytotoxic properties of Vachellia species and other published research papers report on other species, except the one investigated in this work (Ngaffo et al., 2020) investigated the cytotoxic effects of a dichloromethane-methanol (1:1) extract obtained from the leaves of Acacia sieberiana. The research focused on various fractions derived from this extract, as well as several isolated compounds. The IC50 values recorded for the different compounds varied significantly across cell types. For A. sieberiana extract, the values ranged from 13.45 μg/mL in leukaemia (CCRF-CEM) cells and 33.20 μg/mL in the breast adenocarcinoma (MDA-MB-231-BCRP) cells. The fractions exhibited IC50 values of 16.42 μg/mL in CCRF-CEM cells and 29.64 μg/mL in breast adenocarcinoma (MDA-MB-231-pcDNA) cells. Meanwhile, ASLb showed a range from 22.94 μg/mL in MDA-MB-231-BCRP cells and 40.19 μg/mL against the resistant colon adenocarcinoma (HCT116 (p53−/−)) cells. These variations highlight the differences in efficacy of each compound against various cancer cell lines.

The hallmark of an effective anticancer drug is its selective cytotoxicity, meaning it should specifically target and kill cancer cells while minimizing harm to normal, healthy cells. This selectivity is crucial in cancer treatment, as conventional chemotherapeutics, such as camptothecin and cisplatin, often lack this precision (Blagosklonny, 2023). These conventional drugs can indiscriminately affect rapidly dividing normal cells, which leads to a range of side effects. Common adverse effects associated with these therapies include hair loss due to the impact on hair follicles, bone marrow suppression resulting in decreased blood cell production and increased risk of infections, anaemia, and gastrointestinal issues like nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea caused by damage to the cells lining the digestive tract (van den Boogaard et al., 2022). Thus, the development of anticancer agents that demonstrate higher selectivity can significantly enhance patient outcomes by reducing the collateral damage to healthy tissues.

The Selectivity Index (SI) in the context of cancer research measures how effectively an extract targets cancer cells versus normal cells. A higher SI indicates greater selectivity, meaning the extract is more toxic to cancer cells and potentially safer for healthy tissues. According to Mahavorasirikul et al. (Mahavorasirikul et al., 2010) an SI greater than 1.5 for a plant extract against cancer cells indicates a degree of selectivity, meaning the extract is more toxic to cancer cells than to normal cells. Table 1 illustrates the selectivity of the tested Vachellia species against cancer cell lines. Vachellia karoo and Vachellia xanthophloea demonstrated moderate selectivity, with selectivity indices (SI) of 2.0 against MCF-7 cells and 1.5 against HCT116. In contrast, Vachellia farnesianaa exhibited a strong selectivity profile, achieving an SI of 1.7 for MCF-7 and 3.1 for HCT116. This suggests that V. farnesiana may be particularly effective in targeting these cancer cells.

A substantial body of research supports the findings of this study, demonstrating the capacity of Vachellia to inhibit microorganisms. The antibacterial properties of bark extracts from Acacia karroo were evaluated using various solvents, including aqueous, acetone, dichloromethane, ethanol, methanol, and petroleum ether, specifically against S. aureus (Chakansin et al., 2022). The results from a micro-dilution assay indicated a minimum microbicidal concentration (MMC) of 0.195 mg/mL for the aqueous extract when tested against S. aureus and C. albicans. For the fungus evaluated, the minimal inhibition concentration (MIC) ranged between 3.125 and 6.250 mg/mL, while the minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) values varied from 3.125 to over 12.500 mg/mL (Mulaudzi et al., 2011). Nielsen et al. (Nielsen et al., 2012) conducted a study to assess the antibacterial properties of methanol extracts derived from the leaves and stems of Acacia karroo. These extracts were tested against various bacterial strains, including Citrobacter, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus, utilizing a liquid micro-broth dilution technique with streptomycin serving as a positive control. The results revealed that the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for the bacteria varied significantly, ranging from 78.12 to 1250.00 μg/mL. Notably, the stem extracts exhibited the lowest MIC of 78.12 μg/mL against both K. pneumoniae and S. aureus, indicating a strong antibacterial potential. Additionally, the study assessed the minimum microbicidal concentration (MMC), which demonstrated a relatively weaker activity, with the lowest MMC recorded at 156.25 μg/mL against S. aureus. Overall, the MMC values for the tested bacteria were found to range between 156.25 and greater than 2500.00 μg/mL, highlighting the varying levels of effectiveness of the methanol stem extracts against different bacterial strains (Rathore et al., 2007; Branches et al., 2024; Kalaichelvi and Dhivya, 2017). It is worth noting that benzothiazole was discovered only in V. farnesiana and not in the other studied species. Benzothiazole compounds have garnered much pharmacological interest because of their broad spectrum of bioactivities, especially anticancer, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antibacterial activities (Irfan et al., 2020). Previous investigations have indicated that drugs containing benzothiazole can inhibit cancer cell proliferation by induction of apoptosis and regulation of oxidative stress pathways. Therefore, the presence of benzothiazole in V. farnesiana may be a major contributor to the improved cytotoxic efficacy seen in the tested cancer cell lines.

V. karroo, V. kosiensis and V. farnesiana had methyl stearate. This fatty acid methyl ester has been shown to possess numerous pharmacological actions, which include cytotoxic, anti-inflammatory and antibacterial activity (Ukwubile et al., 2019). It may also be responsible for the cytotoxic and antibacterial effects identified in these species, in V. farnesiana and V. karroo. Additionally, 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid bis(2-methylpropyl) ester, usually included in the group of phthalate derivatives, was found solely in V. farnesiana. Phthalates are often reported as natural or endophytic microbial metabolites from medicinal plant extracts, although recent studies have reported their antibacterial and antifungal properties. (Rajamanikyam et al., 2017; Roy, 2020). Thus, the presence of this chemical may contribute to the inhibitory activity of V. farnesiana on Staphylococcus aureus infection and Candida infection.

The cytotoxicity activity of V. kosiensis may be related to the occurrence of germanicol, a pentacyclic triterpenoid that is found only in V. kosiensis. Antiproliferative and anticancer effects of Germanicol were described before via induction of apoptosis and inhibition of tumour cell proliferation (Gao et al., 2019). This is the first report of this chemical in V. kosiensis, which may be a chemotaxonomic marker and a potential contributor to the cytotoxic profile of this species.

Nonanal was also discovered in V. karroo, V. kosiensis and V. sieberiana. Nonanal is an aliphatic aldehyde which has been previously demonstrated to have antibacterial activity against several pathogenic microorganisms (Muroi et al., 1993). This may partly explain the antibacterial activity shown in these species. It is interesting to note that neophytadiene, supraene and dl-α-tocopherol were found in all the studied Vachellia species. These metabolites are extensively linked to antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxicity activities (Adem Endris et al., 2024; Das Gupta and Suh, 2016; Sianipar et al., 2024). The widespread presence of these compounds in the species studied in this work suggests that they could be possible core bioactive metabolites, possibly contributing to the broad-spectrum biological activities reported.

The GC–MS results generally suggest that the biological activities of the Vachellia species studied are probably mediated through complex synergistic interactions of several phytochemicals rather than by the action of a single molecule. Thus, the interspecies variation in the anticancer and antimicrobial activities reported may be related not only to the presence or absence of the metabolites but also to their relative abundance, chemical interactions, and possible synergistic or antagonistic effects. These results underscore the therapeutic potential of Vachellia species as promising sources of multifunctional bioactive compounds and advocate for future studies including bioactivity-guided fractionation, metabolomic quantification, molecular target validation and mechanistic studies to identify the principal compounds responsible for the observed pharmacological activities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this work provides persuasive evidence that species of the genus Vachellia represent a valuable and hitherto largely untapped reservoir of bioactive phytochemicals with considerable cytotoxicity and antibacterial potential. The extracts showed significant cytotoxic action against breast cancer and colorectal cancer cells, with relatively reduced toxicity towards the normal MRC-5 cell line, indicating their potential for therapeutic selectivity. The most promising species investigated were Vachellia karroo, Vachellia kosiensis and Vachellia xanthophloea, which exhibited significant anticancer activity which requires more mechanistic and translational exploration. On antimicrobial activity, the extracts were found to be most active against clinically important pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans. The bioactive phytochemicals were investigated using the techniques, revealing various bioactive compounds that are probably responsible for cytotoxicity and antimicrobial activity in different species of Vachellia. The promising results of Vachellia species not only contribute to the study of their therapeutic value but also to the study of bioactive properties as a next step towards the search for new and beneficial natural treatments.
