Enhancing thai native chicken semen quality and fertility through kaempferia parviflora supplementation during cold storage
Authors: Theerapat Kheawkanha, Maruay Pimprasert, Wuttigrai Boonkum, Vibuntita Chankitisakul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKaempferia parviflora (KP) extract was evaluated as a natural antioxidant supplement in rooster semen extender during 72 h of storage at 5°C. In Experiment 1, six KP concentrations (0.5–3.0 mg/mL) were tested in a split-plot CRD to determine effects on motility, viability, plasma membrane integrity, and malondialdehyde (MDA). KP at 1.5 mg/mL consistently preserved semen quality across 24-72 h and lowered MDA relative to the control (p < 0.05), whereas ≥2.5 mg/mL showed diminishing or adverse effects. In Experiment 2, fertility and hatchability were assessed after artificial insemination using semen stored 0-72 h with or without KP. The 1.5 mg/mL group yielded higher fertility and hatchability than the control at 24-72 h (p < 0.05). These results identify 1.5 mg/mL KP as an optimal, dose-dependent additive that mitigates oxidative damage and extends functional longevity of cooled semen.Keywords: Sperm quality, Antioxidant, Cooled semen, Rooster, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Native chickens, particularly the Pradu Hang Dum (PD) breed, play a vital role in Thailand’s rural economy due to their superior meat quality and nutritional profile compared to commercial broilers (Charoensin et al., 2021; Mekchay et al., 2014; Tunim et al., 2021). However, genetic progress in these populations remains slow, primarily due to reliance on natural mating and smallholder-based breeding practices (Padhi., 2016).

Artificial insemination (AI), particularly when combined with semen storage technologies, offers a promising strategy to enhance reproductive efficiency and support genetic conservation in native breeds. A major barrier, however, is the rapid decline in sperm viability during cold storage, which significantly reduces fertility outcomes. Avian spermatozoa are highly susceptible to oxidative stress during storage due to their high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), making them susceptible to lipid peroxidation (LPO) initiated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Mussa et al., 2021). This oxidative stress impairs critical functions such as motility, DNA integrity, and mitochondrial activity, ultimately compromising fertilization potential (Masoudi et al., 2019; Sangani et al., 2017).

Although improvements in semen extender formulations have been achieved, maintaining sperm viability beyond 24–48 hours remains a significant challenge, particularly in native chicken breeds. In this context, natural antioxidants derived from medicinal plants have emerged as potential additives to improve sperm preservation by mitigating ROS-mediated damage during storage (El-Sheshtawy and El-Nattat, 2020; Ros-Santaella and Pintus, 2021). Kaempferia parviflora (KP), or “Krachai Dum”, is a medical plant rich in methoxyflavones with potent antioxidant properties (Azuma et al., 2011; Malakul et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2023; Tewtrakul et al., 2008). While previous studies have demonstrated KP’s beneficial effects on male reproductive function through oral supplementation in mammals and avian (Authaida et al., 2023; Fungfuang et al., 2016; Temkitthawon et al., 2011), its direct application in avain semen extenders remains largely unexplored, despite its ethnopharmacological relevance (Singh et al., 2023).

Recent research in boars and Thai bulls has shown that direct KP inclusion in semen extenders enhanced post-thaw sperm quality (Loetjettanarom et al., 2025; Saiyamanon et al., 2025), indicating potential for broader application. However, avian sperm differ substantially from mammalian sperm in lipid composition and oxidative sensitivity, necessitating species-specific evaluation (Partyka and Niżański., 2021; Silvestre et al., 2021). Furthermore, improper antioxidant dosing may lead to paradoxical oxidative effects, as high concentrations can induce redox imbalance in birds (McGraw., 2011).

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effect of KP extract, directly supplemented into a semen extender, on semen quality and fertility outcomes during 72-hour cold storage in Thai native chickens. Specifically, we investigate the optimal concentration of KP and its effect on motility, viability, oxidative stress markers, fertility, and hatchability. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly assess the effects of Kaempferia parviflora extract as a semen extender additive in avian species. Findings from this work may contribute to improved semen preservation protocols and support sustainable genetic conservation in native poultry populations.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The KP extract used in this study was purchased from AsianBioplex Co. (Bangphra, Si-Racha, Chonburi, Thailand). The extract was a water-based powder with a bulk density of 0.30 to 0.60 g/cc and a moisture content below 10 %. The antioxidant properties of the extract were previously characterized in our related study (Saiyamanon et al., 2025). Briefly, the KP extract exhibited a total phenolic content (TPC) of 16.13 ± 0.67 mg GAE/g, and a total flavonoid content (TFC) of 1.02 ± 0.01 mg QE/g, determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu and aluminum chloride colorimetric methods, respectively. Its antioxidant activity, assessed by DPPH radical-scavenging assay, showed 58.31 ± 2.87 % inhibition, confirming strong free radical neutralization capacity.

Unless otherwise noted, all other chemicals used in this study were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Animals and management

Twenty-eight adult Thai native roosters (Pradu Hang Dum, PD; 12–18 months of age) were selected for the experiment. The roosters were housed individually in open-environment cages (60 × 45 × 45 cm) to ensure adequate space and environmental conditions. Standard management practices were followed, including twice-daily feeding of commercial breeder feed (approximately 130 g per rooster) and ad libitum access to water.

For fertility testing, two hundred eighty PD hens (40–44 weeks of age, with an egg production rate ≥50 %) were used. The hens were housed individually and provided with approximately 110 g of layer feed per day, along with water ad libitum.

The study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Research Council of Thailand (Ethical approval number: IACUC-KKU-18/68). All animals were housed at the Network Center for Animal Breeding and Omics Research, Khon Kaen University, Thailand.

Experimental design

Experiment 1: Effects of KP Supplementation on Semen Quality During 72 h Cold Storage.

This experiment aimed to evaluate the effects of different concentrations of KP extract on the quality of rooster semen during chilled storage for up to 72 h. Six concentrations of KP (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 mg/mL) were prepared by dissolving the extract in IGGKPh extender, using a vortex mixer for 3 min, followed by overnight incubation at 5°C. Semen samples were collected from multiple roosters, pooled to minimize individual variation, and then divided equally into seven treatment groups: one control (0 mg/mL KP) and six KP-supplemented groups.

Each semen sample was extended to achieve a final concentration of 150–250 × 106 sperm/mL. The extended semen was gradually cooled from 25°C to 5°C over 60 min, and stored at 5°C for up to 72 h. Semen quality parameters, including sperm motility, viability, membrane integrity, and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, were evaluated at four time points: 0 (T0), 24 (T24), 48 (T48), and 72 (T72) h post-cooling. Each treatment was replicated at least six times to ensure statistical reliability.

Experiment 2: Effects of KP Supplementation on Fertility and Hatchability.

This experiment evaluated the effects of KP concentration and semen storage time on fertility and hatchability. Based on the treatment groups established in Experiment 1, chilled semen extended with seven concentrations of KP (0–3.0 mg/mL) was stored at 5°C and used for insemination at four time points (0, 24, 48, and 72 h), resulting in a total of 28 treatment combinations.

A total of 280 hens were randomly assigned to these 28 treatment groups (n = 10 per group). Each hen received weekly intravaginal artificial insemination for four consecutive weeks using semen from its assigned treatment group. Fertility and hatchability outcomes were assessed following the procedures described in the Fertility Test section.

Semen collection

Semen was collected using the dorsal abdominal massage method as described by Burrows and Quinn (1936). Each ejaculate was collected in a 1.5-mL microtube containing 0.1 mL of IGGKPh diluent, which consisted of: 0.14 g potassium citrate, 1.40 g sodium glutamate, 0.21 g sodium dihydrogen phosphate, 0.98 g sodium hydrogen phosphate, 0.9 g glucose, and 0.9 g inositol per 100 mL of deionized water (pH 6.95, osmotic pressure 380 mOsm/kg) (Chankitisakul et al., 2022). The same technician collected semen samples under standardized conditions to minimize variability and avoid contamination.

Semen quality evaluation

Total sperm motility (MOT)

MOT was assessed using a computer-assisted semen analysis system (Hamilton Thorne Biosciences, version 10 HIM-IVOS, Beverly, MA, USA) with the following settings: frame rate = 60 frames per second, number of frames = 30, head brightness minimum = 165, minimum cell size = 2 µm, maximum cell size = 50 µm (Rachamak et al., 2023). Semen samples were diluted at 1:10 (v/v), and at least five fields containing a minimum of 300 sperm were evaluated for each sample.

Sperm viability

Sperm viability was assessed using dual-fluorescence staining with SYBR-14 and propidium iodide (L7011, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A 300 µL aliquot of semen was incubated with 5 µL of SYBR-14 solution at 25°C for 10 min. After incubation, 5 µL of propidium iodide was added, and the sample was incubated at 25°C for an additional 10 minutes. The fixed samples were analyzed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX71, Tokyo, Japan) at 400 × magnification. At least 200 sperm were counted to determine the percentage of live (SYBR-14 positive) and dead (propidium iodide positive) sperm.

Plasma membrane function

Plasma membrane integrity was evaluated using the hypoosmotic swelling test (HOST). A 30 µL aliquot of diluted semen (30 × 106 sperm/mL) was mixed with 300 µL of a hypoosmotic solution (9 g fructose and 4.9 g sodium citrate in 1000 mL of deionized water) and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The fixed mixture was analyzed under a phase-contrast microscope at 400 × magnification. At least 200 sperm were counted to determine the percentage of sperm with swollen tails (indicating normal plasma membrane function) and non-swollen tails (indicating abnormal membrane function).

Lipid peroxidation

Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in diluted semen were measured as an indicator of lipid peroxidation using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay, based on the method described by Chauychu-noo et al. (2021). A 0.30-mL aliquot of diluted semen (250 × 10⁶ sperm/mL) was mixed with 0.25 mL of ferrous sulfate (0.2 mM) and 0.25 mL of ascorbic acid (1 mM). The mixture was incubated in a water bath at 37°C for 60 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of trichloroacetic acid (15 % w/v) and 1 mL of thiobarbituric acid (0.375 % w/v) were added to the mixture. The sample was then boiled for 10 min and immediately cooled in a 4°C water bath. The supernatant was separated by centrifugation at 800 × g for 10 min at 4°C. MDA levels in the supernatant were measured spectrophotometrically at 532 nm using a UV-1200 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) and expressed in nmol/mL.

Fertility test

Fertility was assessed following AI in hens. Each hen received a single intravaginal insemination on Day 0 using 0.1 mL of chilled semen containing approximately 150 × 10⁶ sperm/dose. Insemination was performed between 15:00 and 17:00 h. This procedure was repeated once per week for four consecutive weeks.

Eggs were collected daily starting from Day 2 after the first insemination and continued until Day 8 following the final insemination. This collection window was selected to fall within the hen’s 7–10-day oviductal sperm-storage capacity, providing a valid interval for fertility assessment (Chankitisakul et al., 2022; Kheawkanha et al., 2021). All eggs were stored on paper trays at 22–25°C until incubation. Fertility was determined on Day 7 of incubation using candling to detect embryonic development. Hatchability was recorded on Day 21 as the number of live chicks hatched from fertile eggs. Fertility was calculated as the percentage of fertile eggs relative to the total number of eggs set, while hatchability was expressed as the percentage of hatched chicks relative to the number of fertile eggs.

Statistical analysis

Experiment 1: A split-plot completely randomized design (CRD) was employed with two factors: KP concentration (0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 mg/mL; whole-plot factor) and day of storage (0, 24, 48, and 72 h; subplot factor). For each replicate, pooled semen was divided into the seven treatment groups. Within each treatment group, four aliquots were prepared for evaluation at each of the four storage durations. The full design was replicated over six independent collection days (n = 6). Collection day was treated as a blocking factor and used as the whole-plot error term to account for batch variation in semen quality.

Experiment 2: A two-factor completely randomized design was used, with KP concentration (same as above) and day of storage (0, 24, 48, and 72 h) as fixed factors. A total of 280 hens were randomly assigned to the 28 treatment groups (n = 10 hens per treatment). Each hen received a single insemination per week for four consecutive weeks, with semen corresponding to one of the four storage times. The order of storage-time treatments was randomized across pens. The individual hen served as the experimental unit, and week (insemination period) was treated as a blocking factor to control for temporal variation.

Prior to analysis, all data were tested for normality and outliers using the PROC UNIVARIATE procedure in SAS (v9.0; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Outliers were flagged if values exceeded ±3 standard deviations from the mean. Data were analyzed using the General Linear Model (PROC GLM) procedure, incorporating the main effects of KP concentration, storage time, and their interaction. Treatment means were compared using Duncan’s new multiple range test, with significance set at p < 0.05. All results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

At the initial time point (T0), no significant differences were found among treatments for any measured parameter (p > 0.05). MOT ranged from 87.18 % to 88.35 %, viability from 88.17 % to 89.70 %, membrane integrity from 86.92 % to 88.24 %, and MDA from 0.3370 to 0.3660 nmol/mL, indicating uniform semen quality across all groups.

At 24 h (T24), the control group showed significantly lower MOT (77.78 %) compared to the KP-supplemented groups, particularly 1.5 mg/mL (83.98 %), with p < 0.05. Viability at 1.5 mg/mL (83.37 %) was significantly higher than in the control (80.13 %, p < 0.05). Similarly, membrane integrity was significantly improved in the 1.5 mg/mL group (82.70 %) versus the control (77.66 %, p < 0.05). MDA levels were significantly lower in the 1.0 mg/mL (0.6066 nmol/mL) and 1.5 mg/mL (0.5806 nmol/mL) groups compared to the control (0.6646 nmol/mL, p < 0.05).

At 48 h (T48), semen extended with 1.5 mg/mL KP again showed the highest quality: MOT (74.19 %), viability (73.94 %), and membrane integrity (74.50 %) were all significantly higher than in the control (69.41 %, 69.60 %, and 65.44 %, respectively; p < 0.05). MDA levels were lowest in the 1.5 mg/mL group (1.4187 nmol/mL) and were significantly lower than those in the control (1.6948 nmol/mL, p < 0.05).

At 72 h (T72), the 1.5 mg/mL group maintained the highest MOT (68.88 %), viability (65.99 %), and membrane integrity (68.59 %), while the control group showed the lowest values (MOT: 56.16 %, viability: 54.38 %, membrane integrity: 49.04 %, all p < 0.05). MDA levels were also significantly reduced in the 1.5 mg/mL group (2.1412 nmol/mL) compared to the control (2.6730 nmol/mL, p < 0.05).

At T0, fertility and hatchability rates did not differ significantly among groups (p > 0.05), with fertility values ranging from 81.11 % to 82.77 % and hatchability rates from 85.14 % to 88.89 %, indicating no initial effect of KP supplementation.

However, storage duration had a marked effect on reproductive outcomes. At T24, fertility was highest in the 1.5 mg/mL group (80.55 %), followed by the 2.0 mg/mL group (74.30 %), both of which were significantly higher than the 2.5 mg/mL group (66.24 %), which showed a significant reduction (p < 0.05). Fertility in the control (67.77 %) and other KP-treated groups (70.36 %–70.56 %) did not differ significantly at this time point.

At T48, fertility in the 1.5 mg/mL KP group reached 65.68 % (p < 0.05), which was significantly greater than the control (48.60 %) and other treatments, which ranged from 53.70 % to 60.29 %. A similar trend was observed at T72, where the 1.5 mg/mL group achieved a fertility rate of 51.84 %, significantly higher than the control (27.36 %) and all other concentrations (p < 0.05).

Hatchability followed a comparable pattern. At T24, hatchability peaked in the 1.5 mg/mL group (83.28 %) and was significantly higher than all other treatments, which ranged from 74.07 % to 77.77 % (p < 0.05). At T48, no significant differences were detected among groups (p > 0.05), although the 1.5 mg/mL group still had the numerically highest hatchability (71.29 %) compared to the other groups (66.40 %–69.63 %). At T72, the control group had the lowest hatchability (21.87 %), which was significantly lower than that of all KP-treated groups (ranging from 53.70 % to 61.11 %, p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study reveals that supplementing rooster semen extender with KP extract significantly improves semen quality and fertility during 72 h of chilled storage. The 1.5 mg/mL dose consistently preserved key semen parameters, including motility, viability, and membrane integrity, and was associated with the lowest levels of MDA among all treatment groups. In contrast, higher concentrations (≥2.5 mg/mL) were associated with reduced semen quality, suggesting a potential threshold above which KP may become detrimental to semen quality. Although previous studies have explored the reproductive benefits of KP through oral administration or in mammalian models, this study is among the first to assess its direct incorporation into avian semen extenders. These findings establish the utility of direct KP addition to the extender for Thai native chickens and support its value for breeding and conservation programs where semen is particularly vulnerable to oxidative deterioration during storage.

In this study, supplementation with 1.5 mg/mL KP attenuated MDA accumulation and preserved sperm motility, viability, and plasma-membrane integrity for up to 72 h. Such a prolonged chilled-storage window is rarely reported in poultry (Khaeruddin et al., 2024; Kheawkanha et al., 2023; Pimpa et al., 2024), underscoring the superior efficacy of direct KP incorporation into extenders. This performance also exceeded the 36 h viability reported for oral KP supplementation (Authaida et al., 2023), highlighting the advantage of local, targeted antioxidant delivery under oxidative stress. Mechanistically, the methoxyflavone-rich KP extract likely exerts protective effects through ROS scavenging and disruption of lipid peroxidation chain reactions. These actions are mediated via hydrogen atom transfer, electron donation, and stabilization of radical intermediates (Mekjaruskul et al., 2012; Singh et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2022), consistent with the reduced MDA levels observed at 1.5 mg/mL and the preservation of sperm membrane integrity during chilled storage (Leão et al., 2021). Additionally, anthocyanidin-rich KP extracts have been shown to preserve mitochondrial function under oxidative stress by modulating mitochondrial membrane permeability, intracellular calcium, and ROS homeostasis (Kim et al., 2022). KP flavonoids also promote endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) expression while suppressing pro-inflammatory markers such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 (Horigome et al., 2017), further supporting their cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory roles.

A clear dose-response pattern emerged. Doses below the optimum (0.5-1.0 mg/mL) were less effective beyond 48 h. While these doses provided some improvement over the control group, their antioxidant activity appeared insufficient to counteract the cumulative oxidative stress encountered during prolonged storage. This finding aligns with previous studies suggesting that suboptimal doses of antioxidants may inadequately scavenge ROS, especially when their activity is not synergized with the sperm’s endogenous antioxidant defenses (Authaida et al., 2023; Davies and Holt, 2018).

In contrast, supra-optimal KP concentrations (≥2.5 mg/mL) were associated with reduced sperm quality, marked by significantly lower motility and elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels compared to the 1.5 mg/mL group. Several mechanisms could explain these findings. One explanation is the plateau effect, where antioxidant activity of KP may reach a saturation point, rendering further supplementation ineffective (Lamia et al., 2023; Taşkıran, 2023). Additionally, excessive concentrations of antioxidants can paradoxically induce pro-oxidant effects, thereby triggering oxidative stress rather than mitigating it (Schafer and Buettner., 2001). This pattern is consistent with previous findings in other species. For example, in Thai native bulls, 1.0 mg/mL KP improved post-thaw motility, viability, and membrane integrity, whereas 1.5 mg/mL resulted in significant declines in all parameters (Loetjettanarom et al., 2025). Notably, MDA levels at 1.5 mg/mL were comparable to the control, suggesting a shift from antioxidant to pro-oxidant activity at higher concentrations. Similar dose-dependent effects were observed in boars, where 2–4 % KP enhanced post-thaw sperm quality, but 6–8 % concentrations impaired it, highlighting a narrow therapeutic window (Saiyamanon et al., 2025). Taken together, these results underscore the need for careful dose optimization when using KP in semen extenders, as excessive concentrations may induce pro-oxidant or cytotoxic effects that undermine its protective potential.

Species-specific differences in sperm membrane composition likely explain why the optimal KP dose differs among cattle, boars, and chickens. Notably, the cholesterol-to-phospholipid (C/PL) ratio of rooster sperm membranes is lower (∼0.30) than that of bulls (∼0.45), with boars exhibiting the lowest ratio (∼0.26) (Park and Lynch, 1992). A reduced C/PL ratio, in combination with high polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) content, increases membrane fluidity and susceptibility to cold shock and lipid peroxidation (Blesbois et al., 2008), thereby influencing the antioxidant concentration required for protection during chilled storage. These structural differences affect membrane phase-transition behavior and cold resistance (Lessard et al., 2000; Pirie, 1965), which in turn modulate how sperm respond to extenders and additives. A similar trend is observed in extender optimization using sericin: the optimal concentration was 0.25 % for bull semen but 0.5 % for rooster semen, reflecting species-specific differences in membrane biophysics (Ratchamak et al., 2020; Yangngam et al., 2021). These findings collectively underscore the importance of tailoring antioxidant supplementation to species-specific membrane properties to achieve effective cryo- or cold preservation.

Oxidative stress during prolonged chilled storage compromises fertility by impairing mitochondrial function, depleting ATP, and inducing DNA and membrane damage (Costa et al., 2023; Kujoana et al., 2024; Umair et al., 2023). In our study, the protective effects of KP at 1.5 mg/mL were not only evident at the cellular level but also translated into flock-level reproductive outcomes. Both fertility and hatchability remained significantly higher than the control group after 24 to 72 hours of storage, demonstrating that biochemical preservation of sperm integrity had practical reproductive benefits across the typical semen storage window. Although post-fertilization events were not directly assessed, the sustained improvement in hatchability suggests possible downstream effects. Reduced paternal oxidative damage may contribute to enhanced early embryonic development, as previously proposed in mammalian systems (Koifman et al., 2022). This hypothesis merits further investigation in avian models to determine whether antioxidant protection during semen storage can influence embryo viability and development beyond fertilization.

While our data support antioxidant-mediated protection at the optimal dose, we did not directly measure mitochondrial function or redox signaling directly. Given KP’s dose-dependent effects and cross-species evidence for mitochondrial involvement and enzyme-level modulation, future studies should incorporate targeted endpoints such as mitochondrial membrane potential (e.g., JC-1, TMRE), ATP content, lipid peroxidation markers (e.g., 4-HNE), and antioxidant defense pathways including SOD, CAT, GPX activity, and redox-regulated transcription factors (e.g., Nrf2/ARE, MAPK). These investigations will help elucidate whether KP acts primarily through ROS neutralization, mitochondrial preservation, or modulation of redox-sensitive gene expression in avian sperm.

From an application perspective, direct inclusion of KP extract (1.5 mg/mL) in the extender is preferable to oral administration. Oral KP requires high daily doses, suffers from poor palatability, and could extend sperm viability for only 36 h (Authaida et al., 2023), whereas the present protocol maintained semen quality for up to 72 h. The KP used here was a standardized water extract that is shelf-stable and does not require emulsifiers or organic solvents, which offers a practical advantage over conventional antioxidants that are formulation-sensitive. For instance, vitamin E is hydrophobic and typically requires emulsification and protection from light and oxygen (Jurado-Campos et al., 2022; Tareq et al., 2009). Similarly, reduced glutathione (GSH) readily oxidizes to GSSG and is often prepared fresh before use (Sigma-Aldrich GSH datasheet), which complicates its application under field conditions. Both antioxidants are sensitive to oxidative degradation and require controlled handling to maintain efficacy (Boonme and Wuttikul., 2018; Riaz et al., 2009).

Although KP extraction methods are fairly standardized, variability in phytochemical composition between batches necessitates quality control. Techniques such as HPLC fingerprinting and quantification of key methoxyflavones, along with TPC and DPPH assays, are recommended to ensure consistent potency (Singh et al., 2023). Considering KP’s local availability and low production cost in Thailand and Southeast Asia, a quality-assured KP-based extender offers a stable, scalable, and cost-effective alternative. This contrasts with conventional antioxidants, which may involve complex synthesis, higher costs, and reduced efficacy due to prooxidant effects at elevated concentrations (Al-Temimi et al., 2023; Kamal-Eldin, 2019). Therefore, KP represents a practical and economically viable option for poultry semen preservation in breeding programs.

Conclusions

The direct supplementation of Kaempferia parviflora (KP) in rooster semen extenders significantly enhanced semen quality and reproductive outcomes during 72 hours of chilled storage. An optimal concentration of 1.5 mg/mL consistently preserved sperm motility, viability, and plasma membrane integrity—evidenced by reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels—compared to the control. Importantly, these in vitro improvements translated into higher fertility and hatchability, demonstrating the practical value of KP as an antioxidant additive in poultry artificial insemination programs. Collectively, the findings identify KP as a practical antioxidant additive for poultry semen extenders when used at an appropriate dose. Broader application will benefit from dose control and batch standardization of KP extracts, with future validation across additional genetic lines and farm settings.
