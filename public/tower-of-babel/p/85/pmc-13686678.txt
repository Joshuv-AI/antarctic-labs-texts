Integrating Sustainability Into Dental Education: Students' Knowledge, Behaviours, and Curriculum Perspectives
Authors: Fatma Nur Kızılay, Türkan Ünal, Emine Gülşen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe healthcare sector contributes approximately 5% of global greenhouse gas emissions, and dentistry represents a notable environmental burden due to high energy consumption, material use, and medical waste generation. This study aimed to evaluate dental students' awareness, attitudes, and perceptions regarding environmental sustainability and to assess the extent to which sustainability is integrated into the dental curriculum.Materials and MethodsThis descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted between March and May 2025 at the Faculty of Dentistry of İnönü University, Türkiye. A total of 600 valid questionnaires were obtained from dental students across all academic years. Data were collected using a 28‐item questionnaire developed based on current literature and expert evaluation (content validity index = 0.88; Cronbach's α = 0.87). Descriptive statistics, χ 2 tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, Kruskal–Wallis tests, and binary logistic regression analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0.ResultsOverall, 82% of students reported being familiar with the concept of sustainability; however, only 28% indicated that sustainability topics were included in their formal education. Students demonstrated generally positive attitudes towards environmentally sustainable dental practises, particularly regarding recycling, energy conservation, and resource management. Significant differences were observed according to gender and year of study in sustainability awareness and perceptions of curriculum integration (p < 0.05).ConclusionAlthough dental students display positive attitudes towards environmental sustainability, the findings suggest that sustainability is not systematically integrated into the dental curriculum. Integrating sustainability into dental education through clearly defined learning outcomes and structured curriculum development may contribute to the training of environmentally responsible oral healthcare professionals.Keywords: dental education, eco‐friendly practises, green dentistry, student awareness, sustainability

Introduction

The global climate crisis and environmental degradation have emerged as among the most serious threats to public health in the contemporary era. The World Health Organization (WHO) identifies climate change as one of the most significant factors threatening human health in the 20‐first century and estimates that, if current trends continue, approximately 250 000 additional deaths per year between 2030 and 2050 may be attributable to climate change–related causes [1]. Paradoxically, the healthcare sector—whose primary mission is to protect and improve human health—accounts for approximately 5% of global greenhouse gas emissions and therefore contributes directly to climate change. Consequently, it is of critical importance that healthcare systems assume a leading role in climate mitigation efforts by reducing their carbon footprint. In this regard, the WHO has published strategic roadmaps aimed at transforming health services into climate‐resilient and low‐carbon systems and encourages healthcare professionals to adopt sustainable practises [2].

As an integral component of healthcare services, dentistry should actively engage in environmental sustainability initiatives. Clinical dental practise involves extensive use of single‐use plastics, chemical agents, and the generation of medical waste, while also requiring substantial consumption of natural resources such as energy and water [3]. Infection control measures intensified during the COVID‐19 pandemic further increased the use of personal protective equipment and disposable materials, thereby markedly expanding the environmental footprint of dental practise. Within this context, the concept of ‘green dentistry,’ which seeks to reduce the ecological impact of the profession, has gained increasing attention [4].

International professional organizations have also issued strategic calls for the adoption of sustainable practises in dentistry. For instance, the ‘Sustainable Dentistry’ policy adopted by the FDI World Dental Federation aims to reduce the environmental impact of oral healthcare services without compromising quality or patient safety. The FDI emphasises that high‐quality, prevention‐oriented oral healthcare can be delivered in an environmentally responsible manner and that dentists can protect both individual health and the future of the planet through ethical and resource‐efficient practises [5]. In line with this objective, the FDI has published guidelines and educational materials aimed at reducing pollution and carbon emissions, thereby promoting global awareness of sustainability within dental practise [3].

In addition, professional initiatives have emerged within dentistry to support the integration of sustainable development goals into daily clinical practise and to promote the transition towards a ‘green economy’ [6]. Within this context, the Minamata Convention, which aims to reduce the environmental harm caused by mercury in dental amalgam restorations, represents a significant global effort to decrease hazardous waste in dentistry. Collectively, these developments clearly demonstrate why environmental sustainability has become a critical priority within the dental profession: the future of dentistry will be defined not only by clinical excellence but also by its commitment to planetary health [7].

Integrating environmental sustainability into dental education represents one of the most effective strategies for preparing future dentists with this awareness. It has been emphasised that environmentally responsible practises should be introduced from the earliest stages of professional training in order to establish a sustainable healthcare culture [8]. Indeed, initiatives to incorporate climate change and sustainable healthcare topics into medical education curricula have gained considerable momentum internationally in recent years [9]. In the field of dentistry, the Association for Dental Education in Europe (ADEE) has emphasised that environmental sustainability should be considered an essential component of modern dental education and has recommended its systematic integration into dental curricula [10]. Studies associated with ADEE further highlight the necessity of structuring sustainability principles within the framework of graduate competencies and learning outcomes [2, 3, 10]. This perspective encourages the reconsideration of dental curricula so that they encompass not only clinical competence but also broader dimensions of environmental and societal responsibility.

In some countries, accreditation bodies have already incorporated sustainable dentistry competencies into graduation requirements. For example, in the United Kingdom, the concept of ‘sustainable dentistry’ has been integrated into professional competency frameworks, while in several European countries and in Australia, environmental sustainability content has been formally incorporated into dental curricula as mandatory coursework [11].

Nevertheless, when considered globally, sustainability cannot yet be regarded as a widely or consistently integrated component of dental education. In particular, the educational infrastructure in many developing countries remains limited. Therefore, it has become increasingly essential for dental faculties to equip students not only with clinical skills but also with environmental awareness [12]. Recent literature indicates that environmental sustainability still occupies a relatively limited place within dental curricula. Reports suggest that the integration of sustainability‐related content in European dental schools remains restricted, and that existing initiatives in this area are often inconsistent and insufficient [13]. In many institutions, sustainability topics are not addressed within a coordinated and long‐term strategic framework; instead, students frequently acquire knowledge through extracurricular sources or individual efforts [14, 15]. Although educators acknowledge the importance of environmentally responsible practises, they often encounter difficulties integrating such content into their courses due to the lack of formal guidance and accreditation requirements [8].

Survey studies conducted in different countries indicate that dental students' awareness of environmental sustainability is generally limited, although their motivation to learn about the topic remains high [8, 14, 16]. For instance, a study conducted across six dental schools in the United Arab Emirates reported that none of the institutions explicitly included sustainability‐related topics in their curricula. Although students were not familiar with the concept, they perceived it as important for their future professional roles and expressed a strong interest in receiving education on the subject. Educators similarly acknowledged that dentistry bears responsibility towards environmental protection and expressed willingness to integrate sustainability‐related content into their teaching. However, barriers such as insufficient knowledge, dense curricula, lack of appropriate educational materials, and time constraints were reported to limit such integration [8]. Likewise, a comparative study conducted in two dental schools in the United Kingdom and the United States found that formal education on sustainability was extremely limited. Nevertheless, both students and faculty members strongly supported the inclusion of sustainability topics in the curriculum. The same study also emphasised structural challenges associated with the integration of such topics, particularly due to intensive teaching schedules and clinical priorities [15].

These findings clearly indicate the need to enhance awareness of environmental sustainability within dental education worldwide. However, current evidence suggests that awareness among both students and educators remains relatively low, while curricular integration is still substantially insufficient. In the Turkish context, only a limited number of studies addressing this issue have been published to date [17].

The present study aims to comprehensively examine environmental sustainability awareness in dental education across multiple dimensions, including knowledge level, attitudes, behavioural tendencies, and perceptions regarding curriculum integration. By providing comprehensive data from Türkiye, this study seeks to contribute a local perspective to the broader discussion on sustainable dental education and to provide a scientific basis for the development of educational strategies aligned with international professional frameworks, including those of the WHO, FDI, and ADEE. In the institution where this study was conducted, environmental sustainability is not offered as an independent course; rather, the topic is addressed indirectly within various courses. Therefore, it was anticipated that students' responses regarding whether sustainability is included in the curriculum would largely reflect their individual perceptions and experiences.

Materials and Methods

This study was designed as a descriptive cross‐sectional investigation aimed at evaluating dental students' levels of environmental sustainability awareness and examining the relationship between this awareness and demographic variables.

Within the undergraduate curriculum of the Faculty of Dentistry at İnönü University, where the study was conducted, environmental sustainability is not included as an independent and structured course. Sustainability‐related content is addressed indirectly and in a fragmented manner within courses such as infection control, medical waste management, public health dentistry, and digital applications. However, there are no explicit learning outcomes specifically dedicated to sustainability, nor is there a standardised educational module on this topic. In the present study, no formal curriculum content analysis or curriculum mapping was conducted. Therefore, findings regarding whether sustainability is included in the curriculum are based primarily on students' perceptions and self‐reported experiences rather than on an institutional content analysis.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Inönü University Health Sciences Ethics Committee prior to the commencement of the study (Decision No: 2025/7832). The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Before the questionnaire was administered, participants were informed about the purpose of the study. It was clearly stated that participation was entirely voluntary and that declining to participate would not result in any academic consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The questionnaires were administered anonymously; no identifying information such as names, student identification numbers, or other personal identifiers was collected. Participants completed the questionnaires individually. During the administration process, the course instructor responsible for the class was not present in the environment in order to minimise potential authority effects and response pressure. All data were stored in a secure environment accessible only to the research team, and all analyses were conducted using anonymised data.

Study Population and Data Collection Process

The study was conducted between March and May 2025 at the Faculty of Dentistry, İnönü University. The study population consisted of 650 students enrolled in the first through fifth academic years of the dental program.

Data were collected using printed questionnaires that were distributed face‐to‐face by the researchers and completed individually by the participants. A total of 612 students participated in the survey (94.2%). Twelve questionnaires containing incomplete or inconsistent responses were excluded from the analysis, and the final analyses were conducted using 600 valid questionnaires. This number represents 92.3% of the total student population.

The distribution of the sample according to gender and academic year was compared with the faculty's official student registration records, and no statistically significant difference was found between the sample and the target population (χ 2 test, p > 0.05). This finding indicates that the sample was representative of the study population (Table 1).

Sample Size

The minimum required sample size for the study was calculated using an a priori power analysis conducted with G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). Assuming a medium effect size (w = 0.20) for χ 2 tests, a significance level of α = 0.05, and 95% statistical power (1 − β = 0.95), the minimum required sample size was determined to be 384 participants.

Questionnaire Development and Validity–Reliability

The data collection instrument was developed by the researchers based on current literature on sustainability, environmentally friendly practises, and dental education.

The questionnaire was prepared and administered in Turkish. The language of instruction at the faculty where the study was conducted is Turkish, and no international or English‐language educational program is offered. Therefore, it was assumed that all participants were able to understand the questionnaire items at a native‐language level. The English version of the questionnaire used in this study is provided in File S1.

The draft questionnaire was evaluated for content validity through expert review by five academics. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated for each item, and the average CVI value was 0.88.

A pilot study was conducted with 50 students enrolled in another dental faculty in Türkiye who were not included in the main study sample. During the pilot phase, the clarity and linguistic comprehensibility of the items were assessed, and necessary revisions were made accordingly.

The overall internal consistency of the scale was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha, yielding a coefficient of α = 0.87 for the total scale. Internal consistency analyses were also conducted for the Likert‐type subdimensions. Cronbach's alpha values were 0.81 for the Knowledge subscale, 0.85 for Attitudes, 0.79 for Behaviours, and 0.83 for Educational Evaluation. These values indicate that the subscales demonstrated acceptable to good levels of internal consistency.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 600 dental students participated in the study. Of the participants, 60.2% were female (n = 361) and 38.7% were male (n = 232), while 1.1% (n = 7) preferred not to disclose their gender. A balanced distribution was achieved across all academic years (Year 1: 20.4%; Year 2: 18.9%; Year 3: 15.4%; Year 4: 24.2%; Year 5: 21.2%). The detailed distribution of the participants is presented in Table 1.

Knowledge Level

Conceptual Awareness

A total of 82.3% of the participants reported that they had previously heard the term ‘sustainability.’ Among students familiar with the concept, social media was identified as the most frequently reported source of information (62.5%). This was followed by university courses and television/news media (both 30.2%). Other reported sources of information included family or friends (19.3%), activities organized by non‐governmental organizations (10.5%), and other sources (16.6%) (Figure 1).

Importance of Sustainability in Dentistry and Conceptual Associations

A total of 75.0% of the students reported that sustainability is important for dentistry, and 52.0% of these respondents rated it as ‘very important.’ Approximately 20.0% of the participants remained undecided on this issue, whereas fewer than 4.0% stated that sustainability is not important for dentistry.

When students were asked about the concepts that first come to mind when they think of sustainability, the most frequently mentioned association was recycling (51.3%), followed by the conservation of natural resources (39.2%). In addition, a considerable proportion of students associated sustainability with reducing the carbon footprint (25.4%), waste management (23.5%), and energy conservation (22.9%). 10.4% of the participants did not report any concept associated with sustainability (Figure 2).

Attitudes and Behaviours

The majority of students expressed support for sustainable clinical practises. The highest levels of agreement were observed for statements regarding the presence of recycling bins in clinics (mean = 4.65), avoiding leaving energy‐consuming devices running unnecessarily (mean = 4.50), monitoring and controlling material stock (mean = 4.52), training auxiliary staff (mean = 4.48), and the use of solar panels (mean = 4.51). The mean values for these items exceeded 4, indicating that participants generally demonstrated positive attitudes towards sustainable clinical practises.

Relatively lower mean scores were observed for the statements ‘Grouping patient appointments may help reduce fuel consumption’ (mean = 3.22) and ‘Reducing the use of single‐use products’ (mean = 3.71). These values do not indicate strong disagreement but rather reflect response distributions concentrated between neutral and moderate agreement. As presented in the questionnaire, the statement regarding grouping patient appointments refers to scheduling visits on the same day or consecutively whenever possible in order to reduce unnecessary repeat travel. This finding suggests that the environmental benefits of organizational planning strategies may be perceived as less visible by students compared with direct clinical practises.

This relatively lower level of support may therefore be associated with the fact that sustainability is perceived as more tangible when linked to direct clinical behaviours, whereas organizational or system‐level strategies may appear less visible. Previous literature indicates that students tend to show stronger support for practises they perceive as part of their individual sphere of responsibility, while system‐level arrangements are often viewed as institutional responsibilities [18]. This pattern suggests that awareness of sustainability may vary depending on the perceived domain of application.

For the statement ‘Environmental impact of antibiotic use,’ the mean score was 4.03, indicating a generally high level of awareness. However, the fact that approximately one‐third of the participants selected the neutral option suggests that the conceptual understanding of this issue may not have been equally internalised by all respondents. This finding may indicate that knowledge regarding the environmental dimension of sustainability is distributed heterogeneously among students.

Educational Evaluation

Educational and Knowledge Expectations

A total of 28% of the participants reported that sustainability‐related topics are included in their educational programs. This proportion reflects students' subjective perceptions. Meanwhile, 47% of students stated that such topics were not included in the curriculum, and 25% indicated that they were unsure.

When the type of educational content was evaluated across the entire sample, 23% of students reported that sustainability topics were addressed within theoretical courses, whereas 5% indicated that these topics were covered within practical training.

A majority of the students (66%) stated that sustainability‐related topics should be incorporated into the dental curriculum. Regarding their preferred modes of learning, 35% of students indicated that this education should be delivered through formal curriculum‐based courses, while 30% preferred seminars or additional training programs. In contrast, 17% of the participants reported that they did not wish to receive additional education on this topic, and 17% remained undecided.

When perceptions of the sources of environmental harm were examined, the most frequently reported factor was the increase in medical waste (36%). This was followed by the use of single‐use plastics (21%) and mercury in amalgam restorations (15%) (Figure 3).

Discussion

The findings of the present study indicate that dental students' knowledge, attitudes, and educational expectations regarding environmental sustainability are shaped at different levels. Although students are generally familiar with the concept, the fact that their associations with sustainability are predominantly limited to the environmental dimension suggests that the economic and social aspects of the concept receive comparatively less emphasis.

In the present study, gender‐related differences were observed for certain variables, whereas no statistically significant differences were identified across academic years with respect to overall sustainability awareness and total attitude scores. Female students demonstrated higher levels of sustainability awareness and more positive attitudes towards environmental responsibility compared with male students. This finding is consistent with previous studies reported in the literature. For example, research conducted in Indonesia reported that female students exhibited higher levels of environmental knowledge and more environmentally responsible behaviours, and studies in the healthcare field have similarly shown that women tend to demonstrate greater awareness regarding medical waste management [19, 20]. However, it would not be appropriate to attribute the observed gender differences to a single sociocultural explanation. Environmental attitudes may be shaped by multiple factors, including individuals' socialisation processes, educational experiences, and levels of exposure to environmental issues. Therefore, the observed difference may be related more to the sociocultural interaction networks to which students are exposed rather than to inherent individual characteristics. Moreover, such demographic differences should not be interpreted deterministically. Environmental attitudes are multidimensional constructs shaped by contextual influences; consequently, the differences observed in this study may reflect the interaction of cultural, educational, and institutional factors.

Although some studies in the literature suggest that sustainability awareness tends to increase with advancing years of education [21, 22], no statistically significant differences were found between academic years in terms of overall sustainability awareness or total attitude scores in the present study. Nevertheless, differences related to academic year were observed in perceptions regarding the presence of sustainability content in the curriculum and in certain specific practise‐related items. This pattern may be associated with the greater exposure of clinical‐year students to curricular content. These findings suggest that general attitude levels and curriculum‐related perception variables should be considered separately. While overall conceptual awareness appears to follow a similar pattern across academic years, perception‐based variables associated with experience may show greater variation among clinical students. This observation may indicate the importance of addressing sustainability education through a more explicit and structured educational approach. The absence of a formally structured and progressively integrated sustainability component within the curriculum of the institution where the study was conducted may partly explain this pattern. In many cases, increases in awareness across academic years are reported in programs where sustainability‐related content is systematically integrated; therefore, our findings may reflect the influence of structural characteristics of the curriculum.

Similarly, studies conducted in the United Kingdom and the United States have reported that students generally demonstrate positive attitudes towards environmental sustainability, while the absence of systematic and structured curricular content leads to variability in knowledge and experience [15, 18]. Reports published by the Association for Dental Education in Europe (ADEE) also emphasise that sustainability themes should be integrated into dental curricula from the early years and structured within the framework of clearly defined learning outcomes [10, 23]. Within this context, demographic differences observed in the present study may be partially associated with structural elements of institutional curriculum design.

Although the findings of the present study indicate that students demonstrate a relatively high level of familiarity with the concept of sustainability, the results suggest that this familiarity may not necessarily translate into a deep and comprehensive internalisation of the concept. In the context of this study, environmental awareness is defined as individuals' capacity to recognise and understand environmental problems and to comprehend their broader impacts on society and the environment [24]. Accordingly, environmental awareness was evaluated through students' familiarity with the concept of sustainability and their attitudes towards environmentally responsible practises.

Despite the increasing visibility of sustainability in public discourse, formal education does not always appear to facilitate a deeper conceptual understanding of the topic. Studies conducted among health sciences students similarly indicate that merely having heard a concept does not necessarily reflect an adequate level of knowledge [8, 25]. Consequently, awareness of sustainability often remains limited to recognition of the concept and a set of restricted associations, rather than reflecting a comprehensive understanding of its multidimensional structure encompassing environmental, economic, and social dimensions. It should also be noted that broader behavioural aspects, such as lifestyle changes, consumption patterns, or environmental advocacy, were not directly measured within the scope of this study.

Gender‐related differences observed in conceptual associations are also noteworthy. Female students tended to emphasise waste‐related environmental issues, whereas male students more frequently referred to resource management. This pattern suggests that different dimensions of sustainability may be prioritised through distinct interpretative frameworks by individuals. However, these differences should not be attributed solely to individual characteristics; rather, they may be associated with broader social discourses and experiential contexts. This observation further highlights the importance of structuring sustainability education within a comprehensive and inclusive framework.

The fact that the present study was conducted in a single dental faculty in Türkiye and included students from all academic years provides an opportunity to examine the position of sustainability within the educational process from a more structural perspective. Taken together, the findings suggest that although positive attitudes and general awareness regarding sustainability are present, these attributes may not necessarily evolve into deep knowledge and professional competence unless they are supported by clearly defined learning outcomes and systematic instructional strategies within the curriculum.

The findings of the present study indicate that students generally demonstrate positive attitudes towards sustainable dental practises. Participants showed strong support for measures such as recycling, energy and water conservation, and reducing the use of single‐use materials, suggesting that sustainability is increasingly recognised as a normative professional value within dental practise. However, it is noteworthy that these positive attitudes do not appear to translate into behavioural engagement or academic initiatives to the same extent. A similar divergence has been reported in the literature, where students acknowledge sustainability as an important professional responsibility but show limited involvement in independent learning or research activities related to the topic [15, 18].

This phenomenon is frequently described in the literature as the **‘intention–action gap’** [26]. Individuals may develop environmentally responsible attitudes; however, when opportunities for participation in decision‐making processes, practical implementation, and institutional support mechanisms are limited, these attitudes may not readily translate into behavioural outcomes. The findings of the present study similarly suggest that students tend to accept sustainability as a normative value, yet the translation of this value into sustained professional practise may remain limited when it is not supported by systematic and structured educational strategies. This discrepancy may also be related to the tendency of students to demonstrate stronger support for practises perceived to fall within their individual sphere of control, such as the use of recycling bins or switching off equipment, while considering organizational or system‐level arrangements, such as appointment scheduling, to be primarily administrative responsibilities [18]. For this reason, the integration of sustainability into the dental curriculum should extend beyond knowledge transmission and include practical behavioural objectives embedded within clinical workflows, supported by institutional measures such as waste segregation protocols or sustainable procurement policies.

Within the European context, sustainability practises in dental education have also been reported to remain largely fragmented and voluntary, rather than constituting a systematic and standardised component of the curriculum in most faculties [10]. Accordingly, the positive attitudes observed in the present study may reflect a value‐based acceptance of sustainability among students; however, unless this acceptance is integrated into institutional structures and curriculum design, it may have limited impact on behavioural and structural transformation.

Indeed, a pan‐European study conducted under the auspices of ADEE reported that educational initiatives related to environmental sustainability remain relatively limited across dental schools [10]. A more recent special interest group report further emphasised that the integration of sustainability into dental education in Europe is still at an early stage, with substantial heterogeneity observed among institutions [23].

From an educational perspective, the fact that a substantial proportion of students indicated that sustainability should be integrated more systematically into the curriculum suggests that the current educational framework may not be perceived as sufficient. At the same time, findings indicating that the intensity of the curriculum in certain academic years may hinder the prioritisation of sustainability topics demonstrate that integration should not be addressed solely through the addition of new content. Rather, it should be considered together with broader curriculum planning and scheduling strategies. This observation underscores the importance of designing sustainability education not as an isolated module but as an integrated component of the overall educational program.

The institutional context in which the study was conducted is also important when interpreting these findings. At the Faculty of Dentistry of İnönü University, environmental sustainability is not offered as an independent and structured course; instead, related topics are addressed indirectly and in a fragmented manner within different courses. This structure may help explain the variability in students' perceptions regarding whether sustainability is included in the curriculum and may also account for the finding that students in clinical years appear to have encountered such content more frequently.

A similar pattern has been reported in the international literature. Studies conducted in different countries indicate that both students and academics perceive sustainability as insufficiently structured within dental curricula [8, 27]. In Europe as well, considerable variation exists among faculties, and in ADEE documents sustainability is generally addressed only indirectly within the framework of professional responsibility [10]. Recent reviews have further highlighted the lack of standardised learning objectives and the limited level of institutional support for sustainability education [11]. Within this context, the findings of the present study may reflect not only a local situation but also a broader educational pattern described in the literature.

Evidence from an intervention study conducted at Michigan State University demonstrated that students who received sustainability education through formal coursework achieved higher levels of knowledge than those who relied primarily on individual learning sources [24]. This finding further supports the critical role of formal education in strengthening sustainability‐related knowledge and competencies.

Students' identification of medical waste, single‐use plastics, and amalgam waste as the primary contributors to environmental impact in dental practise suggests that a basic awareness of key environmental risk areas is present. This perception is consistent with the major sources of environmental burden described in the literature [28]. However, considering the contribution of the global healthcare sector to the overall carbon footprint and the role of dentistry in this context through energy consumption and intensive material use [29, 30, 31], merely recognising environmental risks is not sufficient to ensure meaningful environmental responsibility.

Although students in the present study were able to identify these environmental challenges, their knowledge regarding waste management practises appears to remain limited. Previous studies in Türkiye have reported that approximately half of dental students lack knowledge about the proper disposal of amalgam waste. Similarly, studies conducted in different countries have identified gaps in dental students' knowledge regarding biomedical waste management [32, 33, 34]. This pattern suggests that a disconnect may exist between the recognition of environmental problems and the internalisation of effective solution mechanisms.

In the literature, a range of ‘green dentistry’ strategies have been proposed to address these issues, including the use of amalgam separators, digital radiography, sterilizable materials, and water‐saving suction systems [35]. In fact, the use of amalgam separators has been regulated in some countries through legislation aligned with the Minamata Convention, and the gradual reduction of dental amalgam use is being promoted internationally [7]. Likewise, digital radiography has been widely adopted in many countries due to its ability to reduce chemical waste associated with conventional imaging methods [28]. However, the extent to which such practises are addressed as competency‐based and systematically defined learning outcomes within dental education programs varies across countries. Consequently, although sustainability principles have gained some presence in clinical practise, a globally standardised educational framework for sustainability integration has not yet been fully established.

In the Turkish context, national policies promoting waste management and recycling practises within healthcare institutions have increased in recent years. In particular, the ‘Zero Waste’ initiative, launched in 2017, has been widely implemented across public institutions with the aim of promoting source‐based waste separation, increasing recycling rates, and supporting environmental sustainability [36]. This policy framework indicates that sustainability has become an important topic not only at the level of clinical practise but also within institutional and administrative agendas.

However, the extent to which such national initiatives are integrated into undergraduate dental curricula through competency‐based and measurable learning outcomes remains unclear. This situation highlights the need to evaluate the alignment between policy frameworks, curricular structures, and clinical practises.

The fact that the present study was conducted in a single public university suggests that the findings may have been influenced by institutional culture, local curriculum design, and administrative priorities. Therefore, comparing these findings with educational practises implemented in different dental faculties may provide a clearer understanding of the structural characteristics of sustainability education.

This study has certain limitations. First, the research was conducted in a single dental faculty; therefore, the results cannot be directly generalised to students from other universities or countries. Furthermore, data were collected through self‐reported questionnaires, which may have led participants to overreport socially desirable attitudes and behaviours (social desirability bias). Nevertheless, the inclusion of students from multiple academic years and the interpretation of the findings within the context of existing literature enhance the interpretability of the results. Future research employing multi‐center designs and mixed‐method approaches supported by qualitative data would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of sustainability awareness in dental education.

Conclusions

This study evaluated dental students' levels of knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding environmental sustainability within the context of dental education. The findings indicate that students are largely familiar with the concept of sustainability and generally demonstrate positive attitudes towards environmentally responsible practises. However, sustainability was predominantly perceived through its environmental dimension, and students' perceptions regarding its presence within the curriculum appeared to be inconsistent.

These findings highlight the need for sustainability to be supported within dental education through clearly defined learning outcomes and structured curricular content. Future research employing multi‐center and mixed‐method study designs would contribute to a more comprehensive evaluation of the scope and impact of sustainability education in dental training.
