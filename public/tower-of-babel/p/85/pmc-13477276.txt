EAT‐Lancet Diet, Plasma Metabolites, and Risk of Peripheral Artery Disease: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Zhenao Zhang, Jingyi Ren, Xiaoya Sun, Siqi Zhu, Yuetong Yang, Yuxia Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Planetary Health Diet Index, developed by the EAT‐Lancet Commission, promotes sustainable and healthy eating, yet its association with peripheral artery disease (PAD) remains underexplored. Furthermore, the metabolic mechanisms potentially linking this dietary pattern to PAD warrant deeper investigation.MethodsIn 192 166 UKB (UK Biobank) participants with 24‐hour dietary recall, Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between the EAT‐Lancet diet index and PAD. In a subcohort (n=93 961), diet‐related metabolites were identified via elastic net regression to construct a metabolic signature, which was analyzed for its association with PAD risk and potential mediating role.ResultsOver a median follow‐up of 10.55 years, 1579 PAD events occurred. Higher adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet was associated with a lower PAD risk (hazard ratio [HR], 0.74 [95% CI, 0.62–0.87]), consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses. Sixty metabolites formed the diet‐related metabolic signature, inversely associated with PAD (HR, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.50–0.88]) and mediating 12.9% (95% CI, 4.6%–28.5%) of the diet's protective effect, with mediating metabolites mainly enriched in the fatty acids subclass.ConclusionsGreater adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet is associated with a lower risk of PAD, with its metabolic signature potentially mediating this relationship, underscoring the importance of dietary interventions and metabolic monitoring for PAD prevention.

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is the third most prevalent clinical expression of atherosclerosis, affecting ≈230 million adults worldwide. 1 Its main feature is the cholesterol accumulation in the arterial intima, resulting in stenosis or occlusion anywhere from the iliac arteries to the pedal arteries. 2 , 3 This significantly increases the likelihood of cardiovascular issues and limb‐related problems, such as amputation. 4 Despite its prevalence and clinical importance, PAD remains underrecognized, with less targeted research and awareness than other atherosclerotic conditions. 5 Consequently, developing strategies to enhance PAD prevention is critical.

Diet is a primary controllable risk factor for preventing global PAD. Multiple studies have shown that consuming foods rich in specific nutrients (such as flavonoids) or adopting a healthy dietary pattern is associated with a reduced incidence of PAD. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 However, the dietary indices adopted in these studies often overlooked the impact on the environment. 10 , 11 In contrast, a global health reference diet, known as the EAT‐Lancet diet, was introduced by the EAT‐Lancet Commission in 2019. 12 This dietary pattern fully considers the complex relationships among health, nutritional intake, and environmental factors, aiming to enhance human well‐being while ensuring the sustainable development of the planet. 12 Overall, this diet encourages a plant‐based diet as the main one, emphasizing a higher consumption of vegetables, whole grains, and fruits, while decreasing the consumption of red meat, sugary foods, and saturated fats. 13 Numerous studies have shown that adherence to this dietary model is linked to a decreased incidence of various diseases and lower overall mortality rates. 11 , 14 , 15 However, as far as we know, no studies have yet explored the connection between this diet and PAD.

The emergence of high‐throughput metabolomics has greatly expanded opportunities to explore the intricate interplay between diet and disease. Dietary patterns may affect disease risk through alterations in metabolic pathways. 16 A growing body of research has identified specific plasma metabolites as potential therapeutic targets to attenuate the pathological development of PAD. 17 , 18 Accordingly, adherence to the EAT‐Lancet reference diet may contribute to a reduced risk of PAD through systemic metabolic alterations. Yet, the precise metabolic mechanisms linking this dietary pattern to PAD have not been fully characterized.

Therefore, this study, leveraging extensive prospective phenotypic data from the UKB (UK Biobank) together with population‐scale metabolomics profiles, was designed with 2 primary objectives: (1) to examine whether adherence to the EAT‐Lancet reference diet is associated with a lower risk of newly diagnosed PAD, and (2) to identify metabolic signatures related to this dietary pattern and assess their mediating role in this association. By integrating prospective cohort and metabolomic analyses, this study aimed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the biological pathways linking diet to PAD risk.

Methods

Data Availability

This study was based on data from the UK Biobank under Application Number 471488. The data are available to researchers through the standard application process. The UKB has received ethical approval from the North West Multicentre Research Ethics Committee (REC reference: 11/NW/0382) to collect and distribute participant data and biological samples. The code used in this study is available from the corresponding authors upon reasonable request.

Study Design

The UKB, an extensive longitudinal cohort study, recruited >500 000 individuals from 22 assessment centers throughout Scotland, England, and Wales from 2006 to 2010 via mailed invitations. Ethical approval for this research was acquired from the North West Multicentre Research Ethics Committee. Details of the UKB have been recorded in previous studies. 19 , 20 All individuals provided written informed consent to participate.

Individuals who filled out at least 1 full online 24‐hour dietary recall survey were eligible for inclusion in this study. After exclusion of participants lost to follow‐up before dietary recall, those with prevalent cardiovascular disease or PAD before dietary recall, and those with unrealistic energy consumption (men: <800 or >4200 kcal/day; women: <500 or >3500 kcal/day), a cohort of 192 166 individuals was used for the principal investigation. Of these, 93 961 individuals were included in metabolomic analyses (Figure S1).

EAT‐Lancet Diet Index Definition

Dietary data were collected using the Oxford WebQ, a validated tool designed for 24‐hour dietary assessments in large‐scale prospective studies. 21 A commonly used food list, comprising 206 foods and 32 beverages, was presented to participants to report their intake over the previous 24 hours. More information can be found at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=100090. A total of 210 810 individuals submitted at least 1 dietary survey. For individuals who completed several dietary evaluations, the mean values of these assessments were used to calculate the EAT‐Lancet diet index score.

Drawing on the food guidelines suggested by the EAT‐Lancet Commission, 12 this study adopted 3 different methods to construct the EAT‐Lancet diet index score to ensure the robustness of the results. 14 The Knuppel EAT‐Lancet diet index was among the first and most extensively applied indices for evaluating health outcomes. 15 The Stubbendorff EAT‐Lancet diet index, which evaluates each component on an ordinal scale aligned with the EAT‐Lancet reference diet guidelines, was also integrated. This enables a more thorough assessment of adherence to the EAT‐Lancet dietary compliance. 22 , 23 The Kesse–Guyot EAT‐Lancet diet index, a continuous proportional scoring framework based on the components and thresholds established by Knuppel et al, was derived through a particular equation, effectively capturing interindividual variability. 24 Higher scores across all 3 indices indicate greater adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet. Consistent with previous literature, the newly developed Kesse–Guyot EAT‐Lancet index was selected for the main analysis, 25 while the other 2 methods were included in sensitivity analyses. Detailed methodologies for calculating the 3 dietary indices are provided in previous literature, while conversions for portion sizes in grams and the cutoff values for each food item are detailed in Tables S1 through S3. 25

Outcome Assessment

In this study, the outcome was defined as PAD. PAD events were identified by linking participants’ medical histories to hospital admissions and mortality records within the UKB. Diagnoses were classified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD‐10: I700, I7000, I7001, I702, I7020, I7021, I708, I7080, I709, I7090, I738, I739) and the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Surgical Operations and Procedures, Fourth Version (OPCS‐4: X093, X094, X095, L216, L513, L516, L518, L521, L522, L541, L544, L548, L591, L592, L593, L594, L595, L596, L597, L598, L601, L602, L631, L635, L639, L667), consistent with definitions used in previous studies. 26 , 27

Metabolomics Measurement

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy of plasma samples was conducted for ≈280 000 participants in the UKB between 2019 and 2020. The UKB provides comprehensive online documentation describing the corresponding quality control and analytical protocols. Altogether, 251 metabolic biomarkers were assessed, including 170 representing absolute concentrations and 81 representing metabolite ratios, covering fatty acids, lipoprotein subclasses, amino acids, ketone bodies, and other metabolites. Comprehensive details on each biomarker are provided in Table S4. Technical variability in the data was adjusted using the R package “UKBNMR” 28 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Metabolite values were processed using rank‐based inverse normal transformation and standard scaling. 28 , 29

Assessment of Covariates

Possible confounding variables were determined on the basis of previous research. 14 , 26 Apart from overall energy consumption, assessed through a 24‐hour diet recall survey, all other covariates were derived from baseline data. These encompassed age (continuous), sex (female, male), education level (college/university degree or professional qualifications, others), family income (<£31 000 or ≥£31 000), race and ethnicity (White, others [Mixed, Asian or Asian British, Black or Black British, Chinese, and other ethnic groups]), physical activity (high, moderate, low), alcohol consumption (current, former, never), smoking status (current, former, never), Townsend deprivation index (continuous), diabetes (no, yes), hypertension (no, yes), family history of cardiovascular disease (no, yes), body mass index (continuous), lipid profile, and use of lipid‐lowering medication (no, yes). A history of hypertension was defined as the presence of an ICD‐coded diagnosis before the final date of dietary assessment, use of antihypertensive medication, or systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg. A history of diabetes was defined as the use of glucose‐lowering medication, a hospital diagnosis of diabetes, or glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5%. Missing covariate data were imputed using the random forest–based missForest algorithm. The proportions of missing covariate data are reported in Table S5.

Statistical Analysis

The EAT‐Lancet diet score was divided into quintiles to facilitate statistical evaluation. 30 Continuous baseline traits were presented as means±SDs or medians (interquartile ranges), whereas categorical traits were expressed in counts (proportions). The duration of follow‐up was calculated from the completion of a participant's final 24‐hour dietary recall until the occurrence of the outcome, death, loss to follow‐up, or the conclusion of the follow‐up period (May 31, 2022, for Wales, August 31, 2022, for Scotland; and October 31, 2022, for England), whichever occurred first.

To assess the relationship between the EAT‐Lancet diet score and PAD risk, multivariable Cox regression models were used to compute hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% CIs. Robust standard errors were used in all Cox proportional hazards models. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, with no breaches detected across the study (P for global >0.05). Using the lowest quintile as the reference, 3 models were fitted: The first model was unadjusted; the second model was adjusted for age, sex, Townsend deprivation index, family income, education level, race and ethnicity, total energy intake, and body mass index; and the third model additionally accounted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, family history of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, lipid profile, and lipid‐lowering medication use. Trend P values were determined by treating the EAT‐Lancet diet score categories as an ordinal variable. A restricted cubic spline model with 4 knots was fitted within a Cox regression framework, adjusted as in the third model to explore the dose–response relationship between the EAT‐Lancet diet score and PAD. Additionally, Cox regression models were fitted for each component in relation to the outcome to evaluate the contributions of individual food components.

Multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed to assess the reliability of the study results. For subgroup analyses, stratification was performed on the basis of age, sex, and Townsend deprivation index. The interactions between the EAT‐Lancet diet score and these stratification factors on incident PAD were also assessed. To address the potential collinearity among metabolites and construct the metabolic signature of the EAT‐Lancet diet index in the metabolomics analysis, elastic net regression was used. This regularization technique integrates L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) regularization terms to alleviate overfitting in the model. 32 The optimal λ parameter was identified through 10‐fold cross‐validation, using the mean squared error for prediction of the EAT‐Lancet diet index as the optimization criterion. The largest λ value within 1 standard error of the minimum mean squared error was selected. Biomarkers with nonzero regression coefficients were weighted by their respective coefficients and summed to construct a metabolic signature score, which was standardized using Z scores (mean, 0±1). Subsequently, linear regression analyses were additionally conducted to investigate associations between the selected individual metabolites and the EAT‐Lancet diet. Multivariable‐adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the relationship between the metabolic signature, specific metabolites, and incident PAD, and statistical significance was ascertained applying a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff below 0.05. Additionally, restricted cubic spline models with 4 knots were fitted to examine potential nonlinear effects linking the metabolic profile and PAD risk. Finally, mediation analysis was conducted using a counterfactual‐based mediation framework implemented in the R package “CMAverse” to evaluate the mediating roles of the composite metabolic signature score and individual metabolites in the association between the EAT‐Lancet diet index and PAD risk. The EAT‐Lancet diet index was treated as the exposure, metabolic markers as mediators, and incident PAD as the outcome. The mediating effect was quantified using the proportion mediated. The analysis assumes no unmeasured exposure–mediator, mediator–outcome, or exposure–outcome confounding, no exposure‐induced mediator–outcome confounding, and correct model specification. Given the observational design, mediation findings were interpreted as exploratory.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0. Statistical tests were 2‐sided, and a P value of <0.05 was the criterion for statistical significance.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Individuals

Our study involved 192 166 participants, with an average baseline age of 55.7±7.9 years, of whom 82 585 (43.0%) were men. A total of 1579 (0.8%) participants developed PAD during a median follow‐up duration of 10.55 (interquartile range, 10.44–11.33) years. Table 1 details the participants' baseline characteristics. It was observed that participants with higher scores in the EAT‐Lancet diet index were prone to be women and better educated, tended to abstain from alcohol consumption and smoking, had a lower risk of hypertension and diabetes, engaged in elevated physical activity, and showed reduced total energy intake and body mass index.

EAT‐Lancet Diet Index and Its Relationship With PAD Risk

Increasing alignment with the EAT‐Lancet diet score was associated with a substantially reduced risk of PAD, with consistent results observed across all models presented in Table 2. In the fully adjusted analysis, HR for risk of PAD in the group with the highest adherence, versus the lowest group, was calculated as 0.74 (95% CI, 0.62–0.87; P for trend<0.001). Moreover, with each SD increment in the diet index, PAD risk was reduced by 11% (HR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.84–0.93]; P<0.001), as illustrated in Figure 1A and Table 2. No evidence of a nonlinear association between the EAT‐Lancet diet index and PAD was identified in the dose–response assessment (P for nonlinear=0.774, P for overall=0.005; Figure 1B). The relationship between individual components of the diet index and risk of PAD is detailed in Table S6. After FDR correction, a notable reduction in PAD incidence was linked to higher nut consumption (HR, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.79–0.95]; P FDR=0.037). Additionally, when contrasted with high consumption levels, moderate intake of red meat (HR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.98–1.00]; P FDR=0.040) and added sugars (HR, 0.67 [95% CI, 0.50–0.90]; P FDR=0.040) were strongly associated with a decreased PAD risk. However, no significant associations were observed for the remaining components.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Subgroup analyses examining the association between EAT‐Lancet dietary compliance and PAD are presented in Table S7. The results indicate that the association between compliance with the EAT‐Lancet diet and risk of PAD was consistent across age, sex, and socioeconomic status, with no significant interactions observed (P for interaction>0.05).

To ensure the reliability of our findings, multiple sensitivity analyses were performed. After the EAT‐Lancet diet index was recalculated using the computational approaches described by Knuppel and Stubbendorff, the results remained consistent with those of the main analysis (Tables S8 and S9). After excluding participants with only one 24‐hour dietary recall (n=75 513) and those diagnosed with PAD within the first 2 years of follow‐up (n=143), we repeated the analysis and found consistent results (Tables S10 and S11). Moreover, when estimated glomerular filtration rate was additionally included as a covariate, the results remained robust (Table S12). Similar results were also observed when alternative approaches for handling missing covariate data were applied, including complete‐case analysis, multiple imputation by chained equations, and inverse probability–weighted complete‐case analysis (Tables S13–S15).

Metabolic Signature in Response to EAT‐Lancet Diet

The metabolic signature associated with the EAT‐Lancet diet was defined as a combination of 60 nonzero coefficients selected via an optimized elastic net regression model (Figure 2), encompassing a diverse range of metabolites, including lipoprotein subclasses, fatty acids, lipids, ketone bodies, amino acids, and other metabolites (Table S16). Among these, the strongest positive contributors to the metabolic signature were in the following order: the percentages of polyunsaturated fatty acids to total fatty acids (PUFA%), and linoleic acid to total fatty acids. In contrast, the percentage of saturated fatty acids to total fatty acids contributed most prominently to the negative coefficients of the metabolic signature (Figure S2).

Metabolic Signature and Its Relationship With PAD Risk

After adjustment for covariates, 21 metabolites within the diet‐related metabolic signature were significantly associated with PAD risk. Among these, glycoprotein acetyls and the ratio of triglycerides to total lipids in large low‐density lipoprotein particles showed significant positive associations with PAD, whereas albumin, PUFA%, and percentage of linoleic acid to total fatty acids were inversely associated with the disease (Figure 2). In further analyses examining the link between the EAT‐Lancet diet–related metabolic signature and PAD risk, we observed that participants in the highest quartile of the metabolic signature had ≈34% lower risk of PAD compared with those in the lowest quartile (HR, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.50–0.88]; Table S17). Results of the restricted cubic spline analysis further confirmed a linear association between the EAT‐Lancet diet–related metabolic signature and the risk of PAD (P for nonlinear=0.491, P for overall=0.003; Figure S3).

Mediation Analysis

In the mediation analysis, we evaluated the roles of the metabolic signature, constructed from the 60 selected metabolites, and individual metabolites in the association between the diet and PAD risk. The metabolic signature was found to mediate 12.9% of this association (95% CI, 4.6%–28.5%). Moreover, among the 60 metabolites comprising the metabolic signature, 19 metabolites significantly mediated the relationship between the diet index and PAD risk and were primarily enriched in the fatty acids subclass (Figure 3 and Figure S4).

Discussion

In this prospective population‐based cohort, higher adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet corresponded to a markedly reduced risk of PAD. We also identified a diet‐related metabolic signature that played a key mediating role in the association between the diet index and reduced PAD risk.

This study appears to be the first of its kind to systematically evaluate the association between the EAT‐Lancet diet index and the risk of PAD. The EAT‐Lancet diet is plant based and similar to the Mediterranean diet, aiming to promote human health while driving the sustainable development of the environment, and an independent analysis also confirmed the pivotal contribution of the EAT‐Lancet reference diet to reducing greenhouse gas emissions and curtailing water and land usage. 12 Previously, a number of studies have used different calculation methods to explore the connection between the EAT‐Lancet diet index and cardiovascular disease, encompassing ischemic heart disease, stroke, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure. 15 , 30 However, the relevant research on PAD remains limited. Unlike prior studies that mainly focused on isolated nutritional factors or circulating biomarkers in relation to broader cardiovascular outcomes, 33 our study specifically examined PAD and integrated dietary pattern analysis with metabolomic evidence. Our research provides new evidence on the relationship between the EAT‐Lancet diet index and PAD risk. These findings align with previous research demonstrating that dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet and anti‐inflammatory diets, are linked to a diminished PAD risk. 6 , 34 In summary, the EAT‐Lancet reference diet is regarded as a vital instrument, which can not only promote the sustainable development of humanity and the Earth, but also has the potential to effectively reduce the risk of PAD.

Currently, various approaches are available for calculating the EAT‐Lancet diet index. In our research, 3 distinct scoring standards and different food group classification techniques were applied for analysis. In contrast with the binary system of the Knuppel EAT‐Lancet diet index and the ordinal framework of the Stubbendorff EAT‐Lancet diet index, Kesse–Guyot's definition was selected for the primary analysis, as individual variations are better captured by this method, consistent with prior studies. 25 The remaining 2 approaches were incorporated into sensitivity analyses. All 3 indicators were consistent and established a robust link between adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet and a reduced incidence of PAD. In terms of the diet index components, we found that higher nut consumption and moderate red meat intake were primarily responsible for the inverse relationship between the EAT‐Lancet diet and PAD risk. This observation was verified more in the early studies. 35 To conclude, the EAT‐Lancet diet not only supports cardiovascular health but also plays a crucial role in reducing the risk of PAD, underscoring its significance as a sustainable and health‐promoting dietary pattern.

The potential biological mechanisms linking the EAT‐Lancet diet to PAD risk remain incompletely understood. Metabolomics, as a promising approach, is frequently used to identify novel biomarkers associated with dietary intake and can overcome limitations inherent to traditional self‐reported dietary assessments. 36 In this study, we identified a set of metabolites significantly associated with the diet index and constructed a corresponding metabolic signature. This signature was characterized by elevated PUFA%, percentage of linoleic acid to total fatty acids, and glycine, alongside reduced percentage of saturated fatty acids to total fatty acids, leucine, and components of low‐density lipoprotein and very‐low‐density lipoprotein. These findings are consistent with previous studies on the EAT‐Lancet diet. 37 , 38 We further quantified the potential mediating effects of individual metabolic biomarkers in the association between the EAT‐Lancet diet and PAD. Notably, PUFA% exhibited the strongest mediating effect, suggesting that PUFA% may play a pivotal role in the metabolic pathway linking adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet with a lower risk of PAD. A higher PUFA% generally reflects a more favorable lipid metabolic profile and enhanced anti‐inflammatory capacity. 39 Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids can improve endothelial function, reduce plasma triglyceride concentrations, suppress vascular inflammation, and attenuate the progression of atherosclerosis, collectively contributing to a lower cardiovascular risk. 40 , 41 The mediating effect of PUFA% observed in this study reinforces the importance of fatty acid metabolism in the biological mechanisms connecting diet and arterial disease. Future investigations should further delineate the roles of specific polyunsaturated fatty acid subclasses, such as linoleic acid and eicosapentaenoic acid, to uncover the precise molecular pathways and to inform the development of targeted dietary interventions.

The study was conducted with a substantial sample and prospective cohort, marking the elucidation of a negative association between adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet index and PAD. This association was robustly confirmed through the use of 3 distinct methodologies for quantifying the EAT‐Lancet dietary guidelines. Furthermore, our study leveraged metabolomics data to demonstrate that metabolic signatures are not only associated with adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet but also linked to long‐term risk of PAD.

Limitations

The study's observational nature restricts the ability to infer causality, despite adjustments for numerous covariates to minimize potential confounding factors. Dietary data were largely reliant on self‐reported information, and although averages from multiple 24‐hour dietary recalls were used, recall bias persists. Furthermore, while the EAT‐Lancet diet's advantage over other indices lies in its promotion of environmental sustainability, the lack of sufficient environmental data prevented an assessment of the interplay between the diet, the environment, and PAD. This aspect remains to be elucidated in future research. Finally, the predominantly European White composition of the study population may limit generalizability to other ethnic and dietary contexts, and validation in multiethnic populations is therefore necessary. At the same time, although the EAT‐Lancet diet was introduced after UKB enrollment, it still provides a relevant framework for evaluating habitual diet in relation to long‐term health.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a robust inverse association between adherence to the EAT‐Lancet diet and the risk of PAD. A metabolic signature of 60 diet‐related metabolites was identified, mediating about 12.9% of this protective effect and implicating key metabolic pathways. These findings highlight the EAT‐Lancet diet as a sustainable strategy for PAD prevention through metabolic modulation.
