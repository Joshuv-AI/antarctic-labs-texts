Spatial characterization of interface dermatitis in cutaneous lupus reveals novel chemokine axis-mediated recruitment of leukocytes that drive disease

ABSTRACT
Chemokines play critical roles in the recruitment and activation of immune cells in both homeostatic and pathologic conditions. Here, we examined chemokine ligand-receptor pairs to better understand the immunopathogenesis of cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE), a complex autoimmune connective tissue disorder. Our objectives were to investigate the immunopathogenesis and therapeutic targets of interface dermatitis in patients with cutaneous lupus erythematosus. Four subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus (SCLE), 4 discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) and 3 healthy margin controls were enrolled for archival tissue sampling for spatial transcriptomics and proteomics. Seven CLE (4 SCLE, 3 DLE) patients and 3 healthy controls were enrolled for fresh tissue blister biopsy sampling of 184 protein analytes in interstitial skin fluid and serum using targeted proteomics, as well as spectral flow cytometry analysis for interface dermatitis cells. An additional 6 CLE and 6 healthy blood donors were enrolled for functional chemotaxis assays. Spatial and targeted proteomics data confirmed elevation of interferon (IFN) and IFN-inducible CXCR3 chemokine ligands. Comparing involved to uninvolved epidermal keratinocytes in CLE samples revealed upregulation of essential inflammatory response genes in areas near interface dermatitis, including AIM2. Targeted proteomics data confirmed upregulation of Caspase 8, IL-18 which is the final product of AIM2 activation, and induced chemokines including CCL8 and CXCL6 in CLE lesional samples. Chemotaxis assays using peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy and lupus donors revealed that T cells respond to CXCL9 and CXCL11, whereas CD14+CD16+ monocyte populations are more sensitive to CXCL6 via CXCR1 and CD14+ monocytes are more sensitive to CCL8 via CCR2. Furthermore, in vitro blocking assays revealed that hydroxychloroquine blocks CCL8 driven CD14+ monocyte migration, whereas reparixin blocks CXCL6 driven CD14+CD16+ monocyte migration. Taken together, our data map a pathway from keratinocyte injury to lymphocyte recruitment in CLE via AIM2-Casp8-IL-18-CXCL6-CXCR1 CD14+CD16+ monocyte recruitment, CCL8-CCR2 CD14+ monocyte recruitment, and IFNG/IFNL1-CXCL9/CXCL11-CXCR3 T cell recruitment, and suggest a mechanism by which hydroxychloroquine blocks CCR2+ CD14+ monocyte migration. We also identify reparixin and its derivatives as potential novel inhibitors of CXCR1+ CD14+CD16+ monocyte migration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60937-5.

Introduction

Cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) is a spectrum of clinically diverse autoimmune connective tissue disorders with shared histopathological features including interface dermatitis and lupus band reaction1. Chronic cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CCLE, including the most common sub-entity discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE)), subacute cutaneous lupus (SCLE), and acute cutaneous lupus erythematosus (ACLE) include the majority of cutaneous lupus clinical subtypes, though there are other more rare entities which all have varying degrees of association with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). In clinical practice, CLE can be refractory to SLE treatments2–4, but successful treatment of cutaneous disease may significantly decrease the risk of systemic involvement5. A common feature of CLE is the presence of interface dermatitis, which refers to inflammation and/or degenerative changes at the dermal-epidermal junction. The mechanisms by which immune cells are recruited to form interface dermatitis in CLE are unknown. Previous studies have found that interferon (IFN)-inducible chemokines are highly upregulated in CLE lesions6,7. One recent study characterized the associations of specific chemokine receptors and ligands in T cell subsets in cutaneous versus systemic lupus, demonstrating different profiles in SLE, DLE and SCLE8. The cellular sources of chemokine ligands binding to cognate receptors have not been fully characterized. To date, many CLE studies have focused on transcriptomics including bulk and single cell RNA sequencing9, but overlooked proteomics approaches and cellular analyses. Here, we used a blister biopsy technique10 to sample interstitial skin fluid and cells from the interface dermatitis reaction from CLE patients and compared to healthy donors. In tandem, we analyzed archival tissue biopsies with spatial transcriptomics and spatial proteomics. To confirm our findings, we also performed chemotaxis assays on peripheral blood from healthy and lupus donors. This study provides important insights into the immune mechanisms and therapeutic targets in patients with CLE.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was performed with internal review board (IRB) approved protocols at the UMass Chan Medical School (H-14848 and H00021295 for fresh blood and skin tissue; H00020503 for archival skin tissue), Yale University Institutional Review Board (Human Investigative Committee no. 15010105235 for archival skin tissue), and/or Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center (STUDY02001542 for PBMCs), and all samples were obtained with written informed consent and were de-identified before use in experiments. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Inclusion/exclusion criteria were as follows: subjects with a diagnosis of CLE by clinical exam performed by a dermatologist were included. Subjects with recent onset of new lesions and objective clinical signs of activity, specifically erythema and/or hair loss/alopecia, were recruited to represent active disease. Patients on treatment were included in the study, as most lupus patients must remain on maintenance therapy.

Additional information on spatial transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, RNA sequencing, cytokine/chemokine/inflammatory proteins serum concentration evaluation, high-parameter flow cytometric analysis for T-cell phenotypes, and statistical methods are provided in the Appendix (see Supporting Information).

Results

Characteristics of patients with CLE and healthy donors employed for fresh and archival tissue studies

We employed 2 patient cohorts for archival tissue analysis, and 3 cohorts for fresh tissue analysis. In total, we analyzed 26 lupus and 22 healthy donors. Skin tissues were analyzed from 16 lupus and 13 healthy donors, and blood samples were analyzed from 16 lupus and 12 healthy donors. All patients had a diagnosis of CLE from a dermatologist, and most patients were on maintenance therapy. Patient characteristics and cohorts are provided in Table 1.

Characterization of the immune infiltrate in CLE interface dermatitis

We performed spectral flow cytometry and targeted proteomics on blister biopsy fluid, and spatial transcriptomics and spatial proteomics on FFPE tissues to determine the cellular composition of CLE interface dermatitis (Fig S1-S2, Table S1). Quantification of cellular infiltrates from blister biopsies revealed significant increases in HLA-DR+ antigen presenting cell populations (p < 0.0021 lesional vs. nonlesional, p < 0.0006 nonlesional vs. healthy) as well as CD8+ T cells (p < 0.0166 lesional vs. healthy, Fig. 1A). Querying the targeted proteomics dataset for cell type specific proteins identified increased T cells (CD5, p = 0.0004 healthy vs. lesional; CD8A, p = 0.0001 healthy vs. lesional) and APC markers (CD302, p = 0.0017 healthy vs. lesional), but not NK cells (CD244 ns) in CLE blister fluid (Fig. 1B). These surface markers are likely shed due to the presence of increased matrix metalloproteinases such as ADAM15 (p = 0.006 healthy vs. lesional). Additional significant protein biomarkers are presented in Table S2.

Next, we examined regions of interest (ROIs) in the spatial datasets. CD3+ CD45+ masks were used to enrich T cells, CD45+ CD3- masks to enrich other immune cells, and geometric autofluorescent CD45- ROIs to enrich epidermal keratinocytes (Fig. 1C, QC in Figs S3-S6). Deconvolution of the immune cell ROIs in the WTA dataset revealed increases in many immune cell types in the dermis including B cells (p = 0.0257 DLE vs. healthy, p = 0.0095 SCLE vs. healthy), CD4+ T cells (p = 0.047 DLE vs. healthy, p = 0.0355 SCLE vs. healthy), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (p = 0.0122 DLE vs. healthy, p = 0.0403 SCLE vs. healthy) and monocytes/macrophages (p = 0.0057 DLE vs. healthy, p = 0.0075 SCLE vs. healthy) as assessed by cell-type specific genes (Fig. 1D). Examination of protein-level expression of CD surface markers using the immune cell profiling and immune cell typing protein modules confirmed the presence of CD3+ T cells (p = 0.0325 DLE vs. healthy, p = 0.0102 SCLE vs. healthy) and HLADR+ antigen presenting cells (p = 0.0139 DLE vs. healthy, p = 0.011 SCLE vs. healthy) in both subtypes. CD14+ monocytes (p = 0.0386 SCLE vs. healthy, p = 0.0952 DLE vs. healthy) and CD68+ monocytes/macrophages (p = 0.0133 DLE vs. healthy, p = 0.0534 SCLE vs. healthy) were elevated in SCLE and DLE, respectively (Fig. 1E). Taken together, the predominant cell types comprising CLE interface dermatitis are HLADR+ monocytes and T cells.

Examining differences in discoid versus subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus reveals unique immune checkpoint expression

One of the remaining questions in the field of CLE is: what immunologic differences underlie the pathogenesis of discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) versus subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus (SCLE)? To begin to answer this, we separated out the CLE subtypes and performed both transcriptomic and proteomic analyses comparing the entities, including donor as a correction variable. Spatial transcriptomic analysis of DLE vs. SCLE skin detected 19 DEGs in keratinocyte ROIs with p < 0.05 and log2 fold change greater than 1 or less than − 1 (Fig. 2A and B). Pathway analysis of these genes revealed terms related to ‘G protein coupled receptors (GPCRs)’ enriched in SCLE (p = 0.001295, normalized enrichment score (NES) 2.481) versus ‘eukaryotic translation elongation’ (p = 0.001295, NES 8.3007) and ‘interferon signaling’ (p = 0.001295, NES 4.8509), which is highly expressed in DLE epidermis (Fig. 2C). We also performed hypothesis testing by querying specific subsets of genes across conditions. Examining calpains, which are a potential treatment target for photosensitivity11, revealed that CAPN1 was enriched in DLE (p = 0.0354 SCLE vs. DLE), whereas CAPN3 was enriched in SCLE epidermis (p = 0.0489 DLE vs. SCLE) (Fig. 2D). Analysis of CD45+ ROIs detected 27 DEGs in DLE vs. SCLE with p < 0.05 and log2 fold change greater than 1 or less than − 1 (Fig. 2E and F). Pathway analysis of these genes revealed ‘keratinization’ was enriched in SCLE (p = 0.004286, NES 2.37288), whereas ‘interferon alpha/beta signaling’ (p = 0.002415, NES 4.03997) and ‘rRNA processing’ (p = 0.002415, NES 3.2216) were enriched in DLE (Fig. 2G). Of note, ‘keratinization’ term includes immune-related genes such as calpains and cathepsins. Examining IL-1 Receptor Associated Kinases (IRAKs), which are a treatment target for skin inflammation12, revealed IRAK1 was enriched in DLE (p = 0.0579 DLE vs. SCLE), whereas IRAK4 was enriched in SCLE immune cells (p = 0.3707 SCLE vs. DLE) (Fig. 2H). Analysis of CD3+ ROIs detected 4 DEGs in DLE vs. SCLE with p < 0.05 and log2 fold change greater than 1 or less than − 1 (Fig. 2I and J). Since so few genes were above a log2 fold change cutoff of 1, we performed analysis of differentially expressed proteins in place of pathway analysis. Interestingly, the immune inhibitory family member B7-H3 was uniquely upregulated in SCLE T cells (p = 0.0189 SCLE vs. DLE, Fig. 2K). Examining Janus kinases (JAKs), which have been successfully inhibited in multiple inflammatory skin disorders, revealed TYK2 is enriched in both DLE and SCLE compared to healthy T cells (p = 0.0882 DLE vs. healthy, Fig. 2L). Taken together, these data suggest that nuances in immune cell and keratinocyte signaling may underlie DLE versus SCLE pathogenesis, and that some treatment targets such as B7-H3, calpains or IRAKs may be subtype specific, while others, like TYK2, are shared.

Epidermal keratinocytes at the inflammatory lesional sites shape chemotactic signals for leukocyte recruitment

We next asked whether we could identify immune signaling pathways in epidermal keratinocytes that could lead to the initiation of inflammation and recruitment of immune cells to form interface dermatitis. Comparing epidermis ROIs that were above interface dermatitis (proximal) versus distal to an infiltrate (Fig. 3A) revealed upregulation of key chemokines including Cxcl9 (p = 0.0005) and Cxcl10 (p = 0.0005) in epidermis proximal to the immune infiltrate (Fig. 3B). Targeted proteomic data in lesional and nonlesional blister fluid was compared to the RNA spatial dataset, and demonstrated that CXCL9 (p = 0.000912, change estimate 2.74 fold) and CXCL11 (p = 0.0151, 2.88 fold) proteins are significantly upregulated in lesional blister fluid (Fig. 3C; Figs. S7-S8). Additionally, increased levels of chemokine ligands CCL8 (p = 0.0389, 1.15 fold) and CXCL6 (p = 0.0112, 1.97 fold) in lesional versus nonlesional blister fluid were identified (Fig. 3C). We re-queried the WTA dataset to analyze potential innate immune pathways that could lead to chemokine expression and found increased AIM2 expression (p = 0.0061, Fig. 3D; Fig. S7). Querying specific members of the AIM2 pathway in the Olink dataset identified significant increases in caspase 813 (p = 0.0278) and the IL1 family member IL1814 (p = 0.0059) in lesional skin relative to both healthy and nonlesional samples (Fig. 3E and F). IL18 induces CXCL6 expression15. Olink also identified significant increases in IFNL1 (p = 0.0203) and IFNG (p = 0.0079) protein levels in lesional CLE skin, which are upstream of CXCL9 and CXCL11 (Fig. 3G and H). CCL8 is optimally induced by combinations of IL1, IFNG and dsRNA16. We also observed a significant increase in TNF (p = 0.0017, Fig. 3I), which has previously been reported in lupus patients17. Taken together, these data suggest that keratinocyte injury can induce AIM2-caspase 8-IL18 pathways and IFN pathways18 to turn on chemokines including CCL8, CXCL6, CXCL9 and CXCL11 for recruitment of immune cells in CLE.

In vitro inhibitor treatment prevents chemotaxis of monocyte populations

To determine the functional consequences of our data, we performed chemotaxis assays focusing on the ligands we identified in the targeted proteomics dataset (Fig. 4A; Figs. S9-S10 and Tables S3-S4). We performed flow cytometry on input cells prior to migration to assess chemokine receptor expression. CCL8 binds to CCR1/2/3/5; CXCL6 binds to CXCR1/2, and CXCL9/10/11 bind to the receptor CXCR3. CCR3 is typically expressed on eosinophils, so we did not assess this receptor, but instead assessed CCR2/5 on monocytes, which are the high affinity CCL8 receptors. Cells were gated on live singlets and were identified with major lineage markers (e.g. CD3+ T cells, CD19+ B cells, CD56+ NK cells, CD14+ monocytes). Both lupus and healthy donor T cells expressed CXCR3 (Fig. 4A and B) and were able to migrate well towards CXCL11 (Fig. 4B). However, lupus donor T cells only exhibited a 2-fold increase in migration towards CXCL9 over media baseline, compared to an average 5-fold increase in healthy samples (Fig. 4C). We hypothesize this may be due to maintenance therapy in lupus donors. Notably, CXCL9 and CXCL11 are not expressed in healthy skin, but are expressed in lupus skin, exemplifying how T cells are poised to respond to IFN-inducible chemokines.

Last, we assessed chemokine receptor expression on monocytes, which were elevated in CLE lesions (Fig. 1; Figs. S10-S11). We gated live, single, CD45+ CD3-CD19-CD56- cells (i.e. non-T/B/NK) followed by CD14 and CD16 expression19. We examined chemokine receptor expression in CD14+CD16-, CD14+CD16+ and CD14-CD16+ monocytes and noted significantly higher percentage of CXCR1 expressing cells in the CD14+CD16+ subset (Fig. 4E-F; Fig. S12). Both healthy and lupus CD14+ cells, but particularly CD14+CD16+ cells, migrated towards CXCL6, providing a possible chemokine receptor: ligand pair that mediates their entry into the skin (Fig. 4G). We hypothesize that the preference for CD14+CD16+ monocyte migration to CXCL6 may be due to CD16 acting as a co-receptor for CXCR1, much like CD4 can be a co-receptor for CCR5. To assess a potential inhibitor of this pathway, we treated healthy donor PBMCs with the CXCR1/2 inhibitor reparixin (REPA) and assessed migration towards CXCL6. REPA treatment reduced migration in all but one donor, whose monocytes also paradoxically did not migrate well towards CXCL6 (Fig. 4H). Taken together, these data identify a potential inhibitor of CD14+CD16+ cell entry into lupus skin.

In contrast to CXCR1, the CCL8 receptor CCR2 was enriched on CD14+CD16- cells (Figs. 4I and J, S12), but not the CCR5 receptor (Fig. S13). CD14+CD16- cells migrated best towards CCL8 (Fig. 4K). To understand the potential effects of hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) on this pathway, we pretreated healthy PBMCs and performed chemotaxis assays. HCQ treatment reduced CCL8 directed migration by CD14+CD16-monocytes (Fig. 4L).

Taken together, these data exemplify how disease states can influence chemokine ligand levels and influence timing and recruitment of specific immune cell populations to the skin during CLE, namely through keratinocyte injury and subsequently through recruited immune cell populations. We propose the model that keratinocyte injury induces activation of AIM2-related pathways that turn on CXCL6 and CCL8 expression to recruit monocytes, which in turn potentiate IFN-related signaling pathways to recruit T cells and other lymphocytes via CXCR3 ligands (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, we found that epidermal keratinocytes produce CCL8 and CXCL6 to recruit monocytes, which in turn secrete CXCR3 ligands to recruit T cells to form interface dermatitis in CLE. Importantly, we demonstrated that hydroxychloroquine blocks CCL8 monocyte migration, whereas reparixin blocks CXCL6 monocyte migration.

Based on targeted proteomics and flow cytometry data, we identified CXCR1 as a key receptor for CXCL6 on proinflammatory CD14+CD16+ monocytes. Billi et al. showed that CD14+CD16+ monocyte/dendritic cell populations are among the first cells recruited to the skin in CLE20. CXCL6 is a particularly interesting target immunologically, given that it may be upregulated by double stranded RNA (dsRNA) or IL121. Rodriguez-Pla et al. showed that IFN priming alone is not sufficient to activate migratory programs in lupus monocytes22, supporting the role of dsRNA/IL1/CXCL6 as another potential mechanism by which these monocytes enter the skin. CXCL6 could be a viable treatment target for CLE, especially given the role of CXCL6 in fibrosis, which occurs in DLE and other forms of chronic CLE23 as well as in renal interstitial fibrosis24. Further studies are needed to understand epigenetic factors that result in increased CXCR1 expression in CD14+CD16+ monocytes.

One of the most highly upregulated chemokine families in CLE lesions is the CXCR3 chemokine family6,25,26. We confirmed expression of CXCR3 ligands by both epidermal keratinocytes and CD45+ populations that had migrated into the skin, thereby amplifying leukocyte recruitment. IFN signaling has been postulated to drive the pathogenesis of all subtypes CLE27. Further, CLE is a photosensitive disorder, and UV light induces upregulation of IFN to amplify CXCR3 ligand production in keratinocytes18,28,29, linking the environmental insult to the recruitment of pathogenic immune cells in the context of CLE. The interaction between CXCR3-expressing T cells and the ligands CXCL9, CXCL10 and CXCL11 have also been associated with tissue damage in several CLE subtypes8.

We also found differences in DLE and SCLE that have not previously been appreciated by using spatial technologies. DLE lesional skin T cells express less B7-H3 than SCLE lesional skin as assessed by spatial proteomics. B7-H3, also known as CD276, is an immune checkpoint within the B7 molecular family that fine tunes immune responses30 and is currently being explored as a cancer immunotherapy target31. B7-H3 plays a pivotal role in mediating the suppressive effects of DCs on T cells and downregulating Th1 responses32. Therefore, a relative reduction in B7-H3 in DLE fits with previously described IFNG signaling nodes identified in DLE by bulk RNA sequencing9.

In addition to insights into immune cell recruitment and potential novel biomarkers, our datasets identify potential treatment targets. Ladarixin, which is in the same drug family as reparixin, is a CXCR1 inhibitor that is currently in clinical trials for cancer33. It would be interesting to test a topical formulation for CLE for prevention of new or spreading lesions. We noted keratinocyte expression of CAPN1 and CAPN3, which are Ca2+-dependent cysteine proteases that modulate cellular functions and homeostasis34. CAPN1 is ubiquitously expressed, and has been reported to play a role in pyroptosis via cleavage of Gasdermin D35. In contrast, CAPN3 is typically thought to be restricted to skeletal muscle but was reported to be elevated in the skin of vitiligo vulgaris patients36. A calpain 1 inhibitor was recently demonstrated to prevent photosensitivity in animal models of UVB exposure11. We noted elevated CAPN1 in SCLE epidermis, which makes this another attractive topical treatment to test. TYK2 inhibitors are currently in clinical trials for SLE37, and several case reports have noted improvement in CLE38. We found elevated TYK2 in T cell ROIs from both DLE and SCLE patients. A small molecule inhibitor of IRAK4 was recently described as a treatment target for psoriatic skin inflammation12. We identified elevated IRAK1 in DLE, and IRAK4 in SCLE immune cells. Given that both TYK2 and IRAKs were elevated in the immune compartment ROIs, these treatments may require oral rather than topical administration for highest efficacy. We hope that these data inspire future clinical trials repurposing drugs for CLE, as well as studies testing topical versus oral routes of administration for more advanced compounds.

While our study provides valuable insights into the immunopathogenesis of CLE, it is essential to acknowledge the study’s limitations. First, our patient cohorts are relatively small, with 4–7 lupus patients per assay, measured at one time point. Future longitudinal studies with larger patient cohorts are warranted to confirm and expand upon these findings. In addition, the age of the tissue blocks may contribute to the ability to detect DEGs, particularly in the SCLE blocks. We also focused on a specific subset of chemokine ligand-receptor pairs and immune cell interactions that we were able to measure with current methodologies. While we did not directly test fresh keratinocytes from lupus donors to determine if they induce migration, it is well-established that keratinocytes express the chemokines we identified in blister biopsies and spatial transcriptomics (reviewed in39). CXCR1 is a classical receptor for neutrophil recruitment to inflamed tissues, and ligation of CXCR1 can enhance NET formation40,41. We were not able to recover neutrophils from blister fluid, and opted to use Ficoll gradients, which remove the bulk of granulocytes, to isolate PBMCs for chemotaxis assays. Future investigations should include methodologies to specifically assess neutrophils and other granulocytes. Likewise, we detected CD20 expression but did not find statistically significant increases in B cells by flow cytometry in blister fluid. This is likely due to the superficial sampling of blister biopsies. Random sampling of blisters from different skin sites could also affect the results, as different skin sites are known to have different neighborhoods for both microbiome and immune signatures42,43. Finally, comparing myeloid cells in the skin of patients with response or resistance to antimalarials would help shed light on limitations of hydroxychloroquine treatment on prevention of monocyte migration into the skin. Nevertheless, these in-depth mechanistic studies identify potential targeted interventions and hold promise for improving therapeutic strategies in CLE.
