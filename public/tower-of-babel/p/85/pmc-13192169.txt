Background

Pertussis (whooping cough), caused by Bordetella pertussis, remains a major public health challenge despite high global vaccination coverage. In young infants, the disease is frequently life-threatening, characterised by paroxysmal cough, respiratory distress, and severe complications including pneumonia, pulmonary hypertension, and encephalopathy [1, 2]. While implementation of diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP) immunisation has dramatically reduced global mortality [3, 4], resurgence has been observed over the past decade in both high-income and low- and middle-income settings [5–8]. The most critical aspect of this resurgence is the disproportionate burden on infants younger than 3 months, who experience the highest rates of hospitalisation and death due to immunological immaturity and incomplete primary immunisation [9–11]. This window of vulnerability, the period between waning of maternal antibodies and achievement of vaccine-induced immunity, necessitates immunisation strategies that provide protection at the earliest possible age [12–14].

The World Health Organization recommends initiating pertussis-containing vaccination at 6–8 weeks of age [15], but national schedules vary considerably. European programmes employ both accelerated schedules (e.g., 2, 3, and 4 months or 2, 4, and 6 months) and extended schedules (e.g., 2, 4, and 11 months or 3, 5, and 12 months), with first doses commonly administered at 2 or 3 months [16]. China historically initiated the primary series of diphtheria-tetanus-acellular pertussis (DTaP)-based vaccines at 3 months of age, administered at 3, 4, and 5 months, followed by a booster dose at 18 months, consistent with schedules adopted in several other countries [17, 18]. In January 2025, China revised its national immunisation schedule, advancing initiation of the DTaP primary series from 3 months to 2 months of age. Under the updated schedule, the three-dose primary series is administered at 2, 4, and 6 months, while the booster at 18 months remains unchanged.

This revision advances the DTaP primary series by one month compared with the previous 3–-4–-5-month schedule, aiming to provide earlier protection during infancy, when pertussis incidence and severity peak. However, evidence supporting this adjustment has largely been derived from pre-licensure and registration studies focusing on component acellular pertussis vaccines [19], whereas data on co-purified acellular formulations remain limited. Given that co-purified DTaP vaccines continue to be used in China’s National Immunization Program, real-world, population-level evidence on the effectiveness and safety of initiating the DTaP primary series at 2 months of age remains insufficient.

Chongqing, a major municipality in Southwest China with a population exceeding 32 million, provides an ideal setting for this evaluation. With sustained pertussis-containing vaccine coverage exceeding 97% and sophisticated digital health infrastructure, Chongqing has successfully linked three city-wide databases: the Intelligent Epidemic Prevention and Control System (CIEPCS), the Immunisation Program Information Management System (CIPIMS), and the National Adverse Events Following Immunisation Surveillance System (CNAEFIS) [20]. Prior to the 2025 policy change, natural variation in vaccination practice existed: while the National Immunisation Program utilised domestic DTaP on a 3-month schedule, availability of the pentavalent vaccine (DTaP-IPV/Hib) allowed a subset of infants to initiate vaccination at 2 months. Leveraging this variation and comprehensive data linkage, we established a population-based retrospective birth cohort of over 78,000 infants. This study aimed to compare the real-world effectiveness and safety of initiating pertussis vaccination at 2 versus 3 months of age and to assess how adherence to dosing intervals influences disease risk. Our findings provide evidence to support China’s recent policy shift and offer insights into optimising infant immunisation schedules globally.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a population-based retrospective birth cohort study in Chongqing, China. All infants born between September 1, 2023, and March 1, 2024, with at least one pertussis-containing vaccine record in the Chongqing Immunisation Program Information Management System (CIPIMS) were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete primary series (fewer than three doses); (2) receipt of heterologous (mixed) vaccine formulations; (3) first dose administered before 2 months of age (i.e., < 56 days); and (4) pertussis diagnosis recorded prior to the first vaccine dose.

Infants were classified according to age at receipt of the first dose: 2 months(≥ 2 months and <3 months of age), 3 months (≥ 3 months and <4 months of age), or other (any age outside these windows) [21]. For the effectiveness analysis, we restricted the primary comparison to infants initiating vaccination within the 2- or 3-month windows. Infants who received the first dose outside these predefined windows (i.e., < 56 days or ≥ 4 months of age) were not included in the main effectiveness comparison but were retained in baseline descriptive analyses.

Data sources

Vaccination and birth data were obtained from CIPIMS, which records each child’s demographic characteristics (sex, date of birth, and residence), vaccine type, administration dates, and doses received. Pertussis cases were ascertained through the Chongqing Intelligent Epidemic Prevention and Control System (CIEPCS), which aggregates all laboratory-confirmed and clinically diagnosed cases reported by healthcare institutions across the municipality (ICD-10 code A37.900). Cases diagnosed between September 1, 2023, and September 1, 2024, were extracted with relevant demographic and diagnostic date fields. Safety outcomes were derived from the China National Adverse Events Following Immunisation Surveillance System (CNAEFIS). Adverse events following immunisation (AEFI) were classified according to the National Surveillance Program for Suspected Abnormal Vaccination Reactions (2022 edition) [22].

Records from CIPIMS, CIEPCS, and CNAEFIS were linked using national identification numbers as the primary key; when these were missing or invalid, records were matched using name, sex, and date of birth (Fig. 2).

Schematic diagram of the multi-source data linkage

Definitions

Single administration was defined as receipt of a pertussis-containing vaccine alone, whereas co-administration denoted a pertussis-containing vaccine given during the same visit together with one or more other vaccines at separate injection sites [23]. Dosing intervals were classified based on the time between consecutive doses: early (< 28 days), on-time (28–42 days), mild delay (43–90 days), and moderate-to-severe delay (≥ 91 days), extending the ACIP-recommended minimum intervals between DTaP doses to operationalise degrees of delay [24]. Non-serious AEFI were defined as events not meeting serious criteria (e.g., fever, local redness, induration). Serious AEFI were defined as events resulting in death, hospitalisation or prolonged hospitalisation, persistent or significant disability, life-threatening conditions, according to national guidelines and the Vaccine Administration Law of the People’s Republic of China [25].

Outcomes

The primary effectiveness outcome was pertussis incidence after the first vaccine dose, expressed as cases per 1,000 person-years. Following WHO guidance, person-time at risk for each dose began 14 days after administration to allow for the development of vaccine-induced immunity [15]. The 14-day risk attribution rule was applied in a dose-specific manner: for the first dose, because no preceding dose exists, events occurring during days 0–13 post-vaccination were neither attributed to vaccine-mediated protection nor to any preceding dose, and were therefore excluded entirely from dose-specific risk-time accumulation, with person-time at risk beginning on day 14; for subsequent doses, events occurring during days 0–13 were attributed to the preceding dose to ensure non-overlapping risk windows across the vaccination series. Person-time for each dose ended on the date of the next scheduled dose, pertussis diagnosis, censoring, or study end, whichever occurred first. The primary safety outcome was AEFI incidence after the first dose. Only AEFI determined by station physicians to be vaccine-related were included in the analysis.

Covariates

Covariates included sex, date of birth, and residence at birth, as recorded in CIPIMS. Additional stratification variables included vaccine formulation (DTaP, DTaP-Hib, or DTaP-IPV/Hib), co-administration status, and dosing-interval category.

Statistical analysis

Infants initiating vaccination outside the 2-month or 3-month windows were described in baseline characteristics but excluded from subsequent analyses. The primary analysis compared pertussis incidence between the 2-month and 3-month initiation groups. Incidence rates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using exact Poisson methods, expressed per 1,000 person-years. Person-time was accrued using a time-updated exposure approach: infants contributed person-time sequentially to dose-specific risk periods until receipt of the subsequent dose, pertussis onset, or end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. Subgroup analyses were conducted by vaccine type, number of doses received, and sex. Kaplan-Meier survival curves compared time to pertussis onset between initiation groups after completion of the primary series, with differences evaluated using the log-rank test. For timeliness analyses, we excluded infants with any early interval (n = 50) given the small number and potential protocol deviations. The remaining infants were classified based on their dosing schedule: timely (both inter-dose intervals between 28 and 42 days) or non-adherent. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To formally compare pertussis incidence between subgroups, including by sex and initiation age group, Poisson regression models with the natural logarithm of person-time as the offset were used to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p-values [26].

Vaccination characteristics

Age at cohort entry and sex distribution were comparable across initiation groups. Vaccine formulations differed markedly: in the 2-month group, 99.78% received DTaP-IPV/Hib, whereas in the 3-month group, 82.31% received DTaP and 14.10% received DTaP-Hib.

On-time completion of the dose 2–3 interval varied by vaccine type and initiation group. Among DTaP-IPV/Hib recipients, 54.35% in the 2-month group completed this interval on time compared with 42.41% in the 3-month group. For DTaP recipients, on-time rates were 58.62% in the 2-month group and 63.44% in the 3-month group. Baseline characteristics of infants initiating vaccination at other ages are shown in Table 1.

The median cumulative follow-up time from the first dose date until censoring (defined as pertussis diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or study end on September 1, 2024) was 6.37 months (range 3.19–9.99) in the 2-month group and 5.58 months (range 2.14-9.00) in the 3-month group. Corresponding cumulative follow-up from the third dose date until censoring was 3.78 months (range 0-7.98) and 3.12 months (range 0-6.96), respectively. These values represent total observation time after each indicated dose and may span multiple subsequent vaccination intervals; they are distinct from the dose-specific person-time used for incidence rate estimation in Table 2, which was accrued within non-overlapping, time-updated risk windows as described in the Methods.

Pertussis incidence by initiation age

Table 2 presents pertussis incidence (per 1,000 person-years) by age at first dose (2 vs. 3 months), stratified by sex, vaccine type, dose count, and co-administration. Among 69,612 infants initiating vaccination at 2 or 3 months, pertussis incidence was 6.84 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 5.99–-7.78). Pertussis incidence did not differ significantly between the 2-month group (6.99 per 1,000 person-years [95% CI 5.24–9.15]) and the 3-month group (6.80 [95% CI 5.84–7.86]) (IRR 0.972, 95% CI 0.721–1.332; P = 0.855; Table 2).

When stratified by sex, incidence rates among infants initiating at 2 months were 8.04 (95% CI 5.46–11.41) per 1,000 person-years in males and 5.91 (95% CI 3.70–8.95) per 1,000 person-years in females. The incidence rate ratio (male vs. female), estimated using Poisson regression with person-time as the offset, was 1.36 (95% CI 0.79–2.35; P = 0.269). Among infants initiating at 3 months, corresponding incidence rates were 7.11 (95% CI 5.77–8.67) in males and 6.47 (95% CI 5.16–8.01) in females, with an incidence rate ratio of 1.10 (95% CI 0.82–1.47; P = 0.53).

Pertussis incidence by number of doses received

Pertussis incidence declined significantly with each additional dose (Table 2). During the post-vaccination periods following the first and second doses, incidence rates were 10.55 (95% CI 8.36–13.13) and 6.59 (95% CI 4.90–8.66) per 1,000 person-years, respectively. After completion of the three-dose primary series, incidence declined to 5.44 (95% CI 4.44–6.60) per 1,000 person-years, based on prolonged follow-up and providing a more stable estimate of post-vaccination protection. The incidence rate ratio comparing the post-third-dose period to the post-first-dose period was 0.516 (95% CI 0.385–0.692; P < 0.001), confirming a statistically significant reduction in pertussis risk with completion of the primary series.

Pertussis incidence by dosing-interval timeliness

Timeliness of vaccination was strongly associated with pertussis incidence (Table 2). Infants who completed both inter-dose intervals on time had the lowest incidence across all initiation groups. In the overall cohort, incidence was 3.99 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 3.15–4.97) among infants with on-time intervals versus 10.66 (95% CI 9.05–12.47) among those with any delayed interval (IRR 2.676, 95% CI 2.046–3.527; P < 0.001; Table 2). This pattern was consistent across initiation groups: in the 2-month group, infants with non-adherent intervals had a significantly higher incidence than those with on-time intervals (9.74 [95% CI 6.78–13.54] vs. 4.52 [95% CI 2.68–7.14]; IRR 2.155, 95% CI 1.237–3.888; P = 0.008), and a similar pattern was observed in the 3-month group (10.96 [95% CI 9.10–13.10] vs. 3.85 [95% CI 2.94–4.95]; IRR 2.848, 95% CI 2.100-3.905; P < 0.001; Table 2). Sensitivity analyses excluding infants with early intervals (n = 50) yielded similar estimates (Supplementary Table S1).

Sensitivity analysis: DTaP-IPV/Hib recipients

To address potential confounding by vaccine type, we restricted analysis to DTaP-IPV/Hib recipients (13,784 in the 2-month group; 2,007 in the 3-month group). Pertussis incidence did not differ significantly between the 2-month and 3-month groups among DTaP-IPV/Hib recipients (7.01 per 1,000 person-years [95% CI 5.25–9.17] versus 7.34 [95% CI 2.95–15.12]; IRR 1.047, 95% CI 0.434–2.150; P = 0.909; Table 3). Results by co-administration status were consistent with the main analysis.

Kaplan-Meier analysis

Kaplan-Meier analysis included 13,665 infants in the 2-month group and 53,984 in the 3-month group, beginning 14 days after completion of the primary series. Cumulative pertussis incidence remained low in both groups, reaching approximately 0.3% by 6 months. The survival curves showed modest separation over time, with the 2-month group exhibiting slightly lower event-free probability (log-rank P = 0.023). However, the absolute difference was small and unlikely to be clinically meaningful (Fig. 3).

Vaccine safety

A total of 268 infants (0.34%) experienced adverse events following immunisation (Table 4). The most common were general reactions, predominantly fever, accounting for 264 of 268 events (98.5%); these were typically mild to moderate and self-limiting. Serious adverse events occurred in 2 infants (0.02%), with one event following the first dose at 2 months and one following the first dose at 3 months. One death occurred in the 3-month group following vaccination; however, this was assessed as unrelated to vaccination. No serious vaccine-related adverse events were identified.

Discussion

Main findings

In this population-based cohort study of 76,844 infants, the primary effectiveness comparison demonstrated that overall pertussis incidence after the first dose was similar between infants initiating vaccination at 2 months and those initiating at 3 months. Using time-updated person-time accumulation and exact Poisson estimation, incidence rates were 6.99 per 1,000 person-years in the 2-month group and 6.80 per 1,000 person-years in the 3-month group, with overlapping confidence intervals and no statistically significant difference. These findings indicate that advancing the first dose to 2 months does not materially alter overall pertussis risk after vaccination compared with the traditional 3-month schedule.

Interpretation of findings

Although fewer cases occurred before the first dose in the 2-month group (3 cases versus 38 cases in the 3-month group), our primary inference does not rely on differences in the pre-vaccination window. Rather, the central finding is that post–-first-dose incidence remained comparable between schedules. Infants younger than 3 months experience the highest rates of severe pertussis, hospitalisation, and death [27]. Advancing the first dose to 2 months extends vaccine-derived protection into this high-risk period, thereby shortening the window of susceptibility. However, the numerically similar post–-first-dose incidence between groups supports the conclusion that immune priming at 2 months provides protection comparable to that achieved at 3 months. Thus, the principal implication of earlier initiation is not superior long-term effectiveness, but earlier onset of protection without increasing subsequent disease risk. These findings align with observational evidence from Europe and North America indicating that earlier priming does not diminish vaccine performance [28–30].

In time-updated analyses, pertussis incidence declined progressively with each successive dose. The relatively higher incidence observed after the first and second doses likely reflects the intrinsically elevated susceptibility of young infants rather than inadequate vaccine-induced protection. After completion of the primary three-dose series, incidence was lower and estimates were more stable owing to longer follow-up. These findings reinforce the importance of completing the full primary series to achieve optimal protection [31, 32].

A key secondary finding was the strong association between dosing-interval timeliness and pertussis risk. Infants with non-adherent intervals experienced substantially higher incidence compared with those who completed both intervals on schedule, and this pattern was consistent across initiation groups. This observation extends previous modelling and epidemiological evidence demonstrating that deviations from recommended intervals increase pertussis risk [33]. Our results suggest that adherence to recommended intervals may be as important as the age at initiation in determining disease risk.

Safety outcomes were reassuring. Adverse events following the first dose were uncommon (0.34%) and comparable between groups, and most were mild and self-limiting, such as fever. Serious adverse events were rare (0.02%), and no vaccine-related serious events or deaths were identified. These findings are consistent with post-licensure surveillance data from China and South Korea [34, 35]. Co-administration with other routine immunisations did not compromise effectiveness or safety, supporting the feasibility of integrated vaccine schedules [36, 37].

Limitations and strengths

This study has several strengths. The integration of city-wide immunisation, disease surveillance, and adverse event databases enabled simultaneous assessment of effectiveness and safety within a single population-based cohort. The large sample size and high vaccine coverage (> 97%) enhanced precision and reduced potential selection bias. Sensitivity analyses restricted to specific vaccine formulations yielded consistent findings, supporting the robustness of the primary effectiveness comparison.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, residual confounding inherent to observational research cannot be excluded. Second, differences in vaccine formulations between groups may have introduced residual confounding despite subgroup analyses. Third, pertussis case ascertainment relied on ICD-10 coding and may have missed mild or atypical cases. Fourth, follow-up duration was limited and did not allow assessment of longer-term protection or waning immunity. Fifth, passive surveillance systems may underestimate adverse events. Replication in other settings would strengthen external validity.

Conclusions

In this high-coverage population in Chongqing, initiation of pertussis-containing vaccination at 2 months demonstrated overall effectiveness and safety comparable to the traditional 3-month schedule after completion of the primary series. The principal difference between schedules lay in the timing of protection onset: earlier initiation shortened the unprotected window in early infancy without increasing post-vaccination incidence. These findings indicate that advancing the first dose to 2 months is a safe and effective alternative strategy that maintains comparable overall protection while reducing vulnerability during early infancy.

Significance and future directions

These findings provide real-world evidence supporting earlier initiation as a feasible strategy to reduce early infant vulnerability while maintaining comparable overall protection. As such, our results may inform policymakers considering schedule adjustments aimed at advancing primary series initiation.

Future research should evaluate the long-term durability of immunity associated with earlier initiation across diverse epidemiological and immunisation contexts, and incorporate immunological assessments and active surveillance data to strengthen the evidentiary framework underpinning these findings. Such efforts would provide more direct empirical support for the optimisation of global paediatric immunisation schedules.
