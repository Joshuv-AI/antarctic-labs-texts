Effects of different forms of single moderate-intensity exercise on inhibitory function and instant emotion of undergraduate students with depressive symptoms: a randomized controlled trial based on ERP
Authors: Feng Gao, Shuqi Jia, Suowang Yin, Zhaosong Yin, Wei Guan, Feng Ding, Xing Wang, Ping Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigated the effects of a single bout of moderate-intensity exercise on inhibitory control and transient mood states in college students with depressive symptoms.MethodsSeventy-five participants with depressive symptoms were randomized into three groups: treadmill exercise, taekwondo exercise (both at moderate intensity), and a non-intervention control group. Pre- and post-intervention assessments included immediate mood states, behavioral performance (reaction time and accuracy) on Go/No-go and Stroop tasks, and concurrent event-related potentials (ERPs). Repeated-measures ANOVA and Pearson correlation analyses were employed to examine exercise-induced changes in inhibitory control and mood.ResultsPost-exercise, both exercise groups exhibited significant improvements in No-go accuracy (treadmill: +10.6%, taekwondo: +20.0%, P < 0.05), though reaction times remained unchanged (P > 0.05). The taekwondo group demonstrated superior Stroop performance, with increased accuracy in congruent (+ 2.9%, P = 0.007, 95% CI [0.698, 4.161]) and incongruent trials (+ 2.7%) and reduced reaction times (congruent: −0.114 s, incongruent: −0.141 s, P = 0.002, 95% CI [− 0.091, − 0.022]), outperforming the treadmill group in reaction time (P < 0.001, 95% CI [− 0.097, − 0.028]). ERP analysis revealed enhanced N2/P3 amplitudes and shortened latencies in exercise groups (P < 0.05), with taekwondo showing broader cortical activation. Immediate mood scores were significantly lower in exercise groups versus controls (F = 5.525, P = 0.006), correlating inversely with inhibitory accuracy (r = − 0.432, P < 0.001) and positively with conflict inhibition reaction time (r = 0.245, P = 0.048).ConclusionA single bout of moderate-intensity exercise confers acute benefits for inhibitory control and mood regulation in depressive individuals. Taekwondo demonstrates greater efficacy than treadmill exercise in enhancing cognitive performance and emotional states, suggesting exercise modality-specific neurobehavioral effects. Improved inhibitory function may mediate mood amelioration, highlighting exercise as a viable adjunct intervention for depressive symptoms.

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) represents one of the most prevalent psychiatric conditions worldwide [1].Globally, approximately 5% of adults are affected by depression, with an average incidence rate of 4.4%, showing a persistent upward trajectory [2]. According to World Health Organization (WHO) data, depression has been identified as the third-leading risk factor for global disease burden, with projections indicating its ascendancy to the foremost position by 2030 [3]. thereby jeopardizing individual health. Depressive symptoms—the clinical manifestations of MDD [5], —are characterized by suicidal ideation, persistent low mood, sleep disturbances, appetite dysregulation, and attentional deficits [6]. These symptoms are recognized as prodromal markers of MDD, where sustained depressive states may transition individuals from health to subclinical status, ultimately progressing to full MDD diagnosis [7]. Consequently, the development of preventive strategies, early assessment protocols, timely interventions, and personalized therapeutic regimens for depressive symptoms has emerged as a critical priority in MDD research.

Inhibitory control (IC), a core component of executive functioning within cognitive domains, plays a pivotal role in regulating neural and behavioral processes. Deficits in IC have emerged as a transdiagnostic feature across psychiatric disorders, critically contributing to cognitive biases and impairments observed in Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) [8]. Empirical evidence suggests that impaired inhibition of negative emotional stimuli may serve as a cognitive vulnerability factor for depressive relapse. IC dysfunction manifests as multifaceted psychological deficits, including compromised attentional allocation, impaired planning capacity, maladaptive decision-making, and deficient self-monitoring of goal-directed actions [9]. Clark’s seminal definition (1996) [10] posits IC as “the ensemble of neural mechanisms that attenuate or terminate psychological, behavioral, or neural activity.” Neurodevelopmental trajectories of IC are characterized by progressive cortical specialization, wherein refined brain activation patterns directly mediate behavioral responses [11]. The neural substrates subserving IC and executive functioning predominantly involve fronto-striatal circuitry—encompassing the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), frontal eye fields (FEF), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and basal ganglia (BG)—with additional contributions from cerebellar networks.

Recent advances in bioscientific methodologies have illuminated the neurobiological underpinnings of depressive symptoms, revealing their mechanistic links to deficits in neural inhibitory control and electrophysiological aberrations [12]. Event-Related Potentials (ERPs)—a neuroimaging technique that records cortical voltage fluctuations elicited by controlled sensory, cognitive, or motor stimuli—have emerged as a cornerstone in contemporary cognitive neuroscience. The N2 component, a hallmark ERP index of response inhibition, manifests as a frontocentral negativity peaking 200–350 ms post-stimulus, reflecting conflict monitoring during inhibitory tasks [13], Complementarily, the P3 component (or P300), an exogenous stimulus-locked potential with maximal amplitude over parietal regions (latency: 300–800 ms), is implicated in attentional resource allocation, working memory updating, and inhibitory process evaluation [14]. Given that depressive pathophysiology involves dysregulation across prefrontal cortical, subcortical, and limbic circuits, ERPs provide unparalleled spatiotemporal resolution for dissecting the cognitive-affective mechanisms underlying these disorders [15]. Convergent ERP-behavioral evidence demonstrates aberrant modulation of P3, N2, and slow-wave components during attention deployment, stimulus anticipation, and affective evaluation in depressed individuals, corroborating behavioral indices of cognitive-emotional dysregulation [16, 17]. The prefrontal cortex (PFC), which orchestrates inhibitory control via sensory gating, memory prioritization, and motor planning, generates excitatory signals critical for suppressing task-irrelevant stimuli. PFC hypoactivity disrupts this circuitry, eliciting exaggerated N2 amplitudes during conflict resolution phases [18]. Collectively, ERPs serve as a robust neurophysiological framework for investigating inhibitory dysfunction and affective dysregulation in depression, particularly in high-risk populations such as college students.

While pharmacotherapy remains a cornerstone of depression management, its therapeutic efficacy is constrained, with response rates of 60–80% and remission rates as low as 30% [19]. Beyond conventional treatments, exercise has garnered substantial empirical support as a cost-effective, ecologically sustainable, and side-effect-free intervention for both preventing and ameliorating depressive symptomatology. Epidemiologically, the global rise in depression prevalence exhibits inverse correlations with physical activity levels [20]. Mechanistically, structured exercise regimens demonstrably enhance neurocognitive functioning, with prospective cohort studies establishing robust associations between regular physical activity and reduced depression risk [21]. Notably, preclinical and human studies reveal exercise potentiates the neurotrophic efficacy of fluoxetine, with brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) emerging as a pivotal mediator. Chronic exercise upregulates hippocampal BDNF expression and phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (p-ERK) signaling in stress-sensitive neural circuits, thereby attenuating stress-induced hippocampal neurodegeneration while enhancing neuroplasticity [22]. Exercise-induced modulation of inflammatory cytokines and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity further contributes to its antidepressant effects [23]. Additionally, exercise counteracts cognitive decline associated with sleep deprivation through noradrenergic and glutamatergic pathway modulation [24, 25]. Meta-analytic evidence [26, 27], demonstrates superior efficacy of moderate-intensity exercise (MIE) over low-intensity (subthreshold activation) and high-intensity (poor adherence, attentional fatigue) regimens in improving depressive affect and inhibitory control. This dose-response relationship is neurophysiologically corroborated by a randomized controlled trial in healthy youth, where acute MIE enhanced conflict resolution efficiency (reduced Flanker task response latency for both congruent/incongruent trials) and augmented event-related potential components (elevated P2, N2c, and P3 amplitudes post-exercise), though without accuracy or peak latency alterations [28].

A critical synthesis of extant literature reveals moderate-intensity exercise (MIE) demonstrates promising efficacy in ameliorating inhibitory control deficits and depressive symptomatology among university populations, yet substantial heterogeneity persists regarding optimal exercise prescription parameters. Current research predominantly employs closed-skill exercise modalities (e.g., treadmill running, cycling, rowing) characterized by predictable, repetitive motor patterns, while open-skill interventions requiring dynamic environmental adaptation remain underexplored. Emerging evidence suggests open-skill paradigms impose heightened sensorimotor integration demands, thereby eliciting superior executive function enhancements through increased cognitive-kinesthetic engagement [29]. Contemporary meta-analytic findings [30] tentatively support open-skill superiority in improving inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility, though null effects in subgroup analyses underscore unresolved mechanistic complexities—potentially involving differential neuroplasticity pathways or attentional resource allocation dynamics. These insights necessitate paradigm shifts in clinical exercise prescription frameworks [29].

Our experimental design accordingly investigates acute neurocognitive and affective responses to single-bout MIE interventions contrasting open- vs. closed-skill modalities in depressive undergraduates. Taekwondo—a structured open-skill martial art—provides unique methodological advantages: (1) Superior ecological validity compared to open-skill team sports through standardized movement patterns; (2) Enhanced intervention fidelity versus group-based open-skill activities via precise intensity modulation; (3) Clinically validated psychophysiological benefits encompassing motor coordination, stress resilience, and self-efficacy [31]. The closed-skill comparator employs treadmill running, a gold-standard controlled aerobic intervention. This matched-intensity, cross-modal comparison elucidates neurobehavioral mechanisms underlying exercise-induced mood regulation while controlling for confounding variables inherent to complex open-skill environments.

Over the past decade, our research team under Professor Xing Wang’s leadership has pioneered work in elucidating the dose-response relationship between exercise modalities and antidepressant efficacy. Through latent class analysis of depressive phenotypes, we identified distinct symptom clusters in undergraduates—particularly differentiating suicidal ideation and cognitive dysfunction subtypes—while establishing physical activity as a robust protective factor against depressive symptom onset [32]. Subsequent cross-sectional investigations revealed symptom-specific interactions: depressive and anxiety symptoms exhibited tight comorbidity, yet higher physical activity volume predicted attenuated symptom severity, supporting personalized exercise prescription strategies tailored to symptom domains [33]. Critically, quantitative electroencephalography (qEEG) emerged as a sensitive biomarker for mapping exercise-depression neurodynamics. Our neurophysiological findings demonstrated sport-specific spectral signatures: ball-game athletes exhibited elevated α1 spectral power (F4-F3 asymmetry), while resistance-trained individuals showed reduced δ-band coherence (C3 + C4) [34]. These discoveries provide compelling empirical support for exercise-induced neuromodulation. Given this specificity, EEG techniques offer a promising approach for analyzing depressive symptoms in undergraduates. We further identified associations between physical activity and abnormal EEG patterns: among regular ball-sport participants, higher activity levels correlated with elevated α1 band activity (F4-F3 asymmetry), while resistance-trained individuals showed reduced δ band coherence (C3 + C4) [34] Building on prior research and our preliminary results, we aim to explore potential mechanisms linking acute exercise to depressive symptom alleviation, specifically examining how single-session moderate-intensity exercise impacts inhibitory function and immediate mood in depressive undergraduates. The experimental design requires participants to complete two inhibitory control tasks (behavioral tasks) immediately post-exercise, with concurrent ERP recordings. Study design and methodology

Sample size estimation

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.2 software. Based on the study design, an F-test for mixed-design ANOVA (repeated measures, within-between interaction) was selected. A priori power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.8) with a medium effect size (partial η²=0.06, converted to Cohen’s f = 0.252) for interaction effects, three experimental groups, and two measurement timepoints yielded a minimum required sample of 42 participants. Accounting for potential attrition rates (≈ 20%), we initially recruited 90 undergraduates. After screening, 89 eligible participants were enrolled, with 75 completing all protocols (final retention rate: 84.3%).

Participant recruitment and group allocation

Recruitment Protocol:Convenience sampling was implemented across Shanghai Songjiang University Town through researcher-administered recruitment posters and flyers distributed in high-traffic zones (libraries, dormitories). Prospective participants underwent preliminary screening against inclusion/exclusion criteria.Randomization Procedure:Eligible candidates (N = 89) were sequentially numbered and allocated via computer-generated randomization (1:1:1 ratio) to three arms: Treadmill Exercise Group (Aerobic Intervention); Taekwondo Training Group (Cognitive-Motor Dual Task)༛Passive Control Group (No Intervention).Ethical Compliance:Full informed consent detailing experimental protocols was obtained prior to enrollment. The study received ethical approval from Shanghai University of Sport Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 102772021RT004), with recruitment conducted October 2023. Final analyzed sample: 75 participants (attrition rate: 15.7%) (Fig. 2) (Table 1).

Experimental procedure

Testing procedure

All participants were contacted 2 days in advance and asked not to do strenuous exercise within 24 h before the visit, and not to drink alcohol, coffee and other stimulant beverages within 12 h before the test. After arriving at the test site, first wash the scalp, then fill in the informed consent and basic information, and then use four computers of the same model to test the implementation of functional tasks and simultaneously detect ERP components. The second test should be separated from the first for more than 72 h to eliminate possible practice effects, and both tests should be conducted in the afternoon. After the exercise group finished the exercise, the subjects were asked to fill in the personal information. After the heart rate returned to the quiet level (about 10 min), the behavioral task and ERP were tested. All tests were performed by a single subject (see Fig. 3a).

Quality assurance protocol

(1)Blind method

Participant blinding: Recruits received standardized briefing materials describing the investigation’s objectives as “evaluating differential exercise effects on psychological adaptation,” with deliberate omission of condition-specific terminology (e.g., martial arts/treadmill designations).

Interventionist blinding: Deliverables were implemented by discipline-specific practitioners (1 WTF-certified taekwondo instructor, 1 IAAF-accredited running coach) operating under scripted protocols. These personnel maintained complete segregation from outcome measurement processes.

Assessor Blinding: Behavioral paradigm administration (Go/No-go, Stroop variants) and ERP data acquisition were conducted by two research associates uninformed about allocation schema. Participant identifiers were encrypted using alpha-numeric codes (G1-G75) that concealed group membership.

Analytical Blinding: Statistical operations utilized triple-anonymized datasets stripped of group labels. Final condition decoding occurred post hoc through a dedicated blinding protocol managed by an independent cryptographic officer, ensuring verifiable separation between data processing and hypothesis testing phases.

(2)Exercise monitoring

The investigation employed compartmentalized training environments: taekwondo practitioners utilized shock-absorbent martial arts flooring while treadmill participants exercised on calibrated motorized units (Technogym Excite Run). Pre-intervention standardization required all subjects to don moisture-wicking athletic attire, with research personnel assisting in proper placement of telemetric chest strap monitors (Polar H10) along the inframammary axis. A structured 5-minute preparatory sequence—incorporating dynamic joint mobilization and rhythmic calisthenics—preceded core exercise phases.

Both cohorts maintained moderate-intensity exertion (64–76% HRmax) verified through real-time Polar Team Pro monitoring. Physiological surveillance included dual-modality verification: automated heart rate logging at five-minute intervals complemented by concurrent Borg CR-10 scale assessments. Exercise physiologists implemented an immediate termination protocol for any RPE values exceeding 18, per ACSM safety guidelines. Post-exercise recovery involved supervised 5-minute parasympathetic activation sequences combining diaphragmatic breathing and static stretching.

Cardiovascular data analysis confirmed protocol fidelity, with mean exercise heart rates across both groups consistently residing within prescribed intensity parameters. No adverse events requiring early termination were recorded, with all participants successfully completing the 20-minute active intervention phase followed by mandated cooldown procedures (Table 3).

Experimental measures and instrumentation

(1) Demographic Profiling

A bespoke demographic inventory facilitated baseline characterization through multidimensional assessment of: anthropometric indices (stature, mass), chronological age, biological sex, academic standing, disciplinary specialization, substance use history (nicotine/ethanol consumption patterns), and psychiatric medical records.

(2) Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II)

This 21-item psychometric instrument quantified depressive symptom severity across cognitive-affective and somatic domains using 4-point Likert scales (0–3 per item). Cumulative scoring protocol stratified participants as: asymptomatic (0–13), mild (14–19), moderate (20–28), or severe (≥ 29) depression. The Chinese adaptation demonstrated exceptional psychometric robustness (Cronbach’s α = 0.948) in our cohort [37].

(3) Physical Activity Rating Scale-3 (PARS-3)

The Sinicized PARS-3 quantified exercise engagement through triaxial evaluation: exercise modality intensity (MET equivalents), session duration, and weekly frequency. Scoring stratification defined: low (0–19), moderate (20–42), and high (≥ 43) activity tiers. This instrument has established reliability and validity in collegiate populations through multiple validation studies [38].

(4) Depression Adjective Checklist (DACL)

The Lubin Depressive Adjective Checklist (DACL) was developed to assess transient depressive mood states, feelings, or emotions rather than chronic, long-term depression. The DACL comprises seven parallel forms: the first four (Forms A, B, C, D) each contain 32 adjective items, while the remaining three (Forms E, F, G) each contain 34 items. Respondents are asked to indicate whether each adjective “fits me” or “doesn’t fit me.” In the initial four forms, 22 adjectives are depression-oriented and 10 are non-depression-oriented; in the latter three forms, 22 items reflect depressive tendencies while 12 indicate non-depressive states. Scoring involves awarding 1 point for either endorsing a depression-related adjective or rejecting a non-depression-related adjective, with higher total scores indicating greater severity of depressive mood. Each form requires approximately 2.5 min to complete [39].

Tasks and procedures

The current study employed the Go/No-go paradigm to assess response inhibition capabilities, while utilizing the Stroop task to evaluate conflict inhibition mechanisms. The experimental design adopted a mixed-model approach, with between-subject independent variables comprising three experimental groups (sedentary control, treadmill exercise, and taekwondo training). Dependent variables included: (1) Go/No-go task performance metrics (reaction time [RT] and accuracy rate [ACC] under Go/No-go stimulus conditions); (2) Stroop task indices (RT/ACC differentials between congruent and incongruent trials); and (3) immediate affective responses quantified via the Depression Adjective Checklist. A 3 × 2 × 3 factorial design was implemented, featuring: (1) Between-group comparisons across three cohorts; (2) Dual behavioral measures (RT/ACC); (3) Task condition variations (Go/No-go, Stroop congruency); and (4) Tri-electrode neurophysiological recording (Fz, Cz, Pz midline placements).

Go/No-go task

The response inhibition assessment in this investigation employed the classical Go/No-go paradigm (see Fig. 3c), with detailed experimental parameters configured as follows: The Go/No-go task was specifically designed to evaluate participants’ response inhibition capabilities through behavioral performance metrics. The task was programmed using MATLAB 2022b (MathWorks®) with Psychtoolbox extensions for precise stimulus control. Stimuli were presented in randomized sequences while recording four key parameters: reaction time (RT), accuracy rate (ACC), omission errors (failed Go responses), and commission errors (false No-go responses) through MATLAB’s data acquisition module. The visual discrimination protocol utilized letter stimuli - ‘M’ as Go signal requiring dominant index finger response (J-key press), and ‘W’ as No-go signal mandating response suppression. The target stimulus disappeared immediately upon response execution, or persisted for 1000 ms before automatic termination during response withholding trials. A 20-trial practice session preceded formal testing to ensure task comprehension and keyboard orientation familiarity. The experimental protocol commenced after successful practice completion (≥ 90% accuracy criterion). During formal testing, stimuli appeared centrally with 800 ms display duration, separated by variable inter-stimulus intervals (200–600 ms) to prevent temporal anticipation.The complete paradigm comprised 120 trials following probability distribution parameters [40]: 90 Go trials (75% frequency) and 30 No-go trials (25% frequency). Each trial’s behavioral data (RT/ACC) were automatically logged via millisecond-precision timestamp recording.

Stroop task

The Stroop task was programmed using MATLAB® 2022b (MathWorks) to assess conflict inhibition mechanisms (see Fig. 3c). Stimulus presentation followed a randomized sequence, with MATLAB’s data acquisition module recording reaction time (RT), accuracy rate (ACC), omission errors, and commission errors. The stimulus set comprised Chinese characters 红 (red), 黄 (yellow), 蓝 (blue), and 绿 (green) displayed in incongruent color-font combinations. Each character appeared in one of four chromatic hues (red, yellow, blue, green), creating 16 possible color-character pairings. This yielded 4 congruent stimuli (character-color matches) and 12 incongruent stimuli (character-color mismatches), presented in pseudorandom order. The formal experiment consisted of two counterbalanced blocks. Congruent trials (C-trials) featured matched color-character pairs (e.g., 红 in red ink), while incongruent trials (I-trials) contained mismatched pairs (e.g., 蓝 in red ink). Participants were instructed to identify character meanings while inhibiting automatic color processing. A 24-trial practice block preceded formal testing to minimize input errors from color-keyboard mapping unfamiliarity. Formal testing commenced only after participants achieved ≥ 85% accuracy during practice. Response mapping followed QWERTY key assignments: D(red), F(yellow), J(blue), K(green). Participants made speeded responses to character meanings regardless of display colors. Performance optimization required balancing speed and accuracy. Stimuli remained visible for 1500 ms, followed by randomly jittered inter-trial intervals (200–1000 ms) to prevent anticipatory responses. Trial-specific RT/ACC data were recorded with millisecond precision. Conflict control capacity was quantified through Stroop interference effects, calculated as: (Incongruent trial RT - Congruent trial RT) and (Congruent trial ACC - Incongruent trial ACC). Smaller interference magnitudes indicated superior inhibitory control. The complete paradigm contained 72 trials (36 congruent/36 incongruent) with equal probability distribution.

Data acquisition and analysis

Electrophysiological data acquisition was conducted in a university-affiliated neuromotor research facility utilizing a 24-channel Ag/AgCl research-grade EEG cap (Brain Products GmbH) configured according to the international 10–20 system. Four Dell Precision 5860 workstations running Windows 11 were deployed in a dual-system configuration: two stimulus presentation units operated through E-Prime® 3.0, synchronized via BNC trigger cables and Network Time Protocol (NTP) with two EEG acquisition stations equipped with Brain Products ActiChamp amplifiers (± 2ms temporal precision). Testing occurred in an electromagnetically shielded chamber with sound-attenuated partitions between stations, employing the NCERP-190,012 high-density EEG system (Shanghai Nuocheng) with mastoid references (M1/M2) and sub-5kΩ electrode-scalp impedances throughout recordings.

Neurophysiological data processing implemented MATLAB® (MathWorks® R2020a) and EEGLAB toolbox (v2021.1) through the following pipeline: (1) Raw data importation with sensor localization; (2) Removal of non-essential biosignal channels (HEOG/VEOG); (3) FIR bandpass filtering (0.3–30 Hz, 24 dB/octave roll-off); (4) Reference conversion to averaged mastoids; (5) Visual artifact rejection with bad channel interpolation; (6) Epoch extraction locked to correct behavioral responses; (7) Time-locked segmentation (−200 ms to + 1000 ms post-stimulus; Go/No-go: 800 ms); (8) Resampling to 256 Hz; (9) ICA decomposition for ocular/myo-cardiac artifact removal; (10) ERP waveform averaging.

Midline electrodes (Fz, Cz, Pz) were analyzed as regions-of-interest given their anatomical correspondence to frontoparietal inhibitory networks and optimal N2/P3 signal-to-noise ratios. Following established protocols [41, 42], component quantification adopted these time windows: Go/No-go N2 (200–400 ms), P3 (300–542 ms); Stroop N2 (170–230 ms), P3 (220–400 ms). Peak amplitudes and latencies were extracted using semi-automated detection algorithms, with statistical exclusion criteria removing outliers exceeding three standard deviations from condition-specific means.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and visualization were conducted using SPSS® 29.0, MATLAB® R2020a, and GraphPad Prism® 8.0. Continuous variables demonstrating approximate normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± SD), with between-group comparisons analyzed through independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)] and evaluated using Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages) with χ² tests for group comparisons.

Mauchly’s test of sphericity was systematically applied, with Greenhouse-Geisser corrections implemented for violations of sphericity assumptions (ε > 0.75). Repeated measures analyses incorporated Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons to maintain family-wise error rate (FWER) at α = 0.05. A priori normality verification for reaction time and accuracy metrics was established through Shapiro-Wilk tests. All inferential statistics employed two-tailed testing with significance threshold α = 0.05.

Behavioral Analysis: Go/No-go Task: 2 (Time: pre-test/post-test) × 2 (Condition: Go/No-go) × 3 (Group: control/treadmill/taekwondo) repeated measures ANOVA for accuracy metrics; one-way ANOVA for Go reaction times.Stroop Task: 2 (Time) × 3 (Group) repeated measures ANOVA for accuracy and reaction times in correct trials.

ERP Analysis: Go/No-go & Stroop Tasks: 2 (Time) × 2 (Condition) × 3 (Region: Fz/Cz/Pz) × 3 (Group) repeated measures ANOVA for N2/P3 component amplitudes and latencies. Greenhouse-Geisser corrections applied where appropriate.

Affective State Analysis: Combined one-way ANOVA with paired-samples t-tests for acute mood assessments.Inhibitory function relationships were evaluated through delta calculations (post-test minus pre-test) with Pearson correlation coefficients.

Discussion

Single exercise can improve the inhibitory function of undergraduate students with depressive symptoms

Influence on behavior

The study found that both single-bout exercise interventions significantly improved accuracy under No-go conditions in the Go/No-go task, indicating enhanced inhibitory control in individuals with depressive symptoms, which aligns with our initial hypothesis. Both the taekwondo exercise group and treadmill exercise group demonstrated significantly higher No-go accuracy during post-testing compared to the control group. From a neural mechanism perspective [43], various exercise modalities may improve cognitive control by modulating prefrontal cortex (PFC) functionality. The No-go task primarily engages the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [44], key regions responsible for inhibiting inappropriate responses, conflict monitoring, and executive function regulation. Exercise-induced increases in brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) release may play a crucial mediating role [45]. As a vital neurotrophic factor, BDNF significantly contributes to neural plasticity and synaptic remodeling [46]. Acute exercise elevates BDNF expression levels, enhances neuroplasticity, and consequently improves cognitive processing accuracy [47]. Notably, only the taekwondo group showed significant accuracy improvements in both Go and No-go conditions, while the treadmill group’s enhancement was restricted to No-go performance. These findings suggest differential effects of exercise types on inhibitory control capabilities [48]. Taekwondo requires rapid decision-making, response control, and complex motor planning. The continuous environmental monitoring and quick action execution during training may induce stronger activation in the PFC and associated cognitive control networks [49]. Compared to treadmill exercise alone, taekwondo may demonstrate superior potential for enhancing executive function and overall cognitive flexibility. However, our study revealed no significant between-group differences in reaction times during Go/No-go tasks, suggesting acute exercise exerts limited immediate effects on task execution speed [50]. This finding aligns with previous research [51] indicating acute exercise primarily enhances inhibitory control rather than response speed. The parietal cortex and basal ganglia play crucial roles in action preparation-execution coordination. Acute exercise may not sufficiently modulate these regions’ functionality within brief durations to alter reaction times. The absence of reaction time changes does not negate exercise’s inhibitory benefits [52], as evidence shows improved accuracy without compromised speed in depressed populations. These results support the executive function optimization hypothesis, positing that acute exercise enhances performance through cognitive control rather than response acceleration. Thus, in accuracy-demanding cognitive tasks, acute exercise primarily reduces errors rather than accelerating responses [53]. In Stroop tasks, only the taekwondo group showed significant pre-post accuracy improvements, outperforming controls during post-testing. This suggests acute taekwondo training enhances conflict inhibition capabilities [54]. Exercise facilitates anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) activation, improving interference suppression during conflict tasks [55]. Taekwondo’s cognitive-motor demands (rapid decision-making, target tracking, movement adjustments) may potentiate ACC-mediated conflict monitoring, optimizing Stroop performance. Taekwondo participants demonstrated significantly shorter reaction times than both control and treadmill groups in post-test Stroop tasks, highlighting its unique executive function benefits. The cognitively engaging nature of taekwondo may enhance cerebral blood flow and BDNF levels, facilitating neural activation and information processing efficiency [56]. Acute exercise increases norepinephrine, dopamine, and acetylcholine release [57], potentially improving vigilance and neurotransmission efficiency in depressed individuals during Stroop tasks.

This investigation demonstrates that acute exercise significantly enhances inhibitory control capabilities. Notably, taekwondo’s heightened cognitive demands and complex motor patterns appear to exert more pronounced effects on cognitive control during high-conflict processing tasks (e.g., Stroop tasks). These findings establish an empirical foundation for implementing diverse exercise modalities in clinical interventions targeting inhibitory function enhancement and mental health optimization.

Effect on nerve electrophysiology

Our multimodal investigation combining behavioral assessments and event-related potential (ERP) recordings revealed differential effects of exercise modalities on conflict inhibition. Specifically, the taekwondo group exhibited significant N2 amplitude enhancement under No-go conditions during Go/No-go tasks, a phenomenon absent in treadmill and control groups. These findings suggest taekwondo training optimizes conflict monitoring mechanisms [58], enabling faster detection and resolution of response conflicts - a conclusion corroborated by congruent behavioral improvements in depressed participants. The cognitive demands of taekwondo (rapid action prediction and adaptive responding) critically engage prefrontal-mediated cognitive control and conflict monitoring systems [59]. ERP analyses demonstrated taekwondo-induced N2 amplitude enhancement at parietal (Pz) electrode sites, potentially reflecting optimized attentional resource allocation during task difficulty adaptation [60]. The automated nature of treadmill running, requiring minimal higher-order cognitive control, failed to elicit comparable neurophysiological changes. While aerobic exercise enhances global cognition via cerebrovascular and neurochemical modulation, its conflict monitoring benefits appear inferior to cognitively-engaging taekwondo training. Both interventions increased Go-trial N2 amplitudes [61], indicating general enhancements in stimulus processing efficiency. Frontal (Fz) and central (Cz) N2 modulations suggested differential neural mechanisms: Fz changes implicated anterior cingulate-mediated conflict monitoring, while Cz variations reflected sensorimotor integration improvements [62]. Stroop task ERPs revealed exercise-induced N2 enhancements across both intervention groups [63], confirming general conflict inhibition benefits in depression. Post-intervention between-group comparisons showed taekwondo-specific N2 superiority over both treadmill and control conditions, highlighting its unique neurocognitive advantages. N2 latency reductions emerged differentially: Treadmill training accelerated parietal N2 latency during Go trials [64], suggesting enhanced response execution speed. Conversely, prolonged frontal N2 latency in treadmill participants under No-go conditions [65] implied lingering conflict resolution difficulties post-exercise. The taekwondo group demonstrated accelerated frontal and central N2 latencies, indicating streamlined conflict monitoring and motor inhibition processes. Neurophysiologically, N2 acceleration correlates with enhanced processing efficiency in prefrontal (particularly ACC)-parietal networks [66] governing conflict detection and cognitive flexibility. The high cognitive load of taekwondo - requiring sustained error suppression - likely strengthens ACC functionality and frontoparietal connectivity, optimizing conflict resolution in Stroop paradigms. Emerging evidence suggests cognitively-demanding exercise may enhance white matter myelination[67], potentially explaining the observed neural efficiency improvements [67]. While aerobic exercise promotes general neural plasticity, its effects on conflict-specific neural circuitry appear less pronounced than those of complex motor-cognitive training.

Second, this study investigated P3 amplitude modulations in university students with depressive symptoms during Go-Nogo tasks following distinct acute exercise modalities, elucidating divergent neurocognitive mechanisms underlying cognitive control and response inhibition. Under Go conditions, the treadmill exercise group exhibited significant P3 amplitude enhancements at the frontal midline (Fz), suggesting improved resource allocation for task processing and enhanced response flexibility. In contrast, the taekwondo group demonstrated significant P3 amplitude increases across Fz, Cz, and Pz electrodes, indicating taekwondo training augmented both cognitive load regulation during task execution and information processing efficiency in conflict resolution [68]. Depressive symptom carriers demonstrated significant improvements in rapid response execution and decision-making precision following high cognitive-engagement exercise (taekwondo), with concomitant neural efficiency enhancements during cognitively demanding tasks[68]. Under No-go conditions, exclusive P3 amplitude modulations at parietal (Pz) loci in the taekwondo group[69] substantiated enhanced response inhibition through optimized conflict monitoring mechanisms. The treadmill group’s absence of significant changes under No-go conditions likely reflects its automated movement patterns requiring lower cognitive control demands [69]. These findings gained corroboration in Stroop paradigm results. During congruent conditions, taekwondo exercisers uniquely displayed P3 amplitude elevations at Pz, implying enhanced attentional modulation capabilities even under low-conflict scenarios [70]. Under incongruent conditions, Fz P3 amplitude enhancements revealed taekwondo training strengthened prefrontal cortex (PFC) engagement in conflict processing while improving cognitive resource allocation during incompatible stimulus resolution [71], concurrent with functional enhancements in the executive control network (PFC-ACC) [72]. Regarding P3 latency, both exercise groups exhibited reduced Fz latencies during Go-Nogo tasks, indicating accelerated cognitive processing [73]. Treadmill exercisers’ latency reductions potentially stemmed from improved cardiovascular function and cerebral blood flow, whereas taekwondo practitioners demonstrated additional Cz latency reductions, suggesting complex motor skill training enhanced cognitive flexibility and information integration [74]. In Stroop tasks, both interventions improved P3 latencies at Fz and Pz, reflecting optimized attentional resource allocation [75]. Crucially, only taekwondo training induced Cz latency reductions, implying high-cognitive-demand exercise enhances cortico-motor network integration for cognitive-motor coordination. Compared to treadmill training, taekwondo’s requirement for real-time environmental stimulus analysis likely potentiates the central cortex’s role in cognitive-motor synergy [76].

In conclusion, acute taekwondo training demonstrated superior efficacy compared to treadmill exercise in enhancing conflict monitoring and inhibitory control, validating our primary hypothesis. This advantage likely originates from its heightened cognitive demands, multimodal sensorimotor integration, and rapid decision-making imperatives—factors that may drive adaptive neuroplasticity in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and associated neural networks. In contrast, treadmill training demonstrated generalized executive function improvements yet exhibited constrained therapeutic specificity for conflict inhibition. These findings provide novel neuroscientific evidence supporting the clinical implementation of cognitively enriched exercise protocols to augment cognitive control capacities in university students with depressive symptoms.

Single exercise can improve the instant mood of undergraduate students with depressive symptoms

The present findings demonstrate significantly lower immediate mood scale scores in depressive-symptomatic university students following acute moderate-intensity exercise compared to controls, with consistent observations across both treadmill and taekwondo groups, thereby validating our hypothesis. Neurophysiologically, post-exercise enhancements in right parietal electrode connectivity—correlated with sustained sympathetic nervous system reactivity during recovery—co-occurred with increased frontal-derived connectivity during resting states and cognitive tasks, collectively facilitating negative affect alleviation [77]. Crucially, emotion regulation emerges as a pivotal mediator, with prior evidence indicating acute exercise attenuates emotion-modulated N1 component alterations [78]. Concurrently, individuals exhibiting amplified emotional responses to positive stimuli post-exercise demonstrated heightened reward sensitivity, suggesting exercise-induced potentiation of positive emotional reactivity in depressive symptomatology [79]. Furthermore, exercise amplified emotional engagement with positive stimuli, potentially counteracting the blunted affective responsiveness to rewarding content characteristic of depression—specifically, selectively enhancing hedonic responsiveness to pleasurable stimuli [80]. Notably, while both exercise modalities effectively reduced negative affect, mechanistic divergences were identified. Taekwondo’s requirement for complex sensorimotor coordination and sustained cognitive control likely drives superior prefrontal cortex (PFC) activation and executive function enhancement, attributable to its demand for real-time tactical adaptation and rapid decision-making. This cognitively intensive modality may preferentially modulate PFC-limbic connectivity, reducing amygdala hyperactivation to optimize emotional regulation. In contrast, treadmill exercise—predominantly aerobic and metabolically driven—appears more reliant on neurotransmitter modulation (e.g., serotonin, dopamine) [81, 82], with established evidence linking sustained aerobic activity to endorphin-mediated euphoria and negative affect reduction [83]. Thus, our findings confirm that divergent acute exercise protocols rapidly ameliorate negative affect through distinct yet complementary neurophysiological pathways. This supports the clinical rationale for implementing brief, moderate-intensity exercise interventions as precision tools for emotion regulation, stress mitigation, and mental health optimization.

The correlation between instant emotion and inhibitory function

The present findings substantiate our hypothesis by revealing significant correlations between acute mood fluctuations and post-exercise inhibitory control changes. Specifically, mood variation scores exhibited a negative correlation with behavioral inhibition accuracy and a positive correlation with conflict inhibition reaction times. These results suggest that enhanced behavioral inhibition performance—indicative of elevated executive agency—facilitates emotional amelioration, whereas shortened reaction times under conflict conditions reflect reduced impulsive responses through heightened cognitive engagement during inhibition-demanding scenarios, thereby contributing to negative affect mitigation.

This study further demonstrates that negative emotional improvements in depressive-symptomatic university students are mechanistically linked to inhibitory control enhancement—a relationship corroborated by prior research [84], which identified significant positive correlations between inhibitory control gains and mood regulation outcomes. The acute metabolic shifts induced by moderate exercise, including elevated cerebral oxygenation and transient upregulation of dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine levels, likely underpin these effects. Concurrently, exercise selectively activates prefrontal subregions critical for emotional regulation, particularly the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) [85]. Emerging evidence confirms that acute exercise elevates neurotrophic factors [86], such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which enhance neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and affective-cognitive integration [87]. Supporting our correlation analyses, Bartholomew et al. demonstrated that acute exercise not only augments cognitive performance but also ameliorates mood in depressive populations [87, 88], potentially mediated by transient neurotransmitter release [89].

Clinically, these findings provide novel evidence supporting exercise interventions as adjunctive therapies for major depressive disorder. Acute moderate-intensity exercise may serve dual therapeutic roles: alleviating affective symptoms and strengthening cognitive control via inhibitory function optimization. In educational contexts, institutions are advised to integrate structured exercise protocols—such as aerobic training or cognitively demanding disciplines (e.g., taekwondo)—into academic schedules to enhance emotional stability and academic performance in at-risk student populations.

Conclusions

A single session of moderate-intensity exercise, regardless of modality, exerts acute positive effects on both inhibitory control and transient mood states in university students with depressive symptoms. Exercise induces proactive behavioral responses in this population, with enhanced inhibitory performance mediating reductions in negative affect. Open-skill exercise paradigms with high cognitive demands (e.g., taekwondo) demonstrate superior acute efficacy compared to closed-skill modalities (e.g., treadmill running) in improving both cognitive and affective outcomes. Based on these findings, actionable recommendations include the following: educational policymakers and clinical practitioners should strategically redesign and implement “exercise prescription protocols.” Open-skill interventions requiring advanced cognitive engagement (e.g., martial arts, interactive sports) should be prioritized in the design and execution of exercise-based therapeutic regimens for individuals with depressive symptoms.
