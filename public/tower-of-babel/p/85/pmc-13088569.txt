Haplotype-based genome-wide association study uncovers QTL for fruit-related traits in the 287 tomato accessions
Authors: Tuong Tuyet Nhung Nguyen, Jaehun Heo, Byounghwan Lim, Sung-Chur Sim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenome-wide association study (GWAS) is a strategy for genetic dissection of quantitative traits with high mapping resolution. Haplotypes based on multiple SNPs provide an effective alternative for exploring loci associated with complex quantitative traits. In this study, haplotype-based GWAS (Hap-GWAS) was conducted for seven fruit-related traits in the 287 tomato accessions, consisting of 237 S. lycopersicum, 30 S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme, and 20 S. pimpinellifolium.ResultsPhenotypic variations of the fruit-related traits (weight, shape, locule number, pericarp thickness, number of flowers, number of fruits, and Brix) were assessed in three-years of field trials. Over 28.7 million SNPs were identified from whole genome-resequencing and a core set of 137,706 SNPs was used to construct 3,884 haplotype blocks across 12 chromosomes. The 11,970 haplotypes in these blocks found high levels of genetic diversity and differentiation with and between species in the GWAS panel. A total of 32 haplotypes, corresponding to 30 QTL, showed significant associations with fruit-related traits at a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P < 0.05 in at least two of the four phenotypic datasets (each of three years and combined), explaining up to 51.67% of the total phenotypic variance. Of these, 22 haplotypes were associated with 1–11 putative novel QTL across the seven traits and three haplotypes exhibited significant associations with multiple traits, suggesting pleiotropic effects of QTL. Furthermore, Hap-GWAS identified additional QTL that were not detected by SNP-based analysis, indicating the complementary power of this approach to capture multi-allelic and LD-based genetic effects underlying complex traits.ConclusionsThese results support the effectiveness of Hap-GWAS in dissecting quantitative traits and facilitate development of an advanced breeding strategy for improving fruit-related traits in tomato.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08494-x.

Background

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is a major vegetable with global production exceeding 192 million tons in 2023 (FAO, 2025) [1]. With the economic value, this crop has been extensively studied to improve fruit-related traits, which are important to determine cultivar’s market values. Biparental populations such as F2, backcross (BC), and recombinant inbred lines (RILs) have been used for genetic dissection of these traits, and major genes, including fw, ovate, sun, lc, and fas, were found in tomato [2–7]. However, this biparental association analysis has disadvantages for favorable allele mining in diverse genetic backgrounds and high mapping resolution [8–10]. During the last few decades, advances in genome sequencing technology have led to discovery of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across chromosomes and facilitated genome-wide association study (GWAS), which is an effective method to explore loci for quantitative traits using historical recombinations accumulated in natural populations and germplasm accessions [11–13].

Several GWAS have identified quantitative trait loci (QTL) for fruit-related traits using various mapping panels in tomato [9, 14–17]. In a tomato collection and two F2 populations, a number of loci for four traits (weight, shape, color, and soluble solid content) were detected on 11 chromosomes using the 547 InDels [14]. The association analysis with 8,550 SNPs in a panel of 192 tomato accessions revealed 41 marker-trait associations (MTAs) for weight, height, width, shape, color, and pericarp thickness [15]. Furthermore, 30 MTAs for eight fruit-related traits were detected using the 34,550 SNPs and three-year phenotypic datasets in the 162 tomato accessions and 16 MTAs suggested potential novel QTL [9]. Using the 37,300 and 21 million SNPs, two additional studies also mapped loci associated with fruit-related traits in the 244 and 214 tomato accessions, respectively [16, 17].

Although GWAS has been successfully used for QTL mapping with high resolution, SNP has a limitation to capture allelic diversity present in a germplasm collection due to its bi-allelic nature at a locus. In addition, SNP-based GWAS is less suitable for complex quantitative traits with epistatic interactions between SNPs, which can be important for trait expression [18, 19]. A number of SNPs can be grouped in a haplotype block based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) and generate multi-allelic haplotypes, which provide an alternative approach to overcome the disadvantage of SNP and improve the power of GWAS [20–22]. The association analysis with haplotypes in maize improved statistical significance of loci and estimation of allelic effects for drought tolerance [23]. Recently, a haplotype-based GWAS (Hap-GWAS) was conducted to investigate genetic architecture of grain yield in four different environments using a collection of spring bread wheat breeding lines [24]. With 519 haplotype blocks, this study revealed 30 stable genomic regions for grain yield across multiple environments. The other Hap-GWAS in a winter wheat mapping panel detected not only additional loci for leaf rust resistance but also increased predictability compared with the SNP-based approach [25]. A total of 784 haplotype blocks were constructed from 5,995 SNPs to investigate loci associated with 25 metabolic traits (sugars, organic acids, and amino acids) and fruit weight in the 163 tomato accessions, including 116 S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme, 31 S. lycopersicum (large-fruit tomato), and 16 S. pimpinellifolium (wild progenitor) [26]. The haplotype-based Bayes model showed superior performance for detecting marker-trait associations relative to the SNP-based Bayes model and the multi-locus mixed model.

To investigate novel QTL, we conducted a Hap-GWAS for seven fruit-related traits (weight, shape, locule number, pericarp thickness, number of flowers, number of fruits, and Brix) in a mapping panel of 287 tomato accessions with diverse genetic backgrounds. NGS-based whole-genome resequencing provided a core set of 137,706 SNPs across 12 chromosomes and these SNPs were used to determine haplotype blocks based on the linkage disequilibrium method. A total of 3,884 haplotype blocks were constructed and the resulting 11,970 haplotypes were used for Hap-GWAS. With four phenotypic datasets from three-year field trials, the BLINK model detected several haplotypes associated with novel QTL for each trait, demonstrating the potential of Hap-GWAS for genetic dissection of the fruit-related traits in tomato. Our results also provide a valuable insight and resource for improving fruit-related traits in tomato breeding programs.

Methods

Plant materials

A collection of 287 tomato accessions was used in this study, consisting of 237 Solanum lycopersicum, 30 S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme, and 20 S. pimpinellifolium. The accessions originated from at least 23 countries, including the Republic of Korea (ROK), Peru, Russia, Uzbekistan, Ecuador, Mexico, the United States, and China (Table S3). Of these, 108 breeding lines were assembled from the National Institute of Horticultural and Herbal Science (NIHH) and the National Agrobiodiversity Center (NAC) in the Rural Development Administration, ROK. The other 96 breeding lines were provided by the NH Nongwoobio company, ROK. In addition, 83 accessions were derived from the Germplasm Resources Information Network (GRIN) in the U. S. Department of Agriculture, the C. M. Rick Tomato Genetics Resource Center (TGRC), and Sejong University.

Phenotypic evaluation

Field trials were conducted over three years (2023–2025) to evaluate fruit weight, fruit shape index, locule number, pericarp thickness, number of flowers, number of fruits, and Brix in the 287 tomato accessions. Six to seven-week-old seedlings were transplanted in the plastic-covered field with 30 cm spacing between plants. A randomized complete block design was used with three replicates per genotype and three plants per replicate. Phenotypic variations for seven fruit-related traits were assessed using ripe fruits harvested from the second inflorescence. The 10 fruits per accession were evaluated for small- and medium-fruited genotypes, and six fruits for large-fruited genotypes, according to fruit size classes defined by fruit diameter as small (< 47 mm), medium (47–67 mm), and large (> 67 mm) [27]. Fruit weight and Brix were measured using the RE500 digital scale (CASKOREA, Seongnam, South Korea) and PAL-1 refractometer (ATAGO, WA, USA), respectively. Image analysis was performed for fruit shape index, locule number, and pericarp thickness. For this, the harvested fruits were cut longitudinally and horizontally through the center, and the planar surfaces were scanned at 300 dpi. The images were processed with the Tomato Analyzer v4.0 software [28] to obtain quantitative descriptors. The numbers of flowers and fruits were evaluated by manually counting all flowers and fruits on the second inflorescence. Counts were recorded for each plant per replicate and averaged. The phenotypic data were corrected for environmental effects using the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) in the lme4 package in R [29]. Additionally, these data collected in 2023–2025 were combined using BLUP. The resulting four phenotypic data (each of three years and combined) were independently analyzed for further analysis. Phenotypic correlations between years and among traits were estimated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient [30]. The genotype × environment (year) interaction was estimated using analysis of variance (ANOVA) based on three-year phenotypic data from 287 accessions. Whole-genome resequencing, SNP identification, and haplotype construction

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from fresh and young leaves of four-week-old seedlings using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The isolated gDNA was diluted to a final concentration of 50 ng/µL for whole-genome resequencing using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared from 100 ng of gDNA per accession following the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Libraries were sequenced in paired-end mode (2 × 150 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked using FastQC v0.11.9 [32] and clean reads were mapped on the Heinz 1706 genome assembly vSL4.0 (https://solgenomics.net) using BWA v0.7.17 [33]. Variant calling and filtering were performed using GATK v4.1.7.0 [34], and SNPs were subjected to quality control with VCFtools v0.1.16 [35]. Bi-allelic SNPs were filtered based on ≥ 5x of read depth, ≤ 15% of missing data rate, ≤ 5% of heterozygosity rate, and ≥ 5% of minor allele frequency (MAF). Subsequently, missing data were imputed using BEAGLE v5.4 [36] and a core set was generated by selecting one SNP per 3 kb window.

Haplotype blocks were defined using the core SNPs in Haploview v4.2 [37], applying the confidence interval method [38]. Strong linkage disequilibrium (LD) was first estimated with an upper 95% confidence bound of D′ > 0.98 and a lower bound of D′ > 0.70. LD statistics were computed for markers within a 500 kb window by default, with run parameters set to a recombination confidence interval upper bound of 0.90 and a minimum informative comparison fraction of 0.95. Haplotypes were then numerically encoded (0 = absent, 1 = present) using the Haploview2gapit Python script [39] and filtered with MAF ≥ 5% for further analysis.

Association analysis

Genetic relationships in the GWAS panel were assessed using haplotype data. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the prcomp function in R [40] and visualized with the factoextra package [41]. Population structure was inferred with the sparse non-negative matrix factorization (SNMF) method, as implemented in the LEA package [42], with 100,000 iterations and 20 replicates for K values ranging from 1 to 10. The optimal number of clusters (K) was determined using cross-entropy cross-validation. Based on the best K, membership coefficients of tomato accessions (Q-matrix) were visualized using Structure Plot v2.0 [43].

To identify QTL for seven fruit-related traits, both haplotype- and SNP-based genome-wide association studies were conducted using the Bayesian-information and Linkage-disequilibrium Iteratively Nested Keyway (BLINK) model [44], as implemented in the Genome Association and Prediction Integrated Tools (GAPIT) [45]. To account for population structure and familial relatedness [12], the model incorporated Q- and kinship matrices as covariates. The kinship matrix was generated using the VanRaden method [46]. Significant haplotype- or SNP-trait associations were determined using a FDR adjusted P < 0.05 threshold based on the Benjamini-Hochberg method [47] for each of the four phenotypic datasets. The significant associations were visualized using the ggplot2 package in R [48]. QTL were classified as putatively novel if no previously known loci were detected within a ± 5 Mb genomic window flanking each significant haplotype or SNP. Candidate genes corresponding to putative novel QTL were identified using the tomato genome assembly vSL4.0 and the International Tomato Annotation Group (ITAG) v4.1 (https://www.solgenomics.net).

Results

Phenotypic variations of seven fruit-related traits in the 287 tomato accessions

The GWAS panel showed broad phenotypic variations for seven fruit-related traits in 2023–2025 (Fig. 1). Fruit weight ranged from 2.99 to 306.79 g across three years, with the means of 62.69 ± 51.93 g (2023), 66.54 ± 54.27 g (2024), and 51.03 ± 46.63 g (2025). The BLUP-based combined data showed a range of 0.67–281.08 g with a mean of 60.24 ± 50.33 g. The Pearson correlation coefficients between years were high, ranging from 0.85 to 0.90 for this trait (Table S1). For fruit shape index, phenotypic variation ranged from 0.62 to 1.87, with mean values of 0.96 ± 0.20 to 0.99 ± 0.20 across the four datasets (each of three years and combined). The highest inter-year correlation was found with coefficients of 0.94 (both 2023–2024 and 2023–2025) and 0.95 (2024–2025). Locule number ranged from 1.86 to 12.55, while pericarp thickness ranged from 1.56 mm to 8.95 mm, with mean values of 3.26 ± 1.61 to 3.61 ± 1.86 and 4.55 ± 1.00 mm to 4.83 ± 1.64 mm, respectively (Fig. 1). Additionally, the inter-year correlation coefficients ranged from 0.90 to 0.92 for locule number and 0.80 to 0.90 for pericarp thickness (Table S1). Substantial phenotypic variations were observed for number of flowers (3.74–99.15) and number of fruits (0.40–31.79) in the four datasets. These traits showed lower inter-year correlation coefficients compared to the other four traits (fruit weight, fruit shape index, locule number, and pericarp thickness), with ranges of 0.78 to 0.80 and 0.66 to 0.85 (Table S1). Brix ranged from 3.28% to 10.57%, with mean values of 4.76 ± 1.01% to 5.89 ± 1.10%. The inter-year correlation coefficients for Brix were also relatively low, ranging from 0.71 to 0.80. We also observed high levels of broad-sense heritability (H2) for seven traits, ranging from 0.50 (number of fruits) to 0.91 (fruit shape index). Although significant genotype × environment (year) interactions (P < 0.001) were detected for all traits, their variance components except number of fruits were smaller than those of the residuals (Table S2).

Phenotypic correlations between traits were also assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) in the four datasets (Figure S1). Fruit weight was positively correlated at P < 0.001 with locule number (r = 0.61 to 0.74) and pericarp thickness (r = 0.69 to 0.81), while it showed significant negative correlations with fruit shape index (r = -0.30 to -0.35), number of flowers (r = -0.48 to -0.38), number of fruits (r = -0.47 to -0.29) and Brix (r = -0.62 to -0.51). Conversely, fruit shape index showed significant positive correlations at P < 0.001 with number of flowers (r = 0.22 to 0.27), number of fruits (r = 0.34 to 0.44), and Brix (r = 0.24 to 0.40). However, it was negatively correlated with locule number (r = -0.52 to -0.49) and pericarp thickness (r = -0.16 to -0.05). We also found different levels of significant correlations between the other traits at P < 0.001. In addition, the 2023 phenotypic data showed large discrepancies in correlation levels relative to the other three datasets (2024, 2025, and combined), particularly between pericarp thickness and number of fruits, and also between numbers of flowers and fruits (Figure S1).

Genome-wide SNP identification and haplotyping

Whole-genome resequencing of 287 tomato accessions provided 4,556.5 billion raw reads with an average length of 151 bp, corresponding to a total length of 688.03 Tb (Table 1). Of these, 99.45% reads were mapped to the Heinz 1706 reference genome vSL4.0, yielding 684,256.5 Gb of mapped sequences with 97.45% genomic coverage. A total of 28,797,776 SNPs were detected, and 1,382,729 confident SNPs were filtered based ≥ 5x of read depth, ≤ 15% of missing data rate, ≤ 5% of heterozygosity rate, and ≥ 5% of minor allele frequency. From the filtered SNPs, 137,706 core SNPs were obtained by selecting one per 3 kb window (Table 1).

Genetic diversity and population structure of the GWAS panel

Principal component analysis (PCA) based on the 11,970 haplotypes showed genetic diversity and differentiation within and between species in the 287 tomato accessions (Fig. 2). The first three PCs explained 13.6% (PC1), 7.4% (PC2), and 4.4% (PC3) of the total variation, respectively (Fig. 2A). Most of S. lycopersicum accessions were separated from both S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme and S. pimpinellifolium accessions. Within S. lycopersicum (n = 237), the accessions were divided into three clusters (Fig. 2B). Population structure was further assessed using sparse non-negative matrix factorization (SNMF). Cross-entropy progressively decreased with increasing K and reached a plateau at approximately K = 5, with minimal changes observed at higher K values (Fig. 2C; Figure S2). At K = 5, individuals were partitioned into five genetic clusters reflecting species composition (Fig. 2D; Table S3). Clusters 1–3 exclusively comprised S. lycopersicum accessions (109, 62, and 19, respectively), whereas cluster 4 included 21 S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme, 45 S. lycopersicum, and one S. pimpinellifolium accession. The other 19 S. pimpinellifolium accessions were clustered in cluster 5 along with nine S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme and two S. lycopersicum accessions. The number of accessions in each cluster showed different levels of admixture (Fig. 2D; Table S3).

Identification of loci associated with fruit-related traits

Haplotype-based GWAS (Hap-GWAS) was conducted for seven fruit-related traits using the 11,970 haplotypes across four phenotypic datasets (Table 3; Fig. 3 and Figure S3). The Q-Q plot of the observed and expected -log₁₀(P) value showed that most points clustered around the 45° line, with no early deviation between the two values (Figure S3). This pattern indicates the absence of inflation or systematic bias and suggests well-controlled population stratification, whereas deviations observed in the upper tail are likely attributable to true association signals. A total of 32 haplotypes were significantly associated with seven fruit-related traits at a FDR adjusted P < 0.05 across at least two of the datasets, suggesting the presence of 30 QTL (Table 3; Fig. 3 and Figure S3). For fruit weight, three QTL were identified on chromosomes 2, 10 and 11, explaining from 0.84–31.62% of the total phenotypic variance (Table 3). The five haplotypes, which correspond to three QTL, showed significant associations for fruit shape index on chromosomes 2 and 8 (Table 3). Three of these, located on chromosomes 2 (C02-HB201-03, C02-HB256-03) and 8 (C08-HB092-03), were detected consistently across all four datasets. The phenotypic variance explained (PVE) by significant haplotypes ranged from 3.56% to 51.67%.

For locule number and pericarp thickness, four and one haplotypes, respectively, were significantly associated at a FDR adjusted P < 0.05, indicating distinct QTL for each trait (Table 3). Two haplotypes on chromosomes 2 (C02-HB254-02) and 11 (C11-HB256-02) showed significant associations across all four datasets and accounted for 19.36–43.44% of the total phenotypic variance for locule number, while the other haplotypes explained relatively small proportions (0.1–4.2%). Furthermore, C11-HB256-02 on chromosome 11 was also significantly associated with fruit weight, suggesting that it represents a pleiotropic QTL. For pericarp thickness, C01-HB331-06 on chromosome 1 explained 0.13% (2023) to 0.57% (combined) of the total phenotypic variance.

A total of 13 haplotypes, corresponding to 12 QTL for number of flowers, were detected at a FDR adjusted P < 0.05 across eight chromosomes (1–5, 8, 9, and 11) (Table 3). The C02-HB276-02 on chromosome 2 showed the largest PVE in the 2025 (33.50%) and combined (33.00%) datasets. However, the other six haplotypes explained less than 10% of the total phenotypic variance, representing minor-effect QTL. Two haplotypes on chromosomes 2 (C02-HB276-02) and 11 (C11-HB265-03) were also significantly associated with number of fruits, suggesting pleiotropic QTL effects. For number of fruits, six significant haplotypes (four QTL) were identified on chromosomes 2, 4, and 11, explaining up to 49.81% of the total phenotypic variance (Table 3). Significant associations with Brix were found with three haplotypes on chromosomes 7, 9 and 10 (Table 3). The C09-HB060-01 on chromosome 9 showed the largest effect, accounting for 36.45–44.06% of the total phenotypic variance across three phenotypic datasets. The PVE of C07-HB033-02 and C10-HB306-04 ranged from 6.43% to 9.11%.

To facilitate comparison and support the Hap-GWAS results, SNP-based GWAS was performed with the 137,706 markers, which were also used to construct 3,884 haplotype blocks. A total of 32 significant SNPs were identified across multiple phenotypic datasets at a FDR adjusted P < 0.05 (Table S4). These SNPs corresponded to 25 QTL including 15 putative novel QTL, whereas Hap-GWAS detected 32 haplotypes representing 30 QTL, including 21 putative novel QTL (Table 3). In addition, 11 QTL (four putative novel and seven known) were consistently identified by both GWAS approaches. However, the number of QTL detected per trait was varied between the two methods. For example, haplotype- and SNP-based GWAS revealed three and one QTL for fruit weight, respectively (Table 3 and S4).

Discussion

The 287 tomato accessions were used as a GWAS panel to explore novel QTL associated with seven fruit-related traits (weight, shape, locule number, pericarp thickness, number of flowers, number of fruits, and Brix) in this study. This mapping panel consisted of 237 S. lycopersicum, 30 S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme, and 20 S. pimpinellifolium accessions. As a wild relative, S. pimpinellifolium produces small red fruits (~ 1 cm in diameter) and is an important source of biotic and abiotic stress tolerances [50–52]. The wild cherry tomato accessions (S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme) were also included in the panel along with the medium- and large-fruited accessions (S. lycopersicum). With these accessions, the GWAS panel showed substantially greater genetic diversity relative to those used in the previous studies [9, 15]. As a result, the broad phenotypic variation across all traits in the 287 tomato accessions facilitated to the identification of novel QTL using a haplotype-based approach.

Multi-allelic haplotypes have been suggested as an effective alternative to the bi-allelic SNPs commonly used for GWAS [20–22]. The haplotype-based GWAS (Hap-GWAS) can enhance statistical power by capturing the genetic architecture of complex quantitative traits and reducing the number of tests required in SNP-based analysis of mapping panels with broad diversity. Recently, Hap-GWAS using 784 haplotype blocks in the 163 tomato accessions detected more significant associations for fruit weight and 25 metabolic traits relative to SNP-based analysis [26]. In the present study, we constructed 3,884 high-density haplotype blocks for Hap-GWAS using a core of 137,706 SNPs that were selected every 3 kb across 1,382,729 SNPs on 12 chromosomes. With one to eight haplotypes per block, a total of 11,970 haplotypes generated sufficient allelic diversity to investigate QTL associated with the seven fruit-related traits. The BLINK model with a FDR adjusted P-value threshold (< 0.05) identified 32 haplotypes significantly associated with seven fruit-related traits, corresponding to 21 putative novel and nine previously known QTL. In comparison, SNP-based GWAS using 137,706 SNPs detected 32 significant associations, which were consolidated into 25 QTL, including 15 putative novel and 10 known QTL. Although a substantial proportion of QTL were consistently detected by both approaches, Hap-GWAS identified several QTL that were not detected by SNP-based analysis. This difference may reflect the ability of haplotypes to capture local allelic combinations and historical recombination patterns. Together, these results support that Hap-GWAS is a complementary approach to SNP-based GWAS, enhancing the genetic dissection of complex quantitative traits.

In tomato, fruit-related traits such as weight and shape have been extensively studied and a number of loci underlying the traits have been identified using SNP-based approaches [53]. However, Hap-GWAS revealed putative novel QTL in addition to known loci for each trait in our study (Fig. 3; Table S5). Two of three haplotypes, which showed significant associations with fruit weight, are located in genomic regions overlapping with the previously known loci, including fw 2 [54], fw 11 [55], and other QTL [9, 15], while the other haplotype indicates the presence of a putative novel QTL (fw 10) with minor effects on chromosome 10. In the fw 10 region, we found a candidate gene encoding gibberellin 20 oxidase 2, which is a key enzyme in gibberellin (GA) biosynthesis involved in the regulation of fruit weight, seed development, and germination in tomato [56, 57]. For fruit shape, two significant haplotypes on chromosomes 2 and 8 supported these regions as putative novel QTL (fsi 2.1 and fsi 8). Candidate gene analysis identified OFP5, a member of the OVATE family associated with fruit shape regulation in tomato [58] and TCP29, a tomato TCP family gene with reported expression in fruits during development and ripening [59]. The other haplotypes were located in the genomic regions of ovate [60] and QTL [15–17, 61]. Furthermore, Hap-GWAS in the present study revealed several putative novel QTL with relatively minor effects for locule number (ln 1), pericarp thickness (pt 1), and Brix (brix 7 and brix 10) across multiple chromosomes in addition to previously known loci associated with these traits, including lc [3], fas [62], LIN5 [63, 64], STP1 [65], SICDPK2 [66], and other QTL [14–16, 61].

Several major genes regulating tomato inflorescence development have been previously characterized, including compound inflorescence (s), anantha (an), falisiflora (fa), single flower truss (sft), and SICOL1 [67–70]. Three haplotypes, which were significantly associated with number of flowers (one) and number of fruits (two), were co-located within the genomic regions of s, an, and SICOL1 on chromosome 2. However, the haplotype associated with number of flowers on chromosome 3 was located approximately 59.9 Mb away from fa and 27.5 Mb from sft, respectively, suggesting the presence of a putative novel QTL. In addition, the other 11 haplotypes associated with number of flowers were detected in the regions of chromosomes 1, 4, 5, 9, and 11, none of which overlapped with known loci. For number of fruits, three putative novel QTL represented by four haplotypes were detected on chromosomes 4 and 11. The candidate gene analysis of these regions identified several genes encoding MYB-related transcript factor [71], MADS-box transcript factor [72], F-box protein [69], and auxin-responsive factor [73].

In conclusion, whole-genome resequencing generated a large number of SNPs (> 28.7 millions) distributed across the 12 tomato chromosomes. Using filtered core SNPs, 3,884 haplotype blocks were defined, and the resulting 11,970 haplotypes were used to identify a total of 21 putative novel QTL, in addition to previously known loci, for seven fruit-related traits. The haplotypes and QTL detected in this study provide a useful resource for further genetic dissection of the fruit-related traits and may also explored in the context of genomic selection (GS), an emerging breeding approach for complex quantitative traits in vegetable crops. Therefore, our results could contribute to future efforts for improving fruit-related traits in tomato breeding programs.
