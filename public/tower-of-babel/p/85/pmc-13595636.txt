Transcutaneous spinal stimulation with upper extremity robotic training in chronic stroke and spinal cord injury: individual neurophysiological and clinical responses
Authors: Jeonghoon Oh, Michelle S. Scheffler, Shane T. King, Erin E. Mahan, Melissa Lee, Catherine Martin, Alexander G. Steele, Jony Sheynin, Jenny Dinh, Argyrios Stampas, Teresa Kaldis, Timea M. Hodics, Marcia K. O’Malley, Dimitry G. Sayenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDamage to the corticospinal tract after stroke and spinal cord injury (SCI) often results in persistent upper extremity (UE) impairment. Transcutaneous spinal stimulation (TSS) and robotic technologies have been explored as approaches to facilitate motor training; however, their combined effects on UE sensorimotor recovery remain poorly understood. The purpose of this study was to examine the effects of TSS combined with UE robotic training in individuals with chronic stroke or SCI.MethodsFive participants with stroke and six with SCI completed a 14-week, sham controlled, single blind crossover study consisting of four total weeks of assessments (one week each pre and post for both training phases), four weeks of UE training with sham TSS, a two-week washout period, and four weeks of UE training with active TSS. Each one-hour session (three days/week) included robotic exoskeleton-assisted UE movements and hand grip training, performed concurrently with sham or active TSS. Assessments included electrophysiological measurements and standardized rehabilitation outcomes.ResultsDescriptive analysis revealed meaningful individual improvements masked by group-level heterogeneity. In the stroke group, three participants showed grip strength improvement (assessed without stimulation) after the active phase (+ 9.4 Newtons [N] to + 23.9 N), with two-to-four-fold increases in forearm muscle activation. Mean Fugl-Meyer overall UE scores improved from 89 to 94.2. In the SCI group, two participants showed grip strength gains. One participant exhibited a six-fold immediate force increase (1.0 N to 6.2 N) during stimulation. Another participant achieved improved grip strength without stimulation (23.9 N to 36.8 N) and a three-fold increase in electromyography (EMG) activity from the flexor carpi radialis and first dorsal interosseous muscles, alongside partial pin-prick sensory recovery and self-reported restoration of previously affected perspiration during the active TSS phase.ConclusionsVaried outcomes in participants confirm that therapeutic effects of combined TSS and robotic UE training are highly individualized. Three critical elements must be blended for the best outcomes of this combinatorial approach: residual UE function, a curated stimulation paradigm, and tailored UE training that provides appropriate challenge, intensity, and salience. The results suggest TSS with UE robotic training hold key potential when considered in the context of the physiological and functional profile of each participant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-026-02036-0.

Introduction and background

An estimated 795,000 people experience a cerebral stroke [1] while approximately 18,000 persons sustain a new traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI) [2] in the United States each year. Damage to the brain motor network or spinal cord, as well as disruption of connectivity between the cortex and cervical spinal cord, can cause deficits in upper extremity (UE) function and contribute to abnormal synergies that interfere with usual movement patterns [3]. In both conditions, impairments in UE function can profoundly impact a person’s ability to navigate the intricacies of the human world, leading to dependence in daily activities and decreased quality of life [4, 5].

Many individuals with stroke or SCI who experience chronic UE impairments seek novel or experimental treatments beyond conventional rehabilitation for improving function, including neuromodulation approaches and robotic technologies [6–8]. Recent work supports the spinal cord as a therapeutic target even when the primary lesion is supraspinal. In individuals with chronic stroke, cervical epidural spinal stimulation has been shown to acutely improve voluntary arm and hand movements, demonstrating that modulation of spinal sensorimotor networks can enhance motor output despite cortical injury [8]. Similarly, in individuals with SCI, epidural spinal stimulation has been shown to facilitate voluntary motor output and functional movements by modulating the excitability of spinal sensorimotor networks below the level of injury, demonstrating that targeted activation of spinal circuits can enable the engagement of residual descending and segmental pathways [9–11]. While epidural stimulation provides strong proof-of-principle, its invasive nature limits broader application in these populations. Therefore, clinicians and researchers are intrigued by the possibility of restoring UE function using less invasive neuromodulation strategies. Specifically, transcutaneous spinal stimulation (TSS) as a neuromodulation approach has been used to aid in restoration of both lower extremity and UE function after SCI, often in combination with rehabilitation [11–16]. Additionally, the intrinsic complexity of human UE functions, the rehabilitation of which is often confounded by strong adaptive movement patterns needed for survival, allows for significant opportunity to explore TSS in restoration of such functions following stroke and SCI.

Moreover, robotic assisted therapy following SCI has been shown to be safe and feasible, and robotics have been used following stroke to assist in recovery of upper limb function [17, 18]. Robotic assisted rehabilitation has potential to provide highly standardized, repeatable, and task-specific movement training, enabling controlled delivery of large numbers of movement repetitions while maintaining consistent kinematics and sensory input. Such experimental control may be particularly advantageous when evaluating neuromodulatory interventions such as TSS, as consistent sensory-motor engagement may facilitate modulation of spinal sensorimotor networks [19]. The combination of both modalities, however, remains underexplored, particularly related to volitional effort, residual pathways, and optimal training paradigms.

We hypothesized that simultaneous repetitive robotic exoskeleton-assisted UE training combined with targeted engagement of spinal networks via cervical TSS could help bridge the disrupted connection between the brain and impaired UE muscles after stroke or SCI. We examined neurophysiological and sensorimotor outcomes across two distinct levels of corticospinal tract damage: individuals with chronic stroke and those with chronic cervical SCI. Our findings led us to articulate key insights into the combination of TSS and robotic exoskeleton training for UE sensorimotor improvement in both populations.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited via physician referrals and local advertisements from the surrounding community. The study was approved by the Houston Methodist Research Institute Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Inclusion criteria for the stroke group were: (1) aged 18–80 years; (2) first-ever hemorrhagic or ischemic stroke resulting in moderate-to-severe UE hemiparesis; (3) stable medical condition; and (4) at least six months post-stroke (chronic phase). All participants with stroke presented with chronic unilateral UE dysfunction, and the trained UE in the study corresponded to the affected side.

Inclusion criteria for the SCI group consisted of: (1) aged 18–80 years; (2) cervical SCI (C7 or above); (3) at least six months post-injury. SCI participants were required to exhibit bilateral UE sensorimotor deficits with partial residual voluntary control in at least one UE. Partial residual voluntary control was assessed and confirmed at baseline by the study therapist. In context of the bilateral nature of tetraplegia following SCI, we offered participants a choice in determining the UE to train for the study. For 5 of the 6 participants, the preferred UE corresponded with their dominant UE post injury. Rationale for each participant choice was consistently articulated as a desire to improve function for that particular UE. Participants in both groups were asked to maintain their usual medication regimens throughout the study.

Study design

The study employed a 14-week within-subject crossover interventional design consisting of two training phases: training combined with sham TSS (hereafter referred to as ‘sham’) and training combined with active TSS (hereafter referred to as ‘active TSS’), separated by a two-week washout period to minimize potential carry-over effects. Each phase included three components: a baseline assessment week, a four-week training period, and a post-intervention assessment week, totaling six weeks per phase (Fig. 1A). Each assessment week consisted of one necessary assessment occurring per day for a total of three days. This design was used to accommodate participant schedules and fatigue. Comparisons were made between the training sessions paired with sham and those paired with active TSS, as the motor training protocol was constant across conditions.

During each intervention phase, participants completed three training sessions per week, for 12 total sessions per phase. Both the stroke and SCI groups followed the same overall timeline and assessment structure. The order of sham and active TSS phases was not randomized; all participants completed the sham phase first to establish individual baselines and ensure safety and tolerability before proceeding to active stimulation.

Cervical transcutaneous spinal stimulation

For both assessment and training sessions, TSS was delivered using a constant-current stimulator (DS8R, Digitimer Ltd., UK) connected to self-adhesive hydrogel electrodes (PALS, Axelgaard Manufacturing Co. Ltd., USA). The cathode electrodes (diameters 3.2–5.0 cm) were chosen for each participant to optimize skin contact and comfort and were placed along the posterior midline of the cervical spine, centered between the spinous processes of C3–C4 (rostral site) and C7–T1 (caudal site). These sites correspond approximately to the motor pools of proximal and distal UE muscles, respectively [20] (Fig. 1B). Two 7.5 × 13 cm oval anodes were placed symmetrically on the anterior iliac crests. The skin was cleaned with alcohol wipes prior to electrode placement, and electrodes were replaced as needed to ensure consistent conductivity.

During assessment sessions, TSS was delivered as monophasic pulses (pulse width = 500 µs) at 0.5 Hz in a double-pulse configuration (interstimulus interval = 30 ms) to elicit spinally evoked responses and generate recruitment curves [19, 21]. This specific pulse width was selected to ensure high-fidelity recruitment curves by providing a high charge density capable of effectively activating spinal motor pools. Stimulation intensity was increased in 5 mA increments starting from 10 mA until either a plateau in response amplitude was observed or the participant’s tolerance limit was reached.

During training sessions, continuous TSS was delivered as biphasic, charge-balanced rectangular pulses (pulse width = 500 µs) at 30 Hz. The change to biphasic pulses was made to prioritize participant comfort and prevent skin irritation during more prolonged stimulation. Stimulation intensity was targeted within a range of 1.0–1.1x motor threshold (MT) levels (defined as minimum intensity required to elicit a peak-to-peak amplitude of at least 0.02 mV in a tested muscle) [22]. Once MT was identified, the intensity was titrated up to 1.1x MT to observe changes in muscle responses and ensure optimal activation of the targeted motor pools. When MT could not be reached, the stimulation was increased up to the maximum tolerated intensity for that participant. Each training session lasted approximately 60 min as noted below.

For the sham condition, identical electrode placement and setup procedures were used. Stimulation was applied for a maximum of 30 s per session to mimic the initial sensory experience of active TSS while minimizing the likelihood of producing sustained neuromodulatory effects.

Target intensity was as close as possible to that used during active TSS, with the goal of reaching MT [22]. After that, stimulation was discontinued for the remainder of the training. Similar paradigms for sham stimulation have been used in other studies [23–25].

Upper extremity training

Training sessions were conducted three times per week for approximately one hour each, totaling three hours of training per week for each participant. The 60-minute session duration is established in the literature [26], is a common rehabilitation session length, and was selected to provide sufficient task-specific practice while maintaining participant tolerance and feasibility within a research-based intervention. This defined schedule balanced clinical feasibility with participant burden while allowing consistent delivery of repeated training sessions. The training sessions consisted of two components: (1) UE robotic training and (2) hand grip training. Both were performed with sham or active TSS as determined by the study phase.

Robotic training

The MAHI Open Exoskeleton (MAHI Open Exoskeleton, Houston, TX) was used for training [27]. The system is a unilateral, four degree of freedom serial robotic exoskeleton for the UE. The exoskeleton was designed with an adjustable forearm length to accommodate different sizes and an open architecture for easy donning and doffing by movement-impaired users. The exoskeleton’s movements were mutually exclusive and corresponded to four of the movements of the arm: (1) elbow flexion/extension (Elbow FE), (2) forearm supination/pronation (Forearm SP), (3) wrist flexion/extension (Wrist FE), and (4) radial/ulnar deviation (Wrist RU) (Fig. 1C). During robotic training, participants performed as many repetitions of each movement as they could in a 10-minute period before resting briefly. The exoskeleton simultaneously provided resistive torques proportional to the angular velocity of the joint. The magnitude of the resistive torque could be preset and scaled from 0 to 100%, with a higher percentage increasing the proportionality of the torque to angular velocity. Robot settings were thus optimized daily to provide a level of resistance designed to challenge the participant for each movement pattern, while accounting for unique and highly variable factors such as fatigue, activity tolerance, fluctuations in spasticity, and residual UE function. The training movements were supplemented with computerized visual feedback to aid in achieving the training goals.

Hand grip training

Hand grip training using the digital dynamometer (ADInstruments, Australia) was incorporated into the training sessions given the absence of a hand or finger module in the MAHI Open Exoskeleton. Participants were instructed to grasp and hold the dynamometer, provide maximum isometric contraction for 5 to 10 s, then release. Participants completed multiple repetitions of this exercise for the last 10-minute block of each training session. The dynamometer was positioned in a standardized manner with hand wrap, foam pads, and elbow stabilization to minimize potential compensatory movements. Participants received computerized visual feedback of grip force output to guide consistent effort and track progress.

Stimulation during training

Stimulation parameters were initially guided by each participant’s baseline electrophysiological assessment (EPA) (see Supplementary Table S1), and stimulation intensity targeted the participant’s MT. While the MT was primarily determined during the four assessment sessions, EMG responses were also monitored during each training session to observe potential shifts in muscle recruitment. This monitoring ensured that the applied stimulation remained at the intended target intensity throughout the training period. Given the variability in participant tolerance, parameters were individually adjusted during each session to balance participant comfort with study interventions. Active TSS was delivered during each training session while the participant was actively completing UE training, with 2–5-minute rest breaks as needed to accommodate participant tolerance. The frequency was set at 30 Hz. Additionally, to independently target proximal and distal motor pools, cervical TSS was delivered via two separate stimulation electrodes during each session. The intensity for the rostral electrode (C3–C4) was titrated based on the MTs of proximal muscles (biceps brachii [BIC] or triceps brachii [TRIC]), while the intensity for the caudal electrode (C7–T1) was independently adjusted to target distal motor pools (flexor carpi radialis [FCR] or intrinsic hand muscles). This configuration enabled site-specific modulation of spinal excitability tailored to the unique recruitment profiles of the participant’s UE musculature.

Training progression and supervision

All training sessions were supervised by an occupational or physical therapist experienced in neurorehabilitation. Stimulation intensity, robotic resistance, and movement targets were adjusted as needed based on each participant’s tolerance, fatigue, and performance to ensure progressive challenge while maintaining safety (Supplementary Fig. S1). Participants were monitored continuously for adverse sensations, muscle fatigue, or autonomic symptoms, and 2–5 min rest breaks (as mentioned above) were provided as needed.

Screening and assessment

In both groups, participants began the study by completing screening procedures, including confirmation of partial residual voluntary control of the UE. These procedures consisted of therapist assessment of active range of motion and strength of bilateral UEs, as well as basic electrophysiological assessments to determine preservation of descending signals to the UE.

Once screening was complete, participants underwent initial study assessments. Each experimental group followed a three-part assessment framework: (1) EPA to evaluate spinal network excitability, (2) Neuromotor assessments (NMA) to quantify motor output, and (3) Functional assessment (FTA) using standardized rehabilitation measures appropriate to each diagnosis, to track UE sensorimotor changes. Each assessment was carried out on a different day in the assessment week to accommodate participant fatigue and scheduling.

Electrophysiological assessment (EPA)

UE motor recruitment curves elicited by TSS with different cathode locations were collected to examine relevant sensorimotor networks. Double-pulse TSS, with a monophasic pulse duration of 500 µs and an inter-stimulus interval of 30 ms, was delivered to determine the MT and assess motor recruitment patterns. MT was defined as the minimum stimulation intensity required to elicit a peak-to-peak motor-evoked potential amplitude of > 20 µV in at least 3 out of 5 consecutive stimuli. The use of double-pulse TSS confirms the reflexive nature of spinally evoked motor responses, as evidenced by a reduced amplitude in the second evoked potential due to post-activation, or homosynaptic, depression [28]. Stimulation intensity began at 10 mA and was increased in 5 mA increments until a plateau in response amplitude or the participant’s tolerance limit was reached.

Neuromotor assessment (NMA)

Hand grip strength was assessed using the handheld dynamometer both with and without TSS. Under each condition, participants completed three consecutive trials consisting of 5–10 s each. The participant was given a minimum of 20–30 s rest break between each trial to recover. The average force value across the three trials was used for analysis. Bilateral EMG was recorded from the UE muscles to quantify muscle activity associated with each movement. Additionally, during NMA, continuous TSS was delivered at 30 Hz with biphasic pulses to evaluate the influence of ongoing stimulation on voluntary motor performance.

Functional assessment (FTA)

UE impairments were evaluated by an occupational therapist trained in standardized UE rehabilitation outcome measures. Diagnosis-specific assessments were selected for each experimental group (Table 1), and changes signifying meaningful individual improvements were interpreted based on established thresholds [29, 30]. Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) scores encompassed UE muscle groups highlighted in the study (except for intrinsic hand muscles). Scores of 1 + on the MAS were converted to 1.5 for analysis.

Electromyographic recordings

Six UE muscles were chosen as a representative sample of muscle groups that could be targeted within the study intervention paradigms. During assessment sessions, wireless surface EMG electrodes (Trigno Avanti, Delsys Inc., USA) were symmetrically placed bilaterally on BIC, TRIC, FCR, extensor carpi radialis (ECR), first dorsal interosseous (FDI), and abductor pollicis brevis (APB) muscles. The EMG data were amplified using the Trigno Avanti amplifier (Delsys Inc.; gain: 909; bandwidth: 20 to 450 Hz) and recorded at a sampling frequency of 2,000 Hz via a PowerLab data acquisition system (ADInstruments, Australia). During the training sessions, the EMG electrodes were applied only to the trained UE to monitor muscle responses during intervention. Raw EMG signals from the recorded muscles were displayed as visual feedback to the participants, to aid in retraining desired movement patterns within the scope of volitional muscle activity.

Data processing and analysis

Data were processed using LabChart Pro 8.1.13 software (ADInstruments, Australia). Raw signals were band-pass filtered (20–450 Hz), full-wave rectified, and segmented around each stimulation pulse. Recruitment curves were constructed by plotting the peak-to-peak EMG amplitude against stimulation intensity for each recorded muscle. The amplitude values were normalized to the maximum response obtained across all stimulation sites for that muscle. Spinally evoked motor potentials were quantified as the peak-to-peak amplitude measured within a 20 ms window beginning 5 ms after stimulus onset. The R2/R1 ratio was computed as the amplitude of the second response (R2) divided by the first (R1) for each muscle, stimulation site, and intensity. Ratios < 1.0 were interpreted as suppression of the second response. Analyses were performed separately for rostral (C3–C4) and caudal (C7–T1) stimulation sites. During training, muscle activity was quantified using root mean square (RMS) values computed from 1-second epochs of continuous EMG signals. RMS amplitudes were used to assess changes in muscle activation under sham versus active TSS conditions, and between with- and without-TSS conditions. Comparative analyses were performed to identify differences in muscle recruitment patterns between intervention phases and movement types.

Data analyses were performed using OriginPro 2025 (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Given the small sample size and exploratory nature of the study, formal hypothesis testing and inferential statistics were not performed to avoid misleading conclusions derived from insufficient statistical power. Instead, analyses focused on descriptive statistics, the evaluation of individual longitudinal trajectories, and functional relevance.

For electrophysiological data, MTs were examined individually using absolute stimulation intensity values to track specific changes in recruitment patterns across intervention phases. For post-activation depression, R2/R1 ratios from paired-pulse TSS were evaluated separately for each stimulation site (C3–C4 and C7–T1) and muscle. The degree of suppression was quantified as the ratio of the second to first evoked response (R2/R1), with values < 1.0 indicating inhibition. For neuromotor outcomes, grip strength was analyzed using absolute force values to characterize the magnitude of recovery relative to each participant’s baseline stability. By contrast, changes in RMS EMG amplitudes were evaluated as relative increases compared to the sham phase or baseline to assess muscle activation gains.

Electrophysiological comparison of sham and active TSS phases

MTs were quantified to characterize muscle recruitment (Fig. 3B). Proximal muscles (BIC and TRIC) generally required lower stimulation currents than distal forearm and hand muscles (FCR, ECR, FDI, APB). MTs varied substantially between individuals (Supplementary Table S1), with proximal MTs typically ranging from 20 to 45 mA and distal MTs from 40 to 75 mA. While participants with clinical AIS A classifications generally demonstrated the highest thresholds, the patterns of excitability were highly participant-specific. For instance, despite identical clinical classifications (AIS A, C4), SCI02 exhibited elevated thresholds, whereas SCI05 showed comparatively lower thresholds across UE muscles. Additionally, no detectable responses were observed in the intrinsic hand muscles (FDI, APB) for two AIS A participants, reflecting a complete lack of access to distal motor pathways.

Individual longitudinal analysis showed decreases in MT values in specific participants during the active TSS phase (Fig. 3C and D). In the stroke group, STR05 exhibited variable changes during the sham phase, including decreases in distal muscles (FCR, FDI, and APB) but stable or increased thresholds in proximal muscles. However, a marked and consistent reduction was observed across both proximal and distal motor pools during the active phase (e.g., BIC: 55 mA to 30 mA; FCR: 55 mA to 40 mA). Similarly, STR03 showed a consistent decrease in thresholds across four of the six tested muscles (BIC, TRIC, FCR, and ECR) following active TSS, while thresholds in the intrinsic hand muscles (FDI and APB) remained relatively stable. In the SCI group, participant SCI06 exhibited a robust reduction in TRIC threshold during the active phase (45 mA to 20 mA). In two other participants (SCI02 and SCI05), cervical TSS failed to elicit detectable EMG responses in the FDI and APB muscles, even at supra-threshold intensities of 70–80 mA. Based on this silence and visible muscle atrophy, these motor pools were characterized as having significant lower motor neuron (LMN) involvement. Consequently, these muscles were excluded from the analysis as they were non-responsive to the spinal stimulation protocol.

Paired-pulse TSS at a 30 ms inter-stimulus interval revealed distinct site- and muscle-specific suppression patterns (Fig. 4). In the SCI group, robust suppression of R2 was observed at intensities near MT (1.0xMT). At the C3–C4 site, both BIC and ECR demonstrated pronounced R2 suppression (median R2/R1 = 0.83 and 0.78, respectively). At the C7–T1 site, suppression was evident in FCR, FDI, and APB (median R2/R1 range: 0.53–0.72). By contrast, the stroke group showed variable patterns with more gradual trends toward suppression. At the C3–C4 site, BIC and TRIC exhibited median R2/R1 ratios of 0.72–0.96, while at C7–T1, TRIC and FDI showed medians of 0.66–0.81. Across both groups, suppression diminished at ≥ 1.2xMT, with R2/R1 ratios approaching 1.0.

Individual longitudinal analysis indicated that while group-level heterogeneity masked average effects, specific participants exhibited clinically relevant gains during the active TSS phase (Fig. 5B). In the stroke group, three participants demonstrated substantial grip strength improvement assessed without stimulation: STR01 (+ 12.0 N), STR02 (+ 9.4 N), and STR05 (+ 23.9 N). These force gains were supported by physiological evidence; for example, participant STR01 showed a two-to-four-fold increase in forearm muscle activation (FCR and ECR) during the active phase compared to the sham phase. Unlike the SCI group, immediate facilitatory effects were not observed in stroke participants; grip force outputs assessed during stimulation were similar to or slightly lower than those measured without stimulation.

In the SCI group, participant profiles varied. Participant SCI06 (AIS D) achieved grip improvement in the absence of stimulation (23.9 N to 36.8 N), accompanied by a nearly three-fold increase in RMS EMG amplitudes across both proximal and distal muscles during the active phase. Conversely, SCI03 exhibited a six-fold immediate force increase (1.0 N to 6.2 N) only during the presence of stimulation, which corresponded with facilitation of intrinsic hand muscle activity (FDI/APB > 1400% of baseline) (Fig. 5A and B).

Sensorimotor outcomes following the two-phase intervention

Kendall Manual Muscle Testing of the UE and the Fugl-Meyer Assessment of Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) were used to evaluate potential improvements in participants with stroke across intervention phases. No meaningful group differences were observed in the Kendall Manual Muscle Testing scores (Supplementary Table S2). However, for the FMA-UE, individual longitudinal analysis revealed that two participants (STR01 and STR05) achieved gains during the active TSS phase, meeting or approaching the minimal detectable change of 5.2 points in FMA-UE scores (STR01: +7 points; STR05: +5 points). A third participant (STR02) exhibited a positive trend (+ 3 points) in Overall score, and participants with higher baseline Overall scores (STR03 and STR04, scores ≥ 95) maintained without decline (Table 3).

Effects on muscle tone

MAS was used to evaluate the modulation of UE muscle tone (Supplementary Table S4). In the stroke group, participant STR02, who exhibited significant grip strength gains, also demonstrated the most robust reduction in hypertonicity. This participant’s average UE MAS scores decreased progressively from 2.5 at baseline to 1.33 following the active TSS phase. The improvement was most pronounced in the wrist musculature, where scores for the wrist flexors and extensors dropped from 3 (severe) to 1.5 and 0 respectively, potentially facilitating improved wrist stability for functional grasp. Other stroke participants presented with milder hypertonicity (UE average MAS < 1.58), exhibiting minimal fluctuations across both study phases. In the SCI group, baseline hypertonicity was generally low (average UE MAS < 1.2 for all participants), limiting the dynamic range to detect potential intervention effects. Nonetheless, SCI01 exhibited a reduction in hypertonicity during the active phase (UE average MAS 0.75 to 0.33), while others remained stable.

Tolerability and safety

TSS delivery was often dictated by participant comfort, and several participants were unable to tolerate consistently the stimulation levels near MT. Consequently, stimulation intensity was adjusted individually to balance tolerability with effective stimulation. MTs and stimulation amplitudes during training were examined for proximal and distal UE muscles to assess their relationship with sensorimotor outcomes (Table 4). In the stroke group, two of five participants (STR02, STR03) reached 100% of their MT during at least one training condition, while others trained at 30–90% of threshold due to poor tolerance of higher intensities. In the SCI group, five of six participants achieved MT levels, while only two (SCI01, SCI06) achieved sensorimotor gains following the active TSS phase.

All participants who completed the study experienced no serious adverse events. Minor events included three episodes of skin irritation associated with stimulation, one episode of anxiety and two episodes of mild pain. These events were temporary and resolved spontaneously. Additionally, one participant experienced an episode of transient autonomic dysreflexia, during which the session was terminated, and the participant was safely and satisfactorily managed with appropriate supportive care. No further complications were observed.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated electrophysiological and sensorimotor effects of cervical TSS combined with UE robotic training in two neurologically distinct populations: individuals with chronic stroke and individuals with chronic cervical SCI. Within these samples (and evidenced by Fig. 2), our longitudinal analysis highlighted distinct physiological and clinical findings across participants, confirming the heterogeneous nature of stroke and SCI lesions and subsequent impairments. Selected participants demonstrated improvements in grip strength and muscle activation, and/or restoration of sensation. These findings indicate that cervical TSS combined with UE training are most optimal when they are highly individualized. As such, the results emphasize the importance of aligning stimulation and rehabilitation strategies with each participant’s residual neurophysiological capacity.

Stimulation parameters and constraints of cervical TSS

Stimulation parameter tuning remains crucial for maximizing the neuromodulatory effects of cervical TSS in research and potential rehabilitation contexts. In this study, stimulation sites (C3–C4 for proximal and C7–T1 for distal motor pools) were selected to approximate the segmental distribution of UE motor pools [36]. In theory, stimulating rostral or caudal segments should preferentially activate corresponding motor pools that control proximal or distal UE muscles, respectively, regardless of the neurologic status of the spinal cord. Stimulation delivered near the MT, coupled with descending volitional effort, may enhance the likelihood of activating spinal interneuronal networks that support UE sensorimotor function [37–39].

However, recent studies highlight several mechanistic constraints that limit the selectivity and reliability of cervical TSS. Preferential motor pool activation during cervical TSS is inconsistent, even in neurologically intact individuals, reflecting broad current spread and reduced depth specificity of surface stimulation [20, 40]. Our physiological data suggest a distinct interaction between stimulation intensity and the mechanism of recruitment. We observed post-activation depression (reduced R2/R1 ratio) at MT intensities, confirming that cervical TSS can engage dorsal root afferents and trans-synaptic pathways [41]. Mechanistically, stimulation preferentially recruits large-diameter afferent fibers, thereby increasing the excitability of motor neuron pools [42, 43]. This effectively lowers the activation threshold, enabling weak residual descending commands to elicit muscle contraction. However, this “therapeutic window” is narrow in the cervical spine due to the intrinsic nature of the cervical spine anatomy. If intensity is too high (supra-threshold), direct efferent activation may override voluntary control, reducing the facilitation of synaptic plasticity. As stimulation intensity increases above the MT, the current may bypass the afferent pathways via volume conduction, directly depolarizing the ventral roots [44, 45]. Thus, cervical TSS likely operates via a mixed mechanism, where the delicate balance between reflex facilitation and direct activation is highly sensitive to stimulation intensity applied in a unique anatomical context.

Complementarily, systematic observations must be made with regards to person variables. For example, recruitment efficiency is influenced by skin impedance, tissue composition, electrode alignment, and vertebral anatomy, all of which vary across individuals. Additionally, stimulation tolerance, residual UE function and musculoskeletal integrity, and condition of spinal cord, corticospinal tract and branching nerves can all influence the application of cervical TSS. Confirmation of a LMN lesion (as observed in specific motor pools of SCI02 and SCI05) via EPA and clinical observations is advised not to determine study exclusion, but to refine the stimulation and training targets. While LMN involvement in certain segments can limit the direct effectiveness of TSS for those specific motor pools, it serves as a critical variable for tailoring the intervention toward muscles with intact LMNs and residual descending connectivity. Recognizing these individual neuroanatomical differences ensures that neuromodulation is directed at the most responsive neural circuits, thereby optimizing the overall therapeutic potential for each participant. Even for individuals with SCI who have upper motor neuron lesions, the variations in scar tissue, possible spine fixation hardware, anatomical structure, and vertebral space may also challenge accurate application and effectiveness of site-specific TSS of desired intensity. For individuals with stroke, the residual UE function as well as possible hypersensitivity and pain perceptions may influence tolerance to stimulation and therefore affect a participant’s ability to achieve the critical MT needed for effecting change within this approach. Nonetheless, the cerebral nature of a stroke (accompanied by an intact spinal cord) may provide explanation for potential improvements even at suboptimal stimulation levels.

Targeted UE training and the importance of volitional engagement

While TSS can modulate the excitability of neural pathways [43], our findings, supported by the systematic titration of TSS intensities (1.0–1.4 MT) during electrophysiological assessments (Fig. 4C), indicate that functional recovery depends on the interaction between stimulation intensity and volitional effort [11, 46, 47]. The corticospinal tract provides the primary conduit for voluntary motor control, and damage to this system weakens descending drive to spinal motoneurons. As a result, voluntary muscle activation is compromised, leading to difficulty initiating and controlling simple and complex UE movements. The neuromodulation of key spinal circuits via spinal stimulation likely enhances the excitability of motoneurons and/or cortico-motoneuronal synapses [39]. Together with curated rehabilitation, it is plausible that improvements may begin to take root in desired movement patterns.

Individuals with stroke may exhibit distinct profiles compared to those with SCI, as stroke typically spares spinal sensory ascending pathways, involving supraspinal circuits [48]. This preserved spinal circuitry may permit gains even when stimulation intensity is constrained, particularly when training emphasizes voluntary effort. By contrast, recovery following SCI is limited by disruption of descending pathways and reduced capacity for reorganization within spinal circuits. These neuroanatomical differences likely contribute to variability in outcomes between groups.

Rehabilitation strategies that incorporate goal-directed, repetitive, salient movements and recruit significant and near maximum effort exerted by the participant hold key potential for improvements [49–52]. While robotic devices have been shown to augment traditional rehabilitation methods, their design, functionality, and feasibility of use are important to consider with participants with neurologic diagnoses and hallmark residual UE impairments [53, 54]. In this study, the robotic component emphasized isolated, single joint movement patterns of low to moderate intensity, with a focus on joint mobility and UE coordination. While this approach may be effective for reducing compensatory movement patterns, it does not inherently provide the nuanced intricacies of traditional rehabilitation that may be needed to consistently translate gains into complex salient UE movements.

By contrast, improvements (as quantified by EMG) were more evident during isometric, high-intensity hand grip training performed with near-maximum voluntary effort. Importantly, the grip training differed from the robotic intervention in the level of voluntary effort required. While the robotic paradigm primarily targeted elbow, forearm, and wrist movements and provided mechanical assistance during movement, the hand grip training required sustained near-maximal voluntary contractions using the dynamometer. Isometric voluntary contraction training, emphasizing static muscle contractions with constant muscle length, has been shown to enhance related motor tasks and increase muscle activation and corticospinal excitability [55, 56] and combining it with task-specific training and spinal stimulation may increase both muscle activity and corticospinal excitability. In context, the heterogeneous nature of observed improvements in this pilot study emphasizes the importance of a curated combinatorial rehabilitation and stimulation approach tailored to each person.

Supplementing these ideas, we posit that the robust reduction in hypertonicity observed in STR02 may provide integral insight into a proof-of-principle that TSS can restore spinal inhibitory balance and amplify the responsiveness of motoneurons to residual descending commands [8]. This suggests that by modulating the excitability of spinal circuits and alleviating antagonist co-activation, TSS effectively ‘unmasks’ latent yet critical volitional control that was previously suppressed by spasticity. These pilot findings may catalyze a future, more in-depth study of how voluntary engagement may play an essential role in shaping various neuromuscular adaptations during combinatorial paradigms.

Three critical elements must be blended for desired outcomes with this combinatorial approach: key residual UE function unmasked by pain or hypersensitivity [57], an individualized stimulation paradigm, and UE training combining volitional effort with appropriate challenge and salience. This process extended beyond simple MT determination; it involved the tailored selection of electrode sizes to optimize current density, fine-tuning of robotic resistance to match each participant’s functional capacity, and the integration of real-time EMG visual feedback to enhance volitional engagement. By calibrating these diverse parameters to the unique neurophysiological and clinical profile of each participant, we sought to maximize the synergy between spinal neuromodulation and task-specific training. This combination was most optimal in SCI03 and SCI06 in the SCI group and STR01, STR02, and STR05 in the stroke group, allowing for greater opportunity to decrease impairment and achieve relevant UE gains in the context of chronic dysfunction.

Limitations

The small sample size of each group compromises statistical power and the generalizability of the results. The study design also introduced potential sources of bias. Participant blinding was not formally assessed. Given the distinct sensory profile of continuous active stimulation compared to the brief sham protocol, participants could have distinguished between these two interventions. The fixed protocol order (sham followed by active TSS) precluded blinding of outcome assessors, which may have introduced detection bias. While the combinatorial approach can be beneficial in effecting change in a chronic population, the methodology does present challenges for isolating effects stemming from a single variable. Furthermore, the within-subject crossover design in the chronic population cannot entirely exclude potential carryover effects (behavioral or other) from influencing results.

Variability in participant tolerance to TSS led to adjustments in stimulation intensity throughout the intervention, affecting consistent stimulation at specific MTs and therefore potentially impacting the outcomes. Additionally, the broad current spread of TSS limits the selectivity in targeting isolated muscles and inevitably may allow stimulation at suprathreshold levels for some muscles while simultaneously trying to achieve MT for other muscles.

The robotic device, which facilitated single-joint arm movements (excluding a hand component), highlights the need for future studies to accommodate either robotic devices targeting comprehensive multi-joint and complex movements inherent in human UE rehabilitation and/or to use traditional UE rehabilitation. In addition, the exoskeleton system shared practical limitations essential to consider for individuals with neurological impairments: device adjustability and comfort, time required for setup and fine-tuning of resistance, user controllability and engagement, limitations in adapting resistive torque to individuals with varying neurologic presentations, and ergonomic constraints. There are also considerations in using ordinal level impairment-based rehabilitation measures to capture subtle changes in UE function in individuals with chronic and severe UE sensorimotor impairments.

Conclusion

Combined TSS and UE robotic training hold potential for improving UE function after stroke or SCI, yet more research can elucidate the mechanisms underlying individual responses to TSS and robotics. To this end, neurophysiological assessment (including electrophysiological measures and functional magnetic resonance imaging) early on can provide key insight into critical variables that may affect desirable responses of interested individuals. Such methodology would in turn frame the need for additional research on the individual level that supports and complements larger group level designs. Additionally, future research should consider a focus on curated combinations of therapeutic modalities (including paired TSS with brain and/or peripheral nerve stimulation) embedded in rigorous study designs, to maximize efficacy in varied clinical populations. These combinations may offer additional avenues to optimize therapeutic outcomes in individuals who respond to an individualized, multimodal approach. Advancing understanding in these areas will be crucial for developing novel, effective interventions and restoring UE function following chronic stroke or SCI.
