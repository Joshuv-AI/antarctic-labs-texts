Strategies to increase attendance in substance use disorder group treatment: results from a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Liliane Cambraia Windsor, Heather A. Jones, Carla Ellis, Kennya Hooper, Andrea Rucker, Salma Musaad, Moses Okumu, Critical Consciousness Collaborative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAttendance in substance-use-disorder group-treatment is challenging with only 32% of patients attending at least one session [16]. The present study tested a set of strategies to increase group session attendance in Community Wise, an innovative evidence-based group-intervention delivered in harm-reduction-community-based agencies and, Community Wise was designed to reduce alcohol and substance use among people with: a history of substance-use-disorders living in predominantly Black and disinvested communities. We examined: (1) if strategies to increase attendance stratified by surveillance status (participation in mandatory programs that use urine screens regularly) would result in a clinically and statistically significant increase in intervention session attendance, with a goal of at least 50% of participants attending at least half of sessions; and (2) the effects of group session attendance and strategies to increase attendance on alcohol and substance use frequency in the past 30 days at three-months-follow-up.MethodsWe conducted a 22 stratified full factorial experiment with 164 adults with substance-use-disorder in East St. Louis, IL. Participants were randomized to combinations of two strategies to increase attendance: Financial incentives (receiving $20 to attend group sessions) and group type (open versus closed groups). Surveillance status was treated as a fixed factor in the analytical model. Logistic and multiple regression models, using effect coding, tested main and interaction effects on number of sessions attended and alcohol and substance use. Analyses followed an intent-to-treat approach, adjusting for demographic covariates, using SAS 9.4.ResultsOf 164 participants, 29% attended ≥ 50% of Community Wise sessions. Paying participants increased odds of attending at least 50% of sessions (OR = 1.97, p = 0.004), while being under surveillance reduced odds by 39% (OR = 0.61, p = 0.036). No main effects were found for group type. Yet, a significant interaction showed financial incentives were more effective in closed versus open groups (OR = 0.58, p = 0.021). Higher attendance predicted reduced percent alcohol and substance use at follow-up (p = 0.011).ConclusionsFinancial incentives effectively improved intervention attendance, while being under surveillance reduced participation. Higher attendance was associated with decreased alcohol and substance use. The Community Wise intervention should be further tested for effectiveness in harm reduction community-based-organizations among people who are disengaged from treatment and experiencing vulnerabilities such as poverty and houselessness.Trial registrationClinical Trials: NCT05934591.

Introduction

Alcohol and substance use (ASU) among people with substance-use-disorders (SUD) remain a critical public health challenge in the United States (U.S.), disproportionately affecting residents of low-income neighborhoods in cities that have more than 50% of their population self-reporting as Black and disproportionately higher rates of crime, people living under the poverty line, open air drug markets, high police presence, and high health inequities [1–3]. These communities have experienced chronic disinvestment—systematic withdrawal of economic, social, and political resources—which has left them underfunded and marginalized, often due to redlining, segregation, and other racially discriminatory policies [4]. These policies created barriers for these communities to thrive and, in combination with racism, have resulted in major health inequities. For instance, ASU rates in these communities are similar to those in predominantly White disinvested communities, yet the consequences of ASU in predominantly Black and Brown communities are far more severe with disproportionately higher rates of incarceration, HIV incidence, and deaths [5–9].

People with SUD living in predominantly Black and disinvested communities also have limited access to treatment infrastructure needed for SUD recovery [4, 10–12]. These inequities are even more pronounced among Black residents. For example, in Missouri’s St. Louis region where the current study takes place, federally funded initiatives to increase access to SUD treatment, specifically medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD) through a “Medication First” model resulted in disproportionately greater gains for White clients than for Black clients. Black clients had lower rates of treatment admission, reduced MOUD utilization, and shorter retention than their White counterparts [13, 14]. Participants described treatment systems as difficult to access, racially biased, and unresponsive to their cultural and social realities. They reported feeling surveilled, distrusted, and stigmatized in clinical encounters, which undermined trust and discouraged sustained engagement. Many emphasized that available services failed to reflect their lived experiences or address the structural conditions shaping their ASU [14]. Meta-analytic worldwide data show a 30% average dropout rate among those who initiated SUD psychosocial treatment in research studies, ranging from 0 to 100% treatment drop out. These rates were significantly higher among Black and low-income participants [15]. U.S. substance use treatment data shows that only 32% of people discharged from treatment reported attending at least one group treatment session in the past 30 days [16]. Barriers such as stigma, institutional distrust, transportation challenges, and competing survival needs often prevent consistent participation [17, 18]. The consequences of such disengagement are significant with untreated SUD often leading to acute health crises, including overdose and early deaths [12, 19].

SUD interventions grounded in equity principles and community partnership are critical to address these SUD inequities. One intervention that attempts to address these gaps is Community Wise, an innovative evidence-based program designed to reduce ASU among people with SUD living in predominantly Black and disinvested communities and empower them to promote health equity [20, 21]. Community Wise was designed and tested in Newark, NJ following the principles of Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR), and it is the first evidence-based intervention in the field of SUD that directly and systematically addresses classism, racism, and sexism at multiple levels [22]. Community Wise is an add-on group intervention for delivery in harm-reduction-programs in predominantly Black and disinvested communities. Participants meet for-2-hours-weekly over 8 weeks to engage in critical dialogue about the impact of oppression on ASU and to develop community-based capacity-building-projects to improve community health. The intervention is delivered by a trained peer facilitator [20].

While Community Wise’s design reflects strong cultural alignment, prior studies testing Community Wise have shown mixed success in engaging and retaining residents of predominantly Black and disinvested communities with histories of SUD [21, 22]. In the first pilot study testing Community Wise with 32 people, 81% of participants attended at least one intervention session and 62% attended more than 50% of the sessions [22]. Most participants in these studies were under surveillance (individuals required to be regularly drug tested by programs such as methadone maintenance, drug courts, parole, or probation) and had more stability (e.g., access to services, and housing) compared to participants not under surveillance including employment. Yet, in a randomized controlled trial with 602 participants, only 42.2% attended at least one session and only 14% attended more than 50% of the sessions. Our data indicated that people in this study were not under surveillance and were unemployed and house insecure. Data showed that low attendance was due to challenges locating participants (especially unhoused people) and delays in starting groups due the study’s randomization (procedures required the enrollment of a minimum of 26 participants at a time and resulted in rigid group schedules and group assignment waiting periods of over one month) [21].

Informed by CBPR principles, the present study aimed to develop and test strategies to increase attendance in Community Wise within a harm-reduction, community-based organization (CBO). Specifically, we examined what combination of different Community Wise delivery strategies would result in a clinically and statistically significant increase in intervention group session attendance, with a goal of at least 50% of participants attending at least half of the sessions. We also tested the effects of session attendance and the strategies that resulted in significantly higher attendance on ASU outcomes at 3 months follow-up (including alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, and opioids).

SUD treatment engagement

Research has identified key protective and risk factors that shape SUD treatment engagement and completion especially among residents of predominantly Black and disinvested communities [23–26]. Protective factors include strong social support networks, access to culturally responsive and trauma-informed care, and the presence of peer SUD recovery specialists. Equally vital are integrated services that address mental health challenges, housing instability, and employment barriers [3, 27]. Programs employing harm-reduction-principles, low-threshold entry, and flexible service models have also shown to enhance engagement and retention [28]. Conversely, risk factors include pervasive stigma, discrimination within healthcare and social service settings, and unmet basic needs such as stable housing, childcare, and transportation [2, 27]. Structural barriers such as limited program availability, punitive drug policies, and fragmented care systems further impede treatment access and completion [29–33]. Community Wise was developed as a closed group intervention because it required engagement in group projects to improve community health. However, the closed group format posed significant barriers to participants when delivered as a part of a randomized controlled trial [21]. Open groups offer a much more flexible approach that could maximize treatment engagement and attendance. A robust body of literature shows that open groups offer accessible, low-barrier spaces where individuals can build social connections, reduce isolation, and receive ongoing encouragement from others with lived experience [34, 35].

Research comparing SUD treatment engagement shows that individuals under surveillance, often have higher initial engagement rates than those not under surveillance [36]. This makes sense given the potential consequences of disengagement in these programs (e.g., withdraw from methadone, reincarceration). It is important to note however that, while surveillance can increase short-term attendance, it is associated with lower rates of therapeutic alliance and sustained SUD recovery [37]. Individuals under surveillance frequently face heightened stigma, restrictive program rules, and punitive responses to relapse, which can undermine long-term treatment retention and autonomy [37]. In contrast, people who voluntarily engaged in treatment tended to report stronger intrinsic motivation, greater trust in providers, and higher rates of treatment completion, particularly within harm-reduction and client-centered care models [22, 37].

Studies support compensating participants for attending intervention sessions, showing that modest, immediate incentives, such as cash, vouchers, or gift cards, can improve participation and retention, particularly among individuals facing structural barriers like poverty, unstable housing, and criminal justice involvement [38, 39]. Research highlights that these incentives can be especially effective in harm-reduction and low-threshold settings, where traditional abstinence-based models may struggle to maintain engagement. Financial incentives have been associated with short-term reductions in ASU and improved health outcomes. However, sustaining these benefits often requires embedding incentives within comprehensive, supportive care models [40, 41]. Despite promising early results, there are knowledge gaps on how financial incentives can be effectively sustained and scaled within diverse, real-world community-based treatment settings.

Community Wise’s development centered resource management as a critical factor in developing a sustainable intervention. Findings from Community Wise’s randomized controlled trial indicated that Community Wise can be delivered for $138 per person. This cost was well below Medicaid’s reimbursable rates, leaving enough room to include modest session attendance payments to the intervention [21]. Participants in all Community Wise trials reported resentment towards receiving payment for completing study activities such as baseline and follow-up, but not for attending intervention sessions. This concern reflected broader debates around equity and financial incentives for participants in community-based research and further supported the inclusion of session attendance payments to the Community Wise intervention.

The current study

In response to these challenges, and supported by the literature, the present study sought to optimize a set of group intervention strategies to maximize attendance in Community Wise within a harm-reduction-community-based organization. We examined whether variations in strategies to increase attendance (offering open groups and paying participants to attend intervention sessions) stratified by surveillance and sex, would improve intervention attendance. We also examined if the strategies that significantly increased attendance influenced ASU outcomes at follow-up. This work focuses specifically on residents of predominantly Black and disinvested communities, including both Black and non-Black residents, with a history of SUD who are frequently disengaged from traditional treatment services.

Methods

Following CBPR principles alongside the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST), we implemented a 22 full factorial experiment with a sample of 164 residents with a history of SUD in East St. Louis. We randomized participants to either attend closed versus open groups and receive payment or not receive payment to attend intervention sessions. Randomization was stratified across four blocks (male vs female and surveillance vs non-surveillance) to maintain demographic balance. The analysis sought to evaluate individual and interactive effects of the presence or absence of the following components on intervention attendance: (1) Incentivizing intervention attendance (paying people $20 per session attendance versus no payment); and (2) Group type: open (people can attend a session at their own pace until they complete the 8 sessions) versus closed group format (the same set of individuals attend all sessions together over 8 weeks). We included sex and surveillance as fixed factors, with the latter also tested as a moderator. Our goal was to optimize Community Wise by including only strategies that increase intervention attendance and reduce ASU.

CBPR and MOST

CBPR and MOST are innovative and complementary approaches useful to developing and evaluating community-based, multilevel interventions that are efficient, effective, and sustainable [42]. CBPR is a collaborative research framework that actively involves community members, organizational representatives, and researchers as equal partners in all aspects of the research process. It emphasizes co-learning, mutual benefit, and the integration of scientific and indigenous knowledge to address health inequities [43]. MOST, on the other hand, is a systematic, engineering-inspired framework for optimizing multicomponent behavioral interventions. It involves a sequence of phases including preparation, optimization, and evaluation to identify the most effective, efficient, and scalable components of an intervention before conducting a full-scale randomized controlled trial [44]. When combined, CBPR ensures interventions are sustainable and community-driven, while MOST enhances intervention effectiveness and resource efficiency through data-driven decision-making [42].

Research site

Community Wise group meetings took place at a CBO located in East St. Louis, IL, U.S. The CBO operates under a harm-reduction approach and serves over 20,000 people per year. Services provided include SUD treatment, from residential to outpatient and methadone disbursement. The town is one of the largest cities in the county with the largest social and health inequity rates (e.g., 38% poverty rate, significant exposure to lead and environmental toxins) [45].

Recruitment and sample selection

We recruited a sample of 164 adults. Research team members posted fliers at reentry, SUD, and HIV/HCV service agencies, probation and parole offices, drug courts, and throughout the community. Men and women interested in participating in the study either called the study’s cell phone number or showed up at the CBO during business hours. A trained research assistant conducted a brief screening to obtain self-reported eligibility on SUD, age, sex, contact information, and whether they are currently under surveillance. Eligibility criteria: Over 18 years of age; living in the county; 50% of sample was under surveillance (parole, probation, drug court or methadone maintenance) and 50% not under surveillance; having a SUD; English-speaker; and able and willing to provide informed consent. We oversampled to include enough women into the study.

Orientation, baseline, and follow-up

Phone screened participants were invited to attend an in-person group orientation session to learn about the study, including randomization procedures and the different forms of Community Wise delivery they may receive. Those interested were invited to complete the baseline survey online in RedCAP [46]. The survey included a screening informed consent and an eligibility screen with a computerized Global Appraisal of Individual Needs-Substance Problem Scale (GAIN-SPS) [47]. Those who were determined eligible and agreed to the procedures were consented and invited to complete the baseline assessment. Follow-up was completed online three-months-post-baseline using RedCAP. Baseline and follow-up included questions about SUD treatment services received over the past 3 months (Treatment Services Review questionnaire) [48], the Timeline-Follow-Back questionnaire to measure alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, and opioid use in the past 30 days [49], and demographics. Follow-ups also included intervention satisfaction developed by the research team (e.g., would recommend the intervention to others), the Engagement Scale from the Group Climate Questionnaire [50], and a question about why the participant missed a session (collected by the group facilitator via a phone call after the session was missed). Assessment took 30 min to complete. Participants received $20 to complete baseline and $30 to complete follow-up assessment. Intervention attendance was recorded weekly in RedCAP by the peer facilitator. After the baseline, participants were asked to provide extensive locator information, including formal and informal contacts who can reach the participant in a variety of contingencies.

Randomization

Computer generated randomly permuted blocks were used to assign participants into one of two experimental conditions stratified by surveillance and sex. Randomization occurred in RedCAP immediately after completing the baseline. At the start of data collection, an error in the randomization file in Redcap resulted in randomization failure for the first 36 participants. The error resulted in more White participants being assigned to the no payment condition. This was corrected before the rest of the sample was recruited. Randomization can be assessed in Table 1 where we show significant differences in key variables at baseline. We corrected for these differences statistically.

Data cleaning

At each time point (baseline and follow-up), average percent of ASU use was estimated by: (a) calculating percent use for each drug by dividing the reported frequency of use over the past 30 days by the number of days, (b) taking the average of the percent use across all drugs by summing the percent use for all drugs (cocaine, opioids, cannabis, and alcohol) and dividing by the number of drugs (4). The difference in average percentage ASU use between baseline and post baseline was calculated. Total attendance was calculated by summing attendance across all sessions. Since there were a maximum of 8 sessions to attend, a variable depicting attendance of at least 4 sessions was created to reflect whether the individual attended at least 50% of the sessions (yes = 1, no = 0). Independent variables were analyzed using effect coding as follows: Surveillance (under surveillance = 1, not under surveillance = − 1), incentivizing intervention attendance (incentive = 1, not = − 1), and group type (open = 1, closed = − 1).

Data analysis

Baseline characteristics and process data were examined using descriptive summaries for continuous variables (mean and standard deviation), and for categorical variables (n and percent). The primary analyses were performed on an intent-to-treat approach, including all randomized individuals. The following covariates were examined: surveillance, age, race and ethnicity, annual household income, sex, marital status, education, number of days paid in the past 30 days, job search status, and employment status. For modeling, potential covariates were selected based on their association (p value < 0.2) with one or more of the experimental conditions (incentivizing intervention attendance, group type) or attendance rate. We used a relaxed p value (p < 0.2) for covariate selection to avoid missing important covariates [44]. Associations between continuous measures were examined using Spearman correlation coefficients. Difference in continuous variables between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon 2-sample test. Differences in the proportion of categorical variables between groups were assessed using the Chi-Square or Fisher’s Exact test. As a result, the demographic factors selected for inclusion as covariates in the models were job search status, sex, marital status, annual household income, number of days paid in the past 30 days, employment status, race and ethnicity.

To test the objective ‘What combination of interventions was associated with higher attendance?’, we used a logistic regression model to test the association of group type (open vs closed), incentivizing intervention attendance (incentive vs not) and surveillance (under surveillance vs not) with the outcome of attending 50% of sessions or more (vs not). The logistic regression model included main effects, as well as all 2-way interactions and a 3-way interaction using multiplicative terms. The model was adjusted for the selected demographic factors (job search status, sex, race and ethnicity, marital status, annual household income, number of days paid in the past 30 days, and employment status). Parameter estimates (coefficients) and their standard errors were reported. Since the outcome for logistic regression model is the log odds of attending 50% or more sessions, parameter estimates (regression coefficients) represent the change in the odds of the outcome on the log scale for a one-unit change in the independent variable (or at one level compared to its referent if continuous). Thus, parameter estimates were transformed by taking the exponent to facilitate interpretation as odds ratio. Exponentiated coefficients are interpreted as the difference in the likelihood (odds) of achieving the outcome between those who received an intervention component compared to those who did not receive it, collapsing (averaging) over levels of the other components and controlling for the demographic factors. To calculate percent change, exponentiated coefficients are compared to the value of one (one means no difference). A value that is greater than one or less than one, indicates that the outcome is more likely or less likely to occur, respectively. Thus, we subtract one from the exponentiated regression coefficient to obtain the magnitude of change in the outcome, and when multiplied by 100 it is a percent change in the outcome.

To test the objective ‘Does higher attendance result in less ASU use?’ we employed a generalized multiple regression model testing the association of attendance rates (continuous variable), incentivizing intervention attendance (incentive vs not), surveillance (under surveillance vs not) and their 2-way interaction with the outcome of percent of ASU at follow up, adjusting for the selected demographic factors and percent of ASU at baseline. We analyzed the incentivizing intervention attendance and surveillance using effect coding (1 vs − 1). Thus, the parameter estimates (regression coefficients) that included intervention components were multiplied by a scaling constant of 2 to obtain the main and interaction effects as per guidelines for using effect coding in multiple regression [44, 51]. We did not include group type in this model because group type did not meet our selection criteria for inclusion in the optimized intervention. Due to the skewness of the ASU variable, we assumed a negative binomial distribution, thus we calculated the exponent of these scaled parameter estimates to facilitate interpretation as the percentage of ASU use. For interpretation, exponentiated coefficients are compared to the value of one to estimate a percent change, as was explained for the logistic regression model.

For all models, fit was assessed using standard criteria including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Pearson Chi-Square, and Wald test. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), using two-sided tests and a significance level of 0.05.

Using the causal mediation approach, we tested the mediation of incentivizing intervention attendance on the relationship between attendance with the outcome of percent of ASU at follow up, adjusting for demographic characteristics and mean percent of ASU use at baseline. Since no mediation effect was noted, no results are shown.

Post-hoc power analysis

Power was calculated for a factorial design with 3 factors (intervention components), thus providing 23 cells, each with at least 16 subjects. An analytic total of 128 subjects achieves 92% power when an F test is used to test the main effect of each factor at a 5% significance level and an effect size of 0.3. The sample size also achieves 80% power when an F test is used to test each 2-way interaction at a 5% significance level and an effect size of 0.25, and achieves 80% power when an F test is used to test the 3-way interaction at a 5% significance level and an effect size of 0.25. Assuming 20% drop out rate the final sample size is 160 [52]. Due to the clustering of individuals within groups, we anticipate outcomes within the same group to be similar. The ICC is a measure of the relatedness of individuals within a group. The total number of individuals is equal to the analytic sample size multiplied by the design effect.

Based on a range of ICCs reported in the literature for behavioral outcomes [53, 54], we assumed an ICC of 0.05, leading to a design effect of 1.75 [1+((16 − 1) x 0.05]. Thus, the final sample size is inflated to 224, making us underpowered for this study.

Participants’ characteristics at baseline by component (N = 164)

Attendance outcome and reasons to miss sessions: 52% of all participants attended ≥ 1 session and 36% completed ≥ 50% of sessions (Fig. 1). Group facilitators called participants who missed a session to check on them and find out why they missed the session. Group facilitators were unable to reach participants 75% of the time when they called to assess why they missed a session. When participants were reached, 10% reported not being interested in the intervention; 6% had a scheduling conflict; 3% had been incarcerated, 4% were sick, and 2% forgot they had a session. The intent to treat (ITT) analysis showed paying people $20 to attend intervention sessions significantly increased their odds of attending at least 50% of the sessions (exponentiated estimate = 1.97 p = 0.004) and individuals under surveillance were 39% less likely to attend 50% or more of the sessions (exponentiated estimate = 0.61 p = 0.0363, Table 2). Group type main effect did not meet our component selection criteria for main effect (p>0.2). Interaction effects analysis showed a significant 2-way interaction between group type and incentivizing intervention attendance (exponentiated estimate = 0.58 p = 0.0213). Process data

Those participating in the intervention reported high satisfaction; 84% indicated they would recommend the intervention to others and rated the intervention as being greatly helpful (M = 5.04, SD = 1.08; scale of 1–6). Most participants reported high levels of engagement in the Group Climate Questionnaire scale averaging 3.24 (1.24) out of a 6-point scale. During one group session two participants had a disagreement that escalated to a threat of violence. These participants were removed from the study, and this was reported to the study’s IRB within 24 h. No changes were made to the protocol and no other adverse consequences were identified.

Intervention optimization decision-making

Following community-engaged research best practices [55], in collaboration with our community partners we used the findings to inform the optimization of the Community Wise intervention delivery. As guided by the principles of MOST [44], we only included components that met our main effects a priori criteria of statistical and clinical significance (p≤0.20 and attending at least 50% of sessions). Only payment and surveillance met our a priori criteria and hence, only these components were considered for inclusion in the optimized delivery strategy. Thus, we will use this delivery format in future effectiveness trials, focusing recruitment on residents in predominantly Black and disinvested communities who are not under surveillance and pay them to attend sessions. Given our small sample, future research should also test whether open versus closed groups increase attendance and reduce ASU among this population.

Discussion

This study was designed to optimize the delivery of Community Wise to increase intervention attendance among residents in predominantly Black and disinvested communities with a history of SUD. We conducted a 22 stratified full factorial experiment to evaluate individual and interactive effects of three delivery strategies on intervention attendance. Specifically, we tested the effects of financially incentivizing session attendance, group delivery format, and participant surveillance on session attendance and ASU at follow-up.

The study demonstrated notably higher rates of attendance (i.e., 52% percent of all participants attended ≥ 1 session and 29% completed ≥ 50% of sessions) compared to other SUD interventions, which is encouraging given the well-documented challenges of participant retention in this population. These rates were significantly higher than those observed in our previous study conducted in a disinvested and predominantly Black community in New Jersey [21]. The literature shows that attendance rates in SUD group treatment vary widely, with completion rates ranging from 20 to 70% in predominantly White samples, but significantly lower among Black, unhoused, and low-income participants [15]. It is plausible that the high levels of engagement found in our study may reflect the relevance of the intervention to participants’ lived experiences, the accessibility of the program, or the strength of community-based partnerships.

Analysis of the intervention’s impact revealed that higher attendance by participants in Community Wise sessions was significantly associated with reduced ASU at follow-up. More specifically, for every session attended, the percent of ASU use at follow-up decreased by about 15% after accounting for baseline ASU use. None of the main or interaction effects of the delivery components (incentivizing and group type) stratified by surveillance were statistically significant in reducing ASU at follow-up. These findings suggest that the intervention delivery strategies do not directly impact ASU, but they are critical to increase attendance which in turn, increase reductions in ASU. These findings align with prior research emphasizing the critical role of treatment engagement and retention in achieving positive outcomes for individuals with SUDs [56, 57]. These promising findings highlight the richness of Community Wise across subgroups once participants are engaged.

Group type main effect did not meet our component selection criteria for main effect (p>0.1). However, for participants who were not under surveillance and who received payment to attend sessions, attending closed group sessions significantly increased attendance rates. Thus, our intent to treat analysis showed that the optimal delivery strategy to maximize participant attendance in Community Wise is to focus recruitment among people who are not under surveillance and pay them $20 per session to attend closed group intervention sessions. Indeed, 53% of participants assigned to the optimized condition completed the intervention. These results show the relevance of the optimized intervention to people who are disengaged from services and often unhoused. The findings align with research indicating that participants who engage voluntarily may have greater autonomy and intrinsic motivation [41], factors that can be strengthened by modest financial incentives [38, 39].

In examining the impact of participant demographics on attendance and ASU frequency at follow-up, our analysis included a range of covariates associated with these outcomes: age, race and ethnicity, annual household income, sex, marital status, education, number of days paid in the past 30 days, job search status, and employment status. None of the demographic characteristics reached statistical significance in predicting ASU frequency. Job search status emerged as the only significant predictor in the attendance analysis, suggesting that individuals actively seeking employment may face unique challenges influencing treatment attendance. These findings must be interpreted with caution, as the study was underpowered to detect small to moderate effects, and larger, adequately powered studies are needed to assess the role of demographic and socioeconomic factors more confidently in intervention attendance and ASU.

Study implications

Our research has significant implications for harm-reduction SUD treatment. Attendance at community-based SUD treatment programs, defined as completing one or more sessions of an intervention, is challenging and typically low, averaging around 40% in SUD treatment interventions [16, 58]. The observation that consistent participation, rather than specific delivery strategy, was associated with reductions in ASU highlights the need to tailor interventions to participant’s lived realities without requiring abstinence as a precondition. We also observed that voluntary engagement, particularly when supported by modest financial incentives, may enhance autonomy and intrinsic motivation, which are critical for sustained behavior change. In our study, we found that paying people who are not under surveillance to attend Community Wise sessions increased attendance which in turn reduced ASU. These findings are exciting given that many of our participants were houseless and disconnected from services. Taken together, these findings highlight the potential of low-barrier, participant-centered models that prioritize accessibility and relevance over rigid structure in fostering long-term engagement and improving outcomes among marginalized individuals who use substances. SUD treatment practitioners and administrators serving this population should advocate for funding to cover attendance expenses. Policy makers should ensure that Medicaid covers intervention attendance to help reduce AUD related treatment disparities disproportionately burdening residents in predominantly Black and disinvested communities struggling with ASU.

While traditional SUD treatments can be moderately effective in reducing ASU among various populations, it remains extremely difficult to engage and support people who are marginalized, experiencing vulnerability (e.g., houselessness), and disengaged from treatment. Community Wise is an evidence-based intervention developed as an add-on component of harm reduction approaches that address ASU by acknowledging racism, classism, and sexism as social determinants of heath, while simultaneously addressing community health issues [21]. Community Wise is particularly suitable for this marginalized and underserved population and for delivery in harm reduction CBO located in predominantly Black and disinvested communities.

Future effectiveness research should enroll people with a history of SUD and test the optimized Community Wise intervention against treatment as usual in harm-reduction SUD CBOs. Community-level outcomes (e.g., community cohesion, civic engagement, successful completion) should be examined as secondary outcomes that may impact ASU.

Limitations

Limitations of the current study included a small sample size which impacted our ability to detect statistically significant differences in our outcomes. This may have particularly impacted the analysis of components effects on ASU. Our study was underpowered because we did not have enough resources to recruit a larger sample. Yet, the ITT analysis showed that two intervention components met the a priori component selection criteria with an alpha. Findings supported paying participants who are not under surveillance to attend closed Community Wise sessions in order to maximize intervention attendance.

Further research is needed to better understand causal pathways and the effectiveness of Community Wise on ASU with larger samples. While this is a randomized controlled trial, an error in randomization resulted in imbalances in the distribution of participants across experimental conditions. We controlled statistically for the variables that were impacted, but caution should be used in ruling out alternative explanations for the changes in our outcomes. Unfortunately, due to the limited funding to support this study, we were unable to offer a sufficient variety of days and times we could offer the Community Wise sessions to the open group participants. This resulted in treatment contamination and impacted our ability to assess the impact of group on attendance. Further research should compare the delivery of Community Wise in open versus closed groups on attendance and ASU. Finally, while the study’s session attendance rate is much higher than the national average in SUD group treatments [16], approximately half of the sample never attended an intervention session. Nevertheless, our research supported our hypothesis and provided further evidence about the impact of Community Wise on ASU.

Conclusion

Our current research combined the MOST and CBPR frameworks to identify intervention delivery strategies that maximize attendance in Community Wise among people with a history of SUD. ITT analysis successfully identified intervention components that increased intervention attendance. The optimized Community Wise intervention should be delivered in harm-reduction CBO to people who are disengaged from treatment and experiencing vulnerabilities such as poverty, houselessness, and they receive payment to attend the intervention sessions. The optimized Community Wise is ready to be tested for effectiveness in community settings. We plan additional research to test the generalizability of our findings in other settings and populations and develop effective implementation strategies.
