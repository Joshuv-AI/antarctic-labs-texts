Influence of blue, yellow, and red light spectra on growth performance and physiological development of Japanese Quail (Coturnix japonica) under a floor-based rearing system
Authors: Nima Sanaei, Reza Alimardani, Hossein Moravej, Pouya Farshbaf Aghajani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of different LED light spectra (blue, red, and sunlight/yellow) on the growth performance of Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) reared on the floor. A total of 300 quails age in day 33(235 females, 75 males) were used. Until week 5, all birds were kept under uniform sunlight to eliminate confounding light related stress. During week 6, they were assigned to sunlight (yellow), blue, or red light groups under controlled conditions (24 ± 1 °C, 55–60% humidity).Body weight was recorded weekly, and data were analyzed using ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD to determine treatment effects. Machine learning (ML) algorithms Linear Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost were applied to predict growth trends and assess model accuracy.Blue light significantly increased body weight (p < 0.001) at week 6, averaging 298.5 ± 3.2 g, followed by sunlight (284.2 ± 2.8 g) and red light (271.6 ± 4.1 g). A notable Gender × Light interaction (F = 29.23, p < 0.001) showed females responded more positively to blue light. After lighting treatments, the female–male difference reached 50.3 g, while none was observed earlier (p > 0.38).Among ML models, XGBoost performed best (R² = 0.90, RMSE = 3.7 g), followed by Random Forest (R² = 0.84). Blue light improved growth and produced calmer behavior, whereas red light reduced performance. Applying blue light during the growing phase and sunlight during laying appears optimal for productivity and welfare. Future work should test hybrid spectra with gradual color transitions under farm conditions.Keywords: Gender effects, Effect of light, Quail egg weight, Growth patterns, Egg laying time

Introduction

Poultry production, encompassing chickens, quails, turkeys, geese, and ducks, represents a fundamental component of global agriculture, contributing substantially to food security and economic sustainability (Scanes, 2007). Poultry meat and eggs serve as affordable and high quality sources of protein, vitamins (B₁₂, D), and minerals (iron, zinc), while eggs also exhibit health promoting properties such as immune enhancement and anti inflammatory effects (Ruxton et al., 2010). Compared with large livestock, poultry species exhibit shorter growth cycles, higher feed conversion efficiency, and greater reproductive capacity. Among them, quails (Coturnix japonica) are particularly valuable due to their early sexual maturity (6–7 weeks) and high egg production (up to 300 eggs per year), making them useful for both commercial farming and scientific research (Genchev, 2012; Varkoohi et al., 2010). Their small body size, low feed and space requirements, and manageable husbandry conditions make them economically attractive for small scale production and favored as a model organism for studies on nutrition, genetics, and animal welfare (Bokkers et al., 2011; Shim and Vohra, 1984).

Light, as a multifaceted environmental cue including its intensity, duration, wavelength, and spectral quality plays a crucial role in regulating poultry physiology, behavior, and productivity. Variations in photoperiod modulate circadian rhythms, stimulate feed intake, and influence growth hormone secretion, ultimately enhancing weight gain and feed efficiency in broilers(Karadavut et al., 2017; Woodard et al., 1969). In laying birds, progressive increases in day length after maturity improve egg laying rate, egg quality (such as shell thickness and weight), and overall reproductive performance (Brickett et al., 2007; Li et al., 2010; Li WenBin et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2017). Moreover, specific light spectra particularly blue and green are reported to reduce stress and promote calmer behaviors in broilers, whereas poorly designed lighting systems may exacerbate detrimental behaviors like feather pecking and cannibalism (Farghly et al., 2019; Rozenboim et al., 2004). The adoption of light emitting diode (LED) technologies has introduced precise control over color, intensity, and photoperiod, offering energy efficient means of optimizing bird performance for different production goals (Hefnawy et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2020, 2021; Shi et al., 2019). Nevertheless, although blue light is reported to regulate circadian rhythms and metabolism, and red light to alleviate stress in certain species, the specific influence of different light wavelengths on quail growth and welfare particularly during rapid juvenile development remains insufficiently explored.

Artificial intelligence is being incorporated into animal research more quickly thanks to recent developments in computer modeling. In order to forecast outcomes like body weight gain, machine learning (ML) algorithms particularly eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Random Forest (RFR) effectively manage complicated, nonlinear interactions among various variables including light wavelength, gender, and environmental factors (Ojo et al., 2022;Ojoa et al., n.d.). These models excel at uncovering hidden patterns in multidimensional biological datasets, offering superior predictive accuracy compared to traditional statistical methods (Ojo et al., 2022; Pitesky et al., 2020; Subramani et al., 2025). XGBoost minimizes prediction errors through gradient boosting and typically achieves high coefficients of determination while RFR’s ensemble method prevents overfitting and enhances model robustness (Benos et al., 2021; Liakos et al., 2018; Mirani et al., 2021). These tools enable data driven optimization of environmental parameters, improving performance, reducing costs, and supporting sustainable decision making in poultry production (Ali et al., 2024; Mia et al., 2025).

Complementary to ML, classical statistical analyses such as analysis of variance (ANOVA)and Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test provide confirmatory validation of treatment effects . (Ahmad et al., 2023; Almeida et al., 2015; Yadav and Chaturvedi, 2015). These tests accurately detect pairwise differences between lighting conditions (e.g., blue vs. red light) and verify statistical significance (e.g., p < 0.000001). Conducting such experiments under carefully controlled environmental conditions maintaining constant temperature (22–25 °C), humidity (50–60%), and light intensity (10–15 lux) ensures that observed differences result primarily from light wavelength effects (Bahuti et al., 2023; Nanda et al., 2021; Soliman and El Sabrout, 2020; Tian et al., 2018; Yadav and Chaturvedi, 2015). Additionally, considering potential sex related variations is critical, since males and females often differ in growth trajectory and light sensitivity, potentially affecting both meat yield and egg production metrics. Addressing these factors provides deeper understanding for developing tailored lighting strategies to improve growth rate, feed efficiency, and reproductive success. Optimizing lighting conditions represents a practical and ethical advancement for modern quail production. Accelerated growth reduces production time and feed costs, enhancing profitability, while proper illumination alleviates stress one of the major welfare issues contributing to poor performance, disease susceptibility, and aggression. Creating a comfortable visual environment through customized LED lighting improves both productivity and welfare (Elbaz et al., 2023; Nasr et al., 2019; Nunes et al., 2016; Yadav and Chaturvedi, 2015; Yano et al., 2023).

This study investigates the effects of red (620–720 nm), blue (475–495 nm), and white (380–750 nm) LED lighting on the growth performance of Coturnix japonica chicks, focusing on weight gain and gender specific responses in a controlled environment. By integrating LED illumination with advanced machine learning techniques and statistical analyses, a real time local control system was implemented. The research aims to determine the optimal lighting conditions for efficient and sustainable quail production.

Materials and Methods

Location and Facility Setup

The study was implemented in Kelardasht, Mazandaran, northern Iran, in a 35 m² rearing house with a 3 m ceiling. An 8 m² section (3 m height) was isolated using wooden frames and thick plastic sheets to make a controlled breeding environment Fig. 1. Unlike conventional cage based systems for meat and egg laying quails, this study utilized a floor based system to enhance chick welfare, prioritizing comfort and natural behavior.

Controlled breeding environment featuring a 12 cm thick sawdust bedding. (b) View of the experimental setup with lighting illumination (10–15 lux) and (25 30) temperature.

Environmental Control

To reduce stress, the plastic walls were painted white up to 1.8 m to block visual distractions inside and outside the facility. The litter was covered with a 12 cm sawdust layer to create a soft, absorbent floor. Heat was supplied by a heater, and a controller with a fan kept the temperature between 22 and 25 °C. Lighting was maintained at 10–15 lux, measured with a lux meter, to support normal activity without discomfort. Humidity was controlled at 50–60% using a hygrometer. The environment was stabilized for three days before chick placement to ensure uniform conditions.

Quail chicks and Health

300 Japanese quail chicks (Coturnix japonica), aged 33 days, were sourced from a reputable supplier in Gilan province. All chicks were visually inspected and confirmed healthy upon arrival. Each chick was individually weighed and fitted with a leg tag for identification, enabling precise monitoring throughout the study.

Feeding and Water Management

Chicks received 30 g of feed daily, formulated to meet nutritional requirements: 3050 kcal/kg metabolizable energy, 19% crude protein, 0.8% calcium, 1.14% lysine, 0.46% methionine, 0.76% threonine, 0.14% sodium, 0.38% phosphorus, and 0.77% methionine+cysteine. Feed was dispensed in designated feeders, and fresh water was available ad libitum in clean drinker.

Lighting

The lighting schedule provided 14 h of illumination (10–15 lux) and 10 h of darkness daily to mimic natural diurnal cycles and promote healthy growth. Light intensity was consistently monitored with a lux meter to ensure compliance with the target range.

Growth Monitoring and Data Collection

Chicks were weighed weekly on a fixed day and time using a calibrated digital scale to track growth rates accurately. This standardized schedule minimized variations due to feeding or activity patterns, ensuring reliable data collection. All procedures involving the weighing of chicks, as well as egg collection and weighing, were performed manually by a single observer to maintain consistency. Furthermore, behavioral assessments including pecking, vocalization, and chasing behavior were recorded exclusively through direct visual observation, without the use of automated or electronic recording devices. To ensure biosecurity and minimize potential stress or disease transmission, all egg collection and handling procedures were consistently performed by the same individual. Regarding egg evaluation, due to current equipment constraints, our analysis was focused specifically on egg weight as the primary metric, which provides a reliable baseline for growth and productivity performance in this study.

Experimental Light Treatment

At the start of week six, chicks were randomly assigned to three groups, each housed in separate rooms exposed to different lighting conditions for one week: red (620–720 nm, longest wavelength), blue (475–495 nm, shortest wavelength), or white (380–750 nm, varying from warm yellowish to cool bluish tones) Fig. 2. The colored lights were selected from the seven primary spectral colors to investigate the impact of wavelength on growth performance. At the end of the week, all chicks were reweighed to assess weight gain under each lighting condition.

Growth Model Equations for Weight Evaluation

To assess the weight gain of Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) chicks, four non linear growth models were employed to quantify growth metrics, denoted as F1 to _4. These models, commonly used in poultry growth studies, capture distinct patterns of weight development over time. Weekly weight measurements (Section 2.6) were fitted to the following equations, adapted from Teleken, Galvão, and Robazza (2017), to derive the growth metrics used in statistical and machine learning analyses:

Logistic Model (F1)

The logistic model describes weight gain with a sigmoidal curve, characterized by rapid growth and a fixed inflection point at approximately 50% of the mature weight. The Eq. (7) is:

In the logistic growth equation, W(t)represents the predicted body weight of the quail in grams at time tmeasured in weeks from hatching, capturing the S shaped trajectory from initial slow growth to an exponential phase and eventual plateau; W∞is the asymptotic mature weight parameter, denoting the theoretical maximum body mass the bird approaches as tincreases (e.g., ∼300 g for females under optimal light); W0is the initial weight at hatching, typically a small value like 8–10 g reflecting neonate size; kis the intrinsic growth rate constant in units of week⁻¹, quantifying the steepness of the sigmoidal curve and how quickly the bird nears maturity; and exp⁡is the base of the natural exponential function, enabling the nonlinear transition modeled here for blue light enhanced gains.

Brody Model (F2)

The Brody model represents growth with a diminishing returns behavior, lacking an inflection point, and is suitable for describing early exponential growth. The Eq. (8) is:

This Brody equation models W(t), the body weight in grams at age tin weeks, as an approach to the mature asymptote W∞(e.g., upper limit under red light conditions) minus a decaying initial deficit (W∞−W0)scaled by the exponential term, where W0is the starting hatch weight in grams (e.g., 9 g baseline); the rate parameter k(week⁻¹) governs the exponential decay speed, reflecting how environmental factors like light wavelength decelerate growth toward equilibrium without a distinct bend, ideal for male quails showing linear like early phases.

Gompertz Model (F3)

The Gompertz model exhibits a sigmoidal shape with an inflection point at approximately 37% of the mature weight, ideal for capturing rapid early growth in quails. The Eq. (9) is:

Here, the Gompertz function defines W(t)as the weight in grams at time t(weeks), starting from near zero and accelerating asymmetrically to W∞, the horizontal asymptote for mature size (e.g., 280 g under white light); the double exponential structure outer exp⁡for sigmoidal lift and inner exp⁡(−k(t−ti))for delay positions the inflection at ti(weeks, the time of maximum growth rate, often ∼2–3 weeks in quails), with k(week⁻¹) as the growth rate parameter controlling curve sharpness, particularly sensitive to blue light's early boost in females.

Von Bertalanffy Model (F4)

The Von Bertalanffy model describes growth with an inflection point at approximately 30% of the mature weight, balancing anabolic and catabolic processes. The Eq. (10) is:

The Von Bertalanffy equation computes W(t), body weight in grams over tweeks, as the cubed power of a monomolecular approach to W∞(mature weight asymptote, e.g., 290 g for red light males), where the term (1−exp⁡(−k(t−t0)))models net anabolic accumulation delayed by t0(initial age offset in weeks, often near hatching); k(week⁻¹) is the anabolic rate constant, embodying the balance between tissue building and breakdown, with the cubic exponent reflecting volume proportional growth in poultry like quails under varying spectra.

The weekly weight data for each quail chick were fitted to these models using non linear least squares regression, implemented in MATLAB R2011a with the Curve Fitting Tool (Teleken et al., 2017), to estimate parameters (W∞, W0, k) Initial parameter values were determined through visual inspection of weight vs. time plots. The resulting growth metrics (F1 to F4) represent the predicted weight trajectories under each model, which were standardized using StandardScaler (Section 2.8.3) and used as output variables in statistical analyses (ANOVA, Tukey HSD, Section 2.8.2) and machine learning models (XGBoost, Random Forest, Section 2.8.3) to evaluate the effects of light color (blue, red, white), gender, and treatment on growth performance. The choice of these models was informed by their applicability to Japanese quail. Although the Richards model is recognized for its flexibility due to a variable inflection point (Teleken et al., 2017). it was not employed in this study because the Gompertz,Logistic, Brody and Von Bertalanffy models provided a superior fit with biological meaningfulness and lower computational complexity for the observed growth data.

Statistical Analysis

ANOVA and Tukey HSD Analysis of Japanese Quail Growth Metrics

ANOVA and Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) tests were conducted to evaluate the effects of treatment (TREATMENT CAT, representing 296 individual treatments), gender (GENDER, Male = 1, Female = 2), light color (LIGHT: White =1, Blue =2, Red=3), and their interactions on the growth metrics (F1 to F4), which correspond to predicted weight trajectories from the Logistic, Brody, Gompertz, and Von Bertalanffy models, respectively (Section 2.9). The analyses include one way ANOVA to assess individual and interaction effects, followed by Tukey HSD tests for pairwise comparisons to identify specific group differences.

According to Table 4: One Way ANOVA Results for TREATMENT CAT, GENDER, and LIGHT, the effect of each factor was assessed individually on the growth metrics. TREATMENT CAT, representing the 296 distinct treatments applied to the quail chicks, showed no significant effect across all metrics (F1 to F4), with p values greater than 0.49 (e.g., p = 0.685 for F1, F = 0.164). This suggests that the specific treatment assignments had minimal influence on the predicted weight trajectories. This outcome was expected, since each quail has unique individual characteristics. GENDER, comparing Male (1) and Female (2) quails, was significant for F1 (F = 10.889, p = 0.001) and F2 (F = 11.162, p = 0.0009), indicating that gender differences strongly affected early growth as modeled by the Logistic and Brody equations, but not for F3 (p = 0.948) or _4 (p = 0.765), corresponding to Gompertz and Von Bertalanffy models.

Effects of lighting conditions on quail growth and behavior

Light conditions (red, blue, white) significantly affect Japanese quail growth and behavior.

Impact on behavior

The study showed that the type of light significantly affects the vocalization levels of Japanese quails. Under white light, the quails exhibited their natural vocalization levels. When exposed to red light, there was a noticeable increase in noise, while blue light significantly reduced the noise levels. These vocal changes were further supported by qualitative visual observations; under red light, the quails displayed increased activity, specifically frequent chasing and pecking behaviors, which appeared to correlate with higher vocalization and stress levels. In contrast, blue light seemed to have a calming effect, decreasing both overt aggressive behaviors and vocalizations. These findings suggest that while blue light may mitigate stress, red light might induce a state of mild agitation, as reflected in the observed increases in both vocal and physical activity.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that different LED lighting conditions, particularly blue light, had a pronounced effect on the growth performance, egg production, and behavior of Japanese quails. Statistical and machine learning analyses consistently showed that variations in light wavelength significantly influenced body weight, growth rate, and egg quality parameters. Overall, female quails achieved higher growth values than males across all models, with significant differences emerging from the second week onward. Blue light promoted the greatest gains in both early and total growth (F₁ and F₂ models), whereas red light was less effective and in some cases associated with moderate behavioral stress.

The machine learning results, especially from XGB and RFR revealed strong predictive ability for biological growth dynamics. Both models captured nonlinear interactions between light, gender, and treatment with high accuracy (R² ≈ 0.90 for XGB and 0.84 for RFR). Their performance supports the growing use of data driven approaches in animal physiology, enabling accurate modeling of complex environmental effects that classical regressions cannot handle. Importantly, these models distinguished gender specific responses: blue light was optimal for females, while males exhibited slightly better adaptation under red light. Such differences likely stem from variations in photoreceptor sensitivity and hypothalamic pituitary regulation of growth hormones and melatonin secretion.

Traditional ANOVA and Tukey HSD analyses validated these machine learning findings. Both GENDER and LIGHT exerted highly significant effects on the early growth parameters (F₁, F₂; p < 0.001), while no differences were observed for the later stages (F₃, F₄). The significant GENDER × LIGHT interaction (F = 29.23 for F₁) indicated a strong synergistic response of females to blue light. Weekly weight analysis reinforced this trend: females were heavier than males from week 2 onward, and by week 6 the mean difference exceeded 50 g. These outcomes confirm that early developmental phases in quails are particularly sensitive to spectral input.

Egg production patterns further supported the growth findings. Under blue light, egg count peaked during the night period (31.4 eggs), while sunlight/yellow light reached its maximum in the evening (31.0 eggs). Red light maintained moderate but stable productivity throughout the day. These circadian differences imply that blue spectra can shift laying rhythms by influencing melatonin or luteinizing hormone release. However, egg weight was inversely related: eggs produced under sunlight (white/yellow) were heaviest (12.0 g), followed by red (10.5 g) and blue (9.1 g) light.

Behavioral observations aligned with physiological data. Birds under blue light displayed calmer activity and reduced vocalization, whereas red light caused an increase in noise and agitation, indicative of higher stress. White/sunlight light maintained natural, balanced behaviors. From a welfare perspective, the calming nature of blue light may also reduce feather pecking and aggression, ultimately contributing to steadier growth and survival rates.

From an application standpoint, the results demonstrate that tailored lighting programs can simultaneously improve productivity and welfare in modern quail farming. Blue light is recommended during growth phases to maximize weight gain and minimize stress, whereas longer wavelength sunlight/yellow (or warm white) lighting is better suited for the laying period to enhance egg weight and overall quality. Because LED systems are energy efficient and easily programmable, they also provide a practical, sustainable solution for large scale integration with IoT based monitoring systems.

In conclusion, this work provides quantitative and mechanistic evidence that blue LED lighting improves growth and wellbeing in Japanese quails, while red light may induce mild stress and lower productivity. The interplay between light spectrum and gender highlights the importance of precision lighting as a management tool. Future studies should focus on mixed spectrum or gradually changing light programs to mimic natural daylight transitions and assess their long term effects on reproduction, physiology, and behavior.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the effects of blue, red, and sunlight/yellow LED lighting on the growth performance, egg production, and behavior(Pecking, Sound and Chasing behavior) of Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) reared in a floor-based system. From weeks 1 to 5 (32-67 days of age), female quails showed a higher growth rate than males, gaining approximately 16 g/week and reaching about 280 g by week 5, whereas males gained about 8 g/week and plateaued at approximately 240 g by week 3. Statistical analysis confirmed a significant effect of gender on early growth traits, with ANOVA showing significance for F1 (F = 10.889, p = 0.001) and F2 (F = 11.162, p = 0.0009). Tukey HSD also showed that females had significantly higher early growth than males for F1 (mean difference = 6.694, p = 0.0011) and F2 (mean difference = 6.737, p = 0.0009).Light color also significantly influenced early growth performance. Tukey HSD pairwise comparisons showed that blue light significantly improved growth compared with white light for F1 (mean difference = 11.497, p < 0.001) and compared with red light for F1 (mean difference = 11.315, p < 0.001), with similar trends observed for F2. In agreement with these findings, machine learning models identified blue light as the optimal condition for female quails, while red light was more favorable for males. Among the tested models, XGBoost showed the highest predictive performance, with R2 values of 0.9122 for F1 and 0.8894 for F4, followed by Random Forest, which also provided reliable predictions.Regarding egg production, blue light resulted in the highest egg number during the evening/night period (31.4 eggs, SD = 6.8), although it produced lighter eggs (9.14 g). In contrast, sunlight/yellow light produced heavier eggs (12.01 g) and also maintained high egg production in the evening (31.0 eggs, SD = 3.5). Red light showed intermediate performance, with a peak egg count of 25.9 eggs (SD = 1.3) and an egg weight of 10.55 g. These findings indicate that blue light is more effective for maximizing egg number, whereas sunlight/yellow light may be more suitable for improving egg weight.Behavioral observations further suggested that light color affected quail welfare. Blue light reduced vocalization and appeared to have a calming effect, while red light slightly increased vocalization and visually observed chasing and pecking behavior, indicating mild agitation or stress. Sunlight/yellow light maintained more natural vocalization patterns.

Overall, the results demonstrate that both gender and light color significantly affect early growth performance in Japanese quails, particularly under blue light for females and red light for males. Blue light appears to be the most effective strategy for improving growth and egg number, while sunlight/yellow light may be preferable for enhancing egg weight. These findings support the use of gender-specific lighting strategies to improve productivity, efficiency, and welfare in quail production systems. Further research is recommended to investigate long-term effects of lighting programs and to optimize lighting schedules under commercial rearing conditions.
