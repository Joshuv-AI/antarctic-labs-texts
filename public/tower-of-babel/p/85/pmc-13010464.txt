A new framework for acupoint specificity: Advancing acupuncture studies using the double-dissociation model
Authors: Younbyoung Chae, Da-Eun Yoon, Cheng-Hao Tu, Myeong Soo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcupoint specificity is a central concept in traditional acupuncture theory but remains controversial in modern scientific studies. Methodological inconsistencies and a predominant reliance on single-dissociation approaches have contributed to this uncertainty. Therefore, we propose a double-dissociation research framework designed to rigorously test the functional specificity of two commonly used acupoints. This model applies double-dissociation methodology to provide stronger causal inferences regarding acupoint-specific effects, where dissociation refers to a pattern in which each acupoint preferentially modulates one physiological domain relative to another. The framework contrasts two theoretically distinct acupoints, PC6 and ST36, with respect to two physiological outcome domains that are hypothesized to be functionally dissociable. In particular, PC6 is predicted to selectively influence cardiovascular measures, such as blood pressure, whereas ST36 is predicted to selectively influence gastric function. Within this framework, a double dissociation would be demonstrated if PC6 produces greater changes in cardiovascular measures than in gastric indices, whereas ST36 produces greater changes in gastric indices than in cardiovascular measures. Evidence of such crossed selectivity would provide support for functional acupoint specificity. The proposed double-dissociation paradigm can be extended to other clinically relevant acupoints and acupoint combinations, and this proof-of-concept article is aimed at stimulating future empirical studies that formally test acupoint-specific effects using such designs. Demonstrating this crossed selectivity—where each acupoint predominantly affects only its predicted outcome—would provide stronger evidence for the functional specialization of acupuncture points. This methodological approach aims to align acupuncture research more closely with contemporary scientific standards.Keywords: Acupuncture, Acupoint specificity, Double dissociation, Experimental design

Introduction

In traditional acupuncture theory, acupoints are defined as specific body locations that exhibit distinct physiological responses.1 Therefore, substantial efforts are required to refine and standardize acupoint terminology, alongside rigorous empirical investigations of their functional specificity.2 Acupuncture point specificity constitutes a fundamental concept in traditional East Asian medicine; however, its empirical basis remains contested in the scientific community.3,4 Several studies have supported the notion of acupoint specificity.5, 6, 7 However, ongoing debate centers on whether these effects reflect acupoint-specific mechanisms or are better explained by generalized somatosensory, contextual, or other non-specific influences.8, 9, 10 Numerous clinical and neurophysiological studies indicate that acupuncture can elicit non-specific systemic effects, complicating the attribution of observed outcomes uniquely to stimulation of specific acupoints rather than to generalized physiological activation.11,12 For clinical and mechanistic studies to progress from associative interpretations toward more robust causal inference, rigorously designed control conditions are essential.13 By isolating the effects of the independent variable from potential confounders, well-designed control conditions allow physiological or clinical changes to be attributed more confidently to the intervention under investigation.

A major methodological limitation in the acupoint-specificity literature is the predominant reliance on single-dissociation experimental designs. In such studies, one acupoint is compared to a control acupoint or a sham point using a single outcome measure.13 Such comparisons can demonstrate that a given acupoint produces stronger changes than a control condition on a particular outcome measure; however, they cannot determine whether this apparent selectivity reflects a truly acupoint-specific physiological mechanism or simply the most prominent expression of a more generalized pattern of physiological activation or differential measurement sensitivity. Thus, single-acupoint-versus-sham designs provide important evidence of efficacy but are inherently limited for establishing functional acupoint specificity in the stricter, double-dissociation sense. Therefore, we propose adapting the methodological framework of double-dissociation, which has been highly influential in cognitive neuroscience. In that field, double-dissociation has served as the methodological gold standard for establishing functional specialization across neural systems.14 The strength of the double-dissociation approach lies in its capacity to demonstrate that two stimuli or manipulations place distinct processing demands on two functionally separable subsystems. Classic applications of double-dissociation in neuroscience have delineated numerous core functions, including semantic versus spatial cognition, object versus action recognition, and vision for action versus vision for perception.15, 16, 17 In our proposed model, the acupoints, such as PC6 and ST36, serve as the experimental manipulations, whereas cardiovascular and gastric outcomes represent the relatively distinct functional domains whose differential modulation is tested. Adapting this conceptual framework to acupuncture studies enables a more stringent evaluation of whether different acupoints modulate distinct physiological systems in a selectively paired manner.

To address this longstanding debate regarding acupoint specificity, we propose a double-dissociation research design, adapted from cognitive neuroscience, to test the functional specificity of two frequently used acupoints: PC6, implicated in blood pressure and cardiovascular autonomic regulation, and ST36, associated with gastric motility.18, 19, 20, 21 By pairing these two functionally distinct acupoints with two separable outcome domains—blood pressure regulation and gastric motility—we aimed to construct an empirical model capable of revealing a double-dissociation. Demonstration of such a crossed interaction pattern would provide robust evidence for functional acupoint specificity, surpassing the inferential limitations inherent in traditional single-dissociation designs.

Defining the limitations of single-dissociation designs in acupuncture studies

A critical limitation of many existing studies is their predominant reliance on single-dissociation paradigms, which test a single acupoint against one outcome measure or compare a single acupoint with a sham point. Such designs are insufficient to rigorously exclude alternative explanations, including generalized somatosensory stimulation, placebo effects, or non-specific physiological activation. More fundamentally, single-dissociation approaches cannot determine whether an acupoint influences its presumed target function or, instead, produces broader, system-wide effects across multiple physiological domains.

In single-dissociation designs, the effect of an individual acupoint on a single primary outcome is evaluated relative to a control condition, such as a sham point or an unrelated acupoint.13 For instance, to evaluate whether PC6 modulates cardiovascular function, studies compare its effect on blood pressure with that of a control point. If PC6 produces a stronger response, this difference is often interpreted as evidence of acupoint specificity (Fig. 1A). However, such designs are insufficient to resolve causal questions as to whether the observed effect reflects a truly selective physiological pathway. Single-dissociation cannot definitively exclude alternative explanations, including generalized somatosensory activation, placebo responses, or non-specific autonomic changes. Consequently, even if PC6 outperforms a control point, this finding does not establish that PC6 engages a dedicated cardiac-autonomic mechanism; it may simply exert the most prominent effect among multiple unmeasured or non-specific physiological responses across other systems (Fig. 1B).

Conceptual issues in single-dissociation and the logic of double-dissociation.

(A) In single-dissociation designs, the effect of PC6 on a primary outcome (blood pressure) is compared to a sham point. A stronger effect is interpreted as evidence of specificity. (B) Such designs cannot determine whether PC6 selectively targets cardiovascular pathways or influences multiple other physiological systems. (C) Double-dissociation logic: Manipulation A affects Function X but not Function Y, whereas Manipulation B affects Function Y but not Function X. This interaction pattern provides strong evidence of functional specialization because it cannot be easily attributed to generalized factors. Similarly, Acupoint A selectively affects Outcome X but not Y, whereas Acupoint B selectively affects Outcome Y but not X, providing stronger evidence for functional specificity.

In efforts to evaluate acupoint-specific efficacy for particular conditions, clinical trials have compared treatment outcomes across disease-affected meridian, non-affected meridian, and sham acupuncture groups.22 For instance, the disease-affected meridian group receiving acupuncture at PC6 and HT5 demonstrated greater reductions in angina attacks compared to the non-affected meridian group (LU6 and LU9) or the sham acupuncture group. This pattern suggests that PC6, located on the pericardium meridian, and HT5, on the heart meridian, may confer superior therapeutic effects for cardiac conditions relative to unrelated control points. However, such findings do not confirm that PC6 and HT5 exert selective effects on cardiac-specific pathways; they may simply represent the most prominent among multiple unmeasured or non-specific physiological effects. Reanalyzing or extending such clinical trials within a double-dissociation framework that simultaneously assesses non-cardiac outcomes (for example, gastric or respiratory indices) would allow stronger inferences about whether PC6 and HT5-related effects are truly specialized for cardiac-autonomic regulation. Therefore, single-dissociation approaches cannot determine whether the action of a given acupoint is restricted to a single physiological domain or extends across multiple interconnected systems.

Implementing double-dissociation in acupuncture studies

The double-dissociation approach provides a rigorous framework for drawing causal inferences about functional acupoint specialization. The logic of double-dissociation can be summarized as follows: Manipulation A affects Function X but not Function Y, whereas Manipulation B affects Function Y but not Function X (Fig. 1C). This crossed interaction pattern offers strong evidence of functional specialization because it cannot readily be explained by generalized factors, such as task difficulty, measurement sensitivity, or global physiological disruption. In contrast, single-dissociation patterns are inherently ambiguous, as an apparently selective impairment may simply reflect differences in task demands or methodological sensitivity rather than true functional independence.

Double-dissociation is a methodological framework developed originally in neuropsychology to demonstrate the functional independence of cognitive systems. Studies over several decades have shown that visual information is processed along two anatomically and functionally distinct cortical pathways: the ventral stream, associated with vision for perception, and the dorsal stream, associated with vision for action.16 This crossed pattern—ventral-stream damage impairing perception while sparing action, and dorsal-stream damage impairing action while sparing perception—constitutes a classic example of double-dissociation. These findings indicate that visual perception and visuomotor control rely on dissociable neural mechanisms rather than on a single, unified system.

Acupuncture studies face conceptual challenges similar to those that led neuroscientists to adopt double-dissociation approaches. All forms of needling activate somatosensory afferents, sham interventions frequently produce measurable physiological effects, and autonomic changes may occur systemically rather than in a point-specific manner. Consequently, distinguishing acupoint-specific effects from generalized physiological responses is methodologically challenging. By using two distinct acupoints and two physiologically separable outcome domains, a double-dissociation design provides a rigorous analytical strategy for isolating acupoint-specific functional pathways.

Based on prior findings, PC6 is expected to exert a strong influence on blood pressure and cardiovascular autonomic regulation, whereas ST36 is expected to affect gastric motility and gastrointestinal autonomic activity.18, 19, 20, 21 For example, animal and clinical studies have shown that electroacupuncture at ST36 modulates gastric motility and functional dyspepsia-related symptoms via vagovagal and sympathetic reflexes, consistent with a preferential influence on gastrointestinal function.20,21 To evaluate whether PC6 exerts a specific effect on cardiovascular function, studies commonly compare PC6 with one or more control points. If PC6 demonstrates superior modulation of blood pressure relative to these controls, this finding is interpreted as evidence of acupoint specificity. Similarly, ST36 is considered specific for gastric function if it produces greater modulation of gastric indices than control points (Fig. 2A). However, single-dissociation designs cannot determine whether PC6 selectively affects cardiovascular regulation or influences gastric motility, nor can they establish whether ST36 selectively affects gastric function or additionally modulates blood pressure. Therefore, under single-dissociation logic, acupoint specificity remains ambiguous, as observed effects may reflect the relative magnitude of physiological responses rather than true functional selectivity. Consequently, single-dissociation designs are insufficient to determine whether the effects of an individual acupoint are confined to a specific physiological domain or extend across multiple interconnected systems. These limitations emphasize the need for a more rigorous analytical framework capable of distinguishing truly selective acupoint effects from generalized physiological responses.

Example of double-dissociation design for acupuncture studies.

(A) Single-dissociation design. PC6 is considered specific for blood pressure regulation if it produces greater cardiovascular effects than a control point, and ST36 is considered specific for gastric motility if it produces greater gastrointestinal effects than controls. However, single-dissociation comparisons cannot determine whether each acupoint selectively affects only its presumed domain or influences other physiological systems. (B) Double-dissociation design. PC6 selectively modulates cardiovascular function but not gastric function, whereas ST36 selectively modulates gastric function but not cardiovascular regulation, demonstrating the functional specialization of each acupoint. This design can reveal crossed specificity consistent with true functional specialization.

To investigate the specific influence of PC6 on blood pressure and ST36 on gastric motility, both outcome variables should be measured simultaneously within the same experimental design. If PC6 selectively modulates blood pressure without significantly affecting gastric motility, and ST36 selectively modulates gastric motility without significantly altering blood pressure, this crossed pattern would constitute a double-dissociation. Such findings would provide compelling evidence that PC6 is functionally specialized for cardiovascular regulation, whereas ST36 is specialized for gastrointestinal modulation (Fig. 2B). Participants will undergo standardized acupuncture stimulation across separate sessions, with concurrent measurement of beat-to-beat blood pressure, heart rate variability indices, electrogastrography signals, and quantitative indices of gastric motility. A 2 × 3 repeated-measures analysis of variance would test the interaction between acupoint condition (PC6, ST36, and control points) and outcome type (cardiovascular vs. gastric function). Within this analytical framework, the critical inferential focus is on the acupoint × outcome-domain interaction term rather than on main effects of acupoint on any single measure. In addition, multivariate or mixed-effects models can be used to partition variance attributable to shared autonomic fluctuations across cardiovascular and gastric indices, thereby isolating relative, domain-preferential effects of each acupoint. Evidence that each acupoint selectively modulates its theoretically predicted physiological domain while exerting minimal influence on the alternate domain would provide strong support for functional acupoint specificity within a double-dissociation framework.

Significance and considerations of double-dissociation in acupuncture studies

The double-dissociation research model represents a significant methodological advance over standard acupuncture trial designs. First, it helps control for generalized somatosensory stimulation by comparing two theoretically distinct acupoints, rather than relying solely on a single acupoint–sham contrast. Second, by pairing two functionally distinct acupoints with two physiologically separable outcome domains, this design enables researchers to test whether stimulation at a specific acupoint exerts a selectively targeted effect on its presumed functional system. Therefore, this approach brings acupuncture research into closer alignment with contemporary neuroscience standards for demonstrating functional specialization. Third, the proposed framework is not restricted to the point specificity of the exemplar acupoints PC6 and ST36. The same double-dissociation paradigm can be generalized to investigate the functional specificity of other clinically important acupoints and acupoint combinations. This article is intended as a proof-of-concept contribution and is designed to stimulate future empirical studies that formally test acupoint-specific effects using double-dissociation designs. To our knowledge, however, no existing study has yet implemented the full double-dissociation paradigm we propose, in which PC6 and ST36 are compared within the same participants using simultaneous cardiovascular and gastric measurements. Although the same logical framework could also be applied to compare acupuncture with non-acupuncture interventions, such as visual or pharmacological stimuli, the present model is specifically intended to address within-modality acupoint specificity rather than cross-modality differences.23

To implement a valid double-dissociation design in acupuncture studies, the two independent variables (acupoints) and the two dependent variables (outcomes) must each represent functions that are, at least in principle, separable into distinct domains. A previous study sought to determine whether pain-specific versus depression-specific acupoints produced dissociable therapeutic effects in patients with comorbid chronic pain and major depressive disorder.24 However, the study failed to demonstrate that pain-specific acupoints exerted superior analgesic effects relative to depression-specific points, or that depression-specific points produced superior antidepressant effects. One likely explanation is that chronic pain and depression are highly comorbid and share overlapping neurobiological pathways, rendering these outcomes insufficiently independent for a double-dissociation design. Moreover, the acupoints selected for pain and depression may not have been sufficiently distinct in their physiological functions to generate a clear dissociation. This negative finding illustrates that when outcome domains are highly comorbid and neurobiologically overlapping, even carefully designed acupuncture protocols may fail to yield a clear double dissociation, underscoring the importance of selecting systems with at least partially separable regulatory circuits.

In contrast, the cardiovascular and gastric systems represent a more promising candidate pair for a double-dissociation design. Both cardiovascular and gastric functions are regulated by parasympathetic vagal pathways and specific autonomic nuclei within the brainstem, such that blood pressure and gastric motility exhibit physiological cross-talk rather than complete independence.25, 26, 27 Rather than assuming complete independence between these systems, our proposed design explicitly treats their shared vagal-brainstem regulation as a common autonomic background against which acupoint-specific interaction patterns can be detected. At the same time, each system is supported by partially distinct efferent pathways and regulatory circuits, rendering it plausible that relative selectivity in how different acupoints modulate these domains could be detected. Because of this shared yet partially segregated vagal–brainstem control, simultaneously assessing both domains within a double-dissociation design is essential to determine whether each acupoint exerts relatively selective rather than broadly overlapping effects. By modeling both cardiovascular and gastric responses within the same individuals, this approach allows shared autonomic reactivity to be handled as common variance while focusing inference on relative differences in how PC6 and ST36 modulate each domain. These considerations emphasize the importance of carefully selecting acupoints and outcome measures to ensure true functional separability and to minimize interpretive ambiguity before applying a double-dissociation framework in acupuncture research.

Conclusion

The double-dissociation approach provides a powerful conceptual and experimental framework for testing acupoint specificity. Using PC6 for cardiovascular regulation and ST36 for gastric function, the proposed approach offers a scientifically rigorous means of differentiating the functional contributions of individual acupoints. Demonstrating a double-dissociation would provide compelling evidence that acupoints exert system-specific effects rather than broad, non-specific physiological influences, supporting a more precise mechanistic understanding of acupuncture. Such findings would strengthen the theoretical basis of acupoint specificity and facilitate the integration of acupuncture within modern biomedical science by emphasizing the distinct neural and autonomic pathways engaged by different acupoints.
