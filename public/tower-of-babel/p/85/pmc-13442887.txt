Research on few-shot fault diagnosis and feature extraction mechanism based on ADPCW-ELCNN
Authors: Pengfei Pang, Tengji Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aiming at weak features masked by noise, disconnected parameter-feature adaptation, and unbalanced generalization-efficiency in rotating machinery few-shot fault diagnosis, an Adaptive Dual-Parameter Collaborative Wavelet Convolutional Neural Network is proposed. Different from existing frameworks relying on data augmentation, transfer learning, or blind structural optimization, it adopts a physics-informed, parameter-collaborative, feature-faithful paradigm. Core innovations include quantitative correlation between wavelet scale and convolution kernel length, global–local adaptive strategy, and cascaded nonlinear enhancement. Experimental results on bearing and gearbox datasets show the model has 9.19 K parameters and 0.02 × 103 M FLOPs, with 99.98% average accuracy. It maintains F1-score over 94% under 20-sample and − 10 dB SNR conditions, providing an efficient solution for limited-sample fault diagnosis.

Introduction

As the key power unit in intelligent manufacturing and energy equipment, the health status of core components in rotating machinery, such as industrial gearboxes and rolling bearings, directly affects production continuity, system safety, and maintenance costs1. Due to prolonged exposure to loads, shocks, and friction, these components exhibit a significantly higher probability of failure compared to others. Moreover, early fault signals, characterized by weak low-frequency impulses, are easily masked by background noise, load coupling, and electromagnetic interference. As a result, fault signals often display low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) and strong non-stationary characteristics2. Additionally, collecting fault samples in industrial settings typically requires equipment disassembly during downtime, which disrupts production and increases costs. These challenges collectively create an engineering bottleneck defined by few-shot and high noise interference, rendering traditional diagnostic techniques inadequate for precise fault identification3.

Fault diagnosis technology has evolved through three distinct stages in response to complex working conditions, each of which exhibits limitations in scenarios with limited samples and strong noise interference. The first stage, which relies on physical models, offers clear physical interpretability but depends on precise equipment parameters4. Under few-shot conditions, this approach tends to overfit, while its diagnostic accuracy drops sharply in the presence of strong noise5. The second stage involves signal processing-based methods6, which extract features through techniques such as Fourier Transform (FT)7, Wavelet Transform (WT)8, and Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD)9. Although these methods eliminate the need for complex physical models, they require manual setting of time–frequency parameters. Consequently, they struggle to cover multiple fault types with limited samples, are prone to misidentification under strong noise, and lack adaptability to varying working conditions10. The third stage comprises intelligent diagnosis methods11. Shallow models, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM)12 and Random Forests (RF)13, demonstrate certain classification capabilities under simple conditions but suffer from inadequate feature representation in few-shot scenarios and a significant decline in accuracy amid strong noise13. Among deep learning approaches, models like Autoencoders (AE)14 and Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBM)15 exhibit low sensitivity to local weak features, while architectures such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN)16, Long Short-Term Memory Networks (LSTM)17, and Transformers18 emphasize global temporal relationships but demonstrate limited focus on localized fault characteristics.

In recent years, various methods have been proposed to enhance fault diagnosis performance via physical priors, mechanism modeling, and cross-condition adaptation including semi-supervised ViT frameworks19, burst guided acoustic emission (AE) diagnosis20, hybrid Convolutional Neural Network(CNN) RNN models21, physically inspired metric learning22, causal graph inference23, fine tuning frameworks for distribution shift mitigation24, wavelet correlation alignment methods for cross condition diagnosis25, TCN-based predictive maintenance26, multi-sensor residual learning with Auto-Permutation Feature Importance27, CWT-integrated CNN for pipeline leak detection28, as well as deep learning-based centrifugal pump fault detection with hybrid feature pool29 and health condition identification via multi-filter processed scalograms and CNN30. However, these approaches still fail to fundamentally address the combined challenges of few samples, strong noise, and variable operating conditions in rotating machinery diagnosis.

Specifically, vision-based methods are constrained by data quality and harsh industrial environments; AE methods are noise-sensitive; hybrid models are complex and hard to deploy; mechanism/metric learning methods lack noise robustness and lightweight design; causal inference methods are computationally intensive for real-time few-shot scenarios; distribution shift-focused methods show limited generalization with extremely few samples and struggle to balance feature fidelity and accuracy. The centrifugal pump fault diagnosis methods based on hybrid feature pool and multi-filter scalograms-CNN also exhibit obvious limitations in few-shot and high-noise scenarios, with the former relying on massive labeled samples for feature pool construction and the latter suffering from manual filter parameter setting and poor adaptability to dynamic fault features. Even the latest TCN-based models26 face trade-offs between temporal dependency capture and computational efficiency, residual learning frameworks27 rely on manual feature engineering, and CWT-CNN architectures28 lack end-to-end optimization of wavelet and network parameters. Most existing methods, including the aforementioned centrifugal pump diagnosis approaches, focus on a single challenge or specific rotating machinery type, failing to achieve synergistic optimization under multiple constraints few-shot learning, strong noise, variable working conditions, and this constitutes the core motivation of this study.

To address the limitations of traditional technologies, few-shot fault diagnosis technology has emerged as a research focus, and the primary objective is to resolve the problems of overfitting and weak generalization ability caused by insufficient samples through three technical approaches, namely data augmentation, model optimization, and transfer learning, thereby achieving accurate fault identification. For data augmentation, the data volume is expanded through signal transformations such as Gramian Angular Field transformation or generative models, among which the GAN-based virtual sample generation method proposed by Yu3 is regarded as representative. For model optimization, the dependence on large-scale sample data is reduced by improving the structure of CNN, with the deformable convolution proposed by Wang31 and the lightweight Transformer-CNN architecture designed by Fang32 as typical optimization schemes. For transfer learning, adaptation to few-shot fault diagnosis scenarios is achieved by leveraging pre-trained models from the source domain, where meta-learning-based parameter transfer31 and ResNet18 pre-training with fine-tuning34 are commonly used implementation strategies.

However, limitations still persist in existing technologies. Insufficient mining of fault features is achieved through data augmentation. A collaborative mechanism between parameters and features is lacking in model optimization. Transfer learning is overly dependent on the source domain, and lightweight performance is not adequately considered. A comprehensive transfer collaborative mechanism integrating data and model has not been established. Consequently, it is difficult to simultaneously balance the efficiency of feature extraction under few-shot scenarios and robustness against strong noise.

To address the aforementioned challenges, two strategies combining wavelet transform with CNN are commonly adopted in existing research35, but both suffer from inherent limitations. The first is a serial architecture, where wavelet transform is employed as an independent pre-signal processing step, and the processed features are subsequently fed into the CNN36. Although multi-scale feature adaptability is enhanced by this method, end-to-end optimization cannot be achieved due to the decoupling of the front-end and back-end modules. Additionally, parameter tuning relies on manual experience, resulting in unsatisfactory performance under few-shot conditions. The second is an embedded architecture, where learnable wavelet functions are used to replace the convolution kernels of CNN, enabling implicit extraction of time–frequency features. Notably, recent wavelet-kernel CNNs (e.g., W-CNN37) and scale-adaptive wavelet networks (e.g., WKN38) still have inherent limitations. W-CNN adopts learnable wavelet kernels but lacks collaborative optimization between wavelet scales and convolution kernel lengths, leading to mismatched time–frequency coverage. WKN achieves scale learning yet relies on complex deep architectures, which leads to feature distortion in few-shot scenarios and fails to balance lightweight deployment and weak feature preservation.

Based on a comprehensive review of existing research, the core bottlenecks in few-shot and strong-noise fault diagnosis of rotating machinery lie in four aspects: lack of end-to-end collaboration between wavelet transform and CNN, insufficient adaptation between convolution kernel parameters and key wavelet parameters (e.g., scale, length), weak feature distortion caused by deep architectures plus excessive model size incompatible with edge deployment, and absence of weak feature enhancement mechanisms for few-shot scenarios.

Drawing on the time–frequency physical characteristics of fault signals and the essence of few-shot learning, this study distills these bottlenecks into two core flaws of traditional methods: disconnection between parameter optimization and physical feature extraction, and contradiction between network depth and feature fidelity. To address them, a solution centered on three key innovations is proposed: quantitative collaboration of dual parameters, global–local adaptive adjustment, and lightweight architecture fidelity. Unlike data augmentation relying on virtual samples, transfer learning relying on source domain data, and traditional model optimization relying on structural adjustments, this method embeds physical priors to achieve efficient feature mining under small-sample conditions, enabling both robustness and deployability without external dependencies.

Based on the aforementioned bottlenecks, and building upon the existing Explainable Lightweight CNN (ELCNN) framework39, an Adaptive Dual-Parameter Collaborative Wavelet ELCNN (ADPCW-ELCNN) is proposed. The model achieves technological breakthroughs through the following innovative designs. First, a quantitative correlation mechanism between wavelet scale and convolution kernel length is established to address the disconnection between parameter adaptation and feature extraction. Second, a global and local scale parameters collaboration adaptation strategy is designed to enhance the model’s matching capability for full-frequency-band features. Third, a nonlinear mapping unit is introduced to simultaneously achieve weak feature enhancement and noise suppression. Finally, a single-layer lightweight architecture is adopted, which avoids feature transmission distortion while reducing the number of model parameters to a level suitable for deployment on edge devices.

Compared with existing research, not only has the performance of the proposed method in fault diagnosis tasks been verified through experiments, but also a collaborative optimization theory covering parameter collaboration, feature evolution, and architecture fidelity has been established at the mechanism level, providing dual support of experiments and theory for the effectiveness of the model. ADPCW-ELCNN proposed for few-shot signals

To address the challenges of rotating machinery fault diagnosis under limited labeled samples and strong noise, specifically weak discriminative features masked by noise, generalization from few samples, and lightweight deployment constraints, we propose the ADPCW-ELCNN. The framework is built on four design principles: First, wavelet-based feature extraction aligns with fault-signal physics, enabling the capture of multi-scale transient and periodic patterns. Second, a global–local collaborative mechanism coupled with a single-layer architecture ensures few-shot adaptability while preserving subtle fault signatures. Third, a square nonlinearity is embedded to amplify fault-related features and suppress Gaussian noise, providing built-in denoising. Fourth, dual-parameter wavelet kernels replace standard convolutions, achieving lightweight efficiency suitable for edge deployment.

As shown in Fig. 1, ADPCW-ELCNN is constructed based on ELCNN to form an end-to-end fault diagnosis process. Vibration signals are processed through an adaptive wavelet convolutional layer for multi-scale feature extraction. By establishing a quantitative scale-kernel length relationship and employing a global and local scale parameters collaboration mechanism, an adaptive mapping is achieved where small scales are matched with short kernels and large scales with long kernels, thereby enabling dynamic parameter adjustment and full-frequency-band feature coverage. Subsequently, the features are enhanced in signal-to-noise ratio via a square nonlinear enhancement unit. Finally, the diagnosis results are output by a single-layer lightweight architecture. Joint optimization design of wavelet scale and convolution kernel length

In conventional CNNs, convolutional kernels are generally initialized through random strategies, resulting in uncertain frequency response characteristics during the early training phase, which makes it difficult to directly match the fault features with clear physical meanings in vibration signals. To incorporate prior knowledge of time–frequency analysis into the model, wavelet functions are employed as the initialization basis for the convolutional kernels. Wavelet functions possess inherent time–frequency localization capability, and their center frequencies and bandwidths can be adjusted via scaling factors, thereby providing an effective mathematical foundation for constructing convolutional kernels related to the physical mechanisms of fault features. The discrete convolution form is expressed as follows.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y(n) = \sum\nolimits_{i = 0}^{L - 1} {x\left( {n + i} \right)} \cdot \psi \left( \frac{i}{a} \right)$$\end{document}where x is the input signal, L is the length of the convolutional kernel, n represents the scanning position of the convolutional sliding operation, i denotes the step index of the sliding process, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{a}}$$\end{document} stands for the wavelet scale parameter.

After wavelet functions are introduced into convolution operations, their scale parameters and kernel lengths become two key parameters affecting the feature extraction effect. A quantitative relationship between the two is established to achieve collaborative optimization, and the matching criterion is derived based on the energy concentration law of wavelet functions. The energy of wavelet functions is highly concentrated in the time domain and frequency domain40. For a given mother wavelet, a time range containing most of its energy can be defined41 Drawing on the idea of 3σ interval quantization in statistics, this energy concentration range can be used as the theoretical basis for determining kernel lengths42.

To ensure that signals within this energy range are effectively captured by convolution operations, the length L of the convolutional kernel should be no less than the time-domain width of this range. Based on this, the following matching relationship is established.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L = 2\left\lceil {ca} \right\rceil + 1$$\end{document}where c is the energy concentration range coefficient of the mother wavelet, which is defined as the temporal half-width containing a specific proportion of energy, such as 99.7%43; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{a}}$$\end{document} denotes the wavelet scale; the coefficient 2 is used to cover the positive and negative symmetric portions of the energy interval; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\lceil \cdot \right\rceil$$\end{document} represents the ceiling operation; and the addition of 1 is performed to ensure that the length of the convolutional kernel is odd, thereby avoiding temporal shift.

This formula reveals the positive correlation between wavelet scale and convolutional kernel length, where larger scales correspond to low-frequency wide intervals and thus require long convolutional kernels for coverage, while smaller scales correspond to high-frequency narrow intervals and can be matched with short convolutional kernels. This quantitative relationship provides the theoretical basis and parameter initialization foundation for the subsequent construction of a global and local scale parameters collaboration optimization mechanism, enabling dynamic parameter adaptation and accurate multi-scale feature extraction.

In summary, the quantitative relationship established by this method has two core advantages: it is independent of wavelet type and sampling frequency, relying solely on the universal energy concentration property of wavelet functions. By adaptively adjusting the energy range coefficient for different wavelets and determining the convolution kernel length based on the time-domain energy width normalized by the sampling interval, this method can be directly applied to data with different sampling frequencies without requiring additional parameter tuning.

Scale adaptive design for global and local scale parameters collaboration

To achieve adaptive extraction of multi-scale fault features, an adaptive mechanism is proposed that simultaneously optimizes global and local scale parameters based on wavelet time–frequency resolution principles. In wavelet transform, there exists an inverse relationship between scale and frequency. Consequently, small scales correspond to high-frequency components, which require short convolutional kernels to enhance temporal resolution for accurately extracting transient impact features. Conversely, large scales correspond to low-frequency components, which necessitate long convolutional kernels to expand the receptive field for comprehensively capturing low-frequency periodic patterns. Through adaptive parameter adjustment, the convolutional kernels form a cooperative relationship where small scales are paired with short kernels and large scales with long kernels, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Design of square nonlinear mapping module

The squared nonlinear mapping module is designed to enhance the model’s ability to capture high-frequency feature components, particularly transient signals such as fault impacts, while optimizing the stability of the gradient update process. This module can reshape the feature energy distribution and suppress noise interference, while synergistically optimizing the scale and length parameters with the adaptive wavelet convolutional layer, enhancing the model’s feature discrimination ability in noisy environments and under few-shot conditions.

Optimization of convolutional kernel scale

The square nonlinear mapping optimizes model performance through the following dual mechanisms.

First, scale parameter adjustment is performed. The amplitude of the features is nonlinearly enhanced by the square transformation, where the energy enhancement for high-frequency fault features is significantly greater than that for low-frequency background signals. This asymmetric enhancement effect drives smaller scale parameters to be adaptively selected by the model, enabling local details of high-frequency faults to be more accurately captured by small-scale convolutional kernels. This mechanism is visually validated in Fig. 3. Before the square mapping, the convolutional kernel scales are generally larger, as illustrated by kernel_2 increasing from 60.3 to 76.3, showing a clear trend toward larger scales. After the mapping, the scales are significantly reduced, as seen with kernel_2 converging to 24.1, demonstrating stable focusing on fine-grained features. This comparative result directly confirms that the square transformation effectively drives the scaling direction of convolutional kernels.Fig. 3Comparison of training processes for convolutional kernel scales before and after square mapping, (a) before square mapping, (b) after square mapping.

Optimization of convolutional kernel length

The square nonlinear mapping module collaboratively optimizes convolutional kernel length parameters through the following mechanisms.

First, length parameter adjustment is performed. A correspondence between scale and receptive field is established by the module. When high-frequency features corresponding to small scales are processed, a smaller receptive field is required by the model to focus on local details. Therefore, short convolutional kernels are utilized to avoid the cross-region blurring effect that may be caused by long convolutional kernels. In contrast, when low-frequency features corresponding to large scales are handled, a larger receptive field is needed to integrate global information, for which long convolutional kernels are effectively employed to cover wide-bandwidth features. This adaptation effect is corroborated in Fig. 5. Before square mapping, the convolutional kernel lengths are generally larger. Taking kernel_2 as an example, its length converges from an initial value of 231.5 to 172.0. After mapping, the length is significantly shortened, with kernel_2 ultimately converging to 161.0. This trend of kernel shortening effectively enhances the model’s ability to capture high-frequency fault features.Fig. 5Comparison of training processes for convolutional kernel lengths before and after square mapping, (a) before square mapping, (b) after square mapping.

In regions with high-frequency fault features, the gradient update is accelerated, driving the length parameters to be adaptively shortened to meet the requirements of small-scale analysis. In low-frequency background regions, the gradient update remains gentle, allowing the length parameters to be kept stable and ensuring the integration capability for global features. This ensures accurate preservation and adaptive optimization of fault features in the time–frequency domain. The main advantages of this architecture are reflected in three aspects. First, the feature transmission path is effectively streamlined to reduce information loss. While mechanisms such as wavelet scale-length collaborative optimization and global–local parameter adaptation are preserved, the capability for adaptive adjustment of time–frequency resolution is maintained. Second, significant lightweight efficiency is achieved through a non-redundant single-layer architecture, enabling it to meet the stringent requirements for real-time fault diagnosis on edge devices. Finally, all modules work in coordination to form a complete chain from parameter optimization and feature fidelity to lightweight deployment.

End-to-end workflow and summary

To address the challenges outlined in “Introduction” Section, namely, weak fault features obscured by strong noise, generalization from extremely limited samples, and the demands for lightweight deployment, the ADPCW-ELCNN model integrates the aforementioned modules into a cohesive, end-to-end diagnostic pipeline. The workflow begins with the input of 1D vibration signals. The adaptive dual-parameter wavelet convolutional layer, through its quantitative scale-kernel correlation and global–local adaptive strategy, performs physics-informed, multi-scale feature extraction. This is followed by the square nonlinear mapping, which enhances discriminative fault signatures while suppressing noise, concurrently optimizing the gradient flow for scale and length parameters. The subsequent pooling operation compresses features while preserving their fidelity. Crucially, the entire process is encapsulated within a single-layer lightweight architecture. This streamlined design prevents the feature distortion common in deep networks under few-shot conditions and ensures computational efficiency suitable for edge devices. Thus, the model successfully unites physics-guided priors, collaborative parameter optimization, cascaded feature enhancement, and architectural compactness, forming a unified solution for robust and efficient fault diagnosis in challenging few-shot, high-noise scenarios.

Experiment

Data description

To validate the diagnostic performance under strong time-varying conditions with few-shot samples, two types of data covering complex scenarios with variable speeds and variable loads are selected, the bearing dataset from the University of Ottawa44 and the gearbox dataset from MCC5 Group and Tsinghua University (MCC5-THU)45.

Ottawa

This dataset is collected from an experimental platform for rolling bearings under strong time-varying speed conditions, where ER16K bearings are used. The research primarily focuses on the characterization of bearing fault features under dynamically varying speed conditions. The experimental design includes four typical dynamic speed scenarios, monotonic increase A0, monotonic decrease A1, increase then decrease A2, and decrease then increase A3. It also covers three bearing health states, Healthy (H), Inner Race Fault (IF), and Outer Race Fault (OF), forming a 4 × 3 combination of fault conditions, with sample labels corresponding to 0 to 2. The data sampling frequency is 200 kHz.

To evaluate diagnostic performance under few-shot constraints, the number of labeled samples per class, denoted as N, is set to 20, 40, 80, and 100, with the total training samples per class constrained to no more than 100. The number of fault classes K is fixed at three, corresponding to the three health states: Healthy, Inner Race Fault, and Outer Race Fault.

For each sample size N, the dataset is divided into training, validation, and test sets in a fixed 7:2:1 ratio, with no overlap between splits. To eliminate potential data dependence, samples from each operating run across all dynamic speed conditions and fault states are strictly separated among the three sets. This ensures that the model learns generalizable fault features instead of memorizing run-specific patterns. The model is trained in a standard supervised manner on the training set, validated on the validation set for hyperparameter tuning, and its final generalization performance is evaluated on the held-out test set. This process is repeated across all four sample sizes to comprehensively assess the model’s few-shot learning capability. Evaluation indicators

The standard deviations are provided to indicate performance stability. Additionally, the model exhibits low sensitivity to key hyperparameters including the number of convolution kernels, initial learning rate and batch size. When these parameters are adjusted within reasonable ranges 6–10 for the number of convolution kernels, 0.005–0.02 for the initial learning rate, 32–128 for the batch size, the variation in diagnostic performance including Accuracy and F1 Score is within ± 1.5%, indicating robust adaptability to hyperparameter tuning and facilitating reproducibility of the experimental results.

Analysis of feature extraction mechanism

Feature evolution mechanism

In few-shot strong-noise scenarios, fault features are often masked by background noise, making it a diagnostic challenge to achieve the precise balance between noise suppression and feature enhancement. Based on this, A0-IF fault samples with an SNR of − 10 dB from the Ottawa dataset are selected. By tracking the time–frequency feature changes of the signal after passing through the adaptive wavelet convolutional layer, the square layer, and the pooling layer, the transformation patterns of each module on fault features are quantitatively analyzed.

To quantitatively evaluate feature extraction performance, three metrics are introduced. Fault-energy ratio denotes the percentage of energy at the 67.9 Hz main frequency and its sidebands relative to total energy. Noise suppression rate reflects the attenuation of high-frequency noise energy above 300 Hz versus the original signal. Feature sharpness is measured by the standard deviation of energy distribution in the fault frequency region, with larger values indicating more concentrated features.

Feature sharpness is significantly improved, clearly highlighting the fault frequency components. Architecture fidelity mechanism

Under few-shot conditions, due to the inherently limited data features, increasing the number of convolutional layers can lead to attenuation or distortion of key features during transmission, potentially introducing pseudo-features unrelated to actual faults. To explore the impact of network depth on feature fidelity, two deeper variant models were constructed, namely a single-layer plus one convolutional layer and a single-layer plus two convolutional layers, based on the ADPCW-ELCNN architecture. By analyzing the feature transmission process focusing on the critical fault frequency of 42.9 Hz on the Ottawa few-shot subset, a quantitative evaluation was conducted from three key dimensions: the feature transmission loss rate, reflecting the retention level of key features; the feature distortion degree, measuring the deviation of features from the actual fault morphology; and the non-fault feature occurrence rate, indicating the extent of pseudo-features introduced by the model.

From the time–frequency feature distribution in Fig. 29, it was observed that the single-layer convolutional structure maintained the key frequency component of 42.9 Hz relatively well. In contrast, the single-layer plus one convolutional layer structure exhibited a noticeable loss of this feature, accompanied by the emergence of a pseudo-feature at 52.6 Hz. The single-layer plus two convolutional layer structure further displayed feature blurring and enhanced energy of pseudo-features. Experimental results indicate that under few-shot conditions, increasing network depth leads to issues such as loss of key features, aggravated feature distortion, and increased introduction of pseudo-features. The single-layer convolutional design adopted in ADPCW-ELCNN effectively avoids defect accumulation in multi-layer iterations through a streamlined architecture, achieving a good balance between feature extraction and overfitting control. This provides important insight for few-shot fault diagnosis tasks, suggesting that under limited data conditions, priority should be given to ensuring the fidelity of feature transmission rather than indiscriminately increasing network depth.

While the single-layer architecture effectively avoids feature distortion and pseudo-feature introduction under few-shot conditions, it still presents certain limitations in handling complex fault characteristics. For slow-developing incipient faults such as gradual gear wear, it lacks sufficient hierarchical feature abstraction to capture weak cumulative signals. In industrial scenarios with multi-source interference such as adjacent equipment electromagnetic interference and pipeline vibration coupling, its limited feature processing capability may insufficiently suppress non-target interference, reducing feature transmission fidelity. This indicates the architecture’s advantage is conditional on relatively pure signal environments.

Feature-enhanced few-shot generalization mechanism

The core contradiction of few-shot fault diagnosis is that the limited labeled samples lead to incomplete learning of the fault feature distribution, making the model prone to overfitting to local noise and individual characteristics of training samples, and thus the generalization ability on unseen test samples is severely degraded. The fault features extracted by ADPCW-ELCNN break through this bottleneck by constructing a physically interpretable, full-frequency covered, high-discriminative and fidelity-preserved feature representation. Based on the quantitative analysis and experimental results of feature evolution, parameter collaboration and architecture fidelity in “Feature evolution mechanism”–“Architecture fidelity mechanism”, this section reveals the intrinsic mechanism by which the extracted features enhance the few-shot generalization ability from four aspects, and forms a synergistic enhancement system for few-shot generalization.

Physics-informed feature prior eliminates overfitting caused by random feature learning

Different from the random initialization of convolution kernels in traditional CNN, the wavelet convolution kernel of ADPCW-ELCNN is initialized by the Mexican Hat wavelet, which embeds the time–frequency physical characteristics of rotating machinery fault signals (transient impact of early faults, periodic meshing frequency of gear/bearing) into the feature extraction process. The extracted features are not pure data-driven random features, but are guided by the physical prior of fault signals. Under few-shot conditions, the model can directly focus on the essential fault characteristics instead of memorizing the local noise and sample-specific irrelevant features of limited training samples, which fundamentally eliminates the randomness of feature learning in few-shot scenarios.

The ablation experiment in Table 4 has verified the necessity of this physical prior: removing the wavelet convolution kernel leads to a sharp drop in the F1-score to 16.17%, because the model loses the physical guidance of fault signals and can only learn superficial features from limited samples, resulting in serious overfitting. In contrast, the physics-informed feature prior makes the model capture the invariant fault essence behind the limited samples, which is the fundamental guarantee for the improvement of few-shot generalization ability.

Full-frequency adaptive feature coverage makes up for incomplete feature distribution of few samples

Few-shot samples can only cover a part of the fault feature distribution due to the small number, especially the multi-band fault features (high-frequency transient impact, low-frequency periodic vibration) are difficult to be fully represented. The dual-parameter collaborative optimization of wavelet scale and convolution kernel length (“Parameter collaboration mechanism” Section) enables the extracted features to realize adaptive coverage of full-frequency band fault information: small scales match short kernels to capture high-frequency transient impact features, and large scales match long kernels to extract low-frequency periodic features.

This full-frequency coverage characteristic makes up for the incompleteness of the feature distribution caused by few samples: even if the training samples only cover partial frequency band features of the fault, the model can adaptively extend to the full frequency band through the dynamic matching of scale and kernel length, and thus accurately identify the unseen test samples with different frequency band fault features. The parameter correlation analysis in “Parameter collaboration mechanism” Section shows that the wavelet scale and kernel length have a significant positive correlation (Pearson coefficient = 0.79), and the dual-parameter collaboration can capture multi-band features such as 42.9 Hz, 67.9 Hz and their harmonics (Fig. 28). This full-frequency feature coverage ensures that the model can learn a more comprehensive fault feature space with limited samples, and effectively improves the generalization ability on unseen samples.

High-discriminative feature reduces classification ambiguity in few-shot scenarios

Under few-shot conditions, insufficient sample learning aggravates the overlap of fault feature distributions and leads to serious classification ambiguity. The square nonlinear mapping module in ADPCW-ELCNN achieves asymmetric enhancement of fault features and efficient suppression of background noise as detailed in “Feature evolution mechanism” Section. Specifically, the fault-energy ratio increases from 3.1 percent at the wavelet convolution layer to 11.0 percent at the square layer, and the noise suppression rate reaches 77.6 percent. This nonlinear enhancement significantly improves the discriminability of the extracted features by sharply concentrating the energy of fault core features and minimizing the interference of noise on feature representation.

These highly discriminative features make the feature boundaries between different fault types such as healthy, inner race fault, outer race fault, GP, MT, and TB more distinct in the feature space. As visualized by t-SNE in Fig. 10, the features extracted by ADPCW-ELCNN form clearly separated and non-overlapping clusters, whereas comparative models with fixed scale or fixed kernel length show severe feature overlap. In few-shot scenarios, this clear feature distinguishability allows the model to accurately classify unseen test samples even with limited training samples. As shown in Figs. 20 and 21, the F1-score remains above 94 percent under the condition of 20 samples per class and a signal-to-noise ratio of minus 10 decibels, which fully verifies the effectiveness of highly discriminative features in improving few-shot generalization ability.

Fidelity-preserved feature transmission avoids pseudo-feature fitting in few-shot conditions

Under few-shot conditions, the model becomes extremely sensitive to pseudo-features. A multi-layer convolutional structure tends to introduce pseudo-features unrelated to actual faults during feature transmission, as discussed in “Architecture fidelity mechanism” Section. With limited samples, there is insufficient information to correct such fitting errors, leading to severe overfitting. In contrast, the single-layer lightweight architecture of ADPCW-ELCNN ensures complete fidelity in fault feature transmission. The feature transmission loss rate remains at only 15.85 percent, and the occurrence rate of non-fault features is zero, as shown in Table 8. This allows the extracted features to remain pure and authentic, entirely free from pseudo-features, thereby preventing the model from fitting irrelevant patterns based on limited data.

Deepened architectures, such as adding one or two convolutional layers, sharply increase the feature transmission loss rate to 56.61 percent and 68.29 percent respectively, while the non-fault feature occurrence rate rises to 2.6 percent and 7.5 percent, as presented in Table 8. Under few-shot conditions, the model is forced to fit these pseudo-features, resulting in a sharp decline in average accuracy to 72.51 percent and 33.33 percent, respectively. This comparison fully demonstrates that feature transmission fidelity is key to avoiding overfitting in few-shot scenarios. By preserving pure fault features, the single-layer architecture ensures that the model consistently learns genuine fault information, thereby maintaining strong generalization ability on unseen samples.

The four attributes of the extracted features, namely physics-informed prior, full-frequency coverage, high discriminability, and fidelity preservation, do not operate in isolation. Instead, they form a synergistic enhancement mechanism that supports few-shot generalization. The physical prior serves as the foundation to eliminate random feature learning, full-frequency coverage compensates for the incomplete feature distribution caused by limited samples, high discriminability reduces classification ambiguity in few-shot scenarios, and feature fidelity prevents the fitting of pseudo-features due to sample scarcity. This synergistic mechanism fundamentally addresses the core challenges of few-shot fault diagnosis. It enables the extracted features to maintain strong generalization capability on unseen test samples even under severely limited labeled data conditions.

The four-attribute synergistic enhancement mechanism physics-informed prior, full-frequency coverage, high discriminability and fidelity preservation performs well on single-fault datasets but faces diverse fault adaptation challenges. For faults with weak impact and strong periodicity such as shaft misalignment, the Mexican Hat wavelet-based prior cannot effectively match characteristics, leading to incomplete full-frequency coverage. Additionally, lack of multi-source information fusion including acoustic emission and current signals makes feature representation overly dependent on vibration signals, which are easily masked in heavy-duty conditions, limiting generalization in scenarios requiring multi-modality support.

Conclusion

This study’s core contribution lies in establishing a novel physics-informed parameter collaborative feature-faithful paradigm for few-shot fault diagnoses, offering a new theoretical perspective to address the engineering bottlenecks of few-shot, strong noise, and difficult deployment. To tackle the core challenges of inadequate feature extraction, weak generalization, and fault feature masking under strong noise in rotating machinery few-shot fault diagnosis, this paper proposes the ADPCW-ELCNN model. By establishing a quantitative relationship between wavelet scale and convolutional kernel length, it overcomes the disconnect between parameter adaptation and physical feature extraction in traditional models. The synergistic design of a cascaded feature enhancement path and single-layer lightweight architecture enhances weak fault feature identification while achieving efficient compression, meeting edge device deployment requirements. Under strong noise and extreme few-shot conditions, the model maintains excellent diagnostic performance, significantly outperforming existing comparative models. Unlike W-CNN’s independent parameter tuning and WKN’s deep structure, it realizes dual-parameter collaborative optimization and feature fidelity via a single-layer architecture, fundamentally resolving the tradeoff between feature extraction efficiency and deployment feasibility in few-shot scenarios.

The model’s strong robustness across a wide SNR range of − 10 dB to 10 dB provides theoretical justification for its ability to handle sensor noise variability and slight installation deviations in real industrial scenarios. The deeply integrated diagnostic paradigm of physical priors and data-driven constructed by the model not only effectively addresses the core bottleneck of few-shot fault diagnosis in high-noise environments but also provides a technical solution for the field of intelligent diagnosis that balances interpretability and deployability, offering important insights for reliable diagnosis in edge computing scenarios.

Notably, the current model has limitations. Experimental validation relies on controlled public datasets Ottawa and MCC5-THU, lacking verification under complex industrial conditions such as mixed faults, extreme temperatures and sensor aging. Cross-site and cross-device generalization remains untested. It depends on predefined Mexican Hat wavelets without adaptive selection for diverse faults. Performance under extreme transients such as abrupt speed and load changes needs verification.

Future research will focus on three directions: collecting real industrial data for complex conditions, developing lightweight on-site calibration techniques, and exploring adaptive wavelet selection; conducting cross-scenario transfer learning to enhance adaptability to different equipment and environments; expanding to mixed-fault diagnosis and improving concurrent fault distinguishability for comprehensive equipment health management support. Specifically, we will validate the model on real-world industrial platforms involving turbines, compressors, and other rotating machinery, covering variable operating conditions such as load shocks, temperature drifts, and multi-source interference to further verify its engineering applicability.
