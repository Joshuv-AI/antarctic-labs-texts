Harnessing AI and chatbots to develop an interactive learning activity: student perceptions and outcomes
Authors: Usean J. Redic, Deavin F. Tendean, Andrea C. Nicholas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArtificial intelligence (AI) has transformed various industries, and education is no exception. This paper details how AI can be utilized to support the development of an interactive, choose-your-own-adventure learning activity for pre-health students. With ChatGPT assistance, we identified and implemented the open-source platform Twine to create a branching, scenario-based activity focused on sexually transmitted diseases (STDs), allowing students to engage with clinical decision-making and apply course concepts. Additionally, ChatGPT’s natural language processing capabilities allowed educators to create a script-based learning experience informed by prior student feedback, providing students with immediate, targeted feedback and support. This study describes how AI was used to develop a novel learning activity that encourages student engagement and application of course concepts. Students who piloted this activity in an upper-division course reported the activity to be an effective and engaging learning experience that helped them apply course content to real-life scenarios. Importantly, the use of AI helped streamline the development of this activity, lowering barriers to creating complex educational activities that may otherwise be difficult to implement. Here, we demonstrate how AI can be leveraged to support the development of interactive, scenario-based learning activities and provide practical guidance for instructors.

INTRODUCTION

To manifest a novel and engaging activity that could teach pre-health undergraduate students about bacterial sexually transmitted diseases (bSTDs), we turned to artificial intelligence (AI) and found it to be a valuable education tool. ChatGPT is a chatbot developed by OpenAI that utilizes artificial intelligence (AI) machine learning to understand and generate responses to user input. Specifically, ChatGPT is based on the Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) architecture, which is a type of deep learning model that uses unsupervised machine learning to process large amounts of text data and learn patterns and relationships between words and sentences (OpenAI).

Like many AI platforms, ChatGPT can respond to prompts and give answers. This can be extremely useful in many educational contexts. However, it also opens possibilities for cheating, plagiarism, and other academically dishonest exploits (1). This also highlights the importance of designing learning activities that encourage student engagement and decision-making in ways that are less easily completed using generative AI tools. As a search tool, ChatGPT may produce responses that appear accurate but require verification as it does not inherently validate sources or ensure the accuracy of generated information (2). Because ChatGPT generates responses based on patterns and relationships learned from large data sets, it can produce high-quality sounding outputs that may be inaccurate or unreliable. This occurs because the model does not perform independent fact-checking or source validation prior to generating responses. Furthermore, at the time of use, it was not designed to consistently provide accurate citations or references for the information generated.

While ChatGPT may have limitations in content accuracy, relevance, and citation, it has proved to be a useful tool when the user takes necessary steps to ensure the quality and credibility of the information it generates. One way to do this is by using ChatGPT in the development of novel approaches rather than relying on its generated content. By using ChatGPT to assist in developing ideas using keywords related to a topic, the educator may uncover new ways to solve problems, thus facilitating creativity. AI can generate unique ideas about how to best teach certain topics given adequate keywords and phrases related to the content that the instructor wants to teach. In this instance, authors utilized keywords and concepts grounded in discipline-based education research to effectively craft prompts to elicit useful responses from the AI model about how to develop engaging activities that help humans learn and consolidate course content. Employing this approach, ChatGPT, ultimately provided a noteworthy suggestion: the development of a choose-your-own-adventure game.

After being prompted, ChatGPT also gave options on how to go about creating this activity. It suggested using a free open-source tool, Twine, to build interactive, nonlinear storytelling experiences in which users navigate branching pathways based on their choices (Fig. 1). Twine allows users to create text-based games, choose-your-own-adventure narratives, and interactive fiction by using a simple visual interface and a combination of hyperlinks and passages. ChatGPT was utilized to provide user support and guidance on how to build the activity within the Twine platform. Twine has gained popularity among writers, game developers, and educators for its user-friendly interface that eliminates the need for traditional coding skills and extensive programming knowledge. This makes the process of creating and connecting user-generated text passages to develop interactive educational stories straightforward. Twine can, thus, be harnessed as an interactive learning tool that is ideal for biology and pre-health education (3).

(A) Example of pathway divergence within the activity, where student choices influence progression and determine which subsequent passages are accessible. (B) Twine storyboard demonstrating the branching structure of the activity, allowing students to navigate between interconnected passages based on their decisions.

Following development, our make-your-own-adventure bSTD activity game was launched to third-year undergraduate students enrolled in an upper-division course on infectious disease and epidemiology. Students were asked to provide survey feedback on their experiences, and content learning outcomes were assessed. The following paper discusses the development of our first AI-assisted learning activity from conception to assessment. While this process required substantial human effort in areas such as prompt development, storytelling, and activity design, the use of AI helped streamline key aspects of development, making the creation of a complex interactive learning activity more practical.

METHODS

Course context

The course was an upper-division, undergraduate, pre-health course developed to provide an in-depth look at the most common infectious diseases as they relate to global issues of epidemiology, providing students with a deeper understanding of human health. The AI-assisted activity was designed to reinforce content for the bSTD portion of the course focusing on the incubation, latency, diagnosis, treatment, mechanism, and epidemiology of chlamydia, syphilis, trachoma, pelvic inflammatory disease, and gonorrhea.

Preliminary survey about course activities

Prior to developing the discussed activity, the authors gathered anecdotal feedback from students through discussion about “past” active learning group work to elicit their feedback about traditional classroom activities and to identify potential issues. Student responses reflected a shared sentiment: that although traditional in-class group activities were seen as beneficial, there was room for improvement in preparing students for the application of learned content for their medical case-based essay exams. Students expressed the desire for a greater emphasis on more engaged critical thinking activities, as opposed to straightforward rubric-based written activities.

Inception of the game’s creation

The process of prompting is vital for ChatGPT to generate creative and informative responses. The quality of the responses from ChatGPT depends on the clarity and specificity of the prompt. One element of prompting is developing the context setting: This is when the chat is provided with a prompt that helps set the context for the model. By framing the question or topic, the author guided ChatGPT’s understanding of what they were looking for to best generate relevant and appropriate responses. For instance, when creating the prompt for this activity, the individual class structure was taken into account (three class sections with eight groups per section, with approximately 6 to 7 students per group), the level of education of the students (3rd and 4th year upper-division biology students), the amount of time provided to complete the activity (50 min), and the topic of the activity (bSTDs).

By generating responses based on patterns and relationships learned from vast amounts of text data, ChatGPT was able to suggest creative and innovative approaches to teaching that the course instructor had not previously considered. This led to the suggestion of building a choose-your-own-adventure game.

Example prompting included requests such as the following: “How can I go about creating an interact activity for 3rd year biology students centred around STDs? Students have expressed a desire to engage in more critical thinking, engage in activities more relevant to exams, and for it to feel less like a ‘task’. Crosswords, Case-Studies, Q&A, and Bingo are examples of activities that students engage in-I would like for this to be fundamentally different from those.”

STD activity content

The activity involved virtual role-playing as either a chromosomal male or female character with varying behavioral, demographic, or physiological risk factors for contracting an STD. The game detailed the risks, presenting symptoms, latency periods, clinical diagnosis, and treatment options for a variety of STDs, including gonorrhea, trachoma, chlamydia, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), and syphilis. In the game, students made decisions that influenced their characters’ sexual health but were also given an opportunity to make decisions in the role of infectious disease specialists about which lab tests to run and treatments to prescribe based on all the given data. The objective was for the activity to foster critical thinking skills among students, empowering them to evaluate situations and choose the most appropriate course of action based on acquired information and game-based feedback.

The game required students to use prior knowledge from the instructor’s lectures, including risk percentages, mechanisms of disease infection, stages of infections, common symptoms, and standard treatments. Thus, students were allowed to access their lecture notes during the game. Furthermore, students were encouraged to play the game multiple times and to intentionally make some misinformed choices to access all the feedback and better understand the consequences of misdiagnosis and inadequate treatment of the STDs. Ultimately, the authors hoped this helped reinforce students’ content learning in an interesting way while removing the burden of having to “get it right.” Example prompting included requests such as the following: “Write a possible patient history for a female with iatrogenic pelvic inflammatory disease” and “make an EMT medical assessment scenario for a female with pelvic inflammatory disease.”

Building the game using Twine

Per the suggestion of ChatGPT, the entire activity was constructed using the online software Twine (https://twinery.org/). Twine’s user-friendly interface eliminates the need for coding skills, making the process of creating and connecting user-created text passages straightforward. However, the passages can be further enhanced with variables, images, CSS, and JavaScript, which do require prior coding knowledge. We did not elect to enhance passages through coding in this implementation. However, generative AI tools such as ChatGPT can assist users in implementing web-based coding languages (e.g., HTML, CSS, and JavaScript) for those who wish to incorporate more advanced features.

The game was built by connecting content passages using text-based hyperlinks (multiple website pages connected to one another). In essence, multiple passages or “blocks” were linked together by converting text within each block into hyperlinks that connected to other passages. This process allowed for the creation of a choose-your-own-adventure activity with multiple endings that were determined by the students’ decisions. The hyperlink, presented as an action rather than a URL (Fig. 1A), led to a new page for students to continue the story, allowing them to explore the consequences of their choices for the patient or physician.

Given the complex nature of this choose-your-own adventure activity and the constraints of a small team consisting of one faculty member and two students with competing academic responsibilities, development without AI assistance would likely have required substantially more time and resources. Within a 10-week academic quarter, the use of AI enabled completion of the activity within approximately 6 weeks, with the final product prepared only shortly before implementation. Without this assistance, it is unlikely that the activity would have been completed in time for use within the course. The use of AI in conjunction with accessible platforms such as Twine helped lower technical barriers, making the development of interactive learning activities more feasible.

Creating the story narrative and fact-checking

The narrative for the activity storyline was developed using lecture materials on infectious disease, input from ChatGPT, internet research for error-checking, and imaginative writing by the authors. ChatGPT played a significant role in generating dialog, treatment plans, and possible scenarios related to the contraction of specific STDs, among other story elements. The AI-generated information was then aligned with course content to construct the story (4–7).

Most of the time spent on the narrative creation was dedicated to developing and refining the storyline, which underwent beta-testing (testing and running the game before release), analysis, and critique by a research assistant who was not involved in the game’s development and the instructor. When creating an activity of this magnitude, the potential for wording errors, coding errors, and other unnoticed mistakes is inevitable, requiring multiple iterations to ensure a smooth experience for students. The activity was reviewed throughout its development, specifically checking for errors in the Twine code and within the gameplay. Thus, any overlooked errors or areas that could be improved were provided through feedback to the primary game developer to ensure necessary edits were made.

Given ChatGPT’s occasional inaccuracies, a meticulous review of various authentic content sources like websites, lecture materials, journal papers, and books was necessary to ensure consistency of information (8, 9). All content information provided by ChatGPT was cross-checked with reliable academic sources, including scientific journals, lecture materials, and textbooks, to ensure accuracy and validity.

In-game points system

As previously stated, students made decisions that influenced their character’s sexual health, which was tracked using an in-game point system. Implementing a point system to serve as the patient’s quantitative health indicator throughout gameplay was another suggestion that ChatGPT made to our team. We wanted to encourage exploration rather than penalize students for incorrect answers. The backend, representing the game’s code and computations, tracked decisions, assigning predetermined positive or negative point changes based on individual storyline decisions made by the player. To prevent students from selecting options they could not afford, choices requiring points were carefully managed throughout gameplay. In this context, points served both as a representation of the patient’s health status and as a constraint on available decisions, requiring students to consider the consequences of their choices before proceeding. Sections diverged from their original path after such decisions (Fig. 1B), ensuring students could not access deleterious choices unless intentionally reconnected by the activity creator. While incorrect answers resulted in point drops, the game did not end; instead, restricting certain decisions maintained engagement and encouraged more thoughtful exploration. The patient’s condition may have worsened, or the treatment provided may have had no effect, prompting students to reflect on why that outcome occurred. Students were given additional opportunities to make new treatment decisions rather than simply repeating previous choices. Furthermore, when replaying the activity, students could apply knowledge gained from earlier attempts to make more informed decisions and observe different patient outcomes.

The game’s point system would increase or decrease student scores based on whether their choices improved or worsened their patient’s outcome. Accumulated points would unlock additional choices. Students would receive immediate alerts within the game when their patient character or the patient’s doctor (both under their control) made helpful or unhelpful choices. For example, in one scenario, students prescribed medication based on observed symptoms and tests. They had two options, with an alert appearing if the incorrect choice was made, indicating symptoms were not alleviated. Students could also gauge their progress by monitoring their points, allowing them to self-assess whether they were making informed decisions as it related to course content and their patient’s subsequent health status.

Launching the activity

On the day of the activity, students were seated in groups at active learning tables using their own technology. Students accessed the game from a shared PowerPoint activity slide that contained a link to the STD activity. The activity itself was hosted on the itch.io website, an online platform dedicated to showcasing and distributing indie games (https://uredi20.itch.io/stdn120b). Twine allows the user to create items as a webpage, and it is saved as an HTML file. However, for the file to be played by others, it needs to be accessible on a sharing website such as itch.io. Once on the website, students could access the activity through their browser and play through it multiple times to see how different choices and scenarios influenced patient outcomes. Students were encouraged to collaborate with others and share their decisions and outcomes with their group members.

The activity was conducted with students from three class sections, each comprising approximately 40 to 50 students. They engaged in the activity for a duration of 35 min, after which they transitioned back to the PowerPoint presentation. Assigned student work groups were presented with a post-activity challenge case study to solve. For example, one group was given the following scenario:

“A woman presents to the clinic with lower abdominal pain and fever. She reports having unprotected sex with multiple partners. The doctor suspects she may have pelvic inflammatory disease (PID). What are some common risk factors for PID?”

Answer: Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria Gonorrhoeae bacterial infections are the most common causes of PID.

Leveraging the knowledge from the lecture and the information gathered during the game, the students were expected to successfully complete the short-written case study homework questions. Subsequently, they submitted their answers to the course instructor for credit. The homework responses were resoundingly accurate, but again, this was expected since students were able to reference learned material while answering. Students were asked to complete a survey about their gameplay experience following the in-class activity. A post-content quiz was given at the end of the week to reinforce and test student comprehension.

RESULTS

In order to gauge the effectiveness of this activity, students were asked to complete a feedback survey; there were 121 responses. One of the key findings was that the majority of students enjoyed the activity and also found the activity to reinforce their learning (Fig. 2). Students were asked to rate their perceptions of the activity using a scale of 1–10, where 1 represented the lowest rating and 10 represented the highest rating. These questions assessed student-reported enjoyment of the activity, perceived reinforcement of learning, and level of interest in the topic. These measures reflect subjective student perceptions rather than objective measures of learning outcomes.

Students were asked to rate their perceptions of the activity (e.g., enjoyment, perceived reinforcement of learning, and interest in the topic of sexually transmitted diseases [STDs]) on a scale of 1–10, where 1 represented the lowest rating and 10 represented the highest rating.

Of the 121 responses, 90.9% (110 students) rated this activity as >8/10 when asked if they liked the activity. Furthermore, 84.3% (102 students) rated this activity as >8/10 when asked if it reinforced their learning. In addition, 64.3% (76 students) rated this activity >8/10 when asked if the activity increased their interest in the topic of STDs.

Students were also given a closed-note post-activity quiz shortly after the conclusion of the activity to assess their knowledge on the STD curriculum. Students were asked to choose the correct diseases based on the information given for eight content questions, and the results showed that students performed well on the post-activity quiz, with most scoring 90% or higher (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

The student feedback in the survey suggests exciting possibilities for integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into the classroom. The approach described here empowers educators to not only enhance existing curricula but also explore innovative ways to create new educational content. AI tools like ChatGPT, when used appropriately, can inspire new ideas, assist in the use of new platforms, and identify activities that a student cannot upload into AI to complete. In this way, AI has the potential to become a supportive tool for educators.

While the results of this study suggest that students responded positively to the activity and performed well on the post-activity assessment, there are important limitations to consider. The post-activity quiz was administered without a pretest or control group, and therefore it is difficult to determine the extent to which student performance can be directly attributed to the activity itself. Additionally, without a baseline measure of student knowledge or comparison to previous cohorts, the observed high scores may reflect factors beyond the intervention, such as prior understanding of the material. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive of student performance within this context rather than as evidence of causal improvement in learning outcomes.

It is crucial to emphasize that the use of AI in education, particularly in the infectious disease class context, was intended as an active learning supplement to the educator’s content lecture. AI’s effectiveness as an activity development tool is dependent on the level of detail provided in the generative prompts. By providing specific keywords and phrases related to the subject, the user can help guide ChatGPT to generate more relevant and useful responses. This can include providing information on the grade level, subject area, and learning objectives of the class, as well as any relevant curriculum or standards. Educators can provide ample information about the class, curriculum, and specific tasks they want the AI to perform. However, the responsibility lies with the educator as the expert who must vet any content information generated by AI. Additionally, practical limitations associated with generative AI tools, such as restrictions on file uploads, session limits, or access differences between free and paid versions, may impact how educators are able to utilize these systems during activity development. While many widely used generative AI platforms (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, and Google Gemini) offer accessible free versions, these constraints may still affect usability depending on the scope and complexity of the task.

The use of AI was undeniable in the inception and creation of the activity discussed in this paper. However, it is important to note that the information provided by chatbots is not always accurate (10). Thus, proofreading by the instructor and activity vetting by the development team are essential. In specific instances, ChatGPT recommended medical procedures for inclusion in the activity that were unrealistic for instruction purposes. Although the recommended procedures existed, it was necessary to consider additional factors that would determine how practical the suggested interventions were for the patients in the storyline. Additionally, since ChatGPT compiles responses based on extensive training data, there is a possibility of errors in its sources. It is known to create references that do not actually exist (11).

The software Twine, in which the game was developed, presented a few challenges during class implementation as the display is automatically tailored for larger screens, causing navigation difficulties for students using mobile devices. Although additional code could have addressed this issue, it was not completed prior to launching the activity in our classroom. Students without laptops were asked to partner with a fellow student in their group who had one to complete the activity.

While ChatGPT and Twine helped address various challenges in creating the activity, human involvement was essential for developing prompts, storytelling, proofreading, and designing. Crafting the theme and story of the activity demanded significant human input. Thus, development of these kinds of activities still requires substantial time and effort. However, AI reduces the development effort required for interactive educational activities in software programs that might have previously seemed daunting.

This study suggests that AI may be a useful tool to aid course instruction, alleviating some creative challenges and technology barriers to educational activity development. In a short timeframe and with a team of three, we found that the utilization of AI streamlined the development of a unique online interactive resource that would otherwise have not been considered possible. The student responses indicate that this activity was perceived as rewarding and beneficial to their learning experience.
