The dual role of exosomes in renal fibrosis and their potential for clinical translational applications
Authors: Rui Hu, Jingyu Wang, Ying Yang, Yutong Ma, Xiao Bai, Weihao Shang, Li Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal fibrosis is the core pathological process in the progression of chronic kidney disease to its end stage. There is to date no novel therapeutic strategies that are both safe and efficient in reversing renal fibrosis in humans. Exosomes, as key mediators of intercellular communication, play significant regulatory roles in renal fibrosis. Translating mechanistic studies on exosome-mediated promotion or inhibition of renal fibrosis into clinically applicable anti-fibrotic strategies remains a challenging issue in this field. In this review, we systematically elucidate the distinct dual role of exosomes, highlighting how they function as either pro-fibrotic drivers or anti-fibrotic protectors depending on their cellular origin. In particular, we examine how renal tubular epithelial cell-derived exosomes promote renal fibrosis through multiple mechanisms by delivering specific cargoes—including miRNAs (e.g., miR-21, miR-19b-3p), mRNA (TGF-β1), and proteins (OPN, TNFAIP8)—via signaling pathways such as PTEN/Akt, NF-κB, and HIF-1α. Furthermore, we discuss exosome-based therapeutic strategies, focusing on the anti-fibrotic potential of mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes and targeted engineering strategies, such as drug loading and surface modification. Finally, we also summarize the value of urinary and blood exosomes as biomarkers in the diagnosis of renal fibrosis. In conclusion, by decoding the dual nature of exosomes, we offer a comprehensive framework to accelerate the clinical translation of these nanovesicles into precise diagnostics and targeted therapeutics.

Introduction

Currently, the global burden of chronic kidney disease (CKD) has shown a concerning increasing trend, making it one of the top ten causes of death worldwide [1]. Renal fibrosis is the common pathological pathway of CKD progressing to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). It is characterized by the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), leading to the destruction of renal structure and the irreversible loss of kidney function [2–4]. Despite the severe clinical impact of CKD, effective methods that can specifically reverse or halt the progression of renal fibrosis remain rare [5–7]. Therefore, a thorough elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying renal fibrosis, along with the subsequent development of novel targeted intervention strategies, holds great clinical significance.

Recently, exosomes have emerged as a highly promising focus for both the diagnosis and treatment of renal fibrosis. As a type of nanoscale extracellular vesicle (typically <200 nm in diameter) originating from endosomes [8,9], the morphology of exosomes can adaptively change in response to their microenvironment [10]. Their membranes are enriched with tetraspanin family proteins (e.g., CD9, CD63, and CD81) [11] and proteins involved in biogenesis (e.g., Alix, TSG101), which serve as key identification markers [9]. Exosomes encapsulate a diverse ‘cargo’ of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (such as mRNA, miRNA, and circRNA) [2,12]. By delivering these molecules to recipient cells via direct fusion, endocytosis, or receptor-mediated uptake, exosomes act as crucial mediators of intercellular communication in both physiological and pathological processes (Figure 1) [3,13,14].

In the specific context of renal fibrosis, exosomes appear to play a paradoxical dual role. Following kidney injury, exosomes released from injured renal resident cells (e.g., tubular epithelial cells and macrophages) often deliver pro-fibrotic signals, thereby accelerating disease progression [2,15]. In contrast, stem cell-derived exosomes hold immense therapeutic promise due to their potent anti-fibrotic and tissue-reparative functions [12,16]. Furthermore, exosomes in body fluids, especially urine, are emerging as novel ‘liquid biopsy’ tools for the noninvasive diagnosis and monitoring of renal fibrosis, as their molecular profiles directly reflect the pathological status of the kidney [17,18]. Notably, during the course of CKD, both the size and the total number of secreted exosomes exhibit significant dynamic variations [19].

This article aims to systematically review the dual regulatory roles of endogenous exosomes in renal fibrosis, thoroughly discuss the latest advances and optimization directions of exosomes as novel therapeutic strategies, and summarize their clinical application potential as biomarkers, which may provide new theoretical foundations for the prevention and treatment of renal fibrosis.

The dual role of endogenous exosomes in renal fibrosis

Endogenous exosomes as drivers of renal fibrosis

Following persistent injury (e.g., ischemia, hyperglycemia, toxins, and inflammation), various renal resident cells and infiltrating inflammatory cells release exosomes carrying pro-fibrotic signals. These exosomes activate fibroblasts, promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), exacerbate inflammatory responses, and remodel the local microenvironment, forming a ‘Fibrogenic Niche’. These promotes continuous fibrotic development [4] and play a central driving role in the initiation and progression of renal fibrosis. Renal tubular epithelial cell-derived exosomes and fibrosis progression

Due to their high energy metabolism, renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs) are both early responders and primary targets in kidney injury. The exosomes they secrete activate fibroblasts and induce extracellular matrix deposition by delivering pro-fibrotic molecules to interstitial fibroblasts, thereby promoting renal fibrosis [15]. Under pathological stimuli including ischemia-reperfusion [20], high glucose [21], proteinuria [22], or TGF-β1 [20], TECs release more exosomes with an elevated and altered composition. These exosomes not only propagate injury signals to surrounding normal TECs [21], but also multi-dimensionally regulate fibroblast behavior by directly activating fibroblasts, promoting their proliferation [20], remodeling the extracellular microenvironment [20,21], and modulating immune cells such as macrophages [22], collectively driving the fibrotic process.

It has been reported that exosomes released by BSA-induced injured human renal tubular epithelial cells (HK2) can be taken up by normal homologous cells, thereby inducing the expression of fibrosis-related markers such as kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1). Using the exosome secretion inhibitor GW4869 could block this effect. Moreover, reinfusing these pathological exosomes into db/db mice, a model of diabetic kidney disease (DKD), directly exacerbated tubular injury and interstitial fibrosis [23]. These findings indicate that tubular epithelial cell-derived exosomes serve as key mediators in the self-amplification of injury and fibrosis within the tubular region.

In addition, TEC-derived exosomes can directly activate renal interstitial fibroblasts through their cargo, demonstrating their potent pro-fibrotic capability [20,24]. First, they can deliver mRNAs encoding profibrotic factors such as TGF-β1, which subsequently trigger the activation of fibroblasts [24]. Second, TEC exosomes can deliver various pro-fibrotic miRNAs, such as miR-21 (targeting the PTEN/Akt pathway) [25], miR-150-5p (inhibiting suppressor of cytokine signaling 1, SOCS1) [26,27], miR-330-3p (inhibiting cAMP-responsive element-binding protein-binding protein, CREBBP) [28], and miR-196b-5p (inhibiting SOCS2 and enhancing STAT3 phosphorylation) [29], thereby activating fibroblasts through multiple pathways. Additionally, the transport and presentation of fibrotic proteins is equally crucial. For example, activation of the β-catenin signaling pathway in TECs prompts them to secrete exosomes carrying osteopontin (OPN), which promotes fibroblast proliferation and activation by binding OPN to CD44 on the fibroblast surface [30]. Besides promoting fibroblast activation, TEC exosomes also ‘maintain’ the activated fibroblast pool by inhibiting apoptosis. Studies have found that TECs under TGF-β1 stimulation release exosomes rich in tumor necrosis factor-α-induced protein 8 (TNFAIP8). When these exosomes are uptaken by fibroblasts, TNFAIP8 promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of the p53 protein, thereby inhibiting fibroblast apoptosis signals and enhancing their survival [31]. Furthermore, TEC exosomes can carry enzymes that modify the extracellular microenvironment. Transglutaminase-2 (TG2) is a Ca2+-dependent enzyme that enhances extracellular matrix protein cross-linking and TGF-β1 activation. Research has indicated that in a fibrotic environment, TECs secrete TG2 loaded into exosomes via a syndecan-4-dependent pathway [32]. These TG2-carrying exosomes released into the interstitium alter the balance of the ECM and promote fibrosis.

TEC-derived exosomes can also promote fibrosis indirectly by inducing phenotypic switching in other cells, most notably in macrophages.

In an adriamycin-induced CKD model, exosomal miR-19b-3p was shown to promote macrophage M1 polarization and sustained inflammation by inhibiting SOCS-1, a key negative regulator of the NF-κB pathway. Importantly, the clinical relevance of this mechanism was confirmed [33]. In DN animal models, proteinuria-stimulated TECs release miR-199a-5p-rich exosomes that, upon uptake by macrophages, induce M1 polarization by targeting and inhibiting Klotho [34]. Similarly, TEC-EVs deliver miR-34a to macrophages, where it targets and inhibits PPARGC1A expression, thereby driving M1 polarization [35]. The activated M1 macrophages create a pro‑inflammatory microenvironment by secreting TNF‑α and IL‑6 [33–35]. Moreover, co‑culture experiments directly demonstrated that these M1 macrophages induce the expression of α‑SMA, collagen I, and fibronectin in renal tubular epithelial cells, thereby promoting extracellular matrix deposition and tubular fibrosis [35]. Interestingly, the pro-fibrotic role is not solely attributed to pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages in the process of renal fibrosis. M2 macrophages with a reparative phenotype have also been demonstrated to be drivers. In Zhang et al.’s study, the CD206+ subset of M2 macrophages was shown to be closely associated with renal fibrosis [36]. M2 macrophages play a seemingly contradictory dual role in the kidney: they act as both stewards of injury repair and promoters of fibrosis. For example, in a mouse model of renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI)-induced renal fibrosis, studies have demonstrated that exosomes derived from ferroptotic TECs are significantly enriched with miR-1306-5p. Upon uptake by macrophages, these exosomes lead to an imbalance in the M1/M2 ratio, with M2 macrophages becoming predominant and secreting pro-fibrotic factors [37]. Similarly, exosomes rich in miR-20a and miR-21 secreted by senescent TECs can target Smad7, relieving the inhibition of Smad3 phosphorylation and thereby inducing macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition (MMT). These M2 macrophages then secrete pro-fibrotic factors, particularly TGF-β, which in turn upregulate the expression of miR-20a and miR-21 in macrophages. This creates a TGF-β-driven positive feedback loop that continuously amplifies fibrotic effects [38]. Moreover, in a DKD mouse model, exosomes derived from TECs promote macrophage glycolysis by stabilizing HIF-1α. Following this metabolic reprogramming, macrophages simultaneously upregulate the expression of IL1β and TGF-β1, driving inflammation and fibrosis [39]. These findings collectively indicate that TEC-derived exosomes can promote fibrosis by reprogramming macrophage biology at transcriptional, phenotypic, and metabolic levels.

Besides acting on fibroblasts and macrophages, TEC-derived exosomes also target glomerular resident cells, driving glomerulosclerosis. A study in a DN model revealed a clear pathway: under high glucose stimulation, TECs deliver miR-92a-1-5p to glomerular mesangial cells (GMCs) via exosomes. This miRNA triggers endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in MCs by downregulating genes essential for ER homeostasis, thereby inducing mesangial-myofibroblast transdifferentiation (MFT)-a process that directly contributes to glomerular fibrosis [40]. In summary, these evidences highlighted TEC-derived exosomes as central players of renal fibrosis, driving the progression of renal fibrosis through specific signaling pathways and intercellular communication. It suggests that targeting specific pathogenic exosomes or their key ‘cargo’ represents a promising strategy to disrupt this progression.

Macrophage-derived exosomes mediate bidirectional intercellular communication and the inflammation-fibrosis vicious cycle

Macrophages, as important immune cells in renal inflammation and fibrosis, secrete exosomes which mediate the crosstalk between inflammation and fibrosis [41,42]. Macrophage-derived exosomes deliver miR-155 to TECs, leading to telomere dysfunction and accelerated senescence in TECs by inhibiting TRF1. This reveals a novel mechanism whereby immune cells actively induce parenchymal cell injury and senescence via exosomes–-a key process in CKD progression and fibrosis [42]. Additionally, MMT is considered a significant source of myofibroblasts in renal fibrosis [38,43,44]. On one hand, exosomes released by senescent TECs can induce MMT by delivering miR-20a and miR-21, indicating that TECs can drive macrophages toward a pro-fibrotic phenotype via exosomes [38]. Conversely, myofibroblasts can also secrete exosomes that further promote MMT by activating the TBK1 signaling pathway in macrophages [44]. Simultaneously, communication between macrophages and TECs is bidirectional. In DN, lipotoxic injured TECs release leucine-rich α-2 glycoprotein 1 (LRG1)-enriched exosomes (EVe), activating macrophages toward a proinflammatory phenotype via TGFβR1. Subsequently, these activated macrophages release tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)-rich exosomes (EVm), which further induce apoptosis in the injured TECs by binding to Death Receptor 5 (DR5) on TECs. The resulting cycle of ‘TEC injury → EVe release → macrophage activation → EVm release → TEC apoptosis’ forms a feedback loop, serving as a potential driver for renal inflammation and fibrosis [45]. Interestingly, modulating the state of macrophages can alter the function of their exosomes. For instance, exosomes derived from Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells-2 (TREM-2) knockout macrophages, which exhibit an elevated MMP-9/TIMP-1 ratio via the HSPa1b/AKT pathway, promote ECM degradation and alleviate fibrosis when taken up by TECs, suggesting that modifying macrophage exosome cargo represents a promising anti-fibrotic strategy [41]. These findings indicate that macrophage-derived exosomes are not only carriers of inflammatory signals but also inducers of cellular phenotypic transformation, playing a central regulatory role in the inflammation-fibrosis axis.

Moreover, recent research has revealed an upstream mechanism in which sympathetic overactivation induces macrophages to release Notch2 intracellular domain (N2ICD)-enriched EVs. Following uptake by renal fibroblasts, N2ICD potentiates TGF-β signaling through Smad3 stabilization to promote fibrosis [46]. Blocking this pathway in animal models effectively mitigated fibrosis, establishing the ‘sympathetic nerve–macrophage–fibroblast’ axis as a driver and highlighting the promise of targeting pathogenic EV cargo like N2ICD.

Glomerular cell-derived exosomes and fibrosis progression

Exosomes from diverse glomerular cells are key drivers of renal fibrosis. Study indicates that podocytes injured by puromycin release EVs enriched with specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-149, −424), which, upon uptake by HK-2 cells, activate ERK/p38 signaling to induce tubulointerstitial fibrosis. This demonstrates that signals of glomerular injury can spread distally via exosome-mediated ‘Glomerulo-Tubular Cross-Talk’[47]. In DKD, the complex crosstalk among podocytes, glomerular endothelial cells (GECs), and MCs within the glomerulus is partly mediated by exosomes [48]. For example, exosomes rich in TGF-β1 mRNA secreted by GECs treated with high glucose can activate MCs by triggering the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway, promoting their proliferation and ECM production, thereby driving glomerulosclerosis [49,50]. The scope of tumor exosome research extends beyond intra-organ communication alone. It has expanded to inter-organ communication. New research indicated that injured skeletal muscle releases miR-21a-3p-enriched EVs into the circulation, which exacerbate kidney injury and fibrosis by targeting PPARGC1A in TECs, uncovering a muscle-kidney crosstalk in CKD progression [51]. These findings indicate that renal fibrosis is a multicellular, multi-organ process. In this process, exosomes acting as key communicative messengers. In summary, targeting exosome production and function may offer a more promising therapeutic strategy compared to targeting a single cell type.

The renal protective potential of endogenous exosomes

Although many endogenous exosomes promote fibrosis under pathological conditions, those from specific sources demonstrate inherent renal protective and anti-fibrotic potential. Renal progenitor cells, such as parietal epithelial cells (PECs), participate in kidney repair following injury. Exosomes secreted by PECs (PEC-Exo) home to injured kidneys and alleviate inflammation and tubular fibrosis [52]. Mechanistically, PEC-Exo are rich in let-7b-5p, which inhibits TGFβR1 and ARID3a in target cells, thereby restoring the cell cycle and promoting tubular repair [52]. Protective exosomes also contribute to inter-organ communication. Contrary to the pro-fibrotic EVs released by injured muscle, EVs produced by skeletal muscle after exercise in mice and humans alleviate renal fibrosis, partly by downregulating the pro-fibrotic molecule miR-21a-3p in muscle [51]. Another study found that exosomes from the normal pregnant placenta confer long-term cardiovascular and renal protective effects in animal models, delaying hypertension progression and alleviating renal fibrosis [53]. These findings indicate that endogenous exosomes play a protective role in facilitating repair. This suggests that mimicking or enhancing such endogenous protective mechanisms may provide a novel therapeutic avenue for renal fibrosis.

Exosomes as a novel strategy for renal fibrosis treatment

Given the crucial role of exosomes in intercellular communication, their utilization as therapeutic vehicles has become a research focus in regenerative medicine and nephrology. In particular, exosomes derived from stem cells are regarded as a promising ‘cell-free therapy’ because they inherit the reparative and immunomodulatory properties of their parent cells while avoiding risks associated with cell transplantation, such as immune rejection and tumorigenicity [16,54]. Research strategies focusing on stem cell-derived exosomes not only investigate their intrinsic anti-fibrotic mechanisms but also explore engineering approaches to enhance their therapeutic efficacy, offering new strategies to address renal fibrosis. The therapeutic cornerstone role of mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs)

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) hold significant promise, which stems from their multidirectional differentiation potential and potent paracrine functions. Substantial evidence indicates that the therapeutic effects of MSCs are largely mediated through their secreted paracrine factors, with exosomes being the key active components [55–58].

Core anti-fibrotic mechanisms of MSC-EVs: multi-target synergistic regulation

Notably, the anti-fibrotic effects of MSC-EVs converge on the inhibition of core pro-fibrotic signaling pathways, including TGF-β/Smad [59,60], Hedgehog/SMO [61], RhoA/ROCK [62], PI3K/Akt, and MAPK [63]. This underscores their characteristic synergistic therapy through complex molecular networks and establishes the theoretical basis for their potent anti-fibrotic capacity.

MSC-EVs effectively suppress EMT, the core pathological process of renal fibrosis, through multi-targeted, multi-pathway synergistic regulation. Studies show that adipose tissue-derived MSC-EVs (ADSC-EVs) can reduce TGF-β1-induced EMT in HK-2 cells by inhibiting the FOXS1/Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [55]. Furthermore, MSC-EVs possess strong anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory capabilities. They can promote macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [64,65] and induce the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby suppressing inflammatory responses [66,67]. MSC-EVs can also inhibit MMT, thereby blocking the transition from inflammation to fibrosis [43,68].

MSC-EVs also exert protective effects through the regulation of autophagy. For instance, human placental MSC-EVs deliver miR-99b-5p to inhibit mTOR signaling, thereby enhancing autophagic activity and mitigating renal injury in DKD [69]. Another study found that MSC-EVs can inhibit aberrant glycolytic metabolic reprogramming by delivering miR-21a-5p, which targets the key glycolytic enzyme PFKM, consequently alleviating fibrosis [70]. Furthermore, MSC-EVs can ameliorate renal hypoxia and subsequently delay fibrosis by promoting angiogenesis and restoring microcirculation—a process involving complex epigenetic mechanisms including the regulation of m6A modification and SIRT1 expression [54].

In another study, MSC-EVs specifically enrich in injured kidneys and deliver microRNA-34c-5p (miR-34c-5p) to inhibit core fucosylation (CF) [71]. By targeting and regulating this post-translational modification of proteins, MSC-EVs simultaneously inhibit the activation of multiple pathological cell types from an upstream hub-like target, thereby suppressing the fibrotic process.

Beyond targeting specific pathways, MSC-EVs can coordinately regulate multiple fibrotic pathways. An in-depth study indicated that breast milk-derived MSCs and their exosomes (Br-MSCs-EXOs) can concurrently: upregulate anti-fibrotic miRNAs (miR-29b, miR-181, and Let-7b) and Smad7; downregulate pro-fibrotic miRNA (miR-34a) and core fibrotic pathway molecules (TGF-β, Smad3, and Col-I); activate the autophagy pathway (upregulate AMPK, ULK-1, Beclin 1, LC3; downregulate AKT, and mTOR, P62) and ameliorate oxidative stress. This multi-pronged approach ultimately leads to significant restoration of renal function and structure in an adenine-induced CKD model [72]. Although preclinical studies robustly confirm the significant potential of MSC-EVs as monotherapy, their future clinical application is more likely to involve combination with existing standard drugs. For example, in a 5/6 nephrectomy (5/6 Nx) CKD rat model, the combination of locally injected, adipose-derived MSC-EVs with Losartan (an angiotensin II receptor blocker) demonstrated significant synergistic renoprotective effects. However, critical parameters such as optimal doses and routes of administration still require further clarification [73].

MSC-EVs and key therapeutic ‘cargo’: delivery of functional miRNAs and proteins

The therapeutic function of MSC-EVs primarily relies on their carried bioactive ‘cargo’ [74], particularly functional miRNAs and proteins. Regarding proteins, Tumor Necrosis Factor-α-Induced Protein 6 (TSG-6) has been identified as a core protein for the anti-inflammatory effects of MSCs. It is primarily secreted in exosomes and promotes M2 macrophage polarization and Treg induction [66]. Milk Fat Globule-EGF Factor 8 (MFGE8) is a key active component in ADSC-EVs that exerts anti-fibrotic effects by inhibiting the FAK/Akt signaling pathway [75]. Furthermore, proteins such as CK1δ and β-TRCP can be delivered via exosomes to promote ubiquitination and degradation of YAP, thereby inhibiting fibrosis [76,77]. The proteins delivered by exosomes are not limited to signaling molecules but also include functional proteins with enzymatic activity. For example, bone marrow MSC-derived exosomes can deliver the E3 ubiquitin ligase Nedd4L to renal tubular epithelial cells. This enzyme effectively inhibits TGF-β1-induced EMT by activating autophagy [78]. Beyond these key protein cargos, various functional miRNAs are more abundant in MSC-EVs and exhibit more diverse mechanisms.

Engineering modifications and optimization of therapeutic strategies

Although natural EVs have promising therapeutic potential, their clinical translation is hindered by limited targeting precision and difficulties in controlling production. Thus, more efforts have been made to enhance their therapeutic efficacy.

Genetic engineering and preconditioning of parent cells

To enrich parent MSC-EVs with specific therapeutic molecules, many studies have genetically engineered the parent MSCs to produce exosomes with enhanced therapeutic functions. For example, MSCs overexpressing TSG-6 [66], GDNF [92], specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-29a-3p [86], miR-13474 [93]), as well as those transfected with let-7i-5p antagomir (anti-let-7i-5p) [94], secrete exosomes with enhanced potency in anti-inflammatory or anti-fibrotic activities. Besides genetic engineering, preconditioning parent cells is also an effective strategy. Hypoxic preconditioning significantly improves the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-EVs from various sources, such as adipose-derived MSCs and human placenta-derived MSCs, potentially through mechanisms involving improved renal fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and mitochondrial homeostasis [95,96]. Beyond simulating hypoxia, preconditioning umbilical cord MSCs in a diabetic-mimetic environment (high glucose, etc.) enriches their secreted Exo-pre with beneficial molecules like miR-486-5p, which significantly enhances their therapeutic effect on DN compared to conventional Exo-Con [65]. Preconditioning MSCs with cytokines like TNF-α and IFN-γ can enhance their immunomodulatory capacity and anti-fibrotic effects [6]. Additionally, treating MSCs with small molecule compounds like Indole-3-carbinol can induce high expression of TSG-6, thereby enhancing the therapeutic potential of their exosomes [66]. Similarly, preconditioning MSCs with melatonin enriches their secreted exosomes with various anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic miRNAs, demonstrating stronger ability to inhibit TNF-α, TGF-β expression, and renal fibrosis in a CKD model [97]. The state of the parent cell is crucial for determining EV function. This is exemplified by MSC-EVs from aged donors, which lose key miRNAs such as miR-294 and miR-133, resulting in diminished anti-fibrotic capacity. Studies confirm that overexpressing these miRNAs in senescent MSCs via genetic engineering can effectively restore their EVs’ ability to inhibit TGF-β/SMAD and ERK pathways [98], underscoring the importance of parent cell modification.

Notably, as demonstrated by the diverse strategies discussed above, studies have utilized MSCs from various tissue origins, including adipose [95], umbilical cord [65], and placenta [96]. However, a critical consensus in the field is that the inherent variability among these different MSC sources significantly influences the baseline composition of EV cargo. This biological heterogeneity, compounded by variations in donor physiological states (e.g., aging) [98], can lead to fluctuating therapeutic outcomes, posing a major hurdle for the reproducibility and clinical standardization of engineered MSC-EV therapies [99].

Surface modification & targeted delivery, cargo loading, and theranostics

To address the issue of widespread distribution and insufficient targeting of exosomes in vivo, researchers have developed various surface modification techniques for precise delivery to injured kidneys. One strategy involves displaying targeting peptides on the parent cell membrane, enabling their integration into the exosome membrane during biogenesis. For example, modifying MSCs with RGD peptides results in exosomes (RGD-MSC-EVs) that efficiently target hypoxic renal tissue, significantly enhancing their anti-fibrotic effect [54]. Another method involves direct chemical modification of exosomes. For instance, modifying exosomes with a surface charge reversal polymer, ε-polylysine-polyethylene-distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine (PPD) enhances their retention and bioavailability in the kidney, improving therapeutic outcomes [79]. Furthermore, exosomes can be conjugated with targeting ligands (e.g., peptides recognizing injured tubule Kim-1) to delivering therapeutic siRNA, achieving precise targeting of key transcription factors involved in inflammation and fibrosis [100]. Co-assembling exosomes with hypoxia-sensitive macrocyclic amphiphiles constructs nanoprobes capable of targeted imaging of renal hypoxic areas while simultaneously exerting therapeutic effects [101].

Beyond these bioengineering and chemical strategies, emerging physical targeting technologies are gaining attention. Decorating MSC-EVs with superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) to create SPION-EVs demonstrates unique advantages of physical targeting. Under external magnetic guidance, SPION-EVs can be efficiently and selectively accumulated at kidney injury sites. Importantly, this approach achieved the delivery of the functional protein CHIP, which induces degradation of the pro-fibrotic core signaling molecules Smad2/3. This resulted in potent renal-targeting anti-fibrotic effects in animal models, significantly improving both delivery efficiency and treatment specificity [102].

It is also a budding approach for combining targeted delivery with targeting multiple genes. Researchers constructed engineered exosomes with kidney targeting mediated by RVG peptide for the synergistic delivery of the anti-fibrotic microRNA-23a/27a/26a cluster. This system not only achieved efficient enrichment of therapeutic RNAs in diseased kidneys but also exerted powerful synergistic anti-fibrotic effects in a DN model by simultaneously inhibiting multiple pro-fibrotic targets (Lpp, Zbtb20, Klhl42) [103]. Loading specific therapeutic ‘cargo’ represents another core engineering approach. In one study, exosomes encapsulating miR-26a (Exo-miR-26a) were successfully constructed and delivered to the kidneys via tail vein injection. This strategy significantly alleviated aldosterone-induced renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis by delivering miR-26a, which targets and inhibits the key pro-fibrotic factor CTGF [104]. In another study, engineered exosomes loaded with siRNA or pre-miRNA were shown to be efficiently taken up by glomerular parietal epithelial cells and achieve functional gene regulation [105], which provides a feasible technical path for future use of exosomes to deliver anti-fibrotic agents (e.g., miRNA or siRNA) to key cells in fibrotic lesions.

Finally, integrating exosome membrane modification targeting peptides (e.g., PBP) with cargo loading technologies enables the construction of engineered delivery systems that possess both ‘active targeting’ and ‘multi-regulatory’ functions. This strategy achieves efficient carrier enrichment in injured kidneys via ligand-receptor-mediated mechanisms, offering potential for noninvasive diagnosis and real-time monitoring. More importantly it enables simultaneous intervention in multiple key pathological pathways through the delivery of therapeutic cargo like miRNA clusters, thereby exerting synergistic therapeutic effects in inhibiting inflammatory infiltration and reversing the pro-fibrotic microenvironment. This provides a promising new strategy for achieving theranostics in renal fibrosis [106].

Undoubtedly, the aforementioned engineering strategies represent a massive leap forward, significantly enhancing the specific accumulation of EVs at renal injury sites compared to native EVs. However, a critical examination of current delivery systems reveals that complete avoidance of off-target effects remains a formidable biological challenge. Biodistribution analyses in highly representative advanced systems—such as RGD-modified EVs [54], Pc/C5A co-assembled EVs [101], SPION-decorated EVs [102], and PBP-targeted EVs [106]—consistently demonstrate a universal hurdle: despite enhanced renal tropism, a substantial proportion of administered EVs is still inevitably sequestered by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), accumulating predominantly in the liver, spleen, and lungs. This inevitable systemic distribution of highly concentrated exogenous cargoes (e.g., therapeutic RNAs or functional proteins) raises valid safety concerns regarding unforeseeable off-target effects and potential interference with cellular homeostasis in healthy organs. Therefore, for future clinical translation, engineering strategies must not only optimize targeting ligands but also minimize RES clearance and systemic toxicity.

Novel production and sustained-release delivery systems

Traditional two-dimensional (2D) culture of MSCs suffers from limited exosome production, which restricts its large-scale application. To address this limitation, a 3D bioprinting-based method for culturing MSC microfilaments was developed, which significantly enhances both the yield and biological activity of the secreted exosomes. Exosomes derived from 3D culture demonstrate superior efficacy to their 2D-cultured counterparts in inhibiting EMT, reducing ECM deposition, and suppressing ferroptosis [107]. Corroborating this, using hypoxic preconditioned Wharton’s jelly-derived MSCs (WJ-MSCs) 3D-cultured in spheroids not only significantly improves exosome yield but also enriches them with the antioxidant miRNA miR-210, resulting in exceptional protective effects in a kidney injury model [108].

Furthermore, to meet the stringent requirements for dose and purity in clinical-grade production, an innovative platform integrating a stirred-tank reactor (STR) with scalable downstream purification has been established. Studies show that expanding MSCs in STR using microcarriers and human platelet lysate (hPL) serum-free medium, followed by pairing tangential flow filtration and anion exchange chromatography, enables the stable production of high-purity exosomes conforming to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards. This integrated process provides a viable solution to the scalability bottleneck [109]. Innovations have also emerged in downstream separation techniques. Traditional ultracentrifugation is inefficient and can compromise exosome integrity, whereas emerging CD63 aptamer-based thermoresponsive copolymer (PNB-aptamer) separation systems show unique advantages. This system can reversibly capture and release exosomes nondestructively via temperature changes, with a separation efficiency (∼62%) significantly higher than traditional ultrafiltration (∼42%), offering a new strategy for the efficient isolation of highly active exosomes [110].

Beyond increasing yield and optimizing purification, achieving sustained and local release of exosomes is equally critical Researchers developed an injectable, matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2) sensitive self-assembling peptide hydrogel as a sustained-release delivery system for exosomes [111]. This hydrogel enables responsive release of exosomes at the injury site, significantly prolonging their duration of action. Compared to EVs alone, it shows improved efficacy in promoting angiogenesis, reducing apoptosis, and inhibiting chronic fibrosis [111].

Despite technological breakthroughs, transitioning engineered MSC-EVs to clinical reality faces profound manufacturing and regulatory barriers. Scaling up to industrial stirred-tank reactors (STRs) introduces shear stress and oxygen gradient variations, potentially altering exosomal cargo and functional potency [109]. Furthermore, achieving batch-to-batch reproducibility is hampered by the inherent biological variability of GMP-compliant supplements like human platelet lysate (hPL). To satisfy stringent dose and safety requirements, scalable and automated downstream processing (DSP) must replace manual isolation [109]. Consequently, successful clinical translation requires enforcing standardized characterization criteria (e.g., ISEV guidelines) across diverse platforms to facilitate robust quality control.

Therapeutic potential of exosomes from other stem cell sources

Not only are MSC-EVs significantly anti-fibrotic, but exosomes from other stem cell sources also demonstrate unique therapeutic potential. As endogenous renal progenitor cells, PECs-derived exosomes can exert potent anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects through the delivery of let-7b-5p, which downregulates TGFβR1 and ARID3a [52]. Human Liver Stem Cell (HLSC)-derived exosomes exhibit anti-fibrotic effects in various renal injury models [112–114]. One identified mechanism involves the exosomal transfer of miR-29b, which interferes with the β-catenin signaling pathway to inhibit fibroblast activation [115]. Another study indicates that the anti-fibrotic mechanism of HLSC-EVs also partly relies on their cargo of matrix metalloproteinases (e.g., MMP-1) directly remodel aberrant ECM [116]. This action not only significantly reverses renal fibrosis, improving both interstitial fibrosis and glomerulosclerosis, but also concurrently ameliorates cardiac fibrosis and improves heart function.

Beyond tissue-specific stem cell-derived exosomes, pluripotent stem cell-derived exosomes possess broader regulatory capabilities. Embryonic Stem Cell (ESC)-derived exosomes have been shown to promote physiological repair and suppress pathological repair (fibrosis) after acute kidney injury by activating Sox9-positive cells [117].

Similarly, induced Pluripotent Stem Cell (iPSC)-derived extracellular vesicles (iPSC-EVs) demonstrate significant anti-fibrotic potential. In a TGF-β1-stimulated mouse mesangial cell (MMC) fibrosis model, iPSC-EVs effectively attenuated inflammation, inhibited aberrant activation of the Renin-Angiotensin System (RAS), and reduced the expression of key fibrosis markers [118]. Some readily accessible stem cell-derived exosomes also possess anti-fibrotic capabilities. For example, Urine-derived Stem Cell (USC) exosomes (USC-Exos), which are rich in miR-122-5p, have been shown to directly target and inhibit SOX2 expression, effectively alleviating renal inflammation and fibrosis and promoting renal pelvis morphology recovery in an obstructive nephropathy model [119]. Taken together, these findings prompt an intriguing hypothesis: the tissue origin of stem cell exosomes may be a key determinant of their therapeutic mechanism. This is attributed to the fact that exosomal cargo biogenesis is a highly regulated process rather than a random sorting of parental cytoplasm. The lineage-specific molecular repertoire of the parent cell governs the selective loading of functional cargoes (e.g., miR-29b and let-7b-5p), thereby dictating the distinct downstream signaling pathways and anti-fibrotic outcomes of the resulting exosomes [120]. Building on this concept, future research may focus on screening for stem cell-derived exosomes with enhanced anti-fibrotic capabilities based on specific sources.

Although the various stem cell-derived exosomes mentioned above demonstrate diverse therapeutic potential, significant gaps must be addressed before clinical application. Mechanistically, the specific active molecules and causal pathways for most therapeutic effects remain incompletely elucidated [115,117,118]. Technically, the heterogeneity of exosome preparations and the diversity of isolation methods hinder the standardization and reproducibility of results [112]. Translationally, most intervention studies are conducted immediately or shortly after injury induction, aiming to block rather than reverse fibrosis; consequently, evidence supporting the efficacy of late-stage interventions (i.e., true ‘reversal’ efficacy) in established chronic fibrosis models is relatively weak [112,116]. Future research should focus on overcoming these bottlenecks to advance stem cell exosomes toward clinical application.

Exosomes as noninvasive biomarkers for renal fibrosis

Renal biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing and assessing the degree of renal fibrosis. However, its invasiveness, sampling variability, and associated risk of complications limit its widespread clinical application, particularly for dynamic monitoring of disease progression. Consequently, the development of reliable, noninvasive biomarkers is crucial for the early diagnosis, prognosis, and evaluation of therapeutic efficacy in renal fibrosis. Exosomes, which act as ‘messengers’ reflecting the status of cells, are considered an ideal source for biomarkers. Studies have shown that enriching exosomes from bodily fluids before molecular analysis can significantly improve detection sensitivity, compared to directly analyzing the overall composition of the fluid [121]. These exosomes are found in a variety of bodily fluids such as urine and blood, and their molecular cargo (e.g., miRNAs, circRNAs, mRNAs, and proteins) accurately reflects the pathophysiological state of their parent cells (particularly renal cells), positioning them as highly promising tools for liquid biopsy [14,122]. Urinary exosomes: ‘liquid biopsy’ for kidney diseases

Urine is an ideal source of biomarkers for kidney diseases because it directly contacts nephron and contains exosomes released from glomerular and tubular cells [18,123]. Compared to free molecules in urine, exosomes protect their cargo of nucleic acids and proteins from degradation, thereby providing more stable and reliable signals [123].

miRNA marker profiles

In lupus nephritis (LN), a urinary exosomal miRNA panel comprising miR-21, miR-150, and miR-29c effectively distinguishes patients with varying degrees of fibrosis and predicts the risk of disease progression [124]. The levels of miR-29c have been found to negatively correlate significantly with the renal chronicity index, making it a potential strong predictor for early-stage renal fibrosis [125,126]. Another study revealed that the level of miR-146a in urinary exosomes is closely associated with disease activity, proteinuria, and histological features in lupus nephritis (LN), and can predict disease relapse [127]. Importantly, the clinical utility of these miRNAs extends beyond diagnosis to predicting treatment outcomes. For example, a prospective study in proliferative lupus nephritis patients revealed that responders to treatment exhibited significantly higher levels of miR-31, miR-107, and miR-135b-5p in urinary exosomes compared to non-responders. Among these, miR-135b-5p demonstrated a more promising predictive capability (AUC = 0.783) for distinguishing the two groups. These miRNAs are produced by renal tubular cells under inflammatory stimulation and promote functional recovery by inhibiting their common target, HIF1A, thereby modulating renal inflammation [128].

In DKD, levels of urinary exosomal miR-192 [129] and miR-26a [130] are elevated, while miR-451-5p has been identified as a sensitive marker for early DKD, increasing prior to the onset of proteinuria and histological changes [131]. Recent investigations continue to expand new candidate markers. One clinical study found that urinary exosomal miR-615-3p expression was significantly higher in DKD patients than in healthy controls or patients with type 2 diabetes alone. Its expression level positively correlated with the degree of renal impairment (e.g., decreased eGFR, increased proteinuria) and with levels of the pro-fibrotic factor TGF-β1. Furthermore, combining urinary exosomal miR-615-3p with the traditional urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) significantly improved the diagnostic performance for DKD [132]. This suggests miR-615-3p is a promising novel biomarker closely associated with the inflammatory and fibrotic processes in DKD. Another study provided deeper mechanistic insight into urinary EV miRNAs as markers of fibrotic activity: it found that miR-145 and miR-126 levels specifically increased in urinary EVs with DKD progression. In vitro studies further demonstrated that renal tubular epithelial cells and podocytes undergoing EMT actively secrete extracellular vesicles enriched in these miRNAs. These findings suggest that urinary miR-145 and miR-126 are not merely correlated with renal fibrosis, but may directly mirror ongoing EMT processes, a central mechanism driving fibrotic progression in the kidney [133].

In addition to miRNAs that increase alongside fibrosis progression, protective miRNAs represent another promising avenue for detection. In a DKD mouse model, de-SUMOylation of the RNA-binding protein RBMX promoted mitochondrial damage and influenced the sorting of protective miRNAs like miR-26a, miR-23c, and miR-874 into urinary exosomes, leading to their reduction and exacerbation of tubulointerstitial fibrosis [134]. This finding suggests that monitoring the levels of these protective miRNAs in urinary exosomes could serve as an indicator for assessing renal fibrosis, although their clinical accuracy and sensitivity require further validation.

In post-renal transplant monitoring, urinary exosomal miR-21 and miR-29c show promise in predicting graft interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA) [122]. Additionally, during the transition from AKI to CKD, dynamic changes in urinary exosomal miRNA (e.g., the miR-200 family) profiles can reflect TGF-β-related fibrotic processes [135].

circRNA markers

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are a class of highly stable, degradation-resistant non-coding RNAs that are gaining attention as potential biomarkers. In a study involving CKD patients, microarray analysis was used to reveal the circRNA expression profile in urinary exosomes from individuals with renal fibrosis. This study found that expression levels of hsa_circ_0036649 were significantly decreased in patients with fibrosis and were correlated with impaired renal function and the degree of tubulointerstitial fibrosis, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for this condition [136]. This preliminary study suggests that urinary exosome-derived circRNAs hold promise as a novel noninvasive biomarker for renal fibrosis. However, the findings are based on a relatively small sample size, and further large-scale studies are needed to validate the results and establish the clinical applicability of circRNAs as reliable biomarkers for diagnosing renal fibrosis.

mRNA and protein markers

Beyond non-coding RNAs, mRNAs and proteins within urinary exosomes also constitute valuable sources of information. Studies have revealed that mRNA levels of the podocyte-associated gene CD2AP in urinary exosomes are significantly reduced in patients with kidney disease. These levels correlate positively with the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and negatively with proteinuria and the extent of fibrosis, suggesting their potential utility as a marker of renal function and fibrosis [137].

Proteomic analysis provides a powerful tool for biomarker discovery. In renal transplant patients, levels of Vitronectin (VTN) in urinary EVs can effectively identify individuals with severe fibrotic lesions [138]. Another study of transplant recipients receiving calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) therapy found an upregulation of members of the uroplakin and plakin families in their urinary EVs, a signature potentially linked to CNI-induced nephrotoxicity [139]. In DKD patients, the levels of CD73 (ecto-5′-nucleotidase) in urinary EVs were increased, which indicates that CD73 serves as a marker for disease progression [140]. Furthermore, senescence-associated proteins like p21 and p16 are elevated in urinary exosomes from patients with renovascular hypertension, reflecting a state of renal cellular senescence [141]. These studies demonstrate that multi-omics analysis of urinary exosomes can contribute to the development of more comprehensive and precise biomarker panels for renal fibrosis.

In summary, urinary exosomes provide diverse biomarkers for the noninvasive diagnosis of renal fibrosis. miRNA research is the most mature, with numerous candidates showing diagnostic and prognostic value. circRNAs are emerging as promising markers, distinguished by their stability. Meanwhile, mRNAs and proteins offer the potential to more directly reflect the functional state and specific pathological alterations of their parent cells. However, the translation of these discoveries from basic research to clinical application faces several common challenges and limitations. First, the breadth and depth of clinical validation remain inadequate. Most candidate markers were identified in small-scale, single-center, cross-sectional studies [125,126,136,137], raising questions about the generalizability of the findings. The prevalent lack of prospective studies and long-term follow-up data [126,130,135] leaves the predictive value of these markers for long-term outcomes (e.g., ESRD, graft survival) largely unestablished. Furthermore, the absence of key clinical data (e.g., 24-h proteinuria) and differences in baseline characteristics (e.g., BMI) between cohorts introduce potential confounding factors [141]. Second, the understanding of underlying biological mechanisms is still superficial. Most studies merely establish correlative relationships, whereas the precise functional roles and causal mechanisms of key molecules (e.g., miR-146a, circNUP98, Plakin proteins) remain elusive [124,127,129]. Discrepancies are observed between the expression levels of many markers in renal tissue and their abundance in urinary exosomes [130], and their precise cellular origins as well as the active mechanisms governing their sorting into exosomes are poorly understood [130,133]. The reliance on limited animal models [135] and limited access to human tissues [141] further restrict in-depth exploration of the biological significance of these markers. Finally, the absence of standardized protocols represents the bottleneck currently faced. Researchers must contend with the heterogeneity of exosomes, diverse isolation techniques, and instable reference protein expression under pathological conditions. These discrepancies directly compromise the accuracy of the resulting data, making it difficult to replicate and even harder to establish reliable comparisons across different laboratories [130,141].

Therefore, future research must establish a framework from discovery to application. Initially, expanding cohort sizes is essential to confirm the clinical value. Subsequently, multi-omics and functional experiments can be employed to elucidate the core molecular mechanisms. Ultimately, there is an urgent need to drive the standardization of exosome research. Only in this way can the full potential of urinary exosomes in the precise diagnosis and treatment of kidney fibrosis be realized.

Circulating exosomes in diagnosis and monitoring

While urinary exosomes provide greater organ specificity for localized renal diagnostics, circulating exosomes offer distinct advantages by better reflecting systemic changes and inter-organ crosstalk [2]. Exosomes in the systemic circulation have broader cellular origins, including not only renal cells but also potentially distant organs. However, research in this field is still in its early stages, frequently limited by small sample sizes, the absence of independent cohort validation, and inconsistent exosome isolation methods [142]. Additionally, many findings lack clear associations with clinical outcomes, and our understanding of the underlying mechanisms remains incomplete [143,144]. More standardized and large-scale studies are needed to validate its clinical value before applying circulating exosomes in the diagnosis and detection of renal fibrosis.

In patients with idiopathic membranous nephropathy (IMN), serum exosome miRNA expression profiles not only distinguish IMN patients from healthy individuals or those with other nephrotic syndromes but also offer valuable insight into predicting treatment responses. This underscores their role in differential diagnosis and their potential to guide personalized therapy [142]. Furthermore, studies on muscle-kidney crosstalk in animal models revealed altered levels of miR-29a in the serum exosomes of unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) mice [143]. This provides additional evidence that circulating exosomes reflect systemic pathology and inter-organ communication.

In DKD patients, circNUP98 in serum exosomes was identified as a diagnostically valuable biomarker [145]. Another study found that serum exosomal hsa_circ_0006382 and hsa_circ_0019539 aggravate renal fibrosis by downregulating fibroblast growth factor 9 (FGF9) via a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) mechanism. These findings position these circulating circRNAs as potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for fibrosis in DKD [144].

Conclusion and perspectives

This review provides a novel structured analysis of exosomal duality, shifting the paradigm of exosomes from mere pathogenic mediators to a comprehensive framework for the development of dual- pronged therapeutic strategies.

Exosome-based therapies, particularly those utilizing MSC-EVs, hold translational promise due to their multi-target anti-fibrotic effects. However, clinical application is currently hindered by the lack of standardized isolation, purification, and characterization protocols for quality control [146], alongside the inherent efficacy and targeting constraints of natural exosomes. To overcome these limitations, future research must shift toward comprehensive bioengineering—integrating parent cell modification, exosome surface engineering, and specific cargo loading, with the development of novel production technologies and sustained-release delivery systems Furthermore, deploying advanced biomimetic platforms, such as ‘renal fibrosis progression on-chip’ models, will be crucial for validating these engineered candidates and precisely identifying the tipping point for early, reversible intervention [147].

Simultaneously, the clinical value of exosomes as noninvasive biomarkers is becoming increasingly prominent. To further expand this diagnostic potential, it is essential to consider a broader spectrum of exosomal non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), such as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs). Recent studies have confirmed that lncRNAs like MALAT1 and H19 are stably enriched in circulating serum exosomes [148]. MALAT1 knockout has been shown to attenuate TGF-β1 induced Smad 2/3-mediated fibrosis [149], while H19 ameliorates renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis via the miR-130a-3p/ACSL1 axis [150]. Moreover, lncRNA TUG1 acts as a crucial regulator in hyperuricemia-induced renal fibrosis [151]. Protected from enzymatic degradation by exosomes, these lncRNAs hold immense promise as stable ‘liquid biopsy’ biomarkers. Piwi-interacting RNA (piRNA) is also a type of ncRNA. However, the research on piRNA derived from exosomes in kidney diseases has not been reported yet. This may be a research direction in the future. The development of integrated biomarker panels based on exosomal multi-omics signatures promises to enable early-stage warning, stratification, and dynamic monitoring of renal fibrosis progression, thereby providing treatment plans tailored to patients with different degrees of severity. Nonetheless, for these promising markers to be translated into clinical practice, they must undergo thorough validation in larger, multi-center prospective cohort studies.

In summary, research on exosomes is transforming their role from ‘mysterious cellular messengers’ into ‘versatile regulatory hubs’ in the field of renal fibrosis. Future exploration needs to focus on overcoming technical bottlenecks and accelerating clinical translation, building upon a clarified understanding of their complex biological functions. Particular emphasis must be placed on the critical time window of AKI-to-CKD transition, which is decisive for the timing of exosome-based therapies [152]. Protecting renal function relies on fully integrating the dual role of exosomes. By intercepting pro-fibrotic exosomal networks while deploying engineered therapeutic exosomes as kidney-targeted delivery systems, we may successfully navigate the organ’s complex structural and physiological barriers, offering a highly precise therapeutic strategy for CKD.
