Addressing student substance use in Canadian schools: an exploration of administrators’ perspectives
Authors: Tonje M Molyneux, Zachary Daly, Chris G Richardson, Ashraf Amlani, Dana Dmytro, Nadine Trépanier-Bisson, Anne Gadermann, Trevor Goodyear, Chris Gilham, Nathan Ngieng, Johanna Sam, Sherri Moore-Arbour, Emily Jenkins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo support the development of evidence-informed guidance for Canadian Kindergarten through Grade 12 (K-12) school administrators, this study examined their perceptions of the scope of student substance use and the challenges they face in responding within school settings. An online, cross-sectional survey collected data from 204 Canadian school administrators, capturing their experiences and perspectives on student substance use, including the types of challenges encountered, time spent addressing them, perceived capacity to respond, and differences across school levels (elementary, middle, and secondary). Responses from elementary/middle and secondary school administrators were compared in the analysis. Approximately 90% of administrators reported frequent student substance use challenges. The most common challenges were vaping (63.7%) and substance use on school property (41.7%). Secondary school administrators reported significantly more challenges and were more likely to spend in excess of 5 hours weekly addressing substance use. Most administrators reported a willingness to change practices (64.7%), but fewer felt equipped or effective in their current approaches. Student substance use poses a significant challenge for Canadian schools, particularly at the secondary level, increasing administrator workload. Administrators report feeling underprepared and insufficiently supported, despite a willingness to act. Findings highlight an urgent need for developmentally informed, evidence-aligned strategies and resources to support school-based responses to substance use and overall school health promotion.Keywords: substance use prevention, youth/adolescent, school administrator, school health promotion, school policy

INTRODUCTION

Substance use among youth in Canada is a pressing and evolving public health challenge (Public Health Agency of Canada 2018). The prevalence of substance use (e.g. cannabis, vape products, stimulants, and prescription opioids) is higher among youth than in other age demographics (Health Canada 2024), and drug-related deaths are a leading cause of mortality among 10–18-year-olds across the country (Public Health Agency of Canada 2018, British Columbia Coroners Service 2024). Moreover, there is a broad and growing body of evidence highlighting substance use-related harms among youth that go well beyond overdose deaths. These harms affect health, social, academic, and legal domains of young people's lives. For example, youth substance use, especially early and frequent use, is associated with higher risk for mental health challenges (Health Canada 2024), lower school connectedness (Allen et al. 2025), reduced academic achievement [Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction (CCSA) 2014], and justice system involvement (CCSA 2022). Harms are often compounded for youth who are in care or homeless, LGBTQIA2S+, Indigenous or racialized, living in rural or underserved regions, or have histories of trauma (Mereish 2019, First Nations Health Authority 2021). In this context, there is a clear need for comprehensive and effective, developmentally informed public health strategies to prevent, reduce, and delay substance use harms across the life course.

Kindergarten through Grade 12 (K-12) schools offer a promising setting for such health promotion efforts. Schools provide a near-universal access point to children and adolescents during key stages of their development, making them an ideal setting for early intervention and prevention before substance use becomes habitual or harmful. For example, research shows that early adolescence (ages 10–14) is a key window of opportunity when youth are especially receptive to health promotion messages and skill-building interventions (Catalano et al. 2012, Onrust et al. 2016, Patton et al. 2016). Schools are also embedded within the social ecology of students’ daily lives—including peer networks, teacher relationships, and institutional norms. These features allow school-based health promotion approaches and programmes to build protective factors (e.g. connectedness, self-regulation skills, and academic engagement) and reduce risk factors (e.g. peer pressure and disengagement) in a sustained way (Bond et al. 2007, Cipriano et al. 2023, Lekamge et al. 2025). Importantly, given that the majority of data on student substance use comes from school-based surveys (e.g. Canadian Student Alcohol and Drugs Survey, Health Canada 2024), we know that students actively using substances are attending schools; thus, reaching them in the school setting is an important avenue for prevention and intervention. Moreover, evidence supports the effectiveness of whole-school health promotion approaches that combine classroom education, staff training, school climate improvement, and family engagement (Langford et al. 2014, Lekamge et al. 2025). For example, a Health Promoting Schools (HPS) framework can embed substance use prevention into broader strategies for student well-being and positive school culture (Langford et al. 2017). Furthermore, schools are a key node for collaboration between health, education, and community sectors, allowing the integration of public health strategies with academic and social development goals. Models such as Communities That Care (CTC) illustrate how schools can partner with families, health systems, and local governments to reduce risk and promote protective factors at the population level (Hawkins et al. 2009).

Despite this promise, existing school-based health promotion and substance use prevention approaches vary widely and have struggled to demonstrate impact (Flynn et al. 2015, Tremblay et al. 2020). For example, the implementation of standalone instructional programmes remains prominent (Faggiano et al. 2008, Bennett 2014, Flynn et al. 2015, Lee et al. 2016), but these programmes often focus on delivering information about the risks of substance use, with students positioned as passive recipients of knowledge. These top-down models have been criticized for focusing on individual decision-making and behaviour while overlooking the broader social, developmental, and structural contexts that shape youth substance use (Skager 2008). Moreover, youth have voiced their distrust in fear-based and authoritarian approaches to substance use education, noting that these approaches lack credibility and fail to engage them (Toumbourou et al. 2007). Such strategies are not only ineffective but also unsupported by evidence (Brown 2001).

School administrators (i.e. principals and vice-principals) are responsible for implementing substance use policies and programming, key aspects of effective health promotion in schools (Evans-Whipp et al. 2004). In their role, they provide strategic leadership that determines whether and how substance use prevention is prioritized, what resources are allocated (e.g. staff time and budget), and whether disciplinary or supportive approaches are emphasized. Thus, if administrators perceive substance use as a low priority or primarily a disciplinary issue, this can block the adoption of evidence-based prevention or harm mitigation initiatives (Allegrante et al. 2024). In many jurisdictions, school boards and Ministries of Education rely on administrator consultation to guide decisions on curricular frameworks, mental health and wellness policies, prevention mandates, and professional development.

Yet the guidance administrators receive remains inconsistent across jurisdictions. This can contribute to system inequities and the proliferation of approaches that are not evidence based (Bruno and Csiernik 2020, Goossen et al. 2024). To inform appropriate and relevant supports and guidance, a grounded understanding of administrators’ real-world challenges and solutions is essential; we need school administrators’ perspectives on the scope of substance use among students in their schools and the challenges they are encountering in responding (Pearson et al. 2015, Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA] 2019). This study attends to this need by exploring school administrators’ understandings and interpretations of current trends in student substance use, the specific types of substance use-related challenges they encounter, and their perceived capacity to respond to these challenges. This study also describes how administrators’ experiences and perspectives vary across elementary, middle, and secondary school settings.

Over the past several years, youth substance use patterns have been changing, particularly in relation to nicotine products, cannabis, and opioids. While cigarette smoking among students in Grades 7–12 declined in Canada from 19% in 2018–19 to 15% in 2021–22 (Health Canada 2024), e-cigarette (vape) use has increased sharply (Hammond and Reid 2025). The Canadian Student Tobacco, Alcohol and Drugs Survey (CSTADS) found that the proportion of Grades 7–12 students reporting past-30-day vape use (with or without nicotine) doubled from 10% in 2016–17 to 20% in 2018–19 (Health Canada 2023). Beyond these quantitative indicators, over half of Canadian youth report that they can access vaping products with ease, and 65% of youth who vaped obtained them from social sources (Smith et al. 2020). Recently, slight declines in vape use have been observed, with 2023–24 data showing the prevalence at 15% (Health Canada 2025). Use was higher among females (18%) compared to males (14%), and among students in Grades 10–12 (22%) compared to those in Grades 7–9 (8%), reflecting key developmental differences (Health Canada 2025).

Evolving drug norms, policies, and markets have added new dimensions to the student substance use landscape. The 2018 legalization of nonmedical cannabis use by adults in Canada, followed by the 2019 amendment permitting the sale of cannabis edibles, marked a significant shift in the Canadian public health policy context (Health Canada 2022). These regulatory changes have been associated with a 69% increase in cannabis initiation among those aged 15–18 (Nguyen et al. 2023). Serial cross-sectional data from the 2018–2019 and 2021–2022 CSTADS showed a 3.8% increase in past 12-month cannabis use among youth, which amounts to a 26% increase in the overall prevalence of cannabis use since legalization (Mital and Nguyen 2025). Canadian youth have described cannabis as ‘normalized’ in media and online, comparing it to socially accepted behaviours such as having a drink with friends (CCSA 2017). An additional challenge is nonmedical opioid use, for which CSTADS data show that 29 000 students in Grades 7–12 reported using oxycodone to get high in the past year (Health Canada 2024). In Ontario, between 2010 and 2015, opioid-related deaths among youth aged 15–24 years nearly doubled from 6.5% to 11.6% (Gomes et al. 2018) and in British Columbia (Bc), drug toxicity (i.e. overdose) has become the leading cause of death for youth aged 10–18 years (Bc Coroners Service 2024). These concerning trends have prompted calls for more school-based substance use harm prevention efforts (e.g. Bc School Trustees Association 2024).

The complexity and shifting landscape of youth substance use highlight an urgent need for comprehensive school-based health promotion approaches to reduce harm. However, little progress has been made since Porath-Waller et al. (2010) from the Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction noted that, ‘a national approach to student drug use prevention has been lacking… Hence, there is a significant gap between evidence-based research findings and programs as currently delivered’ (p. 710). In the absence of regulatory guidelines or contemporary standards, the responsibility for selecting and implementing substance use programming regularly defaults to individual school administrators and teachers, oftentimes resulting in inconsistent and ineffective approaches. For example, an Ontario study of Grade 6 substance use programming found that 38% of schools were not delivering any substance use education, while another 30% employed abstinence-based programmes that do not align with current evidence (Bruno and Csiernik 2020). Similar results have been reported by researchers in Bc, who found that schools are using a wide range of substance use prevention programmes, often without formal evaluation (Goossen et al. 2024).

MATERIALS and METHODS

Study overview

Given the current context, our team at Wellstream: The Canadian Centre for Innovation in Child and Youth Mental Health and Substance Use is leading a pan-Canadian initiative aimed at transforming substance use harm prevention in K-12 schools. This work is rooted in a collaboration between the education and health sectors, youth advocacy groups, and multiple tiers of government. Guided by the World Health Organization's (WHO) HPS framework (WHO 1997), our approach to substance use incorporates principles of health equity and human rights, as well as our team's interdisciplinary and intersectoral expertise in youth substance use, intervention design and implementation, and education practice and systems change.

This article draws on data from one component of this broader effort: the initial round of the ‘Substance Use and Canadian Schools: National Examination of School Administrators’ Experiences and Perspectives survey’. This cross-sectional survey was developed for annual dissemination to help characterize the evolving policy and practice landscape and guide actions to support school-based responses to substance use. The objectives of the present study were to assess school administrators’ experiences and perspectives related to (i) the types of substance use challenges encountered in Canadian K-12 schools, (ii) the amount of time spent addressing these challenges, (iii) perceived capacity within the education systems to respond to these challenges, and (iv) how these challenges vary across elementary, middle, and secondary schools.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the University of British Columbia Behavioural Research Ethics Board, Ethics Certificate #: H23-02699.

Data collection

School administrators working in Canadian K-12 schools were recruited between 25 June and 15 September 2024, to participate in an anonymous online survey (Qualtrics) offered in English and French. Recruitment was facilitated through collaborations with national and regional professional associations of school administrators. Information about the project, along with the survey link, was distributed via electronic and face-to-face channels, including newsletters, membership publications, conferences, and email communications. Participants provided informed consent prior to starting the survey and created a unique identification code to allow future data linkage and longitudinal tracking of change. Participants were invited to enter a draw to win one of 50 $50 gift cards.

Measures

Survey items were developed by our research team and informed by current scientific evidence and practice guidance, including from the Public Health Agency of Canada’s Blueprint for Action (2021), and widely used population survey measures of youth substance use. Demographic questions captured school administrator and school community characteristics. To explore commonly encountered substance use challenges, participants were asked: ‘Thinking about the past 12 months in your role, what are the youth substance use challenges that you most frequently manage in your school?’ If respondents selected the challenge ‘Substance use on school property’, they were prompted to indicate the types of substances (e.g. cannabis, tobacco, and alcohol) based on standard checklists used in Canadian population surveys (e.g. Smith et al. 2024). The amount of time spent each week engaging in activities related to student substance use was assessed using a set of ordinal response options. Administrators were then asked if the time spent had changed in the past year. If their time had increased, they were asked to select what they perceived as the reasons for this change. Finally, participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they agreed with statements regarding their perceived capacity to respond to substance use challenges in their schools and the availability of supportive resources (e.g. How confident are you: (i) managing incidents of substance use on school property, (ii) implementing guiding policies related to substance use, and (iii) discussing substance use issues with students in ways that support their well-being?).

Data analysis

Based on an approach outlined by Wang et al. (2023), analysis began with a systematic process to review data and remove any suspected fraudulent cases (i.e. data submitted by bots or ineligible individuals). Of 426 survey responses received, 79 (18.5%) were removed for not progressing past the demographics, and 143 (33.6%) for being suspected bots/ineligible individuals, leaving a final sample of 204. To characterize the sample and understand the magnitude of substance use issues within K-12 school settings, descriptive statistics were produced using SPSS 29. Analysis then proceeded using bivariate statistics. Given developmental considerations, differences by school level were explored with responses from secondary schools compared to those from elementary/middle schools (combined). Chi-square was used to examine differences in proportions between secondary schools and elementary/middle schools; when cell size assumptions were not met, Fischer's exact test was used. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to examine differences in time spent managing substance use and changes in time spent between secondary schools and elementary/middle schools. Pairwise deletion was used to handle cases of missing data, and the rate of missingness for analysed variables ranged from 0.0% to 7.8%.

RESULTS

Participants

Of the 204 participants in the sample, the majority were school principals or headmasters (57.8%) employed on a full-time basis (96.6%). More than half identified as women (58.3%). The respondents were largely experienced educators, with 59.8% holding a master's degree and 8.8% a doctoral degree. Additionally, 69.1% had over 15 years of experience in K-12 education.

Administrators from schools across Canada participated in the survey, with most participants residing in Ontario (63.2%) and Bc (14.2%). The majority reported working in schools in large urban communities (62.3%) or medium population centres (20.6%). While participants represented various school systems, most were employed in Public English (37.7%), Catholic English (29.4%), and Independent English (24.0%) schools. Types of substances used and challenges encountered

School administrators indicated that the most common substances used on school property were cannabis (33.3%), tobacco and other nicotine products (30.9%), caffeine/energy drinks (29.4%), and alcohol (23.5%). There were significant differences between responses from administrators in secondary schools compared to those working in elementary/middle schools. For example, cannabis use on school property was reported by 44.9% of secondary school administrators compared to only 14.3% of elementary/middle school administrators (P < 0.001). Psychedelics, stimulants, or synthetic drug use (combined) was reported by 18.9% of secondary school administrators compared to only 7.8% of elementary/middle school administrators (P < 0.001). The number of school administrators reporting that substance use was not occurring at their schools was higher for elementary/middle schools than secondary schools (76.6% vs. 47.2%) (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

The substance use challenges encountered by school administrators over the past 12 months are reported in Table 3. These were most frequently related to vaping (63.7%), substance use on school property (41.7%), students impacted by substance use in the family (41.7%), and co-occurring mental health and substance use challenges (39.2%). The challenges also varied by school level, with secondary school administrators reporting more challenges than elementary/middle school administrators. Indeed, only 3.9% of secondary school administrators indicated they encountered no youth substance use issues at their school, in contrast to 20.8% of elementary/middle school administrators (P < 001). However, managing issues related to students impacted by substance use in their family was reported at similar rates by secondary school administrators (40.2%) and those in elementary/middle schools (P = 0.57). This finding adds strength to the validity of these data, as this issue would not be expected to vary by the developmental level of the student.

Time spent addressing substance use challenges

The amount of time spent by administrators each week engaging in activities related to student substance use, as well as whether the amount of time has changed in the past 12 months, is shown in Table 4. Over half (55.4%) of participants reported spending between 1 and 5 hours per week managing issues related to student substance use, while a smaller proportion indicated spending 11–20 hours (3.9%) or more than 20 hours (3.4%). Based on Mann–Whitney U tests, secondary school administrators reported spending more time in activities related to student substance use than those at elementary/middle schools (U = 6036; P < 0.001), and there were also differences in whether the overall time increased, stayed the same, or decreased (U = 4701.5; P = 0.003). Notably, 48.8% of secondary school administrators reported an increase in time spent compared to 26.0% of elementary/middle school administrators.

Capacity of administrators to respond to substance use challenges

Most respondents (64.7%) indicated a willingness among their school administration and teaching staff to engage in practice change to better respond to youth substance use. This sentiment was more prevalent among secondary school administrators (70.9%) compared to those in elementary/middle schools (54.5%). Secondary school administrators were also more likely to report a strong understanding of current youth substance use trends compared to elementary/middle school administrators (54.3% vs. 42.9%). Fewer than half of secondary school administrators (47.2%) and one-third of elementary/middle school administrators (32.5%) reported the belief that their approach to managing student substance use was effective (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

Drawing on data from over 200 school administrators working in elementary, middle, and secondary schools across Canada, this study is among the first to characterize the evolving landscape of school-based substance use prevention from school administrators’ perspectives. It explored the types of substance use challenges encountered in K-12 schools, the amount of time spent addressing them, perceived capacity within the educational system to respond, and how these challenges differed by school level. The perspectives of school administrators, those responsible for managing these issues within schools, are crucial for informing targeted and effective health promotion and harm mitigation strategies. Indeed, approaches that do not evolve with administrator insight risk becoming outdated, underused, or irrelevant to actual school concerns (Hawkins et al. 2009, Patton et al. 2016).

Substance use challenges requiring frequent management in K-12 schools were reported by ∼90% of participants. A key finding of this study is the significant variability in substance use issues between secondary and elementary/middle school contexts. This signals a strong need for developmentally differentiated substance use programming, including the critical opportunity to deliver upstream preventive initiatives, integrated within broader health promotion efforts, in earlier grades to positively influence later trajectories. For example, cannabis use on school property was reported by a substantially higher proportion of secondary school administrators compared to their elementary/middle school counterparts. This finding is aligned with current population data on cannabis use prevalence, which shows higher use rates among Grades 10–12 students compared to Grades 7–9 students (Health Canada 2025). Differences in the substance use landscape by education level underlines the importance of tailoring school-based approaches and content to students’ cognitive, social, and emotional development (Onrust et al. 2016)—aligning with the understanding that schools serve as key developmental contexts during childhood and adolescence (Eccles and Roeser 2011)—with the potential to improve both educational and health outcomes across the life course (Wong et al. 2022).

Although Health Canada (2025) reported a slight downward trend in vaping rates among youth compared to previous years, vaping remains a prominent issue in schools. Our survey respondents identified vaping on school property as a particularly frequent challenge, with nearly 64% reporting that they manage it often, making it the most commonly reported substance use issue faced by administrators. This suggests that even modest declines in national prevalence may not immediately reduce the burden on schools, particularly if vaping is prevailing in certain age groups. Management of vaping was more frequent for secondary (73.2%) versus elementary/middle administrators (48.1%), consistent with population-based data identifying vaping as more common among older than younger youth (Health Canada 2025). These findings corroborate multiple recent media reports that schools are on the front lines of responding to this evolving issue (e.g. Pires 2025, Wilson 2025); however, without formal service linkages and comprehensive action plans, school-based efforts can remain fragmented, under-resourced, or reactive rather than proactive and coordinated. Beyond the health concerns for those who engage in vaping (e.g. increased respiratory and lung health risks; Hammond et al. 2020), these articles have noted an impact on students who do not vape, such as unsafe conditions in school bathrooms and peer pressure to try vaping. These far-reaching impacts demand a comprehensive school health approach to fully address all levels of the socioecological system affected by this issue.

Our findings demonstrate that a significant proportion of administrators’ time is allocated to addressing substance use challenges in schools. Over half of participants reported spending 1–5 hours per week on substance use-related activities. Secondary school administrators reported higher time demands, with nearly 20% indicating that they spend 11–20 hours per week compared to just 5% of elementary/middle school administrators. Nearly half of school administrators reported an increase in the amount of time spent managing student substance use in the past year, highlighting the growing burden placed on school leadership. This trend highlights the need for enhanced resources and stronger external partnerships to help mitigate these mounting demands. Collaborating with external partners can strengthen capacity, connect students to care, and align school efforts with broader community health systems [HIDTA 2024]. It also points to the need to revisit and revise current school-based approaches to substance use prevention and responses. Standalone, school-based substance use programmes have demonstrated little evidence of effectiveness in reducing substance use harms (Tremblay et al. 2020) despite rigorous efforts to support implementation (e.g. Hodder et al. 2017). More research is needed to guide comprehensive, evidence-aligned strategies that incorporate curricular and cross-curricular content (i.e. integrate substance use prevention concepts and skills across multiple subject areas rather than confining them to a single course), intervention (i.e. administrator-led responses to student substance use), and whole-school approaches to foster well-being and student success (Jourdan et al. 2021). This approach can help ensure that prevention is meaningful, developmentally appropriate, and embedded in daily school life.

The limited effectiveness of school-based substance use initiatives may not be due to a lack of receptivity from school personnel. Nearly two-thirds of our respondents indicated a willingness among school administrators and teaching staff to change their practices related to youth substance use. Fisher (2019) reported similar sentiments in their study of New York school and community service providers on the implementation of a comprehensive school-based substance use strategy. Their findings revealed an overall interest in implementing school-based substance use programming while also highlighting the need for more training and support. School administrators, although receptive, also noted challenges in transitioning away from punitive approaches to student substance use, which remain widely implemented. Among our respondents, only half of secondary school administrators and about one-third of elementary/middle school administrators agreed or strongly agreed that they felt equipped to lead change in their schools to improve responses to youth substance use. School officials and personnel recognize the urgent need for effective cross-sectoral approaches to address substance use, yet remain hindered by a lack of guidance and training. For example, the Bc School Trustees Association (BCSTA 2024) issued an urgent call for meaningful consultation, expert guidance, resource development, standardization, and implementation support, as well as monitoring and evaluation to strengthen school-based solutions to this issue, yet such efforts remain in their infancy. This study supports the critical need to engage knowledge users, such as school administrators and school-based staff, in a codevelopment process to foster innovation related to substance use harm prevention in the education ecosystem (Waller et al. 2017). Such efforts should be further grounded in an implementation science approach to ensure a coordinated strategy for mobilizing evidence into practice and supporting the adoption and implementation of new insights. Moreover, future efforts should include contemporary, evidence-aligned guidance based on developmental science and education frameworks, such as the Comprehensive School Health approach.

Limitations

This study has important limitations to consider. We employed convenience sampling to recruit participants, which can influence representativeness and generalizability. Although we utilized several means of recruitment, including electronic and face-to-face channels, our final analytic sample did not include equal representation from all regions in Canada. Furthermore, survey respondents may have been motivated to participate for a variety of reasons, including personal experiences with student substance use challenges. Despite these limitations, our sample was adequately powered to permit our analytic approach, and in future administrations of this survey, we aim to increase our sample size and include a more nationally representative sample of K-12 school administrators.

CONCLUSION

Student substance use has been adversely affecting schools and communities across Canada, with some districts naming it a ‘youth health crisis’. Responding to this issue demands a coordinated, comprehensive school health approach that includes school-based substance use education, intervention, and whole-school approaches. Yet, school administrators are navigating this terrain without clear regulatory guidance or contemporary standards. This study provides a critical understanding of the challenges and perspectives of Canadian K-12 school administrators regarding student substance use and related initiatives. Findings highlight the key substance use-related issues that school administrators are experiencing, the increasing time dedicated to substance use management, and the perceived lack of effectiveness of current approaches. These findings underscore the need for developmentally informed, evidence-aligned strategies and effective resources and guidance for educators. A shift towards a codevelopment approach, rooted in implementation science, can help to bridge the gap between research and practice and to more effectively address the complex and evolving landscape of youth substance use in Canadian schools.
