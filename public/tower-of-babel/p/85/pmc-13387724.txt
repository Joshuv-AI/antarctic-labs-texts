Assessing the intergenerational impact of head start on offspring mental health in the United States
Authors: Julia P Schleimer, Anjum Hajat, Gail Joseph, Min Sun, Frederick Rivara, Ali Rowhani-Rahbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To estimate the association between maternal exposure to Head Start and offspring mental health.Methods:In this retrospective cohort study using a difference-in-differences approach, we leveraged the quasi-random rollout of Head Start in the United States in the 1960's and the timing of birth cohorts in the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979 to estimate associations between maternal Head Start exposure and offspring depression (among those aged 15+ in years 1994–2020) and suicidal ideation (among those aged 15+ in years 2012–2020). We examined heterogeneity by offspring race, ethnicity, and sex.Results:Maternal Head Start exposure was associated with 0.79 (95% CI = 0.62, 0.99) times the risk of depression among female Hispanic/Latina offspring and 0.66 (95% CI = 0.50, 0.87) times the risk of depression among female White offspring. We found null associations for other races, ethnicities, and males, and for the outcome of suicidal ideation.Conclusions:Maternal Head Start exposure may meaningfully reduce risk of depression among Hispanic/Latina and White female offspring—groups who tend to report higher risk of depression.Policy implications:Findings suggest social policies that invest in the social, economic, and human capital of vulnerable children can improve mental health outcomes across generations.Keywords: Head start, Early childhood education, Mental health, Depression, Suicidal ideation, Quasi-experiment

Introduction

Depression and suicidal ideation, attempt, and death are pressing public health problems in the United States (US). In 2024, 15.4% of adolescents (3.8 million people) aged 12–17 and 8.2% of adults (21.4 million people) aged 18+ in the US had a major depressive episode in the past year (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2025). That year, 2.6 million adolescents and 14.3 million adults reported serious thoughts of suicide, and 700,000 adolescents and 2.2 million adults reported attempting suicide (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2025).

Risk for poor mental health is partly rooted in intergenerational social and economic conditions which shape access to resources, exposure to trauma and toxic stress, and biopsychosocial development (Vidal-Ribas et al., 2024; McLaughlin et al., 2010). For example, due to structural oppression and inequities in opportunity, a third of Americans who grow up in poverty remain in low-income households in adulthood (Committee on Policies and Programs to Reduce Intergenerational Poverty, Board on Children, Youth, and Families, et al., 2024). Exposure to poverty in early childhood—a sensitive period for neuro-cognitive, biological, and socio-emotional development—is associated with later-life mental health problems (Gilman et al., 2002), highlighting one pathway through which the socioeconomic conditions of one generation can affect the mental health outcomes of the next.

Social policies that lift children out of poverty and promote optimal development can help shift the life trajectories of marginalized populations and disrupt the intergenerational transmission of disadvantage. Yet little research has examined the intergenerational effects of policies that target social and economic resources on offspring mental health.

We examined the impacts of the nation's oldest and largest high-quality early childhood education policy on the next generation's risk of depression and suicidal ideation by leveraging the quasi-random rollout of Head Start—a federally-funded, locally-run preschool program for low-income children ages 3–5—in the 1960's. Research shows that Head Start and other high-quality preschool programs have life-long socioeconomic, behavioral, and health benefits for program participants (Reynolds et al., 2015). A handful of studies have examined whether these benefits extend to the next generation, i.e., participants' offspring (Barr and Gibbs, 2022; García et al., 2021; Heckman and Karapakula, 2019; Schanzenbach and Bauer, 2016; Rossin-Slater and Wüst, 2020; Akresh et al., 2018; Mazumder et al., 2019). All found meaningful protective associations, including for offspring educational attainment, teenage pregnancy, and crime. We extended this scholarship to examine offspring depression and suicidal ideation. Given differences in the burden of these outcomes across population subgroups (Brody et al., 2018; Forrest et al., 2023) and prior work suggesting heterogenous effects of Head Start and other high-quality preschool programs (Reynolds et al., 2015; Barr and Gibbs, 2022; García et al., 2021), we additionally examined variation in the associations by offspring intersectional social identities (sex, race, and ethnicity).

Methods

Study design and population

Data for this retrospective cohort study were obtained from the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979 (NLSY79) and linked survey data on the biological children (n = 11,551) of female NLSY79 respondents via the Child and Young Adult Cohort (NLSCYA). NLSY79 and NLSCYA respondents were born 1957–1964 and 1970–2014, respectively. We excluded 1077 NLSCYA respondents whose mother moved to the US after age 5 since exposure was defined by birth county. As done previously (Barr and Gibbs, 2022), our primary analysis further restricted to NLSCYA respondents whose maternal grandmother had less than a high school degree, as this group was more likely eligible for Head Start.

Measures

We defined maternal Head Start exposure by quasi-random county-level variation in Head Start rollout during the 1960's and mothers' year and county of birth (Bailey et al., 2021). Using this natural experiment, we compared outcomes of children born to mothers of different birth cohorts in counties that launched Head Start at different times or not at all by 1970, when all mothers in NLSY79 were age-ineligible (yielding a difference-in-differences comparison). We compared outcomes among offspring whose mothers were exposed to Head Start for any amount of time to those whose mothers were never exposed. Head Start rollout was obtained from the National Archives and Records Administration and Head Start program directories, made available by Bailey and colleagues (Bailey et al., 2021). Mothers' birth year was obtained from public use NLSY79 files, and mothers' birth county was obtained from restricted-use NLSY79 geocode files.

Outcomes were self-reported depression and suicidal ideation among NLSCYA respondents ages 15+ years (Table A.1). Depression was defined as a score of 8+ on the 7-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies scale (Levine, 2013) in any survey from 1994 to 2020. Suicidal ideation was defined as an affirmative response to the question “Have you ever seriously considered attempting suicide?” in any survey from 2012 to 2020 (the only years this question was asked). We excluded 26 people who were never age-eligible to answer the questions.

We examined modification by offspring intersectional social identities—sex, race, and ethnicity—reflecting systemic (dis)advantages. See Table A.1 for details.

Covariates included baseline (1960) characteristics of mothers' birth county, sourced from the US Census and included in Bailey and colleagues' replication files, and individual-level covariates from NLSY79. See Fig. A.1 and Table A.1 for details on covariate selection and definitions.

Statistical analysis

We used a difference-in-differences approach to define our contrast and estimand (i.e., the average treatment effect on the treated) and longitudinal parametric g-computation (sometimes called standardization) for estimation. We estimated how average risk of depression and suicidal ideation among the second generation would change had mothers who were ever exposed to Head Start been counterfactually unexposed. Following Wooldridge, 2023, we used a flexible logistic regression model which avoids recognized problems with traditional two-way fixed effect models for staggered exposures (Wooldridge, 2023). The model included indicators for the year mothers' birth county launched Head Start, mothers' birth year, and Head Start exposure (interaction of county launch and birth cohort), allowing coefficients to differ by exposure intensity (i.e., the number of years mothers were exposed to Head Start from ages 3–5). The primary assumption of our difference-in-differences approach is that birth cohort effects for offspring of unexposed mothers reflect birth cohort effects that offspring of exposed mothers would have experienced in the absence of Head Start exposure (i.e., parallel trends). We plotted event study estimates to assess this assumption (Fig. A.2) and incorporated heterogenous linear trends to correct for potential violation of it (Wooldridge, 2023) (results without heterogenous linear trends are in Fig. A.3). We presented risk ratios (RR) and risk differences (RD) and associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs), calculated with a clustered bootstrap. See the Supplement for additional methodological details.

As a falsification test, we limited to NLSCYA respondents whose maternal grandmother had a high school degree or higher. Since this more highly-educated group was less likely to be in poverty and thus eligible for Head Start, we considered them as a negative control population. That is, we expected them to be exposed to other county-level changes that shaped community health and wellbeing (i.e., confounders) but unexposed to Head Start (as they were largely ineligible to participate). Observing an association between Head Start and outcomes among the negative control population could indicate that primary results were confounded.

Analyses were conducted in R version 4.1.1. NLSY79 and NLSCYA were approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) at the Ohio State University and NORC. This study was considered not human subjects research by the University of Washington IRB since the data were de-identified.

Results

There were 4734 NLSCYA respondents in the primary analytic sample. Approximately 30% of mothers were exposed to Head Start (i.e., lived in a county in which Head Start was available) at some point when they were aged 3–5 years (Table 1). About 50% of offspring were male, one third were Black, and one quarter were White. Overall, 38.2% of offspring reported depression (51.0% among those who remained uncensored), and 9.5% of offspring reported suicidal ideation (13.8% among those who remained uncensored).

Estimates for the overall association between maternal Head Start exposure and offspring depression were not distinguishable from the null (Fig. A.4 A). However, in subgroup analyses, we estimated that female Hispanic/Latina offspring whose mother was exposed to Head Start had 0.79 (95% CI = 0.62, 0.99; Fig. 1A) times the risk of depression than they would have had if their mother was not exposed to Head Start (RD = −14.2 percentage points, 95% CI = −29.1, 0.81), and female White offspring had 0.66 (95% CI = 0.50, 0.87; Fig. 1A) times the risk of depression than they would have had if their mother was not exposed to Head Start (RD = −24.9 percentage points, 95% CI = −43.0, −6.9).

Estimates for the association between maternal Head Start exposure and offspring suicidal ideation were imprecise and not distinguishable from the null, overall (Fig. A.4B) and among intersectional groups (Fig. 1B).

Results of our falsification test (Fig. A.5) support the specificity of primary results, limiting concerns about residual confounding.

Discussion

Findings from this national retrospective cohort study suggest that maternal Head Start exposure meaningfully reduced depression risk among Hispanic/Latina and White female offspring. As these groups tend to report higher risk of depression than several other intersectional subpopulations (Brody et al., 2018; McGuire et al., 2024), results indicate that Head Start could help narrow disparities. For example, nationally representative estimates of US adults from 2024 found that 11.1% of White females and 8.6% of Hispanic/Latina females reported past year major depression, respectively, compared with 7.1% of Black females, 7.0% of White males, 5.3% of Black males, and 5.1% of Hispanic males (SAMHSA Data Tools, 2024).

While no prior studies to our knowledge have examined intergenerational impacts of early childhood education programs on the next generation's mental health, our findings suggesting protective effects for depression are consistent in direction and magnitude with prior studies of other outcomes (Barr and Gibbs, 2022; García et al., 2021; Heckman and Karapakula, 2019; Schanzenbach and Bauer, 2016; Rossin-Slater and Wüst, 2020; Akresh et al., 2018; Mazumder et al., 2019). For example, previous intergenerational studies have estimated that parents' exposure to early childhood education was associated with increases in their offspring's educational attainment (Barr and Gibbs, 2022; Heckman and Karapakula, 2019; Rossin-Slater and Wüst, 2020; Akresh et al., 2018; Mazumder et al., 2019), general health (García et al., 2021), and employment (García et al., 2021; Heckman and Karapakula, 2019), and reductions in their risk of teenage pregnancy (Barr and Gibbs, 2022), school suspension (García et al., 2021; Heckman and Karapakula, 2019), divorce (García et al., 2021), and criminal involvement (Barr and Gibbs, 2022; García et al., 2021; Heckman and Karapakula, 2019). We may have found null results for suicidal ideation—a relatively rare outcome in our sample—because of low power. Protective effects of Head Start could also be muted by mothers' subsequent exposure to adversity, or suicidal ideation could be more strongly explained by proximal context, stressors, and secular trends.

At a time when continued federal funding for Head Start is uncertain, our findings add novel evidence on the potential intergenerational benefits of Head Start, informing ongoing public policy considerations on the value of social policies that invest in the social, economic, and human capital of vulnerable children. For example, recently, some Head Start classrooms and state offices closed due to funding freezes or layoffs, the federal government proposed millions of dollars in cuts to Head Start (which did not pass), and the Department of Health and Human Services reclassified Head Start, making it inaccessible to immigrants (an action being challenged in court). Coupled with the large body of existing evidence, our results suggest that access to high-quality early childhood education should be expanded, not restricted.

This study has limitations. First, there may be residual confounding of Head Start rollout by time-varying factors that differentially affected mothers in counties that did and did not launch Head Start. Further, our approach assumes no unmeasured confounding of censoring and the outcomes. While we adjusted for a range of hypothesized confounders at baseline and follow up, to the degree this assumption is violated, our results may be biased. There may also be exposure misclassification (if mothers' birth county differed from their county of residence during ages 3–5 years, or if they were exposed to Migrant and Seasonal Head Start, which began in 1969) or measurement error in outcomes, modifiers, and covariates (e.g., social desirability bias could affect survey responses). Associations may not generalize to paternal Head Start exposure. The survey only included offspring of female NLSY79 respondents known to the Bureau of Labor Statistics; thus, there may some degree of selection bias which we were unable to account for. We lacked data and sufficient sample size to examine additional racial and ethnic groups, which is an important limitation, for example considering that American Indian and Alaska Native individuals have among the highest burdens of depression and suicide of any racial group in the US. This study also has several strengths. It used data from a large, intergenerationally linked national cohort survey, leveraged a natural experiment, and employed a difference-in-differences analysis with robustness checks to assess and limit confounding.

Conclusions

This national retrospective cohort study estimated that maternal Head Start exposure was associated with a reduced depression risk among Hispanic/Latina and White female offspring. Results advance our understanding of the potential long-term benefits of early childhood education and suggest access to high-quality early childhood education should be expanded.
