Pre-operative deltoid muscle volume predicts post-operative elevation after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty in patients with pre-operative contracture
Authors: Yota Kin, Yuichi Nagase, Kazuya Tamai, Sakae Tanaka, Masashi Naito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) can restore shoulder function in patients with rotator cuff deficiency, it relies heavily on the deltoid muscle. Low muscle volume, particularly in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), may lead to suboptimal outcomes. The purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between pre-operative deltoid muscle volume and post-operative range of shoulder elevation, focusing on the influence of pre-operative joint stiffness.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 45 consecutive patients (49 shoulders) with RA or cuff tear arthropathy (CTA) who underwent rTSA. The mean follow-up period was 30 months. Active and passive range of motion were evaluated pre-operatively and post-operatively. Pre-operative deltoid cross-sectional area was measured on magnetic resonance imaging to calculate the "deltoid index" (area [mm2]/height2 [m2]). Correlations between the deltoid index and post-operative elevation were analyzed, stratified by pre-operative contracture status.ResultsPre-operative shoulder contracture was significantly more frequent in the RA group (59%) than in the CTA group (23%). The deltoid index was significantly smaller in patients with RA compared to those with CTA (738 vs. 946 mm2/m2, P = .0038). The deltoid index showed a strong positive correlation with post-operative active shoulder elevation only in patients with pre-operative contracture (r = 0.61, P = .0020). No significant correlation was observed in patients without contracture.ConclusionPre-operative deltoid muscle volume (deltoid index) was an independent predictor of post-operative shoulder elevation after rTSA, particularly in patients with pre-operative contracture. rTSA may compensate for moderate deltoid atrophy in mobile shoulders, whereas the combination of severe deltoid atrophy and contracture may limit post-operative elevation.Keywords: Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, Deltoid muscle, Rheumatoid arthritis, Joint stiffness, Pre-operative contracture, Prognostic factor

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) is a procedure originally developed to restore shoulder elevation in patients with rotator cuff insufficiency.1,6 By medializing the center of rotation and lengthening the deltoid lever arm, rTSA enables the deltoid muscle to function as the primary elevator of the shoulder.1 Since its introduction by Grammont, rTSA has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes.14 With advancements in implant design and surgical technique, complication rates have declined.14 Consequently, indications have expanded beyond cuff tear arthropathy (CTA) to include rheumatoid arthritis (RA), fracture sequelae, and revision arthroplasty.7,17

Despite these successes, a subset of patients fails to achieve satisfactory post-operative shoulder elevation.3,21 Given that rTSA relies primarily on the deltoid muscle for function, adequate deltoid strength is theoretically a prerequisite for successful elevation. While numerous studies have investigated prognostic factors for poor outcomes, the specific impact of pre-operative deltoid muscle volume remains controversial. Some reports suggest a correlation between deltoid condition and outcomes, while others, such as the study by Wiater et al,22 argue that pre-operative deltoid size does not significantly influence post-operative range of motion (ROM).

One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that the influence of deltoid muscle volume may depend on the pre-operative mechanical environment of the shoulder. In particular, shoulders with pre-operative stiffness or contracture require greater muscular force to overcome passive resistance during elevation. In such conditions, the biomechanical advantage provided by rTSA may be insufficient to compensate for severe deltoid atrophy. Conversely, in mobile shoulders with minimal passive resistance, moderate reductions in deltoid volume may have limited functional impact. This issue is clinically relevant in patient populations in whom both joint stiffness and muscle atrophy frequently coexist, such as those with RA. However, it remains unclear whether pre-operative deltoid muscle volume differentially affects post-operative shoulder elevation depending on the presence of pre-operative contracture.

The purpose of this study was to elucidate the relationship between pre-operative deltoid muscle volume and post-operative range of shoulder elevation after rTSA, with a specific focus on the interplay between muscle volume and pre-operative joint contracture in patients with RA and CTA.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This study included patients who underwent rTSA for RA or CTA at Tokyo Metropolitan Tama Medical Center between July 2014 and September 2018. Patients with neuromuscular diseases, such as Parkinson disease, were excluded due to potential difficulties in adhering to the rehabilitation protocol. We identified 45 consecutive patients (49 shoulders) who met the inclusion criteria. The retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tokyo Metropolitan Tama Medical Center (Approval No. 5-30).

Surgical technique and rehabilitation

All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia combined with an interscalene block, with the patient in the beach-chair position. A deltopectoral approach was utilized. The joint capsule was released circumferentially from the glenoid. Two types of inlay humeral prostheses were used: the Aequalis Reversed (Tornier Inc./Wright Medical, Edina, MN, USA) was used primarily from July 2014 to October 2016, and the Trabecular Metal Reverse Shoulder System (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) was used from August 2016 to September 2018. An exploratory analysis showed that implant type was not significantly associated with post-operative elevation (P = .97), and both systems used a similar inlay humeral design. Post-operatively, the shoulder was immobilized in a sling for 6 weeks. Pendulum exercises were initiated on the first post-operative day. Passive elevation exercises began at 2 weeks, and active elevation exercises were permitted starting at 6 weeks post-operatively.

Clinical evaluation

Active ROM was evaluated pre-operatively and at 6 and 12 months post-operatively. External rotation was measured with the arm at the side. Internal rotation was assessed by the highest vertebral level reached by the thumb and converted to a numerical score based on the Constant score (T7: 10 points; T12: 8 points; L3: 6 points; lumbosacral joint: 4 points; buttock: 2 points; lateral thigh: 0 points). Pre-operative shoulder contracture was defined as passive anterior elevation of less than 100°, based on criteria from previous studies.8,13,20 This threshold is clinically meaningful because a restriction below 100° represents severe passive resistance that significantly increases the muscular demand on the deltoid to achieve functional elevation. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Constant score and the Shoulder36 (Ver. 1.3). The Shoulder36 is a patient-reported outcome measure developed by the Japan Shoulder Society,11 consisting of 36 questions across 6 domains: pain, ROM, muscle strength, general health, activities of daily living, and sports ability. Responses are scored on a 5-point Likert scale, and domain scores are calculated as the average of the items. The Shoulder36 has been validated for reproducibility and consistency.19 In this study, we analyzed 5 domains, excluding sports ability, as the majority of the elderly patients in our cohort did not participate in sports activities.

Radiographic evaluation

The cross-sectional area of the deltoid muscle was measured on pre-operative magnetic resonance imaging. Using T1-weighted axial images, the cross-sectional area was calculated at the level of the base of the coracoid process. Measurements were taken from 3 consecutive slices using DICOM viewer software (ShadeQuest/ViewR version 1.23, Yokogawa Medical Solutions Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and averaged. This specific level was chosen as a consistent anatomical landmark representative of the main muscle belly to ensure measurement reproducibility. All measurements were performed by a single investigator who was blinded to the patients' clinical results. To assess intraobserver reliability, measurements for 10 randomly selected shoulders were repeated after a 2-week interval; the intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.93, demonstrating excellent reliability. To normalize for body size, the deltoid index was calculated by dividing the mean cross-sectional area (mm2) by the square of the patient's body height (m).

Statistical analysis

We retrospectively analyzed pre-operative factors influencing post-operative shoulder elevation at 12 months. To ensure consistency across the cohort, the 12-month post-operative time point was used as the standardized endpoint for the primary ROM analyses. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for parametric data or median (interquartile range) for nonparametric data. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Student t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. To assess the impact of deltoid volume on outcomes, correlations between the deltoid index and post-operative ROM were analyzed using Spearman rank correlation coefficient. A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of post-operative elevation, including contracture status, RA diagnosis, and the deltoid index. The variables included in the multivariable model (pre-operative contracture, RA diagnosis, and the deltoid index) were selected based on their clinical significance as potential drivers of functional recovery and their impact on biomechanical outcomes in previous studies. In addition, sex-based comparisons of the deltoid index and post-operative ROM were performed using Student t-test. To assess potential confounding, sex was also included in a separate multivariable regression model as a sensitivity analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.37 for Windows (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan).10 A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics

Patient demographics are summarized in Table I. The cohort consisted of 29 patients (32 shoulders) with RA and 16 patients (17 shoulders) with CTA. The mean age was 73 ± 6.4 years in the RA group and 76 ± 4.4 years in the CTA group. Consistent with the epidemiological characteristics of the diseases, the proportion of female patients was significantly higher in the RA group (88%) compared to the CTA group (53%; P = .013). The mean follow-up period was 30 months (range, 12-63 months). Regarding sex-based differences in deltoid muscle size, the deltoid index was significantly higher in male patients than in female patients (993 ± 261 vs. 773 ± 234 mm2/m2; P = .0083). The RA group consisted of significantly more female patients than the CTA group (88% vs. 53%; P = .013), which contributed to the overall lower deltoid index observed in the RA cohort. However, no significant difference was observed in post-operative active anterior elevation between sexes (136 ± 28° in males vs. 132 ± 34° in females; P = .68).

Clinical outcomes

Comparison of pre-operative and post-operative ROM in both groups is presented in Table II. Mean active anterior elevation improved significantly from 76 ± 27° to 128 ± 34° in the RA group and from 84 ± 42° to 142 ± 27° in the CTA group (P < .001 for both). Although the RA group exhibited lower mean elevation than the CTA group both pre-operatively and post-operatively, these differences were not statistically significant. In addition, there was no significant difference in post-operative active anterior elevation between the Aequalis Reversed and the Trabecular Metal systems (131 ± 32° vs. 131 ± 34°, P = .97). The Constant score improved significantly in both groups (Fig. 1). While scores in the RA group tended to be lower than in the CTA group, the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .05 pre-operatively; P = .07 post-operatively).

Pre-operative patient status and deltoid volume

The prevalence of pre-operative shoulder contracture (passive elevation <100°) was significantly higher in the RA group (59%, 19 of 32 shoulders) compared to the CTA group (23%, 4 of 17 shoulders) (Table III). Regarding muscle volume, the deltoid index was significantly smaller in patients with RA (738 ± 228 mm2/m2) than in patients with CTA (946 ± 273 mm2/m2, P = .0038) (Fig. 3).

Influence of pre-operative deltoid volume on post-operative elevation

To analyze the influence of deltoid muscle volume on post-operative function, we stratified cases into 2 groups based on the presence of pre-operative contracture. In patients with contracture, the pre-operative deltoid index was strongly correlated with post-operative active elevation ROM (r = 0.61, P = .0020). Conversely, in patients without contracture, no correlation was found between the deltoid index and post-operative elevation (r = 0.07, P = .85) (Fig. 4). Furthermore, severe deltoid atrophy was not observed in the noncontracture group.

Predictors of post-operative elevation

Multiple regression analysis was performed to determine independent predictors of post-operative elevation (Table IV). The model included the presence of contracture, diagnosis of RA, and the deltoid index. The analysis revealed that the deltoid index was the only significant predictor of post-operative elevation (estimate: 0.046, P = .038). This suggests that deltoid muscle volume is a critical factor for achieving satisfactory shoulder elevation after rTSA. In a separate multivariable regression model that included sex as a covariate, the deltoid index remained the only significant independent predictor of post-operative elevation (estimate: 0.046, P = .038).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that pre-operative deltoid muscle volume is a significant predictor of post-operative active anterior elevation after rTSA, specifically in shoulders with pre-operative contracture. Crucially, this correlation was observed only in shoulders with pre-operative contracture. In patients without contracture, deltoid volume did not significantly influence post-operative elevation.

Previous literature has identified several risk factors for poor post-operative elevation, including female, nondominant extremity, and low pre-operative Constant score.3,9,18 However, the specific role of deltoid muscle volume has been controversial. Wiater et al reported that the pre-operative deltoid size did not affect post-operative ROM while it correlated with clinical scores. They concluded that appropriate deltoid lengthening is more critical than muscle volume itself.22 Our findings offer a potential explanation for this discrepancy, suggesting that the impact of deltoid volume is dependent on the pre-operative joint status.

The biomechanical advantage provided by rTSA typically allows patients to elevate the arm even with moderate deltoid atrophy, provided the joint is mobile. In such cases, the implant's design effectively compensates for muscle weakness. However, in the presence of stiffness or contracture, the mechanical force required to overcome passive resistance and elevate the arm is significantly higher. In these scenarios, a severely atrophied deltoid muscle may lack the necessary power to achieve functional elevation, even with the mechanical benefits of rTSA. This mechanism explains why the deltoid index was strongly correlated with outcomes in our contracture group but not in the noncontracture group.

In particular, this finding is clinically significant for patients with RA. Our data showed that the RA group had a significantly higher prevalence of pre-operative contracture and a lower deltoid muscle volume than the CTA group. Chronic synovitis in RA leads to joint destruction, capsular stiffness, and disuse atrophy of the surrounding musculature.12 Historically, earlier studies reported higher complication rates and inferior outcomes for rTSA in patients with RA compared to those with CTA.7,16,23,24 Consequently, patients with RA represent a high-risk cohort where the functional reserve for deltoid atrophy is reduced due to concomitant stiffness. Although recent systematic reviews suggest that short- and midterm outcomes of rTSA in RA are generally comparable to those in CTA,2,4,15 our study highlights that functional restoration may be compromised in severe cases with both contracture and atrophy. This potential for suboptimal outcomes is reflected in the lower post-operative Shoulder36 scores observed in our RA group compared to the CTA group; baseline patient-reported outcomes were also lower in the RA group.

Clinically, these results underscore the necessity of comprehensive pre-operative assessment. In patients with significant stiffness, defined as passive anterior elevation <100°, the status of the deltoid muscle becomes a critical prognostic indicator. Surgeons should be cautious in counseling patients with stiff shoulders and severe deltoid atrophy, as rTSA may not fully restore elevation in this specific subgroup.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Specifically, the small number of male patients in our cohort may have limited the power of subgroup analyses regarding sex-related differences. Second, the retrospective design carries inherent biases. Third, the deltoid index used in this study is a practical surrogate of deltoid muscle size based on single-level axial magnetic resonance imaging cross-sectional measurements and does not directly quantify 3-dimensional muscle volume. Although intraobserver reliability was excellent in a subset analysis, muscle quality (eg, fatty infiltration) and interobserver reliability were not assessed. Therefore, our analysis reflects deltoid muscle quantity rather than a combined assessment of muscle quantity and quality. Furthermore, it has been reported that functional results of rTSA may deteriorate after 6-8 years, potentially due to deltoid fatigue.5,7 Although our previous study demonstrated that clinical scores in RA patients were maintained for at least 4 years,13 careful long-term follow-up is necessary, particularly for RA patients with low muscle volume who may be susceptible to earlier fatigue. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and qualitative muscle assessment are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, pre-operative deltoid muscle size (deltoid index) was an independent predictor of post-operative shoulder elevation after rTSA, particularly in patients with pre-operative shoulder contracture. While rTSA is an effective procedure for restoring function, it may not fully compensate for severe deltoid atrophy when combined with substantial joint stiffness. Our findings suggest that rTSA may compensate for moderate deltoid atrophy in mobile shoulders; however, the combination of severe atrophy and contracture represents a clinical limitation in which the prosthesis alone may not overcome the resistance of a stiff joint to achieve full functional elevation.
