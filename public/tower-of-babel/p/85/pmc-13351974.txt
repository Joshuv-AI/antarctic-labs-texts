Quantifying the effect of magnesium ions on nucleosome stability in chromatin fibers
Authors: Luuk JC Daris, Lieke A Lamers, Kes van Blitterswijk, Camila Delgado Zuvanic, Jorine M Eeftens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChromatin structure governs DNA accessibility and thereby a wide range of DNA-mediated processes. Magnesium ions are known to influence chromatin structure across organizational scales, but a thorough quantification of the effect on nucleosomes in the context of individual chromatin fibers is currently lacking. Using single-molecule force spectroscopy, we determined how Mg2+ affects nucleosome stability in reconstituted chromatin fibers by assessing two force regimes that represent distinct phases of force-induced fiber unfolding. We found that Mg2+ destabilizes the nucleosome inner turn in the extended conformation of the chromatin fiber. In contrast, Mg2+ stabilizes compacted chromatin fibers in the low-force regime, indicating stabilization of the nucleosome outer turns. Taken together, these results reveal that Mg2+ has differential effects on the inner and outer nucleosomal turn.

Significance

The ionic environment plays a crucial role in genome organization across scales. Although the significance of Mg2+ on genome compaction has long been recognized, its effect on nucleosome stability within individual chromatin fibers remains incompletely understood. Here, we examine the effect of Mg2+ on mechanical stability of nucleosomes in reconstituted chromatin fibers using single-molecule force spectroscopy. We found that the effect of Mg2+ ions on nucleosome stability is dependent on the degree of nucleosomal DNA unwrapping. These results highlight that the conformation of nucleosomes within chromatin fibers is an important consideration in assessing its response to biological cues.

Introduction

In order to accommodate the genome inside the confined space of the nucleus, eukaryotic organisms have evolved chromatin to organize and compact their genetic material. The fundamental repeating units of chromatin are nucleosomes, consisting of an octamer of histone proteins that assemble into a spool-like structure through tight association with ∼147 bp of DNA. This packing serves an inherently repressive role by compacting the DNA and decreasing its accessibility to DNA binding proteins (1). The nucleosome thus plays a pivotal role in achieving the high level of control required for the coordination of a wide range of nuclear processes including replication, transcription and DNA repair (2,3). Chromatin is not a static or inert structure, but rather an instructive DNA scaffold that dynamically responds to external signals, thereby maintaining genome integrity while regulating its accessibility (4).

Electrostatic interactions are essential in the formation and organization of the chromatin fiber. The negatively charged DNA binds to positively charged residues on the surface of the histone octamer to assemble nucleosomes, which are connected by short segments of linker DNA to form the chromatin fiber. Association of the histone octamer with DNA only partially neutralizes the DNA’s negative charge, and together with the linker DNA, the chromatin fiber thus retains a substantial net negative charge (5,6). Consequently, electrostatic repulsion between different regions of the fiber promotes an extended conformation under low-salt conditions. Cations can shield the repulsive electrostatic interactions of the DNA, so the stability of increasingly compacted chromatin states is strongly influenced by the ionic environment (7,8,9,10,11). At high concentrations, monovalent cations can compact chromatin to a limited degree, resulting in fibers that are not folded in an orderly manner (12,13,14). Compared with monovalent cations, divalent cations have a higher charge density, which generally allows them to interact with greater affinity and specificity with macromolecules (15). This results in preferential association of divalent ions and increased charge screening of DNA via ion-ion correlation effects (16,17).

The effect of Mg2+ on condensation of the chromatin fiber is evident across organizational scales. For instance, it has been demonstrated that the mean diameter of isolated nuclei increases depending on the Mg2+ concentration, as a direct consequence of oligomerization and clustering of chromatin fibers (7). Furthermore, a transient rise of free Mg2+ during mitosis contributes to chromosome condensation, highlighting the functional importance of Mg2+-mediated genome compaction on the level of chromosomes (18). In line with this, physiological levels of ∼1 mM Mg2+ induce a drastic compaction of individual chromatin fibers, as shown for chromatin fibers with different sizes and folding geometries (19,20,21). This effect is far beyond the condensation state achievable with elevated levels of monovalent cations (8,22). This raises the question of how this divalent cation modulates higher-order compaction through mechanisms beyond screening the electrostatic repulsion of the DNA backbone.

Although the role of Mg2+ ions in chromatin compaction is recognized, a thorough quantification of its effects on the level of nucleosomes in the context of chromatin fibers is currently lacking. To better understand this, we probe the effect of Mg2+ on individual chromatin fibers with force spectroscopy using dual optical tweezers. This allows direct quantification of the effect of Mg2+ on the stability of chromatin fibers in different force regimes, it enables reversibility, and it provides high spatial and temporal resolution. We distinguish how Mg2+ influences the stability of chromatin by assessing two force regimes that correspond to inner and outer turn unwrapping of nucleosomal DNA. We find that the nucleosome inner turn is destabilized by Mg2+. In contrast, Mg2+ stabilizes the outer turn. We conclude that the electrostatic effects of cations on chromatin vary depending on changes in nucleosomal conformation upon fiber stretching.

Materials and methods

Generation of 12 × 601 plasmids

To generate the 12 × 601 plasmid with 100-bp linkers (NRL247), four different 601 fragments were created by PCR amplification of the 601 segment of a donor DNA template (FM5_601_stdLinker_mCh) using four distinct sets of In-Fusion primers, containing a homology arm for either the cloning backbone or another 601 repeat. 601 amplicon A connects to the empty pSuperCos backbone downstream of the MscI site. Amplicon B connects seamlessly to amplicon A and restores the upstream MscI site for subsequent cloning reactions. This created a 2 × 601 vector that was used as the backbone for the following reaction, which utilizes 601 amplicon C to connect to the MscI-digested 2 × 601 vector and amplicon B to connect to amplicon C and again restore the MscI site. This cycle was repeated until a 10 × 601 vector was obtained, incorporating two 601 repeats for each successive cloning reaction. Cloning reactions were conducted using the In-Fusion Snap Assembly master mix (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. To decrease the size of the final 12 × 601 NRL247 plasmid, the 10 × 601 NRL247 vector was digested with MscI and NdeI to enable insertion of an additional amplicon C and an amplicon D that closes the backbone at the NdeI restriction site, effectively replacing the NdeI to MscI segment (1131–12,870; 11.7 kb) of the 10 × 601 vector with two additional 601 repeats. To ensure that only the linker length varied between the two 12 × 601 constructs, pSuperCos was also used as the cloning backbone for the 12 × 601 plasmid with 20-bp linkers (NRL167). To prepare the backbone for cloning, it was digested with MscI and NdeI (10 U/μg), and the MscI to NdeI fragment (12,870–1131; 8.9 kb) was isolated from an agarose gel (0.7% in 1xTAE). The complete 12 × 601 fragment was obtained by PCR amplification from pWM_12 × 601_20bpLinker (Addgene, plasmid #157787) using In-Fusion primers containing homology arms suitable for replacement of the NdeI (1131) to MscI segment (1131–12,870; 11.7 kb) of the pSuperCos backbone with the 12 × 601 amplicon. All DNA isolations and gel purifications were performed with the NucleoSpin Gel & PCR Clean-up (MACHEREY-NAGEL). Whole plasmid sequencing was performed by Plasmidsaurus using Oxford Nanopore Technology.

Chromatin reconstitution

Template DNA for chromatin reconstitution was linearized with BssHII (10 U/μg), and the 5′ overhangs were filled with dGTP and biotin-16-dCTP using Klenow fragment (Jena Bioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Linearization with BssHII yields DNA handles approximately equal in size (∼4.1 and 4.8 kb), with sufficient spacing between the nucleosomal array and the bead surface. Chromatin reconstitution was performed by salt gradient dialysis in 10K MWCO Slide-A-Lyzer MINI Dialysis Devices (Thermo Fisher) using a multiheaded peristaltic pump (Masterflex) based on previously established protocols (23). Reconstitution mixtures contained 0.179 μM 601 DNA in addition to recombinant human histone octamers (EpiCypher, 16-0001) with increasing molar ratios of histone octamer to 601 DNA. To determine the degree of saturation of nucleosome assembly at the 601 sequences, an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was conducted. In brief, reconstituted chromatin samples were digested with DdeI and NdeI (10 U/μg). Glycerol was added to a final concentration of 5%, and the samples were run on an agarose gel (0.6% agarose in 0.2× TB) for 45 min at 150 V to determine the optimal octamer/601 ratio, which was typically around 1.5. The number of assembled nucleosomes varied between 9 and 14 but centered around 12. Nucleosomal arrays were stored at 4°C for up to 6 weeks to ensure consistent sample quality.

Single-molecule force spectroscopy

Force spectroscopy experiments were performed using dual optical traps in a multichannel laminar flow cell connected to a microfluidics system (C-trap, LUMICKS). Before all measurements, the flow cell was cleaned and subsequently passivated using 20 mM Hepes-NaOH (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% (w/v) casein, and 0.01% (w/v) BSA. Measurements were performed in a buffer containing 20 mM Hepes-NaOH (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 0.2% (w/v) BSA, and 0.02% (v/v) Tween 20, supplemented with the indicated concentration of MgCl2. To perform force spectroscopy measurements, two streptavidin-functionalized polystyrene beads with a diameter of 1.76 μm (Spherotech BV) were optically trapped, and a force calibration was conducted using a power spectrum. Biotinylated nucleosomal arrays were flow-stretched and tethered between the trapped beads, and tether formation was verified by gentle displacement of one of the traps until a force of maximum 3 pN was registered in order not to disrupt potential stacking interactions. Before each measurement, the force offset was set to 0 pN. Force spectroscopy measurements were performed by moving the trap at a constant velocity of 20 nm/s. Forces on the stationary bead were registered using back focal plane detection of scattered optical trapping light, whereas distance is calculated from the brightfield camera image. The stiffness of both traps along the direction of trap movement was ∼0.40 pN/nm.

Data analysis and statistics

Data analysis was performed in Python (version 3.9.7) with custom-written scripts using the lumicks.pylake package (version 1.2.0). The area under the curve was determined by numerical integration (SciPy Simpson). Worm-like chains were fitted using Odijk’s extensible worm-like chain model (24). To obtain average stretching curves in the low-force regime, distance data were interpolated as a function of force. The number of nucleosomes per tether was counted by the discrete steps for inner turn unwrapping in the high-force regime. ΔF step distributions were obtained by downsampling raw data to 1 Hz and calculating the difference between each datapoint as described previously (25). Data between 1.5 and 3.5 pN were fitted to a Hookean spring, and spring constants were determined from the slope of the fit. For all statistical analyses, data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data sets were compared using Student's t-tests, whereas nonnormally distributed data sets were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to compare the distribution in ΔF steps. In all cases, the threshold of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Mg2+ affects the contour length of DNA

To study the effect of Mg2+ on the stability of reconstituted chromatin, we first created a suitable DNA template with 12 tandem repeats of the 601 nucleosome position sequence. The 601 sequence as described by Widom and Lowary contains a 147-bp core sequence that associates with the core histone octamer (26). Here, “601” refers to this 601 core sequence only. In-Fusion cloning was used to construct a plasmid containing twelve 601 sequences spaced by 100-bp linkers, resulting in a nucleosome repeat length (NRL) of 247 bp. The relatively long linker DNA in NRL247 is presumed to prevent nucleosomal interactions (“stacking”) within chromatin fibers. The plasmid was digested, and the 5′ overhangs were biotinylated. Linearization was performed such that the nucleosome position sequences were approximately in the middle, flanked by relatively long DNA handles (∼4.1 and 4.8 kb) (Fig. 1 A). Force spectroscopy experiments were performed in a flow cell connected to a microfluidics system in which streptavidin-coated polystyrene beads were optically captured within the two traps. Biotinylated DNA was tethered between the beads and stretched by displacement of one of the lasers. The elastic response was measured in either the presence or absence of 2 mM Mg2+. Both buffers contain monovalent cations (>100 mM Na+).

Effect of Mg2+ on the mechanical properties of DNA. (A) Schematic of DNA construct generation for single-molecule force spectroscopy. The 12 × 601 template DNA was linearized and biotinylated, which enables tethering between streptavidin-functionalized beads. (B) Averaged force-distance curves of DNA in the absence (0 mM, n = 27) or presence (2 mM, n = 72) of Mg2+, obtained by interpolation of the recorded forces. (C) Mg2+ causes a minor but statistically significant reduction in the contour length of DNA (Student's t-test, p < 0.001, n = 27 and n = 72 for 0 mM and 2 mM, respectively). (D) Persistence length remains unaffected by Mg2+ (Student's t-test, ns, n = 27 and n = 72 for 0 and 2 mM, respectively). The box shows the quartiles of the data set, and whiskers show distribution excluding outliers.

The averaged force-distance curves show a qualitative difference in the trajectory of the traces between the two conditions, indicating that Mg2+ affects the physical properties of DNA (Fig. 1 B). The data of each force-distance curve was fit to Odijk’s extensible worm-like chain model (eWLC) to quantify the effect of Mg2+ on DNA (24,27). It was found that Mg2+ significantly decreases the contour length (Lc) of dsDNA, indicating that Mg2+ slightly compresses the DNA helix (Fig. 1 C), which is in accordance with prior studies on larger TAD-sized reconstituted chromatin fibers (20). In contrast, Mg2+ did not affect the persistence length (Lp), suggesting that the bending stiffness of DNA remains similar (Fig. 1 D). Together, these results indicate that Mg2+ primarily modulates DNA mechanics by slightly decreasing the spacing between basepairs while leaving the structural flexibility unchanged under these experimental conditions.

Elastic response of reconstituted chromatin fibers

To study the effect of Mg2+ on the stability of nucleosomal arrays, chromatin fibers were reconstituted by salt gradient dialysis (23) (Fig. 2 A). 12 × 601 Template DNA is mixed with recombinant histone octamers in a high-salt buffer. Upon gradual decrease of the ionic strength, nucleosomes spontaneously assemble on the nucleosome positioning sequences in stepwise fashion. The (H3-H4)2 tetramers bind the DNA first, and upon further reduction of the salt concentration, two H2A-H2B dimers associate with the tetramers to form full nucleosomes. Because this reaction critically depends on the molar ratio of histone octamers to 601 DNA, reconstitutions were performed in a titration series, aiming for complete saturation of the 601 sequences without nucleosome assembly on the DNA handles. The degree of saturation was determined with an EMSA, and the optimal ratio was selected for single-molecule force spectroscopy (Fig. 2 B).

Assembly of nucleosomal arrays for single-molecule force spectroscopy. (A) Schematic of chromatin reconstitution. Recombinant human histone octamers were assembled on the 601 sequences by salt gradient dialysis. (B) Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to determine the degree of saturation of nucleosome assembly on the 601 sequences. The reconstituted chromatin fibers were digested with NdeI and DdeI to liberate the 12 × 601 segment (upper band, lanes 2–8) and further digest the DNA handles (other bands). The shift in the 12 × 601 segment indicates an increase in nucleosome assembly on the 601 sequences with increasing octamer:601 ratio until it plateaus, indicating saturation. A shift in the electrophoretic mobility of the DNA handles at high octamer:601 ratios indicates off-target nucleosome assembly. (C) Typical force response of a 12-mer nucleosomal array at >100 mM NaCl, in absence of Mg2+. Chromatin fibers were tethered between streptavidin-coated polystyrene beads and stretched by gradual displacement of one of the optical traps in a dual optical trap setup. The force response of chromatin can be divided into a low- and high-force regime, which is attributed to unstacking and outer turn unwrapping, and inner turn unwrapping, respectively.

The behavior of nucleosomal arrays under applied force is characterized by multiple phases that correspond to distinct features of chromatin fiber unfolding (Fig. 2 C). To capture every organizational signature in detail, fibers were stretched from 0 to 45 pN with a modest velocity of 20 nm/s. The force response of compacted chromatin fibers in the low-force regime is generally attributed to outer turn unwrapping and, depending on NRL, the disruption of internucleosomal stacking interactions (28,29,30). For forces between 10 and 30 pN (further referred to as the high-force regime), discrete steps signify unwrapping of the inner turn of nucleosomal DNA from the histone octamer periphery (31).

Mg2+ decreases inner turn stability

After establishing a reliable chromatin template, we investigated the influence of Mg2+ on the stability of the nucleosome inner turn by measuring the force response of nucleosomal arrays in the presence or absence of 2 mM Mg2+, again in a background of monovalent salt. In the high-force regime, inner turn unwrapping events are characterized by discrete steps in the force-distance curve that are reflected by a sudden decrease in the recorded force, correlated with an increase in extension of the fiber (31) (Fig. 3 A). This allows for counting of the number of nucleosomes per fiber and quantification of their respective rupture forces (Figs. 3 B and S1). Inner turn unwrapping forces increased for each successive unwrapping event, in line with the noncooperative nature of the process and reflecting the predicted statistical effect (31,32,33). Although this trend is similar in the presence and absence of Mg2+, the unwrapping forces are reduced significantly by Mg2+. This difference is present for the first unwrapping event and remains similar for each consecutive step, showing that Mg2+ facilitates inner turn unwrapping.

Mg2+ destabilizes the nucleosome inner turn for NRL247 chromatin fibers. (A) Unwrapping of the nucleosome inner turn is characterized by discrete steps in the elastic response of chromatin fibers. Differences in contour length of eWLCs (gray dashed lines) demonstrate ∼76 bp of unwrapped DNA per step. (B) The force required to unwrap the nucleosome inner turns is decreased significantly in the presence of 2 mM Mg2+ (n = 20 for 0 mM, n = 13 for 2 mM, Student's t-test per nucleosome: p < 0.05 for nucleosome 1–9, ns for nucleosome 10–12). Error bars represent SEM. (C) Force-distance curves depicting five stretch-relax cycles illustrate that a fraction of the disrupted nucleosomes is able to reform. (D) Nucleosome reassembly after repeated stretch-relax cycles is decreased in the presence of 2 mM Mg2+ (n = 15 for 2 mM, n = 14 for 0 mM. Student's t-test per cycle, ns for cycle 3 and 4, p < 0.05 for cycle 2 and 5). Error bars represent SEM.

Previous studies have reported nucleosome rewrapping upon repeated stretch-relax cycles, with the fraction of remaining nucleosomes per fiber decreasing after each cycle (31,32,34,35,36). To investigate the effect of Mg2+ on nucleosome reassembly, five stretch-relax cycles were conducted where the arrays were expanded to 40 pN and contracted back to 0.25 pN with velocities of 50 nm/s and 200 nm/s, respectively (Fig. 3 C). Nucleosome rewrapping was observed both in the presence and absence of Mg2+, with the number of nucleosomes per fiber decreasing after each cycle. However, we found that the presence of Mg2+ marginally reduces nucleosome reassembly (Fig. 3 D). After five cycles, ∼25% of nucleosomes were reformed in 2 mM Mg2+, whereas this was ∼40% in the absence of Mg2+. Since the outer turn is unable to reform independently from the inner turn, we assume this effect is due to the inability of the inner turn to rewrap, in agreement with the destabilization of the inner turn observed in Fig. 3 B.

Mg2+ increases stability of chromatin fibers in the low-force regime

Next, we quantified the effect of Mg2+ on the force response of the compacted chromatin fiber in the low-force regime (Figs. 4 A and S2 A). In contrast to the high-force regime, chromatin fibers can be repeatedly unfolded and refolded in the low-force regime without notable hysteresis, as this process is reversible (29,31) (Fig. S2 B). Since individual unwrapping steps cannot be distinguished for unwrapping of the nucleosome outer turns, we instead determine the work required to stretch the chromatin fiber in the low-force regime as a measure of fiber stability (between 0.5 and 5 pN (green area), Fig. 4 B). The presence of Mg2+ significantly increases the work required to stretch the fibers up to 5 pN, thereby stabilizing compacted chromatin (Fig. 4 C). This aligns with previous findings showing that Mg2+ induces significant compaction of chromatin fibers, whereas the underlying DNA remains largely unaffected (20). When we quantify the energy required to stretch DNA in the same force range, we find a reduction in energy in 2 mM Mg2+ when compared with monovalent salt only (Fig. S2 C).

Similarities in the trajectories imply a comparable unfolding mechanism. (B) Representative force-distance curve of a NRL247 nucleosomal array depicting stretching of the compacted chromatin fiber in the low-force regime. The work required for stretching as a measure of chromatin fiber stability was obtained by determining the area under the curve (AUC, green area). (C) The work required to stretch the chromatin fiber is significantly higher in 2 mM Mg2+ (Student's t-test, p < 0.001). The box shows the quartiles of the data set, and whiskers show distribution excluding outliers.

NRL is a key factor for the compaction of nucleosomal arrays. Fibers with long NRLs require linker histones to achieve maximum compaction (37,38,39,40,41), whereas NRLs of 187 bp or less can achieve maximum compaction independently of linker histones through nucleosome stacking. These nucleosome-nucleosome stacking interactions are also disrupted at low forces. In our case, the chromatin fibers with 100-bp linkers are presumably devoid of such stacking interactions, suggesting that the observed effect of Mg2+ in the low-force regime can be attributed to stabilization of the nucleosome outer turns. These results show that Mg2+ stabilizes compacted chromatin in the low-force regime via the outer turn, whereas it significantly decreases inner turn stability.

The Effect of Mg2+ is NRL independent

To investigate the extent to which the observed effects of Mg2+ on chromatin fiber stability depend on NRL, force spectroscopy measurements were repeated on fibers with 20-bp linker DNA (NRL167) between adjacent nucleosomes. As expected, the presence of Mg2+ reduced rupture forces in the high-force regime, confirming that it decreases the stability of the nucleosome inner turn (Fig. 5 A). This is consistent with an extended conformation of chromatin in the high-force regime, where the fiber resembles a repetitive sequence of partially unwrapped mononucleosomes, rendering the effect of Mg2+ independent of NRL.

Mg2+ reduces inner turn unwrapping forces and increases chromatin fiber stability in the low-force regime for NRL167, similar to NRL247. (A) The force associated with unwrapping of the nucleosome inner turns is decreased significantly in the presence of 2 mM Mg2+, indicating that Mg2+ destabilizes the inner turn (n = 26 for 0 mM, n = 25 for 2 mM, Student's t-test per nucleosome: p < 0.05 for nucleosome 1–9, ns for nucleosome 10–12). Error bars represent SEM. (B) Interpolation of distance data yields average force-distance curves between 0.5 and 5 pN for 2 mM Mg2+ (n = 25) and 0 mM Mg2+ (n = 26). Differences in the trajectories of the averaged force-distance curves indicate variation in the unfolding mechanism of chromatin fibers between the two conditions. (C) The work required to stretch the chromatin fiber is significantly higher in 2 mM Mg2+ (Student's t-test, p < 0.01). The box shows the quartiles of the data set, and whiskers show distribution excluding outliers.

We next quantified the work required to stretch the chromatin fiber in the low-force regime by determining the area under the curve as described before (Fig. 5 B). Similar to NRL247 fibers, the work was increased significantly in the presence of 2 mM Mg2+ (Fig. 5 C), reiterating the earlier finding that Mg2+ stabilizes compacted chromatin fibers in the low-force regime. However, as the 20-bp linker effectively supports stacking interactions, the topology of these fibers is likely different from NRL247 fibers (28,29,42,43). The force-induced unfolding in the low-force regime is likely composed of outer turn unwrapping and unstacking events. The force plateau at ∼4 pN in the force-distance curve of NRL167 fibers in 2 mM Mg2+ is assumed to be indicative of the disruption of stacking interactions (28,29,30,42,44) (Figs. 5 B and S3B). Note that such a plateau is absent for NRL247 fibers (Figs. 4 A and S3 A), with NRL247 curves showing nearly linear behavior for forces up to ∼3.5 pN.

To quantify these differences in shape, force-distance curves were downsampled to a frequency of 1 Hz. As fibers are stretched with constant velocity, each second corresponds to an increase of ∼20 nm in distance. The ΔF corresponding to each step was then determined, reasoning that increased stiffness followed by a plateau (as for NRL167) would result in a wider distribution of ΔF steps than a steady incline (as for NRL247). Indeed, comparing the step distribution between NRL247 and NRL167 shows a broader distribution for NRL167, reflecting the wider variety in step sizes (Fig. S3 C). We furthermore approximated the elastic behavior before the force plateau as a Hookean spring (29), where the slope of a linear fit is the spring constant k. Comparing spring constants across conditions showed that the spring constant was larger for NRL167 fibers than for NRL247 both in the absence and presence of 2 mM Mg2+, with a greater difference in the presence of Mg2+ (Fig. S3, D–G). Although chromatin topology cannot be definitively inferred from these curves, these data suggest that the quantified variations of the elastic response are indicative of underlying differences in structure. Nevertheless, this confirms that Mg2+ increases chromatin fiber stability when folded, whereas the stability of the partially unwrapped nucleosome in the extended conformation of the fiber is compromised by Mg2+.

Discussion

Single-molecule force spectroscopy is a valuable tool to study chromatin structure and stability. Here, we dissect the role of Mg2+ on the force-induced unfolding of chromatin fibers. We found that the forces corresponding to inner turn unwrapping are generally reduced in the presence of Mg2+, independent of linker length. In contrast, Mg2+ stabilizes compacted chromatin fibers in the low-force regime, by stabilization of the nucleosome outer turns, facilitation of stacking interactions, or both.

We find that Mg2+ decreases the contour length of DNA, implying that electrostatic screening leads to slight compaction of the molecule. In this low-force regime, the applied work primarily compensates for entropic resistance arising from thermal bending fluctuations. Notably, once nucleosomes are introduced, an increase in the work required to stretch NRL247 fibers in the low-force regime indicates that Mg2+ stabilizes the nucleosome outer turn. The stabilization of the outer turn is in line with AFM work showing that nucleosomes are more frequently fully wrapped in presence of Mg2+ (45). Although histone proteins are positively charged, fully wrapped nucleosomes remain highly negatively charged (46). In fully wrapped nucleosomes, the two gyres of DNA are in close proximity and only partially neutralized by positive charges on the histone proteins. Together with linker DNA, this causes electrostatic repulsion that necessitates screening in folded chromatin fibers (47). Even in a background of monovalent salt, the addition of Mg2+ strongly contributes to this screening and thus provides mechanical stability in the folded state, thereby increasing the work required to stretch compacted fibers in the low-force regime. As chromatin is stretched, nucleosomal DNA gradually unwraps, and the fiber becomes more extended. Consequently, the net charge of the nucleosome progressively increases as negatively charged DNA dissociates. When the outer turn is unwrapped, the nucleosomes have a nearly neutral net charge (48) and are more distantly spaced. Further unwrapping is highly unfavorable, as it exposes both the positively charged histones and the negatively charged DNA, resulting in a large increase in the electrostatic free energy (6). Mg2+ mediates unwrapping of the remaining DNA from the histone octamer periphery by stabilization of the exposed inner turn DNA, resulting in a decrease in the associated rupture forces, consistent with observations on mononucleosomes (5,49). The electrostatics involved in changes of chromatin fiber conformation thus explain the differential response.

We demonstrate that Mg2+ has similar effects on the high-force and low-force regime for NRL247 and NRL167. NRL167 is a commonly used NRL that has been proposed to effectively support stacking interactions (28,29,42,43). In contrast, the longer linker DNA presumably limits stacking in the NRL247 fibers. These assumption are broadly supported by studies on multiple scales of chromatin organization and include x-ray crystallography (43), EM (37,40), AU (39,40), and magnetic tweezers (28,29,30,42). Previous single-molecule work showed that interactions between the H4 tail and the acidic patch on the H2A-H2B dimer are key mediators of nucleosome stacking for NRL167 (42,50). This interaction is likely limited in NRL247 fibers as a consequence of the increased nucleosome spacing, thereby impeding stacking interactions. Accordingly, the effect of deletion of the H4 tail has been shown to be NRL dependent (30). Although we cannot definitively distinguish with our assay, the differences in shape of the unwrapping curves (most notably the presence of a force plateau for NRL167 at 2 mM Mg2+) are indicative of different topologies. Under this assumption, our results suggest that stability of both stacked and unstacked chromatin fibers is affected by Mg2+, indicating that Mg2+ affects both internucleosomal interactions and outer turn stability.

We quantified differences in the low-force regime based on the signature of our experimental force-distance curves, determining that 0.5–5 pN encompassed all relevant events. If we run the same analysis based on cutoffs in distance, still covering relevant low-force events, we recover the same conclusions (Fig. S4, A and B). In addition, different cutoffs for the low-force regime have been used in literature, generally ranging between 0.1 and 10 pN (28,29,31,42,51). To ensure our conclusions were not dependent on this selection of parameters, we repeated our analysis with different cutoffs and reproduced the same differences (Fig. S4, C–F). The analysis controls indicate our conclusions are not dependent on the selected regime. Calculating the energy required for unfolding per nucleosome after subtracting the work required for stretching DNA yields ∼8 kBT per nucleosome, in excellent agreement with earlier simulation work (30).

In conclusion, we found that Mg2+ differentially affects chromatin fiber stability depending on the phase of unfolding in terms of fiber conformation. How histone posttranslational modifications, linker histone H1, or chromatin remodelers influence this interplay is still to be explored. Importantly, future work should consider that irregular nucleosome spacing, as common in vivo, likely promotes heterogeneous folding and clustering, rather than uniform stacking (20). In addition, rigorous testing of the effect of mixtures of cations could better clarify how physiological ionic conditions influence chromatin organization in the nucleus. For instance, K+ and Na+ have been shown to have diverging effects on chromatin folding and association between fibers (21). Chromatin condensation involves not only longitudinal fiber folding, but also interactions between fibers, and recent evidence highlights the role of Mg2+ and linker length in the formation of phase-separated chromatin condensates (52,53). The lack of regular stacking interactions could facilitate a more liquid-like higher-order organization, with highly irregular nucleosome packing (54). A wide range of nucleosome configurations can exist, suggesting that chromatin structure might be dynamic and nonuniform even on the nucleosome level (55,56). Integrating findings across scales will be crucial to advance our understanding of how eukaryotic cells modulate chromatin structure.
