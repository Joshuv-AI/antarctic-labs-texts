Bilateral atypical femoral fractures following unsupervised long-term alendronate use: a case report
Authors: Elaheh Mianehsaz, Seyed Mohammadreza Tabatabaee, Golnaz Shafiei, Seyed Hamidreza Akhavan Hejazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe report a case of bilateral atypical femoral fractures in a 70-year-old Asian (Iranian) woman following 15 years of unsupervised alendronate use for osteoporosis. Although bisphosphonates are widely used to reduce fracture risk, long-term therapy without periodic reassessment may result in serious complications such as atypical femoral fractures (AFFs).Case presentationA 70-year-old Asian (Iranian) woman presented with bilateral atypical femoral fractures following 15 years of unsupervised alendronate use for osteoporosis. This case is notable because the medication was obtained through a pharmacist family member without specialist monitoring or consideration of drug holidays. Additionally, the patient deliberately avoided calcium supplementation due to concerns about constipation and kidney stones, which may have further compromised bone health. Mild prodromal symptoms were initially misattributed to age-related conditions, and radiographic evaluation was delayed until complete fractures occurred.ConclusionUltimately, further clinical follow-up was not feasible due to the patient’s relocation to another city and subsequent unavailability. This case illustrates the risks of unsupervised long-term bisphosphonate therapy and underscores the need for structured prescription monitoring, patient education on balanced supplementation, and early imaging of prodromal symptoms.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a prevalent chronic condition characterized by reduced bone mineral density (BMD) and increased fracture risk, particularly in elderly postmenopausal women [1]. Bisphosphonates, such as alendronate, are first-line treatments that effectively reduce the incidence of vertebral and hip fractures [2]. However, long-term use may suppress bone remodeling, leading to the accumulation of microdamage and rare complications like atypical femoral fractures (AFFs) [3]. While AFFs are uncommon, their occurrence has been increasingly recognized in patients receiving bisphosphonates for extended periods, especially without specialist supervision [4]. This report describes a unique case of bilateral AFFs in a patient who had been taking alendronate continuously for 15 years, without periodic evaluation or consideration of treatment holidays, and who obtained the medication through a non-clinical source. The case also highlights missed prodromal signs and systemic shortcomings in medication monitoring, including inadequate evaluation of differential diagnoses.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old Asian (Iranian) woman with an 18-year history of osteoporosis presented to the emergency department the sudden onset of severe bilateral thigh pain, resulting in an inability to stand or walk. She was diagnosed with severe osteoporosis based on two BMD tests performed 18 and 15 years earlier; however, the original BMD reports were not available. After her diagnosis, she had been taking oral alendronate 70 mg weekly for approximately 15 years without regular medical supervision. The medication was obtained through a pharmacist relative. She had avoided calcium supplementation because of concerns about constipation and kidney stones.

Her past medical history included hyperlipidemia treated with atorvastatin 10 mg daily. She reported no history of smoking or alcohol use. Approximately 1 month before admission, her symptoms began while she was following a home exercise program on television. During these exercises, she developed bilateral thigh pain, which gradually worsened over the following weeks and led to increasing difficulty with walking. She subsequently sought outpatient care and was diagnosed with degenerative joint disease of the hips, knees, and lumbar spine. Analgesics, chondroprotective agents, and topical treatments were prescribed; however, her symptoms progressively worsened. During the two weeks before presentation, she began taking diclofenac 100 mg daily because of escalating pain.

On the day of admission, she experienced sudden, severe pain in her right thigh, followed almost immediately by the same pain in her left thigh. While standing in her kitchen and pouring tea, she described the sensation as if both thigh bones were breaking. She fell into a seated position and was unable to stand or walk afterward. Upon arrival at the emergency department, she was in bed and unable to ambulate. Inspection revealed mild swelling of both thighs. Palpation elicited marked tenderness along the femoral shafts, with maximal tenderness over the mid-shaft region bilaterally. Range of motion of the hips and knees was severely limited by pain. Neurovascular examination was normal, with intact distal pulses, normal capillary refill, and no motor or sensory deficits. Her vital signs were within normal limits.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated normal complete blood count with differential, as well as renal and liver function. Serum calcium levels, 25‑OH vitamin D, parathyroid hormone, alkaline phosphatase, and acid phosphatase were not measured. Further history revealed that she had been receiving alendronate without ongoing medical supervision. Her sister-in-law, a pharmacist, had been dispensing the medication for years without the need for renewed prescriptions. The patient avoided calcium supplements because of concerns about constipation and kidney stones and had not undergone bone mineral density assessment in recent years.

Following the diagnosis, the patient elected to undergo surgical management in another city. Operative details and postoperative outcomes could not be obtained despite attempts to retrieve them, as the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up.

Imaging

The radiographic findings are consistent with the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research (ASBMR) Task Force 2014 criteria for atypical femoral fractures. In both femora, the fractures are located along the diaphyseal region. The fracture pattern is predominantly transverse with a short oblique extension medially, and a characteristic medial spike is present in the complete fractures. The fractures are non-comminuted, with no evidence of fragmentation. Additionally, localized thickening of the lateral cortex is clearly visible. No minor features, such as generalized cortical thickening or delayed healing, were identified in this case (Fig. 1).

Discussion

AFFs typically occur in the subtrochanteric or diaphyseal regions of the femur—areas not commonly affected in typical osteoporotic fractures [5]. These fractures are often associated with minimal or no trauma and are frequently preceded by prodromal pain lasting for weeks or even months [6]. In this case, the patient’s anterior thigh pain and altered gait were not adequately investigated, leading to a missed opportunity for early diagnosis. Long-term use of alendronate suppresses bone turnover, which can impair the repair of microdamage and might ultimately increase skeletal fragility [7]. This mechanism may have contributed to the patient’s bilateral femoral shaft fractures.

Current clinical guidelines recommend reassessment of bisphosphonate therapy after 3 to 5 years and consideration of drug holidays in patients at low risk of fracture [8]. This reassessment is commonly performed using updated dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) measurements together with clinical fracture risk assessment tools such as the fracture risk assessment tool (FRAX) score. Unfortunately, in this case, the patient continued taking alendronate for 15 years without clinical follow-up. The medication was dispensed by a relative who was a pharmacist, omitting key aspects of osteoporosis management, including BMD evaluations, reassessment of fracture risk, and monitoring for musculoskeletal symptoms. Despite multiple consultations, the patient's prodromal symptoms were misattributed to age-related degenerative changes, underscoring a gap in clinical vigilance. While this single case cannot support broad conclusions, it illustrates how gaps in clinical vigilance and medication monitoring could contribute to delayed diagnosis. Importantly, early recognition of the warning signs of AFFs could have prevented this outcome.

The patient had not been taking calcium supplements during long-term bisphosphonate therapy. While this may have influenced bone health, the specific impact cannot be determined due to the absence of relevant biochemical and BMD data. In Iran and many other countries, misconceptions regarding calcium—such as its perceived link to kidney stones or constipation—often deter older adults from adhering to supplementation recommendations. However, current evidence indicates that calcium intake within recommended levels, especially when paired with adequate hydration, poses minimal risk [9]. Targeted public education and clinician-led counseling are essential to correct these misunderstandings and support adherence to osteoporosis treatment plans. Importantly, because laboratory data and recent BMD values were unavailable, the contribution of calcium avoidance in this specific case cannot be definitively established.

This case highlights how prolonged, unsupervised bisphosphonate use can be associated with adverse outcomes and may point to areas where clearer prescription oversight and follow‑up processes could be beneficial [10, 11]. While broader regulatory implications cannot be concluded from a single case, this report raises the possibility that improved monitoring systems—such as clinical alerts for long‑term bisphosphonate use—might help reduce the risk of delayed reassessment and complications. Similarly, limiting unsupervised access to long‑term medications without specialist oversight could be considered as part of a broader patient‑safety framework.

Limitations

A major limitation of this report is the lack of follow-up data, including surgical management and outcomes. Additionally, the absence of bone turnover markers and recent BMD measurements limits the ability to directly establish causality between long-term alendronate use and the bilateral AFFs.

Conclusion

This case underscores the potential risks associated with prolonged, unsupervised bisphosphonate use. Prodromal symptoms—such as mild thigh pain and gait disturbances—should be recognized as early warning signs that warrant prompt imaging and therapeutic reassessment. Safe long-term management of osteoporosis requires regular specialist follow-up, patient education, and adherence to drug holiday protocols. Adequate calcium and vitamin D supplementation is generally recommended to support bone health; however, in this case, the contribution of calcium intake to fracture risk cannot be determined due to the absence of relevant laboratory measurements and recent BMD data. Accordingly, the role of calcium avoidance in the development of fractures remains uncertain and no causal inference can be made in this single case. Finally, while broader regulatory implications cannot be firmly drawn from a single case, this report suggests that clearer monitoring systems and limits on unsupervised access to long‑term medications may help support patient safety.
