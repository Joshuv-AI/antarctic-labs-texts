Quality of life among healthcare workers in Gaza during the October 7 war: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Belal Aldabbour, Asem Alnabih, Muath Alsarafandi, Abd Al-Karim Sammour, Osama Sweid, Kareem Alrawabdeh, Yousef Barakat, Latefa Ali Dardas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare workers (HCWs) in Gaza face extreme personal and professional strain amid the ongoing war since October 2023. Prolonged violence, healthcare collapse, and widespread displacement have likely worsened their quality of life (QoL). This study assesses QoL and its associated factors among HCWs in Gaza’s leading governmental hospitals.MethodsThis cross-sectional study, conducted in November–December 2024, surveyed HCWs across seven hospitals using convenience sampling. Eligible participants were physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and laboratory technicians. Data collection included sociodemographic information, war-related exposures, and the Arabic version of the WHOQOL-BREF instrument. Bivariate analyses (independent t-tests and ANOVA) were performed to examine subgroup differences in QoL scores. Variables significant at p < 0.05 were included in multiple linear regression models to identify independent predictors of overall and domain-specific QoL scores.ResultsA total of 403 HCWs participated (56.6% male, mean age 31.16 years ± 7.6). Nurses (44.9%) and physicians (36.2%) were the primary respondents. Most held bachelor’s degrees (74.2%) and had ≤ 5 years of experience (62.5%). War-related trauma was widespread: 57.6% lost a friend, 56.3% a coworker, and 46.9% reported loss of family livelihood. The mean overall QoL was 46.9 (SD 23.07). The environmental domain scored the lowest (24.08), while the social domain scored the highest (46.38). Multivariate analysis revealed that loss of family livelihood was consistently associated with lower QoL across psychological, social, and environmental domains. Female sex, residence in the southern enclave during the Strip’s partition by the Israeli army at the time of the study, and the death of a second-degree or distant relative were also associated with lower environmental QoL. The death of a friend was unexpectedly associated with higher psychological QoL. No significant predictors emerged for physical QoL.ConclusionsThis study highlights the severe decline in the QoL of HCWs in Gaza during the war, particularly in the environmental and psychological domains. The findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive support strategies to protect HCWs’ well-being as a cornerstone of healthcare system resilience in conflict zones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13423-z.

Introduction

The Gaza Strip is a densely populated, resource-limited territory that has been under Israeli occupation since 1967. It is a recognized part of the State of Palestine and is home to more than two million residents, the majority of whom are refugees from the 1948 Nakba. Due to decades of conflict and instability, Palestinians in the Gaza Strip live in conditions marked by vulnerability and scarcity [1, 2]. For the last two decades, Gaza has been subjected to a protracted blockade and repeated military campaigns, which have left the Gaza Strip economically and geographically isolated and have led to soaring unemployment and poverty, underdeveloped infrastructure, inadequate healthcare, and chronic shortages of electricity and water, with the majority of the population depending on international aid for sustenance [1, 2]. Meanwhile, the repeated military confrontations have resulted in tens of thousands of casualties and immense destruction. Along with a chronic fiscal collapse, the blockade and political violence have devastated the healthcare system, which has endured critical shortages of essential medicines, equipment, and fuel, while the medical workforce has been drained by the poor living conditions and the stress of working under such circumstances [3].

The war in Gaza that began in October 2023 has significantly deepened this humanitarian crisis. By August 2025, the war had resulted in the deaths of over 62,000 Palestinians and left more than twice that number wounded [4]. Furthermore, it has been marked by immense humanitarian suffering, including starvation, mass displacement affecting nearly 90% of the population, and the collapse of the different aspects of healthcare [1, 5–9]. For instance, from October 2023 to January 2025, the Gaza Strip was divided by a military corridor into two isolated enclaves: a northern one (comprising the North and Gaza Governorates) and a southern one (encompassing the Middle, Khan Younis, and Rafah Governorates). Hundreds of thousands of people from the northern enclave were forcibly displaced to the southern enclave by an Israeli military order issued at the war’s onset. Most internally displaced persons (IDPs) in the southern enclave endured poor living conditions and, until early 2025, were not permitted to return to their areas in the north [1]. The mental health impacts of the war have been severe, with rates of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress unparalleled in recent conflicts [1, 6].

Healthcare workers (HCWs) in Gaza were disproportionately affected by this unprecedented demand on healthcare infrastructure and personnel. Besides physical danger, they faced significant psychological distress from witnessing mass casualties, treating severely injured civilians, and working under extreme resource limitations. The siege and targeting of hospitals have worsened the situation. Dozens of health facilities have been damaged or become nonfunctional, leaving the remaining staff overwhelmed with patient volumes far beyond capacity [10, 11]. Outside their duty hours, HCWs have, like the general population, suffered from displacement and faced the constant threat to their safety.

This was further intensified by the severe shortage of humanitarian aid, medical equipment, medications, fuel, and other vital supplies, which critically limited HCWs’ ability to provide care and meet their own basic needs. These combined stressors put HCWs at high risk for adverse psychological outcomes, including burnout, anxiety, depression, and reduced quality of life (QoL) [12]. QoL is a multidimensional construct encompassing physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environmental factors. It is an essential outcome to track during humanitarian crises, as it reflects both immediate suffering and long-term resilience capacity [13].

Understanding QoL among HCWs during humanitarian crises has implications not only for individual well-being but also for broader health system resilience and workforce sustainability. The ability of healthcare systems to recover, adapt, and deliver essential services during protracted emergencies is closely tied to the physical and psychological capacity of their frontline workers [13, 14].

The WHOQOL-BREF, a widely used tool developed by the World Health Organization (WHO), has been extensively utilized to evaluate QoL across diverse populations, including HCWs worldwide. Previous research using the WHOQOL-BREF has highlighted how occupational stress, trauma exposure, and working conditions influence QoL among HCWs in both stable and crisis-affected environments [13, 15–19]. However, few studies have looked at these dynamics in active war zones—especially in Gaza—where HCWs face ongoing psychological and material hardships.

This study evaluates QoL and its associated factors among HCWs in the Gaza Strip during the ongoing war. It can offer valuable insights into how various stressors related to working in a conflict zone affect HCWs’ well-being and is essential to identifying the support and resources they need to deliver care under such difficult conditions. Additionally, the findings can help policymakers and stakeholders improve the well-being of HCWs in Gaza and other conflict areas.

Methods

Design, settings, and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in November and December 2024. Eligible participants were physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and laboratory technicians who had worked in health services for at least six months during the current war. The study excluded those who had worked for less than six months during the war and those who refused to participate. Recruitment took place in seven hospitals, four of which were located in the Gaza Governorate: Shifa Hospital, Public Aid Hospital, Al-Ahli Baptists Hospital, and Al-Helou Hospital; one was situated in the Middle Governorate (Aqsa Hospital), while two were in the Khan Younis Governorate (Nasser Medical Complex and European Gaza Hospital). These institutions were government-operated and served as the main providers of tertiary healthcare in their respective regions at the time of data collection. The two remaining governorates in the Gaza Strip (the North and Rafah Governorates) evacuated residents in November due to ongoing Israeli ground offensives and had no active healthcare facilities. Data was collected by senior medical students. A total of 470 HCWs were approached, of whom 403 completed the survey (response rate 85.7%).

Sample size

The latest available statistics indicate that there were nearly 25,000 health professionals from the four included disciplines in the Gaza Strip in 2021, including 6053, 10,984, and almost 3000 registered physicians, nurses, and pharmacists, respectively [20]. Using EpiInfo software with the standard settings of a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence interval, the required sample size was calculated as 379 participants. Due to logistical and safety constraints in the conflict zone, convenience sampling was employed.

Study instrument and procedures

A data collection sheet was created for this study, comprising three sections. The first section gathered sociodemographic and work-related data; the second collected information on living conditions and the forms of harm endured by participants due to the war; and the third included the Arabic version of the WHOQOL-BREF instrument, which was utilized after obtaining a license by the second author via the WHO portal. This instrument consists of 26 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale (one means very poor/dissatisfied and five means very good/satisfied). The questions span four domains: physical health (7 items), psychological health (6 items), social relationships (3 items), and environment (8 items). Additionally, two items assess overall quality of life and general health and are scored separately from the domains. The Arabic version of the WHOQOL-BREF used in this study has been validated across diverse Arabic-speaking populations. Ohaeri and Awadalla (2009) demonstrated its reliability and validity among adults in Kuwait [21]. Dalky, Meininger, and Al-Ali (2017) confirmed its psychometric soundness in Jordan [22]. Malibary et al. (2019) reported strong reliability among Arabic-speaking adults in Saudi Arabia [23]. Furthermore, Dardas and Ahmad (2014a, 2014b, 2015) validated the Arabic WHOQOL-BREF through multiple studies conducted among adults in Jordan [24–26]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients demonstrated acceptable internal consistency for the overall scale and its four domains.

Senior medical students and interns electronically collected data through face-to-face interactions using the Zoho application, installed on iPads and smartphones. Each participant independently completed the survey on one of these devices.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the Islamic University of Gaza (IUG). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, emphasizing confidentiality and voluntary participation, and no identifying information was collected. To reduce potential distress caused by the sensitive nature of the questions related to the war’s impacts, data collectors were trained to recognize signs of emotional discomfort and respond in a supportive manner. Participants could skip any question or withdraw from the survey at any time, and those experiencing distress were offered brief support and referrals to local mental health services when necessary.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 28. Figure 1 was generated using Python (v3.9). The analysis followed a structured approach to examine the sociodemographic characteristics of HCWs, their exposure to war-related adversities, and their perceived QoL across four domains as measured by the WHOQOL-BREF instrument: physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environmental factors. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means (standard deviations “SD”), and medians, were used to summarize the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, war-related experiences, and domain-specific QoL scores.Fig. 1Multivariate independent predictors of quality of life among healthcare workers in Gaza during the war

Raw scores for each WHOQOL-BREF domain were calculated by summing the relevant item responses after reverse scoring negatively phrased items (Q3, Q4, and Q26). To make the scores comparable with the WHOQOL-100, domain raw scores were multiplied by 4. These were then transformed to a 0–100 scale. This scoring method follows the WHOQOL-BREF guidelines to ensure standardized and interpretable domain scores across respondent groups. The WHOQOL-BREF does not provide clinical cutoff points. Consistent with previous literature, we interpreted domain scores on a 0–100 scale as follows: scores below 40 were considered indicative of poor QoL, scores between 40 and 60 denoted moderate QoL, and scores above 60 indicated good QoL [27, 28]. These interpretive thresholds were used descriptively to contextualize findings and should be interpreted cautiously due to the lack of universally validated clinical cutoffs.

To identify factors associated with HCWs’ QoL, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with the overall QoL score and each of the four WHOQOL-BREF domain scores (physical, psychological, social, and environmental) as dependent variables. Independent variables were selected based on theoretical relevance and significance in bivariate analyses (p < 0.05), including sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., sex, number of children, duration of service), war-related exposures (e.g., loss of family livelihood, displacement, death of coworkers or relatives), and housing status. To assess the bivariate differences in QoL scores across subgroups, independent samples t-tests were used for dichotomous variables (e.g., sex, area of living “northern vs. southern enclaves”, housing type, trauma exposure). For categorical variables with more than two groups (e.g., age brackets, duration of service), a one-way ANOVA was employed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In cases of unequal variances, Levene’s test was conducted, and appropriate adjustments (Welch’s t-test) were applied. Potential multicollinearity in the multivariate model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with a cutoff of < 5. Normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and linearity were checked via residual plots and histogram inspection. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Standardized beta coefficients (β), unstandardized coefficients (B), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 403 HCWs participated in the study, comprising 56.6% males and 43.4% females, with a mean age of 31.16 years (median 29 years). Most were young, aged 26 years or less (33.8%), followed by those aged 30–35 years (26.4%). Marital status was evenly split, with 50.4% married and 49.6% single, divorced, or widowed. Professionally, nurses made up 44.9%, followed by physicians (36.2%), lab technicians (10.7%), and pharmacists (8.2%). The majority held a bachelor’s degree (74.2%); smaller proportions held diplomas (8.7%), master’s degrees (8.9%), board certifications (6.7%), or PhDs (1.5%). The mean work experience (i.e., the total number of years the participant has worked in the healthcare sector) was 6.65 years (median, four years), with 62.5% having worked for five years or less. The average duration of service during the war was 9.7 months (SD = 3.9, median = 8.3), with 25.1% of participants serving ≤ 6 months, 30.3% between 7 and 9 months, 24.1% between 10 and 12 months, and 20.6% for more than 12 months at the time of the study. Factors associated with hcws’ QoL

To examine factors associated with HCWs’ QoL, we first present results from bivariate analyses comparing mean QoL scores across sociodemographic, occupational, and war-related variables. These are then followed by findings from a multiple linear regression model across different domains.

Bivariate analyses

Sociodemographic and work-related factors

Male participants reported significantly higher QoL scores than females in both the psychological (p = 0.04, Cohen’s d = 0.206) and environmental (p = 0.012, Cohen’s d = 0.251) domains. Having one or no children was associated with significantly higher social QoL scores (p = 0.012, Cohen’s d = 0.253). Environmental QoL scores also varied based on the duration of service during the war, with those serving more than 12 months reporting higher scores than those with shorter service durations (p = 0.008, Cohen’s d = 0.29). Geographical location was another significant factor. HCWs living in the southern enclave (Middle and Khan Younis governorates) reported higher mean overall QoL scores (M = 48.04, SD = 22.15) compared to those in the northern enclave (North and Gaza governorates) (M = 42.92, SD = 25.76), although this difference approached but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.089, Cohen’s d = 0.302). A significant difference emerged in the environmental domain (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.697), with HCWs in the northern enclave reporting notably higher scores than those in the southern enclave. No statistically significant differences in QoL scores were observed based on marital status, age group, years of experience, profession, or educational attainment (Supplementary table S1).

Living conditions and trauma exposure

Participants who were residing in houses reported significantly higher scores in the physical (p = 0.02) and environmental (p = 0.02) domains. Those staying in hospitals during the war similarly reported higher psychological (p = 0.041) and environmental (p = 0.037) QoL. Among trauma-related exposures, the death of a friend was associated with lower psychological QoL (p = 0.021, Cohen’s d = 0.231), while the death of a coworker was associated with lower environmental QoL (p = 0.028, Cohen’s d = 0.221). The strongest negative associations were related to loss of family livelihood, which was significantly associated with lower QoL across all domains, especially psychological (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.382) and environmental (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.442). The death of a first-degree relative was marginally linked to higher environmental QoL (p = 0.05), while the death of a second-degree or distant relative was associated with significantly lower environmental scores (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.45). No significant links were found between QoL scores and shelter living, injury, detention, or partial/complete house destruction (Supplementary table S2).

Multivariable analyses

Variables that were significant in bivariate analyses were included in a multiple linear regression model to identify independent predictors of QoL across the psychological, social, and environmental domains (Table 4). For the psychological domain, loss of family livelihood remained a significant negative predictor (β = −0.190, p < 0.001), while experiencing the death of a friend was positively associated with psychological QoL (β = 0.109, p = 0.028). In the social domain, both the number of children (β = −0.129, p = 0.009) and loss of family livelihood (β = −0.152, p = 0.002) were linked to lower scores. For the environmental domain, significant negative predictors included female sex (β = −0.277, p = 0.003), residence in the southern enclave (β = −0.745, p < 0.001), loss of family livelihood (β = −0.415, p < 0.001), and the death of a second-degree or distant relative (β = −0.212, p = 0.028). Discussion

The present study is one of the few that have assessed the Quality of Life (QoL) of healthcare workers (HCWs) in the Gaza Strip in general, and it is the first to have been conducted during the ongoing war since 2023. Our findings showed low to moderate QoL scores, with the environmental domain scoring the lowest among the four assessed domains. We also identified significant associations between several sociodemographic factors, work-related factors, and war-related harms and QoL, with the loss of family livelihood notably reducing QoL across all domains. Additionally, regional differences were evident, as residents in the southern enclave scored significantly lower in the environmental domain. These results highlight the negative impact of the ongoing war, which has compromised infrastructure, exacerbated shortages of essential resources, and intensified the psychological and physical burdens on HCWs.

The QoL scores observed in our study were markedly lower than those reported in pre-war assessments conducted in Gaza. For example, Younis et al. [13] reported higher means across all WHOQOL-BREF domains—physical (52.16), psychological (55.69), social (64.44), environmental (51.61), and overall QoL (55.98)—from data collected in 2020. In contrast, the present study revealed scores of 43.75, 43.27, 46.38, 24.08, and 46.90, respectively. The most profound decline was observed in the environmental domain, which captures critical elements such as safety, transportation, access to healthcare, and financial resources—all of which have been severely disrupted by the war and mass displacement. Furthermore, even the 2020 figures were already lower than the average scores reported in a 2022 multi-country study of HCWs in 19 Arab nations, underscoring the long-standing vulnerability of Gaza’s healthcare workforce. The compounding impact of historical instability, economic deprivation, and now war is associated with lowering HCWs’ QoL to alarmingly low levels.

To understand the extent of these findings, it is essential to examine each of the four QoL domains independently, as they illuminate the distinct yet interrelated aspects of well-being during times of crisis. The following sections examine the physical, psychological, social, and environmental domains, taking into account both the quantitative scores and the contextual realities surrounding healthcare work in Gaza during wartime.

The environmental QoL domain—which includes aspects such as safety, housing, financial resources, and access to services—is particularly sensitive to basic living conditions and socioeconomic shocks. In this study, it had the lowest mean score among all domains (24.08), which is considerably lower than the pre-war mean of 51.61 reported among HCWs in Gaza [13]. While certain traumatic experiences, such as the death of relatives or coworkers, also appeared to affect environmental QoL, the most consistent and substantial associations were found with material and resource-related factors. For instance, individuals with stable housing reported higher environmental QoL, while those living in tents reported significantly lower scores. Participants who stayed in hospitals reported higher environmental QoL than those who did not, a finding that, although counterintuitive, may be explained by increased access to healthcare services and humanitarian aid in hospital settings.

Loss of family livelihood was strongly associated with lower environmental QoL, underscoring the profound impact of financial insecurity. Surprisingly, those who experienced the death of a first-degree relative reported slightly higher environmental QoL, possibly reflecting increased social or institutional support following bereavement. In contrast, participants who lost a coworker reported lower environmental QoL—perhaps due to the disruption of daily routines and support systems, especially in a context where work relationships are deeply intertwined with emotional and economic stability. Similarly, those who experienced the death of a second-degree or more distant relative reported significantly lower environmental QoL, which may indicate broader community-level trauma or the cumulative toll of multiple losses. In collectivist cultures like Gaza’s, the loss of extended family members can directly affect shared housing, caregiving responsibilities, and financial interdependence—all of which are closely linked to environmental well-being. Finally, despite enduring the brunt of destruction during the early months of the war, participants residing in the North and Gaza governorates reported significantly higher environmental QoL scores than those in the southern areas. This finding emphasizes the negative effects of forced displacement and the associated crowding and poor living conditions, which previous studies and reports have documented [1, 29]. Notably, a recent survey (Aldabbour et al., 2025) conducted during the same period as this study found that patients with non-communicable diseases in the southern enclave reported significantly lower perceived quality of life compared to those in the northern enclave, supporting our findings [7]. Additionally, another recent study (Aldabbour et al., 2025) reported a significantly lower birthweight among newborns delivered in the southern enclave, further illustrating how displacement and poor living conditions in the south affect residents’ health [8].

The poor scores in the environmental domain highlight the urgent need for targeted infrastructure investments to restore healthcare facilities and living conditions. Additionally, policy responses must focus on the physical protection and well-being of HCWs, who continue to provide care under life-threatening circumstances. Reinforcing institutional resilience and ensuring dignity for frontline staff are crucial parts of post-crisis health system recovery.

The physical QoL domain, which assesses daily activities, energy, mobility, pain, and work capacity, had a mean score of 43.75 in our study, making it the second highest among the four domains. This score is lower than those reported in previous studies, such as Younis et al. [13] and Alnazly et al. [30], which were conducted among HCWs in Gaza and Jordan, respectively, reflecting considerable physical strain. Notably, studies conducted on 3,170 HCWs from 19 Arab countries (Ghazy et al.) reported much higher physical domain scores [31]. These discrepancies underscore the particularly harsh reality faced by Gaza’s HCWs, who contend with widespread deprivation, overwhelming workloads, and exposure to physical trauma, all of which significantly impact their physical well-being.

Participants residing in houses reported higher physical QoL scores compared to those living in tents or shelters, which likely reflects the more stable and secure living conditions in houses, with fewer concerns about the provision of basic necessities such as water and sanitation. In contrast, individuals living in tents are more likely to face overcrowding, lack of infrastructure, and increased vulnerability to infectious diseases, all of which can exacerbate physical health issues. The relative stability of house living may also alleviate some of the physical demands placed on individuals, as it reduces the stress associated with displacement and the search for basic resources.

The physical well-being of HCWs in Gaza has been deeply affected by the overwhelming demands placed on them in a war zone. The combination of direct physical trauma, stress, and the loss of essential life resources contributes to the negative impact on their health. Despite the relatively higher physical scores observed in those living in houses, the overall lower physical QoL in Gaza compared to other regions underscores the severe toll that the ongoing conflict and lack of basic services have had on the healthcare workforce.

The psychological QoL domain, encompassing self-image, emotional state, concentration, and the ability to learn, had a mean score of 43.27 in this study. This is substantially lower than the pre-war scores reported among HCWs in Gaza and the broader regional average [13, 31]. As observed in other QoL domains, participants who reported living in hospitals had higher psychological QoL scores than those who did not. This may be due to the presence of professional peer support, structure, and a sense of continued purpose in their work environment. These factors might have helped mitigate feelings of isolation, helplessness, or psychological disintegration, which are commonly experienced during crises.

On the other hand, participants who lost their livelihoods exhibited lower psychological domain scores, likely reflecting the heightened financial stress, uncertainty, and the burden of supporting dependents in a challenging environment. Surprisingly, those who lost a friend during the war reported higher psychological QoL scores than those who did not. This counterintuitive finding may reflect the phenomenon of post-traumatic growth (PTG)—a positive psychological transformation that can happen after experiencing deeply distressing events, including loss and bereavement [32]. PTG may involve enhanced appreciation for life, redefined priorities, or stronger relationships, which could partly explain the unexpected rise in perceived psychological well-being among bereaved individuals. However, longitudinal studies are necessary to confirm the existence and development of PTG among HCWs exposed to armed conflict, as PTG cannot be definitively inferred from a single time-point assessment and may represent temporary adaptive coping rather than lasting psychological change.

The social QoL domain, reflecting personal relationships, social support, and satisfaction with social connections, received the highest mean score among the four domains in this study. This finding aligns with the earlier study by Younis et al., which also reported the social domain as the highest among HCWs in Gaza [13], yet it contrasts with the findings of Ghazy et al., who reported a lower mean across 19 Arab countries [31]. Despite being the most preserved domain in the current study, the significantly lower mean compared to prior data underscores the disruptive impact of war on social relationships, particularly in a context where familial and communal bonds are traditionally strong. The relatively higher social scores may, in part, be attributed to the collectivist cultural structure of Gazan society, where social cohesion, mutual aid, and extended family networks are deeply valued and prioritized. Even in times of crisis, these bonds may offer emotional support and a sense of solidarity.

Still, the war’s toll is evident: participants who lost their livelihoods reported significantly lower social QoL scores, likely reflecting the strain that economic hardship places on social functioning. In contexts of widespread poverty and displacement, individuals burdened by financial insecurity may find it difficult to maintain social ties, particularly when they are unable to reciprocate support or meet the basic needs of their families. Additionally, participants with fewer children (one or none) reported higher social QoL scores, possibly because smaller family sizes are associated with reduced caregiving demands and more flexibility to engage with social networks. In contrast, larger families may amplify the stressors associated with economic scarcity, limiting opportunities for social interaction and increasing social withdrawal. Taken together, these findings suggest that while cultural resilience and social solidarity remain important buffers against complete social deterioration, the prolonged and pervasive pressures of war have nonetheless weakened even this most durable domain of quality of life among Gaza’s HCWs.

The multivariate regression analysis offered further insights into the independent factors associated with QoL among HCWs in Gaza. For instance, loss of family livelihood independently predicted lower scores across all four domains, even after adjusting for other sociodemographic and trauma-related variables (p < 0.001). Female HCWs consistently scored lower in the environmental and psychological domains, supporting previous research on gender disparities in war-related mental health outcomes (e.g., Alnazly et al., 2023; Cruz-Ausejo et al., 2023) [30, 33]. These results emphasize the complex factors driving impaired QoL in humanitarian crises and highlight the need for intersectional and targeted approaches. Importantly, no variable was identified as a significant predictor of physical QoL in the adjusted model, indicating that physical well-being may be more uniformly impacted across the population or affected by unmeasured factors.

Limitations

The use of a convenience sampling approach, while logistically necessary due to the ongoing war, represents a limitation. This method may introduce selection bias, as participants who are accessible and willing to respond may differ systematically from those who are not. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings to all HCWs in Gaza should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the absence of significant associations in some of the bivariate analyses may be due to the complex and overlapping nature of war-related stressors, which are not fully captured by sociodemographic indicators alone. Additionally, several potential confounding variables, such as PTSD symptoms, individual coping strategies, and access to institutional or psychosocial support, were not measured in this study and may have influenced participants’ QoL scores. Finally, the study was conducted between November and December 2024, during a particularly acute phase of a protracted, multi-phased war that has now lasted nearly two years. The conflict has evolved through varying levels of displacement, violence, and systemic collapse, each exerting different pressures on healthcare workers. This specific timeframe was chosen to capture the immediate impact of that phase on their quality of life. However, given the cross-sectional design and the dynamic nature of the war, the findings represent associations limited to that specific period and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships. To fully grasp how such prolonged crises affect HCWs over time, longitudinal studies are essential to track both immediate and long-term outcomes across different stages of conflict.

Conclusion

This study offers one of the most comprehensive assessments to date of the QoL among HCWs in Gaza during the unprecedented war in the region since 2023. The findings reveal an alarming decline in overall QoL scores, particularly in the environmental and psychological domains, reflecting the complex and multifaceted burdens borne by HCWs in an active conflict zone. Compared to both regional benchmarks and pre-war data from Gaza, all QoL domains showed substantial deterioration, underscoring the devastating impact of prolonged violence, economic collapse, displacement, and systemic healthcare breakdown on the personal and professional lives of frontline workers. While the social domain emerged as the most preserved area of well-being, likely due to Gaza’s strong collectivist social fabric, even this was significantly diminished relative to historical data. The lowest scores in the environmental domain, which encapsulates safety, financial security, and access to essential services, point to a humanitarian crisis that directly threatens the physical and psychological stability of healthcare providers. Future emergency preparedness strategies must integrate structured mental health and housing support for frontline providers.

These findings have critical implications for policymakers, humanitarian actors, and international health agencies. Efforts to strengthen healthcare systems in conflict zones must extend beyond facility reconstruction to include comprehensive strategies for protecting and supporting the well-being of HCWs. This includes ensuring access to safe housing, a reliable income, mental health resources, and sustainable working conditions. Without addressing the human cost borne by healthcare professionals, the resilience and functionality of the health system itself cannot be sustained.

The war in Gaza has profoundly disrupted every domain of HCWs’ QoL, threatening not only their personal well-being but also the stability of healthcare delivery under crisis. Ensuring the safety and well-being of HCWs is both a moral imperative and a prerequisite for any meaningful health system recovery in war-affected settings.
