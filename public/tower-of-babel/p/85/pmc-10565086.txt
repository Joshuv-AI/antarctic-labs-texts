Inguinal hernia repair in an elderly patient with moderate aortic stenosis under sole epidural anesthesia: A case report
Authors: Sagar Devkota, Yubaraj Thapa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey Clinical MessageIn resource limited settings, epidural anesthesia can be a safe option for elderly patients with aortic stenosis for lower abdominal surgeries as the hemodynamic changes are known to be less as compared to spinal or general anesthesia.AbstractSpinal or general anesthesia in an elderly patient with aortic stenosis is always a challenge for anesthesiologists. It is even more challenging in a resource limited setting. The marked hemodynamic changes associated with either spinal or general anesthesia increase the morbidity and mortality. We present a case of right inguinal hernia in a 71‐year‐old patient with hypertension, bradycardia, and moderate aortic stenosis planned for open mesh repair under sole epidural anesthesia.Keywords: aortic stenosis, elderly, epidural anesthesia, inguinal hernia, mesh repair

INTRODUCTION

Aortic stenosis (AS), a fixed cardiac output state, is always a challenge for anesthesiologists as high risk for severe hypotension, ischemic heart disease, and cerebral hypoperfusion should be considered in such patients. 1 Regional anesthesia particularly subarachnoid block is known to be associated with a rapid and massive decline in systemic vascular resistance (SVR) due to potent sympatholysis leading to reduction in venous return, and the patient may not be able to compensate these changes sufficiently due to fixed cardiac output state. 2 General anesthesia (GA) can be an option in such patients but may have an even more pronounced cardiovascular effect as compared to neuraxial anesthesia due to an induction‐triggered hypotension with a decrease in venous return and reduced vascular tone in combination with its negative inotropic effect. 3 In addition, the use of muscle‐relaxant drugs and positive airway pressure further complicate the physiological response in such patients. 3 Regional anesthesia in the form of peripheral nerve blocks can be an option but require expertise and ultrasound with/without peripheral nerve stimulator. 4 This is not feasible in resource limited settings. The other option which can be used for conducting lower abdominal surgeries in such patients is epidural anesthesia. Epidural techniques are particularly effective at providing dynamic analgesia, allowing the patient to mobilize and resume normal activities unlimited by pain. 5 Furthermore, epidural anesthesia is known to be associated with less rapid and intense hemodynamic changes as compared to spinal or general anesthesia. 6

We present a case of a 71‐year‐old male with right inguinal hernia with hypertension with bradycardia with moderate aortic stenosis planned for open mesh repair under sole epidural anesthesia.

CASE REPORT

A 71‐year‐old male presented to surgery outpatient department with swelling over right inguinal region for 3–4 months. He was diagnosed with right inguinal hernia and planned for open mesh repair. He had history of hypertension and was currently under losartan and hydrochlorthiazide. His recent echocardiography done for an incidental finding of ejection systolic murmur revealed moderate aortic stenosis with no regional wall motion abnormalities. He had no any other significant past or family history. On examination, his vitals were within normal range except for bradycardia (heart rate ranged from 49 to 54 beats per minute, regular, normal volume). All his blood investigations were within normal limits. His electrocardiogram revealed sinus bradycardia and chest X‐ray was normal.

Based on his cardiovascular status and taking into consideration the hemodynamic changes associated with spinal or general anesthesia and the limited resources at disposal, his surgery was planned under sole epidural block.

After taking informed written consent, the patient was shifted to operating room. He was preloaded with 500 mL Ringer's Lactate. Vasopressors like phenylephrine and noradrenaline were kept ready. Baseline vitals were recorded. Invasive monitoring was not done due to unavailability of resources. Under all aseptic precaution, in sitting position and midline approach, 18G Tuohy needle was inserted into L2‐L3 interspace. Loss of resistance was noted at 5 cm. Epidural catheter was inserted, and test dose of 3 mL of 2% xylocaine with adrenaline (1:200000) was given. No changes in heart rate and blood pressure were noted after which catheter was fixed at 9 cm. The patient was kept in supine position, and a total of 10 mL of 0.125% plain bupivacaine was given via epidural catheter. After the desired level of anesthesia (T8 level was achieved at 5 minutes) was achieved, the surgery was commenced. Epidural infusion was started at 5 mL per hour with 0.125 percent plain bupivacaine. The total intra‐operative period was uneventful and lasted for 2 h. (Table 1).

After completion of surgery, he was shifted to post‐operative ward with 0.0625 percent plain bupivacaine at the rate of 5 mL per hour via epidural catheter for post‐operative pain management and intravenous paracetamol for rescue analgesia. On the first post‐operative day, he was lying comfortably in bed with visual analog scale (VAS) pain score of 4 on movement and 2 at rest. The epidural catheter was removed on the second post‐operative day, and he was discharged on oral medications with follow‐up at 1 week.

DISCUSSION

Regional anesthesia particularly neuraxial block is relatively contraindicated in patients with AS due the hemodynamic alterations associated with sympatholysis. Sudden reduction in systemic vascular resistance leading to peripheral vasodilation may lead to reduction in venous return which ultimately reduces the cardiac output in an already reduced output state due to aortic stenosis. 7 Continuous spinal anesthesia can be used to minimize the hemodynamic effects associated with standard single shot spinal anesthesia 8 ; however, this was not available. Similarly, general anesthesia with induction induced hypotension, positive pressure ventilation, and muscle relaxation may have a further deleterious effect on the already diminished cardiac reserve in patient with AS thereby leading to hypotension, arrhythmias, or ischemia. 3 Regional anesthesia in the form of peripheral nerve blocks could also have been an option 9 but it was avoided due to distorted anatomy on ultrasound. Landmark technique for peripheral nerve blocks and infiltration of surgical site with local anesthetic agents was avoided due to high chances of failure and local anesthetic toxicity. 10 So, considering the lesser hemodynamic fluctuations 5 with epidural anesthesia as compared to spinal or general anesthesia and possibility of titration of doses of local anesthetic agent, epidural anesthesia was considered for this patient. Furthermore, a reduced need for intra‐operative and post‐operative opioids reduced the risk of post‐operative nausea and vomiting and no loss of motor tone and excellent pain control allowed for an earlier mobilization. However, vigilant pre‐ and intra‐operative hemodynamic monitoring is vital and it may include an arterial catheter (if available) to detect early changes in blood pressure. Systemic hypotension should be avoided as it may lead to myocardial ischemia and a downward spiral of reduced contractility causing further falls in blood pressure and coronary perfusion. Drugs to maintain the systemic vascular tone such as norepinephrine, phenylephrine, or metaraminol should be available before starting the case and hypotension should be treated aggressively with these drugs initially, followed by management of the underlying cause, for example, hemorrhage. Administration of vasoconstrictors by infusion, rather than boluses, facilitates cardiovascular stability; the aim is to maintain blood pressure at normal pre‐anesthetic values. Prompt treatment of any new onset arrhythmias and maintenance of sinus rhythm and adequate intravascular volume should be the priority to maintain ventricular filling and ultimately the cardiac output and systemic perfusion. 11

CONCLUSION

Epidural anesthesia can be considered as a safe option for lower abdominal surgeries in patients with aortic stenosis where sudden drop in blood pressure and changes in heart rate need to be avoided to prevent significant morbidity and mortality. However, a larger multicenter study or review would be essential to consider sole epidural anesthesia as a safe option in patients with aortic stenosis where general and spinal anesthesia may be associated with greater hemodynamic fluctuations.
