Differences in heart DNA methylation between sudden infant death syndrome and other sudden deaths
Authors: Chad M. Aldridge, Cornelius A. Normeshie, Fern R. Hauck, Josyf C. Mychaleckyj, Keith L. Keene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS) remains a leading cause of infant mortality worldwide, yet its underlying mechanisms are largely unknown. SIDS etiology is complex and involves the interplay between a vulnerable infant, a critical developmental period, and external stressors. Cardiac function, including cardiorespiratory autonomic control, is considered a major contributor to SIDS. Emerging evidence suggests that epigenetic modifications of heart tissue, particularly DNA methylation (DNAm), may contribute to SIDS risk. We conducted an epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) to determine if DNA methylation patterns of post-mortem heart tissue differ between SIDS cases and non-SIDS controls, and to evaluate the role of DNAm in timing of SIDS events.MethodsUsing a case–control study design, we generated DNA methylation data using Illumina EPIC Methylation V2 arrays and performed single site EWAS, differentially methylated region (DMR), and epigenome network cluster analyses to assess differential DNA methylation between SIDS cases and non-SIDS controls. Post-mortem heart tissues from 324 infants (202 SIDS cases) were obtained from the Chicago Infant Mortality Study (CIMS) and the NIH NeuroBioBank (NBB). Inclusion criteria required SIDS cases to meet standardized SIDS definitions, as determined from a through case investigation (i.e., autopsy, death scene examination, and clinical history).ResultsSeven CpG sites were differentially methylated between SIDS cases and non-SIDS control (adjusted (adj.) p < 0.05). Two were located within DTNB (cg1703363; adj. p = 0.02) and NDUFS8 (cg12070987; adj. p = 0.02). There were two differentially methylated regions overlapping the KRTCAP3 and RUNX3 genes. Of the top ten most significant CpG-SIDS diagnosis interactions, two of them were located within mitochondrial genes, ACSL1 and AK2.ConclusionsThis study identified differentially methylated CpG sites and regions from heart tissue in SIDS cases versus controls, notably in DTNB, NDUFS8, KRTCAP3, and RUNX3. Our findings highlight pathways enriched for mitochondrial function, immune cell regulation, and cardiac dysfunction, which indicate the heterogenous nature of SIDS and supports the need for larger multi-tissue collaborative studies to identify SIDS subtypes, underlying pathologies, and determine the causal nature of such findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02103-2.

Background

Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS), a sudden unexpected infant death that remains unexplained after autopsy, investigation of the death scene, and review of the medical history [1], is a leading cause of sudden unexpected infant death under 12 months of age in the U.S [2]. SIDS is multifactorial in origin and the “triple risk hypothesis” [3] proposes that SIDS results when three factors coincide: a vulnerable infant, a critical developmental period in homeostatic control, and an exogenous stressor (e.g., being placed for sleep in the prone position [3]). Despite advancements in research and a marked reduction in SIDS rates following public health safe sleep campaigns, SIDS etiology and validated biomarkers of SIDS risk and diagnosis remain elusive. Growing evidence implicates dysregulated epigenetic mechanisms, particularly DNA methylation (DNAm), the addition of a methyl group to cytosine residues in CpG dinucleotides, playing a critical role in predisposing certain infants to SIDS [4–7].

DNAm regulates gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence. Aberrant methylation patterns are associated with developmental disruptions in neural, immune, and cardiac systems, all of which are hypothesized to contribute to SIDS pathology [7–9]. Notably, channelopathies like long QT syndrome [10], which are implicated in up to twenty percent of SIDS cases, may arise from epigenetic dysregulation of ion channel genes (e.g., KCNQ1, SCN5A). These findings suggest methylation disruptions in cardiac electrophysiology may precipitate lethal arrhythmias during critical developmental periods. We hypothesize that differential DNAm patterns in heart tissue exist between SIDS and non-SIDS deaths. The objective of this observational study is to explore single site and regional DNAm patterns that may provide insights into biological pathways and identity candidate biomarkers pertaining to cardiac development and function underlying SIDS risk.

Methods

Cohorts

The Chicago Infant Mortality Study (CIMS), as previously described [11, 12], was conducted between November 1993 and April 1996. and included infants who died suddenly and unexpectedly from birth to 1 year of age for SIDS cases and 1 month to 1 year of age for non-SIDS controls. Following thorough case review (autopsy, death scene examination, clinical history), post-mortem tissues and data were collected using standardized protocols including questionnaires on medical history, sleep environment, and circumstances preceding death [11–13].

The NIH NeuroBioBank (NBB) was established in September 2013 as a national resource for investigators utilizing human post-mortem brain tissue and related biospecimens for research to understand conditions of the nervous system (http://www.neurobiobank.nih.gov). We requested donated heart biospecimens from NBB infants who had a SIDS diagnosis under the age of 1 year old, and non-SIDS controls aged between 1 month and 1 year to match the CIMS cohort criteria. Tissues were collected within 24 h (NBB) or 48 h (CIMS) post-mortem and stored at − 80 °C.

NBB infant deaths were re-adjudicated to match the following SIDS definition: the sudden and unexpected death of an infant less than twelve months of age that remained unexplained after a review of the clinical history, a complete autopsy, and a death scene investigation [14].

Due to small numbers, causes of death other than SIDS (n = 139) are summarized by combining CIMS and NBB and grouping into broad categories: asphyxia (23%), respiratory infection (18%), homicide (10%), undetermined (8.6%), and other infection (7.9%) with the remaining 32.5% consisting of dehydration, congenital, injury, malnutrition, and prematurity with counts less than ten in each category. The undetermined designation was given due to various reasons, including a full investigation unable to be completed to meet the SIDS criteria, conflicting stories from observers/family members, concern for foul play, etc.

DNA mesthylation data

Genomic DNA was extracted from 375 heart tissues using a salting-out method. Sample quality was assessed using the Genomic Screen tape assay (P/N 5067–5365, Agilent Technologies) on the Tapestation 4200 (Agilent Technologies) and DNA concentration was measured by Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher). A total of 500 ng of each DNA sample was bisulfite converted (Zymo EZ DNA Methylation Lightning Kit (P/N D5046, Zymo Research), added to the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip, stained, and imaged on the Illumina iScan instrument, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. GenomeStudio was used to analyze the intensity data for ~ 930,000 CpG sites across the genome.

DNAm data underwent strict quality control using the “minfi” and “wateRmelon” R packages [15, 16]. Samples were removed with an average of methylated and unmethylated median log2 raw intensities < 10.5 (n = 1) or sex-mismatches (n = 8). Probes were removed if bead count < 3 in 5% of samples (n = 341) or detection p-value > 0.05 in ≥ 1% of samples (n = 14,806), using Y-chromosome masking for females. Raw Red/Green (dye) intensity values were normalized using the normal-exponential out-of-band (NOOB) algorithm, then DNAm beta values were calculated. The DNAm beta values represent the proportion of methylation at a specific CpG site, ranging from 0 (no methylation) to 1 (fully methylated).

There was no evidence of batch effects using MDS plots of pre-analytic factors. Secondarily probe filtering removed cross-reactive (n = 5,105), sex-chromosomes (n = 22,983), SNP-linked (n = 12,506), non-genomic (n = 6,839), and non-CpG (n = 1,915) probes, resulting in 872,512 probes from 366 samples. The DNAm beta values were further normalized using the Beta-Mixture Quantile (BMIQ) procedure to correct for bias due to differences in type I and II probes within each sample [17]. Supplement Fig. 1 summarizes the overall study design and the statistical analysis plan.

Statistical analysis plan

Descriptive statistics

We utilized R (R-project Vienna, Austria) for all statistical analysis. Numeric and categorical clinical descriptive variables were compared between SIDS cases and non-SIDS controls with parametric and non-parametric tests as appropriate.

Epigenome wide association analysis

Epigenome-wide association analyses were performed using single-site CpG linear regression models using the “limma” R package [18]. for both DNAm beta values and M-values (log2(beta/(1-beta)). The regression model used DNAm beta (and M-values) as the outcome with SIDS diagnosis as the independent variable, while adjusting for infant age at death (in days), sex, post-mortem interval (PMI) in hours, cohort (CIMS/NBB), and the first ten genetic PCs. Subsequent sex-stratified differential methylation analyses of DNAm M-values were performed. For each set of analyses, we used the “eBayes” function to moderate t-statistics and calculate the log-Fold Change of each CpG site using an empirical Bayes approach [19]. To account for multiple testing, we considered a CpG site significantly differentially methylated with an adjusted (Benjamini-Hochberg) p-value < 0.05.

Differentially methylated region (DMR) analysis

We used the “DMRcate” R package (lambda set at 1000, C at 2, and an adjusted p-value cut-off of 0.05) to identify Differentially Methylation Region (DMR) associations using DNAm M-values.

DNA methylation cluster analysis

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) [20, 21] was used to identify clusters of CpG sites based on their geometric distances and pairwise correlations utilizing the CIMS samples (n = 260). This allowed us to assess correlations between clusters and SIDS risk factors in addition to SIDS diagnosis. To address WGCNA’s inability to account for confounding among variables, we implemented elastic net regression to filter down to CpG sites associated of SIDS diagnosis without penalizing age in days, sex, PMI, and genetic ancestry (PCs 1–10). CpG sites, grouped by chromosome, were chunked into sets of CpG sites that numbered no more than nine times the cohort sample size (maximum = 2,340 CpG sites), limiting overfitting. Overfitting was further addressed using tenfold cross-validation. The CpG sites with non-zero beta coefficients were analyzed with WGCNA using the following parameters: power threshold of 8, minimum module size of 20, maximum block size of 24,000, deep split value of 2, a signed Topological Overlap Matrix. The WGCNA utilized Pearson correlation coefficients. After clustering, cluster specific eigen vectors were calculated and investigated for Module-Trait relationships with clinical data: SIDS diagnosis, sex, age at death (in days), birth weight (in grams), bed sharing at time of death, PMI in hours, and gestational age in weeks.

Gene enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis

Gene enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses used the genes linked to the CpG sites and DMRs from the EWAS analysis. For each WGCNA cluster associated with SIDS diagnosis, we perform gene enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses as well (Supplement Fig. 1). For Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichments we used the “enrichGO()” function from the clusterProfiler R package [22] with a FDR correction cutoff of 0.05. Next, we utilized the “bitr()” function to map the gene symbols to Uniprot IDs, which were passed to the “enrichKEGG()” function for KEGG pathway analysis.

Epigenome-wide interaction with SIDS diagnosis on length of life

To determine if there is a joint relationship between SIDS diagnosis and DNAm Beta-value with the age an infant died (days), we conducted an EWAS using a log-normal model with each CpG site’s Beta-value, SIDS diagnosis and their interaction on age as the outcome. We adjusted for sex, PMI, NBB cohort, and the first 10 PCs.

Differentially methylated region results

Two DMRs were identified. The first encompassed the KRTCAP3 gene (chr2:27,442,439–27,442,771, 4 CpG sites, HMFDR p = 0.005), and the second overlapped the RUNX3 gene (chr1:24,931,655–24,931,773, 5 CpG sites, HMFDR p = 0.004).

Gene enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis including genes—DTNB, NDUFS8, KRTCAP3, RUNX3—revealed GO term associations with NADH dehydrogenase activity (NDUFS8) and transcription corepressor and coregulator binding (RUNX3), and KEGG pathway associations with “Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation” and “Epstein-Barr virus infection” (RUNX3) and with “diabetic cardiomyopathy”, “thermogenesis”, and “oxidative phosphorylation” (NDUFS8).

DNA methylation clustering results

The elastic net regression procedure selected 3,033 (0.35%) CpG sites which were included in the WGCNA software, yielding eight clusters (N CpG sites ranged from 166 to 1,393), with seven clusters significantly associated with SIDS diagnosis (fdr < 0.05). None of the clusters were associated with SIDS risk factors or race/ethnicity before p value adjustment (Fig. 2). The most significant cluster associations with SIDS diagnosis were MEgrey (p = 3 × 10–18; 1,393 CpG sites), MEyellow (p = 8 × 10–10; 231 CpG sites), and MEturquiose (p = 2 × 10–9; 330 CpG sites). The MEblack and MEyellow clusters had GO Term enrichment for “myeloid cell development” (GO: 0061515) and for multiple interleukin-6 related terms (GO:0032635, GO:0032675, GO:0070741). The MEyellow cluster had pathways linked to “PD-L1 expression in cancer” (hsa05235) and “Acute myeloid leukemia”. Discussion

Epigenetic modifications, such as DNAm, may play a critical role in predisposing certain infants to SIDS [4–7]. We conducted an epigenome-wide association study of SIDS using post-mortem heart tissue. Single site association analyses identified seven CpG sites differentially methylated between SIDS cases and non-SIDS controls, two within the DTNB and NDUFS8 genes. DTNB (Dystrobrevin Beta) is a sarcolemma component of the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DPC). DPC disruption results in various forms of muscular dystrophy. Specific to heart disease, DTNB is differentially expressed between adults with dilated cardiomyopathy and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [23].

NDUFS8 (NADH Ubiquinone Oxidoreductase Core Subunit S8) plays a role in electron transfer process for mitochondria. Pathogenic variants in this gene have been linked to infantile disease onset and severity in Leigh syndrome, cancer, and diabetes mellitus [24]. SIDS cases showed 2.1% higher DNAm at NDUFS8 (cg12070987, adj. p = 0.04) than controls. As DNAm of NDUFS8 increases, subsequent protein expression would decrease, which could reduce mitochondrial electron transport.

Recently, Liu et al. [25] demonstrated that methylation of RUNX3 plays a critical role in CD8 + T-cell infiltration and differentiation [25] and RUNX3 DNAm levels has been suggested as a useful biomarker of clinical response to immunotherapy. This is consistent with our KEGG pathway analysis enrichment for the “Th1 and Th2 Cell differentiation pathway” (hsa04658). In the heart, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation is involved in immune responses contributing to inflammation and fibrosis [26], and tissue repair and remodeling [27]. Several studies have linked immune dysregulation and infections [28–30] prior to SIDS death, and further implicate the thymus, where immature T cells mature and differentiate, as an important organ in SIDS etiology. Data from our Chicago Infant Mortality Study showed higher mean thymus weight in SIDS infants [13], which is further supported by findings showing elevated thymic cytokine expression (and thymus weight) may be influenced by subclinical infection [31].T cell maturation and differentiation is critical for the immune system’s ability to identify and destroy pathogens, therefore disruptions in this process could influence SIDS risk and represent a subset of SIDS events driven by infection prior to SIDS death. Furthermore, buccal studies suggest RUNX3 DNAm may be sensitive to cigarette smoking, including e-cigarettes [32]. Smoke exposure is a known risk factor for SIDS [33].

Small differences in DNAm have the potential to be clinically meaningful in diseases, because small differences may reflect subgroups of SIDS cases. As a result, these subgroups may have larger DNAm differences than non-SIDS controls. To visualize if this may be occurring, we clustered all samples using the top twenty-five most differentially methylated CpG sites (Supplement Fig. 2). Supplement Fig. 2 displays a clustering of SIDS cases generally to the left and non-SIDS controls to the right. Moreover, there are non-SIDS controls interspersed among SIDS cases suggesting that some SIDS cases are more similar to non-SIDS controls than other cases. We would expect this data behavior when the causes of SIDS cases result in heterogenous DNAm differences [34, 35]. For example, Castela Forte et al. [21] demonstrated that clustering methods can stratify “cases” into risk gradients instead of binary “high” and “low” risk groups when applied to an heterogenous ICU population [34], suggesting that clustering methods can illuminate clinically important subgroups. Wani reinforces this conclusion by showcasing how hierarchal clustering can quantify and separate subgroups from heterogenous data [35]. However, clustering methods like k-means and hierarchal clustering can struggle with high-dimensional CpG methylation data due to high dimensionality, and inappropriate assumptions about cluster geometry that may overlook co-methylation dependencies [35]. Our approach partially addresses these limitations by using elastic net regression first to select a sparse, subset of CpG sites associated with SIDS diagnosis. This reduces noise and multicollinearity. The filtered CpG sites enable WGCNA to identify co-methylation modules, yielding module eigengenes that robustly correlate with SIDS diagnosis and improve interpretability.

When we considered co-methylation of CpG sites across the epigenome, we observed multiple clusters of co-methylation. For example, the Black cluster had an association with the cytomegalovirus disease pathway. Cytomegalovirus is known cause of infant death [36, 37] and is part of the family of herpes viruses [38]. These viruses are known to have the ability to affect heart issue leading to arrhythmias [37, 39]. More specific to heart morphology, there was an enrichment of the Wnt signaling (hsa04310) including the Grey cluster as well, which is involved in cardiogenesis and congenital heart disease [40]. The slightest alteration in expression of Wnt-activated cardiac markers in early development is related to congenital heart deficits [40]. Lastly, we assessed interactions between a CpG site’s DNAm and SIDS diagnosis in relation to when an infant died. Notably, cg1652959 in the RUNX3 gene, also identified in DMR analysis, had a linear combination of a 0.734 times shorter life span for each 10-percentage point increase in DNAm suggesting that SIDS cases died at a faster rate (younger age) than non-SIDS controls. Pathway analysis revealed multiple terms related to cardiac function (e.g., “Dilated cardiomyopathy”, “Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy”, and “Tight junction”). The top CpG site is located within the ACSL1 gene. ACSL1 is responsible for the management and removal of long-chain fatty acyls [41] in mitochondria, and is consistently upregulated in the context of neonatal sepsis in humans [42]. There is some evidence that ACSL1 protein levels in human milk are affected by environmental factors [43], which may indicate an environment-DNAm-ACSL1 mechanism in some cases of SIDS. In other words, the DNAm of ACSL1 may be an imprint of an environmental exposure.

Like ACSL1, AK2 is a mitochondrial protein regulating cellular energy homeostasis by catalyzing ATP and AMP conversion to ADP [44]. It deficiency causes neutrophile apoptosis resulting in severe congenital neutropenia [45]. Mutations of the AK2 gene cause have led to severe combined immunodeficiency in children from Chinese [46] and Indian populations [47]. This disease is characterized by the absence of T and B lymphocytes and most myeloid cells [44]. It is unknown how this relates to heart tissue specifically. However, mitochondria are highly abundant in cardiac cells due to the huge demand for ATP compared to other organs, making the heart dependent on continuous mitochondrial energy output [48]. As mitochondrial energy output decreases, heart rhythm and contraction suffer [49].

Interestingly, a microRNA (MIR4729) related CpG site (cg22754951 adjusted p = 1.8 × 10–5) was also in the top ten with a significant interaction with SIDS diagnosis on infant age (Table 3). MIR4729 is located in an intergenic region of chromosome 17 (17.q25.3). It suppresses METTL14 (m6A RNA methyltransferase) thereby reducing global m6A levels, which ultimately regulates TIE1 mRNA m6A modification in vascular endothelial cells [50]. TIE1 (Tyrosine Kinase with Immunoglobulin-like and EGF-like Domains 1) is an endothelial-specific receptor expressed on vascular and lymphatic endothelial cells, where it modulates angiogenesis, vascular remodeling, and integrity [51]. In mice, two TIE1 missense variants resulted in lymphatic edema, and lethal for homozygous mice [52].

Several consistent pathways—mitochondria, infection/immune system, and cardiogenic dysfunction (e.g., arrhythmia, myopathy) were identified in our analyses. While these biological themes have been described in SIDS for years, they have only been linked to gross and microscopic examinations or to variants of known candidate genes. Our study suggests DNAm may play a role in SIDS pathology, which has been invisible for these traditional post-mortem approaches.

Our study provides novel candidate epigenetic loci and genes as potential markers of SIDS in heart tissue, however, there are notable limitations. First, SIDS is a diagnosis of exclusion and represents a heterogenous group with varying underlying biological pathways contributing to infant death. Our study is underpowered to detect DNAm-SIDS relationships across subtypes, increasing false negative rates and highlighting the need for larger collaborative studies with sufficient power to identify SIDS subtypes.

Secondly, only SIDS cases had intensive interviews, site investigations, and re-enactments coupled with autopsy, while non-SIDS controls did not. This limits this studies ability to control for important SIDS risk factors like maternal smoking during pregnancy in our analyses comparing SIDS cases to non-SIDS controls. However, the WGCNA analysis demonstrated no strong correlations with maternal smoking or illicit drug use, bed sharing, sex, or race with the CpG clusters derived from only SIDS cases (Fig. 2). This demonstrates a lack of strong DNAm differences among risk factor exposures within SIDS.

While this study utilized an unbiased approach to discover associations between SIDS diagnosis and DNAm, it utilizes post-mortem samples preventing us from determining true causality or risk prediction. Furthermore, this design limits our ability to interpret differences in DNAm and timing of mortality between infants that died due to SIDS or other causes. While the potential for reverse causation (SIDS event is contributing to the changes in methylation) exists, this is partially mitigated by our study design (inclusion criteria) which includes deceased infants in both case and control groups, minimizing significant differences in methylation due to death. DNAm differences may reflect SIDS causation, environmental exposures, or post-mortem factors including resuscitation attempts. Even so, it is important for the field to have new candidate markers to replicate and validate prospectively. Additionally, our findings are specific to heart tissue. They do not necessarily reflect patterns of differential methylation between SIDS cases and non-SIDS control in other tissues such as whole blood, brain, or liver.

The Illumina EPIC Methylation V2 array excels in cost-effective, high-throughput profiling of ~ 936,000 CpG sites with strong reproducibility and expanded coverage of enhancers, open chromatin, and CTCF-binding regions compared to prior versions, making it suitable for EWAS [53]. However, it captures only ~ 3% of total CpGs genome-wide, thereby underrepresenting gene bodies, intergenic regions, and non-CpG methylation, potentially biasing discovery toward promoters and regulatory elements while missing distal or tissue-specific signals may be critical for heterogeneous etiologies like SIDS [54].Consequently, array limitations necessitate validation with more comprehensive technologies (e.g. bisulfite sequencing) to confirm biologically relevant subgroups.

Lastly, the EWAS models accounted for genetic ancestry through principal components that were derived from the 1000 Genomes Project, then projected onto the CIMS and NBB genotyped data. While this addresses bias from population stratification that may drive DNAm and SIDS status associations, it does not account for the social and environment influences that affect specific populations.

Conclusion

This study identified differentially methylated CpG sites and regions in SIDS cases versus controls, notably in DTNB, NDUFS8, KRTCAP3, and RUNX3. GO and KEGG pathway analyses showed enrichments for mitochondrial function, immune cell regulation, and cardiac dysfunction. We also found CpG sites located with the mitochondrial genes, AK2 and ACSL1, that interacted with post-mortem SIDS diagnosis on the infants’ age. Our findings highlight pathways implicated in SIDS heterogeneity, supporting the need for larger multi-tissue collaborative studies to identify SIDS subtypes and underlying pathologies. Similarly, other “omic” data (e.g., gene expression) will be critical in determining causal directions between exposures and markers of SIDS susceptibility. Combining epigenetic findings with genetic testing, reviews of family history, and screening methods, such as pulse oximetry, have the potential to identify infants at greatest risk for SIDS and discern SIDS subtypes, for example infants with cardiac dysfunction or developmental issues.
