Mortality effect of albumin fluid resuscitation in adults with septic shock: a systematic review and dual frequentist–bayesian meta-analysis of randomised trials
Authors: Henrique Mendes, Henry Kinkei Wen, Harry de Souza, Yuanyuan Peng, Kush Deshpande, Siebe G. Blok, Pieter R. Tuinman, Ary Serpa Neto, Marcus J. Schultz, Manu L. N. G. Malbrain, Giacomo Grasselli, Frank M. P. van Haren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo determine the effect of albumin-based fluid resuscitation on mortality in adults with septic shock.DesignSystematic review and dual frequentist–Bayesian meta-analysis of randomised clinical trials (RCTs), following PRISMA guidelines and preregistered protocol (CRD420261325998).Data sourcesPubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, and Scopus were searched from inception to February 2026.MethodsEligible RCTs comparing albumin-based resuscitation strategies versus crystalloid-based resuscitation in adults with septic shock were included. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at the longest available follow-up (up to 90 days). The analysis utilised random-effects models, subgroup analyses, and Bayesian methods.ResultsSeven trials (n = 3273) were included. Three trials primarily recruited septic shock patients, while data from the remaining were derived from subgroups or extracted strata. At the longest available follow-up, albumin-based fluid resuscitation was associated with a statistically significant 10% reduction in the relative risk of all-cause mortality (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83–0.99; p = 0.02; I² = 0%). Bayesian analysis under the primary weakly informative prior yielded a posterior probability of mortality reduction of 94.7% (P[RR < 1.0]). Pre-specified subgroup analyses by albumin formulation, dosing strategy, and trial-level baseline serum albumin did not show evidence of effect modification.ConclusionIn adults with septic shock, albumin-based resuscitation strategies are associated with a statistically significant reduction in mortality at the longest available follow-up, with a directionally concordant Bayesian estimate. A mortality benefit is therefore plausible, but the supporting evidence is indirect and imprecise (GRADE: low certainty). Adequately powered trials specifically addressing albumin fluid resuscitation in septic shock are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-06172-w.

Introduction

Septic shock remains associated with substantial mortality despite decades of progress in intensive care medicine [1]. It is a circulatory emergency in which arterial vasoplegia, hypovolaemia from venodilation and capillary leak, and sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction coexist in varying proportions [2]. Resuscitation fluids are intended primarily to restore effective circulating volume and augment preload; however, their clinical effect depends not only on the degree of initial volume expansion but also on its persistence within the circulation, redistribution across an injured endothelial barrier, and downstream effects on tissue oedema, renal function, and acid-base status [3, 4]. Accordingly, the comparative value of different resuscitation fluids is uncertain [5].

Human serum albumin is the most abundant plasma protein and the principal regulator of intravascular oncotic pressure. In septic shock, hypoalbuminaemia is nearly ubiquitous, driven by capillary leak, haemodilution, and decreased hepatic synthesis [6, 7], and each 10 g/L fall in serum albumin has been linked to a substantial rise in the odds of death [8]. Exogenous albumin has been proposed as a resuscitation adjunct on the basis of several interconnected mechanisms beyond oncotic pressure restoration: stabilisation of the endothelial glycocalyx with reduced capillary permeability [9–13], scavenging of reactive oxygen species, modulation of nitric oxide bioavailability, and prolonged plasma persistence [14–16]. By achieving haemodynamic effects with lower early cumulative fluid balance [17], hyperoncotic albumin-based strategies may plausibly limit fluid accumulation syndrome. Whether these properties translate into a mortality benefit remains the central question this review seeks to address.

Critically, existing syntheses pooled across the full sepsis severity spectrum and often mixed albumin with other colloids [18–23]. Current guidelines reflect this gap. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026 [24], the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine 2024 [25], and the International Collaboration for Transfusion Medicine (ICTMG) 2024 [26] each conditionally recommend crystalloids over albumin for resuscitation in sepsis or septic shock (moderate certainty), with the ICTMG explicitly identifying the then-ongoing ARISS trial [27] as a key evidence gap for septic shock. ARISS has since been published, with no significant mortality difference found. None of these guidelines included data from recent randomised clinical trials (RCTs) or were informed by a meta-analysis restricted to septic shock.

Furthermore, all prior meta-analyses relied solely on frequentist methods, which can be difficult to interpret when effect sizes are small, and the clinical question may be better framed as a probability of benefit [28]. Bayesian frameworks provide a natural complement, quantifying posterior probabilities that map directly onto clinical decision-making [29]. This approach is particularly valuable and has been used in studies comparing different fluid types, where true absolute risk reductions can be small [30].

We therefore conducted a systematic review with a pre-specified dual frequentist–Bayesian meta-analysis of RCTs, including subgroups or extracted strata, to determine the effect of albumin-based fluid resuscitation, compared with crystalloid-based resuscitation, on mortality in adults with septic shock.

Methods

Following a preregistered protocol (PROSPERO CRD420261325998), a systematic review with dual meta-analysis (frequentist and Bayesian) was conducted. The study was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [31]. The full protocol and search strategy are provided in Supplement 1.

Population, intervention, comparator, and outcomes

Eligible studies were randomised trials enrolling adults with septic shock, or broader randomised trials of mixed-severity sepsis reporting separately extractable treatment effects for septic shock. Hence, mixed sepsis population trials were not pooled unless outcome data for the septic shock stratum were separately extractable. Consistent with Cochrane guidance for studies including both eligible and ineligible participants, when a broader randomised trial reported separately extractable data for the eligible septic shock stratum, only that stratum was included in the meta-analysis [32]. For this review, septic shock was defined as documented evidence of acute circulatory failure occurring in the context of sepsis. Per protocol, septic shock-equivalent strata were defined as sepsis with acute circulatory failure. We accepted formal Sepsis-2 or Sepsis-3 [33] criteria or trial-defined septic shock, including vasopressor-dependent shock, SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) score cardiovascular component score of 3–4, or refractory hypotension after fluid administration.

Trials whose enrolment was restricted to a non-target population were excluded: specifically, trials strictly limited to post-surgical, obstetric, trauma, or burns patients, or trials limited to patients with decompensated cirrhosis or to patients with active malignancy as their defining inclusion criterion. Trials of mixed ICU populations that incidentally enrolled some patients with cancer or a surgical history were eligible.

The intervention was a protocolised albumin-based fluid strategy, using either hyperoncotic 20–25% or iso-oncotic 4–5% albumin, provided that the treatment window included the early haemodynamic resuscitation. Eligible comparators were crystalloid-based or usual-care fluid strategies in which albumin was not protocolised, including normal saline, balanced crystalloids, or buffered crystalloids. Trials comparing albumin with other colloids, including starches, gelatins, or dextrans, and trials comparing two albumin dosing strategies without a crystalloid-based or usual-care comparator were excluded.

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at the longest available follow-up, up to 90 days post-randomisation. Pre-specified sensitivity analyses included 28-day mortality (or the closest available timepoint ≤ 30 days), exclusion of high-risk-of-bias trials, and a fixed-effects model. Secondary outcomes included organ-support-free days (composite, ventilator-free, vasopressor-free, and renal-replacement-therapy-free days to day 28, when available). Pre-specified subgroup analyses included albumin formulation (iso- vs. hyper-oncotic), dosing strategy, and baseline serum albumin level. Bayesian hierarchical modelling was also applied for the primary outcome and sensitivity analyses.

Identification of records

A systematic search was conducted to identify all randomised controlled trials (RCTs) meeting the eligibility criteria. Electronic bibliographic databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Scopus) were searched from inception through February 2026 using prespecified search strings that combined septic shock population terms, albumin intervention terms, and validated RCT design filters. No date or language restrictions were applied. References of included articles and relevant systematic reviews were hand-searched, and trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP, EudraCT) were searched for ongoing, completed, and terminated trials.

Non-randomised studies and trial protocols were also excluded. Abstracts without a corresponding full-text publication were included, provided sufficient methodological detail was available for eligibility confirmation and risk-of-bias assessment; otherwise, they were excluded, with the reason documented.

Study selection and data extraction

Search records were imported into reference management software for semi-automated deduplication with manual verification. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant studies had their full texts retrieved and independently assessed by the same two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or referral to a third reviewer. Two reviewers independently extracted data using a piloted extraction form.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

Two reviewers independently applied the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomised trials (RoB 2.0) [34] to assess the risk of bias for each study, resolving discrepancies through consensus. This analysis informed the overall certainty of evidence assessment using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system [35].

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Dichotomous outcomes (mortality) were analysed as risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and continuous outcomes (organ-support-free days) as mean differences (MD) with 95% CIs. The primary analysis used a random-effects inverse-variance model with between-study variance (τ²) estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The I² statistic was used to quantify heterogeneity. Meta-analyses were conducted using the metafor package (R, version 4.5.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Cochrane Review Manager 5.4 (RevMan). A web-based platform [36] was used for trial sequential analysis (TSA) of the primary mortality outcome, using two-sided α = 0.05 and β = 0.20 and a heterogeneity-adjusted required information size based on the weighted control-event rate and a prespecified 15% relative risk reduction (RR 0.85), with sensitivity analyses at 20% and 10%.

The Instrument to Assess the Credibility of Effect Modification Analyses (ICEMAN) [37] was employed to evaluate the credibility of any observed subgroup effects. Baseline characteristics are described using the randomised population or reported subgroup for each trial, whereas pooled effect estimates were calculated from the outcome-specific denominators available for each mortality endpoint. Detailed subgroup definitions, forest plots, and ICEMAN assessments are provided in Supplement 3.

Bayesian analysis

A per-protocol Bayesian hierarchical meta-analysis was conducted using arm-level binomial-logit models via brms with the cmdstanr backend, with analyses prespecified and reported in accordance with recommended practices [38, 39]. Four prior families for the pooled treatment effect were specified in log odds ratio (log-OR): a weakly informative Normal(0, 1.5) as the primary prior; a sceptical Normal(0, 0.354); an optimistic Normal(− 0.15, 0.28), informed by the ALBIOS septic shock subgroup; and a pessimistic Normal(+ 0.15, 0.28). Between-study heterogeneity was regularised with half-Student-t(3, 0, 1) priors, and an LKJ(2) prior was placed on the intercept-slope correlation. Posterior draws were summarised as median risk ratios with 95% credible intervals (CrI) via marginal standardisation. Two prespecified Bayesian endpoints are reported: P(RR < 1), the probability of any mortality benefit; and P(RR < 0.95), the probability of ≥ 5% relative risk reduction or approximately 15–20 additional survivors per 1,000 treated. Posterior probabilities were interpreted along a gradient: above 0.95 as strong evidence, 0.90–0.95 as moderate, 0.80–0.90 as suggestive, and below 0.80 as inconclusive, following recent literature [29, 40]. Robustness was assessed with leave-one-out refits and a correlation-constrained sensitivity analysis. Rationale, prior specifications, arm-level input data, posterior summaries, diagnostics, and posterior-predictive checks are provided in Supplement 2, eTables A–C, eTables 1–6 and eFigures 1–4.

Results

Study selection and patients

Seven RCTs (n = 3273; 1630 albumin, 1643 crystalloid) met the protocol inclusion criteria and were included in the primary analysis (Fig. 1). Full-text exclusions were consistent with the prespecified criteria, such as studies enrolling exclusively patients with cirrhosis [41, 42] or active cancer [43], using a broad colloid strategy [44], or involving mixed-severity sepsis populations without extractable septic shock data [45, 46]. No additional eligible studies were identified through reference list screening or trial registry searches. Funnel plot asymmetry was assessed qualitatively (Supplement 1, eFig. S1).

Mortality at longest follow-up

Albumin fluid resuscitation was associated with a statistically significant reduction in all-cause mortality at the longest available follow-up (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83–0.99; p = 0.02), corresponding to a 10% relative risk reduction (Fig. 3). Estimated between-study heterogeneity was negligible (χ² = 2.54, df = 6, p = 0.86; I² = 0%).

The Bayesian hierarchical meta-analysis demonstrated a high posterior probability of benefit across all four prior families. Using the primary weakly informative prior (Normal (0, 1.5)), the model yielded an 8% relative risk reduction in mortality (RR 0.92; 95% credible interval 0.84–1.02). The P(RR < 0.95) was 73.9%, and P(RR < 1.0) was 94.7% (Fig. 4). Sensitivity analyses under alternative priors, fixed-effect models, the 28-day mortality endpoint, along with diagnostics and visual posterior predictive checks are presented in Supplement 2.

Because the optimistic prior was informed by the ALBIOS subgroup, which also contributes to the likelihood, that prior’s posterior is not independent of the data and is reported only as a sensitivity bound; the primary inference used the weakly informative prior and is unaffected (Supplement 2).

The certainty of evidence was rated as low under GRADE, downgraded for indirectness. Several major contributors enrolled broader sepsis populations, with septic shock data derived from extracted strata [SAFE], a post hoc subgroup [ALBIOS], or a prespecified subgroup of a feasibility trial [ICARUS-ED]. A second downgrade was applied for imprecision. Although the pooled 95% confidence interval marginally excluded the null, its upper limit [0.99] is consistent with a negligible effect and the accrued information reached only 63% of the optimal information size at the observed effect, with the Bayesian credible interval [0.84–1.02] also including the null.

The corresponding absolute benefit is uncertain: although the point estimate is approximately 36 fewer deaths per 1,000 patients treated (number needed to treat 28), the 95% confidence interval is wide, ranging from approximately 4 to 64 fewer per 1,000, with the lower bound representing a clinically negligible effect (Table 2 and Supplement 3).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses also aligned with the primary result: fixed-effect pooling produced an identical estimate (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83–0.99; p = 0.02). Excluding the two trials judged to be at high risk of bias (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82–0.99; p = 0.03) did not impact the estimates. In Bayesian leave-one-out analyses, no single-trial exclusion reversed the direction of effect, although the posterior probability of benefit was attenuated most after exclusion of ALBIOS (Supplement 2).

Further pre-specified sensitivity analysis for 28-day mortality included four trials (n = 2045). The pooled risk ratio was 0.88 (95% CI 0.77–1.01; p = 0.08; I² = 0%), directionally consistent with the primary analysis (Supplement 3, eFigure S5). Bayesian analysis under the primary prior yielded P(RR < 1) = 94.9% and P(RR < 0.95) = 82.2% (Supplement 2).

Secondary outcomes, subgroup analyses and exploratory data

The prespecified secondary outcome of organ-support-free days could not be quantitatively synthesised: no included trial reported a harmonisable composite endpoint to day 28, and the available proxies were not protocol-equivalent and were frequently reported only for the broader parent cohorts (Supplement 3, Section S3). Within these constraints, ventilator outcomes did not differ in ALBIOS, SAFE, ARISS, or Cusack, with a numerically higher early ventilation requirement with albumin in ICARUS-ED. Renal-replacement use was similar in ALBIOS and SAFE, with small non-significant excesses in the albumin arm in ICARUS-ED, and ICU and hospital length of stay did not differ where reported.

Vasopressor-related outcomes were the only domain with a directionally favourable signal (shorter time to vasopressor cessation in ALBIOS; lower requirement in ICARUS-ED; more catecholamine-free days in EARSS), whereas ARISS showed no difference and vasopressor days were numerically longer with albumin in Cusack. Exploratory measures were more coherent: cumulative net fluid balance was lower with albumin and statistically significant in ALBIOS (347 mL vs. 1220 mL; p = 0.004), and 24-hour fluid administration was lower with albumin in ARISS (3796 vs. 4314 mL; non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals), although the 24-hour fluid balance did not differ.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses by albumin formulation, dosing strategy, and trial-level baseline albumin category showed no statistically significant interaction (Supplement 3, eFigures S6a–c and eTable S6). Although trials classified in the ≤ 25 g/L baseline albumin subgroup (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.80–0.98; 3 trials) had a more favourable pooled estimate than those in the > 25 g/L subgroup (RR 1.16, 95% CI 0.80–1.67; 2 trials), the test for subgroup differences (χ² = 1.95, df = 1, p = 0.16) was not significant and the ICEMAN assessment did not support credible effect modification. Detailed secondary outcomes, exploratory data, subgroup forest plots, and ICEMAN assessments are provided in Supplement 3.

Trial sequential analysis

TSA indicates a consistent direction of effect but uncertainty about its precise magnitude (Supplement 3, eFigs. S2a-c). At the primary assumed relative risk reduction of 15%, the accrued information size (AIS 3,273 participants) exceeded the heterogeneity-adjusted required information size (HARIS 2,258 participants; 145%), and the cumulative Z-curve crossed the significance boundary. At an assumed relative risk reduction of 10%, corresponding to the pooled point estimate, only 63% of the HARIS was reached, and the TSA-adjusted boundary was not crossed.

Discussion

This systematic review and dual frequentist–Bayesian meta-analysis of randomised evidence found that albumin-based fluid strategies in septic shock, compared with non-protocolised albumin or crystalloid-based usual care, were associated with lower mortality at the longest available follow-up. The direction of effect was consistent across frequentist and Bayesian analyses and remained stable across alternative priors, supporting the interpretation that the signal was driven primarily by the accumulated trial data rather than prior assumptions [54].

The certainty of the evidence was judged to be low, mainly because much of the data was indirect. Additionally, the 28-day mortality sensitivity analysis did not reach statistical significance, despite being directionally consistent and of similar magnitude. This probably reflects fewer trials and patients contributing data. Overall, the evidence supports a plausible, though uncertain, mortality benefit, with the magnitude of the effect remaining imprecise. These findings should be interpreted within a broader fluid-stewardship framework in septic shock, where both the type and timing of fluid administration may influence outcomes [55, 56].

A defining feature of this review is its exclusive restriction to septic shock. Prior reviews [18–23], by pooling across different severity strata, risked diluting a real treatment effect in this subset of septic shock patients. By adopting a restrictive inclusion, this analysis targets the population in which albumin replacement may be most clinically relevant. Our findings are consistent with prior subgroup analyses of major RCTs [17, 50, 57, 58].

Trial sequential analysis (TSA) helps distinguish confidence in the direction of effect from confidence in its magnitude. At an assumed relative risk reduction of 15%, the accrued information exceeded the required size, crossing the monitoring boundary. However, at 10%, which matches the observed pooled estimate, only 63% of the required information was reached. This pattern, consistent with the Bayesian finding that P(RR < 0.95) remained below 80% under all prior specifications and with the low-certainty GRADE assessment, suggests that the current evidence supports a possible mortality reduction but remains insufficient to define the precise magnitude with high confidence.

Using ICEMAN to assess prespecified subgroups, we found no credible evidence that trial-level baseline albumin category, albumin formulation, or dosing strategy modified the treatment effect. For the hyperoncotic versus iso-oncotic comparison, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that formulations are clinically interchangeable. In the major contributing trials, the administered mass of albumin during the early treatment period may have been broadly similar despite different formulations, meaning that subgrouping by 4–5% vs. 20% concentration may not capture biologically relevant exposure. Moreover, albumin formulation, cumulative dose, timing, treatment duration, and accompanying crystalloid exposure varied across trials. Similarly, the trial-level baseline albumin analysis cannot determine whether individual patients with hypoalbuminaemia derive greater benefit.

This review has several limitations. The principal limitation of this review is indirectness. A substantial proportion of the evidence came from subgroup extractions rather than trials designed exclusively around a septic shock population with mortality as the primary endpoint. Although the inclusion of separately extractable eligible strata from broader trials is consistent with Cochrane guidance [32], these estimates remain indirect evidence for the target population because the parent trials were not always designed, powered, or primarily reported for septic shock. Quantitatively, the three trials supplying septic shock data from broader populations (SAFE, ALBIOS, ICARUS-ED) account for approximately two-thirds of the pooled statistical weight, with the ALBIOS post hoc subgroup alone contributing about half. Trial-level point estimates varied, including two trials with unfavourable estimates above unity (ICARUS-ED and Cusack), but these together contributed less than 6% of the pooled weight. The pooled point estimate was not materially altered across prespecified sensitivity analyses; however, most of its precision is derived from extracted septic-shock strata rather than from purpose-designed septic-shock trials, reinforcing the indirectness and imprecision already reflected in the GRADE rating.

The definitions of septic shock varied across trials, both in diagnostic criteria and in the absence of any haemodynamic phenotyping; no trial characterised the relative contributions of preload, afterload, and myocardial function, or reported standardised assessments of fluid status and vascular resistance. Consequently, this synthesis cannot determine the cardiovascular mechanism of any mortality effect, nor whether the vasopressor requirement reflected hypovolaemia, vasoplegia, or myocardial dysfunction; resolving this would require individual-patient haemodynamic data that the constituent trials did not collect.

Third, mortality endpoints differed across trials, potentially capturing different phases of the disease. Fourth, the mortality signal was not accompanied by consistent improvements in organ-support outcomes. Even though the planned secondary outcome analysis was limited by data availability and aggregation incompatibility, the available proxies showed no clear benefit on ventilator or renal-replacement outcomes, with only an inconsistent signal toward reduced vasopressor requirement. Finally, aggregate trial-level data prevented assessment of individual-level modifiers such as baseline serum albumin, shock severity, cumulative albumin dose, and fluid balance.

Two trials (Rackow et al. and EARSS) were at high risk of bias, though excluding both left the estimate unchanged (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82–0.99). EARSS contributed roughly a quarter of the participants but remains available only as a 2011 non-peer-reviewed abstract, with event counts derived from reported mortality percentages. Likewise, the SAFE septic shock subgroup counts appear in no SAFE publication and were obtained via personal communication [19], a source of extraction uncertainty separate from indirectness.

Further limitations include the span of more than four decades across included trials, with substantially different eras of sepsis definitions and background care, and the open-label design of most trials, which may influence co-interventions. Critically, the negligible statistical heterogeneity (I² = 0%) should not be read as clinical homogeneity. With only seven trials, I2 has limited power to detect inconsistency, and the secular, formulation, dosing, and comparator differences noted above can coexist with a low I2 value [59].

Importantly, the observed effect estimates may also be influenced by heterogeneity in albumin exposure across trials, including differences in cumulative dose, oncoticity, timing of initiation, and co-administration of crystalloids, which were not uniformly reported, limiting evaluation of albumin within a broader fluid stewardship framework [60]. Comparator fluids also differed across eras and trials, including saline-based and balanced crystalloid strategies. Because balanced crystalloids may themselves affect outcomes compared with saline, variation in the comparator fluid may have influenced the apparent effect of albumin. Finally, our findings should not be extrapolated beyond septic shock; the SAFE-TBI experience [61], suggesting harm of albumin in traumatic brain injury, is a reminder that albumin effects may be strongly population-specific.

Acknowledging these limitations, the current evidence base is not yet strong enough to support overly categorical claims about universal benefit, optimal formulation, ideal timing, or the patients most likely to benefit. While no safety concerns were identified in the included trials, albumin is substantially more expensive than crystalloids and less readily available in resource-limited settings; these factors that may influence its adoption [62].

An important interpretive issue is contamination of the control arm. In both ALBIOS (37.1%) and ARISS (48.6%), a substantial proportion of patients assigned to crystalloid nevertheless received albumin. Such a crossover would be expected to attenuate the apparent treatment effect (between-group separation) and bias treatment estimates towards the null, making the observed signal more notable, albeit complicating causal interpretation and subgroup analyses.

Future research should prioritise adequately powered septic shock trials with mortality prespecified as the primary endpoint and systematic collection of fluid balance, serum albumin trajectories, renal and electrolyte complications, and control-arm albumin exposure. Although our subgroup analyses did not show credible effect modification, future trials should stratify by baseline serum albumin levels to test whether hypoalbuminaemia identifies patients more likely to benefit. Individual patient data meta-analysis may better define responsive subgroups, while mechanistic studies of glycocalyx integrity and microcirculatory function may clarify the biological basis of the mortality signal. The now-completed ALBIOSS-BAL (NCT03654001), ongoing SWIPE2 (NCT05208242), and ALCAMIST (NCT05148286) trials are awaited. These studies range from episodic haemodynamic support to sustained correction of hypoalbuminaemia and may therefore not be directly comparable, but may clarify albumin’s role in limiting fluid accumulation, supporting deresuscitation, and informing personalised fluid strategies. Because all use 20% albumin, the effect of different formulations will remain uncertain.

In summary, in adults with septic shock, this dual frequentist–Bayesian meta-analysis of direct and subgroup-derived randomised evidence is consistent with a mortality benefit from albumin-based fluid strategies. The direction of effect was broadly consistent across sensitivity analyses and formulation subgroups, although the effect size remained uncertain. Such a benefit is therefore plausible, but the supporting evidence is indirect and imprecise. Adequately powered trials specifically addressing albumin-based fluid resuscitation in septic shock are warranted.
