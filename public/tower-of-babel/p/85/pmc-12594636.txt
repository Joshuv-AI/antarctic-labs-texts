Microstructural characterization and osteogenic efficacy of PVA/PVP blended mineral scaffold for bone tissue engineering
Authors: Swetha Sankameswaran, Lalitha Arulmani, Sureshkumar Senthuran, Shilpa Perumal Narasimhamoorthi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in bone tissue engineering and dental regenerative medicine have made strides in the development of several biomaterials. Optimizing the chemical and physical milieu of scaffold is required to induce osteogenesis for faster bone regeneration. In this study, polymer blend of Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA) and Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) doped with nHAP-ZnO Np was prepared by a solution casting technique. Structural and physiochemical characterization was performed. In vitro cytotoxicity analysis was performed through tetrazolium-based assay (MTT) assay and the differentiated cells were subjected to alkaline phosphatase assay (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) analysis respectively. Scanning Electron microscopic (SEM) analysis showed a rough and uniform matrix arrangement of the PVA-PVP blend. Crystallites properties and functional groups was confirmed by X ray diffractometer (XRD) analysis and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) respectively. The optimal water absorption capacity was observed in PVA-PVP-nHAP-ZnO Np scaffold (P3) and also degradation pattern was analysed for PVA-PVP (P1), PVA-PVP-nHAP (P2) and PVA-PVP-nHAP-ZnO Np (P3) scaffolds where P3 scaffold holds high stability compared to P1 and P2 scaffolds. In the thermal stability analysis, PVA-PVP (P1) and PVA-PVP-nHAP-ZnO Np (P3) scaffolds showed an overall stability up to 270 °C. Highly miscible blends of PVA-PVP and 1 wt% nHAP – ZnO Np was observed with semi-crystallinity in Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) analysis. The mechanical property of the PVA-PVP-nHAP-ZnO Np (P3) scaffold has shown an increasing trend in tensile strength analysis. The cytotoxic study of scaffolds showed 84% of cell viability confirming high biocompatibility than compared to plain scaffold. the elevated level of ALP and calcium deposition was observed in loaded scaffold (P3). Thus, PVA-PVP-nHAP-ZnO Np (P3) scaffold supports and induces osteogenesis and can be used as biomaterial in bone regenerative medicine.

Introduction

Metabolic bone diseases such as osteoporosis and traumatic fractures often result in extensive loss of bone tissue, posing significant clinical challenges. Traditional treatment strategies-including metal implants, bone grafts, and tissue transplants—aim to restore mechanical integrity and facilitate bone regeneration; however, these methods often come with limitations such as immune rejection, limited availability, and insufficient integration with host tissue [1] In recent years, bone tissue engineering (BTE) has emerged as a promising alternative, offering biologically guided solutions to address these limitations. BTE involves the fabrication of biocompatible scaffolds designed to mimic the natural extracellular matrix (ECM), serving both as mechanical support and as a biological platform that promotes osteogenesis by releasing growth factors and activating cellular signalling pathways critical for bone repair [2].

Modern scaffold designs incorporate a wide range of bioactive materials like bioactive glass (SiO₂, P₂O₅, and B₂O₃), bioceramics (calcium phosphate, hydroxyapatite, β-tricalcium phosphate) and natural (e.g., chitosan, collagen) and synthetic polymer (poly(caprolactone), polyvinylpyrrolidone, poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid), polyvinyl alcohol, and poly (glycolic acid) that enhance cellular interactions and accelerate healing [3]. Polymeric scaffolds are particularly significant due to their favourable intrinsic properties like adjustable solubility, customizable crystallinity and adequate mechanical strength, which make them desirable for load-bearing applications across various fields such as ophthalmology, dentistry, and orthopaedic bone repair [4]. PVA and PVP are widely used synthetic polymers in bone tissue engineering due to their excellent biocompatibility, mechanical strength, and stability. PVA offers chemical resistance and bio-inertness, while PVP enhances cell adhesion and physiological compatibility. Their low cost, miscibility, and strong hydrogen bonding make the PVA–PVP blend a promising scaffold material for bone regeneration [5, 6]. During bone regeneration, Ca:P minerals release Ca²⁺ ions, promoting osteoblast differentiation and ECM formation [4, 7, 8]. To mimic this, scaffolds are incorporated with β-tricalcium phosphate, hydroxyapatite, and bioactive glass which replicates Ca:P chemistry. In this context, nHAP has been included with PVA–PVP scaffolds that mimics bones inorganic matrix. Despite extensive research on the individual biomedical applications of nano-hydroxyapatite (nHAP) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONps), their combined incorporation into a miscible PVA–PVP polymer matrix remains largely unexplored. To address this gap, the present study integrates both nanomaterials into a PVA–PVP scaffold, aiming to harness their complementary bioactive and mechanical properties to enhance scaffold performance and osteogenic potential. The primary goals include characterizing the scaffold’s chemical modifications, crystallinity, structural integrity, fluid absorption, mechanical strength, and thermal stability. In addition, the study aimed to evaluate the biological efficacy of the scaffold through its effects on cell viability, adhesion, proliferation, and matrix mineralization, using assays like ALP and alizarin red on osteoblast-like SaOS2 cells.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of nano-HAP

Following the wet chemical method, an alkaline pH of 11 was maintained throughout the entire synthesis using NaOH at an optimal temperature of 80 ± 5 °C. Firstly, 0.03 M of aqueous (NH4)2HPO4 was added dropwise onto 0.05 M Ca (NO3)2.4H2O aqueous solution and subjected to constant stirring till pH reaches alkaline and forms white precipitate. Upon completion of the reaction, the solution was sonicated for 8 h in the bath sonicator along with the precipitated deposits. The produced nHAP was collected by centrifugation at 9000 rpm for 30 mins and the collected powder was dried for 24 h at room temperature. Then this powder was calcinated at 600 °C for 1 h to retrieve the purified nHAP [9].

Synthesis of ZnO NPs

One pot synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) was prepared using the chemical co-precipitation method. 0.02 M zinc acetate dihydrate was dissolved in 100 ml distilled water and stirred vigorously. Then 2 M NaOH was added drop by drop at room temperature till it attained a pH 12 and this was placed in a magnetic stirrer for 2 h. Upon the completion of the reaction white precipitate was formed which was washed with ethanol to remove the impurities and set for drying in a hot air oven overnight at 60 °C. Finally, complete drying ensures the production of ZnO NPs from Zn (OH)2 [10].

Doping of ZnO with nHAP

1 g (W/V) of nano-hydroxyapatite (nHAP) was soaked in 0.012% of ZnO Np and it was stirred for 24 h at 200 rpm. Further centrifugation of this solution at 10,000 rpm resulted in nHAP-ZnO Np and the unreacted supernatant solution was washed using distilled water. These recovered particles are collected and dried at 60 °C. As a final step, a fine powder was obtained by grinding the particles with a mortar and pestle [11].

Scaffold fabrication

UV and ethanol sterilization was strictly maintained throughout the scaffold preparation in a sterile hood. PVA and PVP hybrid scaffold was prepared in a 6:4 ratio as an optimal blending combination for which 24 ml of 11 wt % PVA (Av.Mol.Wt 60000–125000) was stirred with distilled water in a hot plate at 95 °C for 30 min and 16 ml of 11 wt % PVP (Mol. Wt ⁓40000) was prepared separately in a hot plate and stirred for 1 h at 100 °C and till the volume rose to 50 ml. 1 wt % nHAP composite solution was prepared by continuous stirring in 50 ml H2O for 1 hr and added into the prepared 50 ml polymer blend solution and let stir for 2 hr at 80 °C. Further, 0.2% of glycerol was added to the total volume of the polymer composite solution right before it is poured into a petri dish and dried at room temperature (RT) to form PVA-PVP-nHAP which is denoted as P2. Similarly, nHAP – ZnO Np composite solution was prepared by adding 1 wt % nHAP- ZnO Np in 50 ml of H2O and allowed it to stir for 2 hr and poured into polymer blend solution forming a PVA-PVP-nHAP-ZnO Np composite which is considered as P3. Simultaneously, Plain polymer without the nHAP or nHAP-ZnO Np composites was also prepared as P1 [12]. (Figure 1) Weight percentages of P1, P2 and P3 scaffolds given in Table 1.

Characterisation

SEM

The surface morphology and roughness of the casted scaffold (P3) of dimension 1 cm*1 cm and thickness of 5 mm were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM JSM – IT800). ZnO Np and nHAP also subjected to SEM analysis.

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was used to study the formation of the polymer blend of the composite scaffold. The spectra were recorded at Resolution 2 cm−1 ranging from 500 - 4000 cm−1 using FTIR IRspirit_DESKTOP-4UREJCM-instrument1

XRD

The crystallinity of ZnO Np, nHAP and P3 scaffold was determined using a powdered XRD instrument- D8 diffractometer at room temperature operating at a voltage of 40 kV. The samples were cut in 1 cm×1 cm dimension and thickness of 5 mm and then scanned at 2θ range of 0◦–90◦ at a speed of 2 min−1.

PSA

Size of ZnO Np and nHAP was determined using particle size analyser for the confirmation of nanosized particles. Malvern Panalytical instrument was utilized for the investigation of size at temperature 25.1 °C. Polydispersity index also analysed.

TGA

To determine the decomposition temperatures of the composite scaffold 200 mg of each of the samples went through aluminium pans for thermo gravimetric analysis at 40–600 °C using a Thermo Gravimetric analyser (TGA 4000– PerkinElmer).

Tensile strength

The mechanical characteristic of scaffold was determined by performing tensile tests with the Column sample of 1 cm × 9 cm and a thickness of 5 mm using Instron universal testing machine E-3000 at 50 KN and cross head speed of 5 mm/min in accordance with ASTM D3039.

Biocompatibility

Scaffold sample preparation and sterilization

The scaffolds were sterilized under UV for 30 min and then subjected to ethanol washing (70%) thrice and air-dried in a laminar hood for 2 h. Before using for cell culture studies, the scaffolds were pre-wetted with 2 ml of the respective media for 1 hr.

Cell culture with DMEM and ODM (osteogenic differentiation medium)

SaOS2 osteoblast-like cells were cultured on the scaffolds in DMEM media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10,000 units/ml penicillin –10,000 μg/ml streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 in the air. The culture media was changed frequently for every three days. Similarly, Cells were cultured on the scaffolds in osteogenic differentiation medium containing 10% DMEM, 10 nM dexamethasone, 5 mM beta-glycerophosphate, and 50 µg/ml L-Ascorbic acid-2-phosphate and this media was changed every 3 days [8].

Cytotoxic assay

Cell proliferation and density of cell attachment of the P1, P2 and P3 scaffold were determined by MTT assay. The three scaffolds were cut into circular discs and placed in the 24 well plates in triplicates. 5 × 104 cells per well on the scaffold was incubated and media alone in a well is considered as blank control and cells seeded on P1 is considered as positive control. After the cells reached their confluency, 100 µl MTT solution (5 mg/ml in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The formazan crystals were then solubilized with 1 ml DMSO, then the OD was measured at 490 nm and 630 nm with a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). Simultaneously, the assay was performed on the cells cultured on scaffolds with the ODM to compare [15].

ALP assay

As an early marker of osteogenesis ALP levels were quantified using direct method. In this assay, Alkaline phosphatase catalyses the cleavage of p-NPP to produce p-nitrophenol and orthophosphate which exhibits a yellow colour. To the 24 h preconditioned scaffolds P1, P2 and P3 in 6 well plate, 1 × 105 cells were seeded onto scaffold with ODM supplements and incubated till 3rd and 7th day with changing of medium every 2 days Fig. 1. At the end of the treatment period the cells attached with scaffolds were washed thrice with PBS (pH 7.4). The cells were lysed with 0.5 ml of lysis buffer (0.1 M Glycine, 1 mM MgCl2 and 1% triton X- 100 in PBS solution) for 45 – 60 min followed by centrifugation for 10 min at 5000 rpm. To the 100 µl of cell lysate, 250 µl of 1 mg/ml pNPP substrate prepared in Glycine buffer was added. This mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and the absorbance was read at 405 nm using microplate reader (Biotek – ELX 800).

Alizarin red assay

Calcium deposition in P1, P2 and P3 composite scaffold was determined qualitatively by direct alizarin red staining. To the 24 h preconditioned scaffolds P1, P2 and P3 in 6 well plate, 1 × 105 cells were seeded onto scaffold with ODM supplements and incubated till 3rd and 7th day with changing of medium every 2 days. At the end of the treatment period the cells attached with scaffolds were washed thrice with PBS (pH 7.4) and fixed with 4% (v/v) formaldehyde at RT for 15 min. Then the cells were stained with 2% ARS solution (pH 4.2) for 15–20 mins at RT in dark. After staining the cells were washed with distilled water and observed using bright field microscope (Optika, Itally). For quantitative estimation, the bound ARS was dissolved in 800 μL of 10% (v/v) acetic acid incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The red-coloured solution obtained was spectrophotometrically measured at 430 nm microplate reader (Biotek – ELX 800) and the samples were run in triplicates [9].

Statistical analysis

All the results are expressed as mean and SD. Results were subjected to one-way ANOVA (Tukey multiple comparison test) showed significant difference between control and treated cells. Treated cells showed high significance compared to control cells.

Discussion

Polymeric nanocomposites have been extensively studied as scaffolds due to the advantages of polymers as matrices, including simpler fabrication compared to ceramics and metals, along with their biodegradability and biocompatibility. While biocompatibility is often a focus, the mechanical strength of scaffolds under physiological conditions remains suboptimal for load-bearing applications, especially for implants used in bone tissue engineering. The current work presents a novel scaffold system composed of a polymer composite with optimized ZnO nanoparticles, which enhances mechanical strength and promotes sustained osteogenesis, ensuring prolonged implant applications providing micro and macro environment. The P3 scaffold (PVA-PVP-nHAP-ZnO Np) fabricated achieves part macro (compact bone) and part nano (HA and another mineral) structure of the bone matrix.

After optimizing the polymer weight percentage, polymer ratios, the quantity of Glycerol as a plasticizer, nHAP, and nHAP-Zn wt% percentage the ideal scaffold was achieved. Weight percentages from 5% to 30% of PVA and PVP have been used in previous studies [5, 11, 16]. Moreover, PVA and PVP with 10–20 wt% were reported to show enhanced stability for a stable scaffold, and in this study, after several optimizations based on stability in PBS, 11 wt% for both polymers were used. The degree of miscibility and biocompatibility determines the elevated property of the polymer blends, though both PVA and PVP are hydrophilic polymers, the instability of PVP in terms of weak mechanical strength in water was mitigated by blending it with PVA at an optimized PVA/PVP solution ratio of 6:4 [7]. Furthermore, ZnO Np addition in nHAP has been demonstrated to significantly improve the osteoblast-like SaOS2 cellular response in previous studies [4]. Thus, P3 with formulation of 1 wt% nHAP-ZnO Np in PVA-PVP scaffold was fabricated using the solution casting method. SEM analysis was conducted to characterize the polymer blends and nanocomposites. As anticipated, the absence of chemical crosslinkers resulted in a lack of distinct pore formation. The addition of nHAP-ZnO nanoparticles aimed to enhance mechanical strength and bioactivity which led to the development of a rough surface texture with particle aggregation. The uniform appearance of the scaffold and absence of phase separation confirmed effective miscibility and proper blending of the polymers.

XRD analysis showed results consistent with FTIR findings, confirming the presence of the nHAP-ZnO nanocomposite. The observed diffraction peaks correspond to hexagonal and wurtzite crystalline structures representing characteristic of the ZnO nanoparticles. Additionally, the PVA–PVP blend exhibited a broad peak below 30°, indicating a slight shift in crystallinity due to polymer blending [10]. Particle size analysis of the synthesized ZnO Nps and nHAP revealed average sizes of 179.9 nm and 195 nm, respectively. These results are consistent with SEM and XRD analyses, further confirming the successful incorporation of both nanoparticles into the composite. FTIR analysis further validated the successful blending of PVA and PVP, along with the incorporation of nHAP particles. A key distinction between the FTIR spectra of the P1 and P3 scaffolds is the presence of a sharp absorption band at 1024.13 cm⁻¹ in the P3 scaffold, attributed to phosphate groups from nHAP. Furthermore, the peak at 552.7 cm⁻¹ in the P3 scaffold—corresponding to metal–oxygen (M–O) stretching—represents the characteristic ZnO absorption and appears more prominently, consistent with previous reports [7, 17, 18].

The swelling behaviour of the scaffolds is important in assessing its performance since excessive water absorption affects the scaffold morphology whereas insufficient absorption inhibits cell growth due to lack of water. P3 scaffold exhibits the highest water retention capacity. This behaviour was attributed to the interaction between the P–OH groups present on the nHAP in the P3 scaffold and surrounding water molecules. The nHAP-ZnO nanoparticle filler due to its high surface wettability facilitates interaction by providing a sufficient interface for water diffusion. Moreover, no visible dissolution was observed upon visual inspection with maintenance of excellent film integrity. This stability may be due to the lower proportion of hydrophilic PVP relative to PVA in the blend resulting in control over excessive water uptake [16, 19–21]. Biodegradation investigation demonstrated that scaffold P1 degraded rapidly, likely due to the hydrophilic characteristics of its polymeric components PVA/PVP. In contrast, scaffold P3 exhibited a slower and sustained degradation profile. This indicates that the incorporation of ZnO nanoparticles enhances structural stability and contributes to a reduced degradation rate, thereby improving the overall biodegradation behaviour of the scaffold [14].

PVA–PVP blends, semi-crystalline polymers characterized by well-defined endothermic peaks. The DSC curve of the P3 scaffold suggests that the addition of the nanocomposite did not significantly affect the crystallinity of the polymer blend. A comparable result was reported by Gökmeşe et al. (2013) [13, 22]. Additionally, as reported by previous studies the glass transition temperature (Tg) of a PVA-PVP blend typically lies under the range of 70 °C-190 °C [23, 24]. In this study, a gradual and broad glass transition temperature (Tg) ranging from 90 °C to 150 °C was observed, suggesting that the Tg of the P3 scaffold did not experience a significant shift compared to the P1 scaffold. The observation of a single Tg indicates that the PVA–PVP blend is fully miscible [19]. The retention of both crystallinity and Tg may be attributed to the relatively low weight percentage of nHAP-ZnO nanoparticles which can be considered an optimal concentration.

The TGA analysis investigating the polymer blend and nanocomposites shows good overall thermal stability for both scaffolds. However, comparing two endothermic peaks of P3 and P1 scaffold showed 40 °C and 10 °C reduction in the melting temperature of PVA at 300 °C, 260 °C and PVP at 440 °C, 430 °C respectively [11, 25]. According to Lilleby Helberg et al. 2020, two separate peaks confirms the presence of two different polymers blended properly with peaks around 290 °C and 410 °C for PVA –PVP respectively [19]. The weight loss percentage indicates vaporized water molecule at various temperatures was higher in P3 scaffold than in P1, due to the presence of nHAP-ZnO Np with significant water retention capacity. Also, the weight loss of the P3 scaffold was observed at 10 °C earlier than P1 scaffold due to the boiling point of nHAP which is higher than ZnO. Due to the introduction of Zn ions into the apatite and the interference of nHAP-ZnONp particles into the PVA lattice,P3 was susceptible to temperature above 270 °C [7].

An ideal scaffold should possess sufficient strength to support tissue growth and load-bearing with flexibility to resist shear fracture. A previous study found that adding up to 5% ZnO improved the compressive strength of the scaffold [26]. Tensile analysis in this study demonstrates that both scaffolds exhibit reliable tensile strength, supporting the optimized weight percentage and blending ratio of the polymers. This change in behavior may be attributed to the flexibility and elasticity imparted by PVA when blended with PVP, as PVA acts as a high molecular weight plasticizer [27]. Several studies on polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) composites with zinc, strontium, and nHAP (PGLA-Zn-Sr-nHAP) have shown that 2.5 wt% Zn enhances tensile strength, while 4 wt% Zn results in a reduction in tensile strength [24]. Similarly, a study on PGLA with 3 wt% Zn-Sr-nHAP alloy showed higher ultimate tensile strength compared to a 6 wt% formulation [28]. Another study involving polycaprolactone (PCL) and hydroxyapatite (HA) with 3 wt% ZnO demonstrated improved tensile strength over a 6 wt% ZnO formulation [29]. These findings indicate that incorporating ZnO at levels below 3 wt% maximizes tensile strength in scaffold materials. This trend aligns with the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) results of the P3 scaffold, which shows enhanced strength with 1 wt% nHAP-ZnO, thus providing greater mechanical stability compared to the P1 scaffold without nHAP-ZnO.

The addition of hydroxyapatite (HAP) was found to enhance the biocompatibility of the scaffold, with the P2 composite showing increased cell viability and improved biocompatibility [21]. The MTT assay conducted with osteogenic differentiation media (ODM) showed a significant increase in cell viability on third day, particularly in P3 scaffold compared to the P2 scaffold. This improvement may be attributed to the formation of zinc phosphate from ZnO, which interacts with nHAP and reduces the toxic potential of ZnO. The P3 scaffold contains 0.012 wt% of ZnO nanoparticles within the acceptable range noted in earlier studies, which reported non-toxic at concentrations of 1.2–1.5 wt% ZnO, while promoting alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and cellular proliferation [13] confirming biocompatibility. Osteogenic differentiation was higher in osteoconductive media compared to normal growth media with an increase in ALP activity and calcium deposition observed on the 3rd and 7th days in P3 scaffold. On day 7, elevated ALP levels were noted with the P3 scaffold due to the enhanced dissolution of Zn²⁺ ions which act as a cofactor for ALP, increasing its catalytic activity. This also leads to a localized spike in Zn²⁺ concentration at the cell-scaffold interface, supporting efficient Zn²⁺ uptake by the cells, rather than its dissolution into the culture media [30, 31]. Additionally, the functional properties of the P3 scaffold were favourable on day 7, supporting the osteoblastic phenotype of the cells [12]. These results align with a previous study where ZnO concentrations below 1.2 wt% were shown to stimulate ALP activity [32]. P3 scaffold also showed high mineralization due to agglomeration of nHAP-ZnO Np on the polymer creating a rough surface as surface topography which influence the adhering cell and matrix mineralization [31]. There was steady increase in mineralization with the P3 scaffold over the 3rd and 7th day denoting ZnO NP with nHAP posess an additive effect on mineralization [13]. nHAP-ZnO Np interrupt the chain mobility of PVA-PVP polymer making it slightly less crystalline which facilitates the release of Ca2+ and inorganic phosphate (Pi) into the extracellular environment forming apatite crystals with other minerals, initiating formation of extracellular matrix (ECM) [10].

Conclusion

In conclusion, our research demonstrates that the nHAP-ZnO nanoparticles are well-dispersed in aggregates, contributing to surface roughness which was observed by SEM morphological analysis. The XRD spectra confirms the presence of nHAP-ZnO nanoparticles without disrupting the PVA-PVP blend structure which was further supported by DSC analysis. The comparison of the nHAP-ZnO scaffold to the plain PVA-PVP polymer scaffold revealed no significant differences, indicating the preservation of the semi-crystalline nature. Additionally, the presence of a single glass transition temperature (Tg) in both scaffolds suggests complete miscibility between PVA and PVP polymers. TGA results showed no detrimental changes in the thermal stability of the P3 scaffold and the water retention assay which recorded minimal fluid absorption while maintaining film integrity.

The incorporation of ZnO nanoparticles at an optimized concentration resulted in enhanced tensile strength. In vitro assays demonstrated that the P3 scaffold is non-toxic to SaOS-2 cells and promotes increased ALP activity and bone mineralization. PVA/PVP polymer blend reinforced with 1 wt% nHAP-ZnO nanoparticles demonstrates strong osteoinductive potential. Overall, the optimized ZnO incorporation significantly improved both mechanical strength and osteogenic activity confirming its potential long-term implant applications.
