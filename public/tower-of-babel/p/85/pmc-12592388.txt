Shaping an interdisciplinary AI ecosystem to drive local and global innovation: an interview with Benjamin Rosman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Benjamin Rosman is a Professor in the School of Computer Science and Applied Mathematics at the University of the Witwatersrand (Wits), in Johannesburg (South Africa), where he runs the Robotics, Autonomous Intelligence and Learning (RAIL) Laboratory. Since November 2024, he is also the founding Director of the Machine Intelligence and Neural Discovery (MIND) Institute at Wits, focused on the fundamental science of intelligence in machines, humans, and animals. Dr. Rosman is a leading voice in artificial intelligence (AI) research and has received prestigious awards for his contributions to advancing AI in Africa and beyond. In August 2025, he was named in TIME’s annual list of the 100 most influential people in AI. In this Q&A, we discussed his career journey, the role of biology in the development of robotics and AI, and his actions to establish Africa as a global leader in foundational AI research.

Can you tell us about your career journey?

Let me rewind all the way back… I was born with a physical disability following a stroke, which left me with cerebral palsy and a right-sided hemiplegia. This means that when I was a kid, I wasn’t into climbing trees and playing sports. Instead, I found my way into computer programming when it was still a niche field, which then led me to get really interested in game design, digital worlds, interactions with machines, and mathematics. This really inspired my early decision-making and career path. During my first college years at Wits, I was studying computer science and mathematics, but somewhere along the line I decided that AI was more interesting than computer games. But at that time, in the early 2000s, AI was really a “new” thing, and there weren’t great options to enter the field in Africa. So, for my Master’s, I went to the University of Edinburgh (Scotland), which had a large and important department with a long history of working with AI. I started off in robotics and wasn’t exactly sure what I wanted to do afterwards, but I really liked my supervisor and the research field, and I decided to stick around for my PhD during which I worked on autonomous decision making, at the intersection of decision theory, machine learning and robotics.

Nowadays, when people talk about AI, it’s all about ChatGPT and similar programs, but at its core, AI is usually about making predictions. Even with large language models, they are predicting the next words, and so on. To me, all these predictions are always in the service of something and can be encapsulated by the AI and robotics problems of continuously interacting with some environment. I always found the way living creatures operate in their environments to be fascinating, considering the decisions they take and the reasons for doing so, which really sparked my current scientific journey. As a result, my research interests focus primarily on reinforcement learning and decision making in autonomous systems, which look at feedback loops between individuals, robots (generally called agents) and the environment. And then in that space, I’m really interested in questions around generality and safety: for instance, how can learning be facilitated through abstracting and generalizing knowledge gained from solving related problems? Or how do we build better agents capable of interacting meaningfully and safely with the human world?

As you mentioned, the robotics and AI research fields have often taken inspiration from living organisms and their interactions with the environment. Does research in biology and biomimicry still contribute to the development of robotics and AI systems?

It’s a very interesting question and there’s always been this debate about the contributions of natural and artificial intelligence to innovation. I think this question is becoming increasingly relevant as there are closer connections between biological and artificial systems than there were ever before. The fields of biology and machine learning have much potential to influence each other and synergize to accelerate science discovery by helping formulate novel hypotheses and ask very targeted questions. This interplay between these fields creates exciting crossovers and new research directions. From an engineering and computer science angle, many ideas in machine learning are at least loosely inspired by biology, and we are always looking toward natural systems for new ideas in building better and more capable AI systems. On the other hand, from a biological angle, AI can help develop simulated models to better understand complex biological systems and provide us with analogs to gain deeper insight into our own brains. In fact, this was a core philosophy behind the creation of the MIND Institute. We currently have 34 academic Fellows and only about a third of them are AI experts. The rest includes neuroanatomists, behavioral psychologists, cognitive neuroscientists, philosophers, evolutionary scientists, and policy specialists. This interdisciplinary mix of different people brings multiple perspectives to improve our understanding of the fundamental science of intelligence in machines, humans, and animals.

The question of using biological knowledge to improve artificial systems appears particularly relevant to the development of energy-efficient AI technologies. For instance, up until recently, we’ve known only one structure able to create poetry, and that’s the human brain. Now we also have large language models. So, this is a kind of existence proof that there are at least two different routes to write poetry–one biological and one artificial. However, there are many differences between them. Artificial systems can run forever, but they need a tremendous volume of data and they consume a huge amount of energy and resources. To solve this issue, one approach would be to build larger power plants and data centers; but a more intellectually interesting solution would be to look for energy-efficient biological systems in the natural environment that developed similar capabilities in brains, using roughly the energy required to power a light bulb.

Given the broad biodiversity in Africa, I would also think that this continent is at a higher advantage than most to develop technological solutions from the natural environment.

Exactly! I am convinced that diversity is a strength in science. If you’re in a place with a large diversity of organisms that have developed to adapt to their specific environment or have learned to solve problems in different ways, it’s undoubtedly an advantage for science. Africa has also a great diversity of human cultures and human ancestries in the fossil record. For instance, there’s the Cradle of Humankind - a paleoanthropological site located about 50 km northwest of Johannesburg - which contains the largest known concentration of hominid fossils in the world. I believe that playing with all these strengths provides this broad tapestry of different data points reflecting how intelligence is used, evolves or is repurposed to solve different problems. Now merging all this data with the modern computational toolbox is a powerful approach to supercharge research and advance innovation for Africa and the world.

One year ago, you became the founding Director of the Machine Intelligence and Neural Discovery (MIND) Institute. What are the MIND Institute’s core missions and research activities?

The African AI community is doing so much exciting work in building impactful AI solutions that address local challenges. We have seen over the last few years a substantial growth of AI across Africa thanks to various movements and initiatives like the Deep Learning Indaba summer school, and many others. A growing number of African researchers and developers have been pioneering the use of AI to tackle the continent’s issues and have been increasingly challenging the concentration of power and technological leadership in AI.

However, until recently, there was no African organization focusing on advancing the science of AI itself. It’s great to have so many people thinking about the downstream applications of the technology, but it is also critical to invest in the fundamentals. This is important to gain strategic insights, ensure accessibility and appropriateness of the developments to local settings, and provide greater economic opportunities. Our core idea in launching MIND was to capitalize on the fact that we’ve been building critical mass in this field at our university for a long time and it was time to take our research work at the next level by making it more interdisciplinary and by leveraging this diversity of thoughts and perspectives. This is where our strength lies. In addition, the reality is that the African academic world is particularly under-resourced. Therefore, if we can increase the concentration of researchers who are thinking about related disciplines, it will be more likely for us to find interesting solutions. And different philosophies and diverse background views can be a recipe to resolve problems that can be valuable globally. For these reasons, the core focus area of MIND is the fundamental science of intelligence, both artificial and natural, as this provides a rich cross section of disciplines that still have much common ground. The institute is meant to bring together a critical mass of academics from various research fields, and to encourage them to collaborate in a radically interdisciplinary way to come up with new projects that aren’t just variants of existing technologies and ideas but really challenge the norms, and build partnerships that place Africa at the heart of global AI. The continent should be a leader in the foundational aspects of the field and not just an end-user of applications or data. For example, one project involves a collaboration between computer scientists, philosophers, and psychologists, aiming to explore new approaches to evaluating how aligned autonomous decision makers are with ordered human moral values, as well as designing systems that are better able to avoid violating these moral norms.

You also co-founded the Deep Learning Indaba summer school. What motivated this initiative?

The Indaba is now an annual event, and the most recent one was held in Rwanda in August 2025 with more than 1300 participants! When we started in 2017, eight of us were involved in organizing it. We were all Africans who had studied or were working abroad, and we wanted to give back to the community in some way. So we thought, let’s run a workshop! The idea was to host a deep-dive technical workshop that runs for several days because we hadn’t seen anything like this here before. We also thought it would be a one-off event. We tried to reach out quite widely and, in the end, 350 people attended the first workshop held at my university in South Africa in 2017, with hundreds more who applied that we couldn’t accommodate. We realized that we were onto something!

What we really wanted to do is strengthen African machine learning. There is so much value in this discipline, which can be used in so many ways to supercharge with other areas in science, various kinds of businesses, or even cultural initiatives. Providing people who are interested in AI with a rich and deep understanding of these incredibly powerful tools opens the door to so much innovation. I feel strongly that what we need in Africa is a new culture of innovation, to create thousands of startups, and to empower young people to invent crazy things. And through the Indaba, it all started by planting the seed of what would soon flourish into an entire AI community. When we started this initiative, I could probably count on one hand the number of people I knew who knew people in more than five different African countries. Now there is this broad community of researchers and trainees who collaborate on different projects across the continent; the Indaba itself is run by more than 80 volunteers from every corner of Africa. We also support satellite events in 47 African countries. It has become a pan-African movement supported by countless people who are teaching, doing academic research or working in industry, launching communities and startups, all together contributing to build and shape a resourceful African AI community of people who care about similar kinds of questions, issues and progress.

There have been several grassroots initiatives that have grown out of the Indaba, such as Masakhane (around African natural language processing), SisonkeBiotik (around AI and health), and AfriClimate AI. These in turn have led to waves of new events, investments, and companies. For instance, I co-founded Lelapa AI with five colleagues, as a startup focused on helping Africans access technology in their own languages. One initiative of the company was to launch InkubaLM, a language model trained on five African languages with broad reach: Swahili, Hausa, Yoruba, isiZulu and isiXhosa. This was Africa’s first multilingual small language model. Other important projects and movements have been developed and launched through the Indaba community to address the adverse effects of climate change, questions around food insecurity, ecological disasters and human conflicts, education and health, finance, and many other areas where new AI technologies are already contributing positively. The Indaba has also provided more visibility to the African AI community, which resulted in more funding flowing into the continent. Tech companies such as Google and Microsoft have now research labs in Africa, many Master programs have been set up, and there are now so many more opportunities for students to go study abroad. The success of the AI grassroots community in Africa has not only been key for the continent, but also a promising path towards inclusive growth at a global level, and the impact of this highly energetic and motivated community is only beginning to be felt.

What is one misconception about AI (and particularly its use in basic research) that you’d like to clarify?

A misconception that I find surprisingly common in basic research and more broadly among people outside of the AI field, is that when communicating with large language models, those are not simply searching a database of known facts. Instead, they construct their responses, word by word, through the activations of artificial neurons, in a manner vaguely akin to how similar tasks are processed in the brain. Having a more accurate analogy to how these systems work is important for understanding what they can and can't do, and how to think about our relationship to them, in everything from their potential benefits to risks.
