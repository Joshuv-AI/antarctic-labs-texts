Health of peri-implant soft tissues adjacent to glazed or polished monolithic zirconia: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Douglas Blum Segalla, Eduardo Rolim Teixeira, Rosemary Sadami Arai Shinkai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEMaterial surfaces can influence the biological response of soft tissues, particularly during the early stages of healing and maturation. This study assessed whether the type of surface finish (glazed or polished) of monolithic zirconia single crowns impacts the health of peri-implant soft tissues during the first 6 months of clinical function. Infrared thermography was employed to assess soft tissue conditions.MATERIALS AND METHODSThis was a prospective, randomized, intra-participant clinical trial. Twenty single crowns, supported by posterior implants, were fabricated in monolithic zirconia using computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing. For each crown, the mesial and distal surfaces were randomly allocated to receive either a glazed or a polished surface finish. Data were collected at 7 (T1) and 180 days (T2) following crown placement, using clinical examinations (assessing pain/discomfort, biofilm formation, bleeding, inflammation, and suppuration) and infrared thermography (to record thermogram values in Celsius). The data were analyzed using both descriptive and inferential statistics.RESULTSNo significant clinical differences in peri-implant soft tissue health were identified between the glazed and polished surface treatments at T1, T2, or across the evaluation period. Infrared thermography revealed a significant decrease in temperature from T1 to T2 for some polished and/or glazed subgroups when comparing the peri-implant mucosal phenotype, dental arch, and tooth regions. However, no significant differences were observed between the polished and glazed groups.CONCLUSIONBoth glazing and polishing are suitable surface treatments for monolithic zirconia and do not adversely affect peri-implant soft tissue health within 6 months after crown installation. Infrared thermography has the potential to be a complementary tool for the objective evaluation of soft tissue healing.

INTRODUCTION

The clinical success of implant-supported prostheses depends on the proper maintenance of peri-implant tissues, which can be influenced by the biocompatibility of dental materials.1 The peri-implant mucosa forms a biological seal analogous to the junctional epithelium surrounding natural teeth, serving as a crucial barrier against microbial infections (a risk factor for peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis).1234 This peri-implant junction is composed of three epithelial types: peri-implant, peri-implant sulcular, and oral. Although the supracrestal connective tissue around the teeth is richly vascularized, the peri-implant tissue has a limited blood supply, with the vessels representing the terminal branches from larger vessels originating from the periosteum.5

Healing of the soft tissue complex (epithelium and connective tissue) around implants occurs within 6–8 weeks after surgery. However, the maturation of the complex can take several months.6 Both the chemical and physical properties of abutment and prosthetic materials can influence the functional seal of the peri-implant mucosa and local biofilm formation.78910 Microbial adhesion, proliferation, and colonization depend on the material surface properties, including biocompatibility, surface free energy, and topography (roughness).

Microorganism colonization around implant-supported prostheses begins immediately upon exposure to the oral environment, and within weeks, the microbiota closely resembles that observed around natural teeth.111213 Moreover, a rough surface can accelerate microorganism adherence and accumulation, with a detrimental impact on soft tissues in contact with the dental restoration.14 Therefore, the material surface finish, such as ceramic glazing or polishing, can modify the biological response of peri-implant tissues. This potential effect may be more critical during the early stages of healing and maturation when soft and hard tissues are significantly susceptible to infection and trauma.15

In relation to restorative materials, the polycrystal-line tetragonal zirconia stabilized with yttrium oxide (3Y-TZP) offers an aesthetic alternative to metallic frameworks and demonstrates mechanical properties superior to those of other dental ceramics.111213 161718 Monolithic zirconia restorations fabricated with computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) technology have also demonstrated good survival rates, satisfactory aesthetics, reduced laboratory and chairside time, and decreased biofilm accumulation. 171920212223 As monolithic zirconia restorations do not require the addition of porcelain, they can be finished with polishing or glazing.242526 Zirconia crowns are often glazed, which can produce a wavy and rough surface, favoring bacterial adhesion.14 Alternatively, ceramic polishing is often recommended,2526 with some in vitro studies demonstrating that polished ceramic crowns have better physicochemical and biocompatibility properties than those of glazed surfaces.2728 However, biofilm adherence may differ between glazed and polished surfaces.27 The literature currently lacks prospective, controlled clinical studies investigating the effect of monolithic zirconia surface finish on peri-implant soft tissues.

The clinical assessment of soft tissue healing and maturation is often based on visual inspection and probing for bleeding, which may introduce a subjective bias owing to variations in clinical expertise. Infrared thermography detects infrared radiation emitted by objects with temperatures above absolute zero.29 This technology is a non-invasive, radiation- free imaging modality that quantifies surface body temperature changes, which serve as direct indicators of inflammatory processes.3031 Inflammation generates heat due to physiological reactions such as the secretion of histamines and kinins, increased vascular permeability, and the release of endogenous pyrogens, resulting in a localized temperature increase.3031 Studies have demonstrated that temperature differences exceeding 3℃ may indicate infection, and thermography can distinguish between various inflammatory conditions based on the extent of temperature rise.30 For instance, in periapical inflammatory lesions, acute abscesses exhibited significantly higher mean temperatures (37.26 ± 0.36℃) compared to those noted in chronic abscesses (35.03 ± 0.63℃) and acute pulpitis with apical periodontitis (36.07 ± 0.45℃).2 This suggests that thermography is capable of detecting inflammatory reactions even in preclinical stages.31 The acquired images and corresponding thermograms were used to generate a pixel map, with temperature values represented using a color scale. As a rapid and non-invasive screening technique, infrared thermography provides real-time data on the dynamics of microcirculation in the skin and mucosa, allowing for the detection of functional, nervous, and vascular changes resulting from inflammatory processes.3233 Previous clinical studies have utilized this objective method to assess inflammatory conditions and/or healing in dental surgery, oral candidiasis, temporomandibular disorders, and periapical lesions.323334

Therefore, this randomized clinical trial aimed to assess whether the peri-implant soft tissues adjacent to monolithic zirconia crowns were affected by the surface finish (glazed or polished) during healing and initial maturation of clinical function. The null hypothesis stated that the zirconia surface finish would not affect peri-implant health. The study aimed to evaluate the effect of surface finish on the hygiene index, the gingival bleeding index, infrared thermography measurements, and overall patient satisfaction within a 6-month follow-up period following crown placement. In addition, the study evaluated the potential of infrared thermography as an objective tool for assessing peri-implant soft tissue conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a prospective, randomized, paired clinical trial with an intra-participant comparison. The research protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committees of the Pontifical Catholic University of Rio Grande do Sul and the Military Polyclinic of Porto Alegre, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05081076) and in Plataforma Brasil (CAAE 45255621.2.0000.5336).

The sample consisted of 12 consecutive patients (seven women) with a mean age of 59.9 ± 9.4 years, in need of oral rehabilitation with single implant-supported crowns and treated by an experienced specialist at the Military Polyclinic of Porto Alegre, in Porto Alegre, Brazil. Twenty monolithic zirconia crowns were supported by posterior implants. Each crown featured one glazed and one polished surface (mesial or distal), resulting in a total of 40 surfaces evaluated.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: adult patients; a minimum of 2-month healing time for extractions in the region of interest, adequate bone volume as assessed by preoperative computed tomography and clinical evaluation, and the need for single crowns supported by bone-level implants in the posterior region of the maxilla or mandible. Patients were excluded if they had a history of major systemic diseases; previous head-and-neck radiotherapy; current treatment with steroids and/or bisphosphonates; neurological or psychiatric disorders that could interfere with oral hygiene; heavy smoking (more than 10 cigarettes per day), or a history of illicit drug use or alcohol abuse.

All the crowns were supported by bone-level implants (Straumann Dental Implant System; bone-level or bone-level tapered type with NC or RC platforms). After selecting the transfers, analogs, and prosthetic components, intraoral scanning was performed. The crowns were milled in monolithic zirconia (Amann Girrbach; Mäder, Austria), using the CAD-CAM technique in a certified dental prosthesis laboratory.

The crowns were internally blasted with aluminum oxide and cemented on metal abutments (Variobase or Anatomical Abutment; Straumann Dental Implant System, Basel, Switzerland) with RelyX U200 Self-Adhesive Cement (3M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany), using implant analogs outside the mouth. The photopoly-merization procedure was performed according to the manufacturer’sinstructions.

For each monolithic zirconia crown, the glazed or polished surfaces were randomized to the mesial and distal sides. Either the mesial or the distal side received one surface finish treatment at random, glazing (VITA AKZENT Plus, Bad Säckingen, Germany) or polishing (EVE DIACERA Finishing and Polishing Kit; EVEErnst Vetter GmbH, Bad Säckingen, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The glaze was applied immediately after milling without any prior polishing, following the standard procedure adopted by a specialized laboratory that fabricated all the crowns. Thus, the cervical region of each zirconia crown had both glazed and polished surfaces on the mesial and distal sides. All crowns were installed in accordance with the standardized routine clinical protocols.

For qualitative surface characterization, single barshaped specimens of monolithic zirconia were subjected to the same finishing treatment as the crowns. Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)(Dimension Icon, Bruker, MA, USA) and NanoScope Analysis 1.7 software were used to illustrate the topography of the glazed and polished surfaces (Fig. 1).

A single examiner, blinded to the treatment allocation, examined all patients at 7 (T1) and 180 days (T2) after crown placement. Before removing the crowns, a soft probe was gently used to assess for bleeding, and the crowns were extracted without rotation to minimize tissue disruption. Data were collected through clinical examination and direct visual inspection (Fig. 2A), with a focus on peri-implant health parameters: oral hygiene index (biofilm), bleeding on probing, inflammation, and suppuration.35 After manual probing, the crown was removed, and the presence of inflammation was recorded. The type of peri-implant mucosa (present or absent) and gingival phenotype (thick or thin) were assessed visually using a periodontal probe and a soft probe by evaluating tissue transparency.36 Patient satisfaction and comfort (pain, irritation, bleeding when flossing, etc.) were also recorded.

Infrared thermography was performed using a portable thermal infrared camera (FLIRC5; Teledyne-FLIRLLC, Wilsonville, OR, USA). Before the clinical appointment, patients were instructed to avoid consuming cold or hot foods for at least 1 hour. For the infrared thermography examination, the patient was acclimated to the room temperature of the dental office of approximately 2℃, instructed to keep their mouth closed for 5 minutes and to breathe normally. Any excess saliva was rapidly swallowed, and no suction or air blast was used. An intraoral image was captured at a distance of 15 ± 5 cm to encompass all soft tissues in the region of interest, with an occlusal angle optimized to visualize the mesial and distal areas of the implant (Fig. 2B).

The thermograms were analyzed using a FLIR THERMAL STUDIO (1.9.58) software with a thermography color scale.23242526 For each implant, the mesial and distal areas were analyzed using a temperature measurement tool (elliptical tool). The highest and the lowest temperatures for each selected area (mesial or distal) were averaged, and the mean value was computed for statistical analysis. The temperature was measured in Celsius centigrade (℃), the unit of measurement used by a handheld thermal infrared camera.

Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics with SPSS version 22.0, at a significance level of 0.05. Categorical data on peri-implant health were analyzed using the chi-square test. Continuous data from infrared thermography were analyzed using a paired Student’s t-test for both inter- and intragroup comparisons.

RESULTS

Overall, no significant clinical differences in peri-implant health (biofilm formation, bleeding on probing, inflammation, suppuration, and pain/discomfort) were observed between the glazed and polished surfaces at T1 and T2 (P > .05). Infrared thermography revealed a statistically significant decrease in temperature from T1 to T2 in certain polished and/or glazed subgroups with differences observed based on peri-implant mucosal phenotype, dental arch, and tooth regions (Table 2). However, no significant differences were observed between the polished and glazed groups.

DISCUSSION

This clinical trial in a real-world dental setting revealed that dentists can safely use glazed or polished monolithic zirconia crowns supported by dental implants in the posterior region. During the initial 6 months of prosthesis function, both surface-finish treatments elicited similar clinical responses in the peri-implant soft tissues throughout the healing and maturation phases. Moreover, the objective measurements obtained via infrared thermography suggested that the peri-implant soft tissues exhibited no signs of subclinical inflammation (higher temperatures) and were not negatively affected by either surface finish treatment over time.

Zirconia has become a popular choice for implant-supported restorations due to the excellent mechanical and aesthetic properties and biocompatibility of the material. Moreover, zirconia is a chemically inert material that does not cause adverse effects or induce tissue reactions.1416171819 Zirconia offers the advantage of being simpler to process than restorations made with ceramics applied in layers, resulting in reduced overall costs. Recently, monolithic zirconia restorations fabricated with CAD-CAM technology have been increasingly used worldwide due to technological advances.17181920212223 For instance, the use of monolithic zirconia for posterior implant-supported crowns reduces complication rates, such as less ceramic chipping or fracture.20 Conscious clinical decision-making requires comparing procedures largely used in the routine clinics that still lack experimental evidence on clinical endpoints.

Most studies comparing glazed and polished ceramic surfaces assessed in vitro surrogate endpoints such as physicochemical and biocompatibility properties, as well as biofilm formation.142425262728 One common outcome assessed in vitro studies is surface roughness, especially the parameter Ra (arithmetic average roughness), which represents the absolute height average in relation to the base length, i.e., the average difference between peaks and valleys.202122 To illustrate the surface topography of the two finishing treatments in the present study, one specimen from each treatment group was observed using AFM. Both surfaces exhibited a pattern of peaks and valleys, with values of Ra of 0.12 micrometers for the glazed surface and 0.07 micrometers for the polished one. Although some studies have identified statistically significant differences in surface roughness,262728 their biological implications may not be relevant in the real world, with the possible interference of patient-related characteristics in the ultimate clinical performance of zirconia crowns.

In this clinical trial, the allocation of a polished or glazed surface in contact with the peri-implant soft tissue was randomized on the mesial and distal surfaces of the same crown. Therefore, the study design (similar to a split-mouth approach) allowed for a comparison between the two types of surface finishing treatments on monolithic zirconia within the same crown, thereby controlling for some intra-participant confounding factors such as oral hygiene, occlusal force, and immune conditions. The finishing procedures followed a standardized protocol by a single specialized laboratory technician. Polishing was carried out using new rotary polishing rubbers and silicone polishers with a high concentration of diamonds developed specifically for zirconia. Glazing was performed using a commercial kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and standard laboratory equipment.

Overall, the study findings supported the use of both surface-finishing procedures in routine dental practice. Regarding the clinical parameters of biofilm, bleeding on probing, inflammation, suppuration, and pain/discomfort, no statistically significant differences in peri-implant soft tissue health were observed between glazed and polished treatments at T1, T2, and over time. Infrared thermography revealed a significant decrease in temperature from T1 to T2 for certain polished and/or glazed subgroups when comparing the peri-implant mucosal phenotype, dental arch, and tooth regions. However, no significant differences were observed between the polished and glazed groups. The magnitude of variation in the measurements using infrared thermography was minimal and may not reflect a clinically relevant difference in temperature. This method offers an objective measure of the local temperature variation, which is significantly evident in large extensions or highly inflammatory processes.29

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first clinical study to assess the peri-implant soft tissue health using infrared thermography. This exploratory clinical study demonstrated the feasibility of the methodology for the peri-implant soft tissue assessment. However, possible refinement of methodological procedures, such as the use of custom-made accessories, may help improve the standardization of image acquisition positioning. As the intraoral region of interest was small and located in the posterior region of the mouth, standardizing image acquisition and strictly controlling for humidity fluctuations during in vivo measurements were challenging in contrast to extraoral evaluations. Further clinical studies should confirm the potential of infrared thermography as a complementary tool for the non-invasive and objective evaluation of peri-implant soft tissues during healing and other inflammatory oral conditions.323334

This clinical study had certain limitations due to a small sample size, which may have affected the statistical power of the study. In addition, clinical data were collected 7 and 180 days after the start of the study, a few months before the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Several patients were classified as a high-risk group for COVID-19, and during 2020 and 2021, strict restrictions on mobility and non-essential activities were enforced in the city. Therefore, for ethical and safety reasons, we had to limit the sample size and reduce the follow-up appointments to the minimum clinically acceptable level, resulting in deviation from the original research protocol. However, intra-participant comparisons (longitudinal comparisons of the polished and glazed surfaces in the same crown) reduced several possible confounders, and the negative results were consistent with clinical observations. Thus, the study findings suggest that no clinically relevant difference in peri-implant health was present in the first 6 months of function. Nevertheless, long-term clinical studies are warranted, as the response of soft tissues may change over time with the modification of systemic health and oral microorganisms.

CONCLUSION

Glazed and polished surfaces of monolithic zirconia demonstrated similar clinical biocompatibility with peri-implant soft tissues over the first 6 months after crown placement in a real-world clinical setting. Infrared thermography has the potential to be a complementary tool for the objective measurement of soft tissue healing.
