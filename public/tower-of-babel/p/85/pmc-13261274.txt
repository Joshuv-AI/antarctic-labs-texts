Body mass index and waist-hip ratio – Risk factors for aortic valve disease: The atherosclerosis risk in communities (ARIC) study
Authors: Chunxiao Zhang, Ethan Moser, Anne Eaton, Jeremy Van’t Hof, Weihong Tang, Amil Shah, Aaron R Folsom, Lin Yee Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElevated body mass index (BMI) has been associated with increased risk of aortic valve stenosis (AS) in observational and Mendelian randomization studies; however, its association, and that of waist-hip ratio (WHR), with aortic valve insufficiency (AI) remains unclear. We investigated the association of BMI and WHR with incident aortic valve disease (AVD) in the ARIC Study.MethodsWe analyzed ARIC participants with echocardiograms at Visits 5 (2011–13) and 7 (2018–19). BMI and WHR were assessed at Visit 5. Incident AVD between Visits 5 and 7 was categorized as aortic valve sclerosis, AI without stenosis, and AS. Multinomial regression models were employed.ResultsAmong 1931 participants (mean age of 73.5 ± 4.2 years, 59.7% female, and 23.2% Black), 572 (29.6%) developed AVD (345 aortic valve sclerosis, 159 AI, and 68 AS). Higher BMI was associated with a greater incidence of aortic valve sclerosis (OR: 1.29 per 1-SD (5.08 kg/m2), 95% CI: 1.13–1.48) and AS (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.00–1.63), but not AI (OR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.75–1.14). Higher WHR was associated with an increased risk of incident aortic valve sclerosis (OR: 1.17 per 1-SD (0.079), 95% CI: 1.00–1.35), AS (OR: 1.44, 95% CI: 1.11–1.85), and also AI (OR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.01–1.53).ConclusionsHigher BMI is a risk factor for incident aortic valve sclerosis and AS, but not AI. Greater WHR is a risk factor not only for incident aortic valve sclerosis and AS, but also for incident AI. Avoiding abdominal obesity may prevent non-rheumatic AVD in older adults.Keywords: Aortic valve disease, Body mass index, Waist-hip ratio, Aortic valve insufficiency, Abdominal obesity, Aortic valve stenosis, Aortic valve sclerosis

Introduction

Aortic valve disease (AVD), comprising primarily aortic valve stenosis (AS) and aortic valve insufficiency (AI), is common among older adults. AVD is associated with an increased risk of various adverse events, including all-cause mortality, incident heart failure (HF), and incident atrial fibrillation (AF) [1,2]. Recent guidelines categorize AVD into four stages (A, B, C, and D) [3], and even early stages (A and B) are associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes [1]. The underlying causes and risk factors for non-rheumatic AVD are not fully established, but current evidence suggests contributions from atherosclerotic risk factors, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes [4,5].

Obesity, particularly the accumulation of visceral adiposity, causes metabolic disorders that contribute to hypertension, dyslipidemia, and atherosclerosis [6]. Specifically, obesity has been increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to calcific AVD. Observational studies and recent Mendelian randomization analyses suggest that higher BMI is associated with increased risk of AS [7,8]. Yet, a recent study using large-scale administrative data indicated that obesity was not associated with the occurrence of aortic valve disease [9]. Few investigations of this association have incorporated repeated, research-grade echocardiographic phenotyping or examined central adiposity measures. In contrast with AS, the few cohort studies of obesity and AI have reported, at most, a weak association [10]. Therefore, important questions remain regarding the relative contributions of overall versus abdominal adiposity to distinct AVD phenotypes. Additionally, previous studies have studied mostly White adults, so there is essentially no information on whether obesity is associated with AVD in Black individuals.

We therefore explored the association between obesity, as assessed by body mass index (BMI) and waist-hip ratio (WHR), and incident AVD in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study, a population-based cohort of mostly Black and White adults. We hypothesized that higher BMI and WHR would be associated with an increased incidence of AS and AI, as documented by repeat 2-dimensional echocardiography (2DE).

Methods

The ARIC Study received approval from the institutional review boards of all participating centers, including Wake Forest University, Johns Hopkins University, University of Mississippi, University of Minnesota, University of North Carolina, and Brigham and Women’s Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from every participant at each visit. The data used in this analysis are available from the ARIC Coordinating Center upon approval of a paper proposal. This study was carried out following the STROBE cohort reporting guidelines [11].

Study design and setting

The prospective ARIC Study recruited mostly Black and White adults aged 45 to 64 years to its Visit 1 baseline exam in 1987–1989. A total of 15,792 participants were enrolled from four U.S. communities: Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi; Washington County, Maryland; and the northwest suburbs of Minneapolis, Minnesota [12,13]. Participants have undergone multiple follow-up visits, along with continuous surveillance for hospitalizations following the baseline exam. Our study focused on the association of BMI and WHR at Visit 5 (2011–13) with the new onset of AVD based on the difference between repeated 2DEs done at Visits 5 and 7 (2018–19).

As depicted in Fig. 1, 6538 participants attended ARIC Visit 5, and 6118 of these underwent the 2DE examination. Participants with prevalent AVD identified by 2DE at Visit 5 were excluded from follow-up, including those with bicuspid aortic valve. We also excluded participants with a history of heart valve replacement or repair, a history of AF or HF, or whose reported race was not Black or White or was Black at the Minneapolis or Washington County field centers (due to low numbers). After these exclusions, 3642 participants, free of any stage of AVD, remained for follow-up after Visit 5. Of these, 1931 returned to Visit 7 and completed the follow-up 2DE examination for incident AVD.

BMI and WHR measurement

BMI at Visit 5 was calculated as technician-measured weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. BMI cut points were defined in accordance with WHO guidelines: normal or underweight (BMI < 25), overweight (25 ≤ BMI < 30), and obese (BMI ≥ 30) [14]. WHR was calculated from technician-measured waist circumference at the umbilicus divided by maximal hip circumference at Visit 5 [15]. The cutoff point of WHR was defined according to the WHO guidelines as women ≥ 0.85 and men ≥ 0.9 [16].

2D-Echocardiographic examination

The Echocardiography Reading Center, located at Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston, MA, developed all the protocols related to 2DE imaging and analysis, the training and certification of sonographers, the quality assurance plan, and the statistical methodologies [17]. 2DEs were obtained using the same protocol at Visits 5 and 7 using specialized Philips iE33 Ultrasound systems equipped with Vision 2011 and X5–1 xMatrix transducers to acquire 2-dimensional, Doppler, and 3-dimensional data.

AVD definition

Trained readers at the Echocardiography Reading Center reviewed the 2DEs at Visits 5 and 7 and identified AVD. AS was defined as a narrowing of the aortic valve opening that restricted blood flow from the left ventricle to the aorta. It was quantified with transvalvular aortic valve peak jet velocity and aortic valve area at Doppler. AI, sometimes called aortic regurgitation, was defined as the leakage of blood through the aortic valve each time the left ventricle relaxes. AI was qualitatively assessed based on the color Doppler signal.

AS and AI stages A to D were classified in accordance with ACC/AHA guidelines [3,18]. Stage A AS was defined as aortic valve sclerosis with peak velocity by Doppler echocardiography (Vmax) from 1.5–<2.0 m/s or bicuspid valve. Stage B AS included mild (Vmax from 2.0–<3 m/s) or moderate AS (Vmax from 3–<4 m/s or mean pressure gradient across the aortic valve by Doppler (ΔP) from 30–<40 mm Hg). Stage C AS was referred to as severe AS (Vmax ≥4.0 m/s or mean ΔP ≥ 40 mm Hg). Stage D AS denoted severe AS with symptoms or angina. Meanwhile, Stage A AI was defined as aortic valve sclerosis with Vmax from 1.5–<2.0 m/s or bicuspid valve. Stage B AI included mild or moderate AI from qualitative measurement from echoes. Stage C AI included qualitatively severe AI. Stage D AI denoted symptomatic and qualitatively severe AI [1].

Considering the stages overlap between AS and AI, especially for stage A, we reclassified AS and AI for analysis into four mutually exclusive categories: (i) no-AVD, (ii) aortic valve sclerosis, (iii) AI without stenosis, and (iv) AS. No-AVD was defined as having no evidence of AI or AS at any stage. Aortic valve sclerosis was defined as having stage A valve disease. AI without stenosis was defined as having stage B, C, or D insufficiency in the absence of any stenosis. AS was defined as having stage B, C, or D stenosis regardless of insufficiency.

Covariate measurements

Covariates included sex, race, and educational level from ARIC Visit 1, and age, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, coronary heart disease (CHD), systolic blood pressure (mmHg), antihypertensive medication use, HDL-c, LDL-c, and eGFR from Visit 5. Participants selected their predefined race categories: American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian or Pacific Islander, Black, or White. The medication usage of participants was documented by examining the medication bottles they brought to the appointments. Blood pressure was measured thrice following a 5-minute rest, and the average of the last two measurements was used for analysis. CHD was identified based on three sources: self-reported physician diagnoses at Visit 1, myocardial infarction diagnosed from an electrocardiogram at an ARIC visit, or hospitalized definite or probable myocardial infarctions through Visit 5 [19,20]. Diabetes was classified by a fasting glucose level ≥126mg/dL, nonfasting glucose ≥200mg/dL, anti-diabetic medication used in the past two weeks, or self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes. HDL-c was measured in plasma collected at Visit 5 using an enzymatic assay from a Beckman Coulter Olympus AU 480 (Beckman Coulter, Inc.). LDL-c was estimated from the Friedewald formula based on direct measures of triglycerides, total cholesterol, and HDL-c [21]. GFR was estimated using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation based on cystatin C [22]. Cystatin C in plasma was measured using a particle-enhanced immunonephelometric assay (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics), followed by standardization and calibration [23].

Statistical methods

Among the baseline characteristics, continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (25th percentile and 75th percentile, (Q1, Q3)), and categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages (%). Student's t-test was used to compare the mean of the continuous variable with a normal distribution. Given that analyses were based on participants free of AVD on the Visit 5 2DE, incident AVD was defined by the identification of AVD on the Visit 7 2DE. To estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) associating continuous or categorical BMI and WHR with the four categories of AVD, we used multinomial regression models. Multivariable models were adjusted as follows: Model 1 adjusted for demographic factors–age, sex, race, and education. Model 2 additionally adjusted for comorbidities, including smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, CHD, systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive medication use, HDL-c, LDL-c, and eGFR.

We performed race-stratified analyses to ascertain the consistency of associations of BMI and WHR with AVD in Black and White participants separately. Multiplicative interactions by race in the fully adjusted multinomial model were tested using the likelihood ratio test. We also estimated population attributable fractions (PAFs), which were the approximate proportions or percentages of AVDs in the population that might hypothetically be prevented by preventing overweight and obesity, using the formula described in the Supplemental Methods.

Because not all participants free of AVD on Visit 5 2DE returned for Visit 7 2DE, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that included incident AVD cases identified through hospitalization discharge diagnoses between Visits 5 and 7 (i.e., hospitalizations from 2011–2021). The hospital discharges for AVD were categorized into one of our four AVD groups, where possible, using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) discharge codes. AVD was represented by ICD-9 code 424.1 (unspecified as to type of AVD) until 2015 and by ICD-10 code I35.X afterward. We defined Unspecified Hospital AVD based on ICD-9 424.1 or ICD-10 I35.9. We also conducted additional sensitivity analyses using waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio as alternative measures of abdominal adiposity in place of WHR. Finally, we examined the associations of waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio with incident AVD through Visit 7 or identified from hospitalization discharge ICD codes.

Results

As shown in Table 1, the 1931 participants at risk for incident AVD at Visit 5 had a mean age of 73.5 ± 4.2 years; 60% were women, and 23% were Black individuals. At Visit 7, 6.5 (SD = 1.9) years on average after Visit 5, the 2DE examination identified 572 (29.6%) participants with new AVD: 60% (n = 345) had developed aortic valve sclerosis, 12% (n = 68) had AS, and 28% (n = 159) had AI without stenosis. At baseline, the participants with incident AVD were 0.7 years older (p = 0.001) and had a 0.6 kg/m2 higher average BMI (p = 0.03), 0.1-unit higher WHR (p = 0.001), and 1.9 mmHg higher systolic blood pressure (p = 0.02) than those without incident AVD.

BMI and incident AVD

Visit 5 BMI, measured as a continuous variable, was associated significantly and positively with both incident aortic valve sclerosis and incident AS but not AI (Table 2). Each 1-SD (5.08 kg/m2) increment of BMI was associated with a 1.30-fold greater odds of aortic valve sclerosis after adjusting in Model 1 for demographics (OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.16–1.47) and 1.29-fold (95% CI: 1.13–1.48) after additional adjustment in Model 2 for comorbidities. Each 1-SD increment of BMI was also associated with 1.30-fold higher odds of incident AS in Model 1 (95% CI: 1.02–1.66) and 1.28-fold (95% CI: 1.00–1.63) in Model 2. Race-stratified results are presented in Supplement Table 1. For both Whites and Blacks, the ORs associating 1-SD increment of BMI with aortic valve sclerosis or AS were >1.14, but the OR confidence intervals were wide given reduced race-specific sample sizes. There was no statistically significant difference in the AVD associations with BMI between Blacks and Whites (interaction p > 0.05).

We also analyzed BMI in categories. As shown in Fig. 2, 73.6% of normal-weight participants remained free from AVD versus 67.5% of obese participants. The proportion with incident aortic valve sclerosis was 13.4% in normal-weight participants versus 21.8% in obese participants, and the proportion with incident AS was 2.5% in normal-weight participants versus 3.8% in obese participants. The incidence of aortic valve sclerosis increased across BMI categories: 13.4% in normal/underweight, 17.3% in overweight, and 21.8% in obese participants. The incidence of AS was 2.5% in normal/underweight and 3.8% in both overweight and obese groups. Waist-Hip ratio and incident AVD

As shown in Table 3, WHR at Visit 5 was positively and significantly associated not only with incident aortic valve sclerosis and AS, but also with incident AI. A 1-SD (0.079) increment in the continuous WHR was associated with 1.24-fold greater odds of AI in Model 2 (95% CI: 1.01–1.53). The corresponding Model 2 OR for aortic valve sclerosis was 1.17 (95% CI: 1.00–1.35) and 1.44 for AS (95% CI: 1.11–1.85).

Associations of WHR with AVD did not differ by race (interaction, p > 0.05). A higher WHR was positively associated with incident AS in both racial groups. In Model 2, each 1-SD increase in WHR (0.079) was associated with a 37% higher odds of AS in Whites (OR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.04–1.80) and 2.24-fold higher odds in Blacks (95% CI: 1.05–4.79), though the CI was wide due to the sample size in Black participants. In addition, higher WHR was associated with increased risk of aortic valve sclerosis among Whites (OR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.02–1.43, Supplement Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

A total of 35 extra incident AVDs were identified via ARIC surveillance of AVD hospitalization discharge codes, among which 8 were aortic valve sclerosis, 8 were AI, 14 were AS, and 5 were unspecified hospital AVDs. When adding these 35 participants to our analytic sample, the results were largely unchanged (Supplement Tables 4 and 5). A logistic regression analysis was also conducted, analyzing the association between categorical BMI and binary incident AVD. The results showed that participants with obesity (BMI ≥ 30) had 1.44-fold greater odds of incident AVD compared to those with normal weight (95% CI: 1.07–1.94, p = 0.017, Supplemental Method and Supplemental Table 6).

The associations between waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio at Visit 5 with incident AVD through Visit 7 are shown in Supplement Tables 7 and 8. Similar to the findings for WHR, waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio were both consistently and positively associated with incident aortic valve sclerosis (OR: 1.32 per 1-SD increment (12.91 cm) in waist circumference, 95% CI: 1.15–1.51; and OR: 1.32 per 1-SD increment (0.077) in waist-to-height ratio, 95% CI: 1.15–1.51) and incident AS (OR: 1.44 per 1-SD increment (12.91 cm) in waist circumference, 95% CI: 1.13–1.83; and OR: 1.54 per 1-SD increment (0.077) in waist-to-height ratio, 95% CI: 1.22–1.94) after full adjustment. However, waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio were not associated with AI incidence. Similar results were observed after additionally incorporating 35 incident AVD cases identified through ARIC surveillance of hospitalization discharge codes into the analytic sample (Supplementary Tables 9 and 10).

Discussion

In this community-based cohort study of older adults, we found that greater BMI and WHR were moderately strong risk factors for incident AVD measured by 2DE after an average of 6.5 years of follow-up. Specifically, BMI and WHR were associated positively and independently with new-onset aortic valve sclerosis and AS; in addition, WHR was also associated positively with pure AI. The observed ORs were similar in Black and White ARIC participants. Remarkably, 26.4% of normal-weight participants, 29.1% of overweight participants, and 32.5% of obese participants developed any AVD (i.e., aortic valve sclerosis, AS, or AI), suggesting a potentially large impact of obesity on the occurrence of AVD.

Although obesity is a common and recognized risk factor for several cardiovascular diseases, relatively little prospective epidemiological research has addressed possible associations of adiposity with AVD. Larsson et al. demonstrated that overweight and obesity, as well as higher waist circumference, were significantly and positively associated with AS incidence in two Swedish cohorts with over 70,000 participants after 15 years of follow-up [8]. Their adjusted hazard ratios of AS were 1.24 (95% CI: 1.05–1.48) for overweight individuals (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m²) and 1.81 (95% CI: 1.47–2.23) for those with obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²) compared with a BMI of 18.5–22.5 kg/m². Their AS hazard ratio was 1.30 (95% CI: 1.12–1.51) for a high waist circumference (≥102 cm in men and ≥88 cm in women) compared with a normal waist circumference (<94 cm in men and <80 cm in women). A few other studies replicated similar positive associations [5,24,25]. Moreover, a Mendelian randomization study provided genetic evidence supporting a causal association between obesity and AS [7]. Specifically, each 1 kg/m² increase in BMI corresponded to a causal risk ratio of approximately 1.52 (95% CI: 1.23–1.87) for AS. Our results are broadly consistent with this genetic evidence. However, not all studies have reported a positive association. A study using the Nationwide Inpatient Sample of the United States database reported that obesity (yes, no) was paradoxically associated with a lower prevalence of AVD after adjustment for demographics (OR 0.81–0.86, p < 0.01), suggesting a potential inverse association [9]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study design, outcome ascertainment based on ICD codes rather than echocardiography, or potential survival and detection biases for individuals with obesity vs. no obesity. Compared to AS, the association of overweight and obesity with incident aortic valve sclerosis has received less attention but is still reported to be positive [25,26].

Few studies have explored the association between obesity and AI, and they have yielded weak associations [10,27]. Results from ARIC indicated that AI was independently associated with lower BMI (OR: 0.8 per 1-SD increment (6.1 kg/m2) in BMI, 95% CI: 0.7–1.0) [10], which was replicated by Stefano et al. as the severity of AI was inversely associated with continuous BMI (OR: 0.972 per 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI, 95% CI: 0.965–0.981) [27]. Our demonstration of a positive association between WHR and incident AI provides additional evidence suggesting that abdominal obesity may contribute to the development of AVD in older adults. The potential mechanisms might include aortic root dilation and aortic valve prolapse. Aortic root dilation plays a critical role in the formation of AI, including the enlargement of the ascending aorta, the sinotubular junction, the sinuses of Valsalva, and the aortic annulus [26]. It causes insufficient coaptation of the aortic valve leaflets and subsequent AI. Many obesity-attributable consequences, such as hypertension and atherosclerosis, can also initiate or accelerate aortic root dilation [28,29]. Aortic valve prolapse happens from a pathological change in the aortic valve per se. The decreased flexibility and fusion of the aortic valve cusps, partially coming from valvular calcification and sclerosis, eventually lead to AI [26]. It is nevertheless difficult to disentangle the pathway between obesity and AI because of the frequent co-occurrence of AS and AI with aging. Our finding that abdominal obesity, but not BMI, is associated with AI implies that people with central obesity might be especially at risk of AI, corroborating previous studies’ conclusions (Fig. 3. In the ARIC cohort of older adults free of AVD at baseline, higher BMI and WHR were associated with increased risk of incident aortic valve sclerosis and AS, while abdominal obesity was also associated with AI. These findings suggest that reducing overall and abdominal obesity may help prevent AVD in aging populations.

AVD, aortic valve disease; BMI, body mass index; WHR, Waist-Hip Ratio; AI, aortic valve insufficiency; AS, aortic valve stenosis.

The public health impact of obesity on AVD occurrence could be sizable. In 2015–2016, 31.6% of adults in the US were overweight, and 39.6% were obese [32]. Using these US prevalence estimates and PAF calculations described in the Supplement [33], we estimate that the percentages of AVDs in the US population that might be prevented by eliminating overweight and obesity are sizable: 39% for AS and 32% for aortic valve sclerosis. Among Black women, whose US obesity prevalence in 2015–2016 was 54.8% [32], the corresponding PAFs would be even higher.

Major strengths of our study are the careful ARIC measurements and excellent follow-up, including the incorporation of research 2DE to define AVD. Limitations include: First, ARIC lacks measurements of the geometry of the aortic root from echocardiography, which prevents us from conducting a mediation analysis between WHR, aortic root enlargement, and pure AI. Second, strictly speaking, the ARIC study can be generalized only to U.S. Black and White individuals. Third, although steps are taken to minimize confounding, as in all observational studies, the possibility of some residual confounding remains.

Conclusion

In this community-based prospective study, higher BMI is an independent risk factor for incident aortic valve sclerosis and AS, but not AI. Greater WHR is a risk factor not only for incident aortic valve sclerosis and AS, but also for incident AI, which, to our knowledge, is a novel finding. These associations are similar for Black and White participants. Avoidance of abdominal obesity may help reduce the occurrence of non-rheumatic AVD in older adults.
