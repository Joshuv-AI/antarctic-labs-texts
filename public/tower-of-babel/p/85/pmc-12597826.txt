Liability threshold model-based disease risk prediction based on electronic health record phenotypes
Authors: Cue Hyunkyu Lee, Atlas Khan, Chen Wang, Chunhua Weng, Joseph D. Buxbaum, Krzysztof Kiryluk, Iuliana Ionita-Laza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Electronic health records have been increasingly adopted as useful resources for genomic research. However, case–control labeling of clinical data from electronic health records is challenging and most studies utilize phenotype codes to define case/control labels, resulting in suboptimal downstream analyses. Here we describe the liability threshold phenotypic integration, a method combining genetic relatedness with phenotypic data, including binary and continuous traits such as diagnosis codes, family disease history, laboratory measurements and biomarkers, to derive new continuous phenotypes for target diseases. The model utilizes an automatic trait selection algorithm that increases performance in disease risk prediction and provides insights into nontarget traits associated with the target disease. Our simulations and applications to the eMERGE network and the UK Biobank data demonstrate consistent performance gains in disease risk prediction and genome-wide association study power compared to conventional phenotype codes, models that solely incorporate family history and the phenotype imputation method SoftImpute, with similar false-positive rate control.

Main

The increasing availability of rich phenotype data from electronic health records (EHR), such as eMERGE1, BioVU2, DiscovEHR3, i2b24 and the UK Biobank (UKBB)5, and their linking to biobanks of DNA samples represent a rich resource for enhancing genetic association studies6–8. Most studies with EHR data use phenotypes defined by International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. However, this approach may be inaccurate, has limited granularity and often suffers from nonrandom missingness. Furthermore, limited case numbers for specific diseases reduces statistical power and may introduce bias9,10. ICD codes define diseases as discrete entities and do not reflect the continuum of disease risk. In contrast, a more quantitative characterization of common diseases may better address risk heterogeneity and enhance the power of genomic studies with EHR-derived phenotypes11.

A number of methods have been recently proposed as a way to impute missing values in the phenotype matrix12–20. For example, SoftImpute15 employs low-rank approximation of the completed phenotype matrix17. Another recent approach, AutoComplete, leverages a deep-learning autoencoder to model nonlinear feature correlations13. Here, we propose an alternative method, the liability threshold phenotypic integration (LTPI), based on the liability threshold model that can leverage rich phenotypic information in EHR and model correlations between underlying liabilities using single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-level genetic covariance estimates. Unlike general machine learning methods, LTPI can distinguish genetic from environmental trait correlations and, importantly, can work even without missing data as its main goal is to move away from a binary coding to a more quantitative characterization of a binary disease trait. LTPI estimates per sample genetic liability conditional on case–control status for the target disease, quantitative and binary traits in EHR and family history (FH) when available.

Recently, a related model, LTFH (liability threshold model conditional on case–control status and family history), which uses only FH to estimate posterior mean genetic liability, was proposed21. LTFH effectively enhances the power of genetic studies, yet it requires resource-intensive FH collection and frequent updates, making FH availability inconsistent across individuals and diseases in EHR. In contrast, LTPI generalizes LTFH by leveraging an individual’s entire health history (including FH when available) to provide a posterior mean genetic liability for a target disease. Key to the operation of the LTPI model is an automated trait selection algorithm that helps identify the best subset of traits to increase the accuracy of the risk prediction and the power of genetic association studies with the derived LTPI phenotype, while minimizing the risk of false-positive genetic associations due to inclusion of information from nontarget traits.

We validate LTPI through simulations and applications to traits in eMERGE and UKBB and demonstrate improved prediction accuracy and genome-wide association study (GWAS) power relative to phenotype codes (PheCodes), LTFH and SoftImpute15,17, with similar false-positive rate (FPR) control. We also show that incorporating sufficient EHR phenotypic information allows LTPI to achieve prediction accuracy comparable to or exceeding that of LTFH.

LTPI’s performance depends on the inclusion of nontarget traits that are ‘informative’ about the target disease. One approach is to do manual selection on the basis of expert advice. However, this approach is low-throughput, intensive and biased by existing knowledge. Instead, we implement an automatic trait selection algorithm (ATSA) on the basis of assessing the genetic contribution of nontarget traits to the target disease, defined here as the proportion of the target disease genetic variance explained by nontarget traits25 (Methods).

Validation of LTPI’s performance using simulations

We conducted extensive simulations to validate LTPI’s performance, focusing on disease risk prediction accuracy (R2), FPR and power for GWAS. We first verified the GHK algorithm’s accuracy against Monte Carlo (Supplementary Note and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Our simulations involved generating genotype and phenotype data for 100,000 parent–offspring trios for a total of 300,000 individuals. We generated 100,000 independent SNPs with minor allele frequencies sampled from a uniform distribution U(0.01, 0.5). Offspring genotypes were derived by combining parental haplotypes, with each haplotype generated from a Bernoulli distribution. Phenotypes were defined using liability scores, combining genetic and environmental factors. Binary traits were assigned on the basis of disease prevalence (default of 0.1), with cases defined as individuals exceeding a liability threshold. Each trait, including the target trait, had a (default) heritability of 0.5. For each simulation, we modeled 500 causal SNPs contributing equally to heritability. The simulations included 100 binary traits (one designated as the target trait) and 100 continuous traits. Our simulations assumed genetic and environmental correlation between target and nontarget traits using an AR(1) model. More simulation details are in the Supplementary Note.

Performance of LTPI in GWAS

We evaluated LTPI’s performance under varying model parameters and assessed the impact of parameter misspecifications (Supplementary Note). Specifically, we tested models with different numbers of traits, LTPI using only FH (similar to the LTFH model), and variations in genetic architecture, including heritability, number of causal variants and disease prevalence. These analyses demonstrated LTPI’s robustness under various parameter settings (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables 1–4).

We further evaluated LTPI’s performance in GWAS under two scenarios: (1) no missing data and (2) 30–50% missing data for the target phenotype. In both scenarios, LTPI used the ATSA to select up to 10 dichotomous and 10 continuous traits from a pool of 200 traits (excluding FH), LTFH relied solely on FH, and traditional GWAS used case–control (PheCode) status. For the second scenario, we compared LTPI with SoftImpute (default settings), which imputes missing values using nontarget phenotypes. All methods used identical sample sizes.

Setting 2 with missing data for target phenotype

We assessed the impact of missing target phenotypes. While the cause of missingness can vary (Supplementary Note), we introduced completely random missingness at rates of 30% and 50% under the default settings. We used the subscript ‘cond’ to denote performance for this scenario (for example, LTPIcond).

Applications to eMERGE

We analyzed the eMERGE-III dataset, which comprises 99,737 individuals (Supplementary Table 5), with EHR phenotypes and genotypes for 7,529,684 common variants imputed using the Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC) panel26. We focused on 42,823 European individuals aged 20–80 years with ICD-9/ICD-10 entries. Using the PheCode Map version 1.227,28 implemented in the PheWAS R library, we generated 1,866 binary phenotypes (PheCodes).

We used chronic kidney disease (CKD; PheCode 585.3), where we identified each CKD case on the basis of ICD-9 codes 585 and 585.0 and compared these to G-stage-defined CKD phenotypes29 derived from an automated algorithm that uses laboratory measurements (estimated glomerular filtration rate by the CKD-EPI equation) and other data to substantially increase the fidelity of CKD diagnosis. Although not exactly gold standard, they are the best available labels for CKD. As expected, the case and control counts differed depending on the definition: PheCode identified 2,434 CKD cases and 40,389 controls, while G-stage identified 4,115 CKD cases (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{e}}{\rm{G}}{\rm{F}}{\rm{R}}\, < 60\,{{\rm{m}}{\rm{l}}}^{-1}\,{\text{min}}^{-1}\,{(1.73\,{{\rm{m}}}^{2})}^{-1}$$\end{document}eGFR<60ml−1min−1(1.73m2)−1 or dialysis or kidney transplant; stage 3 or more) and 38,708 controls (Supplementary Table 6).

CKD risk estimation

We obtained covariance estimates from PheWeb30 using SAIGE GWAS summary statistics for 1,402 distinct PheCodes31, based on over 400,000 UKBB samples with 20 million variants imputed with the HRC30 panel. We used the eMERGE sample prevalence of each PheCode as a proxy for population prevalence. We considered two approaches to select traits for constructing the LTPI score.

For a manual selection informed by previous research32, we manually identified 110 binary traits associated with CKD. We excluded 23 traits owing to absence of GWAS summary data and 25 traits with out-of-range heritability estimates, leaving 62 binary nontarget traits (Supplementary Data 1). We refer to this analysis as ‘Manual’.

For the ATSA, we started with 1,402 PheCodes and filtered them on the basis of heritability and prevalence (Methods). To the 165 remaining nontarget binary traits—each with prevalence exceeding 0.01 and heritability P < 0.05—we added 62 binary traits selected from Manual (if they were not already present), ensuring a direct comparison. This step yielded 215 PheCodes. We then excluded any PheCodes equivalent to CKD or its subcategories: 585.3, 585.31–34 and 585.4. We retained the top 62 traits (the same number as for Manual) in terms of their genetic contribution to CKD without any stopping criterion in the ATSA (Supplementary Data 2).

Our final dataset included 42,823 European individuals with complete (no missing) case–control status across all 62 binary traits (Supplementary Data 3).

LTPI analysis using Manual

We generated LTPI risk scores for 42,823 European samples and built a prediction model with a G-stage phenotype as the outcome and a LTPI score as the predictor. This analysis included 12,115 individuals with G1 stage CKD as controls and excluded those with missing phenotypes and 15,605 individuals with G2 stage CKD (less severe CKD), leaving a final set of 16,230 individuals (G1, G3a, G3b and G4) for validation. Among them, severe CKD cases with stage G3 and above were classified as case samples, resulting in 4,115 cases and 12,115 controls.

Case and control groups differed notably in mean LTPI percentiles (Supplementary Fig. 7a). By partitioning LTPI scores into 60 quantile-based bins, we found a significant positive correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r=0.947{;}P < 3.1\times {10}^{-30}$$\end{document}r=0.947;P<3.1×10−30) between case fraction and LTPI percentile, confirming LTPI’s effectiveness in identifying individuals at high CKD risk (Supplementary Fig. 7b).

We performed additional validations using three binary phenotypes defined by G-stage (Supplementary Table 6). With controls fixed at 12,115, the number of CKD cases varied by disease stage. The G-stage R2 values showed that the Manual approach outperformed PheCode (Supplementary Table 7). Specifically, LTPI versus G-stage had an R2 of 0.325 for G3 and above, compared to 0.252 for PheCode. Additionally, LTPI demonstrated strong predictive performance, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.954 for G4 and above and 0.798 for G3 and above (Supplementary Fig. 7e).

Some of the 62 traits may reflect CKD complications, such as hormonal and electrolyte imbalances or vitamin deficiencies, rather than predisposing factors. Therefore, we removed 30 traits likely to occur after CKD onset (Supplementary Data 4). We recalculated LTPI scores with the remaining 32 traits (Manual32), which reduced the AUROC only slightly (Supplementary Fig. 8). For G3 and above, Manual32 achieved an AUROC of 0.773 compared to 0.798 for Manual. For G4 and above, the AUROC was 0.919 for Manual32 compared to 0.954 for Manual.

Applications to the UK Biobank

We applied LTPI to 404,981 white British participants in the UKBB, using data on 1,693 PheCodes and 36 continuous blood measurements. Discussion

While genomic studies of biobank data often rely on binary phenotypes such as PheCodes, their limited statistical power and interpretability constitute significant challenges for such studies. We proposed LTPI, a method that leverages rich EHR features to estimate individual disease risk. LTPI uses genetic correlations between traits, linking observed phenotypes to underlying liabilities, and aligns with widely used models such as LDSC. It also includes an ATSA based on genetic contribution of nontarget traits, reducing the need for expert-driven feature selection and minimizing false positives in GWAS. LTPI is also computationally efficient (Supplementary Table 22).

LTPI fundamentally differs from phenotype imputation methods such as SoftImpute and AutoComplete. While phenotype imputation focuses on filling in missing data, LTPI aims to replace binary phenotypes with continuous risk scores, applicable even when no data are missing. Unlike general imputation methods, LTPI distinguishes between genetic and environmental factors and explicitly uses genetically correlated nontarget traits.

Simulations showed that LTPI approximates genetic liability more accurately as genetic correlations between target and nontarget traits increase. Furthermore, LTPI improves power relative to conventional case–control GWAS, FH-based models such as LTFH, and imputation methods such as SoftImpute, especially when useful nontarget traits are available. Our applications to eMERGE and UKBB show that LTPI enhances clinical risk stratification and increases GWAS power. With additional refinements, both at the model level and by expanding phenotypic features to unstructured data such as clinical notes, LTPI’s performance can be further improved with important impact in clinical risk prediction at the individual level.

A limitation of our phenotype prediction evaluation is the lack of gold standard labels for validation and a reliance on imperfect labels such as PheCodes. Beyond PheCodes, we have tried, when possible, to consider validated phenotyping algorithms such as for CKD. Therefore, the prediction accuracies reported for the different diseases need to be taken with a grain of salt.

Another potential concern with methods that incorporate information from nontarget traits is the risk of genetic discoveries that are not specific to the target trait15,44. In our framework, the ATSA aims to identify nontarget traits that are genetically correlated with the target trait and hence will limit the inclusion of irrelevant nontarget traits. It is important to validate the results from GWAS analyses using LTPI using measures such as the genomic inflation factor or existing, independent GWAS studies for the target trait.

LTPI is broadly applicable to different EHR systems. Indeed, our applications to eMERGE and the UKBB illustrate this potential. The UKBB is a population-based cohort of individuals aged 40–70 years in the UK and aims to represent the general population, whereas eMERGE targets a patient demographic aged 20–80 years in the USA, often involving hospital visitors during the collection period. This difference in design implies that while eMERGE’s patient-based approach may allow for deeper phenotype analyses and improved disease signal detection, it introduces a higher risk of ascertainment bias compared to the UKBB’s broader population sample.

In summary, LTPI provides an efficient and automatic method for clinical risk stratification and underscores the significant potential of multitrait analysis models for EHR data in disease risk prediction and subsequent applications to GWAS.

Methods

Overview of LTPI construction

LTPI is based on a liability threshold model that incorporates both PheCodes and biomarkers from EHR. We first standardize the quantitative phenotypes using rank-based inverse normal transformation45. The liability threshold model operates under the assumption that an underlying liability distribution exists for polygenic binary traits, and an individual is a case if this continuous liability is above a threshold21,46–51. This is a reasonable model for polygenic complex traits46. The model allows for the incorporation of a broad array of phenotypic traits from EHR data, both binary and quantitative, and FH when available.

We assume that each trait has a unique liability distribution and model the vector of liabilities for multiple traits as following a multivariate normal distribution, with correlations between these liabilities modeled using SNP-level genetic covariance estimates. We can leverage existing methods for estimating genetic correlations, both global correlation such as the LDSC52,53 and local correlation such as the local analysis of covariant association25. Critical to the performance of LTPI is the ATSA, which not only aims to improve phenotype prediction but also helps select nontarget traits to improve GWAS association power while minimizing false positives due to the inclusion of information from nontarget traits.

Missing value handling in EHR data

Missing data are a common feature for EHR data, especially for continuous traits such as biomarkers. One main strategy to deal with missing data is imputation. Different imputation methods make different assumptions on the missing data mechanisms, including missing completely at random, missing at random, and MNAR. Most missing data in EHR are probably MNAR, which is difficult to model correctly, and hence the efficacy of imputation methods will depend on how accurately they can model the underlying missing mechanisms. Several phenotype imputation methods have been proposed, including SoftImpute15,17, a matrix completion method that assumes the missing data is missing at random, and AutoComplete13, a deep-learning autoencoder method that should have advantages when there are nonlinear correlations among phenotypic features. We compare LTPI with SoftImpute. Details on dealing with missing data in LTPI are described in the Methods and Supplementary Note.

LTPI score

The final LTPI score is a combination of the LTPIb and LTPIc scores and is obtained as follows: If no continuous scores are being used, then LTPI is LTPIb. If all individuals have continuous scores available, then LTPI is LTPIc. If only a subset of individuals has continuous scores, then LTPI will be a combination of LTPIb and LTPIc scores.

Optimizing trait selection for covariance estimation in ATSA

The ATSA requires a covariance matrix Ω as its primary input. We derive this covariance matrix using LDSC applied to GWAS summary statistics from PheWeb30, which include 20 million imputed variants using the HRC for 400,000 white British participants. Generating this matrix with LDSC, however, is computationally intensive. We therefore focus on traits with a disease prevalence above 0.01 and those with significant heritability (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}^{2}$$\end{document}h2’s P value is under 0.05). This selective approach led us to identify 165 and 220 binary nontarget PheCodes for eMERGE and UKBB applications, respectively, reduced from initial pools of 1,402 and 1,693 PheCodes.

LTPI employs phenotype data from EHR for estimating disease risk, and GWAS summary statistics from PheWeb for estimating the covariance structure between traits. Disease prevalence is an important variable in both analyses. In LTPI, disease prevalence informs the thresholds for the liability distribution of traits. LDSC also uses disease prevalence as the input. LTPI operates under the assumption that the sample prevalence (from EHR) mirrors that of the target population, which may not be true when using EHR data. Given these issues, our analyses exclusively focus on traits with a sample prevalence of 0.01 or higher in both datasets (eMERGE and UKBB) for the purpose of the ATSA, and in the UKBB for LDSC.

Stringent trait selection criteria for GWAS analysis

To minimize false positive GWAS findings due to inclusion of nontarget traits, we avoided selecting nontarget traits with minimal contributions to target traits, including those with low genetic correlation with the target or with overlapping contributions explained by already selected traits. We set an increment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{\mathrm{OT}}^{2}$$\end{document}ROT2 at 0.001 as the threshold for the saturation point of genetic contribution.

Assessing LTPI’s performance in risk prediction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}$$\end{document}R2)

Here we describe our approach to assess performance in simulations. We deployed a linear regression model, dividing our data into a 4:1 ratio—80% for training purposes and the remaining 20% for testing. Estimate PRSpleiotropy

We evaluated the specificity of PRS derived using different phenotype prediction methods with a recently proposed measure, PRSpleiotropy15. This metric is the ratio of two R2 values, measuring the proportion of variance explained by the PRS in a nontarget phenotype relative to the target phenotype (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{{\rm{n}}{\rm{o}}{\rm{n}}{\rm{t}}{\rm{a}}{\rm{r}}{\rm{g}}{\rm{e}}{\rm{t}}}^{2}/{R}_{{\rm{t}}{\rm{a}}{\rm{r}}{\rm{g}}{\rm{e}}{\rm{t}}}^{2}$$\end{document}Rnontarget2/Rtarget2), where R2 estimates are on the liability scale.

In our simulations, we compute the PRS using a pruning and thresholding approach. We assumed SNPs in the simulations to be independent; therefore, we did not perform any pruning. We applied a P value threshold of 0.05 to all tested variants. We first conducted GWAS on the default 100,000 samples (training set). We then simulated genotypes and phenotypes for 25,000 additional offspring, representing our test set. We constructed PRS using genotypes for individuals in the test set and SNP effect sizes estimated on the basis of the training set and evaluated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{\mathrm{test}}^{2}$$\end{document}Rtest2.

Each time we used 90% of the individuals to perform GWAS and then built the PRS on the 10% test set from GWAS results with PRSice version 2 (ref. 60), evaluating predictive accuracy for target and nontarget phenotypes. We then evaluated the predictive accuracy and PRSpleiotropy for both target and nontarget phenotypes.

SoftImpute

We conducted phenotype imputation using SoftImpute with open-source code published on GitHub15,62. Comprehensive analysis framework using the UKBB

Below we describe the analysis process for constructing risk scores, disease risk prediction and GWAS analysis used in the UKBB analysis.

LTPI score construction

For each target trait, we applied the ATSA using nontarget traits. The ATSA stops selecting nontarget traits when the increment in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{\mathrm{OT}}^{2}$$\end{document}ROT2 became smaller than 0.001. We always selected up to 30 binary and 20 continuous traits at maximum. We then performed LTPI analysis with the selected nontarget traits. GWAS was executed with the final LTPI scores for each target trait. For LTFH, we utilized posterior mean genetic liability that had been derived from the FH of nine UKBB diseases. For the conventional GWAS analysis, we used the case–control status for each target trait.

Identifying independent disease risk loci

We defined independent GWAS loci similar to ref. 63. Initially, we identified independent significant SNPs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 5\times {10}^{-8}$$\end{document}P<5×10−8) that are also independent at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}^{2} < 0.6$$\end{document}r2<0.6. These SNPs were then represented by lead SNPs. We defined associated genomic risk loci by merging lead SNPs that were physically overlapping (linkage disequilibrium blocks <250 kb apart), using the UKBB genotype data for linkage disequilibrium information.

To explore shared risk loci among LTPI, LTFH and GWAS, we first merged the genome-wide significant variants from these three methods. We then identified independent risk loci for this merged set as described above. For each risk locus, we determined whether a method-specific, independent, significant SNP was within the locus range. If so, we considered the locus as identified by that method. Finally, we computed the total number of independent disease risk loci by aggregating the independent loci identified per method.

GWAS association tests

GWAS testing was performed using Regenie34,35. We began with genotype and sample quality control (QC), utilizing Plink for this process. This QC phase targeted European samples and focused specifically on autosomal SNPs, while excluding the human leukocyte antigen region on chromosome 6, spanning from 29 MB to 33 MB. For SNP selection, stringent criteria were applied: we excluded SNPs with a minor allele frequency under 1%, a minor allele count under 100, genotype missingness exceeding 10% and a Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium P value beyond 10−15. Additionally, we removed samples exhibiting more than 10% missingness. Following these QC measures, our dataset comprised 7,362,467 biallelic SNPs from 404,976 European samples.

Our GWAS procedure involved a two-step Regenie analysis on each target trait, executed on a single-thread CPU. We adhered closely to the default settings recommended on the Regenie webpage34, with two key exceptions: we did not utilize the binary trait flag and opted out of using firth and saddle point approximations, typically employed for adjusting case–control imbalance. Each SNP association test includes five principal components, genotyping batch, gender and age as the covariates. This analysis procedure was consistently applied across LTPI, LTFH and GWAS, ensuring methodological uniformity.

Replication analyses

We replicated loci identified through GWAS and LTPI (LTFH) in some of the largest GWAS studies for four diseases, including CAD, type 2 DM, BrCa and PrCa40–43. We calculated the replication slope, which is the regression slope of standardized effect sizes in case–control replication data versus UKBB discovery data, for all genome-wide significant loci identified by GWAS and LTPI (LTFH).

Specifically, we filtered linear regression analysis results on European individuals to retain SNPs that (1) appear in the replication studies and (2) are significant in LTPI, LTFH or GWAS. We then identified both shared and disease-specific independent risk loci, following the procedure described in the previous section ‘Comprehensive analysis framework using UKBB.’ We then determined the standardized effect sizes for each lead SNP per locus in both replicated GWAS and UKBB discovery data. Lastly, we computed the replication slope for a comparative analysis of UKBB GWAS discovery versus UKBB LTPI (LTFH) discovery data.
