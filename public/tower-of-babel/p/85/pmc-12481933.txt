Solving the light chain mismatch of IgG-like bispecific antibody by utilizing 2A peptide based FaBody platform
Authors: Dong Li, Pengfan Zheng, Xuejing Yao, Baopeng Zhang, Jiekun Zhang, Yaocheng Qu, Shasha Yun, Yanzhen Li, Shanshan Chen, Jianmin Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bispecific antibodies (BsAbs), engineered to target multiple antigens or epitopes simultaneously, promise enhanced therapeutic efficacy over traditional monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). However, the complex BsAbs structure presents significant production challenges, particularly chain mismatch issues. This study presents a novel approach utilizing 2A peptides within a FaBody platform to address light chain mismatches in IgG-like BsAbs. By leveraging self-cleaving 2A peptides, stable expression in mammalian cells significantly improves the accuracy of antibody chain assembly. This strategy markedly enhances the production of correctly assembled IgG-like BsAbs, providing a promising solution to critical challenges in BsAbs drug development.

Introduction

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), composed of two identical heavy chains (HC) and two identical light chains (LC), have become a cornerstone in therapeutic applications due to their high specificity, consistency, and reproducibility [1], [2]. However, as diseases such as cancer and autoimmune disorders become increasingly complex, there is growing interest in bispecific antibodies (BsAbs). These engineered antibodies can simultaneously bind two different antigens or epitopes, potentially leading to synergistic therapeutic effects and enhanced biological functions [3], [4], [5].

Although BsAbs offer advantages, their development is often hindered by structure complexity, especially the challenge of achieving the correct assembly of HC and LC [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]. Chain mismatches, such as incorrect HC and LC pairing, are a significant obstacle to efficient BsAb production. While several platforms have been established to address these mismatches, only a limited number of BsAbs drugs have gained global approval [11]. Therefore, efficient and reproducible production methods for BsAbs are crucial for advancing their clinical applications [12].

One promising solution to the chain mismatch issue is the utilization of 2A peptides. Originating from the foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) [13], 2A peptides possess self-cleaving properties. These peptides, comprising a specific sequence of 19–22 amino acids, undergo cleavage between a glycine and proline residue at their C-terminus. This allows for two distinct protein productions from a single continuous polypeptide chain translated from one mRNA sequence, enabling the co-expression of the proteins to form a single mRNA strand. This characteristic renders them particularly suitable for BsAbs production. The efficiency of 2A peptide cleavage can be further enhanced by introducing furin, a protease that cleaves at the R-X-R/K-R motif, ensuring more precise separation of protein chains [14], [15], [16].

In this study, we explored the use of the 2A peptides in producing IgG-like BsAbs, which we named the “Fabody Platform”. This platform stands for “Furin-2A-Mediated bispecific Antibody Generation Platform”. Its main concept involves using the 2A peptide to connect the Fab regions at both ends of the bispecific antibody. Due to the 2A peptide’s natural self-cleaving ability, the linker breaks on its own, forming a complete bispecific antibody structure. Introducing additional furin cleavage sites and adding furin enzymes to the system further reduces leftover amino acids from the 2A peptide after cleavage, helping to minimize the impact on the resulting bispecific antibodies. Our results demonstrate that this approach significantly enhances the proportion of correctly paired light chains (LCs) and markedly improves the overall efficiency of BsAbs production in mammalian cells. By conducting a comparative analysis of various 2A peptide sequences [17], we have identified the most effective variant for BsAbs production, thereby establishing a robust platform for large-scale antibody manufacturing. These findings provide potential strategies to address the challenges associated with BsAb development, thereby improving their efficacy and facilitating clinical translation.

Protein structure prediction

The ESMFold software (Wemol, Wecomput™) [18], [19] was utilized to predict protein structures within the database based on sequence information. Users can input original amino acid sequences, after which the software computes the structures (processed at the back end) for comparison with homologous protein structures in the MSA database. The scoring of the predicted structure mainly depends on two indicators, pLDDT and pTM, which assess the model’s accuracy. pTM predicts the TM-score and provides a global view of the protein, while pLDDT (predicted LDDT) is a metric focused on the local structure. We filtered to retain high-confidence structures where both pTM > 0.8 and pLDDT > 80. The visualization of the protein model was accomplished utilizing the pymol-open-source (Schrodinger, LLC) [20].

Plasmid construction

To construct the plasmids, full-length heavy and light chains of the antibodies were amplified using specific primers. These sequences were then tandemly linked to various 2A peptides via overlap extension PCR techniques. The light chain was situated upstream of the 2A peptide, whereas the heavy chain was downstream, to optimize light chain expression [21]. Additionally, a different furin recognition sequence was inserted between the C-terminus of the light chain and the 2A peptide to facilitate the removal of amino acids post-cleavage (Fig. 1). To minimize heavy chain mismatches, the knob-into-hole (KIH) technique was applied [22], [23]. The final constructs were cloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector using the ClonExpress II one-step cloning kit (Nanjing Novazan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Cat. No. C112-01), and plasmid DNA was purified using a plasmid extraction kit (Tiangen Biochemical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Cat. No. DP103).

Cell culture, transient transfection, and protein purification

HEK293SQ cells were cultured in Expi293 expression medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. A1435102) and transiently transfected using the ExpiFectamine 293 transfection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. A14525). The transfection ratio of antibody heavy chains and light chains varies based on construct design. When HC and LC are linked by a linker, the two linked chains are mixed at an equal mass ratio; otherwise, the two distinct HCs are mixed at a 1:1 mass ratio, with LCs added at a 2:1 mass ratio relative to the total HC. A transfection enhancer was added 20 h post-transfection, and the supernatant was collected 120 h later. After centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered and loaded onto a HiTrap MabSelect PrismA Protein A column (Cytiva, Cat. No. 17549851) for purification. The target protein was eluted using a 50 mM citric acid buffer at pH 3.5. The Tris–HCl buffer (pH 8.5) was added to adjust the pH value of the final solution to 5.0–6.0, and subsequently exchange the target protein into PBS buffer as required by the experiment. The yields mentioned in the text represent the total amount of protein obtained per liter of cell culture. This value directly reflects the actual quantity of protein produced after introducing an equivalent total amount of plasmid. Using NanoDrop OneC (Thermo Fisher Scientific), the protein concentration is determined by measuring the sample’s absorbance at 280 nm and applying the specific extinction coefficient of the protein.

SDS-PAGE, HPLC-SEC, and Tm analysis

Protein samples were prepared under reducing and non-reducing conditions for SDS-PAGE analysis. Samples were loaded onto a 4%–12% SurePAGE Bis-Tris gel (Genscript, Cat. No. M00654), and electrophoresis was conducted using an EPS 100 system (Tanon) in 20X NuPAGE™ MOPS SDS Running Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. NP000102) until the bromophenol blue dye front reached the gel bottom. Gels were stained and destained using the eStain L1 instrument (Genscript, Cat. No. L00657C), followed by imaging with the GelDoc Go system (Bio-RAD).

For HPLC-SEC, a TSKgel G3000SWXL column (TOSOH BIOSCIENCE) connected to an Agilent 1260 Infinity II LC system was used. The system was equilibrated before analysis, and chromatographic data were analyzed with the integrated software.

Melting temperatures (Tm) were determined using the reagents in Table 2, with 1X PBS as the control. Samples were placed in a quantitative PCR plate, centrifuged for homogeneity, and analyzed in the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The thermal cycling program followed the manufacturer’s instructions, and fluorescence was recorded at 470/570 nm to generate protein melting curves. The Tm values were calculated using Protein Thermal Shift™ Software v1.4, with each sample analyzed in triplicate for accuracy.

Reduced and nonreduced intact mass spectrometry

RemeGen Co., Ltd. performed mass spectrometry analysis to determine the specific composition of the samples. To minimize interference from non-target proteins, all antibody proteins were subjected to de-N-glycosylation before testing. For reduced molecular weight analysis, 30 μ g of the sample was mixed with 1 μ L of N-glycosidase and 2 μ L of 1 M ammonium bicarbonate, then incubated overnight at 37 °C. Afterward, 2 μ L of 1 M dithiothreitol was added, followed by a 2 min incubation at 37 °C. For intact molecular weight analysis, another 30 μ g sample underwent the same incubation procedure with N-glycosidase and ammonium bicarbonate. Post-reduction with dithiothreitol (Sigma, Cat. No. D9163-5G), samples were analyzed using a UPLC system paired with a tandem mass spectrometer (Waters, Xevo G2-XS QTof), employing an ACQUITY BEH C4 column and detection at 280 nm.

ELISA binding and dual binding assay

The efficacy of bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) interacting with human antigens (HER2 and c-Met) was assessed using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). A 96-well immunoplate was coated overnight at 4 °C with 0.5 μ g/mL His-labeled antigen protein, followed by blocking with casein for 1 h at room temperature. Diluted samples of BsAbs and a control antibody were then added and incubated for 2 h. Next, an HRP-conjugated IgG antibody (Abcam, Cat. No. ab97225) was added and incubated for 1 h. After rinsing, TMB chromogenic solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 002023) was added, and readings were taken at 450 nm using the SpectraMax ABS Plus.

For the dual binding assay, human HER2 or cMet antigens were coated overnight at 4 °C, followed by blocking and incubation with test antibodies. After adding the HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG Fc antibody (Abcam, Cat. No. ab97225) and TMB substrate, signals were read at OD450 nm. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software).

All of the data were analyzed from three independent experiments.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

The kinetic rate constants (ka and kd) and affinity data (KD) for antibody–antigen binding were measured using the BIACORE Protein A chip (GE Healthcare, Cat. No. 29127556). Antibody samples were diluted to a concentration of 1 μ g/mL using HBS-EP+ (1×) buffer (Cytiva, Cat. No. BR100669). Human-HER2 (G&P Biosciences, Cat. No. FCL0220) and human-cMet (Sino Biological, Cat. No. 10692-H08H) antigens with His tags were diluted to 2.5 μ g/mL and 40 μ g/mL, respectively, and a two-fold dilution series was created for five concentration points. The binding of the antibody with specific human-HER2 and human-cMet antigens was analyzed using the Biacore T200 system (GE Healthcare). All measurements were performed in the sample buffer at room temperature (RT). The antibody was immobilized onto Protein A biosensors at a concentration of 1 μ g/mL. At the same time, the antigens were serially titrated in two-fold decrements to achieve the rate constants and affinity measurements. The KD values were calculated using the vendor-supplied software.

Cell viability assay

A CCK-8 assay (Sigma, USA) was performed to evaluate the effect of Fabody samples on the proliferation of HER2-positive BT-474 cells (ATCC, Cat. No. HTB-20) cultured at 1 ×105 cells/mL. A total of 100 μ L of the cell suspension was added to each microplate well. The antibody was diluted to 50 μ g/mL and then further diluted through eight gradients before being administered to the cells for 5 days. After removing the medium, 100 μ L of diluted CCK-8 solution (Dojindo, Cat. No. CK04) was added and incubated for 4 h. The optical density at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax ABS Plus microplate reader (Molecular Devices) to determine viable cell counts. Data were collected from three independent experiments and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software).

Western blotting assay

Resuspended OE33 cells (COBIOER, Cat. No. CBP60496), which strongly expressed phosphorylated cMet antigens, were seeded into 12-well plates at 1E6 cells/well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Culture medium was aspirated, and cells were starved in RPMI-1640 medium for 6 h. Antibody at 10 μ g/mL was added and incubated for 50 min. Then, human HGF (Sino Biological, Cat. No. 10463-HNAS) was added at 50 ng/mL and incubated for 10 min. Cell protein extracts were prepared with lysis buffer including protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a Trans-Blot Turbo totipotent protein transfer system (BIO-RAD). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were employed for detection, with imaging performed using a Pierce ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 32106) and a ChemiDoc system (BIO-RAD). All experiments followed standard protocols from Abcam.

Computational immunogenicity prediction

Four modules – AlphaMHC v2.0, AlphaMHC v3.0 beta, WeADApt v4.0.2, and WeADApt v4.1.0 – were utilized to assess proteins’ immunogenicity risk. The AlphaMHC algorithm is trained using extensive immunogenicity-related datasets and validated with FDA and EMA clinical data. It achieves an accuracy rate exceeding 80% and the ROC-AUC of 0.87. While the WeADApt is a multi-model fusion system designed for immunogenicity prediction, capable of effectively simulating the immune response to biopharmaceuticals. The correlation between MolScore and ADA incidence is found to be R = 0.68. All the immunogenicity analysis modules used here are provided by WeMol [19].

Results

The influence of 2A peptides on the expression of bispecific antibodies

To investigate the impact of 2A peptide on bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) expression, we inserted either non-cleavable (GGGGS)n or a furin cleavage site (RAKR) and F2A peptide fused linkers between the N-terminus of the heavy chain (HC) and the C-terminus of the light chain (LC). The F2A peptide was chosen here because its length is closest to the (GGGGS)n linker we used. Adding a linker on the left or right side (semi-added ligands, labeled l- or r-, respectively), on both sides (symmetric 2A ligands, labeled d-), or no extra linkers (N-linker) resulted in six different BsAbs. Heavy chain mismatch was minimized using the knob-into-hole technique [22], [23] (Fig. 2a–2b).

After transfection into HEK293SQ cells and purification, we evaluated the antibody yield. The yields ranged from 73.5 mg/L to 235.8 mg/L (Fig. 3a). These results confirm that 2A peptide, like the (GGGGS)n linker, effectively mediates the expression and assembly of BsAbs, consistent with prior reports [24], [25], [26], [27]. The variability in yields among the constructs might be attributed to differences in the structural properties of the linker peptides or their influence on overall antibody stability.

Characterization of antibody constructs

We performed SDS-PAGE under both reducing and non-reducing conditions to assess the structural integrity and assembly of BsAbs. The gel electrophoresis results revealed three distinct protein bands corresponding to the heavy chain (approximately 80 kDa), and light chain (around 52 kDa and 27 kDa) components (Fig. 3b). The successful assembly of the antibodies was confirmed. The light chains containing the F2A peptide migrated slightly slower than those with the (GGGGS)n linker, likely due to residual amino acids remaining after cleavage. This slower migration pattern supported the hypothesis that incomplete cleavage at the 2A peptide junction could impact the final antibody structure.

The results showed that the constructs with the symmetric 2A linker exhibited a more favorable assembly pattern. Under non-reducing conditions, a single band at approximately 150 kDa indicated the formation of a dimerized antibody containing two heavy chains and two light chains. However, additional bands observed under both reducing and non-reducing conditions suggested some degree of improper antibody polymerization. This phenomenon may be caused by the presence of individual light chain-heavy chain monomer proteins with intact linkers throughout the entire system. The d-F2A construct exhibited an acceptable proportion of properly assembled antibodies (Fig. 3c) and the highest thermal stability among the tested constructs (Fig. 3d). In contrast, the constructs with the (GGGGS)n linker and the single-sided 2A linker showed less optimal assembly profiles. Excellent thermal stability is essential for antibodies. The thermal stability of antibodies is closely connected to their biological activity, folding, structural integrity, and functionality. This is mainly because the function of antibodies depends on their specific three-dimensional structure. High temperatures can cause denaturation of antibody structures, which impairs their binding capacity and biological activity. At elevated temperatures, antibodies with poor thermal stability may aggregate or precipitate, decreasing their effectiveness and safety in biological therapy. Therefore, maintaining the thermal stability of antibodies not only enhances their effectiveness in biomedical research and treatment but also ensures their safety in drug development and biotech applications. This finding aligns with previous reports that symmetric antibody structures tend to promote better assembly and stability, which is crucial for both the functionality and therapeutic potential of the antibodies [28], [29]. At the same time, it also highlights the potential of our platform in improving the thermal stability of antibodies.

Biological activity of 2A-mediated bispecific antibodies

We assessed the biological functionality of the 2A-mediated bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) through enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) using immobilized human tumor-associated antigens (HER2 and cMet). The TAA-targeting antibodies exhibited binding activities comparable to their parental antibodies (Fig. 3e). This finding was consistent with the structural predictions, which indicated that the use of either a (GGGGS)n or an F2A linker resulted in minimal alterations to the Fab structure (Fig. 2c). Consequently, 2A peptide-mediated expression effectively preserved the overall biological activity of the BsAbs. Among the constructs, d-F2A showed relatively high purity (86.36%), thermal stability (Tm = 62.6 °C), and binding affinity, further substantiating its optimal balance between efficient expression, stability, and functionality.

To enhance the rigor of our control, we developed a new d-GS control, which features a positive control with a (GGGGS)n linker introduced in both Fab regions of the antibody. Additionally, we created an N-linker as a negative control, which has no linker fixation (Fig. 2a–2b). We utilized surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to more accurately assess the affinity differences between the Fab regions on both sides of the bispecific antibody molecule, as well as between the monovalent and bivalent forms of the parent molecule. The results shown in Fig. 4a–4i indicate that the affinity difference between the monovalent and bivalent forms of the parent molecule is not significant. Furthermore, the data for our bispecific antibody molecules fall within the same order of magnitude as those of the parent molecule.

Furin cotransfection for improved cleavage efficiency

Considering that the expression mediated by the 2A peptide results in the addition of extra amino acid residues at the C-terminus of the light chain, we investigated the enhancement of cleavage efficiency through the co-transfection of furin. Furin is a protease known for cleaving the R-X-R/K-R motif in proteins [30], [31]. Here, we chose to use RAKR as the recognition site for furin to enable a parallel comparison. We conducted tests using four distinct transfection ratios (heavy chain, light chain, and furin at 1:1:0, 5:5:1, 3:3:1, and 1:1:2). Antibodies were expressed at different transfection ratios. Since the total amount of plasmids in our transfection system is fixed, increasing the amount of the furin protease plasmid results in a corresponding decrease in the amount of the plasmid expressing the antibody. This makes it challenging for us to assess the expression levels of the antibodies accurately. To address this, we recalculated a series of theoretical expression levels based on the input ratio of the antibody plasmid in the system. This approach enables a more equitable comparison of antibody production across various transfection ratios. The results indicated that antibodies’ theoretical production varied between 35.12 mg/L and 88.92 mg/L at different transfection ratios (Fig. 5a). Subsequently, we evaluated the resultant antibody constructs via SDS-PAGE, HPLC-SEC, Tm analysis, ELISA, and mass spectrometry.

The results demonstrated that elevating the furin concentration improved cleavage efficiency, particularly at the C-terminus of the light chain. This was evidenced by the accelerated migration of the d-F2A (1:1:2) construct on reducing gels, affirming successful cleavage at the furin recognition site (Fig. 5b) and despite the enhancement of cleavage efficiency through furin cotransfection, residual amino acids persisted at the C-terminus of the light chain, suggesting that the cleavage process has not yet been fully optimized.

The results of HPLC-SEC analyses further confirmed the cleavage efficiency of furin and 2A peptides (Fig. 5c). After introducing furin protease, the purity of d-F2A decreased. This could be due to the cleavage of the furin recognition site, leaving behind amino acids with varying degrees of residual activity. These findings align with the results of the mass spectrometry analysis, which showed that the heavy chain was entirely cleaved; however, residual amino acids (RA/RAK/RAKR) persisted at the C-terminus of the light chain (Table 3, Fig. 6a–6c). The presence of 17 expected disulfide bonds, including 5 inter-chain and 12 intra-chain, confirmed the proper assembly of the BsAbs. It was observed that as the amount of furin protease introduced increased, the cleavage at the recognition site became more efficient. Although there may still be a small number of intact and uncut linkers in the system, their presence can ultimately be controlled to within 3%. Comparison of different furin recognition sequences and 2A peptides

Considering the suboptimal cleavage efficiency of the previously selected furin recognition sequence, RAKR, we proposed replacing it with a more efficient sequence. Furthermore, we conducted a comparative analysis of four commonly used 2A peptides (F2A, P2A, E2A, and T2A) [32] to systematically assess their effects on the expression levels and cleavage efficiency of bispecific antibodies. We engineered bispecific antibody constructs by linking light and heavy chains with different 2A peptides and adjusted the furin recognition sequence to RRKR. The constructs, designated as d-F2A RRKR, d-P2A RRKR, d-E2A RRKR, and d-T2A RRKR, were transfected into cells at an HC:LC: furin ratio of 1:1:2. The yield results (Fig. 7a) show that after changing the recognition site, the yield decreased slightly. Among the different 2A peptides, the antibody produced by T2A mediation had the highest yield, reaching 54.41 mg/L, followed by the antibody produced by E2A mediation.

SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that all constructs were capable of forming correctly assembled antibodies. Furthermore, no additional abnormal protein bands were observed when T2A was utilized as the linker (Fig. 7b). Regarding purity, as shown in Fig. 7c, d-F2A RRKR and d-T2A RRKR achieved purities exceeding 50%, significantly outperforming the other constructs. The thermal stability of antibodies produced by different 2A peptides does not show significant variation and remains consistently above 65 °C. When compared to the two control groups, d-GS and N-linker, there is a notable improvement (Fig. 7d). Among the tested variants, d-T2A RRKR exhibited the highest melting temperature (Tm) of 73.5 °C. This enhanced thermal stability may be attributed to its ability to minimize improper folding or aggregation during the expression process, which is critical for therapeutic applications.

The bridging-ELISA results showed that after changing the enzymatic cleavage recognition site to RRKR, the binding affinity of the antibody produced by F2A mediation was comparable to that of the positive control d-GS and stronger than that of antibodies produced by other 2A peptide-mediated methods (Fig. 7e). The top value of d-T2A RRKR is slightly lower than that of d-F2A, but it is also significantly better than the other two 2A-mediated antibody molecules. Both d-F2A RRKR and d-T2A RRKR outperform the N-linker control in binding affinity. Notably, d-F2A RRKR and d-T2A RRKR exhibit the least change in affinity, indicating that this structure has the minimal impact on the overall function of bispecific antibodies. Therefore, these two molecules are promising candidates for maintaining antibody function while improving cleavage efficiency.

Mass spectrometry results provided comprehensive insights into the cleavage efficiency of different 2A peptides. While most proteins were efficiently cleaved at the 2A peptide recognition sequence, the furin recognition site showed lower cleavage efficiency. Following the recognition sequence modified to RRKR, the maximum number of residual amino acids remaining in the light chain post-furin cleavage was reduced from four to three. Specifically, d-P2A RRKR exhibited the highest self-cleavage efficiency, with no long-chain amino acids remaining at the C-terminus of the light chain. d-T2A RRKR demonstrated superior furin cleavage efficiency. In contrast, d-E2A RRKR had the lowest cleavage efficiency at both the furin and 2A peptide recognition sites (Fig. 8a–8c). This is consistent with previous conclusions that of the four commonly used 2A peptides, P2A generally has the highest cleavage efficiency, followed by T2A, E2A, and F2A [14]. These variations in cleavage efficiencies underscore the importance of selecting the appropriate 2A peptide for specific applications in bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) production and stability optimization.

The broader application scenarios of Fabody platform

To assess the platform’s capability in developing a wider variety of bispecific antibodies, we selected two candidate drugs that target different antigen combinations: Glofitamab [33] (an already marketed drug) and CM-350 [34] (currently in Phase 1/2 clinical trials), which target CD3/CD20 and CD3/GPC3, respectively. Our goal was to utilize this platform to generate bispecific antibodies against additional antigen pairs. Furthermore, we combined the EGFR antibody RAA22 [35] from AstraZeneca’s bispecific antibody with the marketed antibody Avelumab (Bavencio) [36] to create a novel bispecific antibody molecule. As a result, we successfully generated CD3/CD20-T2AR derived from Glofitamab, which consists of two kappa light chains; CM350-T2AR derived from CM-350, which contains one kappa and one lambda light chain; and EGFR/PD-L1-T2AR derived from RAA22 and Avelumab, both of which have lambda light chains (as shown in Fig. 10a). These findings demonstrate the platform’s potential for developing bispecific antibodies with various combinations of light chains.

As illustrated in Fig. 10b, our platform effectively facilitates the generation of these bispecific antibody molecules. Although the percentage of plasmids producing functional antibodies in the three 2A peptide-mediated bispecific antibody transfection systems was relatively low due to the additional introduction of the furin enzyme, the final expression levels were comparable to those of the corresponding controls or parental antibodies. Notably, the thermal melting temperature (Tm) of CM350-T2AR and EGFR/PD-L1-T2AR was significantly higher than that of the parent molecules and the control, suggesting that the platform may enhance molecular stability (Fig. 10d). HPLC-SEC data further confirmed that the purity of the three generated molecules was markedly improved compared to the control (Fig. 10c), indicating that the platform not only increased the Tm value but also enhanced antibody purity.

These findings lead us to have high expectations for the future application of this platform in the production of bispecific and even multispecific antibodies. As shown in Fig. 11, the platform applies not only to different targets and combinations of light chains but also to bispecific antibodies or even multispecific antibodies of varying valence states. Conclusion and discussion

Our study has systematically investigated the use of 2A peptides in the production of IgG-like bispecific antibodies (BsAbs), providing valuable insights into this field. We have demonstrated that 2A peptides can effectively mediate the expression and assembly of BsAbs, with performance comparable to that of (GGGGS)n linkers. The transient expression of the three engineered bispecific antibody constructs in HEK293SQ cells yielded from 73.5 mg/L to 235.8 mg/L. This finding indicates that 2A peptide significantly facilitates successful antibody production, which is consistent with previous studies highlighting their role in enhancing antibody expression levels [25]. SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed the proper assembly of BsAbs into their expected heavy and light chain components. However, minor discrepancies in migration patterns were observed due to residual amino acids from incomplete 2A peptide cleavage. These residues caused slight shifts in band positions but did not significantly affect the overall structure. This was further supported by the results of functional tests, which showed no significant change in antibody function whether the F2A peptide or the (GGGGS)n linker linkage was used.

Notably, the d-F2A construct demonstrated the highest proportion of correctly assembled antibodies, suggesting that the symmetry inherent in the antibody structure may significantly enhance the assembly efficiency. This finding aligns with previous reports indicating that symmetric antibody structures promote better assembly and stability. Conversely, asymmetric configurations or structural asymmetry at the heavy-light chain interface could impede optimal folding and dimerization.

To enhance the cleavage efficiency of residual amino acids at the light chain C-terminus, we investigated the introduction of furin, a protease capable of cleaving at the R-X-R/K-R motif. Our data demonstrate that using RAKR as the recognition sequence for furin and in conjunction with furin co-transfection significantly improves cleavage efficiency. This improvement is evidenced by the accelerated migration of the d-F2A (1:1:2) construct on reducing gels, suggesting more efficient cleavage at the C-terminus of the light chain. However, despite these improvements, some residual amino acids, predominantly RA and RAK, persisted at the light chain C-terminus. Meanwhile, we also investigated the optimal dosage of furin enzyme in the transfection system. By adjusting the transfection ratio of furin to antibody components, we performed a series of optimization experiments. Specifically, we co-transfected the two light chain and heavy chain complexes of the antibody with furin in different proportions (1:1:2, 1:1:4, 1:1:6, 1:1:8, and 1:1:10). As shown in Table 5, increasing the proportion of furin in the transfection mixture was associated with a decrease in the percentage of antibodies with incomplete linkers, reducing the level of complete residues to below 1.6%. These results indicate that enhancing furin’s cleavage activity can effectively reduce residual amino acids at the light chain’s C-terminal. This highlights the need for further optimization of furin cleavage efficiency. The incomplete cleavage may be attributed to the partial occlusion or hindrance of the furin recognition site by the 2A peptide sequence itself, which requires in-depth investigation in future studies.

Mass spectrometry analysis confirmed that while the heavy chain was completely cleaved, the light chain retained residual amino acids, predominantly RAK and RAKR. This partial cleavage underscores that furin cleavage efficiency remains a limiting factor. The presence of 17 expected disulfide bonds, including inter-chain and intra-chain bonds, indicates the correct assembly of the bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) and validates the efficacy of F2A peptide-mediated expression. Intriguingly, the persistence of RAK/RAKR sequences at the C-terminus of the light chain may impact the overall thermal stability and immune responses of the BsAbs. The comparative analysis of various 2A peptides and furin recognition sequences revealed significant differences in expression and cleavage efficiencies. Constructs utilizing d-F2A and d-T2A exhibited higher purities compared to others, suggesting that these peptides may be more effective in promoting proper antibody assembly. The distinct cleavage efficiencies observed among the peptides can likely be attributed to sequence variation in their cleavage recognition motifs, which influence processing efficiency within the expression system. Notably, d-F2A and d-T2A demonstrated minimal impact on the affinity of bispecific antibodies relative to parental antibodies, suggesting that it preserves the functional integrity of the therapeutic agent. This characteristic is critical, as any substantial decrease in affinity could compromise the therapeutic potential of the bispecific antibody.

Mass spectrometry results showed that d-P2A had the highest self-cleavage efficiency with no long-chain amino acids remaining at the C-terminus of the light chain. In contrast, d-T2A achieved the highest efficiency at the furin cleavage site. The enhanced self-cleavage activity of P2A minimized the need for external proteases and reduces potential interference with protein folding. These findings emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate 2A peptides, suitable furin recognition sequences, and optimizing furin co-transfection conditions to achieve the desired cleavage and expression profiles. Additionally, the ability of P2A to cleave efficiently without relying on furin could be beneficial in systems where furin expression is limited or where its overexpression might lead to cellular toxicity.

Immunogenicity prediction results showed a low risk of immunogenicity for both 2A peptides and (GGGGS)n linkers. Specifically, with or without the introduction of additional furin recognition sites, T2A showed a lower risk of potential immunogenicity across all four immunogenicity prediction software, similar to commonly used (GGGGS)n linkers.

Indeed, numerous platforms have been developed to address the light chain mismatch issue. For instance, platforms such as elg technology (MacroGenics) [37], HOT-Ig technology (Hengrui Medicine) [38], and the IL-15/IL-15Ra bispecific antibody designing (Qure Bio) [39] introduce additional foreign sequences to replace the constant region of one Fab arm. Alternatively, strategies similar to ours involve LegoBody [40] and YBODY (YZY MED) [41] via adding extra linkers to the one side of the Fab region to solve the problem. Despite these existing solutions, our Fabody platform retains distinct advantages.

First, our platform significantly reduces the introduction of additional amino acids relative to alternative strategies. After performing a comprehensive comparative analysis of the immunogenicity associated with other platforms designed to address light chain mismatch issues, we found that the T2A linker exhibits similar or markedly lower immunogenicity than other exogenous linkers (Table 6). Furthermore, we have validated that our platform effectively guides the correct assembly of bispecific antibodies. Lastly, we have confirmed that our platform does not compromise the antibodies’ structural integrity, characterization, or functional properties. This endows the Fabody platform with a broader spectrum of applications, thereby enhancing its potential for future utilization in antibody production.

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the use of 2A peptides and furin co-transfection in BsAbs production. The 2A peptides significantly facilitate the expression and assembly of antibodies. Additionally, modifications to the furin recognition sequence and the implementation of cotransfection processes have been shown to enhance the cleavage efficiency of residual amino acids; however, achieving complete elimination continues to pose a considerable challenge. The comparative analysis of different 2A peptides emphasizes the importance of careful selection and optimization to achieve optimal BsAbs expression and biological activity. Future studies should focus on several key aspects. Firstly, it is crucial to investigate how residual peptides affect not only antibody assembly but also immune properties such as immunogenicity and in vivo stability. Secondly, further optimization of furin cotransfection conditions or establishment of furin-stable expression host cell lines is needed to improve cleavage efficiency. Additionally, exploring additional 2A peptides or modifying existing ones to enhance self-cleavage activities and reduce interference with antibody folding and assembly could lead to significant improvements in BsAb production.
