Comparative Evaluation of Animated Video and Verbal Communication as a Preoperative Intervention in Reducing Dental Anxiety in Children Using Children's Drawings: Hospital Manual, Pulse Rate, and Facial Image Scale—A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Shilpa Shetty Naik, Shagorika Choudhury, Rachna Sharma, Deepshikha Mehrotra, Nikitha Balasubramanian, Shirin Chavan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo compare and evaluate the effect of an animated video and verbal communication [tell-show-do (TSD)] as a preoperative intervention in reducing the level of anxiety among children aged 6–8-year-old undergoing dental treatment.MethodologyA total of 72 children were divided into two groups based on the preoperative intervention provided to them (group A: animated video and group B: verbal communication). Following this, an oral examination was performed, and children in both groups (groups A and B) were asked to draw (first drawing). Anxiety levels were measured using objective (pulse rate) and subjective [facial image scale (FIS)] parameters at baseline (before commencement of the procedure), during treatment, and post-treatment. Post-treatment, the children were once again asked to draw (second drawing). The drawings were then analyzed by a child psychologist using Children Drawing: Hospital (CD:H).ResultsA statistically significant difference (p = 0.026) was found between the control and study group for the mean change in FIS during and after the procedure. A positive linear correlation was found between objective anxiety, subjective anxiety, and CD:H scores using Pearson's correlation test.ConclusionAnimated video was as effective as verbal communication (TSD) as a preoperative intervention in reducing dental anxiety in children when evaluating using CD:H manual.How to cite this articleNaik SS, Choudhury S, Sharma R, et al. Introduction

Dental fear and anxiety affects between 5% and 33% of children worldwide, making it the fourth most prevalent phobia. Children who exhibit high levels of preoperative anxiety at their first dental visits are more likely to exhibit maladaptive behavior in subsequent appointments, thereby making the dental treatment challenging.1

Behavior guidance techniques, both nonpharmacological and pharmacological, are used to mitigate anxiety, instill a positive dental attitude while performing reliable oral care safely and effectively. Several nonpharmacological techniques, such as tell-show-do (TSD), distraction, modeling, and hypnotism, have been employed to minimize anxiety during dental treatment.2

Tell-show-do is a commonly employed effective technique for the management of children's anxiety at their pretreatment visit. The TSD method is a frequently taught behavior control strategy based on learning theory. It requires that the child be fully informed before a procedure is carried out and that a simulator be used to show exactly what will happen before the process is started.3

Video modeling is an efficient means for behavior modification; it has been applied in numerous fields of medicine.4 However, video modeling is not routinely used in the dental settings due to the lack of extensive study on the same.5

An array of subjective and objective parameters is available to measure anxiety and the effectiveness of a behavior guidance technique on an individual. The most common physiologic indicator of anxiety is increase in heart rate.6 Pulse rate is a simple subjective biological parameter used to measure dental anxiety in a nonthreatening manner without making the child anxious. Facial image scale (FIS) can be employed with very young children, as compared to other methods of verbal self-reporting for children. It is relatively quick and easy to administer, which gives immediate feedback to the clinician.7

Drawing is the highest expression of thoughts, feelings, and experiences of child's communication. As a result, drawing has been recognized not only as a cognitive ability but also as a method for assessing a child's psychological well-being.8 Children learn to draw and color at a young age, and all children are attracted to activities that can be utilized as a form of nonverbal communication by them.

Children Drawing: Hospital (CD:H) was developed in 1999 based on the experiment conducted on 1101 children where they were asked to draw an image of a dentist at work documented by Taylor et al. in 1976.9 According to Clatworthy et al., children's drawings are an effective way to subjectively assess the personal experiences and emotions of hospitalized children.10

The evidence regarding the effect of animated video modeling on the dental anxiety of children using the CD:H method is scarce. Limited literature is available to correlate CD:H manual with conventional parameters. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the effect of animated video on the dental anxiety level of children receiving dental treatment using pulse rate, FIS, and child's drawing analysis (CD:H score).

Study Design

The protocol of this clinical trial (IREB/2022/PEDO/10) was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution during the period August 2022 to November 2022. Before enrolment, written informed consent in English and local language was obtained from the parents/guardians of eligible children participating in this study. The study was registered in Clinical Trials Registry CTRI/2023/11/060361.

The estimated sample size was calculated using two-sample Z-test, and was 36 participants in each group with a probability of 0.05 and power at 80%.

Children aged between 6 and 8 years old without previous dental treatment experience. Children who require procedures oral prophylaxis, pit and fissure sealants, fluoride application, and minimally invasive restoration, diagnosed according to the International Caries Detection and Assessment System, second edition (ICDAS II) (score 2–3)11 were included in the study.

Children with a score I–II on Frankl behavior rating scale12 and moderate to severe anxiety as per the modified dental anxiety scale13 were examined on the first visit and included in the study.

Children whose parents did not give consent for the study, were medically compromised, or refused to draw a picture were excluded from the study.

An initial oral examination was performed, following which 72 children fulfilling the inclusion criteria were randomly assigned to two groups using computer-generated numbers. The numbers were entered into sealed envelopes that were opened prior to the commencement of their treatment. As a preoperative intervention, participants in group A were individually exposed/subjected to an animated video demonstrating the dental treatment to be performed on them. Verbal communication using the TSD technique was employed in group B as a preoperative intervention.

Following this, participants in both groups were asked to draw (first drawing) a picture of the dental setup/dentist or a person in that setting. Any queries asked by the participants were answered in a way that would not influence the child. In a few instances, the original instructions were repeated to provide clarity to the participants. A table of appropriate height, along with a chair, was provided with an A4-size sheet and a set of crayons (red, purple, blue, green, yellow, orange, black, and brown) according to the manual. The drawing was coded for each child, among other data.

To further evaluate the effectiveness of the interventions employed in this study, the pulse rate (objective parameter) was recorded using a pulse oximeter (Buerer pulse oximeter PO30). The FIS, a self-reporting scale, was used as a subjective parameter. Both subjective and objective parameters were recorded at three stages: baseline, during treatment, and post-treatment.

Post-treatment the child was asked to draw (second drawing) with the same set of instructions as before.

Statistical Analysis

All data were entered into a computer using a coding system and checked for any entry errors. The compiled data was organized using Microsoft Excel (version 2019, Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA). Statistical analysis was carried out with the help of the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 26.0, IBM). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data—frequencies and percentages for categorical data, and mean with standard deviation for numerical data. An independent t-test was used for intergroup comparison, and Pearson's correlation test was applied to assess the correlation between variables.

For all the statistical tests, p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant, keeping α error at 5% and β error at 20%, thus giving a power to the study of 80%.

Results

The mean age of participants in the study group (group A) and control group (group B) was 6.94 ± 0.89 years and 6.69 ± 0.89 years, respectively.

The mean pulse rate for participants in the study group (group A) at baseline was 100.5 ± 9.97 beats per minute (bpm), and in control group (group B) was 103.61 ± 11.4 bpm. Mean pulse rate during the procedure in the study group was 102.64 ± 8.86 bpm, and in the control group was 106 ± 10.7 bpm. The mean pulse rate after the procedure in the study group and control group was 99 ± 1 bpm and 102 ± 8 bpm, respectively. There was a statistically nonsignificant difference observed for mean pulse rate at three stages (p = 0.266) (Table 1).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to incorporate video modeling in terms of an animated video and evaluate its effect as an intervention in children with dental anxiety.

Their experiences were documented through drawings that were interpreted by a child psychologist.

Children's drawings are considered to be a gateway into their inner lives, conveying a spectrum of emotions as well as information on their intellectual ability, psychological state, and social preferences. Most children conceal their underlying anxieties regarding traumatic dental experiences, which adds to the limitations of verbal communication. Some children viewed drawing as a form of reward, and overall, they found the activity to be relieving and comforting.

In our study, children aged 6–8 years were selected as they were more likely to have the cognitive ability and motor skills to self-report their anxiety.14 It was observed that incorporating elements such as drawing and watching animated videos intrigued children towards accepting dental treatment with greater enthusiasm. Children were drawn to such activities that can be utilized as a form of nonverbal communication by them, which instils positive behavior towards their oral health practices.

Therefore, it is necessary to employ various nonverbal, self-report techniques probing the subject's inner emotional state. One such method is the employment of drawings, an enjoyable activity that tends to project a child's feelings are significant.10

Guner Onur et al. investigated children's drawings in correlation with Frankl's behavior rating scale and concluded that drawings can be utilized as an alternative technique to assess children's anxiety during dental treatment.8

Aminabadi et al. used CD:H manual test to investigate the effectiveness of drawing as a measure for a child's emotional distress in pediatric dental settings and found that drawing is highly correlated with Frankl scale.15 The results of our study are consistent with those of Clatworthy et al., who discovered that hospitalized children's drawings are an effective diagnostic tool for assessing their emotional state.10

A video demonstration of expected procedures can help decrease disruptive behavior and enhance cooperation in young children (Hine et al., 2018).5 Technology-driven interventions may be useful as an auxiliary to conventional dental care (Gujjar et al., 2019).16 Various studies have shown positive effects of video modeling on behavior shaping. However, video modeling is not widely adopted by dental professionals due to the limited availability of randomized clinical trials supporting its effectiveness. Animated video was curated for each procedure performed. Therefore, video modeling in terms of animated video was integrated in our study, and a video for the specific treatment carried out was provided to the participants.

Although no statistically significant difference in mean pulse rate was seen, a decrease in mean pulse rate value was observed in both groups. Values for both mean pulse rate and FIS were seen to be much lesser for study group when compared to control group. Statistically significant difference was seen in FIS between both groups during procedure.

In general, the study group had greater success in reducing dental anxiety than the control group, presumably because the test group was aware of the steps in the procedure that were going to take place after watching the video, eliminating fear of the unknown.

Animated video was as effective as verbal communication (TSD) as a preoperative intervention in reducing dental anxiety in children when evaluating using CD:H manual.

Drawings can be considered an effective tool in assessing children's anxiety and perceptions about the dentist and/or dental treatment.
