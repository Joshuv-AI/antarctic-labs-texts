Herpes zoster vaccination and cognitive disorders in older adults
Authors: Jiyeon Oh, Kyeongmin Lee, Dongjin Yeo, Jaehyeong Cho, Tae Hyeon Kim, Jinseok Lee, Hayeon Lee, Ho Geol Woo, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONPrevious studies suggested a reduced risk of dementia after live zoster vaccination, but were limited by short follow‐up and narrow populations. This study assessed the long‐term association between live zoster vaccination and memory disorders and Alzheimer's disease (AD) in a nationwide Korean cohort.METHODSThis study, using linked national databases, included 2.5 million adults aged ≥ 50 years from 2012 to 2024. After exposure‐based overlap weighting, Cox models estimated adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and restricted mean survival time (RMST).RESULTSAmong 1,041,964 participants (mean age, 61.8 years; 46.1% male), vaccination was associated with lower risks of memory disorders (aHR 0.88; 95% confidence interval: 0.87–0.90) and AD (0.75, 0.71–0.78), corresponding to RMST gains of 15.5 and 11.3 days per decade, respectively. Protective effects attenuated over time and were less pronounced among smokers and drinkers.DISCUSSIONLive zoster vaccination was associated with delayed onset of cognitive decline, suggesting benefits beyond herpes zoster prevention in aging populations.

BACKGROUND

Recent evidence suggests that live‐attenuated vaccines may elicit broader immunomodulatory effects beyond their targeted pathogens. These so‐called “off‐target effects” include reductions in all‐cause mortality and protection against certain chronic diseases, potentially through trained innate immunity or long‐lasting immunomodulation. 1 , 2

The live zoster vaccine, developed to prevent herpes zoster (HZ) caused by reactivation of the varicella zoster virus (VZV), is a widely implemented preventive measure for older adults. While its primary role is to reduce incident HZ and its complications, such as postherpetic neuralgia and HZ ophthalmicus, 3 , 4 recent interest has turned to its potential for broader protection. This is particularly relevant given that VZV is a neurotropic virus capable of inducing acute and chronic neuroinflammation. Accumulating evidence implicates HZ in the pathogenesis of some neurological conditions, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) 5 and Parkinsonism. 6 Moreover, our recent large‐scale population‐based study has revealed that reduced incident risks of cardiovascular diseases could be an off‐target effect of live zoster vaccination. 7 Although a recent quasi‐experimental study suggested a protective association between vaccination and dementia in the very old adult population aged ≥ 70 years, 8 supporting evidence in populations with a broader age spectrum, longer follow‐up duration, and consideration of potential effect modifiers remains limited.

Thus, we aimed to examine the risks of memory disorders and AD, including AD‐related dementia, in a large, population‐based cohort in South Korea. In addition, we assessed whether this association varied by alcohol intake and smoking status—two important behavioral risk factors for cognitive decline—and assessed temporal patterns of vaccine effectiveness over the follow‐up period. These findings could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential neuroprotective role of zoster vaccination in the general population.

METHODS

Data source

We conducted a population‐based cohort study of 2,519,582 individuals aged ≥ 50 years in South Korea. The cohort was constructed by linking three national databases managed under South Korea's universal health insurance system, which covers 98% of the South Korean population. 7 Three databases used in this linkage are as follows: (1) outpatient and inpatient claims, prescription records, and mortality data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service of Korea (HIRA); (2) vaccination data from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA); and (3) national health examination records from the National Health Insurance Service of Korea (NHIS). The integration of these datasets allows for robust longitudinal follow‐up, adjustment for a wide range of potential confounders, and stratified analyses by key behavioral and clinical risk factors (e.g., smoking status or alcohol intake).

Data linkage and anonymization were done by the Korea Healthcare Big Data Center, under the Ministry of Health and Welfare. The study protocol received approval from the Korea Healthcare Big Data system (No. 2022‐00061) and three relevant government agencies, including the HIRA, the KDCA, and the NHIS. As all data were anonymized, individual informed consent was not required. 9 Additional details on the data sources can be found in the Supplementary Methods and Figure S1 in supporting information.

Exposures and outcomes

The exposure is live zoster vaccination for at least one dose between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2021. In this cohort, 1,093,860 individuals received live zoster vaccination in the observation period. In South Korea, the live zoster vaccine is administered on a single‐dose schedule. 10 Individuals who received at least one dose during this exposure assessment period were classified as vaccinated, and those without any vaccination record were classified as unvaccinated.

The primary outcome is the onset of cognitive and memory disorders, including memory disorders and AD. Only events that occurred beyond 30 days after vaccination were included as outcomes to avoid reverse causation. We provide a table of the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision codes for outcomes (Table S1 in supporting information).

Covariates

Demographic and clinical information was collected by integrating data from three distinct national databases. First, the HIRA database provided demographic variables, including age, sex, household income, and region of residence. Second, clinical measurements, such as body mass index, blood pressure, glomerular filtration rate, and fasting blood glucose, were obtained from the NHIS health examination database. Third, additional clinical and behavioral data were retrieved from the health insurance claims, which included diagnostic codes, medication prescription records (e.g., medication history for hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and coronary artery disease), and self‐reported smoking status and alcohol consumption collected through standardized questionnaires administered during national health check‐ups. For each participant, all covariates were collected from the most recent data available before their respective index date.

Overlap‐weighting

Individuals who met the following criteria were precluded: (1) with insufficient demographic information or health examination data, or died before the index date; and (2) with a history of any cognitive and memory disorders before the index date (excluded n = 198,038; Table S2 in supporting information). We then applied propensity score overlap weighting to balance the vaccinated and unvaccinated groups. 11 The distributions of propensity scores before and after weighting are presented in Figure S2 in supporting information. Propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression including all baseline covariates. Overlap weights were then assigned by giving vaccinated individuals a weight of (1 – propensity score) and unvaccinated individuals a weight of the propensity score. This weighting approach emphasizes individuals with overlapping treatment probabilities and creates a pseudo‐population in which baseline characteristics are well balanced between groups. After applying overlap weighting, we evaluated covariate balance by comparing the standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all covariates. An SMD < 0.1 was considered indicative of adequate covariate balance between the two groups. Covariates used in the overlap weighting were as follows: age; sex; household income; region of residence; Charlson comorbidity index; body mass index group; blood pressure; fasting blood glucose; glomerular filtration rate; history of medication for hypertension, dyslipidemia, and coronary artery disease; smoking; weekly alcohol intake; and aerobic physical activity status.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review: Prior studies have reported a reduced incidence of dementia following live zoster vaccination, suggesting possible neuroprotective effects. However, most studies were limited to older populations aged ≥70 years, lacked behavioral stratification, and had relatively short follow‐up durations. No prior nationwide studies have comprehensively assessed whether zoster vaccination lowers the long‐term risks of amnesia and Alzheimer's disease in adults across a wider age spectrum. Interpretation: In this nationwide cohort study of over one million adults aged ≥ 50 years, live zoster vaccination was associated with significantly lower risks of both amnesia and Alzheimer's disease. These associations were strongest within the first four years post‐vaccination and attenuated thereafter. The results were consistent across subgroups, though the magnitude of protection was smaller among smokers and alcohol users, suggesting lifestyle factors may influence vaccine‐associated neuroprotection. Future directions: Further studies should investigate the immunological mechanisms underlying these associations and determine whether similar effects are observed with the recombinant zoster vaccine. Long‐term follow‐up and international replication are warranted to clarify whether vaccination primarily delays or prevents cognitive decline and to guide vaccination policies for healthy cognitive aging.

Statistical analysis

For the vaccinated group, the date of live zoster vaccination was defined as the index date. To mitigate immortal time bias in the unvaccinated control group, we applied a randomization process by generating values from a uniform distribution. 12 Based on these randomly generated values, the index date was assigned to control participants in a manner that mirrored the distribution of index dates in the vaccinated group. 9 , 12 The follow‐up period ended on January 31, 2024, or upon the occurrence of cognitive and memory disorders or the death of the subject.

We used Cox proportional hazards models to examine the association between live zoster vaccination and the incident risk of cognitive and memory disorders, with estimates of adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The models were fitted in the overlap‐weighted population to account for differences in baseline characteristics between groups. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no meaningful violation was detected. Covariates included in the models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and previous literature. 13 Based on the aHRs, we calculated the number needed to treat (NNT). To complement the relative effect estimates from Cox models, we additionally conducted restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis to quantify the difference in event‐free survival time between vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals. RMST represented the average time free of cognitive and memory disorders up to a pre‐specified truncation time point of 10 years. The between‐group difference in RMST was used to evaluate whether vaccination leads to complete prevention or merely a delay in disease onset. To evaluate whether our findings could be influenced by unmeasured confounding or healthy vaccinee effects, we incorporated four negative control outcomes—neuromuscular junction disorders, self‐harm, alopecia, and appendicitis—which are considered unrelated biologically to zoster vaccination and have been used as negative controls in prior vaccine studies. 14

We further performed the following analyses: (1) subgroup analyses according to alcohol intake and smoking status, (2) stratified analyses based on the covariates used for matching, and (3) time‐specific incidence of outcome at predefined intervals (< 1, 1– < 2, 2– < 4, 4– < 6, 6– < 8, and ≥ 8 years). A summary of the sensitivity analyses is provided in Figure S3 in supporting information. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4). A two‐tailed P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In this cohort, live zoster vaccination was associated with a significantly reduced risk of developing cognitive and memory disorders in older adults, including memory disorders (aHR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.87–0.90]) and AD (aHR, 0.75 [0.71–0.78]). The NNT was 1294 for memory disorders and 2547 for AD. Compared to the unvaccinated individuals, vaccinated individuals showed a minimal increase in event‐free time over 10 years for both memory disorders (RMST, 15.48 days [95% CI, 13.70–17.27]) and AD (11.34 days [10.62–12.07]; Figure 2 and Table S5 in supporting information).

To further explore potential modifiers of the association, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by current smoking and alcohol intake status (Figure 3 and Table S6 in supporting information). In the vaccinated group, 3.8% (n = 1315) were current smokers. For memory disorders, while associations remained true regardless of current smoking status, we found no meaningful difference between smokers and non‐smokers, with aHRs of 0.88 (0.87–0.90) for non‐smokers and 0.87 (0.82–0.92) for smokers. In contrast, for AD, the protective association appeared attenuated among smokers (aHR, 0.88 [0.78–1.00]; P = 0.049) compared to non‐smokers (aHR, 0.74 [0.70–0.77]). Regarding alcohol consumption, 5.9% (n = 13,696) of the vaccinated group were classified as drinkers. An attenuated association was observed among drinkers for both outcomes: memory disorders (aHR, 0.98 [0.95–1.00]; P = 0.032) and AD (aHR, 0.84 [0.79–0.88]) compared to non‐drinkers (memory disorders: aHR, 0.79 [0.77–0.81]; AD: aHR, 0.65 [0.61–0.69]).

Subgroup analysis of the risk of cognitive and memory disorders after live zoster vaccination by current smoking and alcohol intake status. aHR, adjusted hazard ratio; CI, confidence interval.

The protective association of live zoster vaccination and cognitive and memory disorders varied by time since vaccination (Figure 4 and Tables S7–S8 in supporting information). For memory disorders, the greatest reduction in risk was observed within the first 2 years post‐vaccination (< 1 year: aHR, 0.90 [0.87–0.94]; 1–2 years: aHR, 0.81 [0.78–0.84]), with a moderate association persisting during the 2‐ to 4‐year interval (aHR, 0.86 [0.84–0.89]). The association diminished over time, with estimates approaching the null beyond 6 years. For AD, a similar temporal pattern was observed. The strongest protective association was evident during the 2 to 4 years after vaccination (aHR, 0.70 [0.65–0.76]), followed by a sustained but attenuated association at 4 to 6 years (aHR, 0.81 [0.73–0.89]). No statistically significant association was detected beyond 6 years. Further stratification analyses showed no sex difference in the association with memory disorders (male: aHR, 0.87 [0.84–0.89]; female: aHR, 0.89 [0.87–0.91]), but greater protection against AD was observed in males (aHR, 0.70 [0.65–0.75]) than females (aHR, 0.78 [0.74–0.83]). In addition, stronger associations for both memory disorders and AD were found among individuals aged < 60 years, those residing in rural areas, and those who had lower household income (Tables S9–s10 in supporting information).

As negative control outcomes, we evaluated neuromuscular junction disorders, self‐harm, alopecia, and appendicitis. None showed a meaningful association with live zoster vaccination (neuromuscular junction disorders: aHR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.82–1.01]; self‐harm: aHR, 0.64 [95% CI, 0.41–1.01] alopecia: aHR, 1.01 [95% CI, 0.97–1.05]; appendicitis: aHR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.93–1.00]; Table S11 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

Key findings

This study examined the association between live zoster vaccination and the risk of cognitive and memory disorders in a nationwide cohort of South Korean adults aged ≥ 50 years. Live zoster vaccination was associated with a significantly lower risk of both memory disorders and AD. Although the HRs indicated a strong association, the absolute difference in disease‐free time over 10 years was small, suggesting that vaccination may contribute to a slight delay in onset rather than complete prevention. This association remained significant regardless of current smoking or alcohol intake status, although the reduction in risk was less pronounced among smokers and drinkers, particularly for AD, suggesting that lifestyle factors may influence the extent of potential benefits from vaccination. For both outcomes, the association was strongest within the first 1 to 4 years after vaccination and diminished over time, null beyond 6 years.

Underlying plausible mechanism

The observed association between HZ vaccination and a reduced risk of cognitive and memory disorders can be explained by several interrelated mechanisms. First, the vaccine prevents HZ and subsequent reactivation of VZV, which has been implicated in neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and cerebrovascular damage, as VZV can invade cerebral arteries and induce vasculopathy, 15 potentially leading to vascular cognitive impairment. 16 Second, VZV infection can also upregulate amyloidogenic proteins and promote intracellular accumulation of amyloid peptides, 17 suggesting a possible link to the development or progression of neurodegenerative diseases. Last, HZ vaccination may exert neuroprotective effects through immunomodulatory mechanisms that are independent of infection prevention, 18 , 19 which may enhance neuroimmune surveillance and reduce systemic inflammation, both relevant to cognitive decline. 18 Consistent with this, prior studies have reported reduced dementia risk after influenza vaccination, 20 , 21 suggesting that immune modulation by vaccines may play a broader role in mitigating neurodegenerative processes. However, while off‐target effects are generally more pronounced in females, 22 we found no evidence for differences in association between sexes for memory disorders and even reversed results for AD. These findings warrant further investigation into sex‐specific immunological responses to vaccination and their implications for cognitive disorders.

The attenuated protective association of live zoster vaccination against AD in smokers and drinkers could be explained by the fact that both smoking and alcohol consumption are established risk factors for this condition. 23 , 24 However, previous research has shown that individuals who smoke or consume alcohol exhibit lower immune responses to vaccines such as COVID‐19 and hepatitis B. 25 , 26 , 27 This suggests that lifestyle factors may compromise the immunogenicity or durability of vaccine‐induced protection. Further work should explore whether immune‐based interventions confer comparable neuroprotection across different population subgroups.

Comparison of previous studies

Several studies have assessed the association between HZ and the onset of dementia; however, findings remain inconclusive. Two studies from Taiwan 28 and South Korea 29 reported an increased risk of dementia after HZ or HZ ophthalmicus, whereas a large UK cohort study found no significant association. 30 These discrepancies may reflect differences in study design, population characteristics, data sources, or residual confounding. A recent quasi‐experimental study in Wales found a reduced risk of dementia after HZ vaccination among older adults aged ≥ 70 years. 8 However, that study was limited by a high mortality rate during the 7‐year follow‐up and did not explore potential effect modifiers of the association.

Our study builds on and extends these prior findings by validating the observed protective association in an independent population using a larger sample size and longer follow‐up (up to 12 years). Leveraging linked data from three national databases in South Korea, we examined the association between HZ vaccination and a broader spectrum of cognitive and memory disorders, including AD and memory disorders, in a large, real‐world setting. The availability of detailed information on vaccination, health‐care use, and health behaviors enabled adjustment for key confounders and stratified analyses by behavioral risk factors such as smoking and alcohol use. These features enhance the robustness and generalizability of our findings and provide more comprehensive insight into the potential neuroprotective effects of HZ vaccination.

Policy and clinical implications

Although the risk reduction was statistically meaningful, the absolute difference in RMST was not large, and the association showed attenuation over time. These findings indicate that while a potential neuroprotective signal may exist, its magnitude and durability appear limited, warranting cautious interpretation regarding its broader clinical relevance. Nonetheless, the results underscore the need for further investigation into whether vaccine‐induced immune modulation meaningfully influences long‐term neurocognitive outcomes, and if so, in which populations and under what conditions. In particular, given the age distribution of the study population and the temporal pattern of effect attenuation, future work with extended follow‐up, immunological profiling, and evaluation in older and higher‐risk cohorts is warranted. In addition to previously reported off‐target associations with cardiovascular and other chronic diseases, 7 these findings add to the broader evidence that live‐attenuated vaccines could elicit systemic immunological effects beyond their primary targets. However, given the minimal absolute effect size, they should be regarded as exploratory rather than as directly translatable clinical implications.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, factors that may influence the development of cognitive and memory disorders, such as occupational complexity or social engagement, may not have been fully accounted for in this study. Second, some cases of memory disorders and dementia may not be fully captured, either because of delays in clinical recognition or the absence of a formal diagnosis during the follow‐up period. Although we have attempted to minimize bias by excluding individuals with outcomes prior to the index date, the lack of data before 2012 may have resulted in incomplete ascertainment of baseline conditions. Third, the results from the time‐dependent analysis should be interpreted with caution due to the smaller number of participants with follow‐up beyond 8 years. Fourth, the adjuvanted recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix, GSK) 31 was approved in South Korea in 2022, and therefore, long‐term outcome data for this vaccine are not yet available. Further research is warranted to assess its long‐term effects on cognitive and memory disorders. Fifth, HZ vaccination evaluated in this study is recommended for adults aged ≥ 50 years in Korea; however, there are no explicit clinical eligibility criteria that determine the timing of vaccination. Consequently, it was difficult to define a clear time zero based on treatment assignment. This limitation may have affected baseline comparability between the vaccinated and unvaccinated groups and introduce the possibility of baseline selection bias. 32 Sixth, although this study used a large, nationwide cohort, the possibility of residual confounding due to both measured and unmeasured factors cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, although several negative control outcomes, including self‐harm, were analyzed, the possibility of a healthy vaccinee effect cannot be completely ruled out. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution, considering these limitations. 33 Finally, our study population consists exclusively of South Koreans, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to settings with different ethnic compositions or vaccination strategies. To address this, the representativeness of the study participants is summarized in Table S2. 34

CONCLUSION

In this large, population‐based cohort of older adults in South Korea, live zoster vaccination was associated with a lower risk of memory disorders and AD. However, the absolute difference in disease‐free time was not large, indicating that the primary benefit may lie in delaying, rather than preventing, disease onset. The association remained consistent across subgroups defined by smoking and alcohol intake status, although the risk reduction was less pronounced among smokers and drinkers, particularly for AD. These findings support the potential neurological benefits of live zoster vaccination and highlight the need for further research into modifiable factors that may influence the outcomes.
