Curcumin relieves gas explosion-induced brain injury via TLR4-mediated M2 polarization of microglia
Authors: Xinwen Dong, Cuiying Li, Zheng Luo, Yue Ma, Jie Gao, Lvfei Deng, Xiaoran Ma, Jiahang Yang, Sanqiao Yao, Weidong Wu, Yichun Bai, Wenjie Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute microglia-mediated neuroinflammation and oxidative stress play important roles in the pathogenesis of gas explosion (GE)-induced traumatic brain injury (TBI). Curcumin has documented neuroinflammatory protective properties; however, its effects on gas explosion (GE)-induced TBI and the underlying mechanism remain unclear.Materials and methodsIn this study, we established a male rat model of acute TBI and an in vitro model of acute microglial impact injury using shockwave physiotherapy. The effects of curcumin on the extent of the brain injury, as well as the levels of inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress indicators, microglia polarization, and toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) protein expression in model rats and microglial cells were evaluated using histological staining, western blotting, qPCR, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and biochemical assays.ResultsThe results showed that curcumin reduced the pathological changes induced by GE and significantly inhibited the expression of neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (P < 0.05), a marker of microglial activation. Curcumin treatment also promoted M2 polarization of microglia (P < 0.05) and reduced the protein expression levels of TLR4, myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88), NF-κB (P < 0.05), and the pro-inflammatory factors tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and NLRP3 (P < 0.05). Moreover, curcumin treatment significantly reduced the levels of oxidative stress factors in rat tissues (P < 0.05).ConclusionsIn summary, our findings indicated that curcumin alleviated acute neuroinflammation and oxidative stress induced by GE in vivo and in vitro by inhibiting TLR4-mediated M2 polarization of microglia, thus providing a new treatment target for the neuroinflammation associated with TBI caused by explosives.Keywords: Curcumin, Microglia, Neuroinflammation, Oxidative stress, NF-κB pathway, Traumatic brain injury

Introduction

Gas explosion (GE)-induced traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a serious health problem, accounting for half of global trauma-related deaths (Wang et al., 2018). In the United States, TBI has been described as a “silent epidemic” (Sauerbeck et al., 2018). The primary causes of blast TBI include military activities, production and manufacturing, and GE accidents in coal mining (Gallun et al., 2017; Li et al., 2022a). Between 2015 and 2019, coal mine GEs in China caused 669 deaths, with GEs accounting for approximately 50% of these casualties. Thus, coal mine GEs pose a significant threat to the safety of male miners and carry substantial economic costs (Dong et al., 2022). The two common sequelae of explosions are TBI and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Baker et al., 2018), leading to increased attention on the pathogenesis and treatment of GE-induced TBI.

During a gas explosion, shockwaves generated by the blast impact the head: part of the energy is reflected, while the remaining energy activates primary cells throughout the brain, inducing TBI (Uzunalli et al., 2021). TBIs are classified into primary and secondary injuries. Primary injury occurs at the moment of impact; if not mitigated, secondary injury develops, manifesting as brain edema, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, neuronal damage, and blood–brain barrier disruption (Kalra et al., 2022). The time interval between primary and secondary injuries offers a therapeutic window to reduce the severity of secondary damage.

Neuroinflammation is a key pathophysiological process in secondary brain injury. Following TBI, microglia-the resident immune cells of the brain—are rapidly activated (Lin et al., 2020; Ran et al., 2021; Witcher et al., 2021). Activated microglia produce inflammatory mediators that recruit peripheral immune cells and activate other glial cells. Acute neuroinflammation, when tightly controlled, can be beneficial for brain repair (Streit et al., 2004; Tweedie et al., 2013, 2016). However, persistent or excessive microglial activation drives pathological changes and promotes neurodegenerative diseases (Chiu et al., 2016). Therefore, inhibiting microglial overactivation represents a potential strategy for treating acute neuroinflammation after TBI.

The Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathway plays a central role in initiating immune responses and microglial activation. Upon brain injury, TLR4 activation recruits the adaptor protein MyD88, forming the TLR4/MyD88 complex and activating nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB). This signaling cascade triggers microglial activation (Gao et al., 2019a; Li et al., 2022b) and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) (Rahimifard et al., 2017), whereas IL-6 is involved in the acute phase of TBI (Sulimai and Lominadze, 2020). In TBI models, inhibition of TLR4/NF-κB signaling has been shown to attenuate neurological injury and cerebral edema (Wang et al., 2021). Continued acute neuroinflammation after TBI leads to brain degeneration, suggesting that modulating this pathway could have significant therapeutic effects (Dai et al., 2018). Identifying drugs that inhibit activation of TLR4 and NF-κB-mediated microglial inflammation could be key to the treatment of acute neuroinflammation induced by a gas explosion.

Curcumin, a natural polyphenol antioxidant with neuroprotective properties, is known as a non-specific inhibitor of NF-κB (Zhou et al., 2018). Studies have shown that curcumin regulates activated microglia and reduces injury in ischemic stroke and cerebral infarction models (Li et al., 2022b). It also inhibits apoptosis and attenuates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress by decreasing IL-6 and TNF-α levels (Kitamura, 2011). In an in vitro model of LPS-induced spinal cord injury in BV2 microglia, curcumin exerted protective effects by inhibiting the TREM2/TLR4/NF-κB pathway (Zhang et al., 2019), and evidence suggests it may promote microglial polarization toward the M2 phenotype (Xie et al., 2023). Short-term curcumin treatment prevented LPS-induced acute neuroinflammation and long-term memory impairment in mice (Sorrenti et al., 2018). Our recent in vivo experiments showed that long-term curcumin treatment alleviated GE-induced TBI via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway (Dong et al., 2024); however, the mechanisms by which curcumin regulates acute neuroinflammation in the context of GE-induced TBI remain unclear. In particular, whether curcumin acts through TLR4-mediated M2 polarization to modulate acute neuroinflammation and oxidative stress has not been established.

The objective of this study is to elucidate the mechanisms by which curcumin modulates acute neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in GE-induced TBI. We hypothesize that curcumin alleviates GE-induced TBI by regulating microglia-mediated acute neuroinflammation and oxidative stress through TLR4-mediated M2 polarization.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Chemicals and reagents used for this study are listed in Table S1.

Animals

Thirty male Sprague-Dawley rats (250 ± 20 g) were acquired from SPF Biotechnology (License key: SCXK (Jing) 2019-0010; Beijing, China). Rats were housed in a humidity- (50% ± 5%) and temperature- (22°C ± 0.5°C) controlled room under a 12-h light and dark cycle, with free access to food and water for at least 5 days before the experiments.

Rat model of gas explosion-induced brain injury

Thirty rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 10/group): the control (Con), model (Mod), and model + curcumin (Cur) treatment (Mod + Cur) groups. The animals in the Con group received a normal diet and were not exposed to gas explosion, animals in the Mod group were subjected to a gas explosion, further induced a gas explosion TBI (geTBI) model, and animals in the Mod + Cur group were administered 100 mg/kg curcumin by gavage immediately after the gas explosion procedure (Dong et al., 2022). Curcumin was dissolved in DMSO, and the final DMSO concentration was less than 0.1%. For the gas explosion procedure, the rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal delivery of 1% sodium pentobarbital and placed in fixed cages with their heads facing the source of the explosion in sealed explosion chambers 200 m from the source of the explosion. The explosion was performed using an air mixture containing 9% methane gas, and the specific parameters of the explosion were as previously described (Dong et al., 2020). At 24 h after the gas explosion, the rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 40 mg/kg (corresponding to an administration volume of 4 mL/kg). Brain tissue was then collected, and blood samples were obtained from the abdominal aorta. After decapitation, fix the skull at the eye socket with hook forceps. The brain tissues were cut along the direction of the foramen magnum, the brain nerves were separated, and the brain tissue was then completely removed. The brain tissue was weighed. Under a dissecting microscope, the cerebral cortex was separated and removed, and immediately stored at −80°C for subsequent protein and qPCR assays. The blood samples were incubated at room temperature for 20 min, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min, and then the serum was collected and stored at −80°C for ELISA. This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Xinxiang Medical University (No. XYLL-2020007), and animal disposal was performed in accordance with animal ethics standards. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Regulations on Animal Experimentation at Xinxiang Medical University and the ARRIVE guidelines.

Real-time RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the cerebral cortex or GMI-R1 microglia using RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent, chloroform, isopropanol, ethanol, and RNase-free water according to the manufacturer's instructions, and then the concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed. Isolated RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript® III RT Super Mix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (R323, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The resulting cDNA was mixed with synthetic primers (Suzhou Jinweizhi Biotechnology, Suzhou, China), which are listed in Table 1, and AcceQ Universal SYBR Master Mix for qPCR. The amplification parameters were 95°C/5 min initial denaturation, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 30 s, 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 60 s, and 95°C for 15 s. β-actin was used as an internal reference gene, and relative mRNA expression was calculated by normalizing data to actin. β-actin served as the endogenous control for normalization. Relative quantification was calculated via the 2ˆ (−ΔΔCt) method.

Histopathological analysis

Isolated rat brain tissues were washed with normal saline and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Next, the tissue samples were dehydrated with ethanol, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a uniform thickness of 3 μm, fixed on a glass slide (G6012-1, Servicebio, China), stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE, G1076, Servicebio, China) and then covered with a cover slip (10212432C, Jiangsu Shitai Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., China) of 0.13–0.16 mm thickness, and then observed under a light microscope.

IHC staining

Brain tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm thick sections. Then, the antigen retrieval process with Citric acid antigen repair solution (pH 6.0, G1202, Servicebio, China) is carried out. Next, the slices are placed in a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution to block endogenous peroxidase, and then 3% BSA is uniformly applied to cover the tissue serum for blocking. The sections were then incubated with antibodies against TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB p65, and neuron-specific enolase (NSE) overnight at 4°C. Next, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody for 20 min at room temperature (25°C ± 2°C), washed three times with PBS (PH7.4) (5 min each) before and after adding the secondary antibody, incubated with peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin for 10 min at room temperature, developed using chromogenic diaminobenzidine, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were obtained, and immunohistochemically staining was analyzed as follows: Positive area (%) = positive area/total area.

In vitro shockwave model

The immortalized rat GMI-R1 microglial cell line was cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. Before the impact injury, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were rinsed twice with 1× PBS and digested with trypsin-EDTA for 2 min. To stop digestion, 2 mL of complete medium was added, and the cells were centrifuged and washed with PBS, and counted. Next, 1 mL cell suspensions (at a density of 1 × 105 cells/ml) were prepared and placed in 5 mL centrifuge tubes. Next, the centrifuge tubes were sealed with sterile sealing film, placed upside down on top of the impact handle of the shockwave therapy instrument (HM08CJ, Wuhan Haomeng Health Management Co., LTD, Wuhan, Hubei), and a coupling agent was applied to the joint. The process of establishing the above model is based on our previous published results (Ding et al., 2022). We determined that the optimal conditions for the model were as follows: energy level, 10 Bar; shock frequency, 10 Hz; number of shocks, 1500; impact density, 7 × 105; and incubation time, 12 h. The detailed verification results can be found in Supporting Material 1 and Fig. S1.

The reproducibility of this system allows precise titration of shockwave parameters (e.g., pressure amplitude, pulse number) to simulate varying severities of blast exposure, from subthreshold mechanical perturbation to overt cellular injury. By decoupling the direct mechanical stimulus from secondary ischemia or systemic immune influx, this platform enables mechanistic dissection of the microglial mechanotransduction cascade-from early calcium influx and cytoskeletal rearrangement to downstream pro-inflammatory gene expression. Furthermore, the use of primary versus immortalized microglial lines can be systematically compared, offering insight into cell-type-specific sensitivities. Given that microglial priming and hyperactivation are implicated in chronic traumatic encephalopathy and post-blast neuroinflammation, this model may serve as a high-throughput screening tool for mechanoprotective or anti-inflammatory interventions before in vivo validation.

To determine the best conditions for establishing the model, the CCK-8 assay was performed. At a shock frequency of 10 Hz, other parameters were varied, including shock time (1000, 1500, 2000, 2500, and 3000 times), energy level (1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10 Bar), culture time (12, 24, or 48 h), and cell density (1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 × 105 cells/ml). Immediately after impact, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well. At the specified time points, 10 μL of CCK8 was added to each well of the plate. After incubation for 2 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SynergyH1M2, Bio Tek). The viability of each group was calculated, and the experiment was repeated five times.

In vitro model: experimental groups and drug treatments

The cells were divided into six groups: the control (Con), curcumin-treated (Cur), model (Mod), model + curcumin-treated (Mod + Cur), model + TAK-242-treated (Mod + TAK-242), and LPS-treated (LPS). The Con group did not receive any treatment, the Cur and LPS groups were treated with curcumin and LPS, respectively, and all Mod groups (Mod, Mod + Cur, and Mod + TAK-242) were subjected to shockwave. After the shock was performed, the Mod + Cur and Mod + TAK-242 groups were treated with curcumin and TAK-242, respectively, and then incubated for 12 h. TAK-242, a selective inhibitor of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), and LPS, a well-known TLR4 agonist, were used to evaluate the role of TLR4 signaling in shockwave-induced microglial responses. TLR4 is a key mediator of inflammatory activation following mechanical injury, and LPS provided a positive control for classical neuroinflammation. These treatments thus help delineate the molecular mechanism underlying the effects of curcumin in this model. The CCK-8 kit was used to determine the optimal concentrations of the treatments. Briefly, GMI-R1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at the density of 5 × 103 cells/well. The concentrations used in subsequent experiments were as follows: 5 μmol/L curcumin, 200 nmol/L TAK-242, and 0.5 μg/mL LPS. The detailed verification results can be found in Supporting Material 1 and Fig. S2.

For the experiment, LPS was dissolved in the culture medium, and TAK-242 and curcumin were dissolved in DMSO and then diluted in the culture medium. After the cells were shocked, they were incubated with 1, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 nmol/L curcumin or 10, 20, 50, 10 and 200 nmol/L TAK-242 for 12 h. Untreated cells were incubated with 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 μg/m L LPS for 12 h (n = 5 for each concentration). After incubation, 10 μL of CCK8 was added to each well, the plate was incubated for 2 h, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The survival rate of the cells in each group was calculated, and the experiment was repeated five times.

Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) live/dead assay

Cells from each group were seeded in 96-well plates, cultured for 12 h, and then the culture medium was discarded. Next, a Calcein-AM/PI live/dead assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were washed twice with PBS, 100 μL of working solution was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. Stained cells were observed using an Axiovert 5 inverted fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Germany). Live cells were stained green, and dead cells were stained red. The experiment was repeated three times.

ROS and lipid peroxidation assays

ROS production in the cells was measured using an ROS assay kit (CA1420, Beijing Solarbio Science&Technology Co.,Ltd.) and a fluorescence microscope according to the kit manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were cultured in a 96-well plate (1 × 104 cells/well) for 12 h. Then, the medium was removed, 200 μL of staining solution (10 μM) was added, and the plate was incubated at 37°C for 40 min. The staining solution was removed, cells were washed twice with PBS, and then 200 μL of PBS was added. Stained cells were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Axiovert 5, ZEISS), and images were acquired. The excitation wavelength was 518 nm, and the emission wavelength was 610 nm. To evaluate SOD and MDA levels, assays were performed according to the instructions provided with the MDA reagent. Five replicate wells were set up for each group, and the absorbance was measured using a Bio Tek microplate reader. The experiments were repeated three times.

Immunofluorescent staining

Cells were seeded on round slides (15 × 105 cells/well) in a 12-well plate and incubated at 37°C for 12 h. After washing twice with PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in the dark for 20 min. After washing again with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were washed again, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin V for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with rabbit anti-NF-κB p65 antibody overnight at 4°C (Table 2). Slides were incubated at room temperature for 20 min, washed three times with PBS, incubated with a secondary DyLight 649-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:200 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature, washed with PBS, and then mounted with 40 μL of Antifade Mounting Medium containing DAPI. Images were acquired using an inverted confocal microscope (DMi8; LEICA, Germany).

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from ipsilateral brain tissues or GMI-R1 microglia treated under different conditions for 12 h. Briefly, the brain tissue and cells were homogenized in a lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors and centrifuged. Then, the supernatants were collected, and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by electrophoresis on a 10% sodium lauryl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h and then incubated overnight with anti-NLRP3, anti-IL-10, anti-IL-6, anti-IL-1β, anti-TLR4, anti-NF-κB p65, anti-MyD88, or anti-β-actin primary antibodies at 4°C (Table 2). The membranes were washed with TBST, incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h (1:5000 dilution) at room temperature, and then washed again with TBST. The protein bands on the PVDF membrane were visualized using a chemiluminescence kit and quantified using ImageJ software. Proteins were normalized to β-actin, and the experiment was repeated three times. The dilution factors for the primary antibodies are listed in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SD. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Multiple comparisons were performed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Tukey's multiple comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 or P < 0.01.

Results

Curcumin prevented shockwave-induced histopathological changes in the rat brain

HE staining of brain tissues from the control, model, and model + curcumin-treated rats (Fig. 1 and Table 3) showed that in the control group, the structure of the brain tissue was normal, with a total pathological score of 0 and no inflammatory cell infiltration detected. In the model group, the cells in the cerebral cortex appeared to be swollen and vacuolated, the nuclei were consolidated, more apoptotic cells were present, and dense masses were observed; the group had a total pathological score of 6 (corresponding to moderate brain tissue injury) and an average of 18 inflammatory cells per high-power field (HPF). In the model + curcumin-treated group, although the cerebral cortex cells appeared to be loose and degenerated, the nuclei and cytoplasm were relatively intact; the total pathological score was significantly reduced to 3, and the number of inflammatory cells was decreased to 5 per HPF, indicating a marked alleviation of brain tissue damage.

Curcumin treatment reduced the protein levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the cerebral cortex of TBI model rats

To evaluate the pro-inflammatory response to shockwave in the brain, we measured the protein levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in the cerebral cortex of rats in different experimental groups (Fig. 2). Our results showed that the protein levels of IL-1β (P < 0.01) and IL-6 (P < 0.05) were significantly higher in the model group than in the control but were significantly lower in the model + curcumin treatment group (P < 0.05) than in the model group. These results suggest that curcumin has an anti-inflammatory effect on acute neuroinflammation after the impact caused by a gas explosion.

Curcumin decreased the expression levels of TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB p65, and NSE in the cerebral cortex of TBI model rats

Immunohistochemical staining showed that the levels of TLR4 (P < 0.01), MyD88 (P < 0.01), neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (P < 0.01), and NF-κB p65 (P < 0.05) in the cerebral cortex were significantly higher in the model group than in the control group (Fig. 3a and b), and curcumin treatment significantly decreased the expression levels of TLR4 (P < 0.01), MyD88 (P < 0.05), NSE (P < 0.01), and NF-κB p65 (P < 0.05) in the model group.

Curcumin inhibited activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway

RT-PCR showed that the mRNA expression levels of TLR4 (P < 0.05), MyD88, NF-κB p65, and NLRP3 (P < 0.01) in the model group were significantly increased at 24 h after impact than those in the control group. In contrast, the expression levels of TLR4 (P < 0.05), MyD88 (P < 0.05), NLRP3 (P < 0.05), and NF-κB p65(P < 0.01) were significantly lower in the model + curcumin treatment group than those in the model group (Fig. 3c).

Curcumin and TAK-242 attenuated shockwave-induced microglial oxidative stress and apoptosis

Shockwave exposure significantly induced oxidative stress in GMI-R1 cells, as evidenced by a marked increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels (P < 0.01), along with reduced superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity compared to control cells (Fig. 4a–c–e). Treatment with curcumin significantly reversed the elevation of ROS and MDA (P < 0.05), although the modest increase in SOD did not reach significance. Notably, the TLR4-specific inhibitor TAK-242 produced a similar or even more pronounced antioxidative effect, significantly lowering both ROS and MDA levels (P < 0.01) than those in the model group. These findings indicate that both curcumin and TLR4 inhibition effectively alleviate shockwave-induced oxidative stress in microglia. Moreover, calcein-AM/PI staining revealed that shockwave or LPS treatment markedly increased microglial apoptosis, an effect that was significantly reversed by either curcumin or TAK-242, suggesting that curcumin protects against shockwave-induced cell death, potentially through TLR4-related pathways (Fig. 4b).

Curcumin and TAK-242 inhibited shockwave-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in GMI-R1 cells

Using an in vitro shockwave model, we further assessed the anti-inflammatory effects of curcumin relative to TAK-242. At 12 h post-exposure, compared with the corresponding levels in the control group, the protein levels of IL-1β, IL-6 and CD86 in the model and LPS groups were significantly higher (IL-1β and CD86, P < 0.01; IL-6, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5a–d). Compared with the corresponding levels in the model group, the expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and CD86 were significantly reduced in the TAK-242 treatment group (IL-1β and IL-6, P < 0.01; CD86, P < 0.05). In the curcumin treatment group, the protein levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and CD86 were also significantly lower (IL-1β, P < 0.01; IL-6 and CD86, P < 0.05).

Furthermore, compared with the control group, the mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and NSE in the model group were significantly higher (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5e–h), while the mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and NSE in the curcumin and TAK-242 treatment groups were lower than those in the model group (IL-1β and NSE, P < 0.05; IL-6, P < 0.01), and the mRNA levels of TNF-α trended downward (Fig. 5g). These results demonstrate that curcumin, similar to the specific TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242, effectively suppresses shockwave-triggered pro-inflammatory responses in microglia, implicating TLR4 as a key target.

Curcumin alleviated shockwave-induced GMI-R1 cell inflammation by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88 pathway and NF-κB expression

To confirm the involvement of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis, we treated shockwave-exposed GMI-R1 cells with the TLR4 agonist LPS or the inhibitor TAK-242. Both shockwave and LPS treatment significantly increased the protein levels of TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6a–d), as well as promoted nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 (P < 0.01). Importantly, treatment with either curcumin or TAK-242 significantly attenuated these changes, reducing both the total and nuclear levels of these signaling molecules (Fig. 6e). These data indicate that curcumin, like TAK-242, mitigates shockwave-induced microglial inflammation by blunting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway activation.

Discussion

Unlike conventional TBI, explosion-induced TBI is caused by shockwaves that travel through the skull into the brain parenchyma (Ling and Ecklund, 2011). Diffuse damage to the gray and white matter of the brain can lead to cerebral edema, nerve cell death, increased blood-brain barrier permeability, and intracerebral hemorrhage (Risdall and Menon, 2011). Several studies have shown that the cell damage in explosive TBI is directly caused by shockwaves (Bailey et al., 2016). During the acute stage (24 h after TBI), the mRNA and protein expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the brain tissue increase, cerebral edema develops, and blood-brain barrier permeability is altered (Morganti-Kossmann et al., 2002; Ng and Lee, 2019). The results obtained using our in vivo and in vitro shockwave injury models showed that the expression levels of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in rat brains and GMI-R1 cells were significantly higher in the model groups than in the control groups. Furthermore, HE staining of rat brain tissues showed inflammatory cell infiltration, and live/dead staining confirmed a significantly higher number of apoptotic cells in the model group, validating the successful establishment of the TBI model. The results of this study on the establishment of the blast injury model are consistent with those of the other blunt impact model (Zhu et al., 2014). Curcumin treatment (100 mg/kg) effectively reversed these pathological and biochemical changes, confirming its neuroprotective potential in acute explosive TBI. The chosen dose falls within the commonly effective range (50–200 mg/kg) in rodent acute neurological injury models; allometric scaling yields a human equivalent dose of ∼16.2 mg/kg, which, despite bioavailability challenges, supports further investigation with enhanced formulations.

Curcumin was shown to reduce the expression levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α after TBI in vitro and in vivo to improve the pathological changes in the brain tissue of model rats and reduce apoptosis of rat microglia in vitro (Bailey et al., 2016), suggesting that curcumin has a neuroprotective effect on explosion-induced TBI. Curcumin treatment significantly decreased the expression of IL-6, which exerts a therapeutic effect on acute neuroinflammation by reducing the expression levels of other inflammatory cytokines and inducing M2 polarization of microglia (Xie et al., 2023).

The molecular mechanism underlying the therapeutic effect of curcumin on acute neuroinflammation is not fully understood. Studies have shown that TLR4 activation leads to oxidative damage and inflammation of cranial nerves, which, in turn, leads to cognitive impairment, suggesting that TLR4 is an important therapeutic target. This conclusion is consistent with another brain injury model related to cerebral hemorrhage (Xiong et al., 2016). TLR4 is widely expressed in microglia (Wang et al., 2020a) and plays essential roles in microglial activation and the development of the inflammatory response. Activation of TLR4 rapidly induces nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 through the MyD88-dependent signaling pathway, leading to increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Rong et al., 2022). Previous experiments using other TBI models have shown that neuroinflammatory responses can be attenuated by inhibiting the microglial TLR4 and NF-κB signaling pathways to treat ischemic brain injury in mice or focal cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury in rats (Qin et al., 2018; Lan et al., 2013). Curcumin has also been reported to promote the polarization of microglia toward the M2 (anti-inflammatory) phenotype by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby alleviating the neurological inflammation associated with subarachnoid hemorrhage (Gao et al., 2019a). In this study, curcumin significantly suppressed shockwave-induced apoptosis of GMI-R1 cells, decreased the gene and protein expression levels of TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65, reduced the expression of the microglial CD86, and inhibited NF-κB nuclear translocation. Further animal studies have demonstrated that lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS)-induced depressive symptoms are associated with microglial activation and neuroinflammation mediated by the MAPK pathway (Huang et al., 2025). Other studies have also indicated that spinal cord injuries and other brain diseases involve microglial inflammation following activation of the NF-κB/MAPK pathways (Hai et al., 2025; Min et al., 2024). This suggests that the alleviation of shockwave-induced microglial activation and neuroinflammation by curcumin may occur through inhibition of the NF-κB/MAPK pathways. Further investigation is warranted.

Within minutes of a TBI, oxidative stress occurs, which plays an important role in the development of secondary brain injury (Smith et al., 2023). In TBI, oxidative stress and inflammation interact, and excessive ROS production can lead to increased inflammation and apoptosis (Hussain et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020b). Curcumin has been used in the treatment of spinal cord injury to inhibit oxidative stress and protect neurons (Gao et al., 2019b). Curcumin has also been reported to reduce ROS production during oxidative stress in HepG2 cells (Huang et al., 2023). Microglia are the main source of ROS in the central nervous system, and excess ROS can intensify the activation of microglia and astrocytes, thereby aggravating neuroinflammation (von Leden et al., 2017; Block et al., 2007). Inhibition of LPS-induced oxidative stress by curcumin has been reported in mouse microglia (Gao et al., 2019b); however, whether curcumin can inhibit shockwave-induced oxidative stress in rat microglia had not been shown. In this study, we examined the antioxidant properties of curcumin using an in vitro model of shockwave-induced GMI-R1 rat microglia. We found that curcumin inhibited the production of cellular ROS, significantly reduced MDA content, increased SOD levels, and reduced apoptosis, which is consistent with the results of previous studies (Huang et al., 2023).

A key advance of this study is the parallel in vitro use of a TLR4-specific agonist (LPS) and antagonist (TAK-242) to dissect curcumin's mechanism of action (Wang et al., 2020c). Our in vitro results demonstrated that curcumin not only reduced oxidative stress (ROS, MDA) and apoptosis in shockwave-exposed GMI-R1 microglia but also closely mimicked the effects of TAK-242 in suppressing the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis. Specifically, both curcumin and TAK-242 similarly inhibited the shockwave- or LPS-induced upregulation of TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65, blocked NF-κB nuclear translocation, and reduced the expression of downstream pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and the M1 marker CD86. Additional animal studies suggest that curcumin's preventive effect in inhibiting the acute effects of neuroinflammation could be valuable for reducing the long-term consequences of brain inflammation (Sorrenti et al., 2018). These findings provide strong mechanistic evidence that curcumin's anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative effects are primarily mediated through the inhibition of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway in microglia.

The role of LPS and TAK-242 in this study is twofold. First, LPS, a classic TLR4 agonist, was used to mimic and amplify the shockwave-induced inflammatory cascade, confirming that shockwave injury shares a TLR4-dependent mechanism similar to endotoxin-triggered neuroinflammation. Second, TAK-242, a specific TLR4 signaling inhibitor, served as a pharmacological tool to validate the pathway specificity. The observation that curcumin produced effects quantitatively and qualitatively similar to TAK-242—reducing oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammatory cytokine expression—strongly supports that curcumin acts as a functional TLR4 pathway inhibitor in microglia. This interpretation is further reinforced by our in vivo data showing curcumin downregulated TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65 in the cerebral cortex of TBI rats.

Moreover, our in vitro findings help explain the in vivo observations. For instance, the reduction in cortical IL-1β and IL-6 protein levels in curcumin-treated rats (Section 3.2) can now be mechanistically linked to the suppression of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB axis, as directly demonstrated in GMI-R1 cells (Section 3.7). Likewise, the decreased expression of NSE and NLRP3 in vivo aligns with the curcumin-mediated reduction of microglial activation and pyroptotic signaling observed in vitro. These parallel data significantly strengthen the conclusion that curcumin targets microglial TLR4 signaling to alleviate acute neuroinflammation following explosive TBI.

The results of this study provide strong evidence for the protective properties of curcumin against acute neuroinflammation. Using the shockwave injury rat model, we showed that curcumin treatment reduced the gas explosion shockwave-induced increases in the expression levels of inflammatory factors and oxidative stress in microglia in the cerebral cortex. Mechanistically, we showed that curcumin exerted its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting apoptosis of microglial cells and alleviating acute neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. Prior study has also indicated that curcumin reduces the production of ROS and inflammatory mediators in HIV-1-gp120-stimulated microglia and protects cortical neurons against HIV-1-mediated apoptosis (Guo et al., 2013). Furthermore, our results showed that curcumin inhibited nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 in shockwave-treated rat microglia, while TAK-242 reduced shockwave effects on acute neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. These results suggested that the protective properties of curcumin were due to the downregulation of signaling through the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the effects of curcumin observed in animal experiments require further experimental verification, such as long-term clinical interventions, before extrapolation to humans. Second, it remains unclear whether pathological mechanisms distinct from those in traumatic brain injury (TBI) contribute to gas explosion-induced TBI. This ambiguity may limit the applicability of our findings to other TBI subtypes. Third, although this study elucidated the short-term effects, future research should prioritize observational and interventional studies on long-term brain damage outcomes, which hold greater clinical significance.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides integrated in vivo and in vitro evidence that curcumin ameliorates shockwave-induced acute neuroinflammation and oxidative stress by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway in microglia. The use of LPS and TAK-242 not only validates the pathway specificity but also positions curcumin as a potential TLR4-modulating agent. Future studies should focus on bioavailability-enhanced curcumin formulations and explore whether this pathway contributes to long-term functional recovery after explosive TBI.
