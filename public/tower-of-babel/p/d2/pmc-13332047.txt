Generative AI for controllable protein sequence design: A survey
Authors: Yiheng Zhu, Zitai Kong, Jialu Wu, Mingze Yin, Weize Liu, Yuqiang Han, Hongxia Xu, Chang-Yu Hsieh, Tingjun Hou, Jian Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe design of novel protein sequences with targeted functionalities underpins a central theme in protein engineering, impacting diverse fields such as drug discovery and enzymatic engineering. However, navigating this vast combinatorial search space remains a severe challenge due to time and financial constraints. This scenario is rapidly evolving as the transformative advancements in AI have been propelling the protein design field into a new era. In this survey, we systematically review recent advances in generative AI for controllable protein sequence design. To set the stage, we first outline the foundational tasks in protein sequence design in terms of the constraints involved and present key generative models and optimization algorithms. We then offer in-depth reviews of each design task and discuss the in silico evaluation approaches and pertinent applications. Finally, we identify the unresolved challenges and highlight research opportunities that merit deeper exploration.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Proteins are indispensable biomolecules that execute a myriad of biological processes fundamental to life, such as catalyzing enzymatic reactions1 and mediating immune responses2. The ability to design novel proteins holds immense potential for addressing critical challenges across medicine, industry, and sustainability-from developing new therapeutics and vaccines to creating biodegradable enzymes and biosensors. Motivated by the versatility exhibited by natural proteins, designing novel amino acid sequences that encode proteins with desired functions has been a central challenge in bioengineering3.

Because the protein sequence space is astronomically large, experimental approaches such as directed evolution4 are often time- and cost-prohibitive. Traditional computational protein design has primarily relied on physical simulations and energy-based optimization methods. Approaches such as molecular dynamics5 and Rosetta-based protocols6 typically required expert knowledge to manually craft sequence profiles and were computationally intensive. While powerful, these methods were largely guided by existing structural templates and struggled to efficiently explore the vastness of novel sequence space. To overcome these challenges, recent years have witnessed the trend of leveraging generative AI technologies to explore the biochemical space intelligently. These methods offer significant advantages: they can generate protein sequences end-to-end, access a broader and more novel functional space, and substantially accelerate the design process by learning complex sequence-structure-function relationships from large-scale datasets. Fueled by advances in generative AI, the field of protein design is undergoing a paradigm shift.

Protein sequences constitute the universal design space, encoding both structural and functional information through the linear arrangement of amino acids7. Every fold, motif, and biochemical activity ultimately traces back to this sequence. In laboratory synthesis, the precise amino acid sequence must be defined, as it directs chemical and recombinant assembly and serves as the benchmark for verifying molecular fidelity. Consequently, this survey focuses on sequence-based protein design. Evolutionary-scale protein sequence databases, as opposed to limited and static structural data that yield a biased subset of the protein design space, lay the groundwork for sequence-based protein design. Deep generative models offer a promising tool for capturing the distribution underlying natural protein sequences to generate novel and diverse proteins. Moreover, making protein design controllable is crucial for the transition from theory to practice. Ideally, a practical method ought to generate proteins that adhere to task-specific structural and functional constraints. To this end, researchers have utilized conditional generative models and optimization algorithms to realize such tailored protein design.

Protein sequence design stands as a rapidly growing field of scientific discovery, necessitating a thorough review of the extensive literature to sketch the contours of the field. Despite certain surveys detailing advancements8 and highlighting controllability9, they may fall short in igniting enthusiasm for controllable protein sequence design among both the life science and machine learning communities due to two main reasons: (1) the majority are framed from a biochemical standpoint, potentially inaccessible for AI researchers without deep domain expertise; (2) existing reviews often emphasize select aspects of the literature, such as de novo design, while neglecting equally important themes like mutation-based optimization. This fragmented presentation fails to provide a holistic view of the field’s methodological diversity. In contrast, our survey aims to bridge this divide by providing a comprehensive and structured overview that integrates conceptual, methodological, and practical perspectives. We seek to demystify protein sequence design for researchers across both life sciences and AI by systematically categorizing controllable design tasks according to the nature of their constraints-ranging from structural and functional requirements to higher-level conditional objectives. Furthermore, we map each task to its corresponding generative modeling framework, encompassing autoregressive, diffusion10, and other emerging models. In this way, practitioners in the life sciences can identify algorithms best suited to their specific tasks, while AI researchers can more readily understand protein design challenges and develop more solid models. Additionally, apart from general proteins, we will focus particularly on several categories of functional proteins that are of significant interest to the industry, such as peptides, antibodies, and enzymes. We hope this survey reaches both the machine learning and biochemistry fields and inspires further collaboration towards developing more sophisticated and controllable algorithms.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: We first outline the foundational tasks involving controllable protein sequence design, along with relevant generative models and optimization algorithms (Sec 2). We then encapsulate the prevalent paradigms for distribution learning on natural sequence space and introduce associated databases (Sec 3). Next, we provide an extensive review of each controllable design task (Sec 4). We also examine the in silico evaluation approaches (Sec 5) and pertinent applications (Sec 6). Finally, we conclude with the open challenges and point to potential opportunities for further exploration (Sec 7). Protein sequence design methods reviewed in this survey are summarized in Table 1.

Preliminaries

Definition of Controllable Protein Design

Within the context of AI, controllable protein design represents an advanced extension of the rational protein design11,12. Both approaches aim to design protein sequences under conditional control informed by prior knowledge. Generally, a protein can be represented as a sequence of amino acids x = [x1, x2, …, xL] of length L, where each amino acid xi is drawn from a vocabulary V consisting of 20 standard amino acids. Controllable protein sequence design aims to generate plausible proteins that satisfy some desired constraints c, which may vary across different tasks. These constraints may represent hard requirements (e.g., backbone structures or binding targets) or soft preferences (e.g., functional or biophysical properties), and may involve trade-offs between feasibility and competing design objectives. Formally, we pose the controllable protein sequence design as the problem of sampling x from p(x∣c).

A fundamental tenet of molecular biology is that “sequence determines structure, structure determines function”7. While this relationship can be more complex in practice due to conformational ensembles and context-dependent effects, it provides a useful conceptual framework for organizing controllable protein design tasks. Consequently, we divide the meaningful control conditions c into three categories, i.e., structural, functional, and higher-level constraints, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Structure-to-sequence Design

Structure-to-sequence design, which is often referred to as the protein inverse folding problem, aims to automatically identify the protein sequences that fold into the given backbone structures. Formally, the structural condition is defined as c=r=[r1,r2,…,rn]∈Rn×4×3 with ri representing the Cartesian coordinates of the i-th residue’s backbone atoms (i.e., N, Cα, and C, with O optionally).

Higher-level sequence design

More sophisticated formats describe higher-level protein properties that beyond mere structures and functions. These formats support design processes tailored to specific conditions, including binding targets and textual prompts.

Such targets may include proteins (e.g., antigens), nucleic acids, or small molecules. Deep Generative Models

Deep generative models have enabled remarkable progress across a spectrum of fields, ranging from image synthesis and text generation to scientific discovery. Herein, we concisely outline the core principles behind representative generative frameworks.

Autoregressive models

Autoregressive models (ARMs)14 learn the high-dimensional joint distribution p(x) by factorizing it into the product of univariate conditionals as follows:

ARMs model the current token xi based on all preceding tokens x1, x2, …, xi−1, and require a predefined order in which the sequence x is generated. While the ordering choice is straightforward for some modalities, such as text and audio, it is less evident for others, including images and graphs. ARMs have long dominated natural language processing and are often adopted as the default probabilistic framework for protein sequences because of their shared sequential nature9. However, unlike natural language, protein sequences do not possess an inherent generative order. Autoregressive factorization can therefore be viewed as one way to parameterize the joint distribution of sequences. Alternative frameworks, such as diffusion models, may provide complementary advantages, including order-agnostic generation.

Variational Autoencoders

VAEs consist of two main components: an encoder and a decoder. The encoder maps an input x into a lower-dimensional latent representation z, typically by learning the parameters of a probability distribution qϕ(z∣x), such as the mean and variance of a Gaussian. This process compresses the high-dimensional data into a compact latent space. The decoder, parameterized as pθ(x∣z), then reconstructs the input data from the latent variable, effectively learning to generate data similar to the original input. VAEs are commonly employed to model the global dependencies within protein sequences16. Moreover, the learned latent space provides a continuous and structured representation of sequence variations, facilitating efficient latent space optimization algorithm (introduced in section 2.3) for protein design and property enhancement.

Numerous variants of GAN have since been developed to deal with different scenarios. For instance, StyleGAN18 and CycleGAN19 enable specialized image synthesis, while Wasserstein GAN20 and f-GAN21 employ the Wasserstein distance and f-divergence, respectively, to measure distributional discrepancies and mitigate mode collapse. More recently, GANs have also been applied to protein design22,23.

To bridge this gap, researchers have explored two main strategies for adapting diffusion models to discrete domains: the first embeds discrete symbols into a continuous space25, and the second defines a diffusion process that inherently operates within the discrete space26.

Optimization Algorithms

We overview the widely-used optimization algorithms designed to optimize the objective function or reward.

Latent space optimization

Latent space optimization (LSO)34 circumvents the discrete search space by performing optimization over the low-dimensional continuous latent space learned by generative models. Following the navigation of the latent space, the optimized latent representations are then decoded back into sequences. For protein sequence design, LSO has emerged as a paradigm that addresses the high-dimensional discreteness of amino acid sequences by optimizing over a continuous latent manifold learned by generative models35. Within this framework, protein sequences are first embedded into a latent space that captures their underlying biophysical or evolutionary features, enabling smooth navigation toward regions associated with desirable properties such as stability, binding affinity, and so on36–38. Depending on the design objective, studies differ in how the latent space is explored: some approaches employ gradient-based optimization using differentiable property predictors to guide the search35, while others sample latent representations conditioned on target attributes through probabilistic or Bayesian updates39. The optimized latent vectors can be decoded back to valid protein sequences using sequence decoders trained jointly with the encoder. Compared with direct optimization in discrete sequence space, LSO provides a more tractable and interpretable framework for objective-driven protein design, particularly when integrated with pre-trained models or structure-aware latent embeddings that preserve functional constraints40.

Evolution algorithms

Evolution algorithms (EAs)41 are popular heuristic algorithms inspired by natural evolutionary processes. Beginning with a population of individuals, where each represents a potential solution within the search space of a given problem, evolution algorithms mimic the stochastic processes of mutation and/or recombination to facilitate exploration within the search space. Individuals with higher fitness values are preferentially selected in this adaptive environment.

EAs are the computational analogue of experimental directed evolution4, a powerful laboratory technique in which proteins are iteratively mutated and screened for improved function. Both share the same underlying principle: variation, selection, and amplification of advantageous traits42. In comparison to directed evolution, EAs conduct this process in silico, evaluating variants using computational models or fitness predictors (e.g., molecular dynamics simulations or machine learning-based fitness landscapes). Thus, computational EAs can complement or even accelerate directed evolution by narrowing the search space, proposing high-potential variants before experimental testing43.

Markov chain Monte Carlo

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)44 is an optimization algorithm that selects the best states when searching each branch of local intermediates randomly. It first constructs a Markov chain π(x) that has the desired posterior distribution as its equilibrium distribution. Then it generates posterior distribution samples based on the Markov chain. The most commonly used MCMC methods are Gibbs Sampling and Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm.

Recent approaches embed heuristic search mechanisms within the MCMC framework to improve convergence efficiency and adaptively adjust proposal distributions based on intermediate feedback from protein-specific scoring functions45,46. Compared with population-based methods such as EAs, MCMC offers a theoretically grounded and fine-grained probabilistic approach for navigating multimodal fitness landscapes, making it particularly suitable for refining protein sequences within energy-consistent structural constraints47–49.

Reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning (RL)50 seeks to learn an optimal policy that maximizes the reward. The RL problem is typically formalized within the Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework, which consists of states, actions, transitions, and a reward function. In RL, agents investigate their environment via a process of trial and error, learning to take actions that lead to the highest rewards. Recently, the RL-like algorithm GFlowNet51 has emerged, offering a method to learn a stochastic policy that sequentially constructs objects with probability proportional to reward, carving a niche for itself in scientific discovery52,53. Compared with heuristic approaches such as evolutionary algorithms or Markov chain Monte Carlo, RL provides a flexible framework that incorporates iterative policy improvement, thereby enabling efficient exploration of rugged fitness landscapes.

RL has been applied to protein optimization by formulating the design process as a sequential decision-making problem. In this framework, actions correspond to either substituting amino acid residues54 or appending new residues to the growing sequence55. Furthermore, RL can be flexibly used to optimize diverse protein properties in both structural56 and functional aspects57.

Bayesian optimization

Bayesian optimization (BO)58 provides a sample-efficient active learning framework for the global optimization of expensive black-box functions. During each iteration, the principal strategy is to build a probabilistic surrogate model that approximates the actual black-box functions on the observed data and then optimize an acquisition function, built upon the surrogate model, to identify informative candidates that offer the greatest utility for the next cycle of evaluations.

In the context of protein sequence design, BO frameworks iteratively propose new protein variants by balancing exploration of uncertain sequence regions and exploitation of known high-fitness candidates, typically using Gaussian process or neural-network-based surrogate models to approximate the underlying sequence-property mapping59. This surrogate function is updated through sequential experimental or computational evaluations60. BO provides a statistically principled framework with explicit uncertainty quantification, making it particularly effective for scenarios where experimental evaluations are costly and data are limited, such as enzyme engineering or stability optimization under structural constraints61,62.

Distribution Learning of Natural Sequences

Comprehending the underlying data distributions of natural protein sequences is crucial for exploring the protein landscape. Deep generative models serve as powerful tools for learning these distributions and generating novel sequences that extend beyond the currently explored regions of the protein sequence space. We delineate the models specifically developed to characterize either the local or global protein space and summarize key associated databases in section 3.3.

Local Sequence Space

Early works train generative models on multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of a specific family of interest to capture statistical patterns of amino-acid relationships and generate novel proteins belonging to that family. Representatively, the Potts model fitted with direct coupling analysis is used to design chorismate mutase enzymes63.

Beyond conventional statistical approaches, deep generative models have also been reasonably applied to capture higher-order dependencies. Pioneering advances in protein design have been achieved through various architectures, including ARMs64,65, VAEs66,67, and GANs22. Specifically, Shin et al.64 presents an ARM for the prediction and design of diverse functional protein sequences without requiring specific formatting. arDCA65 employs a shallow ARM coupled with generalized logistic regression, enabling epistatic coupling extraction and novel sequence generation. Additionally, MSA VAE66 trains VAEs on a dataset of approximately 70,000 luciferase-like oxidoreductases to generate novel, functional variants of the luxA bacterial luciferase. ProteinVAE67 proposes a VAE to generate synthetic viral vector serotypes lacking epitopes recognizable by pre-existing neutralizing antibodies. Besides, ProteinGAN22 customizes a GAN to learn the evolutionary information of protein sequences and designs highly diverse variants with natural-like physical properties.

Global Sequence Space

Protein language models

The advent of autoregressive large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-368, which can generate text with human-like capabilities, has opened new avenues in protein sequence design due to the structural parallels between natural language and protein sequences. Building on the flexible and powerful Transformer architecture, protein language models (PLMs) pre-trained on a large-scale corpus of protein sequences succeed in capturing intrinsic sequence features and generating novel protein sequences that exhibit properties analogous to those of natural proteins. ProtGPT269 is an autoregressive PLM with 738M parameters, showcasing the GPT-2 architecture’s capabilities in generating novel protein sequences de novo. It utilizes the BPE algorithm for tokenizing sequences rather than directly employing amino acids as tokens. Having been pre-trained on nearly 50M protein sequences, ProtGPT2 is capable not only of generating sequences that conform to the patterns of natural proteins but also of sampling unexplored regions of protein space. ProGen270 models are based on the GPT-J architecture and have been scaled up to 6.4B parameters, exhibiting enhanced proficiency in capturing the distribution of observed evolutionary sequences and making zero-shot fitness predictions. xTrimoPGLM71 adopts the backbone of the general language model and utilizes in-place token prediction alongside autoregressive blank infilling as training objectives. This innovative pre-training framework enables xTrimoPGLM to perform both de novo design and targeted sequence infilling.

Scaling laws for LLMs72 delineate the correlation between model performance and size, thereby providing the theoretical basis for the strategic enlargement of these models. Hesslow et al.73 present RITA, a suite of generative PLMs with up to 1.2 billion parameters, and conduct the first systematic analysis of how capabilities evolve relative to model size within the protein domain, revealing benefits associated with scaling up. More recently, xTrimoPGLM-100B and ProGen374 significantly outperform other advanced PLMs across both protein understanding and generation tasks, further emphasizing scaling effects.

Diffusion models

The emergence of diffusion models as dominant frameworks in image synthesis75–77 has attracted considerable interest in their application to protein design78, offering distinct advantages over left-to-right ARMs, including flexible iterative refinement enabled by order-agnostic decoding and the trade-off between sample quality and inference efficiency79. EvoDiff28, developed on the OADM80 framework, is the first foundation diffusion model for protein design trained on evolutionary-scale protein sequence data. Extensive experiments show that EvoDiff can generate diverse and structurally plausible proteins encompassing the breadth of natural sequence and functional spaces. Owing to the universal applicability of the sequence-based design paradigm, EvoDiff can produce proteins inaccessible to structure-based models, especially those with disordered regions, while preserving the capacity to engineer scaffolds for functional structural motifs. Besides de novo generation, DPLM81 can be adapted for conditional generation based on modalities and properties with plug-and-play classifier guidance.

Databases

Controllable protein sequence design using generative AI models heavily depends on the availability and quality of training data. Numerous open-access databases have significantly advanced model development (see Table 2). UniProt82 serves as an extensive protein repository, documenting hundreds of millions of proteins spanning all branches of life. It offers three unique databases: UniProtKB, UniRef, and UniParc. Among these, the UniRef databases, which cluster sequence sets at various levels of sequence identity, are broadly utilized in pre-training PLMs83. InterPro84 provides curated protein families, offering raw sequences for individual domains annotated with functionally important regions and conserved sites. Notably, the previously widely-used Pfam85 website features have been integrated into the InterPro database. Additionally, BFD86 represents one of the most extensive publicly accessible repositories of protein families, aggregating sequences from reference databases, metagenomic, and metatranscriptomic sources.

Although general protein databases encompass a wide range of biological information, they are insufficient for accurately modeling functionally specialized proteins. Here, we summarize datasets for several key classes of functional proteins, including peptides, antibodies, and enzymes.

Peptide

Short peptides can generally be obtained by applying length filters to comprehensive protein databases such as UniProt82. For functional peptides, such as antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), signal peptides, and immunopeptides, specialized databases are available. For instance, StarPepDB87 consolidates data from 40 bioactive peptide sources focusing on antimicrobial properties. SignalP88,89 provides a specialized database of signal peptides alongside cleavage site prediction tools, encompassing 29,000 sequences categorized into three groups. The Immune Epitope Database (IEDB)90 catalogs over 2.2 million epitopes associated with infectious, allergic, and autoimmune diseases, as well as transplant rejection. Additionally, IEDB offers computational tools for epitope prediction, visualization, and analysis.

Antibody

The Observed Antibody Space (OAS) database91 is a specialized resource designed for curating and annotating immune repertoires to facilitate large-scale analytical research. It provides detailed nucleotide and amino acid profiles for each antibody, supplemented with additional sequence annotations. OAS comprises an extensive collection of over 2.4 billion unpaired and more than 3 million paired antibody sequences.

Enzyme

The BRaunschweig ENzyme DAtabase (BRENDA)92 is the most comprehensive and widely used resource for enzyme-related information, comprising over 5 million data entries for approximately 90,000 enzymes from around 13,000 organisms. Manually curated from nearly 157,000 primary literature sources, the database is further enriched with text-mined data, integrated datasets, and predictive algorithm outputs. It offers detailed enzyme annotations, including protein sequences, EC numbers, kinetic parameters, metabolic pathways, substrate-enzyme interactions, and structural data.

Controllable Protein Sequence Design via Generative AI

Structure-to-sequence Design

The goal of structure-to-sequence design (commonly referred to as the inverse folding problem) is to generate protein sequences that fold into the given backbone structures. Typically, Protein inverse folding models consist of an encoder that represents the protein backbone and a decoder tasked with generating the protein sequence93. The backbone structure is commonly represented as a proximity graph over amino acid nodes94. According to the generative manner, modern inverse folding models are categorized into autoregressive, one-shot, and iterative models.

Autoregressive generation

Ingraham et al.93 firstly framed inverse folding as a structure-to-sequence learning problem, proposing to predict protein sequences from N to C terminus in an autoregressive manner using backbone features extracted by the structure encoder. They define a local coordinate system at each amino acid node to derive relative distance, direction, and orientation for edge features, and compute the backbone dihedral angles to constitute node features. Further, they proposed two variants, StructTrans and StructGNN, to serve as the structure encoders. Following Ingraham et al.93, a succession of studies has adopted the autoregressive framework, achieving further enhancement in performance. For instance, GVP-GNN94 replaces the standard multi-layer perceptrons in GNNs with geometric vector perceptrons, which can effectively operate on both scalar and geometric features. ESM-IF95 augments training data by incorporating 12M structures predicted by AlphaFold296, and examines three model architectures, namely GVP-GNN, GVP-GNN-large, and GVP-Transformer. The results demonstrate that larger, more complex models can effectively leverage this additional data, achieving an improvement of nearly 10 percentage points in sequence recovery for structurally held-out native backbones. Building on the foundation of StructGNN, ProteinMPNN97 verifies that incorporating distances between N, Cα, C, O, and a virtual Cβ as extra input features and employing edge updates in the encoder can lead to a marked enhancement of performance. To facilitate a wide array of applications, ProteinMPNN adopts an order-agnostic ARM that samples a random decoding order. Remarkably, ProteinMPNN has been successfully integrated into de novo protein design pipelines for real-world wet-lab experiments98, leading to the discovery of several important functional proteins99–101. Recently, DynamicMPNN102 extends inverse folding to multi-state protein design. Instead of designing sequences for a single backbone, DynamicMPNN jointly encodes multiple conformations and learns sequences that satisfy structural constraints across the entire conformational ensemble, enabling the design of proteins compatible with multiple functional states.

One-shot generation

Autoregressive models often suffer from low inference speed, prompting research into one-shot methods that enable parallel token generation. For instance, PiFold103 replaces the autoregressive decoder with stacked PiGNN layers, simultaneously enhancing quality and efficiency. This work provided the first evidence that an expressive encoder could play a more crucial role than the autoregressive decoder in inverse folding. To be precise, PiFold integrates a broader range of features, along with learnable virtual atoms designed to capture information not accounted for by real atoms, and employs PiGNN layers to effectively learn from multi-scale residue interactions by accounting for feature dependencies at the node, edge, and global levels. Advancing in this direction, VFN-IF104 introduces a more expressive encoder called Vector Field Network, which extracts hierarchical residual representations at both the real atom and frame-anchored virtual atom levels, thereby surpassing PiFold.

Iterative refinement

Another promising approach involves generating an initial sequence, then iteratively refining the generated sequence. Motivated by the insight that PLMs can learn evolutionary knowledge from the universe of natural protein sequences, LM-Design105 reprograms sequence-based PLMs, such as ESM-1b106 and ESM-227, for inverse folding. By integrating a lightweight structural adapter, LM-Design equips PLMs with structural comprehension. LM-Design undertakes training through conditional masked language modeling, which allows it to reconstruct a native sequence from a corrupted version, facilitating the iterative refinement of the predicted sequence. KWDesign107 also capitalizes on the common protein knowledge extracted from PLMs to refine low-quality residues. Specifically, KWDesign introduces a confidence-aware knowledge-tuning module that fuses multimodal pre-trained knowledge to generate more rational sequences. GRADE-IF108 controls the intermediate refinement process by applying the principles of discrete diffusion probabilistic models (D3PM)26. To constrain the generative space to a reduced subspace reflecting evolutionary pressures, the Blocks Substitution Matrix (BLOSUM) is employed as the transition matrix to guide the forward diffusion process. A primary benefit of GRADE-IF is its capacity to encompass diverse plausible solutions. Bridge-IF109 is the first diffusion bridge model to formulate iterative refinement as a Markov bridge process. Its superior performance indicates that this bridge-based formulation more effectively leverages structural priors and models the refinement process.

Datasets and Benchmarks

Conventionally, inverse folding models are benchmarked using the CATH4.2 and CATH 4.3 datasets110, with perplexity and recovery rate serving as the key evaluation metrics. Additionally, the TS50 and TS500 datasets111 are commonly used as independent test sets for evaluating the generalization ability. Advanced challenges encompass the design of multi-chain complexes, antibodies, and de novo proteins105. Recently, Gao et al.112 established ProteinInvBench, the first comprehensive benchmark for inverse folding, aiming at extending state-of-the-art models to new tasks and evaluating them based on a variety of metrics such as confidence, diversity, and sc-TM.

Function-to-sequence Design

The goal of function-to-sequence design is to generate diverse and biologically plausible proteins with desired functions. Based on the manner in which the objective is achieved, we roughly divided existing methods into three distinct categories: De novo generation, De novo optimization, and mutation-based optimization. Specifically, de novo generation involves creating novel protein sequences ab initio, typically by sampling from the distribution of natural proteins learned by generative models. This method is particularly powerful for exploring novel folds and functions absent in nature. In contrast, de novo optimization aims to discover novel sequences that maximize a specific property, such as stability, binding affinity, or expressibility. Meanwhile, mutation-based optimization enhances a fitness function through iterative modifications of a wild-type sequence. This approach is particularly effective when a seed protein with promising initial functionality is available, as it provides a solid foundation for refinement. Owing to its straightforward experimental workflow and compatibility with high-throughput screening techniques, mutation-based optimization is one of the most widely used methods in practical protein engineering. While distinct, these approaches are not mutually exclusive; a design pipeline may integrate them sequentially. For instance, de novo generation can produce initial candidates, which are then subjected to property optimization and final refinement through comprehensive mutational scans.

Note that most optimization methods can be adopted as the acquisition function optimizer in BO, enabling online model-based optimization. For the sake of conciseness, we will focus solely on the optimization mechanisms of these methods without delving into the specifics of their application in BO.

De novo generation

The most direct approach is to train generative models on known active proteins. The family-specific generative models discussed in Sec ?? exemplify this methodology. Despite that they are capable of generating functional proteins, their generalizability to sequence space outside the scope of the training family is limited. Consequently, later works tend to train a unified conditional model across diverse protein families. ProGen13 designs a decoder-only transformer for autoregressive generation and incorporates control tags (e.g., protein family, biological process, and molecular function), which are metadata prepended to the protein during training, to enable controllable protein design. MSA Transformer113 pre-trained with masked language modeling, has been leveraged to generate synthetic MSAs by performing an iterative mask-then-fill procedure on the input natural MSAs114.

De novo optimization

Another promising direction is to guide an unconditional generative model with a discriminative surrogate model to produce protein sequences that exhibit elevated levels of functionality, referred to as fitness. Given the intensive demands of laboratory experiments, surrogate sequence-function models are commonly built to act as substitutes for wet-lab validation.

RL provides a flexible framework that guides modern deep generative models to navigate the protein fitness landscape. DyNA-PPO55 is a pioneer in employing model-based policy optimization and diversity-promoting reward functions for biological sequence design. DyNA-PPO formulates the design problem as an autoregressive MDP, where each action appends an acid amino token to a partially constructed sequence. Likewise, GFlownet-AL52 strives to learn a stochastic policy that can sequentially generate sequences proportionally to a reward function, proposing a batch of diverse candidates. Fine-tuning the pre-trained PLMs with RL has the potential to enhance performance. Inspired by the achievements of ChatGPT, ProteinRL56 has emerged as the first work to design full-length proteins for specific properties by fine-tuning a generative PLM, namely ProGen2.

Diffusion models can be guided to generate continuous samples with the desired conditions, circumventing the necessity for fine-tuning by employing classifier guidance. Recent advancements have broadened this strategy to encompass general discrete diffusion models, facilitating guided protein design. NOS115 skews the categorical sampling distribution of the diffusion models by exerting gradient guidance upon the continuous representations of discrete sequences, which are expressed in the form of hidden states.

Mutation-based Optimization

Contrary to de novo optimization, mutation-based optimization focuses on improving a fitness function by modifying an existing wild type, defined as the sequence occurring in nature. In general, the wild type demonstrates decent fitness under the natural selection evolutionary process towards the target function. Traditionally, biologists have approached this problem using a model-free paradigm known as directed evolution, which involves iterative cycles of obtaining protein variants by random mutagenesis, followed by screening and selecting high-fitness variants. However, this approach is limited by its reliance on high-throughput functional assays and a greedy hill-climbing search algorithm, which can lead to substantial costs and convergence to local optima. Recent efforts have concentrated on proposing more efficient optimization methods to improve search efficiency.

In classical evolutionary algorithms, only top-fitness sequences are selected and mutated within the evolution cycle, leading to limited diversity. To alleviate this issue, AdaLead116 incorporates recombination of mutations to promote diversity and avoid local optima, establishing itself as both an accessible and robust baseline method. To leverage the natural property of the fitness landscape that a concise set of mutations upon the wild type are usually sufficient to enhance the desired function, PEX117 prioritizes the evolutionary search for high-fitness mutants with low mutation counts. Recently, the zero-shot fitness prediction capability of PLMs has been employed to guide mutations118.

Another promising approach is to leverage RL to intelligently guide the search. DES119 employs a convolutional neural network as the policy network to propose specific edits on a target sequence, and these edits are defined as a combination of the intended mutation site along with the choice of replacing amino acids. The policy network is subsequently trained by policy gradient. EvoPlay54 also mutates a single-site residue as an action to optimize protein sequences. Likening the protein optimization process to playing pieces on a chessboard, EvoPlay implements an iterative look-ahead Monte Carlo tree search procedure to generate samples for training a policy-value neural network, which in turn is then used to guide the search.

By employing gradient ascent, LSO methods can progress from wild type towards regions of higher fitness. For instance, ReLSO36 trains a transformer-based autoencoder with a fitness prediction network jointly, producing a latent space organized by sequence-function relationships. It also introduces norm-based negative sampling and an interpolative sampling penalty to regularize the latent space, further enhancing the optimization process. LaMBO59 maps discrete sequences to continuous representations using a denoising autoencoder. During the optimization phase, LaMBO follows the gradient in the latent space to reconstruct sequences that have been partially masked.

High-level constraints

Beyond explicit structural and functional constraints, tremendous knowledge exists in other formats describing proteins’ higher-level properties.

Binding targets

Target-specific protein design paves the way for the design of functional proteins that possess distinct specificity. A prime example is the engineering of antibodies tailored to bind selectively to peptide antigens. LaMBO2115 integrates BO and NOS to optimize the hu4D5 antibody for improved expression yield and binding affinity to the HER2 antigen. TCR-dWAE120 is a disentangled Wasserstein autoencoder, can isolate the function-related patterns from the rest and is utilized for engineering T-cell receptors (TCRs) to bind specifically to peptide antigens.

Text

Text-guided protein design allows practitioners to describe their demands in natural language without formal specifications. ProteinDT121 is a pioneering method that leverages textual descriptions for protein design. ProteinDT consists of three modules: ProteinCLAP that aligns the representation of two modalities, a facilitator that generates the protein representation from the text modality, and a decoder that generates the protein sequences from the representation. Building on a hierarchical training paradigm incorporating multi-modal alignment and cross-modal generation stages, ProtET122 achieves text-guided protein editing toward the optimization of desired properties. InstructProtein123 equips LLMs with the ability to comprehend protein language and further improve the performance of LLMs on protein design tasks through knowledge instruction tuning. Extracting knowledge from biomedical texts, MutaPLM124 adopts chain-of-thought to prioritize novel mutations with desirable properties.

In Silico Evaluation

Assessing the quality of samples produced by generative models presents a significant challenge. Sole reliance on statistical metrics, such as amino acid recovery and perplexity, does not accurately capture the true characteristics of designed proteins in practical applications. Moreover, wet-lab experiments are typically time-consuming and resource-intensive. In this context, in silico evaluation can pre-screen candidate proteins for structural stability125 and desired functional properties126, thereby improving the efficiency of experimental synthesis and increasing the success rate of discovering truly functional proteins127. In this section, we introduce in silico evaluation approaches for assessing protein quality from a biologically informed perspective (see Fig. 2).

In silico evaluation approaches for protein sequence design.

The evaluations commonly involve two dimensions, in terms of structural and functional perspectives. The structural evaluation involves estimating model confidence and performing sequence alignment to assess structural reliability and similarity. In contrast, the functional evaluation encompasses multiple dimensions supported by specialized tools, including physicochemical property analysis (e.g., charge distribution, surface hydrophobicity, and folding stability), protein-protein interaction prediction, and assessment of specific functionalities such as functional classification, subcellular localization, and enzymatic or binding activity.

Structural perspective

A common structural evaluation involves employing structure prediction models such as AlphaFold96,128 and ESMFold83 to predict protein structures. Alongside the predicted structures, the predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score is used to quantify structural confidence. Additionally, the agreement between native and predicted structures can be assessed using the TM-score129 to verify whether structure-based designs meet the specified structural constraints. Moreover, FoldSeek130 enables rapid and accurate protein structure similarity searches.

Functional perspective

Understanding protein function requires an examination of physicochemical properties, interaction patterns, and functional roles. Analyzing physicochemical properties is essential for evaluating designed proteins. Rosetta131 is a powerful tool for computing protein folding energy, solvent-accessible surface area, and secondary structure stability. In traditional protein sequence design, Rosetta has been extensively employed to perform in silico mutagenesis132, energy minimization133, and conformational sampling134 for identifying low-energy structures with favorable stability and functionality. In modern generative modeling, Rosetta is frequently utilized to validate or rescore designed sequences97, estimate structural stability and binding affinity135, or act as an external energy oracle that provides reward signals for reinforcement learning136. By integrating physics-based evaluation with data-driven generation, Rosetta provides a rigorous biophysical foundation that enhances both the reliability and interpretability of AI-driven protein design. Similarly, ExPASy137 provides a comprehensive suite for protein property analysis, enabling calculations of molecular weight, isoelectric point, extinction coefficient, hydrophobicity, flexibility, rigidity, and stability indices. For protein interaction studies, GROMACS138 enables efficient molecular dynamics simulations and protein-ligand interaction modeling.

Additionally, specialized computational tools are available for evaluating distinct classes of functional proteins. For AMP design, HydrAMP139 trains machine learning-based surrogate models to predict the antimicrobial potential of engineered peptides. In antibody design, SAbPred140 provides a suite of analytical tools, including complementarity-determining region modeling, antibody structure simulation, side-chain prediction, sequence numbering, and humanization assessment. NetMHC141 evaluates protein immunogenicity and their capacity to elicit immune responses. For enzyme engineering applications, CASTp142 quantifies pocket, cavity, and channel volumes, facilitating the analysis of active site geometry and surface properties.

More recently, computational benchmarking of sequences generated by deep generative models has proven effective in enhancing experimental success rates143. To be specific, Johnson et al.143 evaluated a comprehensive set of 20 diverse metrics-including sequence144, alignment145, and structure-based146 measures-to assess three distinct generative models. Collectively, these complementary layers capture multiple facets of sequence quality: sequence metrics assess intrinsic plausibility, alignment metrics reflect evolutionary validity, and structure metrics estimate folding accuracy and stability. Across three experimental rounds, the proposed computational filtering approach increased experimental success rates by 50–150%. Overall, these benchmarking strategies and evaluation metrics provide a robust foundation for advancing protein engineering by guiding the selection of promising variants for experimental validation and establishing performance standards for generative models.

Limitations and best practices in in silico evaluation

Despite their widespread use, in silico metrics should be interpreted with caution, particularly regarding the following aspects. First, commonly used structural confidence scores such as pLDDT reflect prediction reliability rather than thermodynamic stability or biological function147. Similarly, favorable Rosetta energies or docking scores do not necessarily imply experimental foldability148, binding affinity149, or catalytic activity150. Second, when generative models are optimized directly against structure predictors or energy functions, there is a risk of “gaming” the evaluator151. Iterative optimization may exploit biases or blind spots in these predictors, producing sequences with high computational scores but unrealistic biophysical properties or limited evolutionary plausibility152. Multi-metric validation and cross-model evaluation are therefore recommended. Third, benchmarking results may be confounded by data leakage153 or family-level similarity between training and evaluation sets154. Overlap in sequence families or structural folds can artificially inflate apparent performance155,156. Careful dataset curation, redundancy reduction, and transparent reporting of similarity thresholds are therefore essential. Finally, evaluation metrics should be selected according to the specific design objective. For example, structure-constrained design may emphasize fold consistency and packing quality157, enzyme engineering may prioritize active-site geometry and substrate interactions158, while peptide therapeutics often require assessment of physicochemical properties, toxicity, and immunogenicity159–161.

Applications

This section transitions from the methodological foundations of protein sequence design to its concrete application in crafting functional proteins. It provides a comprehensive overview of how computational and experimental strategies have been successfully deployed to design three major classes of therapeutically and industrially relevant proteins: peptides, antibodies, and enzymes. A summary of the corresponding methodologies is shown in Table 3.

Summary of protein design methods for applications.

Peptide

Peptides are short chains of amino acids with lengths varying from 2 to 50 units, exhibit a wide range of biological activities. Instead of merely screening existing libraries, peptides with desired properties can be rationally designed using generative models162. Current applications of such peptides span diverse functions, including cytokines with signaling163, delivery164, or immune-modulating roles165, as well as antimicrobial properties139,166–168.

As an effective solution to the global issue of bacterial resistance, the design of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) has become a major focus in both academic and industrial research. For the de novo design of AMP sequences, PepCVAE169 employs a conditional VAE guided by an AMP classifier, designing sequences through sampling and decoding from the latent space. Recent studies have also introduced PLMs into AMP sequence design. Inspired by the workflow of ChatGPT, AMP-Designer170 first pre-trains a GPT2 model on a large-scale peptide dataset and then fine-tunes it on the AMP dataset via instruction tuning, yielding a specialized model called AMP-Prompt. In addition to directly training PLMs, some approaches leverage the semantically meaningful latent spaces of pre-trained PLMs for AMP design. For instance, ProT-Diff171 and AMP-Diffusion172 apply diffusion models within the latent spaces of ProtT5 and ESM-2, respectively, combining the powerful representational power of PLMs with the generative capabilities of diffusion models. In practical applications, AMPs must often satisfy multiple criteria, including high antimicrobial activity, low toxicity, and target specificity. Consequently, there is increasing demand for computational models capable of generating AMPs with multiple desirable properties. AMPGAN23 employs a GAN conditioned on a vector encoding attributes such as target microbe, mechanism of action, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), and sequence length, enabling controllable AMP generation under diverse constraints. Similarly, Multi-CGAN173 adopts a three-stage pipeline to generate AMPs with desirable antimicrobial activity, low toxicity, and specific secondary structures. The pipeline first trains on single-attribute peptide datasets to generate sequences with individual properties, then integrates these into a multi-attribute dataset, and finally fine-tunes the model to produce peptides that simultaneously satisfy multiple desired characteristics.

For optimizing the antimicrobial activity of existing AMPs, one direct approach is to change specific positions, namely, mutation-based optimization. Yoshida et al.174 employed genetic algorithm to optimize AMP sequences, using both in silico and in vitro evaluations to iteratively select improved variants. In contrast, GFNSeqEditor175 leverages GFlowNet to identify and optimize sub-optimal positions. Another method, deepAMP176 introduces a novel framework based on the construction of optimization data pairs. A BERT model pre-trained on general peptide sequences is used to fill masked fragments in natural AMPs to degrade their antimicrobial activity. The model is then fine-tuned to learn the transformation from degraded peptides back to the original AMPs, effectively optimizing their activity. Beyond position-specific modifications, de novo optimization methods have also been explored. For instance, HydrAMP139 employs a conditional VAE framework, using latent-space classifiers to guide sampling toward more optimal peptides. Regarding multi-objective optimization, recent approaches have emerged to address trade-offs between competing properties. PepZOO177 utilizes directed evolution for multi-objective zeroth-order optimization, whereas MoFormer178 combines multiple property predictors with Pareto-based optimization to balance various requirements.

Beyond antimicrobial peptides, designing peptides with specific binding affinity toward target proteins has become an emerging focus in generative AI-driven biomolecular design. Immunoregulatory peptides participate in peptide-protein interactions that mediate antigen presentation and recognition by T cells, thereby initiating immune responses. Models such as DeepImmuno165 and GANDALF179 employ GANs to directly generate functional immunogenic peptides. HYDRA180 adopts a hybrid diffusion model that integrates the three-dimensional structures of binding proteins to design peptides with balanced stability, binding affinity, and receptor awareness. For optimization tasks, PepPPO181 applies a RL agent with mutation strategies to iteratively convert random input peptides into positively presented epitopes.

Signal peptides represent another critical class of short peptides that guide newly synthesized proteins into the secretory pathway. Wu et al.163 formulated signal peptide generation as a translation-like task and employed a Transformer-based ARM to generate functional signal peptide sequences.

Given the vast diversity of functional peptides, providing an exhaustive overview is unfeasible. For readers interested in the design of other types of peptides, please refer to Wan et al.162.

Antibody

Antibodies are large, Y-shaped immunoproteins with both conserved parts and highly variable regions, formed by a heavy and a light chain, each comprising one variable domain and several constant domains. The variable domain is partitioned into a framework region and three complementarity-determining regions (CDRs). The diversity inherent in CDRs empowers antibodies to confer immunity by specifically targeting and binding to pathogens. Thus, the majority of antibody design methods focus on redesigning or infilling CDRs while retaining the parental framework regions71,115,182,183. Concretely, LaMBO-2115 innovatively leverages diffusion sampling and ensemble-based uncertainty quantification to select edit positions based on acquisition value saliency. When applied to the real-world antibody design task, LaMBO-2 generates antibodies with a higher expression yield and binding affinity to several therapeutic targets, attaining a 99% expression rate and 40% binding rate in exploratory in vitro experiments. Drawing inspiration from natural language processing, the pre-trained foundational models have emerged as the pivotal technology to interpret the language of biology. xTrimoPGLM71, at an unprecedented scale of 100B parameters, explores the compatibility of auto-encoding and autoregressive learning paradigms. The resultant models are capable of not only generating de novo protein sequences following the principles of natural ones, but also enabling programmable redesign of antibody CDRs to optimize binding-related physicochemical properties. Furthermore, PG-AbD184 utilizes the synergy of GFlowNet51 and pre-trained PLM to significantly improve the diversity of designed antibody candidates. Guided by the reward function constructed from pre-trained PLM, GFlowNet samples positive-negative pairs to facilitate contrastive learning. Given the integral role of antibody spatial structure to directly determine biological functions, S2ALM182 holistically incorporates complementary insights from 1D sequences and 3D structures to facilitate the generation of antibody CDR subfragments. Guided by the desired antigen-binding specificity, the sequence-to-sequence framework is utilized to accomplish the antigen-conditioned de novo generation of antibody heavy chain CDRs. PALM-H3183 demonstrates effective antibody design capabilities, generating high-affinity antibodies with potent neutralization against diverse SARS-CoV-2 variants, as confirmed through in silico analysis and in vitro assays.

Recent advances have enabled full-length antibody sequence design through innovative computational approaches185,186. IgLM185 employs an infilling language modeling technique, framing antibody design as a variable-length text-infilling task. This approach facilitates either de novo generation of full-length antibody sequences or targeted redesign of specific regions. As a result, IgLM can diversify antibody loops to construct high-quality libraries with favorable biophysical properties. Building on an uncoupled score-based model for learning noisy data distributions, dWJS186 enables fast, non-autoregressive sampling with discrete outputs. When applied to de novo antibody discovery, dWJS successfully generated 270 novel antibodies, achieving a 97.47% success rate in in vitro expression and purification. Additionally, HuDiff187 proposes an adaptive diffusion model for humanizing antibodies from scratch, and produces humanized nanobodies exhibiting higher nativeness than those generated by the Lammanade pipeline.

Enzyme

Protein enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions. These substances are essential for life, performing numerous critical roles within the body, such as aiding in digestion and metabolism. For enzyme design, one may either locally modify an existing enzyme to enhance its activity54 or synthesize diverse enzymes from scratch, thereby expanding the functional enzyme sequence space13.

Biswas et al.188 proposed a paradigm for low-N protein engineering, which distills knowledge from global and local natural sequence landscapes and uses a small amount of labeled data to build an accurate fitness prediction model. The low-N paradigm, combined with MCMC sampling, enabled the discovery of over 1,000 TEM-1 β-lactamase variants with improved functionality using only 24 random mutant sequences. EvoPlay54 is an RL-based approach for protein engineering, which leverages a neural network-guided Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) to design gain-of-function mutations on a single sequence or a pool of sequences. EvoPlay enhanced the activity of Gaussia luciferase (GLuc) with a success rate exceeding 70%, reaching up to a 7.8-fold improvement. The in silico directed evolution of enzymes can also be guided by structural constraints. MutCompute189 is a 3D CNN model that maps the protein structure to amino-acid probability distribution at each position. By applying MutCompute to engineer PET hydrolases, the authors identified FAST-PETase, an enzyme with enhanced stability and efficiency under low temperatures and a range of pH conditions. Despite their demonstrated successes, these methods are limited to local modifications of existing proteins and cannot generate novel proteins beyond the known protein space. Deep generative models now provide a promising approach for de novo enzyme design.

Generative models for de novo design of enzymes can be further classified into family-specific models and general-purpose models. Russ et al.63 proposed a Potts model called bmDCA to capture the overall statistics of a protein family and successfully design diverse chorismate mutase (CM) enzymes with natural-like catalytic activity. Xie et al.190 further explored the applicability of the bmDCA model and found that the inferred statistical energy is strongly correlated with enzyme activity and stability in the active site region and the more distant region, respectively. ProteinGAN22 is a GAN-based generative model that also learns evolutionary dependencies between amino acids based on MSA, but with a black-box neural network model. ProteinGAN was applied to the Bacterial malate dehydrogenase (MDH) family and expanded its functional sequence space up to 4-fold. Leveraging the global evolutionary information learned from large-scale pre-training over 280M natural protein sequences, ProGen13 can be fine-tuned on a specific class or family of enzymes to explore functional space beyond natural proteins. Fine-tuned ProGen generated diverse lysozyme sequences with natural-like catalytic efficiencies, despite sharing as low as 31% sequence identity with known proteins. The sequence log-likelihood provided by the fine-tuned ProGen shows a strong correlation with enzyme activities, outperforming bmDCA and ProteinGAN in the case study of CM and MDH enzymes. Generative protein sequence models can create novel sequences, but predicting whether generated proteins will fold and function remains challenging. Johnson et al.143 evaluates 20 computational metrics to assess enzyme sequences generated by these models. For two enzyme families, over 500 natural and generated sequences (70–90% identity to natural ones) were experimentally tested to benchmark computational metrics for predicting in vitro enzyme activity. Over three rounds of experiments, a computational filter was developed, improving experimental success rates by 50–150%.

One limitation of these family-specific approaches is that they require sufficient homologous samples of the target function and cannot generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions. ZymCTRL191 is a conditional language model for artificial enzyme generation using the enzyme commission (EC) numbers as a prompt. By tokenizing the EC numbers by subclasses, ZymCTRL is able to transfer knowledge from populated to underrepresented classes. ZymCTRL successfully generated highly functional carbonic anhydrases and lactate dehydrogenases. Recently, ProCALM192 introduced a conditional adapter to implement parameter-efficient fine-tuning of PLMs and incorporate condition information in the continuous latent space. This approach enables ProCALM to flexibly accommodate various types of conditioning and offers enhanced potential for generating sequences under previously unseen conditions.

Given the structure (optionally including the sequence) of the functional motif, Wang et al.193 introduced constrained hallucination and inpainting approaches to design amino acid sequences that folds up to a 3D structure matching the geometry of the specified site. The functional site scaffolding algorithms have been applied to in silico design of the active sites for carbonic anhydrase II and Δ5-3-ketosteroid isomerase (KSI). To create enzymes with higher substrate specificity, Yeh et al.194 proposed RFDesign, a deep-learning-based ‘family-wide hallucination’ approach to generate novel scaffolds with native-like topology and optimize enzyme sequence according to substrate-scaffold interactions. The de-novo-designed luciferases show comparable catalytic efficiency and higher specificity to native luciferases. Unlike RFDesign, EnzyGen195 is a unified generative model that is applicable to all enzyme families. EnzyGen jointly generates the enzyme sequence and backbone structure conditioned on functionally important sites and the target substrate. GENzyme196 is a flow-based model for reaction-conditioned artificial enzyme design, which takes both substrates and products as input and sequentially generates catalytic pockets, full enzyme structures, and the enzyme-substrate binding complexes. The enzyme sequence is subsequently predicted through inverse folding of the designed backbone structure.

Challenges and Opportunities

In this section, we discuss the open challenges that we face in protein sequence design in terms of data, algorithms, and evaluation, and identify potential opportunities.

Challenges

Lack of assay-labeled data

The scarcity of assay-labeled data continues to be a primary bottleneck for reliable model training and validation. Experimental measurements of activity, stability, and binding affinity are expensive and time-consuming, limiting the availability of high-quality labeled datasets188. In low-data regimes, surrogate discriminative models often fail to provide sufficient guidance for optimization, resulting in a significant gap between computational predictions and experimental performance197. Addressing this challenge will require data-efficient learning strategies, such as few-shot learning198, active learning199, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning200.

Concurrently, there is an urgent need to develop larger, more diverse, and carefully curated datasets with high-quality annotations. In structural biology, repositories such as the Protein Data Bank (PDB) serve as foundational resources for experimentally determined protein structures201. However, beyond these foundational databases, the field requires large-scale, community-standard benchmark datasets for controllable protein design-analogous to the pivotal roles that ImageNet202 has played in computer vision and that LAION203 and WMT204 have played in multimodal and natural language processing. Establishing such datasets would enable the aggregation and harmonization of diverse protein data, including sequences, structures, and functions, into unified, machine learning-ready repositories that can accelerate the development and evaluation of generative models for controllable protein sequence design.

Lack of interpretability

Owing to the ’black box’ nature of deep learning models, which serve as the foundation for current protein sequence design approaches, there is a notable interpretability challenge. Consequently, practitioners struggle to elucidate the critical functional sites involved in the protein generation or optimization processes, nor can they provide effective remedies for failures encountered during in silico experiments. Although improving model interpretability is crucial, current research in this area remains nascent and warrants further investigation205,206. Future work should integrate explainable AI techniques207 and biologically grounded representations to elucidate functional determinants and provide actionable insights for experimentalists.

Lack of reliable evaluation protocols

Currently, the evaluation of protein sequence design models primarily depends on distribution-based computational metrics, which often lack biological relevance and robustness208. The community urgently requires standardized, biologically meaningful, and task-specific benchmarks that correlate with experimental outcomes143. Establishing such metrics is essential for assessing the practical utility of generative models and fostering reproducible scientific progress.

Opportunities

Build comprehensive benchmarks

Scientific progress can be substantially accelerated if comprehensive and universally recognized benchmarks are established. We should therefore also strive for more reliable and comparable in silico benchmarks. Within the realm of protein sequence design, researchers have recently established the first benchmark for protein inverse folding, ProteinInvBench112. However, there remains an urgent need to establish more realistic benchmarks that enable fair comparisons across diverse models designed for the broad range of protein design problems, while integrating both computational and experimental evaluation criteria.

Leverage large language models

LLMs have revolutionized natural language processing and are now beginning to transform protein science. Current research primarily focuses on developing specialized PLMs trained on large-scale protein datasets. However, effectively leveraging these models for controllable design remains an open challenge209. RL, a widely adopted approach for goal-directed fine-tuning of LLMs-as exemplified by ChatGPT210-offers a promising framework for controllable protein design. RL enables the optimization of pre-trained PLMs toward user-defined objectives while maintaining sequence naturalness211. Although the integration of RL with PLMs is still in its infancy, it holds considerable potential to guide generative models toward sequences exhibiting desired biochemical or structural properties. Moreover, inspired by advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs)212, emerging multimodal PLMs capable of jointly reasoning over sequence, structure, and textual annotations could lay the foundation for next-generation models that understand and generate proteins in a truly holistic manner213.

Explore more generative frameworks

Considering the evolutionary origins of proteins, which often involve non-sequential and multi-path exploration of structural and functional spaces, adopting non-autoregressive generative modeling approaches is crucial for protein design. Unlike ARMs that generate sequences token by token in a fixed order, non-autoregressive methods can produce entire sequences in parallel, thereby more accurately reflecting the natural, non-linear exploration of protein sequence space during evolution28. This parallel generation capability enables more efficient and flexible exploration of diverse protein configurations, making non-autoregressive architectures particularly suitable for modeling the complex, high-dimensional landscape of protein fitness.

In this context, it is insightful to explore the effectiveness of diverse non-autoregressive generative modeling approaches beyond diffusion models. Innovative frameworks including GFlowNet51 and Schrödinger Bridge109,214,215 present viable alternatives that merit further investigation within this scientific field. These methods enable flexible traversal of the sequence space and can incorporate various constraints and objectives, aligning well with the multi-modal nature of protein optimization.

A major advantage of non-autoregressive models lies in their capacity for in-place optimization of existing protein sequences. Unlike ARMs that typically generate new sequences from scratch, non-autoregressive frameworks can iteratively refine existing sequences while preserving their core structural and functional features. This property is particularly advantageous for protein engineering applications aimed at enhancing specific attributes of naturally occurring proteins-such as stability, binding affinity, or catalytic efficiency-without entirely redesigning the sequence187. By enabling efficient local search and controlled perturbation of protein sequences, non-autoregressive models provide a powerful paradigm for targeted protein optimization that closely mirrors the evolutionary process of gradual refinement.

Discussion

In this survey, we systematically reviewed the rapidly evolving landscape of generative AI technologies for controllable protein sequence design. We present typical tasks, principal methodologies, and promising future directions in the field. Specifically, Sec 2 introduces the foundational tasks of controllable protein sequence design, along with the corresponding generative models and optimization algorithms. Sec 3 summarizes the prevailing paradigms for distribution learning over natural sequence spaces and describes the associated databases. Sec 4 offers a comprehensive review of various controllable design tasks, while Sec 5 discusses in silico evaluation approaches. Sec 6 highlights representative applications. Finally, Sec 7 discusses the open challenges and potential opportunities.

Looking ahead, future breakthroughs in controllable protein design are likely to emerge from the synergistic integration of large-scale data, interpretable generative architectures, and experimental feedback loops. Realizing this vision will require a closer coupling between computational modeling and the disciplines of structural biology, biophysics, and systems biology. We hope that this survey will provide a clear picture of this rapidly evolving field, encourage cross-disciplinary partnerships, and outline a roadmap to guide both researchers and practitioners in advancing the frontier of protein design.
