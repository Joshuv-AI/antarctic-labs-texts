Selective laser etching fabrication of stacked microporous membranes for multisize particle separation in 3D microfluidics
Authors: Diego Duran-Arteaga, William Chen, Darius G Rackus, Virgilio Valente
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlass substrates are widely utilized in microfluidic applications due to their exceptional properties, including optical transparency, biocompatibility, chemical and thermal stability, and compatibility with standard microfabrication techniques. These characteristics enable high-resolution micro- and nanopatterning through methods such as wet and dry etching, laser ablation, and photolithography, facilitating the fabrication of complex and reproducible microfluidic components—such as microchannels, microchambers, micropumps, mixers, sensors, and membranes. In this study we leverage femtosecond technology to fabricate a novel multilayer microfluidic system that integrates two porous membranes, with precisely engineered pore geometries. Fabrication of the multilayer device was based on a selective laser etching process (SLE) using a glass 3D printer (LightFab GmbH, Germany), to obtain microchannels and membranes with pore sizes of 5 m and 25 m. The SLE sequence was optimized to minimize thermal ablation, preserving pore integrity and achieving high fidelity in pore size and distribution. Potassium hydroxide (KOH) was used for wet etching, leveraging the selectivity of fused silica to further refine pore geometry. A microwelding technique was optimized to achieve a consistent interlayer gap, essential for structural integrity and effective filtration. Results of filtration tests demonstrated that 30 m particles were selectively trapped in 25 m-pore membranes and 8 m particles were selectively trapped in 5 m filters, while both membranes allow the passage of 2 m particles. These results validate the ability of the system to perform size-based separation in microfluidic environments, highlighting the potential of femtosecond laser-based fabrication to produce robust, scalable, multilayer filtration devices for high throughput applications. This approach opens new avenues for developing integrated microfluidic systems capable of simultaneous filtration, separation, and analysis, paving the way for automated lab-on-a-chip applications.Subject terms: Materials for devices, Nanoscale materials, Techniques and instrumentation, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Microfluidic devices are essential tools to manipulate small volumes of fluids in channels with dimensions ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers, offering precise control over chemical and biological processes in applications such as diagnostics, drug delivery, and lab-on-a-chip systems1. Membranes play a vital role in automating sample preparation and are widely adopted for cell separation, sorting, trapping2, with more recent applications focused on accelerating the process of identifying rare cells in human blood, such as circulating tumor cells3,4. Size-based sorting is especially important when studying highly heterogeneous mixtures of cells in animal and human samples. Simple filtration can be achieved using porous membranes. Integration approaches of membrane in microfluidic devices used for applications including cell and protein research, gas detection, micro-reactors, chemical reagent and drug detection were recently reviewed5. Permeation across membrane filters is closely linked to the concentration of pores within the membrane, a characteristic known as porosity, which is defined as the ratio of the total pore volume to the total membrane volume6,7. Typical porous membranes require porosities ranging between 5 and 20 for cell culturing, 50–80% for filtration 30–70% for ultrafiltration. Biomedical applications require lower porosity ranges for tasks such as cell sorting, molecular separation, and biosensing. Fabrication of filters needs to balance porosity with mechanical strength under constant flow conditions6. The ability to fabricate high quality pores is heavily influenced by the material of the membrane, as different materials require distinct fabrication methods to achieve the desired pore structure for specific applications. Traditional fabrication techniques of porous membrane are based on silicon wafers and photolithography processes8,9. Silicon-based fabrication enables very well-defined pore structures and stress-tolerant membranes but rely on complex fabrication techniques. Soft lithography is scalable and cost-effective, replicating microstructures from a master mold using elastomeric materials. Polypropylene, for instance, can be easily heated and stretched to modify its crystalline structure, forming small pores throughout the material7. Polymers such as polysulfone (PSf), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) often employed in a process called phase separation. In such process, a polymer solution is cast into a film or heated, precipitating a solid porous membrane, known as a phase-separation membrane7,10,11. Polymers are versatile materials but have several disadvantages, including the need to fabricate a master mould, reliance on manual steps, limiting throughput and reproducibility, absorbance of different types of molecules, chemical and physical instability, as they react to organic solvents and environmental conditions such as temperature and pH. In addition, the mechanical limitations of polymers make them unsuitable for the fabrication of robust, multilayered membranes12,13.

Glass substrates have been extensively adopted in microfluidic applications, thanks to their numerous benefits, including optical transparency, biocompatibility, chemical and thermal stability, compatibility with microfabrication techniques14,15. Glass allows for high-resolution micro- and nanopatterning using techniques such as wet or dry etching, laser ablation, and photolithography, enabling complex and reproducible microfluidic components, including microchannels and microchambers, micropumps, mixers, sensors and membranes16,17. Femtosecond laser fabrication techniques have emerged over the last decades as low-cost, scalable alternatives to fabricate porous membranes and microfluidic devices on a wide range of substrates, inlcuding silicon, polymers and metals18–20. A 2022 study demonstrated the fabrication of micromembranes on both polymer and multilayer metallic substrates using a femtosecond laser setup without the need for molds or masks. Precise control over pore size (8 m and 18 m) and distribution was achieved through direct laser drilling19. Direct laser drilling can result in local heating, causing cracks and debris around the pore edges, which affect pore quality. Additionally, ablation dynamics make it difficult to achieve high aspect ratio pores. Selective laser etching (SLE) is especially favourable to achieve direct production of high quality porous membranes by separating substrate modification and material removal (etching) steps, thus significantly limiting thermal stress. In addition SLE can guarantee higher throughput since etching removes all targeted substrate sections in parallel. Selective laser etching of glass substraes was first demonstrated by Marcinkevicius and colleagues21, who developed 3D microchannels inside fused silica using a two-step process involving femtosecond-laser irradiation, followed by hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching. Subsequently, Hermans and colleagues refined the process replacing HF with potassium hydroxide (KOH) which resulted in higher selective etching rates22. SLE is currently adopted for a wide range of applications including lab-on-chip components23, through-glass vias24, ion traps25, mechanical and optical components26.

Femtosecond lasers have also been applied for microwelding. An early example of successful laser welding of silica glass was demonstrated by Tamaki et al.27 . While early femtosecond laser welding techniques required optical contact between substrates to avoid welding cracks and residual stress28,29, more recent advancements have enabled non-optical-contact microwelding, allowing precise joining even when surfaces are separated by small gaps or irregularities30,31. Femtosecond laser welding has also been investigated to bond borosilicate glass and fused silica, and fused silical and aluminum, achieving strong and precise joints between dissimilar materials27,30.

Building upon recent advances, this work demonstrates a maskless SLE-based process to integrate two stacked microporous membranes of different sizes in a multi-layer (3D) microfluidic device. The fabricated device was tested with synthetic microparticles of different sizes and demonstrated successful and consistent particle trapping and filtering. Simple filtration methods rely on single-layer membranes or basic pore structures and are often inadequate for handling the heterogeneous and dynamic nature of biological samples. Multilayered filtration systems can manage the complexity of biological media more effectively32,33. To the authors’ best knowledge, however, the integration of micropores of sizes as small as 5 m with larger microfluidic channels in multilayered devices has not been achieved to date using a single process (SLE). This capability is made possible by the unique advantages of SLE, which enables an unprecedented level of scalability and miniaturization. The fabricated device was tested with synthetic particles and demonstrated successful and consistent particle trapping and filtering.

Compared to existing microporous membrane designs based on polymers or direct laser ablation11,19, our proposed design represents a number of key advances. The adoption of glass substrates ensures superior thermal, chemical, and mechanical stability, enabling reliable operation in harsh environments where polymer membranes may deform, swell, or degrade. Furthermore, by employing SLE rather than direct ablation, we have achieved high-aspect-ratio pores with smooth, debris-free edges and minimal structural stress. In addition, the same process can be adopted for bonding multiple glass layers via laser microwelding, enabling the seamless integration of complex three-dimensional microfluidic architectures without adhesives or intermediate bonding agents. This capability not only preserves the device’s thermal and chemical resilience but also allows precise alignment of functional layers, including stacked membranes, channels, and sensing elements. The SLE process has been optimized for the fabrication of 3D glass microfluidics with microchannels34. However, its application to fabricate high-density, stacked microporous membranes integrated within three-dimensional microfluidic architectures has, to the authors’ knowledge, not yet been demonstrated. The unique combination of selective laser etching for pore formation and laser microwelding for layer integration, shown in this work, enables unprecedented design flexibility and functional density in glass-based microfluidic systems. This study contributes to the advancement of precision microfabrication techniques for complex microfluidic devices, enabling enhanced filtration and particle separation applications. The demonstrated approach provides a robust framework for integrating multilayer architectures with diverse functionalities thus benefiting the fields of biomedical diagnostics.

Methods

CAD model

A computer automated design (CAD Rhino 5, https://lightfab.de/) model was used to specify the regions of the substrate to be removed. Based on the CAD model, a series of planar cross-sections, or slices, are generated to define the three-dimensional geometry of the structure along the z-axis. These slices establish the spatial boundaries and edges of the target volume to be etched. Additionally, hatches are used within each slice to determine the laser scanning paths for efficient and uniform material removal. This structured approach enables precise control over the microfabrication process. Figure 1 shows the CAD model used for the pore design of the device, based on slices and hatches. The size of the hatches can be defined as ’cubes’ to achieve the required pore resolution,(e.g. 25 25 25 m hatch ’cubes’ at and for 25 m pores and and 5 5 5 m cubes for 5 m pores).

Materials

Double-sided polished 100-mm, 500 m-thick fused silica wafers were obtained from UniversityWafer (Boston, MA, USA). SLE processes were performed with a LightFab 3D printer (LightFab, Germany). Polystyrene particles 50 m in diameter and Poly(methylmethacrylate) (PMMA) particles 8 and 2-m in diameter were used to perform fluidic experiments. Fabrication and characterization was performed at the Centre for Research and Applications in Fluidic Technologies (CRAFT) at the University of Toronto (Toronto, Canada). Fluidic experiments were performed with a Pump 11 Elite Syringe Pump (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA) and an Axio Observer D1 microscope (Zeiss, Germany) with a phantom high-speed camera (Ametek, USA). Image processing and pore analysis was performed with ImageJ software (Fiji, USA).

A high value () corresponds to MPI, where multiple photons combine to ionize the material, while a low value () signifies tunneling ionization, where the laser field distorts the Coulomb barrier sufficiently for electrons to tunnel through it35. The nonlinear effects of photon absorption create an interaction area known as the voxel volume. Due to the short pulse duration, the voxel volume is inherently small, allowing for precise modifications. However, if excessive photons are absorbed, the material may surpass its damage threshold, leading to cracks, ruptures, or unwanted expansion of the voxel volume36–38. Pore size estimation

The pore sizes were estimated using a digital microscope (VHX-7100, Keyence, USA). Pore diameters were measured from a representative sample, and the microscope automated measurement tool was employed to count all pores across both membranes. The measured pore data were extracted into a CSV file for further analysis, and the results were processed to generate histograms illustrating the distribution of pore sizes using Python.

Laser writing

To elicit SLE on the fused silica substrate, the frequency of the pulse was set to 1507 Hz with a divider of 2 and the pulse width was set to 1 ps . A power level measurement was conducted to determine the power percentage corresponding to 350 nJ. During the SLE process, the corresponding power percentage fluctuated between 25 and 28% of the laser power. The final parameter set for the print is the mark speed of the laser for both the slices and hatches of the design. The mark speed was set to 200 mm/s. The prepared CAD design was then loaded to the LightFab software, where a coordinate system is referenced and calculated based on the dimensions of the design and desired location for 0,0,0. The laser was then focused in two stages, first through a coarse focus which is done manually where the top surface of the substrate is located within 100 m of the true surface. The second stage then requires the use of an autofocus program where the laser then focuses on the true surface of the substrate. Prints of channels, filters and outlets were done in multiple stages to avoid cracks by over exposure and to ensure greater details of design features. The 25m-pore membranes were fabricated using a two-step process: The process involved printing 25 m diameter slices at 5-m intervals in the z-direction, resulting in precise pore depths within the 50-m-thick membrane. Observations revealed that starting the printing from the top surface and progressing downward in 7.5-m steps ensured complete penetration of the membrane, effectively accounting for the laser 3-m voxel size in the z-direction. The second printing step, which introduced a single hatch across the center of each slice, further refined the pore structure. Two alternative processes were used to fabricate the 5 m-pore membranes. The first method involved printing the pores similarly to the 25-m design, followed by 12 h of KOH etching. The second method followed a similar approach but reduced the initial etching time to 6–8 h. This allowed the pores to extend about halfway through the membrane thickness. The pores were then reprinted on the opposite side and etched for another 6–8 h.

Etching

The printed features were etched in potassium hydroxide (KOH), at C. The etching rate of the exposed fused silica glass is 60–80 m per hour in all directions. To reduce the etching time, specific techniques were employed during the printing process, such as the use of “ice cube” hatches. These hatches, measuring 25 25 25 m, covered a larger area of the substrate compared to standard hatches. As KOH permeated the perimeter of each ice cube, larger sections of the substrate were efficiently etched away. Additionally, standard hatches were printed on the bottom surfaces of walls, channels, and other features to ensure that, as KOH reached these layers, the substrate was cleanly etched across the defined surface, removing any residual ice cubes. This combined approach resulted in a total etching time of 10 hours for the membranes, channels, and outlets, with an additional 8 hours required for fabricating the pores.

Substrate bonding and microwelding

The LightFab system was also used to weld the four layers together. The pulse frequency was set to 2034.5 Hz with a divider of 1, and the power required to weld fused silica was 1400 nJ, corresponding to a power percentage fluctuating between 50 and 70%. The mark speed was set at 15 mm/s. For a successful weld and a tight seal, the laser must be precisely focused at the junction between the two layers, with a gap of less than 3 m. To achieve this, the laser was focused on the top surface of the bottom layer, then lowered by 50 m in the z-direction to ensure that the heat-affected zone covered 50 m on each layer. The LightFab system includes a gap calculation tool that measures the distance between layers. This tool takes baseline surface measurements and then measures the gap at the junction between the two layers. To maintain a tight seal, through-holes were distributed from the bottom layer to the third layer, creating a suction effect through the vacuum on the LightFab stage. However, as the suction force diminishes with each additional layer, it becomes challenging to maintain the required 3-m gap between all layers. To address this issue, the layers underwent a series of cleaning, activation, and conditioning steps. First, the samples were cleaned with soap to remove any residual stains from the etching process, along with fingerprints or dust particles. They were then dried with a nitrogen gun to remove all water particles. Next, the samples were cleaned with ethanol to remove any oils, followed by another nitrogen drying. After cleaning, the bonding surfaces were activated using a plasma treatment with a plasma wand, with each surface treated for up to a minute to ensure activation. Once activated, a thin layer of deionized water was applied to one of the surfaces, just enough to cover it without saturation. The non-wet sample was then carefully slid over the wet sample and pressed firmly to remove excess water and establish contact. The samples were placed in a warming chamber at C for 15–20 min to evaporate remaining moisture. After warming, they were placed on the LightFab stage and positioned with the machine vacuum for welding. Welding began at the outer edges of the samples and moved inward to create a tight seal around the channels and filters of the device. This process was repeated to bond all four layers together, ensuring a strong, reliable bond for the assembled structure.

Both 5 m and 25 m-pore filters were tested with a solution containing 30 m polystyrene particles and 8 and 2 m PMMA particles, applied using a dropper. Results

SLE-fabricated microfluidic device

Figure 2a shows a schematic diagram of the 4-layer microfluidic devices. The SLE-fabricated individual layers and fully bonded microfluidic device are shown in Figure 2b,c. The overall dimensions of the device are 40 mm by 25 mm with a thickness of 2 mm. Length and width of the microchannels in layers 1 and 3 are 5.06 mm by 0.5 mm and 7.06 mm by 0.5 mm, respectively.

25-m-pore membrane

The 25 m-pore membranes demonstrated consistent pore sizes with minimal variability, indicating the effectiveness of the two-step printing process. Observations revealed that starting the printing from the top surface and progressing downward in 7.5 m steps ensured complete penetration of the membrane, effectively accounting for the laser 3 m voxel size in the z-direction. The second printing step, which introduced a single hatch across the center of each slice, further refined the pore structure. Laser exposure is guided by a CAD mask, which defines multiple regions divided into hatches (lateral areas across the membrane) and slices (axial divisions through the membrane). Due to the programming of the LightFab system, the laser consistently returns to the origin of each hatch after every cycle. This repeated repositioning leads to localized heat accumulation at those points, resulting in the ablated areas shown in Fig. 3a. By splitting the printing process into two steps (hatches defining channels and membrane in the first step and slices defining pores in the second step), laser heat is more efficiently distributed across the different structures, thus preventing ablation. Results showed that implementing this two-step approach mitigated thermal ablation, as demonstrated in Fig. 3b.

Membrane porosity

The membrane with 25-m pores (layer 2) had a total of 560 pores with a pitch of 75 m across the 2-mm diameter surface. The resulting porosity was calculated to be 0.0876. The membrane with 5-m pores (layer 3), had a total of 4955 pores with a pitch of 50 m across the 2 mm diameter surface. The resulting porosity was calculated as 0.0309. The calculations of porosity assume the pores have a uniform shape across the height of the membrane.

Maximum stress tolerance

The estimated maximal stress tolerance for each membrane was determined by calculating an estimated drag pressure. For the first membrane, the estimated drag pressure was calculated as 10.33 Pa, for an associated maximal stress tolerance of 7.18 MPa. For the second membrane, the estimated drag pressure was calculated as 82.58 Pa, for an associated maximal stress tolerance of 57.47 MPa. The higher drag pressure for the second membrane is in line with the smaller porosity, suggesting that the force per unit area exerted by a fluid flow on the membrane will be higher as the fluid has a smaller area to flow through, meaning there will be a larger resistance to flow. Both membranes exhibit a drag pressure that are lower than their maximum stress tolerances. The welding strength was tested during microfluidic experiments under different flow conditions, resulting in the device withstanding flows up to 75 l/min.

Discussion

This study presents the design, fabrication and experimental validation of a novel multilayer microfluidic device with integrated microfilters, capable of precise separation of particles of three different sizes. Femtosecond laser technology was leveraged to achieve precise and consistent selective laser etching and welding of microchannels and microfilters. We developed an optimized process to minimize the occurrence of membrane cracking, enhance flow capacity and facilitate efficient bonding. Thermal ablation during SLE process emerges as a challenge in achieving consistent pore geometry and structural fidelity. Splashing is most pronounced during the fabrication of smaller features, such as the 5-m pores, where localized energy concentrations creates unintended material displacement. This effect not only distorts the edges of etched features but also introduces variability in pore size and shape, potentially impacting filtration efficiency. To address this issue, staged printing was implemented, whereby larger structures, such as membranes, are fabricated before introducing smaller, more delicate features like pores. By reducing continuous exposure in these areas, thermal accumulation is minimized, leading to more precise etching. Variability in KOH etching rates poses a challenge, particularly for smaller pores. Anisotropic etching results in slight asymmetries at both ends of the pores. These discrepancies are largely influenced by temperature control and etchant saturation. When samples are deposited into the KOH bath at C, etching commences at the top surface of the pore and continues as the etchant reaches the bottom of the sample. The process results in conical shapes that would be exacerbated when the samples are cleaned and inspected. To mitigate this issue, the sample can be deposited into the KOH bath at room temperature to ensure it reaches the small spaces before etching the top surfaces. Additionally, the power of the KOH diminishes with prolonged use, therefore it is essential to ensure the quality of the etchant is consistent especially when planning the etching time. For the 25-m membrane, the majority of the pores falls within the desired range of 20–30 m, with the peak frequency centered close to the target diameter of 25 m. This consistency demonstrates the effectiveness of the controlled printing parameters in producing uniform pore sizes, which is critical for ensuring the membrane filtration performance. For the 5-m membrane, pore size distribution indicates that the majority of the pores are closely centered around the target diameter, with some variability extending up to 8 m. This consistency highlights the effectiveness of the two-step etching process in achieving the desired pore dimensions while maintaining a narrow size distribution. However, the slightly larger mean diameter and right-skewed distribution suggest a minor tendency for over-etching during fabrication. These results, combined with the observed geometric tortuosity of the pores, confirm the precision of the each method in producing the pores of the desired range. Further optimization of etching parameters could help tighten the distribution further and improve alignment with the target diameter.

Profile analysis of the 25 m pores shows geometric tortuosity levels close to 1, representing quasi-vertical walls through the glass substrate. The 5 m pores have tortuosity levels of approximately 1.5 which is suboptimal and represents a 45-degree angle (conical shapes). Methods to achieve lower tortuosities for smaller pores (i.e. straighter walls) and lower roughness levels using femtosecond laser processes are currently under investigation in the authors’ labs. Conical pores of sizes of 5 m and constant angles of have been fabricated by assisted molding of UV curable polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA)11. In addition to the need to fabricate master molds, membrane stacking and integration with microfluidics was not demonstrated. MEMS methods (e.g. Deep reactive ion etching) can usually adopted to achieve straight walls but they require several fabrication steps and result in very costly and time consuming processes.

A range of techniques to improve pore quality, especially in the case of smaller pores, are under investigation, including (1) laser parameter tuning, based on repeated laser dosing at lower pulse energy and adjusting laser timing to obtain more uniform modification, and (2) potential pre-treatment strategies to optimize surface chemistry and wetting conditions in the KOH process, for more uniform etches. This approach would not greatly alter the 2-step SLE process and would still ensure high-throughput without post-processing steps. As an alternative or supplementary strategy, micro-polishing, including chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP) or electropolishing42, may further improve pore quality after fabrication, but the addition of processing steps adds overall fabrication time and cost.

The bonding process underwent two major iterations. The first iteration involved welding layer 1 with layer 2, and layer 3 with layer 4, followed by welding the two combined substrates. This method failed due to insufficient vacuum force, resulting in excessive gaps between layers. Furthermore, this method resulted in poor laser focus on the junctions between the combined substrates. In the second iteration, based on an optimized bonding process detailed in the Methods sections, layer gaps were maintained below 3 m with the four layers welded sequentially, which resulted in robust interlayer bonding and minimizing defects. This process still presents a number of limitations, related to challenges in ensuring small gaps between the layers using the LightFab tool, as shown by the presence of Newton rings in Fig. 8(b). Future work will investigate the adaptation of non contact welding methods recently introduced by Jia et al. and Chen et al.31,43 to our process.

Our presented method did not result in any observable leakage at the selected experimental flow rates up to 75 L/min. In addition, thanks to the large number of integrated pores, the filtering performance of the membranes did not degrade during the experimental measurements, as no significant change in flow rate was observed. Long-term experiments with continuous flow of particles of different sizes, however, are expected to result in large agglomerations at the membranes, altering their permeability, inducing biofouling and hence hindering filtering performance44,45. Periodic backwashing and forward flushing can be adopted as mitigation strategies in the experimental protocol to maintain consistent filtration performance, improve calibration and mitigate experimental bias46,47. Our fabricated membranes were characterized by low porosity, which resulted from a conservative approach in selecting pore-to-pore distance to prevent inter-pore cracks during etching. Future work will focus on exploring and optimizing tradeoffs between membrane porosity and structural integrity using SLE processes.

The proposed device represents a leap forward in size-based particle separation and sorting, and can be easily customized for particle sizes in the range between 2 and 50 m. Compared to alternative active separation methods based of centrifugation, dielectrophoresis (DEP) and acoustofluidics (AF), microfilters fabricated using SLE processes offer more precise and scalable solution than centriguration, without the need for complex integration of electrodes and transducers required in DEP and AF. Additionally, multilayer filters offer medium to high throughput, with the potential for efficient scalability for parallel processing.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates the successful application of femtosecond laser technology and selective laser etching in the fabrication of multilayer microfilters. By integrating 25-m and 5-m-pore membranes into a single microfluidic system, this work achieved efficient particle filtration and separation, validated through systematic filtration experiments. Results underscore the potential of this technique to address challenges in microfluidic environments, offering precise control over pore size and geometry, high fidelity, and robust performance under various flow conditions. These advancements pave the way for the scalable and reproducible development of more complex and integrated microfluidic systems that combine filtration, separation, and analysis in a single device.
