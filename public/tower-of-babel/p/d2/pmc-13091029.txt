Open dataset of femoral cross-sectional geometry across ontogeny, including high resolution CT-derived structural variables, estimated ages, and proteomic sex identification
Authors: Julia Muñoz-Guarinos, Laura Rodríguez, José Miguel Carretero, Miguel del Nogal Sánchez, Ana María Casas-Ferreira, José Luis Pérez Pavón, Rebeca García-González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ontogenetic changes in long bone structure provide critical insights into the interplay between growth, mechanical loading, and skeletal development, yet open-access datasets focusing on non-adult individuals remain limited. This study presents an open dataset of femoral cross-sectional geometric properties spanning infancy to adolescence, derived from high-resolution computed tomography (CT) scans of archaeological human remains from the San Pablo Convent (Burgos, Spain). The dataset includes 72 unfused femora representing a continuous developmental sequence from 2.00 to 16.18 years of age.Cross-sectional geometric variables were extracted at four standardized and biomechanically meaningful locations along the femur: distal diaphysis, midshaft, proximal diaphysis, and femoral midneck. For each section, the dataset provides measurements of cortical and medullary areas, area ratios, second moments of area, and torsional rigidity, enabling detailed assessments of structural strength and biomechanical adaptation during growth. Age at death was estimated primarily from dental development, with complementary femoral-length-based regressions when dentition was unavailable. A key feature of this dataset is the inclusion of biological sex estimation based on amelogenin peptide identification from tooth enamel using a minimally invasive proteomic approach, allowing the investigation of sexual dimorphism prior to skeletal maturity. This resource supports reproducible research in bioarchaeology, biomechanics, and developmental skeletal biology.

Value of the Data

These data offer a unique resource for studying ontogenetic changes in femoral structure and biomechanics.•Reliability: sex estimation is provided through amelogenin peptide identification using proteomic analysis, a highly accurate and minimally invasive method that increases reliability in immature individuals for whom morphological sexing is not feasible. Background

Ontogenetic variation in long bone structure provides critical insights into how growth, mechanical loading, and functional demands shape the human skeleton [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. As the primary weight-bearing bone of the lower limb, the femur is particularly informative for biomechanical and bioarchaeological investigations of locomotor behaviour, activity patterns, and structural adaptation across the life course [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Cross-sectional geometric properties provide a quantitative framework for assessing these processes; however, most available datasets focus on adult individuals. Ontogenetic series spanning multiple developmental stages are therefore essential for contextualizing adult bone structure and interpreting biomechanical signals in archaeological populations [6]. Despite this need, standardized datasets documenting femoral cross-sectional geometry across ontogeny remain scarce.

The dataset presented here addresses this gap by providing CT-derived femoral cross-sectional measurements at four anatomically and biomechanically meaningful locations along the diaphysis and femoral midneck. Covering a continuous ontogenetic sequence, these data enable investigation of how femoral structural properties emerge, reorganize, and scale during growth, as well as how mechanical demands are reflected in bone geometry prior to skeletal maturity. By making this dataset openly available, this resource supports biomechanical modelling, comparative analyses of growth trajectories, and the integration of developmental perspectives into bioarchaeological research.

Data Description

The dataset comprises CT-derived cross-sectional geometric measurements from human femora recovered at the archaeological site of San Pablo Convent (Burgos, Spain) [7]. The San Pablo skeletal collection dates to the medieval and modern periods (13th–18th centuries), comprises 433 individuals, and is currently housed at the Laboratory of Human Evolution, University of Burgos [7]. The assemblage represents an ontogenetic series spanning infancy to young adulthood and includes only unfused femora. A total of 72 individuals is included in the dataset, each represented by a unique identifier. All individuals were selected based on the preservation of at least one femur suitable for cross-sectional analysis. When available, dentition associated with the skeletal remains was used to complement individual-level metadata, including biological sex estimated through amelogenin peptide analysis.

The dataset is provided as a single spreadsheet file (.xlsx) composed of four worksheets corresponding to different femoral locations: Midneck, Subtrochanteric, Midshaft, and Distal femur (Fig. 1). Each worksheet contains cross-sectional geometric data extracted from the specified anatomical level. Each row represents a single femoral cross section derived from a CT scan. All worksheets share an identical column structure, combining individual-level metadata with quantitative cross-sectional geometric variables. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

CSP extraction and acquisition

Computed tomographic (CT) scans for all specimens were acquired using a YXLON Compact X-Ray industrial multi-slice CT scanner housed at the University of Burgos. Specimens were positioned with the femoral diaphysis aligned to the scanner’s longitudinal axis and the proximal epiphysis oriented toward the gantry. Scanning parameters were 160 kV and 4 mA, with a slice thickness and inter-slice spacing of 0.5 mm, a reconstruction interval of 0.5 mm, and an 18.52-cm field of view. CT datasets were visualized in Slicer 5.6.2.

Because femoral size and shape change substantially during ontogeny, we adopted a landmark-based protocol to minimize intraobserver and interobserver error [8]. Because all individuals in this dataset present unfused epiphyses, percentage-based cross-section locations along the femoral shaft were referenced to dry-bone diaphyseal length, measured excluding epiphyses (Fig. 1). To maintain comparability with adult conventions based on biomechanical length, diaphyseal sections in unfused femora were extracted at 15 % (distal diaphysis), 45 % (midshaft), and 75 % (proximal diaphysis) of intermetaphyseal length, corresponding approximately to the standard adult locations at 20 %, 50 %, and 80 % of biomechanical length [9]. This adjustment accounts for the substantial distal epiphyseal contribution to overall femoral length in fused individuals.

Femoral midneck cross sections were extracted after reorienting each femur along its own neck axis [10,11] in Slicer 5.6.2 (Fig. 2). To define this axis in 3D, two anatomical points were identified on each specimen: (i) the midpoint of the vertical diameter of the femoral neck, placed at the narrowest part of the neck as the midpoint between the superior and inferior neck margins (i.e., the midpoint of the neck’s superior–inferior diameter), and (ii) the midpoint between the superior and inferior margins of the maximum superior–inferior diameter of that metaphyseal surface. The straight line connecting these two points defined the specimen-specific neck axis. Each femur was then rigidly rotated so that its neck axis coincided with a reference axis of the working coordinate system (i.e., the specimen was reoriented to place the neck axis in a standardized direction), and the alignment was visually checked in orthogonal views to ensure that the axis passed through the intended neck and metaphyseal landmarks. Neck length was subsequently measured along this neck axis, from the proximal head/neck boundary to the intertrochanteric ridge. The midneck level was defined as the point located at 50 % of this neck length (half the distance between the metaphyseal surface of the head and the intertrochanteric ridge measured along the neck axis). Extracted cross sections were analyzed in ImageJ using the EPMacroJ plugin [12]. Periosteal contours were obtained automatically, whereas endosteal contours were digitized manually [10]. Brightness and contrast were optimized automatically to improve delineation of cortical boundaries and to standardize grayscale values between air and cortical bone. Manual landmark spacing for endosteal tracing ranged from 4 to 9 pixels, depending on section resolution. Trabecular bone frequently obscured endosteal margins, particularly at the distal femur and femoral midneck, necessitating digital removal of trabecular struts following established procedures [13]. While the inferior neck generally presents a well-defined cortical margin, the superior aspect shows reduced cortical clarity in both adults and non-adults. To ensure continuity of the cortical shell in midneck sections, a minimum one-pixel cortical layer was enforced following previously described methods [14].

Measurement error

To facilitate downstream reuse of the dataset, we quantified intra-observer repeatability. This was assessed by repeating the complete workflow on a random 30 % subset of the total sample (n = 22), reprocessed in two independent sessions by the same observer. Specimens were selected randomly within each age group to ensure adequate representation of the ontogenetic range. Repeatability analyses were restricted to the raw geometric properties (TA, CA, MA, Ix, Iy, Imax, Imin and Theta); ratios and other derived variables (such as %CA or J) were not tested because they can be computed directly from these primary measures. Age estimation

Age at death was estimated from the calcification and formation of dental crowns and roots. Mineralization stages for each tooth class were assessed through 3D volume renderings derived from CT scans. Developmental stages of the permanent and deciduous dentition were scored following established standards and converted into chronological age using adjusted prediction models [15,16,17]. When dentition was not available for the femoral remains, or only one tooth was available, age at death was inferred through predictive regression techniques. To this end, femoral diaphyseal length (FDL) was modelled against dental age, and both variables were log-transformed to mitigate the effects of accelerated diaphyseal growth during ontogeny. Using this approach, age was estimated from dentition in 57 individuals and from femoral length in 15 cases. The sample spans from 2.00 to 16.18 years.

Sex estimation

Sex determination has recently been performed using the sexually dimorphic amelogenin peptides preserved in human tooth enamel, a method that has demonstrated 100 % accuracy [18,19]. This approach relies on the nano–liquid chromatography–high resolution mass spectrometry analysis of two enamel peptides: AMELX-(44–52), with the amino acid sequence SIRPPYPSY, and AMELY-(58–64), with the sequence SM(ox)IRPPY. Biological sex is inferred from peptide presence or absence: individuals exhibiting both AMELX and AMELY are classified as male, whereas those lacking the AMELY isoform are classified as female.

In this work, sex determination was conducted using a modified version of established protocols [18,19], with methodological adaptations described elsewhere [20]. This modification eliminates the chromatographic separation step, reducing total analysis time from >60 min to 2.1 min per sample. In addition, a low-resolution mass spectrometer was employed instead of a high-resolution instrument, substantially decreasing analytical costs and enabling wider laboratory implementation. Given the low peptide signal observed in our samples, we also modified the sample preparation workflow by applying the acid etching step to the entire tooth surface. In summary, each tooth was first rinsed with 3 % (v/v) hydrogen peroxide and ultrapure water. The specimen was then immersed in 300 μL of 5 % (v/v) HCl for a 2-minute etching step, which was discarded. A second etch was performed by placing the tooth into a fresh 300 μL aliquot of HCl for 5 min; this solution was retained as the working extract.

Peptides were concentrated and desalted from the working extract using Zip-Tip® pipette tips containing 0.6 μL of C18 reversed-phase sorbent (Merck Millipore, Ireland). The sorbent was conditioned with three 10 μL cycles of acetonitrile followed by three 10 μL cycles of 0.1 % (v/v) aqueous formic acid. Peptide binding was achieved by aspirating and dispensing 25 cycles of 10 μL of the working extract through the tip. The sorbent was then washed with six cycles of 10 μL of 0.1 % formic acid, and peptides were eluted using 8 μL of a 60:40 (v/v) acetonitrile–water mixture containing 0.1 % formic acid. Eluates were dried under nitrogen and reconstituted in 12 μL of 0.1 % (v/v) aqueous trifluoroacetic acid prior to analysis. Samples were analyzed on a Waters Acquity UPLC system (Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a Xevo TQS mass spectrometer. No chromatographic column was included. The carrier phase consisted of a 90:10 (v/v) mixture of ultrapure water and acetonitrile with 0.1 % formic acid, delivered at 0.1 mL/min. Injection volume was 5 μL, and total run time was 1.0 min (2.1 min including injection). AMELX and AMELY were quantified using Selected Reaction Monitoring (SRM), acquiring three precursor–product ion transitions per peptide: 540.3→879.5, 714.4, and 366.2 m/z for AMELX, and 440.2→645.4, 532.3, and 279.1 m/z for AMELY.

Limitations

Because age at death was estimated using either dental or femoral development, the differing error margins of these two methods may have influenced our results. Dental development is generally less sensitive to environmental factors than skeletal growth, which means that discrepancies between the two indicators are expected. In individuals for whom age was estimated from femoral development (due to the absence of teeth) delayed puberty could have slowed femoral growth, leading to an underestimation of their true age.

Ethics Statement

The authors have read and followed the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief and confirm that the present work does not involve living human subjects, animal experiments, or any data collected from social media platforms. The study is based exclusively on archaeological human skeletal remains preserved at the Laboratory of Human Evolution (University of Burgos). No destructive analyses were performed. The CT data were acquired and processed using non-invasive methods for documentation, research, and educational purposes only. All the data contained therein are anonymous in accordance with Spanish Law (Act 14/2007, of July 3rd, Chapter III, Article 20, on Biomedical Research).
