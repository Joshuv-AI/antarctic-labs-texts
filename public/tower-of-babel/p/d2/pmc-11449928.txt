Causal effects of plasma metabolites on autoimmune hepatitis (AIH): a bidirectional two-sample mendelian randomization study
Authors: Zhen Zheng, Dahua Chen, Jiaming Lv, Juan Du, Kaitai Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune hepatitis(AIH) is a chronic progressive inflammatory liver disease induced by loss of immune tolerance. The role of circulating metabolites in disease pathogenesis is unclear. This study aimed to investigate potential causal links between plasma metabolites and AIH risk by employing a two-sample Mendelian randomization approach. A comprehensive bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis was conducted using genome-wide significant variant-metabolite and variant-AIH associations in European ancestry individuals. Various methods assessed causal relationships among 1400 metabolites and AIH, incorporating sensitivity analyses to evaluate pleiotropy and heterogeneity. Fifty-eight metabolites displayed possible associations, including increased AIH risk with genetically predicted higher kynurenine (p = 2.79 × 10− 5, OR: 1.64, 95% CI 1.30–2.07) and a protective effect for the dopamine sulfate ratio (p = 1.06 × 10− 5,OR: 0.62, 95% CI 0.49–0.79). Reciprocal analysis revealed a causal effect of AIH on kynurenine( p = 2.79 × 10− 5, OR: 1.64, 95% CI 1.30–2.07), but not on the dopamine sulfate ratio(p = 0.691, OR: 1.05, 95% CI 0.67–1.64). Our genetics-based approach provides evidence supporting a causal role for specific metabolite levels in AIH risk. The results deliver evidence supporting a causal effect of a specific metabolite ratio(dopamine 4-sulfate/dopamine 3-O-sulfate) on AIH risk. Introduction

Autoimmune hepatitis(AIH) is a chronic progressive liver disease caused by the immune system attacking self liver cells, characterized by elevated serum transaminases, positive autoantibodies, and hypergammaglobulinemia. Pathologically it presents as interface hepatitis, lymphocytic and plasmacytic infiltration in portal tracts, and rosette formation of hepatocytes1. The incidence is around 1–2 per 100,000 persons in European and American populations, and prevalence is about 10–20 per 100,000 persons2. Epidemiological data in Asian populations is limited, but some studies indicate slightly higher morbidity and poorer prognosis compared to Europeans and Americans3. Without effective treatment, it can gradually progress to cirrhosis, eventually leading to decompensated liver function and death or requiring liver transplantation. Currently, immunosuppressive therapy has shown some efficacy in halting disease progression4,5. Based on advances in serum immunology, AIH is currently categorized into two types: type 1 AIH (AIH.1), the most common form, is associated with antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-smooth muscle antibody (SMA), anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (p-ANCA) and/or anti-soluble liver antigen/liver pancreas antibody (anti-SLA/LP); type 2 AIH (AIH.2) features anti-liver/kidney microsomal antibody 1(anti-LKM1)6. The etiology and pathogenesis of both AIH types remains unclear and is still a research hotspot. Currently, substantial evidence indicates it is caused by genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers leading to loss of self-tolerance, which induces an immune response dominated by T lymphocytes against liver cell antigens, ultimately resulting in liver tissue damage7,8. Among the recently characterized regulatory T cell subsets, including natural killer T (NKT) cells, T-helper 3 (Th3) cells, T-regulatory 1 (Tr1) cells, CD8 + CD28- T cells, and γδT-cells, CD4 + T-cells that constitutively express the α chain of the interleukin-2 receptor (IL-2R) (CD25) have emerged as a predominant subset that is critical for the maintenance of immune tolerance9. These CD4 + CD25 + regulatory T-cells (Tregs), comprising 5–10% of circulating CD4 T-cells in healthy individuals, modulate innate and adaptive immune responses by inhibiting the proliferation and effector functions of autoreactive T-cells. Studies conducted primarily in vitro indicate that Tregs can suppress target cells directly or indirectly through antigen presenting cells10.

Metabolites present within the local tumor microenvironment or circulating systemically in plasma play multifunctional roles. In recent years, some studies have reported associations between various metabolites and the pathogenesis of certain autoimmune diseases11–14. However, research examining links with AIH has been scarce. Nelson et al. demonstrated that a genetically-supported metabolite-disease association doubled the likelihood of eventual market approval for a drug targeting that metabolite15. Over the past decades, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have successfully identified multiple genetic factors associated with AIH risk16. Meanwhile, advancements in mass spectrometry now enable GWAS to uncover genetic determinants regulating broader aspects of the metabolome15,17,18. Repurposing these rich GWAS datasets presents an opportunity to explore potential causal relationships between plasma metabolites and AIH.

Mendelian randomization (MR) employs genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal effects19. Here, we conducted a comprehensive bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis to disentangle the reciprocal effects between plasma metabolites and AIH development. The results provide valuable etiological insights to inform clinical practice, and may support future development of probiotic therapies targeting implicated plasma metabolites for AIH.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was performed according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) Statement and followed the recommended checklist20. Ethics approval was not required for this study. We performed a two-sample Mendelian randomization study to investigate the causal association between 1400 plasma metabolites and AIH risk using European ancestry dataset, which were accessible through MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) OpenGWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), encompassing a sample size of 821 cases and 484,413 controls. A workflow diagram of the analysis approach is provided in Fig. 1. The genetic instruments utilized satisfied three key assumptions of Mendelian randomization: (1) the selected genetic variants serving as instrumental variables exhibited significant genome-wide associations with the metabolite exposures; (2) these variants were independent of measured and unmeasured confounders; and (3) the instruments were specifically associated with the outcome through a plausible biological pathway.

Study design of a two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis to assess the effect of genetically predicted plasma metabolites on autoimmune hepatitis. Significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were selected for 1400 plasma metabolites and AIH, and bidirectional causal relationships were explored. Three key assumptions of mendelian randomization analysis are depicted in this directed acyclic graph: relevance, independence, and exclusion restrictions.

Data sources

GWAS data for 1,091 metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios were obtained from 8,299 unrelated European individuals in the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging21. Subjects underwent comprehensive genome-wide genotyping and plasma metabolomic profiling. Metabolite quantification was performed using UHPLC-MS/MS (Metabolon HD4), which identified 1,458 distinct plasma metabolites. The dataset was subsequently refined to include 1,091 metabolites with less than 50% missing values. Batch-normalized metabolite levels with minimal missing data were utilized for analysis. The Human Metabolome Database facilitated the identification of 309 metabolite pairs sharing common enzymatic or transporter elements. Metabolite ratios were calculated and curated to within 3 standard deviations of the mean, followed by inverse-rank normalization. Correspondingly, genetic associations for AIH were acquired from 220 human phenotype genetic correlation maps, encompassing 821 European patients and 484,413 European controls, involving 24,198,482 SNPs (GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST90018785). The phenotype data were derived from electronic health records, and the diagnosis of AIH was based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. The genotype data were generated using a genome-wide genotyping array, and the SNPs were imputed using a reference panel. The genetic associations were calculated using a logistic regression model, adjusting for age, sex, and population stratification. Details on the sourced GWAS datasets are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Key variables in the datasets are complete, precluding the need for missing data handling techniques such as multiple imputation or weighted modeling.

Mendelian randomization analysis

In this study, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with blood metabolites at genome-wide significance (p < 1 × 10− 5) in published GWASs and not in linkage disequilibrium (LD r2 < 0.001 and kb = 10,000) with other variants were used as instruments. Additionally, SNPs with limited association strength (F-statistic ≤ 10) were excluded, where F-statistic was calculated per (beta/SE)2 using the European 1000 Genomes reference panel.

After harmonizing the exposure and outcome summary data, the central Mendelian randomization analysis employed the inverse variance-weighted (IVW) MR method to elucidate the potential causal link between plasma metabolites and AIH22. Additional methods were applied to assess potential violations of MR assumptions, including Simple mode, Weighted mode, weighted median, and MR-Egger. The primary method used was the IVW approach, assuming the validity of all SNPs used as instrumental variables.

To verify the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. Initially, the Cochran’s Q test evaluated the presence of heterogeneity within the data23. Subsequently, the MR-Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MRPRESSO) methodology were employed to detect and eliminate SNPs with potential horizontal pleiotropic outliers, mitigating the potential impact of pleiotropy on causal estimations24. In cases where significant horizontal pleiotropy was detected in the MR-PRESSO global test, SNPs identified as outliers (with a significance level of p < 0.05) were excluded, and based on previous Mendelian randomization results, metabolites with inconsistent odds ratio (OR) directions among the five methods were removed. The remaining SNPs underwent re-analysis. Furthermore, the MR-IVW and MR-Egger regression intercepts were employed to assess potential pleiotropy in the SNPs, with a p-value ≥ 0.05 indicating the absence of horizontal pleiotropy. An False discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p-value threshold of < 0.2 was established, which we used as a heuristic to define evidence for the association between protein and the risk of AIH. Additionally, a two-sample MR analysis was conducted on each individual SNP. Subsequently, we performed reverse Mendelian randomization on the filtered exposure and outcome factors to ensure that the association between exposure and outcome was not driven by the outcome itself. Lastly, a leave-one-out analysis was carried out to detect any pleiotropy stemming from each individual SNP. The outcomes were presented in terms of odds ratios (OR) alongside their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). All reported p-values were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined at the 5% threshold. Analyses were performed using the “TwoSampleMR,” and “MRPRESSO” packages in R software (version 4.2.3).

Results

Instrument variables for plasma metabolites

The GWAS data detailing genetic variants associated with 1,091 blood metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios were acquired from a previously published report. Supplementary Table S1 presents detailed information including effect size estimates (beta coefficients), standard errors (SE), effect alleles, alternate alleles and additional metrics with the F-statistic values of selected SNPs ranged from 19.50 to 5380.35, supporting the absence of weak instrument bias.

Causal effects of the plasma metabolites on AIH

Using Mendelian randomization Egger, weighted median, inverse variance weighted, simple mode, and weighted mode, 1400 plasma metabolites displayed potential causal relationships with AIH. The outermost circle labels catalog IDs for genetic loci. Inverse variance weighted analysis identified 58 plasma metabolites as potentially AIH-associated (Supplementary Table S2). Additional analyses using simple mode, weighted mode, weighted median, and MR Egger assessed potential violations of Mendelian randomization assumptions (Supplementary Table S3). Overall trends remained consistent, albeit some lost significance. Extracting metabolites with consistent effect direction across five methods, FDR-corrected p < 0.2 and MR pleiotropy p > 0.05 yielded two significant predictors. Genetically predicted higher kynurenine conferred increased risk by IVW (p = 2.79 × 10− 5, OR: 1.64, 95% CI 1.30–2.07), weighted median (p = 3.70 × 10− 3; OR: 1.67; 95% CI1.18–2.37), simple mode (p = 0.0179; OR: 2.04, 95% CI 1.18–3.537), weighted mode (p = 0.0132; OR: 1.81; 95% CI 1.17–2.78), and MR Egger (p = 0.599; OR: 1.22; 95% CI 0.59–2.51). The Dopamine 4-sulfate to dopamine 3-O-sulfate ratio demonstrated a significant protective effect by IVW (p = 1.06 × 10− 5,OR: 0.62, 95% CI 0.49–0.79), weighted median (p = 8.85 × 10− 3; OR: 0.64, 95% CI 0.46–0.87), simple mode (p = 0.132; OR: 0.61, 95% CI 0.33–1.13), weighted mode (p = 0.117; OR: 0.62; 95% CI 0.35–1.10), and MR Egger (p = 0.370; OR: 0.73; 95% CI 0.37–1.44) (Fig. 3).

Forest plots presenting causal effect estimates and confidence intervals of genetically predicted kynurenine and dopamine 4-sulfate to dopamine 3-O-sulfate ratio on susceptibility to autoimmune hepatitis. Elevated levels of kynurenine were associated with an increased risk of AIH [IVW: p < 0.001; odds ratio (OR) (95% confidence interval (CI)): 1.642 (1.302–2.070)]. The dopamine sulfate ratio demonstrated a significant protective effect [IVW: p < 0.001; OR (95% CI): 0.623 (0.491–0.792)].

Causal effects of the AIH on the selected plasma metabolites

AIH represented the exposure and metabolites the outcome in reciprocal Mendelian randomization incorporating MR-PRESSO. Inverse variance weighted analysis revealed a significant causal relationship between AIH and kynurenine (p = 2.79 × 10− 5,OR: 1.64, 95% CI 1.30–2.07). And no significant causality emerged between AIH and the dopamine sulfate ratio across all five methods - inverse variance weighted (p = 0.691,OR: 1.05, 95% CI 0.67–1.64), weighted median (p = 0.434; OR: 1.02, 95% CI 0.97–1.07), simple mode (p = 0.569; OR: 1.02, 95% CI 0.95–1.10), weighted mode (p = 0.418; OR: 1.03; 95% CI 0.96–1.10), and MR Egger (p = 0.970; OR: 1.00; 95% CI 0.91–1.10). The dopamine sulfate ratio was therefore retained as an AIH risk factor.

Several additional analyses affirmed the dopamine sulfate ratio as an AIH risk factor. Firstly, we visually examined the forest plot (Fig. 4). Discussion

AIH is a chronic inflammatory hepatic disease influenced by genetic factors, environmental exposures, and immune system dysregulation25,26. Diagnosis requires evaluation of clinical manifestations, biochemical indicators, serological features, and histopathological characteristics27. Despite these diagnostic modalities, there are presently no disease-specific biomarkers. Standard treatment involves corticosteroids, either solitary or with azathioprine28,29. Although most patients respond favorably, intolerance and treatment failure occur. Animal models have furthered comprehension of pathophysiological mechanisms and exploration of prospective novel therapies30–32. However, extant models do not fully replicate human AIH complexity. Future directions may include optimized pharmacological regimens, interrogating roles of gut microbiota, targeting cytokine pathways, among others. The protean nature of AIH necessitates a multifaceted investigation from various perspectives to enhance understanding and engender more efficacious therapies.

We have detected 1,091 blood metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios that exhibit significant involvement in AIH. Importantly, our findings elucidate a bidirectional positive causal association between the dopamine sulfate ratio and AIH, emphasizing the potential establishment of a detrimental feedback loop in the initiation and progression of AIH. Remarkably, our investigation represents the inaugural utilization of the Mendelian Randomization framework for the examination of the causal interconnection between plasma metabolites and AIH. Notably, the dopamine sulfate ratio emerges as a protective factor against the development of AIH in our study.

Circulating metabolites have proven utility in developing diagnostic biomarkers for diseases including autoimmunity and cancer33–36. Autoimmune diseases exhibit altered metabolic pathways with aberrant metabolites. Plasma metabolomics enables systematic profiling to garner insights into autoimmune disease metabolism. Over the past decade, plasma metabolomics has undergone application for biomarker discovery in diseases like celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)11–14. However, knowledge gaps remain regarding prognostic biomarkers and treatment guidance. A recent metagenomic analysis of Japanese participants evaluated gut microbiome-plasma metabolome-host genetic associations using multi-omics data. Metabolite-microbiome analysis identified bile acids showing significant relationships with multiple microbial traits and annotated gene orthologs37. Additional studies revealed causal impacts of specific bacterial features on AIH risk38,39. Specific microbiota have confirmed correlations with AIH severity40, including Veillonella, exhibiting the strongest disease association41. Veillonella abundance positively associates with serum aspartate aminotransferase and hepatic inflammation grades41. Bifidobacterium decline relates to heightened disease activity and failure to achieve remission42. Recently, Yugang Fu et al. performed a Mendelian randomization study using the most comprehensive GWAS data available to probe the gut microbiome-AIH relationship. Notably, the Clostridium inoculum group displayed a significant protective effect against AIH (OR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.49–0.93)38. Together, these findings indicate numerous gut microbial constituents may associate with circulating dopamine and additional metabolites, consequently affecting AIH pathogenesis. This warrants further probing.

Relatively few studies have probed plasma metabolite-AIH relationships. Our analysis revealed contrasting findings for Ruminiclostridium, with presence increasing AIH risk. Dopamine 4- and 3-O-sulfate represented endogenous dopamine metabolites. Dopamine has known immunomodulation roles in multiple sclerosis and SLE43–46. In multiple sclerosis, dopamine depletion exacerbated disease in encephalomyelitis models, emphasizing its critical autoimmunity role47. Abnormal dopamine receptor expression in SLE patient T cells and associations with lupus-like disease further support dopamine as an immune mediator44,46. Dopamine sulfate isomers may differentially interact with dopamine receptors on immune cells48, particularly T cells, which are crucial in autoimmune diseases like AIH and SLE. Recent studies in SLE have provided insights that may be relevant to AIH pathogenesis. Wang et al. found increased plasma dopamine concentrations and abnormally high expression of D1-like dopamine receptors on lupus CD4 + T cells, especially T follicular helper (Tfh) cells46. This high expression was associated with disease activity and promoted Tfh cell differentiation, possibly via the PI3K/AKT/FOXO1/Klf2 pathway. Ferreira et al. demonstrated distorted expression of dopamine receptor genes in SLE, with underexpression of the dopamine receptor 2 gene (DR2) and overexpression of DR4 in T cells43. DR2 underexpression may contribute to reduced regulatory T-cell function, while DR4 overexpression might affect T-cell quiescence. These findings could potentially explain our observed protective effect of a higher dopamine 4-sulfate to dopamine 3-O-sulfate ratio in AIH. The different sulfate isomers might interact differently with the altered dopamine receptor expressions, influencing immune responses and disease progression.

Despite scarce examination in AIH, our innovative Mendelian randomization study uncovered specific dopamine species potentially significant in disease onset and progression. The causal effect of the dopamine sulfate ratio on AIH development presents an intriguing biological hypothesis. Dopamine, beyond its neurotransmitter role, is increasingly recognized as an immunomodulator. The dopamine sulfate isomer ratio may serve as a proxy for overall dopamine metabolism, potentially reflecting systemic dopaminergic tone. This ratio might influence immune function through several mechanisms. Firstly, dopamine directly modulates immune function via receptors expressed on various immune cells. Changes in dopaminergic signaling could alter immune cell function and differentiation, potentially contributing to autoimmune processes48. Secondly, the dopamine system is integral to neuroimmune communication, affecting the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system, thus indirectly modulating immune responses45. Thirdly, dopamine metabolism is linked to oxidative stress, which has been implicated in AIH pathogenesis49,50.

Investigating plasma metabolite changes holds substantial clinical import for AIH. Firstly, metabolite alterations may serve as biomarkers for disease screening, diagnosis, and prognostication. Secondly, current therapeutic strategies are limited, centering on prednisone and azathioprine combinations51. Elucidating causal metabolite-AIH links is therefore pivotal to uncovering new molecular targets and therapeutic agents, thereby engendering more efficacious targeted treatments. Although the reverse Mendelian randomization analysis does not support a causal effect of AIH on the ratio of dopamine-sulfate isomer, AIH-associated inflammatory processes may potentially modulate dopaminergic pathways across multiple tissue types. The liver, as the primary site of AIH pathology and a significant contributor to peripheral dopamine metabolism, could experience alterations in dopamine sulfation processes due to inflammation52. Additionally, AIH-related systemic inflammation might indirectly influence dopamine metabolism in other tissues, such as the gut and central nervous system, through various inflammatory mechanisms53,54. These potential interactions between AIH and dopamine metabolism warrant further investigation.

Despite the comprehensive nature of our Mendelian randomization analysis inferring plasma metabolite-AIH causality, some limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, validation utilizing experimental and clinical cohorts is necessitated, as conclusions were solely derived from genetic analyses without further phenotypic validation. Secondly, while we focused on circulating metabolites, the role of tissue metabolites remains unaddressed, as the peripheral metabolome may differ considerably from tissue metabolites. Thirdly, underlying mechanisms for specific metabolite-disease causal links, particularly regarding operative cell types and signaling cascades, require further interrogation. Finally, clinical overlap among AIH subtypes can complicate differential diagnosis. Comparing subtype-specific results is more prudent than relying solely on individual subtype conclusions to definitively infer metabolite-disease causality.

Conclusion

Our study provides evidence supporting the causal effect of specific metabolites on the risk of AIH. Specifically, we found that the dopamine 4-sulfate/dopamine 3-O-sulfate displayed a significant protective effect against AIH (IVW: p = 1.06 × 10− 5). The plasma metabolites displayed a significant protective effect. Further longitudinal and experimental studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore targeted probiotic potential for AIH management.
