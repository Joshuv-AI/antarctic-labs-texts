The Relationship Between Receipt of Opioid Settlement Funds and Reductions in Opioid Overdose Deaths in the U.S.
Authors: Ryan K McBain, Jonathan H Cantor, Aaron Kofner, Zoe Lindenfeld, David Powell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This study examined opioid settlement funds and overdose deaths across U.S. states.•Each extra $1 per capita is linked to a 2.46% decline in overdose deaths in 2023.•States with higher past overdose deaths received larger settlement disbursements.•Settlement funds may help reduce opioid-related mortality early in implementation.Keywords: Opioids, addiction, health policy, substance use, overdose

INTRODUCTION

The national opioid crisis continues to devastate communities throughout the U.S., with nearly 70% of the 107,000 overdose deaths reported in 2023 attributed to opioid use.1 Amidst this crisis, the ongoing distribution of funds from the national settlement agreements with opioid distributors and manufacturers presents a unique opportunity to mitigate the morbidity and mortality associated with opioid use and addiction.2 Since 2021, over $50 billion in national settlements have been reached with over a dozen firms that have manufactured, sold, and dispensed prescription painkillers that contributed to the opioid crisis in the U.S.2,3 To prevent a repeat of errors made within the Tobacco Master Settlement Agreements, in which <10% of funds were used on tobacco control, funds from the opioid settlement are earmarked specifically for opioid abatement purposes, with strict oversight of spending.4 Approved spending activities include treatment, prevention, recovery, and harm-reduction services such as naloxone distribution or syringe access programs, among others.5

To guide the allocation of settlement funds, the distribution formula indicates that funds should be allocated on the basis of the documented impact of the epidemic,6,7 with the hope that this will lead to reductions in opioid-related overdoses and harms. However, previous research has not assessed the relationship between settlement funds and overdose deaths in the disbursement period. In this study, the authors employ an event study design to examine whether larger per-capita disbursements correspond to greater reductions in opioid-related overdose death rates since 2021. As a validation step, the authors also assess whether states with higher baseline opioid-related overdose death rates (2017–2021)—a key metric cited in the settlement allocation formula—receive correspondingly higher per-capita disbursements. Findings from this study can provide critical insight into whether the settlement funds are being directed toward serving their intended purpose of mitigating the impact of the opioid crisis.

METHODS

This study constitutes a repeated cross-sectional analysis of state-level population and health measures across all 50 U.S. states. The manuscript follows the STROBE reporting guidelines for repeated cross-sectional studies, and it received exemption from RAND’s IRB review.

Study Population and Measures

The authors obtained annualized state-level opioid-related overdose death rates (2017–2023) from the National Center for Health Statistics and data on opioid settlement disbursements (2022–2023) from the Kaiser Family Foundation.3,8 Specifically, this includes the total dollar amount of settlement payouts received by each state in a given year. The authors scaled deaths and disbursements by population (per 100,000 residents) using values from the 2017–2022 U.S. Census Bureau. Overdose deaths were identified using ICD-10 codes X40-X44, X60-X64, X85, and Y10–Y14.9 Opioid involvement required T40.0–T40.4 or T40.6.

Statistical Analysis

For the primary analysis, the authors estimated an event study using a Poisson regression, following standard methods outlined by Wooldridge (1999).10 This constitutes a quasiexperimental approach because it leverages state-level temporal variation in opioid-related overdose death rates before and after the initiation of settlement fund disbursements. By comparing trends in overdose deaths during the predisbursement period (2017–2021) with those observed in the postdisbursement period (2022–2023), the authors sought to isolate the potential impact of opioid settlement funds from general trends and other secular factors influencing overdose mortality rates. The Poisson regression included state- and year-fixed effects, allowing the control of unobserved state-specific characteristics and nationwide temporal fluctuations. CIs included a robust adjustment to account for state-level dependence, ensuring that findings were not artificially precise owing to correlated observations within states.

For the secondary analysis focused on monitoring whether settlement funds were disbursed in greater quantities to high-need areas, the authors quantified the magnitude of settlement disbursements (per 100,000 population) in 2022–2023 relative to opioid-related overdose death rates (per 100,000 population) in the 5 preceding years (2017–2021). This constituted linear regression, again with state- and year-fixed effects. Further details on the precise configuration of the event study design are provided in the Appendix Material (available online). All analyses were performed using Stata MP, Version 17.0.

RESULTS

The event study analysis found limited evidence of pretrend differences (p>0.05), confirming that—prior to opioid settlement disbursements—there were no significant differences in trends of opioid-related overdose deaths across states. However, in 2022–2023, the magnitude of decline in opioid-related overdoses corresponded to the magnitude of state-level disbursements: each additional per-capita dollar of settlement funds was associated with a 0.99% larger decline in opioid-related overdose deaths in 2022 (95% CI= −1.90%, −0.08%; p=0.03) and a 2.46% decline in opioid-related overdose deaths in 2023 (95% CI= −4.17%, −0.75%; p=0.01) (Table 1). Point estimates imply that 0.41 fewer annual deaths per 100,000 residents were associated with each additional per-capita dollar of settlement funds disbursed.

DISCUSSION

The authors found that larger state-level per-capita disbursements corresponded to greater declines in opioid-related overdose death rates in 2022 and 2023. The quasiexperimental event study approach can be replicated during the full disbursement period (2022–2038) to monitor ongoing progress. Although some states have not started distributing received funds, others have made considerable progress,6 making it important to monitor the process as disbursements continue. Effective monitoring, coupled with transparency from state and local governments, is crucial to ensure that settlement funds are directed toward addressing the opioid crisis.2 Furthermore, there have been substantial reductions in federal funding allocated to states for behavioral health services over the past several months.11 As a result, accurate data on opioid settlement disbursements and where they are being spent will become even more important. That said, the finding that opioid settlement disbursements in 2022–2023 were more heavily allocated to states with greater need—as indicated by opioid overdose death rates in the prior 5 years (2017–2021)—provides a validation that the current allocations are on an appropriate trajectory.2,6 It also provides novel insight into the magnitude of investment differences: namely, each additional opioid-related overdose death per 100,000 residents in 2017–2021 was associated with a $0.87 greater per-capita disbursement in 2022–2023.

Limitations

Study limitations include the use of observational data and a narrow focus on opioid-related overdose death rates rather than other measures of opioid-related harms used as criteria for disbursement. Likewise, population density can be seen as a driver of need: areas that have lower overdose death rates but larger populations could generate higher numbers of overdose deaths in absolute terms. This study also has limited geographic and temporal resolution. For example, there is substantial variability in how states are allocating and managing settlement funds, and the proportion controlled by states versus county and city governments varies significantly. However, the analysis does not include data on how or where funds are being spent within states, which has been largely unavailable to date.12 Study estimates reflect the average change in observed mortality as a function of disbursement levels, which internalizes attenuated effects among states that have yet to disburse their funds, alongside larger effects among states that have disbursed funds more efficiently.

CONCLUSIONS

Despite these limitations, the authors provide valuable initial evidence that opioid settlement disbursements are being directed toward states with higher baseline opioid-related overdose death rates, and the size of disbursements is associated with early reductions in these death rates. Future research should assess whether receipt of settlement funds at the county level is associated with reductions in opioid overdose deaths as well as account for variations in settlement spending targets. For example, studies could examine whether areas that allocate the majority of their settlement funds toward expanding treatment and harm-reduction availability have differentially greater reductions in mortality than areas that allocate more funds toward law enforcement.
