Single-cell transcriptomic profiling reveals PDK4 upregulation and impaired VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling in cardiac lymphatic endothelial cells in diabetic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Yufeng Yan, Xunuo Wang, Congfei Zhu, Zihao Jiang, Jing Wang, Junjie He, Lei Xu, Xiaobin Zhou, Qing Ge, Zuoying Hu, Guangfeng Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is characterized by microvascular dysfunction. While vascular endothelial cells (ECs) have been widely studied, the role of cardiac lymphatic ECs (LECs) in DCM remains unknown.MethodsA mouse model of DCM was induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) with streptozotocin (STZ) injection. We performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on cardiac tissues from mice with DCM and control mice. We then applied clustering, RNA velocity, pseudotime analysis, and CellChat cell-cell communication analysis to define lymphatic endothelial cell (LEC) heterogeneity and underlying regulatory networks.ResultsOur analysis revealed profound pathological remodeling of the cardiac lymphatic architecture in diabetic hearts, marked by decreased LEC numbers and impaired function. Cell-cell communication analysis demonstrated impaired VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling between venous ECs (VECs) and LECs in diabetic hearts. We identified the upregulation of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4) expression in diabetic LECs as a key driver of metabolic reprogramming, leading to impaired cell migration.ConclusionsThis study mapped the profile of LECs in diabetic hearts and revealed a dual pathogenic mechanism involving metabolic disorders driven by PDK4 and disruption of venous-lymphatic communication. These findings may provide new strategies for the treatment of DCM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01571-9.Keywords: DCM, scRNA-seq, LECs, PDK4, VEGFC

Introduction

The prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) is continuously increasing worldwide (Sun et al. 2022). In patients with diabetes, cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of mortality (Dinesh Shah et al. 2015). Heart failure (HF) is among the most common cardiovascular diseases among patients with diabetes (Wong, et al. 2023). Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is characterized by myocardial structural and functional abnormalities directly attributable to diabetes, excluding conditions such as coronary artery disease, hypertensive heart disease, and valvular disorders (Paulus, et al. 2013; Seferović et al. 2024). There is still a lack of effective treatment methods for DCM. The development of HF in patients with DCM involves multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. These mechanisms include myocardial metabolic disturbances (Montaigne et al. 2014), oxidative stress (Liu et al. 2013; Seferović et al. 2024), myocardial fibrosis (Russo, et al. 2016; Van Linthout et al. 2008), and vascular dysfunction (Yang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024).

Hyperglycemia and excessive free fatty acids damage vascular endothelial cells (ECs), leading to endothelial dysfunction, decreased capillary density, and compromised blood flow reserve (Kaur et al. 2018). Many studies have focused predominantly on the effects of diabetes on vascular ECs and the resulting myocardial capillary rarefaction and arterial remodeling (Zhang et al. 2024). However, the maintenance of cardiac microenvironment homeostasis depends not only on vascular ECs but also on lymphatic ECs (LECs)(Guo et al. 2025; Rossitto et al. 2020). The cardiac lymphatic network is responsible for tissue fluid balance, immune cell surveillance, and metabolic waste clearance and plays a vital role in maintaining cardiac function (Cuijpers et al. 2020). DCM is accompanied by systolic and/or diastolic myocardial dysfunction in patients with diabetes (Seferović et al. 2024). Recent research has indicated that enhancing lymphangiogenesis can improve cardiac function in patients with HF with a preserved ejection fraction (Guo et al. 2025). Furthermore, numerous studies have demonstrated that lymphatic vessel damage following myocardial infarction and that targeting lymphatic function can improve HF outcomes (Bai et al. 2024; Henri et al. 2016). Several studies have shown that lymphatic function is impaired in patients with diabetes and that enhancing lymphatic function is beneficial for treating this disease (Scallan et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2018). We hypothesize that in the heart of patients with diabetes, this disease may similarly selectively damage LECs, leading to impaired lymphatic drainage and exacerbated local inflammation and interstitial fibrosis, collectively contributing to the progression of DCM.

However, the damage to cardiac LECs under diabetic stress and its underlying mechanism remain unclear. This greatly hinders the effective prevention and treatment of DCM. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology provides significant assistance in addressing this knowledge gap (Bajpai et al. 2019; Papalexi, et al. 2018). It can not only generate a complete map of cardiac cells at single-cell resolution (Su et al. 2024) but also precisely identify and track rare cell populations such as LECs, revealing their dynamic gene expression profiles and regulatory networks under pathological conditions.

Thus, in this study scRNA-seq was performed on a mouse model of DCM. We focused on the pathological changes in cardiac LECs, providing insights into the function of LECs in DCM.

Methods

Construction and validation of the HFD/STZ-induced DCM model

DCM was induced using a combination of a high-fat diet (HFD; Research Diets D12492) and streptozotocin (STZ: S0130). In brief, after being fed a HFD for 8 weeks, C57BL/6J mice received intraperitoneal injections of STZ (35 mg/kg) for three consecutive days (Wang et al. 2024). STZ was prepared immediately before use in 0.05 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5). For comparison, age-matched control animals were maintained on a standard laboratory diet and received vehicle injections (citrate buffer only). Diabetes was confirmed two weeks post-injection by measuring postprandial blood glucose levels from the tail vein. Only mice exhibiting persistent postprandial glucose levels exceeding 16.7 mmol/L were classified as diabetic. These animals were maintained on a HFD for an additional 16 weeks to facilitate the progression of DCM. The animal care and experimental protocols were in compliance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines.

Glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT))

For the GTT, the mice were fasted for 16 h and then administered an intraperitoneal injection of D-glucose (2 g/kg). To perform the ITT, the mice were fasted for 6 h followed by an i.p. injection of insulin (0.5 U/kg). Tail vein blood glucose was measured using a glucometer at 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min post-injection. Data integration and area under the curve (AUC) calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism software.

Frozen heart tissue nuclear suspension preparation

The frozen tissue was minced, mixed with prechilled lysis buffer, and homogenized with a glass Dounce homogenizer (7–8 strokes), followed by lysis on ice for 5 min. The lysate was filtered through a 40 μm strainer, rinsed with elution buffer, and centrifuged at 500×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in cold DPBS, passed through a 20 μm strainer and centrifuged at 500×g. The pellet was resuspended again, and an elution buffer/PBS step gradient was formed by adding PBS on top of the elution buffer, followed by centrifugation at 3000×g for 10 min at 4 °C (acceleration 9, deceleration 1). After the supernatant was discarded, the pellet was washed with cold DPBS, centrifuged again, and finally resuspended in DPBS for quality control evaluations.

Library construction and sequencing

snRNA‑seq libraries were generated using the 10X Genomics Chromium Controller and Chromium Single-Cell 3’ V3.1 Reagent Kits (10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Briefly, nuclei were concentrated to approximately 1,000 nuclei/µL and loaded into each channel to generate single‑cell gel bead‑in‑emulsions (GEMs). After the reverse transcription step, the GEMs were broken, and the resulting barcoded cDNA was subsequently purified and amplified. The amplified barcoded cDNA was fragmented, A‑tailed, ligated with adapters, and index PCR amplified. The final libraries were quantified using the Qubit High-Sensitivity DNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the size distribution was determined using a high-sensitivity DNA chip on a Bioanalyzer 2200 system (Agilent, USA). All the libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, USA) with a 150 bp paired‑end run. A total of four male C57BL/6J mice were used in this experiment: two in the control group and two in the DCM group. Each heart was processed individually for 10x sequencing. Nuclear isolation from all frozen heart tissues and subsequent 10x Chromium loading were performed in parallel on the same day. The control and DCM samples were loaded onto different channels of the same chip and processed in parallel.

scRNA-seq analysis

To generate a unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrix, raw FASTQ data were mapped against the mm10 mouse reference genome (GENCODE vM23/Ensembl 98). Downstream analysis was conducted primarily using Scanpy (v1.8)(Wolf et al. 2018). We implemented stringent filtering criteria: cells were excluded if they possessed (1) < 500 or > 60,000 UMIs, (2) < 200 or > 8,000 detected genes, or (3) > 5% mitochondrial gene content. After quality control, 61,748 high-quality single cells were retained for further investigation. For data normalization, we employed the pp.normalize_total function (scaling factor = 10,000), followed by log-transformation via pp.log1p. After highly variable genes were identified via the method described by Macosko et al (Macosko et al. 2015), we performed principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. To mitigate technical variations and batch effects across different samples, we applied the Harmony algorithm to integrate the datasets within the PCA space. A nearest-neighbor graph was then constructed to support graph-based clustering, with the results visualized using t-SNE in 2D space. Marker genes and DEGs for each cluster were identified using the Seurat R package via the FindAllMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test)(Hao et al. 2021). Significance was defined as an adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| >0.25, with the additional requirement that the gene be detected in at least 10% of cells in either group (min.pct = 0.1). Functional enrichment analyses (Gene Ontology [GO]) were conducted via ClusterProfiler on the basis of hypergeometric distributions (Wu et al. 2021). All P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Only Gene Ontology (GO) terms with an adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 and Q < 0.2 were considered statistically significant. The results were visualized using the clusterProfiler and enrichplot R packages.To explore cellular dynamics and interactions, several advanced computational tools were integrated: RNA velocity was estimated using velocyto (v0.17) to infer developmental directions (Bergen et al. 2020; La Manno et al. 2018), and partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) provided partition-based graph abstraction (Wolf et al. 2019). Monocle 2 was utilized for pseudotime trajectory reconstruction(Qiu et al. 2017), and CellChat was applied to delineate cell-cell communication networks(Jin et al. 2021). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package. Enrichment scores (ES) and normalized enrichment scores (NES) were calculated for each gene set based on 1,000 permutations. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Gene sets with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |NES| > 1.0 were considered significantly enriched. Finally, the transcription factor (TF) regulatory network was constructed using pySCENIC (v0.11)(Aibar et al. 2017). The proliferation index was defined as the proportion of cells in either S or G2/M phase. Cell cycle phases were assigned to each LEC using the cell cycle scoring function from Seurat. All differentially expressed genes and GO enrichment results are provided in Supplementary Material 2–10.

Echocardiography

We performed transthoracic echocardiography in mice with a Vevo® 3100 high-resolution preclinical imaging system. Inhalation anesthesia was induced with 3% isoflurane. When the mice did not respond to the forceful pressure applied to their hind paws, the isoflurane concentration was adjusted to 1.0-1.5% to maintain the heart rate at approximately 450 beats per minute, after which image acquisition was initiated.

M-mode images were acquired to evaluate systolic function. Diastolic function was assessed using pulsed-wave and tissue Doppler imaging. All parameters were measured at least three times per animal, and the mean values were used for subsequent analyses.

Histological analysis

Hearts were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. To ensure standardized morphological comparisons among groups, 5.0 μm thick transverse sections were obtained exclusively at the mid-level of the left ventricle using a sliding microtome (Leica, Nussloch, Germany) for all subsequent analyses. Following routine deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining. All histological images were captured using a light or fluorescence microscope, and quantitative analyses (collagen area fraction and cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area) were performed using ImageJ software. For LYVE1 staining, heart sections were incubated with an anti‑LYVE1 antibody (Abcam, av281587, 1:500) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with a secondary antibody (1:200) at 37 °C for 60 min, DAPI counterstaining, and imaging via confocal microscopy.

Cell culture and treatments

Mouse LECs (MLECs; Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology) were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ScienCell, 1001, CA), which includes fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a standard component. Cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 before any treatment. To facilitate the cellular uptake of exogenous fatty acids, palmitic acid was pre-conjugated with fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA) to generate PA-BSA complexes. After being starved for 12 h, the MLECs were randomly divided into two groups: 33 mM D-glucose plus 200 µM palmitate as the PA + HG group and 5.5 mM D glucose as the LG group. MLECs were then cultured in specific media for 48 h. Furthermore, to investigate whether PDK4 inhibition could reverse the induced cellular injury, cells were concurrently treated with 30 µM PDK4-IN-1(MCE, HY-135954)(Deng et al. 2026). MLECs were pretreated with 1 µM dovitinib (MCE, HY-50905) or an equal volume of vehicle control (DMSO) for 1 h. Following pretreatment, the cells were stimulated with 10 ng/mL recombinant VEGFC (MCE, HY-P74474) for 24 h.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors. To ensure complete lysis, the lysates were kept on ice and vortexed every 10 min for a total of three times. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant fractions were collected, mixed with SDS loading buffer, and denatured by boiling. The protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes in precooled transfer buffer. Following blocking, the membranes were incubated with antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then washed three times with TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST), followed by incubation with the corresponding fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After three final washes (10 min per wash), protein bands were detected and imaged with the GeneGnome XRQ system (Syngene). Detailed information for all antibodies used in this study is provided in Table S1.

SiRNA transfection

To knockdown endogenous pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4), LECs were transfected with PDK4-specific small interfering RNA (siPDK4, 50 nmol/L; GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for transfection, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Total protein was extracted to determine the effectiveness of siRNA transfection by western blotting. The sequences of the siRNAs are provided in Table S2.

Plasmid transfection

The plasmid encoding mouse Pdk4 (Mus musculus pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4) was purchased from GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). MLECs were seeded into 6-well plates one day prior to transfection. To overexpress Pdk4, the plasmid was transfected into MLECs using Lipofectamine 3000 and P3000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After a 6-hour incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere, the transfection medium was replaced with endothelial cell medium. The cells were cultured for an additional 48 h to allow for optimal PDK4 overexpression. Finally, total cellular protein was extracted to verify the overexpression efficiency via Western blotting prior to subsequent experiments.

Seahorse bioenergetic measurements

Mouse LECs were seeded into XF96 cell culture microplates (Seahorse Bioscience) at a density of 6,000 cells per well and cultured overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ to allow adherence. For groups containing PDK4-IN, cells were treated with 30 µM PDK4-IN for 24 h. To measure the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), the culture medium was replaced with XF Base Medium supplemented with 2 mM L‑glutamine (pH: 7.4). The cells were equilibrated at 37 °C in a non‑CO₂ incubator for 1 h. The ECAR was then measured using an XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) following sequential injections of 20 mM glucose, 2 µM oligomycin, and 50 mM 2‑deoxyglucose (2‑DG). To measure the oxygen consumption rate (OCR), the culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF Assay Medium (pH: 7.4) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, 1 mM pyruvate, and 10 mM glucose. The cells were equilibrated at 37 °C in a non‑CO₂ incubator for 1 h. The OCR was assessed using the same analyzer with sequential injections of oligomycin (1.5 µM), FCCP (1 µM), and a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (0.5 µM each). The data were analyzed using Seahorse Wave software (Agilent Technologies). The basal respiration, ATP‑linked respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity were calculated on the basis of the OCR; basal glycolysis and compensatory glycolysis were derived from the ECAR. All values were normalized to the total protein content per well.

Scratch wound healing assay

LECs were seeded into a 6-well plate and cultured until they reached approximately 80% confluence before the experiment. A 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a consistent scratch across the cell monolayer. Wound healing was observed and photographed at 0 and 24 h post-scratch using a Leica DFC550 microscope (Germany).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. The results are shown as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between two groups were assessed by Student’s t test (paired or unpaired), while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Fisher’s exact test was used to compare differences in cell proliferation index and cell counts.

A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Development of a mouse model of DCM

We established a DCM model using a HFD combined with STZ (Wang et al. 2024)(Fig. 1A). Twenty-four weeks post-modeling, the mice exhibited a mild reduction in ejection fraction and diastolic dysfunction (Fig. S1A). Compared with those in the control group, cardiac fibrosis in the DCM group was exacerbated, and cardiomyocytes (CMs) were dilated at 24 weeks (Fig. S1B-D). The body weights of the experimental animals are presented in Fig. S1E.We subsequently performed the GGT and ITT with both groups of mice, and the results of which confirmed that mice with DCM exhibited impaired glucose tolerance and insulin resistance compared with control mice (Fig. S1F and G).

scRNA-seq-based clustering analysis of the hearts of mice with diabetes

We systematically examined the pathological profiles of diabetic heart injury by sequencing cells from the hearts of both control mice and those with diabetes. Following quality control, we obtained a total of 61,748 high-quality cells, including 29,900 from the control group and 31,848 from the DCM group. Cells were clustered on the basis of their unique molecular signatures. The overall cellular landscape of the cardiac tissues from both control mice and mice with diabetes is shown in Fig. 1B. Based on the expression of lineage-specific marker genes (Fig. 1C and D), we identified 34 distinct clusters. Without altering the initial clustering resolution, these subclusters were annotated and categorized into 13 major cell types: cardiomyocytes (CMs, markers: Ttn and Myh6), cardiac fibroblasts (CFs, markers: Dcn, Col1a1, Col1a2, and Pdgfra), macrophages (MACs, markers: Csf1r, C1qa, Ms4a7, Fcgr1, and Adgre1), smooth muscle cells (SMCs, markers: Tagln, Acta2, Cd3g, and Myh11), ECs (markers: Cdh5, Pecam1, Npr3, and Flt1), B cells (markers: Cd79a, Cd79b, Cd19, and Ms4a1), T cells (markers: Cd3d, Cd3e, Trac, and Cd3g), Schwann cells (markers: Cdh19, Sox10, and Plp1), neutrophils (markers: Csf3r, S100a8, and S100a9), pericytes (markers: Pdgfrb, Kcnj8, Cald1, Rgs5), endocardial cells (marker: Npr3), epicardial cells (marker: Msln), and natural killer T cells (NK cells, markers: Ncr1, Nkg7, Klrd1). According to the cluster identification results, we quantified and visualized the proportional abundance of each cell type in control and diabetic mouse hearts (Fig. 1E). The expression patterns of these signature genes in each identified cluster are shown in Fig. 1F and Table S3. Tables S4 and S5 show the detailed clusters, cell numbers, and proportions in the two groups.

Characterization of the heterogeneity of cardiac vascular ECs

To further clarify the key role of ECs in DCM, we next explored their heterogeneity. Based on the expression of the marker genes Cdh5 and Pecam1, 12,599 cells (5,859 from the control group and 6,740 from the DCM group) were defined as ECs and were partitioned into 10 clusters (Fig. 2A). A heatmap displaying the characteristic genes for each cluster is shown in Fig. S2A. Correlation analysis between clusters revealed a high degree of correlation between Cluster 3 and Cluster 4 (Fig. 2B). To better reveal the distinct pathological characteristics of different endothelial cell subpopulations in DCM hearts, we performed GO enrichment analysis comparing the DCM and control groups. Cluster 2 was significantly enriched in pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Cluster 3 exhibited markably high enrichment in the negative regulation of cell migration and actomyosin cytoskeleton organization. Cluster 4 showed aberrant activation of long‑chain fatty acid uptake and lipid metabolic pathways. Given the high correlation between Cluster 3 and Cluster 4, we performed differential gene expression analysis on these two clusters(Fig. S3). Cluster 3 represents a classic LEC phenotype with high expression of Prox1, Flt4 (Vegfr3), and Reln (Fig.S4). Cluster 4 represents a stress‑associated LEC subpopulation characterized by upregulation of Lilr4b, Itga11, and Cbr2, and a slightly lower but still robust Flt4 expression (63.9% in cluster 4 vs. 72.8% in Cluster 3), indicating a specialized stress‑responsive state within the LEC lineage.

To functionally annotate these subsets without altering the initial clustering resolution, EC subtypes were classified using established marker genes: arterial (“Dll4”, “Efnb2”, “Sema3g”, “Nos3”), venous (“Ephb4”, “Nr2f2”), capillary (“Btnl9”, “Cd36”), and lymphatic (“Lyve1”, “Prox1”). This classification yielded: Arterial endothelial cells (AECs) (Clusters 1 & 6), venous ECs (VECs) (Cluster 2), LECs (Clusters 3 and 4), capillary ECs (CECs) (Clusters 0, 7 and 8), and mixed ECs (MECs) (Clusters 5 and 9) (Fig. 2C). Based on the cluster identification results, the proportional changes in each cluster across different experimental groups were first analyzed (Fig. 2E and Fig. S2B). Compared with that in the control group, the proportion of CECs in the DCM group increased, whereas the number of VECs decreased. Comparable proportions of mixed, arterial and LECs were maintained in the two groups (Fig. 2D). Tables S6 and S7 show the endothelial clusters, cell numbers, and proportions in the two groups.

To highlight the pathological differences between experimental groups, we performed a cluster-wise differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis. By comparing DCM and control cells within each EC subset, we identified subset-specific DEGs and performed GO enrichment analysis to reveal their functional changes (Fig.S5A-E). AECs displayed a prominent metabolic stress phenotype, marked by enriched “oxidative phosphorylation,” “aerobic respiration,” and “fatty acid oxidation” pathways. Both AECs and VECs were markedly enriched in “muscle system processes” and “actomyosin structure organization.” CECs were predominantly enriched in pathways linked to the “negative regulation of cell migration” and “actin cytoskeleton organization.” The stress response profile of MECs was pronounced, with strong enrichment in the “regulation of NLRP3 inflammasome complex assembly,” “alpha-beta T cell activation,” and “long-chain fatty acid import,” which is consistent with a chronic inflammatory and lipotoxic state. Notably, LECs displayed severe structural and functional remodeling, characterized by enrichment in “response to hypoxia”, “cell junction assembly”, and “negative regulation of cell migration” pathways. These subset-specific alterations provide a novel perspective on the pathophysiological basis of diabetic vasculopathy.

Dynamic lineage transitions and regulatory networks underlying endothelial heterogeneity in diabetes

To elucidate potential differentiation directions among endothelial subsets across both the control and DCM groups, we performed RNA velocity analysis by calculating the ratio of unspliced to spliced mRNA. RNA velocity analysis revealed a differentiation trajectory originating from MECs toward venous, arterial, and CECs. Additionally, the velocity streamlines indicated that both arterial and VECs tended to transition toward capillary ECs, whereas lymphatic ECs followed a distinct and independent trajectory (Fig. S6A and B). We further performed pseudotime analysis with Monocle2 to characterize the differentiation status of these endothelial subsets (Fig. S7A and B). The resulting trajectory revealed that while AECs, VECs, and CECs clustered near the starting point, LECs were uniquely positioned at the distal terminal of the trajectory, further corroborating the RNA velocity findings (Fig. S7C). We designated the major bifurcation as the “fate decision node“(Fig. S7D). Genes enriched prior to this node represent early events in endothelial activation, whereas those significantly differentially expressed along the branches define divergent cell fates. Notably, pseudotime analysis revealed a gradual downregulation of the capillary marker Cd36 as cells progressed along the trajectory, accompanied by an upregulation of the venous marker Nr2f2. Crucially, the expression of the classical lymphatic marker Prox1 was specifically and highly upregulated along the terminal LEC-specific branch (Fig.S7E-J). We reconstructed the differentiation trajectory of ECs using pseudotime analysis (Fig. 3M). Starting from cells in the initial state, the trajectory diverged into two distinct paths: State 1 (lymphatic lineage-specific branch) and State 2 (pathological remodeling branch). To further characterize these transitions, we performed GO enrichment analysis on genes associated with the dynamic changes along the trajectory (Fig. S8). Our analysis revealed that the transition toward State 1 was characterized by the upregulation of multiple lymphangiogenesis-related pathways. Conversely, the transition toward State 2 was marked by a significant enrichment in mesenchymal remodeling and inflammatory pathways. To investigate the underlying gene regulatory networks (GRNs) that maintain the identity of these endothelial cell subpopulations, we employed single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) to identify active TF regulons. The AUC matrix heatmap (Fig. S9A) reveals the distribution of regulon activities at the single-cell level. These results demonstrated that each endothelial subpopulation possesses a distinct regulatory landscape, confirming a high degree of heterogeneity at the transcriptional regulatory level. A regulon specificity score (RSS) heatmap (Fig. S9B) was constructed to identify the most representative core TFs for each subpopulation. CECs are robustly regulated by factors such as Ebf3(+). LECs strongly activated Hoxd4(+) regulons. Zfb764(+) is a specific TF for AECs. Taken together, these findings indicate that specific TFs serve as key molecular drivers that direct endothelial cell differentiation and maintain specialized physiological functions. In conclusion, our integrated dynamic and regulatory analysis provides a model in which the diabetic myocardial microenvironment disrupts endothelial cell homeostasis.

Vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGFC) mediates venous-to-Llmphatic endothelial communication in the heart and is disrupted in DCM

After clarifying the heterogeneity of ECs and the differences in their differentiation trajectories, we further explored how the cardiac microenvironment supports the homeostasis of these cellular subpopulations. Given that pseudotime analysis revealed a distinct and independent functional state for LECs, we focused on identifying exogenous signals that are computationally predicted to regulate their functional maintenance. We performed a systematic computational analysis of the cellular communication network using CellChat. Communication heatmaps revealed that LECs maintain extensive signaling connections with various cell populations; however, the inferred communication frequency between LECs and other cells was significantly lower in the DCM group than in the control group (Fig. S10A-B). Next, we focused on the interactions among ECs, which maintain high-frequency communication (Fig. S10 C-D). Circle plots were employed to further visualize the number of interactions directed toward lymphatic ECs acting as receptors (Fig. 3A-B). The results demonstrated that when LECs functioned as receiving cells, their communication with other endothelial cells (arterial, capillary, and venous) decreased. Notably, this reduction was more pronounced in the crosstalk between LECs and arterial/capillary endothelial cells.

The classic VEGFC-vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 3 (VEGFR3) signaling axis is a key molecular mechanism that mediates physiological lymphatic function (Su et al. 2025). However, further analysis revealed that the predicted intensity of VEGF signaling communication between LECs and cells such as fibroblasts and CMs was weakened in the DCM group compared with that in the control group (Fig. 3C). Circle plot visualization further verified the atrophy of the VEGF signaling pathway, with a particularly significant downregulation of the interaction between LECs and VECs in the DCM group compared with that in the control group (Fig. 3D). Next, we used bubble plots to compare the ligand–receptor status between different ECs, and the results indicated that the intensity of VEGFC-VEGFR3 communication was reduced in the DCM group (Fig. 3E). In addition to the attenuation of VEGF signaling, these analyses revealed a striking reduction in semaphorin–plexin interactions. Specifically, the Sema6a–Plxna2 and Sema6a–Plxna4 signaling axes, which are directed from VECs to LECs, were significantly downregulated in the DCM group (Fig. 3E). To validate these findings at the molecular level, we analyzed the spatial expression distribution of the ligand Vegfc and the receptor Flt4 (VEGFR3). The results revealed that Vegfc was enriched primarily in fibroblasts and ECs, whereas Flt4 was highly and specifically expressed in LECs (Fig. 3F and H, Fig. S10E and Fig. S10G). This expression pattern supports a clear paracrine signaling model: VEGFC is secreted mainly by VECs and fibroblasts and subsequently acts on LECs that highly express VEGFR3. With respect to the communication patterns between endothelial cell populations, we identified two distinct modes from our bioinformatic analysis. The interaction between LECs and VECs primarily revolves around VEGF signaling input (incoming patterns). In the output mode (outgoing patterns), LECs exhibit a unique inferred communication profile that is focused mainly on SEMA3 and other signals, further reflecting the unique role played by LECs in different physiological and pathological processes (Fig. S11). Finally, violin plot analysis revealed significant suppression of Vegfc expression in DCM hearts (Fig. 3G and I), whereas Vegfr3 expression did not notably differ between the two groups (Fig. S10F and 10 H). We next assessed VEGF-C protein expression levels in the heart tissues of DCM mice by Western blot. VEGF‑C expression was decreased in the hearts of DCM mice compared with that in the hearts of control mice (Fig. S12). These results indicate that impairment of the “VEGFC-VEGFR3” signaling axis under diabetic conditions.

Characterization of LEC heterogeneity and metabolic reprogramming in diabetic hearts

To further elucidate the cellular landscape and functional heterogeneity of LECs in DCM, we performed unbiased clustering analysis on all LECs. LECs were divided into nine distinct subgroups (clusters 0–8) (Fig. 4A). The correlation map between cell subgroups shows the correlations among different subgroups (Fig. 4B). T-SNE plots for Ctrl and DCM separately were showed in Fig. 4C and D.We named each group on the basis of its top marker genes (Fig. 4E). We identified DEGs in each LEC subcluster between the DCM and control groups and performed GO enrichment analysis on these genes (Fig. S13). The results showed that Clusters 1 and 2 were highly enriched in pathways related to actomyosin organization” and mechanical stress adaptation, strongly suggesting that a subset of LECs undergo an endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition in response to microenvironmental stress. Clusters 0 and 3 exhibited immunomodulatory signatures characterized by the “positive regulation of neutrophil and leukocyte adhesion,” reflecting the active role of LECs in mediating cardiac inflammation. Strikingly, Cluster 4 was significantly enriched in the “negative regulation of VEGF signaling” and “endothelial differentiation.” Furthermore, Cluster 6 displayed a severe lipotoxic signature driven by “ceramide and sphingolipid biosynthetic processes.” The proportional distribution of these LEC subsets differed between hearts of control mice and those with diabetes (Fig.4F). The results showed a declining trend in the overall global proliferation index of total LECs under DCM conditions (Fig. S14A). We then calculated the proliferation index for each LEC subcluster. The proliferation index of Cluster 0 dramatically decreased from 52.6% in the control group to 39.5% in the DCM group. In contrast, although minor numerical fluctuations were observed in the proliferation indices of the other subclusters, none reached statistical significance (Fig. S14B). Considering the altered proliferation index and impaired VEGF-C signaling in LECs, we assessed cardiac lymphatic vessels via LYVE-1 staining, which revealed a decreased lymphatic vessel density in DCM tissues compared with that in control tissues (Fig. S15). Based on the expression of canonical markers (Fig.S16), these subpopulations were precisely classified into four major functional groups. Owing to the significantly high expression of Ccl21a, Reln, and Flt4, Clusters 0, 1, and 2 were identified as capillary LECs. Cluster 3 exhibited a mature collecting LEC phenotype, driven by the specific upregulation of Foxp2 expression. To elucidate the lineage relationships, we performed RNA velocity analysis (Fig. 4G) and constructed a PAGA velocity graph (Fig. 4H). SCENIC analysis revealed distinct TF activity among LEC subsets. The Flt1 + cluster (cluster 4) was governed primarily by Wt1, which is consistent with its proangiogenic character.

To identify the genes that regulate the function of LECs, we first analyzed the DEGs between the DCM group and the control group (Fig. 5A). A volcano plot revealed that PDK4 was significantly upregulated in the DCM group compared with the control group. The top 5 genes with the most significant differences are shown in Fig. 5B. Specifically, GSEA revealed marked upregulation of metabolic pathways, particularly those associated with fatty acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation and adipogenesis, in the DCM group, whereas pathways related to apical junction and angiogenesis were significantly inhibited (Fig. 5D–E and Fig. S19A–C). We subsequently used Western blotting to measure PDK4 expression levels in LECs. Western blot analysis confirmed that PDK4 protein levels were significantly increased in LECs treated with high glucose and palmitic acid (HG + PA) (Fig. 5F). Notably, high-glucose stimulation alone can also increase PDK4 expression in lymphatic endothelial cells (Fig. S20). Given that PDK4 serves as a critical metabolic regulator, we assessed both OCR and ECAR via Seahorse extracellular flux analysis. The HG + PA stimulation decreased the oxygen consumption rate (OCR), which was reversed by PDK4 inhibition (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, HG + PA decreased basal respiration and ATP production, which were relieved by PDK4 inhibition (Fig. 6B). Conversely, HG + PA increased the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), which was blocked by PDK4 inhibition (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the HG + PA stimulation increased glycolysis, glycolytic reserve, and glycolytic capacity, which were abrogated by PDK4 inhibition (Fig. 6D).

To further explore the function of PDK4, we used siRNA to successfully knockdown PDK4 expression in LECs (Fig. S21A). Scratch wound healing assays demonstrated that compared with normal control conditions, metabolic stress induced by HG + PA severely impaired the migration ability of LECs, while PDK4 silencing effectively restored this capacity (Fig. 6E). In addition, treatment with a PDK4 inhibitor also rescued the migration ability of lymphatic endothelial cells. To further confirm the role of PDK4, we overexpressed PDK4 protein in LECs using a PDK4 plasmid (Fig.S21A). Scratch assays revealed that PDK4 overexpression impaired the migration capacity of LECs even without HG + PA stimulation(Fig. 6F). In summary, our results suggest that PDK4 expression modulates the migratory function of LECs.

Discussion

There are still no effective treatment methods for DCM. Our study revealed the critical involvement of the cardiac lymphatic system in the pathogenesis of DCM. We used scRNA-seq to investigate the phenotypic characteristics of LECs in DCM. These findings provide novel insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of cardiac lymphatic vessels in this disease.

First, our findings demonstrate that the cardiac lymphatic system undergoes prominent pathological remodeling in DCM. Lymphatic vessels undergo compensatory lymphangiogenesis in pressure overload and ischemic HF models (Henri et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2021). However, in our DCM model, the proliferation index of LECs was reduced, indicating impaired proliferative capacity. These findings are consistent with recent evidence from an obese hypertension model (Guo et al. 2025), indicating that lymphatic dysfunction represents a primary driver of the early progression of diastolic dysfunction. Combined with the findings of previous studies (Itkin et al. 2021), this disruption of the lymphatic architecture impairs myocardial interstitial fluid clearance and thus contributes to progressive ventricular dysfunction.

The interstitial fibrosis and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy observed in DCM mice likely represent downstream consequences of defective lymphatic clearance. Notably, in myocardial infarction models, maladaptive lymphatic remodeling prolongs edema, whereas therapeutic lymphangiogenesis via VEGF-C restricts fibrosis and recovers cardiac function (Henri et al. 2016). Impaired lymphatic drainage leads to chronic myocardial edema and proinflammatory cytokine retention. This edematous and inflammatory microenvironment exerts mechanical stress and molecular cues that synergistically promote fibroblast-mediated collagen deposition and compensatory cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Brakenhielm et al. 2020). Consequently, edema and fibrotic stiffness severely impair cardiac function.

The preservation of lymphatic homeostasis relies on signaling networks derived from the surrounding microenvironment (Oliver et al. 2020). Emerging evidence has demonstrated that fibroblast‑derived VEGFC serves as a critical regulator during lymphangiogenesis (Wang et al. 2020). Using cell-cell communication analysis, we identified the core signaling networks responsible for maintaining the function of cardiac LECs. ScRNA sequencing data revealed that CFs and VECs represent the primary cellular sources of VEGFC, which mediate crosstalk with LECs via the canonical VEGFC‑FLT4 (VEGFR3) signaling axis. These findings further confirm the central regulatory role of VEGFC in cardiac lymphangiogenesis (Gancz et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2019). Importantly, the crucial signaling pathway, which is transmitted from VECs and fibroblasts to LECs, is markedly impaired in DCM. VEGFC signaling is a central regulator of the proliferation, migration, and survival of LECs (Klotz et al. 2015). Impairment of this signaling cascade in DCM consequently leads to defective lymphangiogenesis and compromised function in LECs. We postulate that such signaling deficiency arises from direct damage to VECs and fibroblasts induced by hyperglycemia and lipotoxicity, which in turn reduces their ability to secrete VEGFC. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that targeting the enhancement of VEGFC signaling to restore the myocardial lymphatic network is a promising therapeutic strategy for improving the prognosis of DCM. Furthermore, in addition to the attenuation of VEGFC signaling, we observed a significant downregulation of the Sema6a-Plxna2 and Sema6a-Plxna4 signaling axes directed from VECs to LECs. As Semaphorin-Plexin interactions are critical regulators of vascular and lymphatic network patterning, the loss of this intercellular crosstalk likely also contributes to lymphatic regression (Deng et al. 2026).

In addition to the impairment of exogenous signals, scRNA-seq analysis revealed significant metabolic reprogramming in LECs. Specifically, PDK4 expression was significantly upregulated in the LECs of DCM mice. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed abnormal activation of the oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) pathways, whereas critical functional pathways, including angiogenesis and cell junction integrity, were significantly suppressed. PDK4 is a key regulator of mitochondrial metabolism and inactivates the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex through phosphorylation, thereby preventing pyruvate from entering the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Atas et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2024). Owing to this metabolic switch, cells rely primarily on FAO rather than glucose oxidation for energy production (Zhou et al. 2025). Numerous studies have confirmed that PDK4 expression is elevated in DCM. Myocardial metabolism is characterized by decreased mitochondrial function, enhanced FAO dependence, and impaired glucose utilization (Ma et al. 2023; McCallinhart et al. 2025). We found that the high expression of PDK4 in LECs in DCM likely leads to the loss of metabolic flexibility. Although FAO is considered an important energy source for lymphangiogenesis (Wong et al. 2017), long-term and dysregulated metabolic reprogramming can induce excessive production of reactive oxygen species (Brakenhielm, et al. 2019; Peng et al. 2023). This in turn promotes oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cytoskeletal damage, ultimately impairing the migratory capacity of LECs.

In vitro experiments confirmed that LEC migration was impaired under high-glucose and high-lipid stress conditions. Transfection with small interfering RNA targeting PDK4 significantly restored the migratory capacity of LECs. These data demonstrate that PDK4-mediated metabolic dysfunction contributes to LEC impairment in hearts of mice with diabetes hearts. Therefore, PDK4 represents a novel therapeutic target for preserving cardiac lymphatic function in DCM.

Given that we observed both impaired VEGF-C signaling and upregulated PDK4 expression, we further investigated whether a direct regulatory hierarchy exists between these two critical events. Notably, our in vitro validation experiments revealed that stimulating LECs with exogenous VEGF‑C did not significantly alter PDK4 expression levels (Fig. S22). These findings indicate that the transcriptional suppression of VEGF-C in the surrounding cellular microenvironment and the PDK4-driven metabolic switch within LECs are independent pathological processes rather than components of a linear regulatory axis. These distinct mechanisms act synergistically to exacerbate pathological remodeling in DCM. This study had several limitations. First, the HFD/STZ model reflects a mixed T1D/T2D phenotype; therefore, our findings regarding PDK4-driven LEC dysfunction are primarily limited to advanced, severe glucolipotoxic states. Future studies using etiology‑specific models (such as STZ‑induced T1DM, db/db mice, or diet‑induced obesity models) are needed to determine whether these mechanisms are applicable to other diabetic conditions. Second, we used only male mice, which restricts our current mechanistic conclusions to male pathophysiology. Under similar metabolic stress conditions, female subjects are highly likely to exhibit sex‑dimorphic responses, potentially displaying delayed or attenuated LEC dysfunction. Future studies must incorporate both sexes and use etiology‑specific diabetic models to fully validate the universal applicability of the PDK4‑mediated pathogenic axis. Third, although cell communication analysis strongly suggested an important role for fibroblast‑derived VEGFC, its function still requires validation through cell‑specific knockout models. Currently, rescue experiments to assess lymphatic drainage and cardiac function following VEGFC supplementation and VEGFR3 agonist intervention are lacking. Fourth, regarding the metabolic regulatory role of PDK4 in LECs, validation using LEC‑specific knockout mouse models is currently lacking; this will be a key focus of our subsequent research. Additionally, our current findings are derived exclusively from murine models, and we have not yet evaluated the expression pattern of PDK4 in lymphatic vessels from human diabetic heart tissues. Validating this in clinical samples is necessary to establish the translational relevance of the PDK4-mediated pathogenic axis. Finally, the precise molecular mechanisms through which PDK4 contributes to LEC dysfunction must be elucidated. Metabolic intermediates, oxidative stress, and energy sensors are highly likely to participate in the regulation of cytoskeletal regulators during LEC migration. These will be key priorities in our future research.

In conclusion, this study provides a transcriptomic atlas of the cardiac microenvironment in DCM and reveals increased PDK4 protein expression in LECs during the pathogenesis of DCM. Furthermore, our results indicate that targeting the key metabolic molecule PDK4 represents a promising novel therapeutic strategy, potentially offering a breakthrough for treating this intractable cardiovascular disease.
