The Australia and New Zealand Diabetic and Ischaemic Foot Outcomes Study (ANZDIFOS)
Authors: Odette Hart, Philip Allan, Shirley Jansen, Robert Fitridge, Claire Murray, Mendel Baba, Prue Cowled, Neil McMillan, Manar Khashram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThere is limited high‐quality data on the factors that contribute to limb loss in patients with diabetes‐related foot disease (DFD) in New Zealand and Australia. Furthermore, wound, ischaemia and foot infection (WIfI) staging has not been validated in the Australasian cohort. The aim of this study was to (1) report on the presentation, management and outcomes of DFD (wound healing, major limb amputation [MLA], amputation‐free survival [AFS] and mortality at 1‐year), and (2) validate the association of WIfI stage on outcomes.MethodsThis was an observational prospective study of patients with DFD and a wound at three centres in New Zealand and Australia from 2020 to 2022. Data on presentation, management, amputation and mortality at 1‐year was collected. Outcomes were stratified by presenting WIfI stage. Wound healing and survival were assessed with Kaplan–Meier curves, time‐to‐healing with Fine‐Gray competing risks, AFS with logistic regression and survival with Cox proportional hazards.ResultsTwo hundred and seventy‐six patients (384 limbs) were recruited, with WIfI stage 1 (47.9%), 2 (21.1%), 3 (19.5%) and 4 (12.5%). At 1‐year, 71.4% of wounds healed, with WIfI stage associated with healing, p < 0.001. At 1‐year 16.6% of limbs had underwent a MLA, with the proportion increasing by WIfI stage. Poorer AFS was observed with increasing WIfI. Mortality at 1‐year increased with WIfI stage, p < 0.001.ConclusionThis study reports on the presentation, management and outcomes from DFD in New Zealand and Australia. WIfI staging appropriately classifies patients with DFD and correlates with healing, MLA, AFS and survival at 1‐year.Keywords: amputation, diabetes‐related foot disease, diabetes‐related foot ulcer, diabetes‐related foot wound, WIfI staging

Introduction

Diabetes‐related foot disease (DFD) is a frequent complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), and worldwide 40–60 million people with DM will experience DFD [1]. A diabetes‐related foot ulcer (DFU) occurs in one quarter of people with DM, which frequently leads to minor or major limb amputations (MLA) [2, 3]. Because 80% of MLA are preceded by a DFU, there is an opportunity for early clinical detection and management to prevent the physical, functional, psychological and financial impact of DFD and MLA on patients [2, 4, 5]. Moreover, early management could have considerable societal and financial benefit for health systems as a whole [4, 5]. Despite this, there is a paucity of high‐quality prospective research investigating the unique wound and patient factors that contribute to limb loss in patients with DFD in New Zealand and Australia.

There are many factors that are associated with the key DFD outcomes of healing, hospitalisation, amputation and mortality [6]; however objective data on these in the New Zealand and Australian context is limited. The international working group for the diabetic foot guidelines identifies eight risk factors associated with these outcomes: end‐stage renal failure, peripheral arterial disease (PAD), loss of protective sensation in the limb and DFU related factors of area, depth, location (forefoot/hindfoot), number (single/multiple) and infection [6]. There is also inconsistency or often a complete lack of wound staging in clinical practice and research, despite the Global Vascular Guidelines suggesting the use of wound, ischaemia, foot infection (WIfI) staging [7] and WIfI has not been validated in the Australasian cohort. Last, both New Zealand and Australian health bodies have advocated for a unified and collaborative approach towards addressing the above gaps in the local context [8, 9].

The aim of this study was to prospectively collect and report on the presentation, management, and outcomes of DFD at three major centres in New Zealand and Australia. The secondary aims were to determine and validate the association of WIfI stage on outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

This was an observational prospective study of patients presenting with DFD and a wound to three major centres (Waikato Hospital, New Zealand; Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital, Australia; and the Royal Adelaide Hospital, Australia) between August 2020 and May 2022. The protocol and methodology for this study have been previously published [10], and the study has been registered at the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12621000337875). The primary outcomes were WIfI stage at presentation, time to wound healing, MLA, amputation‐free survival (AFS) and overall mortality at 1‐year. The secondary outcomes were the predictors of wound healing and the influence of WIfI staging on MLA and mortality at 1‐year.

Inclusion criteria were patients with a diagnosis of DM and a foot wound, defined as ulceration, gangrene, or a non‐healing surgical wound. Exclusion criteria were a previous bilateral MLA, a palliative condition with a life expectancy < 12 months, a person that could not engage in follow‐up of at least 12 months, or an unwillingness or inability to consent.

Patients were recruited in a pragmatic and broad approach over a 1‐year period from multidisciplinary diabetic foot clinics, vascular clinic, dialysis unit and from hospital admissions, in order to encompass a wide range of presentations and severities. The duration of follow‐up was 1‐year, with patients assessed at baseline, 1, 3, 6 and 12 months. Clinical management was not standardised between centres in line with the observational nature of this study. Healing was defined as complete re‐epithelialisation of the previous tissue defect.

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the New Zealand Central Health and Disability Ethics Committee (20/CEN/122), Waikato DHB Research Department (RDO020044), Quality Improvement HoD Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital (39715) and the Central Adelaide Local Health Network (CALHN) Human Research Ethics Committee (13928). Reporting of this observational study followed the Strengthening of the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [11].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean (SD) for parametric data and median (IQR) for nonparametric data. Clinically important covariates such as age, sex, WIfI stage, renal disease and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels were selected a priori according to the published literature to be included in all adjusted regression models. Chi‐square was used to compare categorical variables, and independent samples t‐test was used to compare continuous variables. One‐year outcomes were stratified by the presenting WIfI stage with analysis for wounds healed (adjusted for the competing risk of death), AFS and survival. AFS was defined as freedom from a MLA and death at 1‐year. For time‐to‐event analysis (wound healing and mortality), Kaplan–Meier curves were performed at univariate levels to determine variables that might influence the outcome of interest. For the binary outcome of AFS, a logistic regression model was performed at a univariate level. Covariates entered into the final model were selected on clinical grounds (from literature) and statistically based (p < 0.05) and underwent forward and backward stepwise analysis. The time‐to‐healing within 1‐year used the Fine‐Gray competing risks model, adjusting for the competing risk of death to estimate the HR and 95% CI. Multivariate analysis of survival at 1‐year was assessed using the Cox proportional hazards model. Each limb was considered unique for the purpose of analysis, with the highest‐grade wound on each limb as defined by WIfI treated as the ‘index’ wound; outliers were kept, missing data was left out of analysis, and data was not adjusted.

Results

Demographics

The baseline demographics and characteristics are shown in Table 1. A total of 276 patients with DFD and a wound were recruited, with 116 in New Zealand and 160 in Australia. The median age was 67 (IQR 57–76) and 74.6% were men. The majority of patients (90.9%) had type 2 diabetes mellitus. Overall, there were 384 limbs with wounds. At presentation, 184/384 (47.9%) limbs were WIfI stage 1, 77/384 (20.1%) were WIfI stage 2, 75/384 (19.5%) were WIfI stage 3 and 48/384 (12.5%) were WIfI stage 4. Wound Healing

During 1‐year follow‐up, 274/384 (71.4%) of wounds healed. The presenting WIfI clinical stage was associated with wound healing at 1‐year (p < 0.001; Figure 1). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that the median time to healing for initial WIfI clinical stage 1 was 15 weeks (95% CI 12.3–17.7), stage 2 was 19 weeks (95% CI 15.5–24.5), stage 3 was 24 weeks (95% CI 17.4–30.6) and stage 4 was 33 weeks (95% CI 10.0–55.0; p < 0.001; Figure 2). Univariate analysis for wound healing demonstrated that a higher WIfI stage was associated with poorer healing at 1‐year (Table 2). Major Limb Amputation

At 1‐year there were 41/384 (16.6%) limbs that underwent a MLA. The proportion of MLA was higher as the WIfI stage increased, with 19/48 (39.6%) limbs with an initial WIfI Stage 4 undergoing MLA at 1 year (Figure 3). The breakdown between New Zealand and Australia is reported in Figure S2.

The univariate analysis for AFS demonstrated that a higher WIfI stage incurred greater MLA/death risk at 1‐year (Table 3). Compared to WIfI stage 1 patients, those with WIfI stage 2 limbs had 1.47 times greater risk of MLA/death (95% CI 0.64–3.37, p = 0.37), stage 3 had 5.85 times higher risk (95% CI 2.95–11.60, p < 0.001) and stage 4 had 16.37 times greater risk (95% CI 7.59–35.30, p < 0.001) at 1‐year.

There were 56 (14.6%) patients who died within one year. The proportion that died was higher as WIfI stage increased (stage 1: 16/184 [8.7%]; stage 2: 9/77 [11.7%]; stage 3: 17/75 [22.7%]; stage 4: 14/48 [29.2%]; p < 0.001; Figure 4). Discussion

This prospective bi‐national study from New Zealand and Australia reports the presentation, management and outcomes of DFD from three major centres. Our data was staged according to WIfI criteria with patients followed up for 1 year. We found that WIfI staging appropriately classifies patients with DFD as it accurately correlates with the overall proportion healed, time to healing, the proportion undergoing subsequent MLA, AFS, mortality and survival at 1 year. This supports the concept that healthcare professionals managing DFD should utilise WIfI during the assessment of patients to promptly identify wounds that are unlikely to heal spontaneously so these patients can be prioritised for advanced therapies.

Previous research examining the predictive performance of WIfI in patients with chronic limb threatening ischaemia (CLTI) and DFD demonstrated that increased WIfI clinical stage is associated with slower time to DFU healing and greater risk for MLA; however, many of these studies have been single‐centre, small or retrospective [12, 13]. This study has demonstrated a similar proportion of MLA within each WIfI clinical stage compared to other studies [12, 13]. In addition, Zhan [13] provided early work that WIfI was associated with MLA and wound healing, and the time to wound healing observed in each WIfI clinical stage was similar to this study.

Impact of Comorbidities

The duration since DM diagnosis and type of DM were not associated with healing, AFS, or survival. This is despite studies suggesting that a longer duration of DM is associated with advanced atherosclerotic plaque formation and increased risk of DFD complications [14, 15]. There was also a negligible association of glycaemic control (HbA1c at baseline) with outcomes of healing (HR 1.0, p = 0.70), AFS (OR 0.99, p = 0.03) and mortality (HR 0.98, p = 0.01). This mirrors some current evidence that strict glycaemic control has not been shown to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events such as stroke, myocardial infarction and cardiovascular death [16], but that it still benefits microvascular disease [17]. Personal history of cardiovascular disease was associated with poorer healing (HR 0.77, p = 0.03) and poorer AFS (HR 1.84, p = 0.018), but not mortality. This correlates with previous studies that demonstrate a greater propensity for MLA in patients with DM and coexisting cardiovascular conditions such as congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction and cerebrovascular disease [18].

Patients with PAD had poorer healing (HR 0.061, p < 0.001) and greater risk of MLA/death (poorer AFS) at one year (OR 3.95, p < 0.001). This aligns with a previous New Zealand study that has reported a greater propensity for MLA in patients with DFD who experience coexisting PAD (HR 12.72, 95% CI 10.62–15.24) [18], and with an international systematic review [19]. End‐stage renal failure was a strong risk factor for decreased AFS (OR 4.82, p < 0.001) and increased mortality (HR 5.06, p < 0.001), but was not related to wound healing. DM and end‐stage renal failure cause progressive atherosclerosis and vascular complications including an increased risk of developing PAD and DFU [20, 21, 22], with a 5.29 to 7.36 times greater risk of MLA for patients with both DM and end‐stage renal failure [18, 19, 22, 23]. Thus, early detection of renal impairment in patients with DM with annual renal function screening and routine foot examination is vital.

Limitations

There are limitations to this study. First, there is potential for selection bias given that patients were recruited from multiple hospital sources (multidisciplinary diabetic foot clinics, vascular clinic, dialysis unit and from hospital admissions) hence it was not possible to recruit patients with DFD consecutively across these sites. Patients that were admitted on weekends or required urgent surgery were also not recruited, potentially creating a tendency towards more stable positions. However, given the extensive and close follow‐up of patients that included phone calls and reviewing patients across multiple sites, there were no patients lost to follow‐up for one‐year outcomes. Second, although major tertiary centres were used and a wide range of wound severities were involved, the results cannot necessarily generalise to the wider population of each country given differences in population diversity between regions. Third, some parameters were not consistently available. For instance, 25.4% of patients had an unknown referral source, hence further analysis of related variables was not possible given the high amount of missing data. Last, variables that relied on questioning of patients, such as history of previous DFU, may be subject to recall bias by patients. To improve accuracy, medical records were thoroughly examined for previous admissions and clinic reviews for prior history of DFU or surgical treatment.

Conclusions

This was a multicentre binational prospective study of a large and diverse sample of patients with DFD. WIfI stage was associated with healing, AFS, and mortality. This work highlights the comorbidities and associated conditions that are important to DFD outcomes within the New Zealand and Australian populations, such as the presence of PAD and end‐stage renal failure. Early identification of patients with these risk factors and who are at risk of poor outcomes is important to improve DFD outcomes.
