Circulating fatty acids, genetic risk, and incident coronary artery disease: A prospective, longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Pan Zhuang, Xiaohui Liu, Yin Li, Yuqi Wu, Haoyu Li, Xuzhi Wan, Lange Zhang, Chengfu Xu, Jingjing Jiao, Yu Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The role of fatty acids (FAs) in primary prevention of coronary artery disease (CAD) is highly debated, and the modification effect by genetic risk profiles remains unclear. Here, we report the prospective associations of circulating FAs and genetic predisposition with CAD development in 101,367 U.K. Biobank participants. A total of 3719 CAD cases occurred during a mean follow-up of 11.5 years. Plasma monounsaturated FAs (MUFAs) were positively associated with risk of CAD, whereas the risk was significantly lower with higher n-3 polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs) and more reductions in risk were detected among TT carriers of rs174547. Furthermore, increased plasma saturated FAs (SFAs) and linoleic acid were related to a significant increase in CAD risk among participants with high genetic risk (genetic risk score > 90%). These findings suggest that individuals with high genetic risk need to reduce plasma SFAs levels for CAD prevention. Supplementation of n-3 PUFAs for CAD prevention may consider individuals' genetic makeup.

INTRODUCTION

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is considered to result from both genetic and lifestyle factors, including diet (1). Thereinto, dietary fatty acids (FAs) have been documented to modulate blood lipid levels, including triacylglycerols and cholesterols (2), which played a critical role in the traditional diet-heart hypothesis. Owing to the vast literature on the distinct effects of dietary fats on cardiovascular health, prevailing dietary guidelines generally recommend consuming foods high in unsaturated fats, especially polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs), and limiting saturated FA (SFA) intake below 10% of total energy to prevent cardiovascular disease (CVD) (3). However, these long-lasting beliefs have been challenged by meta-analyses showing no significant association between SFA intake and CVD risk (4, 5). Likewise, not all studies found a lower cardiovascular risk associated with higher intake of PUFAs (6, 7). Notably, although powerful cardioprotective actions of the n-3 PUFAs have been demonstrated in numerous studies, intervention studies yielded inconsistent results (8, 9). Great heterogeneity between previous studies may result from the error-prone FA intake assessment using self-report questionnaires or food records. In contrast, objectively assessed circulating FAs are not subject to recall bias and represent the combined effect of dietary intakes and biological processes in vivo (10), including absorption, synthesis, and metabolism. Thus, as the internal exposures to FAs, circulating FAs levels reflect the metabolic status of FAs. However, evidence on circulating FAs in relation to CAD development is lacking and inconsistent (7, 11).

The substantial and well-recognized within-population heterogeneity in response to FAs may also stem from genetic variation and interactions between FAs and genes. FAs can regulate gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms to positively or negatively affect metabolic outcomes. Therefore, understanding FA-gene interactions are essential to tailoring nutritional therapy to control CAD risk. Although some studies have indicated statistically significant interactions between FAs and certain genetic variants on coronary disease or cardiovascular risk factors (12, 13), none of the interactions have been robustly replicated, which might be due to the insufficient statistical power. In the past few years, large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWASs) (14, 15) have successfully identified >243 CAD risk loci that determine the genetic susceptibility to CAD and cardiovascular events (16). Integrating the number and effect sizes of these risk alleles to construct a genetic risk score (GRS) can continuously and quantitatively depict the genetic risk of CAD (16). Thus, GRSs are better suited than single risk alleles to investigate the interaction between FAs and the full genetic burden of CAD. However, whether the effects of FAs on CAD risk are modified by genetic risk of CAD remains unknown. Moreover, circulating FA levels are also determined by endogenous synthesis and metabolism (10). Notably, circulating SFAs and monounsaturated FAs (MUFAs) can also be endogenously synthesized using carbohydrates and alcohol through the de novo lipogenesis (DNL) pathway (17). Gene variants at key enzymes in the PUFA metabolism, such as FADS1/2 and ELOVL2, could influence tissue PUFA availability. So far, 16 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have been identified to be associated with specific circulating FAs in meta-analyses of GWASs (18–20). It was possible that these SNPs could interact with specific FAs on CAD development, whereas little evidence has been found (21). Overall, evaluating the interaction of circulating biomarkers of dietary FAs, such as n-3 PUFAs, with genetics on CAD risk could help develop personalized dietary intakes based on the genotype. Discovering the interaction of genetics with circulating SFAs and MUFAs could also advance the understanding of the pathophysiology of CAD and be informative for preventative approaches.

To address these major gaps in knowledge, we analyzed data from 101,367 individuals from the U.K. Biobank study to evaluate the associations of internal exposures to FAs (plasma FAs) with incident CAD; to test the potential interactions of specific plasma FAs with genetic risk of CAD, captured by a GRS; and to assess whether relations between plasma FA and CAD were modified by FA-associated variants. We hypothesized that circulating FAs interacted with GRS or FA-associated variants to affect the risk of developing CAD.

RESULTS

Population characteristics

Baseline risk characteristics of the study population according to quartiles of plasma FAs are summarized in Table 1. Participants who had higher plasma levels of SFAs and MUFAs were more often white, current smokers, and hypertensive patients and tended to have a higher body mass index (BMI) and lower education level. These participants also had lower household income and poorer diet and used vitamins and minerals less frequently. Individuals with higher plasma PUFAs were younger, more likely to be non-white and women, wealthier, more physically active, and had a lower BMI and higher education level. They were less likely to drink alcohol and smoke; had a healthier diet; took less aspirin, lipid-lowering, antihypertensive and hypoglycemic drugs but more vitamins and minerals; and had a lower prevalence of hypertension and hypercholesteremia. For blood biochemical indicators, higher plasma SFAs levels were related to higher serum concentrations of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), while higher plasma PUFAs levels were associated with higher serum high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and lower TG, TC, and LDL-C levels. There was a weak correlation between plasma SFAs and MUFAs (r = 0.26; table S5). Plasma concentrations of PUFAs had negative correlations with MUFAs (r = −0.86) and SFAs (r = −0.69). GRSs were similar across quartiles of plasma FAs. Spearman correlation coefficients between plasma FAs and dietary FAs are shown in table S6. Plasma MUFAs were weakly correlated with dietary MUFAs (r = 0.05; P < 0.001), while the correlation of plasma n-3 PUFAs with dietary n-3 PUFAs was stronger (r = 0.26; P < 0.001).

Relations between plasma FAs and incident CAD

Over 1,168,262 person-years of follow-up (an average of 11.5 years), we documented a total of 3719 participants with incident CAD. In the analyses adjusted only for age and sex, the plasma level of SFAs was positively associated with incident CAD (P trend < 0.001; Table 2). However, the positive relation was attenuated to null (P trend = 0.915; model 4) when we further accounted for lifestyle factors and other types of plasma FAs. The MUFA level in plasma was associated with a higher risk of CAD in all models (all P trend < 0.001; models 1 to 4). The multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) [95% confidence intervals (CIs)] across increasing quartiles of plasma MUFAs were 1.17 (1.02 to 1.33), 1.25 (1.07 to 1.46), and 1.42 (1.17 to 1.72). Plasma total PUFAs and n-6 PUFAs were not significantly associated with CAD risk in the fully adjusted model (P trend = 0.403 for PUFAs and 0.570 for n-6 PUFAs). Nonetheless, plasma n-3 PUFAs were significantly associated with reduced risk of CAD (P trend = 0.004). Compared with the first quartile, participants in the highest quartile of n-3 PUFAs had a 16% lower risk of CAD (HR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.75 to 0.95). Each SD increment in n-3 PUFAs was related to a 5% (1% to 9%) decreased CAD risk. In addition, plasma docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) (P trend = 0.003) and non–linoleic acid (LA) n-6 PUFAs (P trend < 0.001) also had inverse relationships with incident CAD (table S7). For each SD increment, multivariable-adjusted HRs (95% CIs) of CAD risk were 1.01 (0.98 to 1.04) for LA, 0.94 (0.89 to 1.00) for DHA, 0.92 (0.87 to 0.97) for non-LA n-6 PUFAs, and 1.00 (0.95 to 1.04) for non-DHA n-3 PUFAs. Increased n-6/n-3 ratio was related to higher CAD risk (P trend = 0.003). The dose-response relationships for these plasma FAs by restricted cubic spline regression (Fig. 1 and fig. S3) were similar to those from category analyses.

Interactions of plasma FAs with genetic risk of CAD

In analyses according to the percentile categories of GRS, increased plasma SFAs were related to a sharply increased risk of CAD among participants in high GRS groups (GRS > 90%; n = 9469) but not among those in the group with 0 to 90% GRS (P for interaction = 0.011). Per 1 SD increment in plasma SFAs were related to 3% (1 to 6%) and 37% (4 to 80%) increased risk of CAD for individuals with 90 to 95% GRS (n = 4735) and >95% GRS (n = 4734), respectively (Fig. 2 and table S8). Similarly, each SD increase in plasma LA was related to a 6% (3 to 9%) higher CAD risk among participants with high genetic risk of CAD (GRS > 90%) (P for interaction = 0.019; fig. S4). Other plasma FAs had no significant interactions with the GRS for CAD risk in category analyses (table S8).

Interactions between plasma FAs and FA-associated alleles on incident CAD

Regarding specific SNPs, we found a significant interaction between plasma n-3 PUFAs and variants at FA desaturase-1 (FADS1) rs174547 (P interaction = 0.039) (table S9). Specifically, increased plasma n-3 PUFAs were related to a significant reduction in CAD risk (HR for 1 SD increment: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.81 to 0.95) only among participants with T/T but not C/C and C/T genotypes (Fig. 2). In addition, there was a significant interaction between plasma LA and rs16966952 at N-terminal asparagine amidase-1 (NTAN1) (P interaction = 0.023), with a higher CAD risk associated with plasma LA detected among A allele carriers (fig. S4).

Subgroup analyses

The subgroup analyses showed that the positive relations between plasma MUFAs and incident CAD were stronger among individuals who were younger, had a lower BMI and did not smoke (table S10). Similar inverse relations between plasma n-3 PUFAs and CAD risk were detected across all subgroups (P for interaction > 0.05). In addition, the positive associations of n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio with CAD risk were more pronounced among smokers, individuals with hypercholesteremia, and those without vitamin supplementation.

Sensitivity analyses

The relationships between specific plasma FAs and CAD risk did not change substantially after further adjustment for antihypertensive and lipid-lowering medications, hypoglycemic drug use and insulin treatment, oral contraceptive or hormone replacement therapy, sleep pattern, or the overall diet quality (table S11). Results were also similar after excluding CAD cases that occurred within 2 years, patients with diabetes, persons with extreme BMIs (<18.5 or >40 kg/m2), or subjects with incomplete covariate data (table S11).

DISCUSSION

In this large prospective cohort, plasma levels of MUFAs were positively associated with future risk of CAD, whereas plasma n-3 PUFAs were inversely associated. Participants with high genetic risk had increased risk of CAD associated with increased plasma levels of SFAs and LA. In addition, the inverse relations of plasma n-3 PUFAs with CAD were modified by variants at FADS1 rs174547 (Fig. 3).

Large-scale gene-nutrient interaction analysis implicates genetic personalization of FAs for CAD prevention.

Analysis of data of 101,367 individuals from U.K. biobank cohort reveals significant interactions of circulating FAs with genetic risk profiles on CAD risk, supporting genetic personalization of dietary strategies for improving plasma FAs profiles in the primary prevention of CAD. SNPs, single-nucleotide polymorphism; LA, linoleic acid; DNL, de novo lipogenesis.

Given that SFAs are correlated with elevated serum total and LDL cholesterols, which may cause atherosclerosis (22), lowering SFA consumption is widely recommended to reduce heart disease. Yet, recent meta-analyses of cohort studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (5–7, 23) failed to demonstrate a significant association of dietary or circulating SFAs with CVD risk with great heterogeneity between studies. Our study also did not observe a significant association of plasma SFAs with CAD risk. However, when considering the genetic background, we found that participants with a high genetic risk, especially those with extremely high genetic risk (GRS > 95%), had a sharp increase in CAD risk associated with increased SFAs. Consistently, intake of SFAs has been reported to increase the risk of CVD among a high-risk population, including those with a family history of coronary disease (24). For putative mechanisms, SFAs have been shown to regulate transcription factors (PGC-1β, SREBPs, and LXR/RXR) to influence expressions of genes involved in cholesterol and triacylglycerol biosynthesis, including LDL receptor (25), and induce myocardial inflammatory injuries via TLR4 activation (26). In humans, a previous study reported that SFA intake was related to higher LDL cholesterol levels only among homozygotes for the APOC3-455T-625T alleles (27). In addition, the effect of APOE polymorphism on coronary heart disease (CHD) risk was modified by SFA consumption in a nested case-control study (28). Together, these data suggest that genetic stratification is needed to select high-risk populations susceptible to the harmful effect of SFAs. However, circulating SFAs levels could also be affected by endogenous synthesis. Excess dietary carbohydrates and alcohol could enhance the production of SFAs by the DNL pathway. Individuals with extremely high genetic risk of CAD may take all measures to lower the plasma SFA levels, including reducing the dietary intake of SFAs, carbohydrates, and alcohol, for CAD prevention.

Associations between dietary MUFAs and CVD outcomes have been controversial, which could be explained by the different food sources of MUFAs. A higher risk of CVD linked with dietary MUFAs from animal sources has been documented by previous studies, whereas a lower risk was observed for increased intake of plant-sourced MUFAs (29). Because MUFA is highly correlated with SFAs and other detrimental compounds in animal sources, the positive association of MUFAs from animal sources with CVD outcomes is likely explained by the confounding of these factors. Regarding circulating levels of MUFAs, a recent study reported positive associations of circulating MUFAs, including oleic acid and palmitoleic acid, with CVD risk (30), which was consistent with our findings. High circulating concentrations of MUFAs may be detrimental for atherogenesis due to the lipotoxic and adipogenic effects (31). In addition, elevated plasma MUFAs levels may also be contributed by endogenous synthesis by DNL pathway. Because DNL has been evidenced to increase intrahepatic fat, this process may also contribute to elevated CVD risk (17). Dietary PUFAs, primary LA, in the prevention of CVD is still highly debated. Meta-analyses of clinical trials concluded that increasing n-6 PUFAs was unlikely to reduce coronary events (23, 32), with the evidence being of very low quality. Actually, the protective results seemed to be detected in RCTs using mixed n-3/n-6 PUFA diets but not n-6 PUFA–specific diets (33). In our study, plasma n-6 PUFAs were not related to the risk of CAD, whereas a significant increase in risk related to plasma LA was observed among participants with a high genetic risk. The literature has shown that oxidized LA contained in LDL could produce harmful oxidized LA metabolites, which activate innate immune cells and thereby induce atherosclerosis (34). LA was also evidenced to suppress ABCA1 expression and attenuate HDL-mediated cholesterol efflux in macrophages, which may accelerate cholesterol accumulation in the artery wall (35). Therefore, high plasma LA levels may exacerbate the predisposition of subjects with high genetic risk to increased risk of CAD. In contrast, we found that plasma non-LA PUFAs were associated with a lower CAD risk, which was supported by a previous meta-analysis demonstrating a 17% lower risk of coronary events for arachidonic acid (AA) but no significant associations for circulating LA (7). A recent pooled analysis of 30 prospective studies also reported an inverse association of circulating biomarkers of AA with CVD incidence (36). Although AA is considered as the precursor to potentially pro-inflammatory leukotrienes, it could also produce key anti-inflammatory metabolites, such as prostaglandin E2, epoxyeicosatrienoic acids, and lipoxin A4 (37). Therefore, more large studies on clinical events are needed to investigate the health effects of AA due to its complex biologic effects.

Although cohort studies reported protective associations of dietary intake of n-3 PUFAs with cardiovascular outcomes (38), RCTs yielded mixed results (8, 9). Although a large RCT (n = 25,871) reported that n-3 PUFA supplementation (1 g/day) for a median 5.3 years did not reduce major cardiovascular events, the incidence of CVD was significantly lower (HR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.67 to 0.98) among participants with low fish consumption (<1.5 servings/week) (39). Compared with RCTs using n-3 PUFA supplements, using circulating biomarkers could better represent bioavailable n-3 PUFA intake. In accordance with our findings, biomarkers of n-3 PUFAs—including α-linolenic acid (ALA), docosapentaenoic acid, (DPA), and DHA—were associated with a lower risk of fatal CHD in a pooled analysis of 19 cohorts (40). A recent Mendelian randomization study found that genetically predicted eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) levels were associated with lower CAD risk (41), whereas little variance (<5%) of EPA levels were explained by selected SNPs, and whether genetic profiles could modify the effects of n-3 PUFAs in CAD risk was not tested. Here, we found no evidence of interaction between GRS and plasma n-3 PUFAs, indicating that increasing n-3 PUFA intake should be recommended regardless of individuals’ genetic risk. However, the reductions in risk were more accentuated among participants carrying more T allele of FADS1 rs174547. The T allele carriers had a higher genetically determined desaturase activity of delta 5 desaturase (42) involved in the synthesis of long-chain n-3 PUFAs from essential plant-derived ALA and thus may have higher availability of long-chain n-3 PUFAs, which are considered to confer more potent cardioprotective effects (9). In addition, previous studies have indicated that dietary n-3 PUFAs could interact with FADS1 variation to affect blood lipids (21). The C-allele of rs174547 was related to a lower blood LDL level only among participants with low intake of n-3 PUFAs (43). Consistent with our finding, in a case-control study of 440 CAD patients and 838 controls, increased dietary marine n-3 PUFAs seemed to attenuate the positive relation of rs174547 polymorphism with CAD risk (44). Together, genetic variants at FADS1 rs174547 may be considered when setting n-3 PUFAs recommendations. Individuals who are homozygous for T allele could be more likely to derive a net benefit from the intake of n-3 PUFAs for CAD prevention.

In our study, participants with and without a family history of CVD had similar diet quality scores and plasma FA levels. The associations of plasma FAs with CAD were also not modified by family history of CVD. Therefore, considering significant interactions for GRS detected in our analysis, the genetic risk may be more useful than family history for detecting populations susceptible to FA exposure. We also observed that the correlations of plasma levels of SFAs, MUFAs, and PUFAs with dietary intakes were low. Because plasma levels of some FAs were determined by both dietary intakes and endogenous synthesis, our findings cannot be interpreted as entirely due to dietary intakes of FAs. Typically, SFAs and MUFAs are also endogenous products of the DNL pathway. Measures to lower these FAs levels from the DNL pathway may include reducing intakes of refined carbohydrates and alcohol and increasing n-3 PUFA intake (17). In addition, modifications in lifestyle behaviors, such as increasing physical activity and weight loss, could also inhibit the hepatic DNL pathway (17). In contrast, marine n-3 PUFAs, including EPA and DHA, were evidenced in dose-response studies to be good biomarkers of dietary marine n-3 PUFAs (10). Therefore, our findings for plasma n-3 PUFAs, especially for DHA, may be informative for dietary recommendations.

This analysis has important strengths, including the use of objectively measured plasma levels of FAs, large sample size, and long follow-up duration. We are the first to investigate whether the associations of FAs with CAD were modified by overall genetic risk. Our main results were robust, indicated by several sensitivity analyses. Potential limitations deserve attention. First, because our study was not randomized controlled, unmeasured or residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out. Specifically, higher plasma n-3 PUFAs seemed to be indicative of a healthier lifestyle; hence, we adjusted for extensive lifestyle factors in our multivariable models. Notably, increased plasma n-3 PUFAs were related to lower CAD risk among both individuals with healthier and unhealthier lifestyles, indicating that the protective associations were independent of healthy lifestyles. Second, FAs were measured once at baseline, and potential changes over time were not taken into consideration. However, this potential misclassification would attenuate the real associations toward the null. Third, the relationships for some subtypes of plasma FAs (e.g., individual SFAs, EPA, DPA, and AA) were not analyzed because of the lack of available data. Fourth, our results were not corrected for multiple testing because findings were hypothesis generating. Last, although our study had a large sample, the included participants were all of the European ancestry, and thus, our findings may not be immediately generalized to other ethnic groups. More large studies are needed to replicate our detected interactions.

In conclusion, higher plasma MUFAs were associated with a higher risk of CAD. Increased plasma levels of SFAs and LA were related to increased CAD risk among individuals with high genetic predisposition to CAD. Plasma n-3 PUFA levels were inversely related to CAD risk, and these protective relations were stronger among rs174547 T/T carriers. Our results suggest that individuals with high genetic risk of CAD may reduce plasma SFA levels for CAD prevention via comprehensive approaches, including decreasing intakes of SFAs, carbohydrates, and alcohol. Dietary supplementation of n-3 PUFAs for CAD prevention may consider individuals' genetic makeup. More researches using biomarkers of FAs and a large sample size of population-wide biobanks are required to collaborate our findings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study cohort

The design and methodology of the U.K. Biobank have been described in detail elsewhere (45). Between 2006 and 2010, a total of 503,317 individuals aged 37 to 73 years in England, Wales, and Scotland consented to participate in the U.K. Biobank study. At 22 assessment centers, they completed touch screen questionnaires, underwent a series of physical examinations, and also provided biological samples. All participants provided written informed consent. The U.K. Biobank study was approved by the National Health Service North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (06/MRE08/65).

We restricted our analyses to 117,876 participants who had measured plasma FAs at baseline. Then, we excluded individuals who subsequently withdrew from the U.K. biobank study, patients with a history of CVD or cancer at baseline, so that the remaining sample consisted of 101,367 persons for the analysis of the associations of plasma FAs with incident CAD (fig. S1). To further test the potential FA-gene interactions on risk of CAD, we restricted our analysis to 94,693 participants who had genetic data and were of white British descent.

Genotyping and calculation of GRS

The genotyping procedures, imputation, and data cleaning have been reported previously (46). We derived a weighted GRS for CAD risk using 300 SNPs that passed quality control and were identified to be associated with CAD in previous GWASs (table S1) (14–16). The GRS for CAD risk was computed by a weighting method (47). A value ranging from 0 to 2 was given for each SNP to indicate the number of CAD-increasing alleles. Then, the number of risk alleles at each variant was summed and weighted by the respective CAD effect sizes. This GRS ranged from 191.7 to 255.7 (fig. S2) and has been validated to be robustly associated with CAD (16). SNPs associated with specific circulating levels of FAs at genome-wide significance (18–20) were also selected (table S2).

Plasma FA ascertainment

Detailed descriptions of plasma FA measurements and quality control can be found at https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/label.cgi?id=220. Briefly, about 120,000 plasma samples were randomly selected from the baseline collection (2006–2010), and plasma FA concentrations were measured by a high-throughput nuclear magnetic resonance–based metabolic biomarker profiling platform (Nightingale Health Ltd.), which is known for the absence of batch effects and high repeatability over time (https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/refer.cgi?id=3001). Total FAs, SFAs, MUFAs, PUFAs, n-3 PUFAs, n-6 PUFAs, LA, and DHA were measured. On the basis of available data, we analyzed plasma concentrations of SFAs, MUFAs, PUFAs, n-3 PUFAs, n-6 PUFAs, DHA, non-DHA n-3 PUFAs, LA, and non-LA n-6 PUFAs as exposures in our study. The biological stability of the biomarker has been validated in a repeat assessment (n = 5000) in 2012–2013. The correlation efficient between plasma FAs measured at baseline and those measured at the repeat assessment ranged from 0.5 to 0.6.

Covariates

Data on the frequency of food consumption were collected via a validated touchscreen questionnaire. We constructed a diet quality score based on the recent definition of ideal consumption of dietary components for CVD to represent the overall diet quality (table S3) (48). The physical activity level was assessed by the metabolic equivalent of task (MET), which was calculated according to the International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form. The Townsend deprivation index (TDI) was calculated as a measure for socioeconomic status. Data on other potential confounders and mediators were also collected, including ethnicity, gender, weight and height, household income, education level, smoking, alcohol drinking, sleeping, disease history, medications, and dietary supplements.

CAD ascertainment

We leveraged all available sources, including self-reported data for diseases and operations, cumulative hospital records from the Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) data available from 1997 to December 2020, and death registry data to define CAD cases. Self-reported heart attack/myocardial infarction and operations—including coronary angioplasty with or without stent insertion, coronary artery bypass grafts, and triple heart bypass—were defined as CAD cases. International Classification of Diseases (ICD)–9 and ICD-10 were used to record diagnosis information in the HES data. Death registries include both primary and secondary causes of death coded in ICD-10. Classification of Interventions and Procedures (version 4) was used to code operative procedures. Detailed codes used to define prevalent and incident CAD are shown in table S4 (16).

Statistical analysis

All plasma levels of FAs were reported as percentages of total FAs and were categorized into quartiles or analyzed continuously per 1 SD increase. Multivariable Cox regression models were applied to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for CAD, with follow-up from the date of attending the baseline assessment center to the date of incident CAD, lost to follow-up, death, or the end of follow-up (31 December 2020). Schoenfeld residuals were used to check proportional hazard assumption. Known or suspected confounders were considered according to previous literature and biological plausibility. The first model was adjusted for age and sex. The second model was further adjusted for centers, ethnicity, education, TDI, household income, physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, history of hypertension, hypercholesteremia and diabetes, family history of CVD, mineral supplementation, vitamin supplementation, and aspirin use. The third model was further adjusted for BMI. The fourth model was additionally adjusted for remaining plasma FAs (SFAs, MUFAs, PUFAs, n-6 PUFAs, and n-3 PUFAs) where appropriate. Missing indicator categories were used for missing covariate data. Potential semiparametric relationships were examined by restricted cubic spline regressions.

The associations of plasma FAs with CAD across percentile categories (49) of GRS for CAD risk or genotypes of FA-associated SNPs were further analyzed to detect the modification effect. The potential interaction between GRS for CAD risk and specific plasma FAs on the risk of developing CAD was tested by adding a multiplicative interaction term (FA × GRS) in our models adjusting for all the above-mentioned covariates (model 4) except for family history of CVD. We also examined the potential interactions between plasma FAs and individual FA-associated SNPs by including a multiplicative interaction term.

We conducted subgroup analyses to test potential modification effects by baseline characteristics. In sensitivity analyses, we further control the influence of antihypertensive and lipid-lowering medications, hypoglycemic drug use and insulin treatment, oral contraceptive or hormone replacement therapy, sleep pattern (50), or the overall diet quality. In other sensitivity analyses, we further excluded participants who developed CAD within the first 2 years to minimize the influence of reverse causation and further excluded persons with prevalent diabetes or those with extreme BMIs (<18.5 or >40 kg/m2) at baseline to see whether the findings remain constant. Last, we restricted our analyses to participants with complete covariate data.

Because all statistical tests were based on priori hypotheses, we did not adjust for multiple testing. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
