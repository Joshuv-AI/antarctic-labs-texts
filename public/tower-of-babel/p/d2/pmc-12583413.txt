Prescription and self-reported use of endocrine therapy among Black hormone receptor-positive breast cancer survivors in the Detroit Research on Cancer Survivors cohort
Authors: Abigail M Fielder, Seongho Kim, Julie J Ruterbusch, Cydnie Martin, Anna Gottschlich, Ann G Schwartz, Jennifer L Beebe-Dimmer, Hadeel Assad, Lauren Hamel, Kristen S Purrington
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBlack women with hormone receptor-positive (HR +) breast cancer are twice as likely as White women to have weakly HR + tumors (1–10% positive cells). Patients with weakly HR + tumors are less frequently prescribed ET and have 60% higher mortality than strongly HR + tumors (> 10% positive cells). We evaluated factors associated with ET prescription and self-reported use among Black women with HR + breast cancer.MethodsAmong 922 Detroit ROCS participants, we evaluated associations between demographics, socioeconomic status, and health, tumor, oncologist, and hospital characteristics and ET prescription intent and self-reported ET use. Logistic mixed-effects regression was used to account for oncologist and hospital group effects.ResultsOncologists intended to prescribe ET to 83.4% of participants (n = 769), of which 54.4% (n = 502) reported use. In multivariable models, participants with weakly HR + tumors were 90% less likely to be prescribed ET (OR = 0.10, p < 0.0001). Other significant characteristics of ET prescription included a BMI of 25–29.9 kg/m2 (OR = 0.45, p = 0.0085), HR positivity > 90% vs. 11–90% (OR = 0.37, p = 0.00045), unknown HR percentage (OR = 0.12, p < 0.0001), OncotypeDx testing (OR = 2.65, p < 0.0001), and receiving radiation (OR = 2.20, p = 0.00016). Self-reported ET use was lower among those with lower health literacy (OR = 0.017, p < 0.001), weak HR positivity (OR = 0.46, p = 0.0053), unknown HR percentage (OR = 0.074, p = 0.034), and older age at diagnosis (OR = 0.88, p = 0.002). Increased ET use was associated with an income between $60,000-$79,900 vs. < $20,000 (OR = 1.54, p = 0.035), higher comorbidity count (OR = 1.09, p = 0.0054), distant stage (OR = 2.03, p = 0.029), and surgery (OR = 2.35, p = 0.001).ConclusionIdentifying multilevel factors related to ET use may inform strategies to improve ET uptake and survival among Black women with HR + breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10549-025-07816-1.Keywords: Racial disparities, Endocrine therapy, Adherence, African American, Prescription

Introduction

Black women in the United States are 50% more likely to die from hormone receptor-positive (HR +), HER2-negative breast cancer than White women, even when accounting for factors including tumor stage and other clinical characteristics [1–4]. While lack of access to high quality healthcare is certainly a driving factor in this disparity, there is also evidence for underlying racial differences in tumor biology among HR +/HER2-negative breast tumors [5, 6]. We have shown that Black women are about twice as likely to have HR +/HER2-negative tumors with lower levels of HR staining (weakly HR + : 1–10% of cells staining positive for estrogen receptor and/or progesterone receptor) compared to White women, and women with weakly HR + tumors have approximately two-fold higher breast cancer mortality compared to women with strongly HR + tumors [7]. Importantly, we and others have shown that women with weakly HR + tumors who are prescribed endocrine therapy (ET) have substantially improved survival compared to those that were not prescribed ET [7, 8], yet these women were also ~ 20–30% less likely to be prescribed ET by their physicians [7, 8].

Breast cancer management requires patients and physicians to navigate a complex array of treatment options including ET, which is pivotal for those diagnosed with HR + breast cancer particularly for preventing breast cancer recurrence [9]. Clinical guidelines universally recommend ET when HR expression is detected in at least 1% of tumor cells. However, the decision becomes more nuanced for women with weakly HR + tumors as these patients might benefit from additional discussions on the comparative risks of ET and potential benefit of chemotherapy. In practice, treatment choices for weakly HR + cancers seem to align more closely with HR-negative protocols, excluding ET in favor of chemotherapy, which points to a gap between guideline recommendations and clinical practice [10]. This is particularly impactful for Black women who are most likely to have weakly HR + tumors and also underlines the need to address specific biological and systemic factors affecting treatment for all Black women with HR + breast cancer.

Adherence to ET is another critical factor influencing outcomes [11–13]. Adherence to ET can be difficult to assess at both the clinical and research levels but has been shown to be troublingly low, with a recent systematic review estimating 5-year adherence at 66% [14]. Adherence rates may be especially low among Black women and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, likely exacerbating disparities in breast cancer outcomes in these populations [15–17]. The reasons for discontinuing ET are varied and likely population- and context-specific, ranging from its prolonged use to adverse effects like hot flashes, fatigue, and more severe complications such as osteoporosis [18, 19]. The landscape of treatment decisions from the perspectives of both prescription and adherence is further complicated by disparities in healthcare access and quality. Black women, for instance, are less likely to receive optimal cancer treatments including surgical interventions and adjuvant therapies [20–22]. While factors such as limited health literacy and less access to experienced physicians and quality medical resources [21, 23–27] have been associated with suboptimal cancer care, a better understanding of the barriers to receiving and adhering to ET among Black women is critical so that we can identify areas for culturally appropriate intervention to improve the equity of care of an underserved patient population.

The goal of this analysis was to explore and identify multilevel factors—including demographic, socioeconomic, and health factors at the individual level, tumor and clinical characteristics, and physician and treating hospital characteristics—associated with prescription and self-reported use of ET among Black women with invasive HR +/HER2- breast cancer. We utilized survey data from the Detroit Research on Cancer Survivors cohort, one of the largest studies conducted exclusively among Black cancer survivors in metropolitan Detroit—linked with the Metropolitan Detroit Cancer Surveillance System registry, the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System (NPPES) and the American Hospital Association (AHA)—to model these relationships, hypothesizing that unique sets of multilevel associations with prescription and self-reported use of ET in this population.

Materials and methods

Study population

We included data from female participants with invasive breast cancer enrolled in the Detroit Research on Cancer Survivors (ROCS) cohort study of Black cancer survivors, which has been described previously [28, 29]. Eligible Detroit ROCS cases between the ages of 20 and 79 were identified through the Metropolitan Detroit Cancer Surveillance System (MDCSS), and included first primary invasive female breast, colorectal, lung, and prostate cancers diagnosed on or after January 1, 2013, and endometrial and other young onset (age at diagnosis ≥ 20 and ≤ 49) cancer cases diagnosed on or after January 1, 2016. First primary status was determined by the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program’s Multiple Primary Coding Rules [30]. Individuals with prior cancer diagnoses, in situ disease, or bilateral disease were not excluded. All participants self-identified as Black or African American. A total of 5069 survivors were enrolled into Detroit ROCS, which included completion of a baseline questionnaire with annual follow-up to ascertain and update medical history, health-related quality of life, health behaviors, and treatment information. Participants complete questionnaires either online, over the phone with a trained interviewer, or by returning a written copy. For this analysis, we included all hormone receptor-positive (HR + : estrogen receptor [ER] and/or progesterone receptor [PR] positive) breast cancer ROCS cases for whom percent ER and PR could be abstracted from pathology reports, resulting in a final sample size of 922 women. The eligibility criteria and selection process for the analytic cohort are summarized in Fig. 1. The Wayne State University Institutional Review Board approved this study (WSU IRB#050417M1F), and informed consent was obtained from all subjects prior to participation.

Patient and tumor-level variables and covariate data

Participants self-reported demographic, socioeconomic, and health status variables at enrollment, including education, marital status, annual household income, health insurance status at diagnosis, health literacy confidence, comorbidities, and smoking history prior to diagnosis on enrollment questionnaires. Body mass index (BMI; body weight in kg/height in meters squared) was calculated from self-reported height and weight one year before cancer diagnosis. The percentage of cells staining positive for ER and PR was abstracted from pathology reports. Using the maximum percent staining value among the two hormone receptors, we categorized tumors as weakly HR + (1–10%), moderately HR + (11–90%), and strongly HR + (> 90%). Clinical characteristics including HER2 status, stage at diagnosis using the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) summary staging system, OncotypeDx testing status and score, and first course of treatment (defined as receipt of therapy for a cancer diagnosis before disease progression or recurrence including surgery), adjuvant chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and radiation therapy were obtained via MDCSS registry linkage. Surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy variables used standard SEER coding conventions and were further categorized into binary “yes/no” variables based on indications of whether the patient was scheduled or prescribed that therapy.

Hospital and physician variables

Primary treating physicians were identified by National Provider Identifier (NPI) using MDCSS data and validated by reviewing source documents in the electronic registry database (SEER*DMS). When more than one physician across multiple specialties were identified, medical oncologist was selected, and if no medical oncologist was listed, then surgical oncologist was selected. Physician data were obtained from the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System (NPPES) Monthly Downloadable File, downloaded from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services website (cms.gov) on September 26, 2023. Physician variables included physician credentials, gender, specialty taxonomy, whether they were the sole proprietor of their practice, and the number of years in practice. Primary treating hospital was coded using a unique identifier and linked to data from the American Hospital Association (AHA) to obtain characteristics including hospital size (number of inpatient beds), presence of a resident training program, and profit vs. non-profit status.

Endocrine therapy variables

ET data were obtained from two sources—MDCSS and self-report on participant questionnaires—to derive two separate variables corresponding to physician intent to prescribe ET and patient-reported use of ET. MDCSS ET data were abstracted from medical records and coded by trained abstractors using North American Association of Central Cancer Registry (NAACR) standard procedures. This includes information from both structured data fields and provider documentation. These provider notes are not part of an open notes system but are derived from the clinical documentation available in the MDCSS medical records. MDCSS treatment data capture only first course of therapy as recorded at the time of initial treatment. Therefore, it does not account for subsequent treatment decisions, changes in prescribing, or therapies initiated later in the patient’s care. MDCSS-derived ET data were operationalized to reflect physician intent to prescribe, categorized as “No” only when there was no indication of physician recommendation for prescription. Intent to prescribe ET was defined as “Yes” when physicians prescribed ET or recommended ET but patients did not take it due to refusal or contraindications. Patient-reported use of ET was obtained from enrollment and yearly follow-up questionnaires using responses to the questions “Have you received hormone therapy (in any form) to treat your breast cancer” (enrollment survey), “In the past year have you received hormone therapy (in any form) to treat your breast cancer?” (Year 1 follow-up survey), or “Since the last survey, have you received hormone therapy (in any form) to treat your breast cancer?” (follow-up surveys in Year 2 and beyond). Enrollment survey ET data were supplemented by reviewing the reported current prescription medication list, where responses corresponded to known ET medications.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using R software v4.3.2 (https://cran.r-project.org/). A nominal two-sided alpha value of 0.05 was set to determine statistical significance. Physician intent to prescribe endocrine therapy (ET) was categorized as a binary variable based on data from the first course of treatment. Self-reported use of ET was categorized as a binary variable at each time point for which data were available in enrollment or follow-up questionnaires.

Associations between study factors and outcomes were assessed using logistic regression models. Statistical significance for individual independent variables in logistic regression models were assessed based on the Walds test statistic. For univariate analyses (Tables 1, 2, 4, and 5), univariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationship between individual patient, physician, or hospital factors and the outcomes of interest. Associations between physician intent to prescribe ET and individual (1) patient and tumor-level characteristics were assessed using simple logistic regression with the “glm” function and (2) using logistic mixed-effects regression with the “glmer” function for physician and hospital characteristics, accounting for clustering within physician and hospital, respectively.

Results

Physician intent to prescribe endocrine therapy

Our final analytic sample included 922 Detroit ROCS HR + participants with invasive breast cancer. Median follow-up time was 3 years (range 1–6 years). Among all cases, the majority (83.4%) were recommended to receive ET by their physicians, while just over half (54.4%) reported using ET across the follow-up period. Table 1 details univariable associations between demographic and health status characteristics regarding physician intent to prescribe ET. Patients classified as overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2) were significantly less likely to be recommended or prescribed ET than those under 25 kg/m2 (OR = 0.45, 95% CI 0.25–0.82), while no associations were observed for patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. With respect to clinical and treatment variables, patients with tumors showing 1–10% and 11–90% HR staining were 91% (OR = 0.09, 95% CI 0.05–0.18) and 63% (OR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.22–0.63) less likely to be recommended or prescribed ET, respectively, compared to those with > 90% HR staining, while those with unknown HR staining percentages also had a reduced likelihood (OR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.08–0.19). Women with regionally staged tumors had a 1.5-times (95% CI 1.02–2.21) higher likelihood of ET recommendation or prescription compared to women with localized tumors. Interestingly, patients who did not undergo OncotypeDx scoring were less likely to be recommended or prescribed ET compared to those with a low risk OncotypeDx score (OR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.20–0.60). Patients undergoing radiation (OR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.36–2.79) and surgery (OR = 1.76, 95% CI 0.99–3.14) were more likely to be recommended or prescribed ET, although surgery was only marginally statistically significant. Among physician and hospital characteristics (Table 2), only specialty (Surgery vs. Medical oncology: OR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.06–0.24) and years in practice (OR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.63–0.89) were associated with ET.

In a multivariable model of physician intent to prescribe ET (Table 3), only patient- and tumor-level characteristics remained associated with the outcome. Patients classified as overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2) were significantly less likely to be prescribed ET than those under 25 kg/m2 (OR = 0.41, 95% CI 0.21–0.80). Patients with tumors characterized as 1–10% (OR = 0.10, 95% CI 0.05–0.20), 11–90% (OR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.21–0.65), and unknown (OR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.07–0.19) HR staining were significantly less likely to receive ET compared to those with greater than 90% HR staining. Additionally, those diagnosed with regional breast cancer were 1.79 times (95% CI 1.16–2.77) more likely to be prescribed ET than those with locally staged tumors. Women who had OncotypeDx testing (OR = 2.65, 95% CI 1.66–4.22) and who were treated with radiation (OR = 2.20, 95% CI 1.46–3.31) were also more likely to be recommended or prescribed ET.

Self-reported use of endocrine therapy

We next examined individual relationships between individual-, physician-, and hospital-level variables and the self-reported use of ET among all participants (Tables 4, 5, 6) and among the subset whose physicians intended to prescribe ET at the time of first treatment (Tables S1–S3). Among all participants (Table 4), we observed that individuals aged over 50 were less inclined to report the use of ET compared to those under 50 years old (50–59 years: OR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.51–1.02; 60–69 years: OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.50–0.99; 70–79 years: OR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.42–1.03). Moreover, individuals with some college education (OR = 1.56, 95% CI 1.02–2.39) or a professional/graduate-level education (OR = 1.63, 95% CI 1.02–2.60) were more likely to report ET usage than those with less than a high school education. Similarly, patients with annual incomes exceeding $60,000 reported higher ET usage compared to those with less than $20,000 annual income ($60–79.9 k: OR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.16–2.53; $80 k + : OR = 1.64, 95% CI 1.16–2.32). Notably, reports of ET usage significantly decreased as self-reported health literacy decreased. Specifically, patients who were not at all confident were 76% less likely to report ET usage compared to those who were extremely confident (OR = 0.24, 95% CI 0.10–0.57). Results among the subset whose physicians intended to prescribe ET at the time of first treatment were largely similar (Table S1), except the magnitude and statistical significance of some associations differed. Age trends were similar but only statistically significant for the oldest compared to the youngest age group (70–79 years: OR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.52–1.07). Education and health literacy effects were consistent, while income effects were slightly attenuated for the $60–79.9 k (OR = 1.51, 95% CI 0.99–2.29) and $80 k + (OR = 1.29, 95% CI 0.89–1.88) groups.

Health status and clinical characteristics associated with reports of ET use were then explored. Among all participants, we found that body mass index (BMI) as a continuous variable showed a significant association with reports of ET use where a 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI corresponded to a 3% (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04) increase in likelihood of reporting ET use. Furthermore, ever smokers were less likely to report ET usage than never smokers (OR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.58–0.99). Women with tumors that were either weakly HR positive or had unknown HR percent were significantly less likely to report ET usage (weakly HR + : OR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.28–0.80; Unknown: OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.54–0.93) than women with strongly HR + tumors. Conversely, women who had undergone surgery (OR = 1.89, 95% CI 1.18–3.02) or radiation therapy (OR = 1.31, 95% CI 1.03–1.66) were more likely to report ET usage than women who did not have those treatments. In the subset analysis (Table S1), BMI, tumor, and treatment effects were largely consistent with the overall analysis. Across credential type, gender, specialty, sole proprietorship status, hospital bed size, presence of residency training programs, and hospital class, no variables reached statistical significance in either the overall or the subset analyses (Table 5, Table S2).

Multivariable analyses were then conducted to evaluate associations with self-reported ET use among all participants (Table 5) and the subset whose physicians intended to prescribe ET at the time of first treatment (Table S3). Among all participants, as age of diagnosis increased, patients were less likely to report ET use (OR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.95). Consistent with our univariate analyses, self-reported health literacy was linked to differences in reported ET use, most notably for those who reported having no confidence at all (OR = 0.17, 95% CI 0.06–0.48) compared to those with high confidence levels. Patients who were"somewhat"or"quite a bit"confident were also less likely to report ET use compared to those who were extremely confident (OR = 0.61 and 0.74, respectively). Patients with an income between $60 k and $79.9 k were more likely to report ET use compared to those with an income less than $20 k (OR = 1.54, 95% CI 1.03–2.31). Although not statistically significant, patients with an income greater than $80 k were also more likely to report ET use (OR = 1.40, 95% CI 0.97–2.03). Increased reports of ET use were also associated with increasing number of comorbidities (OR = 1.09, 95% CI 1.03–1.17), distant compared to local cancer stage (OR = 2.03, 95% CI 1.08–3.83), and having surgery (OR = 2.35, 95% CI 1.38–3.99). HR staining once again showed importance as patients with tumors that stained 1–10% HR (OR = 0.46, p = 0.0053) and those with unknown HR percent staining (OR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.55–0.98) were significantly less likely to report ET use compared to patients with tumors staining greater than 90%.

In the multivariable model among the subset whose physicians intended to prescribe ET at first treatment (Table S3), age at diagnosis showed a similar inverse association with ET use as in Table 6 but did not reach statistical significance and income dropped out of the model entirely. Health literacy patterns were consistent, although the “quite a bit” confident group did not reach statistical significance (OR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.59–1.02). Number of comorbidities (OR = 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.15), stage 3 vs. stage 1 (OR = 2.94, 95% CI 1.52–5.69), and surgery (OR = 2.57, 95% CI 1.45–4.56) were significantly associated with higher ET use in both analyses. ER/PR staining showed weaker associations in Table S3 with 1–10% vs. > 90% (OR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.36–1.22, p = 0.187), 11–90% vs. > 90% (OR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.59–1.04, p = 0.090), and unknown vs. > 90% (OR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.56–1.05, p = 0.093) compared to the overall analyses.

Discussion

The goal of this analysis was to improve our understanding of the various factors influencing both prescription and self-reported use of ET among HR + breast cancer patients within a population-based cohort of Black breast cancer survivors. Our findings are novel, as we evaluated two related outcomes—physician intent to prescribe ET and patient adherence to ET—among Black women. Additionally, unlike previous studies that often focus exclusively on patient or physician and hospital-based variables, our study distinctly integrates both aspects. By analyzing demographic, clinical, and treatment-related variables alongside physician and hospital characteristics, we offer a comprehensive evaluation of potential determinants of ET prescription and usage.

We found that physicians were less likely to intend to prescribe ET to women with tumors exhibiting 1–10%, 11–90%, and unknown HR staining compared to those with over 90% HR staining. Patients with weakly HR + tumors or unknown HR staining percentages were also much less likely to report using ET compared to those with tumors that have over strongly HR + staining. This is consistent with previous research showing that clinicians are less inclined to prescribe ET for weakly positive breast cancer [7, 10], despite evidence-based guidelines recommending ET for all patients with ER + breast cancerl [18]. Clinicians may perceive weakly HR + breast cancer to be less sensitive to ET, contributing to their reluctance to prescribe ET for this group, particularly given potentially significant side effects such as hot flashes, fatigue, and bone density loss [18, 19]. This, however, is in contrast to national guidelines as well as findings from recent studies showing that women with lower levels of HR staining who receive ET have survival rates comparable to women with strongly HR + tumors [7, 8].

Other tumor characteristics were also associated with prescription and use of ET, namely OncotypeDx testing and tumor stage. Physicians use OncotypeDx testing to inform decisions about adjuvant chemotherapy treatment [31], but the test is not directly linked to adjuvant ET decision-making outside of the fact that recurrence scores inherently reflect estrogen signaling levels [32]. However, we found that undergoing OncotypeDx testing was associated with a substantial increase in the likelihood of physician intent to prescribe ET. This could be indicative of other factors related to medical eligibility for systemic therapy in general such as poor general health and fitness at the time of treatment, which could simultaneously disqualify a patient for chemotherapy and endocrine therapy [33]. While ET prescription rates were high overall regardless of stage (80–87%), we found a significant increase among patients diagnosed at regional compared to local stages. This may indicate that potential adverse side effects of ET are more heavily weighted in early-stage breast cancer cases, which are less likely to recur compared to tumors with lymph node involvement [34]. In contrast, however, women diagnosed with distant stage tumors were twice as likely to report using ET, possibly because ET can be a critical component of extending life expectancy and quality at this incurable stage [35].

We observed a non-linear association between BMI and physician intent to prescribe ET, where only overweight patients were significantly less likely to be prescribed ET compared to normal weight, which may be reflecting the fact that overweight and obesity have complicated relationships with breast cancer outcomes. These patients are at higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease and diabetes, and there is evidence for increased risks of developing these co-morbidities after using ET [36]. However, obesity is associated with worse disease-specific and overall survival, which would argue for more aggressive treatment including the use of ET [37]. There is also the issue that ET does not adequately suppress serum estrogen levels in obese patients, increasing risk of recurrence, although there is evidence that anti-estrogens are more effective than aromatase inhibitors [38–41].

Our findings also highlight a significant relationship between health literacy and self-reported use of ET, where individuals with low health literacy demonstrated markedly lower use of ET compared to their highly confident counterparts, particularly among those who were not at all confident. Health literacy has been shown to be lower in minoritized populations, which is related to issues of structural racism, socioeconomic status, resource availability, medical discrimination, poor communication and mistrust, and provider biases [42–44]. Previously studies have shown that health literacy is lower in minoritized populations, which can lead to a variety of health outcomes among breast cancer patients including developing chronic conditions, poor self-management and medication adherence, increased hospitalization, and worse quality of life [45–48].

This study has important limitations to consider. Our analysis relied on self-reported endocrine therapy (ET) use rather than insurance claims or pharmacy data, which were not available. Additionally, we were only able to evaluate physician intent to prescribe ET at the first course of treatment because of limited access to complete medical records and reliance on MDCSS registry data.

Although under-reporting of ET use is a recognized limitation of population-based cancer registries, our use of both registry-derived physician intent to prescribe ET and patient self-reports likely mitigated this concern. This approach is supported by prior research demonstrating a high degree of agreement between self-reported ET use and medical records, with one study reporting strong concordance. (kappa = 0.94) [49].

The timing of certain variables, such as patient trust, also raises the possibility of reverse causality. As surveys were administered after diagnosis and, for many participants, after completion of first-course treatment, reported trust may have been shaped by treatment experiences rather than solely reflecting pre-existing attitudes.

The absence of data on the race or ethnicity of treating physicians limited our ability to explore the potential influence of patient-physician racial concordance. Including this information in future research could provide important insights into how physician characteristics interact with patient experiences and treatment outcomes. [49] A major strength of this study is the utilization of data from the population-based Detroit ROCS cohort, covering patients from the tri-county area of Detroit, Michigan. This minimizes selection bias compared to hospital-based studies, and ensures that our findings are based on a diverse and representative population that enhances the generalizability of the study’s conclusions to a broader population of Black breast cancer patients. Detroit ROCS also conducts yearly follow-up among its participants, allowing us to evaluate use of ET at multiple timepoints and account for changes in ET use over time. Conducting this study among a population of Black cancer patients, who are most likely to experience adverse outcomes, and who remain underrepresented in biomedical research, is also a major strength.

The goal of this exploratory analysis was to enhance understanding of the factors influencing ET prescription and self-reported use among Black breast cancer survivors with HR + disease. The study identified several clinical and patient-level characteristics that were associated with these outcomes. Our study highlights a significant gap between the recommendation and use of ET among these patients and patterns that warrant confirmation and further investigation in future studies. There is evidence indicating that early discontinuation and non-adherence to endocrine treatment is linked to increased mortality among breast cancer patients [11, 50] while ET is known to reduce the risk of breast cancer recurrence and overall mortality [50, 51], these side effects can significantly impact a patient’s quality of life and lead to non-adherence. Therefore, the development of targeted, multilevel interventions that address both clinical and patient-level barriers will be critical to increase guideline-concordant prescription and use of ET. Further research is warranted to develop interventions and ensure they are effectively integrated into standard HR + breast cancer care.

Our study provides valuable insights into the factors influencing both physician intent to prescribe and patient-reported use of endocrine therapy (ET) among Black breast cancer survivors within the Detroit ROCS cohort. Findings show that clinicians are less likely to prescribe ET to patients with lower levels of hormone receptor (HR) positivity, even though guidelines recommend ET for all HR + cases. Patients with lower HR staining (1–10% and 11–90%) or unknown HR staining were significantly less likely to receive an ET recommendation compared to those with higher HR positivity. Additionally, women with regionally staged tumors had a higher likelihood of receiving an ET recommendation or prescription than those with localized tumors.

In terms of patient-reported ET usage, women with weakly HR-positive tumors or unknown HR staining were notably less likely to report using ET compared to those with strongly HR-positive tumors. This discrepancy in ET usage highlights how patient demographics, health literacy, and socioeconomic factors impact adherence to ET, emphasizing the critical role of health literacy in reported treatment continuation.

These findings are significant as they reveal potential disparities in ET treatment practices and adherence among Black breast cancer survivors. Identifying the specific clinical and patient-level factors associated with ET prescription versus self-reported use can guide the development of multilevel interventions to increase ET uptake, ultimately aiming to improve survival outcomes for Black women with HR + breast cancer. Addressing these disparities can help to create a more equitable standard of breast cancer care and support better long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.
