Quantitative analysis of representation asymmetry in human cadaveric white matter dissection literature
Authors: Aysegul Esen Aydin, Seckin Aydin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human cadaveric white matter dissection studies constitute a fundamental component of microsurgical neuroanatomy. However, whether individual white matter pathways are represented uniformly across the cadaveric dissection literature has not been systematically quantified. This study aimed to construct a tract-level anatomical dataset and to examine how major white matter pathways are represented across published human cadaveric dissection studies, with particular attention to fiber-class differences and potential methodological sources of uneven representation. A literature-based quantitative anatomical analysis was conducted using published human cadaveric cerebral white matter dissection studies identified through a structured study selection process. Twenty predefined major white matter pathways were classified into association, projection, and commissural fiber systems. Tract demonstration status was extracted and organized into a standardized representation matrix. Tract-level prevalence, fiber-class differences, study-level asymmetry, effect size estimates, and inequality-based representation metrics were analyzed using nonparametric statistical methods. Forty-two studies, encompassing 691 cerebral hemispheres, were included. Tract representation varied considerably across anatomically distinct fiber systems. Association pathways demonstrated greater overall representation frequencies compared with commissural systems, while nonparametric analyses confirmed significant differences among fiber classes (Kruskal–Wallis H = 9.27, p = 0.0097). The inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus demonstrated the highest overall representation prevalence, whereas deep projection and smaller commissural pathways, particularly the auditory radiation, posterior commissure, and habenular commissure, exhibited markedly limited representation. The present study shows that tract representation in the cadaveric white matter dissection literature is uneven and appears to be shaped by both anatomical organization and methodological factors, favoring superficially accessible pathways. These findings provide a novel quantitative framework for evaluating representation patterns, methodological bias, and tract-level variability within microsurgical white matter dissection research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-60280-9.

Introduction

White matter consists of myelinated axonal pathways that establish structural connectivity between different cerebral regions1. These pathways are traditionally categorized into association, projection, and commissural fiber systems2,3. Association fibers interconnect cortical regions within the same hemisphere, projection fibers connect cortical and subcortical structures, and commissural fibers provide interhemispheric connectivity.

Human cadaveric white matter dissection using the Klingler technique remains one of the principal methodologies for investigating the three-dimensional organization of cerebral fiber systems4,5. Compared with animal-based preparations, human cadaveric dissections provide substantially greater anatomical accuracy and direct microsurgical relevance. In recent years, these anatomical investigations have increasingly been complemented by diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) tractography, allowing closer correlation between microsurgical dissection findings and radiological connectivity analyses6.

Despite the increasing number of human cadaveric white matter dissection studies, the extent to which individual fiber pathways are consistently represented across the anatomical literature has not been systematically quantified. In particular, potential differences in tract representation related to fiber-class anatomy, dissection accessibility, and methodological orientation remain poorly characterized.

In the present study, we constructed a structured tract-level anatomical dataset derived from published human cadaveric white matter dissection studies and performed a quantitative analysis of representation patterns across cerebral white matter pathways. We evaluated tract prevalence, hemispheric sampling density, and fiber-class distribution to investigate representation asymmetry, methodological trends, and systematically underrepresented components within the cadaveric white matter dissection literature.

Materials and methods

Study strategy

This study was designed as a structured quantitative anatomical analysis of published human cadaveric white matter dissection studies. The primary objective was to systematically evaluate representation patterns of major cerebral white matter pathways across the cadaveric dissection literature and to quantitatively assess differences in tract representation among association, projection, and commissural fiber systems.

Rather than serving as a purely descriptive literature summary, the study aimed to construct a standardized tract-level anatomical dataset enabling quantitative evaluation of representational asymmetry, hemispheric sampling distribution, and methodological variability across published dissection studies. By integrating tract prevalence, study-level tract diversity, and fiber-class distribution patterns, the study sought to identify systematic trends and potential representation biases within the existing body of microsurgical neuroanatomical literature.

Accordingly, the study was structured as a literature-based quantitative synthesis and analytical modeling framework focused on tract representation patterns in human cadaveric white matter dissections. Because the study did not involve pooled clinical outcomes, intervention-based comparisons, or effect-size aggregation, formal meta-analysis methodology was not considered methodologically appropriate.

Identification of studies and data sources

Although a PRISMA-based framework was used to ensure transparent study identification and selection, the present study was not designed as a systematic review or meta-analysis. Instead, it represents a descriptive, quantitative anatomical analysis of published cadaveric white matter dissection studies. Cadaveric studies focusing on white-matter fiber dissections were identified through database searches performed in MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, and Web of Science. The search was restricted to English-language articles reporting white matter dissections of human cadaveric brains published up to January 1, 2025 (last search date), and duplicate records across databases were removed prior to screening. During the search process, a structured combination of keywords was used to enhance the identification of relevant studies. These included terms related to white matter anatomy (“white matter,” “fiber tract,” “white matter tract,” “white matter pathway”), dissection methodology (“cadaveric,” “fiber dissection,” “Klingler”), and neuroanatomical context (“neuroanatomy,” “microsurgical anatomy”). To improve search sensitivity and reduce the likelihood of missing relevant studies, additional terms reflecting connectivity-based terminology (“white matter connectivity,” “brain connectivity”) and imaging-supported anatomical studies (“tractography,” “diffusion tensor imaging,” “DTI”) were also incorporated. The search strategy was adapted as needed for each database while maintaining consistency in the core search concepts and was designed to identify studies combining cadaveric dissection with anatomical or tractographic validation, while excluding studies based solely on imaging without dissection to maintain methodological consistency.

In addition, the reference lists of retrieved articles were reviewed to identify additional relevant studies for inclusion in the dataset, and backward citation searching was performed to identify additional eligible studies. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were evaluated independently by two investigators, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus. The study selection process was conducted as per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting framework, and a PRISMA flow diagram was used to illustrate the identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion of studies in the final quantitative anatomical dataset.

For each included study, the reporting status of commissural and association tracts (demonstrated or not demonstrated during dissection) was systematically documented, and a structured representation matrix reflecting tracts identification across the included studies was constructed. Tract identifications were independently reviewed by two investigators, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus.

A structured dataset was generated by manually extracting and standardizing data from the included studies. Each study was coded for tract representation, number of hemispheres, and study-level characteristics. The resulting dataset was organized into a binary presence/absence matrix for each tract across all included studies. The full dataset is provided as a supplementary Excel file to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and supplementary quantitative analyses were conducted to evaluate representational imbalance and tract-level distribution patterns within the cadaveric white matter dissection literature.

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, interquartile range (IQR), coefficient of variation (CV), and prevalence percentages, were calculated to summarize tract representation frequencies and hemispheric sampling characteristics across studies. To assess differences in tract representation among the three predefined fiber classes (association, projection, and commissural fibers), nonparametric statistical methods were applied because of the non-normal distribution and tied structure of the dataset. Overall group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. When statistically significant differences were identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. To further evaluate within-study representation asymmetry across fiber classes, a Friedman test was performed using study-level prevalence distributions, followed by calculation of Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (Kendall’s W) as an effect size measure. To quantify representational imbalance across white matter pathways, coefficient of variation (CV) and Gini coefficient analyses were performed. The Gini coefficient was used as a quantitative indicator of inequality in tract representation across the anatomical literature.

Correlation analyses were additionally performed to evaluate potential relationships between publication year, hemispheric sample size, and tract diversity across studies. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used because of the nonparametric structure of the data. Effect size estimation for the Kruskal–Wallis analysis was calculated using epsilon-squared (ε2). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Data visualization was performed using frequency distribution plots, heatmap-based tract representation matrices, and box-and-whisker plots to illustrate variability and representational heterogeneity among fiber classes and across included studies.

A structured quantitative anatomical dataset was generated from 42 eligible human cadaveric white matter dissection studies, encompassing a total of 691 cerebral hemispheres. Across these studies, 20 predefined major cerebral white matter pathways were analyzed and classified into association, projection, and commissural fiber systems. The dataset enabled tract-level and study-level quantitative analyses of hemispheric sampling distribution, fiber-class variability, and white matter tract representation across the cadaveric dissection literature. The analyzed studies collectively demonstrated substantial variability in tract representation patterns and hemispheric sampling density. Tract-level representation patterns

Tract representation prevalence was calculated as the proportion of cerebral hemispheres in which each white matter pathway was identified relative to the total number of hemispheres included in the dataset (n = 691). Considerable variability in tract representation was observed across anatomically distinct fiber systems.

Among association fibers, the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF) demonstrated the highest overall representation, being identified in 484 hemispheres (70.04%) across 29 studies. U-fibers and the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) also showed high representation frequencies, identified in 483 hemispheres (69.89%) and 456 hemispheres (65.99%), respectively. In contrast, the middle longitudinal fasciculus (MdLF) demonstrated substantially lower representation, being identified in 254 hemispheres (36.75%) despite its anatomical proximity to other frequently reported long association pathways.

Projection fibers demonstrated comparatively lower overall representation frequencies. The optic radiation (OR) was the most frequently identified projection pathway, represented in 350 hemispheres (50.65%) across 20 studies. Conversely, the auditory radiation (AR) demonstrated the lowest tract representation among all evaluated pathways, being identified in only 35 hemispheres (5.06%) across two studies. Thalamic radiations (TR) also exhibited relatively limited representation, identified in 200 hemispheres (28.94%).

Among commissural fibers, the corpus callosum (CC) was the most consistently represented commissural pathway, identified in 328 hemispheres (47.46%) across 20 studies. In contrast, midline commissural structures, including the posterior commissure (PC) and habenular commissure (HC), demonstrated markedly limited representation, each being identified in only 30 hemispheres (4.34%). Similarly, the commissure of the fornix (CF) demonstrated low overall representation, being identified in 60 hemispheres (8.68%).

Overall, tract-level analyses demonstrated a consistent predominance of superficially accessible association pathways, whereas deeper projection fibers and anatomically restricted commissural systems showed substantially lower representation frequencies throughout the cadaveric dissection literature (Table 2).

Fiber-class comparison and effect size analysis

Comparative quantitative analysis demonstrated substantial differences in tract representation among the three predefined white matter fiber classes (Fig. 3). Association fibers showed the highest overall representation, with a mean prevalence of 56.7% ± 12.5%, followed by projection fibers (35.1% ± 18.7%) and commissural fibers (20.4% ± 21.5%).

Fig. 3Distribution of tract representation across association, projection, and commissural fiber classes. Association fibers demonstrated higher median representation prevalence and lower dispersion compared with commissural pathways.

Distribution of tract representation across association, projection, and commissural fiber classes. Association fibers demonstrated higher median representation prevalence and lower dispersion compared with commissural pathways.

Nonparametric comparison using the Kruskal–Wallis H test demonstrated a statistically significant difference in tract representation among the fiber classes (H = 9.27, p = 0.0097). Effect size analysis yielded an epsilon-squared (ε2) value of 0.427, indicating a moderate-to-large effect size and suggesting that fiber-class category substantially influenced tract representation patterns across the literature.

Post hoc pairwise comparisons using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction demonstrated significantly greater representation of association fibers compared with commissural fibers (adjusted p = 0.013). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between association and projection fibers (adjusted p = 0.145) or between projection and commissural fibers (adjusted p = 1.000) after correction for multiple comparisons.

Median representation values and interquartile ranges additionally demonstrated progressive reduction in tract representation from association to projection and commissural systems, further supporting the presence of systematic representational asymmetry across anatomically distinct white matter fiber classes (Table 3).

Study-level representation asymmetry

To further evaluate whether representation differences among fiber classes were consistently observed across individual studies, study-level prevalence distributions were analyzed using repeated-measures nonparametric testing.

Friedman analysis demonstrated a statistically significant within-study difference in representation patterns among association, projection, and commissural fiber systems (χ2 = 28.57, p < 0.000001). Effect size estimation using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance demonstrated moderate agreement across studies (Kendall’s W = 0.340), indicating that the observed representational asymmetry was not attributable to isolated studies but instead reflected a recurrent pattern throughout the literature.

Across the majority of included studies, association fibers consistently demonstrated greater representation compared with projection and commissural systems. In contrast, commissural pathways showed the lowest overall representation frequencies, particularly in studies employing predominantly lateral-to-medial dissection strategies.

These findings indicate that tract representation patterns within the cadaveric white matter dissection literature are not randomly distributed but instead demonstrate reproducible study-level asymmetry favoring anatomically superficial and surgically accessible fiber systems.

Representation imbalance and inequality metrics

Substantial heterogeneity in tract representation was observed across the analyzed white matter pathways. The mean overall tract representation prevalence across all evaluated fibers was 37.4% ± 19.3%, indicating marked variability in anatomical coverage throughout the cadaveric dissection literature. Quantitative assessment of representational imbalance demonstrated a coefficient of variation (CV) of 0.515, reflecting moderate-to-high variability in tract representation frequencies. In addition, inequality analysis using the Gini coefficient yielded a value of 0.277, indicating a non-uniform distribution of tract representation across the literature and supporting the presence of systematic representational imbalance among cerebral white matter pathways (Fig. 4).

Notably, anatomically related tracts frequently demonstrated markedly different representation frequencies despite close spatial proximity and partially overlapping anatomical trajectories. For example, the IFOF demonstrated substantially greater representation than the MdLF among association pathways, whereas the optic radiation (OR) demonstrated markedly greater representation than the auditory radiation (AR) among projection systems. Similarly, the corpus callosum (CC) showed considerably greater representation than other commissural pathways, particularly the posterior commissure (PC) and habenular commissure (HC).

Together, these findings indicate that tract representation in cadaveric dissection studies is influenced not only by anatomical anatomy, but also by technical and methodological factors. Methodological accessibility, dissection orientation, and technical visualization constraints additionally appear to influence representation of specific fiber systems.

Correlation and trend analyses

Correlation analyses were performed to evaluate whether tract representation diversity was associated with publication year or hemispheric sampling size across the included studies. No statistically significant correlation was identified between publication year and tract diversity score (Spearman’s ρ = 0.142, p = 0.368), suggesting that the breadth of tract representation has not substantially increased over time despite recent advances in microsurgical neuroanatomical techniques and tractography-supported anatomical studies.

Similarly, no significant association was observed between the total number of dissected hemispheres and tract diversity score (Spearman’s ρ = 0.239, p = 0.128). These findings indicate that larger hemispheric sample sizes alone did not necessarily result in broader tract representation within individual studies. Collectively, these analyses suggest that representational variability across the cadaveric white matter dissection literature is more strongly influenced by study-specific methodological focus and dissection strategy than by publication period or sample size alone.

Discussion

The present study provides a quantitative evaluation of tract representation across the human cadaveric white matter dissection literature and identifies clear differences in how anatomically distinct fiber systems are represented across published studies. Association pathways were represented more frequently than projection and commissural systems across most included studies, suggesting a recurring imbalance in tract representation across published studies. Inequality analyses additionally indicated that certain anatomically accessible pathways are represented disproportionately more often than deeper or technically less accessible fiber systems.

Previous studies have primarily emphasized the technical principles, procedural refinements, and educational value of Klingler-based white matter dissection, with comparatively limited attention to cumulative tract-level representation patterns across the published literature47. By constructing a standardized tract-level dataset, the present study moves beyond descriptive anatomical reporting and provides a quantitative framework for identifying representation imbalance, methodological trends, and underrepresented components within cadaveric white matter dissection studies.

The predominance of association fibers represents one of the principal findings of the present analysis. This pattern likely reflects the combined influence of anatomical accessibility and commonly employed dissection trajectories. In lateral-to-medial Klingler-based dissections, superficial cortico–cortical association systems are typically encountered early following cortical and subcortical gray matter removal. U-fibers, which form short cortico–cortical association pathways immediately beneath the cortical mantle, are therefore more readily exposed and consistently documented than deeper projection or midline commissural systems48,49.

The predominance of association pathways likely reflects technical and anatomical accessibility rather than anatomical importance alone. Instead, tract accessibility, dissection orientation, and ease of microsurgical exposure appear to strongly influence how individual pathways are reported across cadaveric dissection studies48,49.

Although the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) and arcuate fasciculus (AF) are frequently grouped together as components of the “SLF complex” in anatomical and tractographic studies, increasing evidence supports their consideration as anatomically and functionally distinct pathways. In the present study, the SLF and AF were therefore analyzed separately to preserve tract-specific representation patterns and avoid obscuring differences potentially related to dissection accessibility, fiber orientation, and methodological focus across studies. This distinction is particularly relevant in cadaveric white matter dissection studies, where tract depth, spatial orientation, and separability substantially influence visualization and reporting frequency50–53.

The marked disparity between representation frequencies of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF) and middle longitudinal fasciculus (MdLF) further supports the presence of representation imbalance within the literature. Although these pathways occupy partially adjacent anatomical regions within the temporo-insular white matter, the IFOF demonstrated markedly greater representation throughout the analyzed studies. This difference likely reflects technical and spatial factors influencing tract exposure during lateral-to-medial dissections. The IFOF forms a relatively compact long association system that is frequently encountered during opercular and insular dissections, whereas the MdLF demonstrates a thinner and less consistently distinguishable anatomical configuration, often requiring more selective dissection strategies for reliable identification54,55. In addition, the MdLF represents a comparatively recently characterized white matter pathway, and relatively few cadaveric studies have specifically focused on its microsurgical anatomy. This limited methodological emphasis may further contribute to its lower reporting prevalence across published studies56.

Thalamic radiations (TR) demonstrated comparatively limited representation throughout the analyzed literature. This finding likely reflects the deep anatomical location and complex spatial organization of these projection systems. TR fibers course medially between the internal capsule (IC) and corona radiata (CR) and demonstrate substantial intermingling with adjacent projection pathways, including the auditory and optic radiations20,45.

Because most studies included in the present analysis employed lateral-to-medial dissection strategies, deeper medially located TR fibers may have been less consistently exposed and anatomically differentiated during white matter preparation. In particular, the medial relationship of TR fibers to the corticospinal and corticobulbar components of the internal capsule may further reduce their visualization during routine lateral dissections40. These methodological constraints likely contributed to the relatively low representation frequency of TR pathways compared with more superficially accessible projection systems.

Auditory radiations (AR) demonstrated the lowest overall representation frequency among all analyzed pathways, indicating marked underrepresentation within the cadaveric white matter dissection literature. This finding likely reflects the complex anatomical course and limited surgical accessibility of these fibers. AR pathways pass through the posterior internal capsule, intermingle extensively with neighboring projection systems, and course in close spatial proximity to the optic radiations and surrounding insular white matter structures57,58. In addition to their relatively thin and transversely oriented anatomical configuration, the substantial overlap of AR fibers with adjacent projection pathways may further complicate their selective delineation during cadaveric dissection20. These technical and anatomical constraints likely contributed to the markedly limited representation of AR fibers observed throughout the analyzed literature59.

Commissural pathways demonstrated the lowest overall representation frequencies within the analyzed literature, particularly for smaller midline-crossing fiber systems such as the posterior commissure (PC), habenular commissure (HC), and commissure of the fornix (CF). One major methodological factor likely contributing to this pattern is the routine hemisecting of specimens during cadaveric white matter dissection preparation. Because commissural pathways traverse the midline, hemisecting procedures may inherently disrupt the anatomical continuity of these fibers prior to detailed microsurgical dissection.

In contrast, larger and more surgically accessible commissural systems, particularly the corpus callosum (CC) and anterior commissure (AC), demonstrated considerably greater representation frequencies throughout the literature. Smaller commissural pathways, including the PC and HC, are anatomically located adjacent to deep midline structures such as the mesencephalon, pulvinar, pineal region, and superior colliculi, making selective exposure considerably more challenging during routine cerebral white matter dissections60,61. Similarly, the thin anatomical configuration of CF fibers may further limit their visualization, particularly in lateral-to-medial dissections40.

Collectively, these findings suggest that commissural underrepresentation within cadaveric dissection studies is strongly influenced by specimen preparation techniques, midline anatomical complexity, and limited technical accessibility of smaller commissural systems. Notably, HC fibers were identified in only two studies included in the present dataset17,40, further emphasizing the reproducibility of this representational asymmetry across the literature.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study. First, the analysis was restricted to major cerebral white matter pathways that were sufficiently represented within the available cadaveric dissection literature. Less frequently studied or recently characterized fiber systems, including smaller association pathways and nonmajor commissural tracts, were not comprehensively evaluated. Consequently, the present dataset may not fully capture the complete anatomical diversity of human cerebral white matter organization.

Second, the study relied on tract representation data extracted from previously published cadaveric dissection studies. As a result, the findings remain inherently influenced by publication bias, study-specific methodological focus, and variability in anatomical nomenclature across the literature. In particular, differences in tract classification, dissection strategy, and reporting methodology among studies may have influenced tract-level representation frequencies despite attempts to standardize data extraction and tract categorization.

Third, the analytical framework was based primarily on binary tract representation rather than quantitative volumetric or morphometric anatomical measurements. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as indicators of representation prevalence within the literature rather than direct measures of anatomical prominence or structural importance of individual pathways.

In addition, most included studies employed lateral-to-medial Klingler-based dissection techniques and frequently incorporated tractography-supported anatomical validation. Although this increased methodological consistency across the dataset, it may also have contributed to systematic overrepresentation of surgically accessible superficial pathways and underrepresentation of deeper or midline fiber systems. Future studies integrating alternative dissection trajectories, advanced tractography methods, and complementary quantitative anatomical approaches may provide a more comprehensive understanding of representational variability within cadaveric white matter dissection research. Despite these limitations, to our knowledge, this study represents the first quantitative evaluation of tract-level representation patterns within the human cadaveric white matter dissection literature, specifically investigating representation asymmetry and hemispheric sampling variability across published dissection studies.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this study provides the first quantitative evaluation of tract representation patterns within the human cadaveric white matter dissection literature. Quantitative analyses identified consistent differences in representation across anatomically distinct fiber systems, with superficial association pathways being represented more frequently than deep projection and smaller commissural systems.

The findings suggest that tract representation within cadaveric dissection studies is influenced not only by anatomical organization, but also by methodological accessibility, dissection orientation, specimen preparation techniques, and technical visualization constraints. Together, these factors likely contribute to the uneven distribution of tract representation across published dissection studies.

By combining tract prevalence data with study-level and inequality-based analyses, the present study provides a quantitative framework for evaluating methodological trends within white matter dissection research. Future studies incorporating alternative dissection strategies, advanced tractography integration, and standardized tract nomenclature may further improve the anatomical consistency and more comprehensive anatomical representation of cadaveric white matter investigations.
