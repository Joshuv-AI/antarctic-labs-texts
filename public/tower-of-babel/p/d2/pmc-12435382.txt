Hydatid cyst (HC) disease, also known as cystic echinococcosis (CE), is a parasitic infection caused by Echinococcus granulosus. In the biological cycle of echinococci, humans are accidental intermediate hosts. The parasite can be transmitted directly through close contact with dogs or indirectly by consuming food or water contaminated with parasitic eggs. After the eggs hatch, the larvae penetrate the intestinal wall and spread through the blood or lymphatic system to various organs, where the cysts eventually develop.1–3

Most cysts are found primarily in the liver (75%) and lungs (5%–15%).3 Bone involvement is unusual, occurring in 0.5%–2% of reported cases, 50% of which infect the spine. Sacral involvement is considered rare among spinal segments.4–6 Spinal cysts are associated with high recurrence rates, significant morbidity, and mortality.7 Effective treatment requires the administration of albendazole before surgery and total cyst excision without rupture to prevent dissemination and chronic recurrence of the disease.7,8

We report a case of an HC on the sacral spine in a patient with a history of HC disease. This report demonstrates the clinical setting for cauda equina involvement at this rare site. It also highlights the importance of considering HCs in the differential diagnosis of cauda equina compression syndrome, particularly in known cases or those living in endemic areas.

This case has been reported in line with SCARE (Surgical Case Report) criteria.9

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 17-year-old male patient from Hebron, Palestine, with a significant past history of HC disease, presented to the neurology clinic complaining of 7 months of low back pain and a history of chronic constipation for 5 years. The back pain radiated to both lower limbs, associated with claudication and a positive Lhermitte’s sign.

The patient had a significant history of HC disease affecting multiple sites, including the liver, lungs, brain, spleen, and sacrum. Cysts were surgically excised from the brain and right lung at the age of 7 years. Postcraniotomy, the patient developed generalized tonic-clonic seizures, which have been controlled with levetiracetam in therapeutic doses to date.

The patient resides in Yata, a rural town in the Hebron Governorate, where most inhabitants raise domestic animals such as sheep and cattle, and stray dogs are abundant.10

On presentation, the neurological examination revealed exaggerated deep tendon reflexes in the lower limbs with positive plantar reflexes. Power and sensation were normal. The remainder of the physical examination showed no abnormalities.

CT and dorsolumbar MR imaging revealed a large, well-defined, multiloculated sacral cyst causing rectal compression (Fig. 1A and B). It extended retrorectally and presacrally from S3 to S5, involving the spine (Fig. 2) and eroding the sacrum (S2 and S3), measuring approximately 6.8 × 4.6 × 3.8 cm in the transverse (TS), anteroposterior (AP), and craniocaudal (CC) dimensions, respectively (Fig. 3). No changes were noted regarding the previously identified multiple liver and splenic calcified lesions. The patient’s laboratory tests were unremarkable, but the serological enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) test was positive, confirming the diagnosis of active E. granulosus infection.

Based on the clinical presentation, imaging studies, and positive serology, a diagnosis of sacral HC was established. The patient was referred to a highly specialized hospital for surgical intervention and began albendazole 400 mg twice daily for 4 weeks.

Operative Technique

The surgery was performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. After aseptic scrubbing and draping, a lower midline laparotomy incision was made in successive layers. The retroperitoneal region was accessed bilaterally adjacent to the rectum, where the HC was located deep in the pelvis near the sacrum. The area was flushed with hypertonic saline. The cyst was punctured and aspirated, followed by injection of hypertonic saline into the cavity. After a 5-minute interval, the cystic contents, including daughter cysts, were aspirated. The cyst was totally excised, and the cavity was deroofed and irrigated with hypertonic saline. An appendectomy was performed simultaneously. A drain was inserted into the cavity, hemostasis was carefully achieved, and the wound was closed in layers.

Postoperative Course

Postoperative follow-up showed improvement in low back pain and remission of constipation (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Observations

The larval stage of E. granulosus tapeworm is the cause of HC disease. It is globally distributed and considered endemic in Western China, Central Asia, South America, Eastern Africa, and Mediterranean countries.11 With an annual incidence rate of up to 50 per 100,000 persons in these regions, it still poses a challenging economic and public health issue.12 According to a study conducted from 2008 to 2017, the average annual incidence of the disease in Palestine was approximately 0.93 per 100,000 persons.13 Hebron is considered a significant focus of the disease, with an average 6-year incidence of 4.2 per 100,000 persons.14

Cysts primarily arise in highly vascularized organs such as the liver and lungs. The involvement of other organs is rare, as HC only affects the spine in 0.2%–1% of all hydatidosis cases.4 Reports indicate that the majority of cysts occur at the thoracic (52%) and lumbar (37%) spines. A minor percentage occurs in the cervical and sacral regions.15

The parasite often spreads across the spine by direct extension from lung, pelvic, or abdominal infection.3,4 Extra-visceral spread can also result from arterial dissemination. Some authors have proposed alternative mechanisms involving venous routes through portovertebral shunts, where the parasite migrates retrogradely to retroperitoneal and epidural venous plexuses from the inferior vena cava.16

The progression of symptomatic disease may vary from acute onset to extended clinical courses.16 The clinical symptoms of spinal echinococcosis vary depending on the degree of vertebral involvement and stage of disease. The early stage of primary infection is typically occult, asymptomatic, nonspecific, and related to the location and size of the lesion. Small cysts may be asymptomatic for many years. When the extent of the lesion increases, symptoms may occur. Because it lacks typical signs and symptoms, spinal CE can manifest as any symptom associated with vertebral bone degeneration or spinal cord compression.17 It has been found that 85% of cases have back pain, 25%–95% have radicular discomfort, and 25%–77% have paraparesis, all attributed to the compression of the spinal cord. In addition, other symptoms, including paraplegia, sciatica, and urinary retention, have been reported.18 Our patient was infested with spinal hydatid disease for 5 years, with constipation as an initial symptom due to the mass effect of the cyst that compressed the rectum, followed by compressive neurological symptoms.

In order to make a definitive diagnosis of HC disease, a combination of diagnostic methods is commonly used, such as imaging studies, serology, polymerase chain reaction, or histology of tissue samples from the patient.11

HC disease of the spine is commonly misdiagnosed because of its rarity and nonpathognomonic presentation; the diagnosis often occurs many years or even decades after the initial manifestation of symptoms.16 Radiographs or CT scans are usually the first imaging modalities used. MRI is more reliable than CT imaging to make a direct diagnosis; it not only shows the typical signs of a pericystic wall and the multilocular character of the lesion, but also can reveal more subtle structures and the relationship between the lesion and the surrounding organs, making MRI the gold standard for diagnosing spinal HC disease.5,6 Imaging results are validated using serology tests, of which ELISA is the main test used because of its high specificity and sensitivity.11,6

In the diagnostic process of our case, CT imaging was the initial imaging modality, followed by MRI that showed results characterizing the HC. On further development of the patient’s complaints, CT scans revealed the extension of the cyst into the spinal canal, and positive ELISA serology confirmed active CE.

The differential diagnosis of spinal CE varies based on the primarily affected anatomical structure and includes tuberculosis, brucellosis, pyogenic infection (osteomyelitis), simple or aneurysmal bone cysts, fibrous dysplasia, spinal metastasis, or malignancy (e.g., multiple myeloma and chondrosarcoma).16

Lessons

Surgical excision is the preferred initial treatment to relieve neurological compression, with irrigation using scolicidal solutions to decrease the risk of recurrence—thus decompressing the spinal cord.7 Along with surgical removal, oral albendazole plays an essential role in the treatment.19 Recurrences are common, and the prognosis is poor in cases of spinal HCs, even with successful surgery, due to the invasive nature of the disease and high risk of dissemination.8,20 Long-term follow-up including serologic, clinical, and radiological tests should be performed.20

Documented surgical complications include perioperative mortality, misdiagnosis with local spread, recurrence, worsening neurological disability, secondary infection, CSF fistula, and anaphylaxis.21,22 In one reported case, a patient’s death resulted from formalin irrigation used to prevent recurrence of a spinal HC. This fatal outcome resulted from a dural tear during the procedure, which allowed formalin to penetrate the intradural space.23

HC disease is a parasitic infection induced by E. granulosus. It is rarely found in the bones, with a particularly uncommon localization in the sacral area. Sacral hydatidosis has an unspecified clinical presentation and an aggressive course, which might result in a misdiagnosis. These lesions are diagnosed with CT imaging, which should be followed by MRI for a definitive diagnosis. While medical treatment can reduce the size of cysts, total surgical removal of the cyst for neural decompression is the definitive cure. This case highlights the significance of sacral HC as a potential differential diagnosis for chronic low back pain and constipation, particularly in patients who present with a history of HCs in an endemic region.
