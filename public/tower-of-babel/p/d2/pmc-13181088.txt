The SUV rear wheel axle under different loading conditions at digital image correlation approach for road safety
Authors: Jarosław Krzysztof Gonera, Tadeusz Szymczak, Paweł Dziedziak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study focuses on measuring changes in the rear-axle wheel alignment of an SUV under various load levels and load arrangements, using the Digital Image Correlation approach with the PONTOS 5 M system. Four load cases were applied, covering the passenger and luggage compartments, with maximum masses of 200 and 300 kg, respectively. Results were recorded in the 3D coordinate system, capturing displacement along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes. The results showed that changes in rear-axle wheel alignment are strongly related to load level, whereas load arrangement strongly influences the displacement path. As a result of wheel movement, there were significant changes in the rear toe-in and rear camber angles. This affects the handling of the vehicle when the luggage and passenger compartments carry heavy loads. During the loading of the rear part of the passenger compartment, different changes in the displacement resultant vector of the individual measurement points were observed compared to the result for the luggage compartment.

Introduction

The number of vehicles on the roads in most countries is increasing annually, driven by higher levels of social status1–3, which creates significant demand for mobility for visits, sightseeing, rest, as well as for the transport of goods for orders, sales and services4–6. Moreover, the average annual mileage of these vehicles has gradually increased, leading to higher operational levels than in earlier decades. This applies to both passenger cars, used mainly for private purposes, and commercial vehicles1–3.

As the number of vehicles increases, road traffic intensity becomes more significant, which lowers the mechanical resistance of many components of steering and suspension systems. Therefore, it is necessary to introduce manners to reduce the risk of road accidents and collisions, as observed in many European countries2,3,7. This can be achieved through tests using modern stand apparatus and the advanced design of new or modified active and passive safety systems7. Additionally, the modernisation of transport means for this problem is carried out to increase their reliability, travel comfort, and reduce their negative influences on the environment. Unfortunately, regardless of the type of transport, different operational behaviour is observed in vehicles, which nevertheless does not enable us to propose a general cause-and-effect classification. In those cases, the following sentence can be formulated very easily: in most cases, the operational stages can reduce the technical and functional parameters of the components, and therefore the problem becomes not individual but general8.

It is worth emphasising that the technical condition of many vehicles on the roads is unsatisfactory due to high mileage, exposure to various road conditions, and often high speeds relative to the zone for travelling9. This can lead to accidents that damage car bodies, steering, and suspension systems. The quality of vehicle repairs is another problem, as it does not enable the attainment of acceptable safety levels10. However, the influence of vehicle repairs on road safety has not yet been considered10,11.

Moreover, the quality of the body, suspension, and steering systems of vehicles that have not been involved in road accidents is also reduced by exploitation. However, this stage has a smaller influence on vehicle components than road accidents8,12,13. In accident-free vehicles operated on high-quality roads, degradation unfolds over time, with no sudden damage13. During exploitation, the main factors contributing to the reduction in component quality are forces and moments. This is largely related to vehicle mileage13,14. It is known that vehicles are subjected to many kinematic and dynamic excitations with a wide frequency spectrum9,15,16. Several types of forces and moments are transferred to the suspension and elements of the steering system, thereby reducing their mechanical resistance. Lower loading values reach the vehicle body15. These and other features are connected to the quality of roads on which vehicles travel. They contribute to the damage of suspensions, steering, and body components9.

In most vehicles, it is not possible to measure many parameters related to wheel geometry because the angles of the wheel camber, tilt, and caster of the steering knuckle are not adjustable. This is because some suspension system elements are mounted directly on the vehicle body. Therefore, when the body geometry changes, the wheel geometry also changes9. Wheel geometry directly reflects the vehicle’s safety level because it affects stability and predictability while driving and the ability to maintain the correct driving track17–19.

Design changes in vehicles are largely aimed at improving safety levels7,20,21. Advanced suspension and steering systems are used to improve handling and reduce the risk of accidents2,22,23. The appropriate design of these systems requires active safety systems because they enable faster, more precise reactions while driving, thereby improving road safety24–27. Changes to the geometry of the suspension and steering systems follow the vehicle’s stability and the driving path assumed by the driver under the influence of disturbances acting on the vehicle28,29.

Wheel geometry may also influence loading levels and their type30,31. This is strongly related to suspension type, as a simple suspension is more sensitive to changes in wheel geometry parameters. This can be observed in the McPherson strut for the front axle or the torsion beam of the rear axle32. With respect to handling stability, it would be beneficial to use engineering solutions in which the wheel geometry parameters do not change because of differences in the value and distribution of the load inside a vehicle33,34.

The suspension system is also responsible for damping large vibrations that can be transmitted to passenger spaces, causing discomfort and affecting passengers’ health35,36. Therefore, manufacturers focus on isolating passengers from road conditions to the extent possible36–38. Other methods are employed to reduce the frequency and amplitude of vibrations33,38,39. Vidya & Dharmana34 and Shim & Velusamy30 examine the effects of changes in vehicle loading on vehicle safety while driving. In addition to affecting vehicle handling while driving over uneven surfaces and during cornering, the suspension system also affects vehicle acceleration and braking40. This results in a change in the weight distribution of the vehicle’s axles.

It was noted that this approach has not been widely discussed in the literature. Only a few similar studies have been conducted to date32. In this approach, the authors qualitatively identified the load’s role in geometry changes. This problem was also considered using a Digital Image Correlation (DIC) system with white-black circular measurement points to track tyre deformation during motion41. This enabled capturing the 1 st mode shape and displacement vectors for the top reversal point of the tyre at 30 Hz. The same measurement method was used by NASA to determine the sinking behaviour of a SCARB rover on a sand road under laboratory conditions42. With this approach, the difference in wheel sinkage between the front and rear axles of the vehicle was captured. In another study43, the influence of loading on tyre behaviour was considered using the DIC method, with the behaviour of a tyre and rim examined up to the load capacity. This experiment indicated the rim’s permanent deformation due to improper tyre selection. Differences in wheel behaviour for a few wheel types were also considered using the DIC approach, reflecting changes in radial deflection values [44].

The paper32 collected data on the influence of mass and load distributions on changes in the individual parameters of the suspension system geometry and steering (wheel geometry) of passenger cars. However, in this case, differences in all-wheel geometry parameters were identified using typical measurement devices, and differences in wheel alignment relative to the three reference planes were not analysed.

Therefore, for this study, a non-contact measurement system (DIC PONTOS 5 M) was used to capture differences in wheel alignment in a three-axis rectangular coordinate system (3D) caused by the vehicle load.

a mileage of close to 50 km. The vehicle possesses a 1.5 dm3 spark-ignition engine and.

The technical condition of the vehicle was assessed using a UNIMETAL 5000 diagnostic line (Fig. 1). This approach enabled the collection of fundamental data on the technical condition of the suspension, steering, and braking systems. No technical defects were observed in the systems inspected. The technical condition of the shock absorbers was assessed using the Vehicle Diagnostic Line (VDL) by measuring their damping coefficients. Measurements were performed using the EUSAMA method developed by the European Shock Absorber Manufacturers Association.

The rear axle of the vehicle was analysed because the wheel alignment of this axle is significantly influenced by the loads in the passenger and luggage compartments.

For each load variant, the mass was placed symmetrically between the right and left sides of the vehicle. Additionally, during all attempts, the front part of the passenger compartment was loaded on the right and left sides with a mass of 75 kg, corresponding to the masses of the driver and passenger. The first measurements were obtained during the simulation of the luggage compartment load, with the load mass changed every 25 kg up to 100 kg, creating the W 01 variant. In the next step, the load of the passenger compartment was also simulated; however, in this case, the load mass was changed every 50 kg up to 200 kg, which was denoted as the W02 variant. When simulating the load in the passenger compartment, the mass was located behind the rear axle of the vehicle in the rear overhang of the body. However, when the load in the passenger compartment was simulated, the introduced load was located directly in front of the rear axle and between the axles of the vehicle. During the simulation of the third load variant – the W 03, the mass was located in the rear part of the passenger compartment and changed in steps of 50 kg up to a total of 300 kg. The last load variant, i.e. the W 04, which represented the load of the rear parts of the passenger compartment, was simulated with a mass of 300 kg in the passenger compartment and 25 kg steps in the luggage zone, ranging from 25 to 100 kg.

The changes in the wheel deflection at the load levels were monitored using the Digital Image Correlation (DIC) method on a PONTOS 5 M device (Fig. 3). The measurements were captured within a three-dimensional (3D) rectangular coordinate system using white-black markers arranged on a plate mounted on the rear wheel of the tested object (Fig. 3), which served as the measuring component for the DIC approach. The plate was supported by rigid adjustable metal rods, enabling mounting on vehicle tyres (Fig. 3b). This component was used on the rear right wheel, along its vertical axis. At each load stage, the DIC system captured 3D deflection values in the form of photographs and graphs. These results were used to analyse the behaviour of the tested zone at the load levels, considering the technical features of the SUV suspension and tyre.

Results

For the paper’s aim, the results are represented by a technical state of the vehicle covering shock absorber damping index and braking efficiency indicator determined in a vehicle diagnostic line, as well as changes in deflection of its rear axis at a few load levels using the DIC approach for results in a three-axis coordinate system. The first kind of the mentioned results is compared with its minimum required values, while the second one follows the vehicle behaviour at a load level up to 300 kg, selecting its steps, distributed in the luggage and passenger compartments. In the case of the DIC approach, results are in the form of displacement components and their resultant vector versus the measurement stages. Their changes at the load value levels are also shown for determining a geometry wheel sensitivity coefficient for the assessment vehicle at the loading condition used.

The SUV in the vehicle diagnostic line

The first test focused on the technical condition of the car by collecting values for the shock absorber damping index and the braking efficiency indicator (Table 2). Analysis of the braking efficiency indicator measurements shows that both the front- and rear-axle shock absorbers were efficient, as the recorded values exceeded the minimum level. The difference in the absorber damping index values between the front- and rear-axle shock absorbers resulted from their different characteristics and suspension types. The results of the braking-efficiency measurements using the roller method are presented in Table 2. This enables the conclusion that the car can be considered technically efficient because the limit value of braking efficiency (min. 50%) was exceeded. The parking brake of the test vehicle was located on the rear axle. For the parking brake, the tested braking efficiency reached 27%, and the minimum value was 25%. Therefore, parking brakes may also be considered technically efficient.

The first substage focused on the influence of luggage compartment loading on wheel geometry. Figure 4 shows the changes in wheel geometry as displacement vectors. The load was varied in 25 kg increments from 0 to 100 kg. Based on the results presented in Fig. 4, the largest absolute values of the wheel displacement components (measured using a plate with markers) occurred along the Z-axis (the longitudinal direction of the vehicle). Compared with the features of the tested zone, this indicates that the wheel alignment is related to these measured values. In this case, the maximum value of the resultant displacement vector was greater than 5 mm, and the minimum was 4 mm. Smaller displacement values were observed along the Y-axis. They ranged from about − 1.6 mm to −1.9 mm. Changes in the component displacements occurred towards the ground. Assuming no tyre deformation, this indicates variations in the camber angle. In addition, the change in this component is marked by displacement along the X-axis. In this case, displacement occurred outside the vehicle. The displacement value was found to be in the range of about 0.7 mm to 0.9 mm, and these reflect the lowest values if an absolute value is used. For the X-axis, the variations in the displacement component were slightly greater in the inner part of the measurement plate (closer to the wheel) than in the other sections. This confirms that the changes in the toe-in of the rear axle wheels move in the positive direction.

Figure 5 shows the variation in displacement values across the examined vehicle regions at individual load levels (the W01) as a function of the number of stages. Stage 0 is the reference and reflects the vehicle before loading. Stages 1 and 2 play the same role as Stage 0 and are used to assess the quality of the measurements. Stages 3–6 include measurements collected at a subsequent vehicle load of 25 kg. Using this approach, linear displacements of point measurements can be observed on the X- and Y-axes, indicating that the components respond linearly to changes in load levels. A similar linear relationship is observed for displacement values along the Z-axis when the luggage mass does not exceed 75 kg. At a vehicle load of 100 kg, a slightly greater increase in all displacement components’ values and a small nonlinearity in the Z-direction are observed. This can be attributed to the tyre’s reaction to the load and the non-linear elastic behaviour of this component.

The nonlinear changes in displacement values in the three-axis coordinate system at different load levels were influenced by many factors. The first was the characteristics of the elastic elements in the suspension system, such as springs, since these components’ behaviour involves significant deflection under heavy vehicle loads, while at a typical load level, they should ensure high ride comfort. However, under substantial loads, they must also protect the vehicle from excessive reduction in ground clearance. Additionally, the elasticity of the rear wishbone bushes contributed to the nonlinear response. In the multi-link suspension of the tested vehicle, there were many wishbone bushes and numerous connections between the rear axle’s suspension components. As the load varied, the relative positioning of these components shifted. It is worth noting that all rear suspension parts can affect the geometry of the rear axle wheels. Furthermore, the nonlinear changes were also caused by the tyres of the tested vehicle. In this case, the vehicle was equipped with low-profile tyres, size 235/55 R19, with a stiff structure. However, they could deform slightly differently under heavy load.

The luggage compartment was loaded with masses ranging from 50 kg to 200 kg (W 02, Fig. 6). These results indicated higher values of the displacement components than those in the previous approach. In the X-axis direction, the values of the resultant displacement vector were approximately in the range of 1.5 mm to 2 mm. In comparison with the previous results, this represents a two-fold increase in displacement. In the Y-axis direction, there was a larger increase in displacement levels, by about 60%, compared with the previous approach. The displacement values ranged from − 3.35 mm to −2.60 mm for this direction. In the Z-axis, displacement components of −14.65 mm and − 11.5 mm were recorded. This indicates an almost three-fold increase in the values of the physical quantities at a two-fold higher mass value compared with the earlier load level. Based on the test results shown in Fig. 6, it can be concluded that the directions of displacement in the individual axles indicate a significant change in the values of the wheel camber angles and the toe-in of the rear wheels of the vehicle, along with changes in the load mass.

The plot of displacement in the X-direction versus stage number (Fig. 7) shows a linear increase in displacement, whereas this behaviour was not observed in the other directions. A significant increase in the displacement component’s value occurred along the Z-axis at load levels of 150 and 200 kg, whereas there was a slight decrease in displacement along the Y-axis at the same load capacity. A nonlinear relationship between the load range and displacement values was observed in both cases. This feature is related to the nonlinear elastic responses of the metal-gum components of the suspension and wheel. It is also evident that an increase in displacement along the Z-axis follows a significant change in the toe-in of the rear wheels in the load range of 100–200 kg. It is worth emphasising that such a clear increase in displacement at the measurement points, and consequently in the wheel displacement and the change in the toe-in of the rear wheels, can have a significant impact on the vehicle’s handling after the luggage compartment is loaded with a greater mass. This can be selected as an important feature for road safety.

The results obtained when the passenger rear compartment is loaded with masses ranging from 0 to 300 kg (the W 03) at a load step of 50 kg also indicate the characteristic response of the region examined (Fig. 8). Compared with the data collected in the previous load cases, changes in the resultant displacement vectors of the individual points can be observed. Although the maximum load was higher than in the previous case, the X-axis displacement values were lower, i.e. the measurement points moved from about 1.3 mm to 1.9 mm. Similarly, smaller displacement values were observed along the Y-axis, ranging from approximately 2 mm to about 2.7 mm. These values were accompanied by displacements of 13.0 mm and 16.4 mm in the Z-axis direction. However, at 200 kg, they levelled off between 8 mm and 10 mm. In the X-axis direction, displacement ranged from 1.1 mm to 1.4 mm, while on the Y-axis, it ranged from 1.8 mm to 2.1 mm. Based on this, we conclude that loading the passenger rear compartment causes significantly lower displacements along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes compared with loading the luggage compartment with the same mass. Therefore, the changes in the toe-in and camber angles of the rear wheels were also smaller when the rear part of the passenger compartment was loaded. It follows that the impact of changes in the load on this part of the vehicle body on vehicle handling is also smaller.

The displacement component values of the measurement points in the 3D coordinate system at the load steps for the rear part of the vehicle passenger compartment (the W 03) followed different courses, as shown in Fig. 9. A linear relationship between the captured values and load levels was not observed for this stage, whereas for the Z-axis, this relationship was linear up to a load of 150 kg (Stages 0, 3, 4, and 5). At a mass of 200 kg (Stage 6), there was a significant increase in the displacement of these points. The curve then followed a linear course in the subsequent loading steps. In the case of the X- and Y-axes, the relationship between the displacement values and load did not appear linear. For both axes, it was observed that, for a body load of 200 kg (Stage 6), the displacement values were significantly smaller when the mass increased from 200 to 300 kg. The dependencies described resulted from the characteristics of the rear-axle suspension system of the vehicle tested. Loading the rear part of the passenger compartment to a mass of 200 kg is crucial. This is reflected in the change in the displacement component values in all axis directions of the 3D rectangular coordinate system.

Figure 11 shows the results of tests at loads in the luggage compartment ranging from 25 to 100 kg (Stages 9, 10, 11, and 12), and a mass of 300 kg in the rear part of the passenger compartment (W04). As a result, at a mass of 100 kg, there was an increase of approximately 45% in the displacement values in the Z-axis direction compared with the data collected at a mass of 300 kg in the rear part of the passenger compartment. The same behaviour was observed in the Y-axis. A much smaller increase in the displacement value was observed in the X-axis direction, amounting to only 5%.

Significant increases in the displacement of the luggage compartment during loading influenced changes in the toe-in and camber angles of the rear wheels of the vehicle. When loading the rear part of the passenger compartment with a mass of 300 kg and the luggage compartment with a mass of 100 kg, the points moved by approximately 1.4 mm to 2.2 mm along the X-axis and by 2.6 mm to approximately 3.8 mm along the Y-axis. By contrast, displacements along the Z-axis were much higher (between 18.7 mm and 23.7 mm). It can be concluded that the wheel moves outwards along the X-axis, downwards along the Y-axis, and towards the front of the vehicle along the Z-axis. This was related to changes in the toe-in towards a positive value and the camber angle towards a negative value.

In both cases, the relationship is linear. However, when comparing the effects of the load variants on the changes in the displacement of the rear axle wheels, it can be observed that, when loading the vehicle with the same mass, higher displacement values occur when loading the luggage compartment than when loading the rear part of the passenger compartment. It can be concluded that loading the luggage compartment is more unfavourable because of the greater changes in the rear axle wheel geometry. In this case, the load significantly influenced the handling of the vehicle.

Higher values of the wheel geometric sensitivity coefficient (Rw) during luggage compartment loading were linked to greater deflection of the rear axle suspension and a more substantial reduction in rear vehicle ground clearance at this load case compared to when the load was applied to the rear passenger compartment section. During the simulation of luggage compartment loading, the load were placed near the rear of the vehicle. This approach had a significantly greater impact on the vehicle’s rear axle, causing larger changes in the rear wheels’ geometry. However, when loading the rear part of the passenger compartment, a substantial part of the mass affected the rear axle but also influenced the front axle’s response, resulting in alterations to the geometry of both axles. This was associated with wheel displacement in all three measurement planes.

The load in the luggage compartment, located in the rear overhang of the vehicle, contributed to an increased load on the rear axle and a reduced load on the front axle. This is due to the creation of a larger lever arm relative to the rear axle compared to the load of the passenger cabin, which is located between the axles. As can be observed, different load scenarios caused changes in the weight distribution between the vehicle’s axles. Comparing two load variants at the same overall load—specifically, the load on the rear passenger zone and the luggage compartment—showed notable differences in mass distribution. When the rear passenger area was loaded, 54% of the mass was on the front axle and 46% on the rear. Conversely, when the luggage compartment was loaded, 48% of the mass was on the front axle and 52% on the rear. As the load on the rear passenger compartment increased, the vehicle’s ground clearance decreased, and the suspension compressed on both axles. However, when the luggage compartment load increased, the ground clearance around the front axle increased, whereas the clearance around the rear axle decreased.

The research findings have important practical implications. They can help enhance road safety. Vehicle users need to be aware that changes in the vehicle’s mass and cargo placement also influence the suspension system’s geometry. These changes in wheel geometry, in turn, affect vehicle safety and the efficiency of braking and acceleration. Changing the wheel settings in a three-dimensional system can affect the vehicle’s handling, its ability to stay on a designated path, as well as braking and acceleration performance. Variations in the position of the analysed points along the Z-axis are linked to adjustments in the rear axle wheel alignment, directly impacting the vehicle’s ability to maintain a straight course and stability on a curved track. Conversely, shifts in these points along the X and Y axes relate to changes in the rear axle wheel camber angles and influence the tyre’s contact patch. A smaller contact patch, caused by alterations in the camber angle, may reduce traction and increase braking distances. It also limits the maximum driving force transmitted to the road, impairing vehicle grip. Moreover, changes in wheel camber angles can affect vehicle handling, especially on curved sections of the road. Additionally, adjustments in wheel alignment parameters, alongside variations in mass and its distribution, can accelerate tyre wear.

The research, which identified three-dimensional wheel displacement differences affecting wheel alignment parameters alongside variations in mass value and its distribution, may also influence the efforts of vehicle manufacturers. Based on these findings, it can be indirectly inferred that, for safety reasons, it would be beneficial for vehicle manufacturers to adopt suspensions that adapt to changes in mass level and its location within the vehicle. Active suspensions would be particularly advantageous, as they would maintain consistent ground clearance, at least on the rear axle. This would minimise wheel alignment issues and reduce variations in this.

Summary

The novelty of this paper lies in applying digital image correlation (DIC) to precisely identify wheel alignment changes caused by variations in mass and cargo distribution within the passenger and luggage compartments. The use of a non-contact measurement system during the study enabled capturing three-dimensional changes in wheel alignment—based on load levels—within a three-axis rectangular coordinate system. Unlike typical wheel alignment measurement methods, the proposed approach does not identify the values of wheel alignment changes; instead, it tracks changes in the position of several dozen analysed points on the wheels in three coordinates with very high accuracy as the vehicle load varies. This innovative approach to vehicle assessment can be automated in the future and utilised for rapid, non-contact measurements of wheel alignment.

The tests using the PONTOS 5 M digital image correlation system enabled determining a rear axle wheel displacement in the 3D coordinate system. This allowed us to analyse the effect of the load level on wheel behaviour for different mass distributions and their values. This was followed by changes in wheel alignment relative to the three reference planes.

It can be concluded that the wheel behaviour is significantly affected by the vehicle body load, and the location of the mass influences the displacement values and their trajectories, causing differences in the wheel reactions. The largest displacement of the wheel occurred along the Z-axis, that is, towards the front of the vehicle. This was mainly due to changes in the toe-in values of the rear axle wheels, depending on the differences in the load value. During the loading of the rear part of the passenger compartment, different changes in the displacement resultant vector at the individual measurement points were observed compared with the result for the luggage compartment. Loading the rear part of the passenger compartment produced significantly smaller displacement values along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes than tests with the luggage compartment loaded with the same mass.

Among the load cases analysed in this study, placing the same mass at the rear of the passenger compartment proved more beneficial than positioning it in the luggage compartment. Positioning a significant amount of mass behind the rear axle, in the vehicle’s rear overhang, was less favourable for operational reasons. This load variant caused greater changes in the wheel position within the three-axis rectangular coordinate system, leading to larger adjustments in wheel geometry parameters. Placing the mass in the luggage compartment, behind the rear axle, increased the load on the rear axle and decreased it on the front axle. Due to the reduced front axle load, the braking efficiency of the front wheels may diminish, as brakes are generally more effective than rear axle wheels under normal conditions. Additionally, the heavier load on the rear axle can considerably reduce the durability of rear suspension components. When cargo is loaded at the rear of the passenger compartment, the load is distributed between the front and rear axles, resulting in additional stress on both. This distribution is more advantageous for braking performance and the longevity of suspension components.

There was a significant change in the rear-wheel convergence and camber angles, which has a significant impact on vehicle handling and road safety. The behaviour of the wheel and its axis can be described by a geometric wheel sensitivity coefficient.
