Maternal propranolol as a safe alternative to invasive fetal surgery in chorioangioma management
Authors: L E van der Meeren, F Slaghekke, S Zaaijer, F Prein, E Lopriore, M C Haak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChorioangiomas are associated with a significant risk of fetal demise due to high‐output cardiac failure. Standard invasive treatment, including fetoscopic laser photocoagulation and intratumoral embolization, shows limited success and has high fetal mortality rates. The biological parallels between chorioangiomas and infantile hemangiomas suggest that these tumors may share common therapeutic targets. Propranolol is a proven therapy for infantile hemangiomas, therefore this established efficacy provides a strong rationale for its consideration in the management of symptomatic or rapidly growing placental chorioangiomas. We hypothesized that maternal oral propranolol administration could selectively target placental chorioangiomas while preserving overall placental function, thereby offering a potential off‐label therapeutic use of the drug. We report a case series of four fetuses that presented to Leiden University Medical Center, Leiden, The Netherlands, between December 2023 and May 2025 with a large chorioangioma and signs of fetal decompensation, varying from hyperdynamic circulation to evolving hydrops, and were treated with maternal propranolol. In all four cases, the fetal condition improved significantly upon propranolol treatment, with normalization of cardiac function and resolution of hydrops, paralleled by tumor regression and thrombosis confirmed histologically. These findings support a shift in the clinical management of chorioangiomas to improve fetal outcomes while minimizing procedural risks. © 2026 The Author(s). CASE SERIES

Large chorioangiomas are associated with major fetal morbidity due to a hyperdynamic circulation and high‐output cardiac failure, often progressing to hydrops and fetal demise 1 , 2 . Invasive interventions, such as fetoscopic laser photocoagulation of the feeding vessels and intratumoral embolization 2 , 3 , are technically challenging and carry high procedural risks, including vascular rupture, intratumoral bleeding and preterm birth. The rate of procedure‐related fetal loss is reported to be 31% 3 , which is likely to be an underestimation due to publication bias favoring successful interventions. Limited global access to fetal surgery underscores the need for safer, non‐invasive alternatives for management of chorioangiomas.

The biological parallels between chorioangiomas and infantile hemangiomas suggest shared therapeutic targets 4 . Both tumors show similar vascular proliferation, driven by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) 5 . Propranolol, a non‐selective β‐adrenergic receptor antagonist, has proven to be highly effective in inducing rapid involution of infantile hemangiomas without damaging the surrounding tissue 4 . This established efficacy of propranolol in infantile hemangiomas provides a strong rationale for its consideration in the management of symptomatic or rapidly growing chorioangiomas, particularly as propranolol is safe to take during pregnancy and previous research has not reported significant maternal adverse effects 6 . Therefore, we hypothesized that the use of maternal propranolol could selectively target large placental chorioangiomas while preserving overall placental function, and explored the potential fetal effects of the medication 7 .

This was a single‐center study of four cases of large placental chorioangioma that were referred to Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC), Leiden, The Netherlands, between December 2023 and May 2025 and treated with a course of maternal propranolol until delivery, following multidisciplinary review. Treatment regimens, tumor dimensions, intratumoral flow, fetal hemodynamics and overall maternal and fetal condition were recorded throughout the study period. All care was provided at LUMC by a dedicated fetal care team with a consistent composition and close internal collaboration. Oral administration of propranolol was employed in each case. Each patient was counseled about the off‐label use of the drug and the absence of its proven benefit for treating chorioangiomas in the literature so far, following extensive counseling on all available treatment options.

Chorioangioma was defined as a well‐circumscribed vascular placental mass on ultrasound. It was measured in three dimensions and volume (in cm3) was calculated using the ellipsoid formula. Cardiac size was expressed as cardiothoracic ratio (CTR), with CTR < 0.5 considered normal 8 . Fetal middle cerebral artery (MCA) peak systolic velocity (PSV) was expressed in multiples of the median (MoM), with MCA‐PSV > 1.55 MoM indicating fetal anemia 9 . Follow‐up ultrasound examinations were performed at least weekly until delivery, assessing the size of the chorioangioma, blood flow within the chorioangioma, fetal cardiac size, MCA‐PSV, amniotic fluid index, signs of hydrops and fetal growth. No maternal adverse events were documented during treatment and no neonatal bradycardia, hypotension or hypoglycemia occurred. Placental specimens were collected for both gross and histological evaluation.

Figure 1 and Tables 1 and S1 provide additional details on each of the cases discussed below. Case 1 was referred to our center at 24 + 4 weeks' gestation with a chorioangioma accompanied by fetal cardiomegaly. Fetoscopic laser photocoagulation was deemed too dangerous due to the caliber of the feeding vessels to the tumor and their proximity to the cord insertion. At 27 + 4 weeks, ascites and fetal hydrops had developed and MCA‐PSV had risen to 1.6 MoM. Corticosteroids were administered and an intrauterine blood transfusion was performed to increase fetal hemoglobin level from 8.39 g/dL to 10.9 g/dL. Maternal propranolol was initiated at a dose of 3 × 40 mg/day, as previously described in the literature 7 . Within 24 h, echogenic areas developed within the tumor, accompanied by a rapid decline in tumor volume from 319 cm3 to 290 cm3.

Given the gestational age (27 + 5 weeks), uncertainty about whether propranolol would improve or stabilize the fetal condition, and the completed course of corticosteroids, the multidisciplinary team decided not to await further effect and proceeded with Cesarean delivery. A healthy female infant was delivered but died on day 8 after delivery due to late‐onset sepsis (caused by Bacillus cereus) and bilateral intracranial hemorrhage.

Case 2 was referred to our center at 19 + 6 weeks with a chorioangioma and cardiomegaly. Due to observed rapid growth of the chorioangioma within 1 week (from 12 cm3 to 25 cm3) and to prevent later fetal deterioration, we proposed initiation of oral propranolol following the findings of Case 1. Treatment with propranolol was initiated at 3 × 20 mg/day in the absence of overt fetal cardiac decompensation. One week later, echogenic areas had developed in the tumor and tumor volume had decreased from 25 cm3 to 21 cm3, but a few feeding vessels remained visible. The propranolol dose was increased from 3 × 20 mg/day to 3 × 40 mg/day and vascular flow ceased completely by 23 weeks. A healthy male infant was delivered at full term.

Case 3 was referred to our center at 19 + 5 weeks. Rapid growth of the chorioangioma (from 24 cm3 to 42 cm3) prompted propranolol initiation (at a dosage of 3 × 20 mg/day) within 2 weeks of referral, in the absence of overt fetal cardiac decompensation. By 22 + 3 weeks, echogenic areas had developed within the tumor and tumor volume had declined to 26 cm3. Vascular flow decreased markedly, although small residual vascularity persisted. The fetal condition remained stable. A healthy female infant was delivered at full term.

Case 4 was referred to our center at 26 + 1 weeks with a chorioangioma accompanied by cardiomegaly, as well as pericardial effusion and ascites, consistent with hydrops fetalis. Cord insertion was at the top of the tumor, rendering surgical intervention infeasible. Maternal propranolol was initiated immediately at a dose of 3 × 40 mg/day. Within 48 h after initiation, echogenic areas had developed within the tumor and tumor volume had decreased from 41 cm3 to 29 cm3. Hydrops resolved in the following week. By 27–29 weeks, intratumoral flow was absent, however low‐velocity peripheral vessels reappeared at 30 + 6 weeks after a 1 week interruption of propranolol therapy due to drug unavailability. A healthy female infant was delivered at 35 + 6 weeks. Postnatally, 28 cutaneous hemangiomas were observed, none of which required treatment.

In all cases, gross placental examination showed a solitary, well‐demarcated nodular lesion (Figure 2a,e,i,m). The surrounding placental parenchyma demonstrated maturation appropriate for the gestational age, with no evidence of degenerative or inflammatory change, or signs of fetal or maternal vascular malperfusion 10 .

Histological analysis demonstrated consistent features of tumor regression and degeneration. In Case 1, the maximum diameter of the chorioangioma was 11.2 cm and it was composed predominantly of capillaries with extensive degenerative changes, including collapsed lumina, perivascular hemorrhage and thrombosis involving 60% of the tumor (Figure 2b–d). Case 2 showed complete homogeneous degeneration (100%) of the chorioangioma (maximum diameter, 3.8 cm) and uniform loss of vital parenchyma, with amorphous eosinophilic appearance of the capillaries and stroma (Figure 2f–h). Case 3 showed a chorioangioma (maximum diameter, 6.2 cm) with partial degeneration (40%) and dark‐red‐to‐grey areas of tissue. Several large vessels exhibited occlusion with thickened vessel walls. Histologically, the lesion was composed predominantly of capillaries. The large occluded vessels showed thrombosis and calcification with increase of fibrous stroma (Figure 2j–l). Case 4 revealed a chorioangioma (maximum diameter, 7.5 cm) with extensive degeneration (80%) and myxoid, hemorrhagic and cystic changes, including fibrinogenic degeneration and thrombosis. Within the viable areas, capillaries with collapsed lumina were present (Figure 2n–p).

DISCUSSION

The use of propranolol in these four cases suggests its role in the regression of large chorioangiomas, and the reversal of hydrops in one case. Further growth of the chorioangioma with expected fetal deterioration appeared to be prevented in two cases. The therapeutic response to propranolol is comparable to that observed in infantile hemangiomas 4 , characterized by the immediate onset of tumor involution, followed by homogeneous degeneration, most pronounced in Cases 2 and 4. This structural change and the extent of degeneration have not been described previously and strongly suggest a medication‐specific effect. In retrospect, initiation of propranolol at 3 × 40 mg/day should have been considered in Case 2, given the rapid growth of the tumor and the subsequent response observed after dose escalation. Case 3, treated with a lower dose of propranolol, and Case 4, which had a 1 week treatment interruption, demonstrated partial but clinically sufficient regression to allow delivery at or near term. These findings support a potential dose–response effect and suggest that treatment may be most effective if initiated before the development of hydrops. Additionally, more time should have been allowed for the resolution of hydrops in Case 1, as preterm delivery at the onset of hydrops is associated with substantial neonatal risk. Conservative continuation of propranolol may provide better neonatal outcomes than preterm delivery at the onset of hydrops in these cases.

Both chorioangiomas and infantile hemangiomas show endothelial proliferation, supported by markers such as CD31, CD34, and CD15‐positive vessel‐forming progenitor cells 11 , hypothesized to be the developmental source for propranolol sensitivity 5 , 11 . By downregulating VEGF and angiopoietin‐like 4 and modulating endothelial survival pathways, propranolol provides a mechanistic basis for the clinical and histological effects observed. The clinical efficacy is consistent with the theory that infantile hemangiomas originate from placental mesenchymal core cells 12 . Importantly, propranolol was associated with selective tumor regression while preserving placental integrity, consistent with the selective effect of propranolol.

Although these four cases strongly support the efficacy of propranolol in chorioangiomas, the effect of spontaneous degeneration is not controlled for. Despite the limitations of a small sample size and lack of a control group, the abrupt onset of tumor regression, uniformity of the histological changes, and absence of features of natural involution make coincidental spontaneous degeneration unlikely 11 , 12 . While optimal timing and dosage of propranolol remain to be defined, our data suggest that propranolol may stabilize the fetus, even in the case of severe fetal decompensation.

Propranolol is widely available and safe. It offers a non‐invasive alternative to surgical intervention in cases of chorioangioma, including in low‐ and middle‐income settings. These findings support further evaluation of propranolol as a first‐line therapy with the potential to fundamentally change current management of this life‐threatening lesion.
