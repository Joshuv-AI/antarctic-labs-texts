Policy-mediated resilience memory in strategic mineral supply chains: How China’s rare earth sector learns from disruption
Authors: Wenyan Song, Yue Shi, Caibo Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryChina’s capacity to maintain supply chain resilience under systemic shocks is of global strategic importance. However, traditional static assessments fail to capture the dynamic feedback mechanisms and the efficacy of policy interventions across different supply chain segments. We develop a resilience evaluation framework integrating multi-attribute decision-making (MADM) and system dynamics (SD) for high-frequency tracking and policy-scenario simulation. Analyzing 2018–2023 data reveals significant adaptive learning, characterized by “macro-resilience memory” where recovery capabilities evolve across repeated shocks. The model demonstrates early warning value, with sharp declines in the resilience curve preceding actual disruption peaks by 1–2 months. Furthermore, evaluating indicator importance and sensitivity identifies ideal leverage points for targeted optimization under resource constraints. Policy simulations confirm that export restrictions and stockpile strategies exhibit context-dependent efficacy tied to external shock characteristics. These findings provide a transferable methodological paradigm for policymakers to dynamically optimize strategic decisions and enhance the security of critical mineral supply chains.Subject areas: Mineralogy, Natural resources

Introduction

Rare earths (REs) are included in strategic mineral resource catalogs in many countries because REs are materials for many critical and high-value products.1 The importance of REs is highlighted by their use in advanced technologies, which range from renewable energy systems to next-generation military applications and influence the economic and geopolitical developments.2,3 The security of supply of REs is, therefore, closely linked to national security, and an accurate picture of the rare earth supply chains (RESCs) should be a key concern for policymakers and regulators.4 Especially after 2010, concerns about the supply risk of REs have intensified as China has controlled the export of RE minerals to overseas.5

This highly concentrated supply landscape creates distinct strategic vulnerabilities for different global actors. For major import-dependent economies, such as the US, the European Union, and Japan, this creates acute supply chain vulnerabilities impacting everything from defense systems to high-tech manufacturing.6,7 This vulnerability has directly triggered significant geopolitical and economic competition. Global competition over RE resources has become more intense because of increasing economic and trade tensions.8 Faced with the dual pressure on RE supply and demand, major global economies are actively implementing supply chain diversification strategies to address challenges posed by China’s dominance in the RESCs. Relevant initiatives include legislative frameworks such as the EU’s Critical Raw Materials Act and the US Inflation Reduction Act.9,10 This intensifying competition can be quantified by the latest market data. According to the latest data from the US Geological 2025 Survey, China accounted for nearly 70% of global RE mining, with its output reaching 270,000 metric tons, compared to a world total of 390,000 metric tons.11 While other countries like the US (45,000 tons) and Thailand (13,000 tons) are significantly increasing their production, China’s market share remains unshaken.

However, although China dominates both in terms of reserves and production, it also faces additional risks in the context of economic globalization.12 In the context of economic globalization, China faces not only the aforementioned competitive pressures but also shares a common vulnerability with all nations to systemic shocks. The suddenness and unpredictability of emergencies cause substantial harm to society, economic structures, and people’s livelihoods.13 The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated how fast-changing disruptions can transform international trade rules while making protectionist policies and unilateral actions more common.14,15 These factors raise worries about RESC weaknesses and strategic risks.16 Thus, a systematic evaluation of rare earth supply chain resilience (RESCR) is essential, and policymakers must be enabled to simulate outcomes of various policy interventions.17 In this study, resilience is operationally defined as the system’s comprehensive ability to resist sudden shocks, react with agility, recover to a stable state, adapt to long-term pressures, and learn from past disruptions to improve future performance (Table 1).18,19,20

Summary of resilience features

However, current research that examines supply chain resilience fails to thoroughly address the complex issues within critical mineral supply chains (CMSCs), especially when focusing on REs. Firstly, most of the studies have examined supply chain resilience from a micro-perspective, focusing on individual firms.21,22,23 Consequently, they lack a systemic viewpoint. This narrow focus means that common indicator frameworks often fail to account for crucial macro-environmental factors. While factors such as geopolitical risk, government efficiency, and macro-economic factors usually have a greater impact on CMSCs, the final assessment results of indicator frameworks that do not take these factors into account may not be able to comprehensively measure resilience. Secondly, resilience assessments relying on isolated indicators are inherently static. They do not allow for dynamic tracking during unexpected disruptions nor do they enable the modeling of resilience trends under hypothetical scenarios.24,25,26 Managers urgently need a tool that can provide timely insights into the state of resilience and underpin policy decisions. Thirdly, most previous approaches do not specifically distinguish between supply chain segments, such as upstream extraction and midstream processing in terms of indicators, and do not recognize their differences.27,28,29 Different links may have different resilience weaknesses and contributions, and if they are not differentiated, the optimization conclusions obtained are not relevant and may result in a waste of resources in the subsequent improvement process. Finally, policy evaluations of interventions like export controls and stockpiling remain static because they do not include feedback loops or model how resilience adapts across different scenarios.30,31

To solve the problems, we employ the DPSIR (driver pressure-state impact response) framework to present a complex analysis that combines geopolitical elements with resource-driven factors and global supply chain dynamics while highlighting synergies between upstream and midstream activities. Then, we integrate multi-attribute decision-making (MADM) with system dynamics (SD) simulations (Figures 1 and 2) to connect static indicator models to real-time predictive capabilities, which allow for both immediate decision-making and long-term policy optimization. Our research applies this framework to analyze China’s RESC dynamics under various policy scenarios and gauges the effects of export controls and reserve stockpiling strategies on resilience through different disruption cycles. More importantly, we constructed a unique and highly timely dataset (Table 2) for China’s RESC, covering the 2018–2023 period. This was achieved by manually reviewing and integrating data from multiple official agencies, including the China Rare Earth Industry Association, the National Bureau of Statistics, the National General Administration of Customs, and the US Geological Survey. Details for our approach and datasets can be found in STAR Methods, supplemental information, and Github: https://github.com/SmallOctober/Resilience-Assessment-RESC.git.

We incorporated macro-environmental factors into our assessment indicators. Our findings show that these factors have a higher importance and sensitivity than those related to the upstream and midstream links. This finding underscores a critical limitation of conventional supply chain resilience models—their disproportionate focus on node-specific optimizations while neglecting the catalytic role of environmental enablers. Particularly, for RESCs, employment size, geopolitical risk, and government efficiency are important for resilience. To assess resilience comprehensively and multidimensionally, our study moves beyond the traditional static perspective. Instead, we chart a unique path of learning and adaptation by analyzing the resilience curve of Chinese RESCs through multiple disruptions. Our findings show that the COVID-19 shock initially showed a lengthy recovery process with low resilience, but later cycles could achieve faster recoveries, with stronger growth patterns. These trends indicate that RESCs exhibit adaptive learning behavior over time, and, in particular, recovery ability evolves in response to repeated shock. We term this cumulative effect macro-resilience memory. Unlike the localized immunity of individual firms, it is not a simple summation of micro-level immunities. Instead, it represents a more resilient systemic capability to withstand subsequent shocks, driven by policy synergies and institutionalized experience. In addition, the findings also reveal that sensitive indicators such as R&D investment and trade balance show high responsiveness to corrective interventions and, therefore, serve as ideal leverage points for swift resilience improvements despite their smaller contribution to overall resilience currently. In fact, distinguishing between supply chain segments reveals heterogeneity between the upstream and midstream links, particularly in their weak points and most important resilience factors. The voice of the mineral transaction price in the upstream extraction link constitutes the main constraint to increasing resilience, while reducing import dependence in the midstream processing link is the key. Furthermore, the effectiveness of export and reserve policies depends on the nature of external shock conditions. Our results show that the implementation of moderate export policies, alongside regular reserve depletion and replenishment cycles, strengthens resilience by boosting production and supply chain operations during typical conditions. Restrictive approaches including reserve stockpiling and export limitations prove more effective than free trade methods during extreme global disruptions like the second COVID-19 wave because they reduce external dependencies and protect supply chains from instability.

Therefore, the findings not only quantify the performance of different capabilities of China’s RESC resilience and provide policymakers with data-driven decision-making tools for short-term responses and long-term optimization strategies but also urge us to pay attention to the role of learning resilience and response resilience in facilitating the effective response to shocks in CMSCs.

Evolution of the resilience curve under multiple shocks

The curve of China’s RESCR from January 2018 to August 2023 was generated based on the descriptive statistics of the indicators (Table 3), as shown in Figure 3. The red line is the number of new diagnoses of COVID-19 in China. The data come from the National Health Commission and cover only the period from January 2020 to December 2022 due to the implementation of national policies. In this study, the number of newly diagnosed cases was used to measure the severity of COVID-19. The blue line represents the resilience value, with higher values indicating a better resilient supply chain. A negative relationship was observed between resilience performance and the number of newly confirmed cases per month. It indicates that RESCR is indeed affected by major public health security crises. Overall, China experienced three major shocks in five years. Among them, the RESCR during 2018–2019 is a normal cyclical fluctuation, 2020–2021 is the complete cycle of China’s RESCR under the first COVID-19 shock, and 2022–2023 is the complete cycle under multiple COVID-19 shocks.

Curve of Chinese RESCR

During the first entire cycle, the first sharp trough in resilience values coincided with the appearance of the first peak in newly diagnosed cases (T1). At the beginning of 2020, the impact of COVID-19, a new coronavirus, was widespread and far-reaching. During the T1 period, resilience peaked in December 2019 at 0.53 and reached its lowest point in February 2020 at 0.46, representing a 13.92% decrease in resilience performance. It can be observed from the curve that RESCR experienced a sudden decrease in a short period of time, with a large slope, which indicates that RESCR was weak at that time and could not effectively respond to the risky consequences of the emergencies. From March to April 2020, the RESCR experienced a modest rebound, with its resilience value rapidly recovering at a rate of 5.41%. This indicates that RESCR had a better react ability at that time and was able to adjust its response quickly after the emergencies. This aligns with the implementation of policies by the Chinese government at the time. However, it took more than a year for the RESCR to recover to its original level (specifically in July 2021). During this period, the index’s rebound trajectory remained relatively gradual, with an average monthly growth rate of just 0.68%, resulting in a relatively flat curve. This rather long recovery time proves that the RESCR was weak during the first cycle. The RESCR still maintained its growth trend in the second half of 2021, and the resilience value even exceeded the highest value in the 2018–2019 period, reaching 0.56. This indicates that the RESCR has a certain degree of learning ability. This lengthy recovery phase of more than a year created key macro-resilience memory imprints for the RESC system. For example, the refinement of the hospital’s emergency response program to deal with COVID-19, the experience of inter-organizational communication between government departments during crisis coordination, and so on. The accumulation of these multi-stakeholder experiences set the stage for the formation of macro-resilience memories.

During the second full cycle, multiple COVID-19 shocks at shorter intervals occurred. The entry of the COVID-19 variant into China at the beginning of 2022 saw a second peak in newly diagnosed cases, which corresponded to a second bottoming out of the RESCR values (T2). During the T2 period, resilience peaked at 0.56 in December 2021 and bottomed out at 0.42 in April 2022, representing a 24.10% decline. The level of COVID-19 impact was close in T2 compared with T1, but the plunge in the RESCR in T2 was much greater. This suggests that the resistance of RESCR did not significantly increase during the preceding two years, which may also be related to the faster spread of the COVID-19 variant and the more severe symptoms of infection. In the following five months, RESCR still showed strong react and recovery ability, the resilience value did not maintain the trough for a long time, and there was a small and timely increase. However, RESCR did not have a long enough recovery time to return to its original level as COVID-19 still had a small impact during the period. At the end of 2022, there was a third peak in the number of newly diagnosed cases (T3) as a result of a more open attitude by the Chinese government. Although the peak in new cases at T3 was much higher than those at T1 and T2, the RESCR did not produce a larger drop. The resilience trough value in T3 did not decline further from that of T2. On the contrary, T3’s resilience trough value reached 0.43817, which is 3.77% higher than T2’s lowest value. Instead, the RESCR demonstrated greater response and recovery ability, with a greater slope and steeper curve in the resilience value rebound. Facing the more severe impact of COVID-19, RESCR rebounded by 17.91% from its undervalued position in just one month. RESCR also returned to its original level in a shorter period of time than in the first cycle, while maintaining a stronger growth trend. This suggests that the recovery and learning ability of RESCR has improved significantly and that China’s RESCs may have put effort into the relevant indicators. It is worth noting that, despite the more severe loss of resistance resilience caused by the repeated shock of the second cycle, the prior macroscopic resilience memory of the RESC system also played an important role by accelerating the resilience recovery rate.

To statistically validate the visually distinct performance periods observed in Figure 3, we conducted a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For this analysis, the calculated monthly resilience score was set as the dependent variable, and the three 24-month periods—phase 1 (2018–2019, pre-COVID), phase 2 (2020–2021, shock 1), and phase 3 (2022–2023, shock 2)—were defined as the independent categorical factors. The ANOVA yielded a highly significant result (F(2, 69) = 276.48, p < 0.001), confirming that the mean resilience scores across the three phases are statistically distinct. To determine precisely which phases differed from one another, we then performed a Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test.

The results (presented in Table 4) are conclusive. The test revealed that all three phases are statistically different from each other (all p-adj < 0.001). This provides robust quantitative support for our narrative: the initial COVID-19 shock (phase 2) caused a statistically significant drop in resilience compared with the pre-COVID baseline (phase 1). Crucially, the system’s subsequent performance in the adaptive phase (phase 3) was also statistically distinct from both the initial shock (phase 2) and the original baseline (phase 1). This confirms that the resilience system did not merely “bounce back” to its original state, but rather evolved into a new, quantitatively different adaptive state, strongly supporting our core findings of dynamic learning and resilience memory.

In particular, Figure 3 also demonstrates the early warning value of the resilience evaluation system. This is because it can be observed that the points in time when the resilience values fall off a cliff in both the 2020–2021 and 2022–2023 cycles are earlier than the observed points in time when COVID-19 formed its peak. This fully demonstrates that the resilience evaluation system we developed not only is capable of accurately evaluating the state of RESCs at all times but also has an early warning effect. In other words, we provide a resilience monitoring and early warning method that can be extended to other CMSCs.

Prioritizing optimization through indicator analysis

The weights of the indicators calculated by the CRITIC method are shown in Figure 4A, and they reflect how important an indicator is to the resilience of the system. The five most important indicators for the upstream of RESCR are: C32 operating profit (0.0343), C44 import dependency ratio (0.0318), C43 balance of trade (0.0316), C51 research and development investment (0.0279), and C35 price (0.0277). The five most important indicators for the midstream of RESCR are: C410 export dependency ratio (0.0425), C47 imports (0.0331), C53 research and development investment (0.0305), C37 operating profit (0.0295), and C63 sustainable development (0.0264). Finally, the three most important indicators for the entire supply chain are: C22 political stability (0.0642), C12 employment scale (0.0598), and C23 regulatory quality (0.0566).

Indicator analysis chart for RESC

From Figure 4A, it can be clearly observed that macro-environmental factors are more important, on average, for RESCR than the factors related to upstream mineral extraction and midstream smelting and processing. The importance of factors like employment size, geopolitical risk, and government efficiency can only be identified when macro-environmental indicators are included in the assessment system. For this study, we operationally defined the geopolitical risk by using the World Bank WGI indicator C22 “political stability.” This confirms that in addition to focusing on the local optimization of single supply chain segments for resilience enhancement, national policymakers should also strive to stabilize macro-environmental factors. In this study, macro-environmental factors refer to the set of indicators within our framework that capture the overarching systemic context, rather than specific operational activities. These correspond to the indicators in our C1 “environment & Security” (e.g., C12 employment scale) and C2 “social administration” (e.g., C21 “government effectiveness”) categories. According to the definition of these indicators, it can be found that the upstream needs to pay attention to how to use the technical advantages of RE mining to improve China’s voice in pricing, and at the same time, on the premise of maintaining the independence of RE ore imports, expand the international trade surplus as much as possible and increase profits. For the midstream sector, the focus should be 2-fold. First, it is crucial to increase the added value of RE products by improving the processing technology and expanding international trade. Second, the sector must also address environmental problems from manufacturing to achieve sustainable development. This means that policymakers and managers involved in the supply chain should consider how to enhance these important indicators, as these can significantly enhance RESCR.

The sensitivity analysis results of various indexes based on the model are shown in Figure 4B. In the sensitivity analysis, the higher the sensitivity value of an indicator, the faster and greater is the effect that can be obtained by adjusting the indicator, while the lower is the sensitivity value and the more stable is the indicator. The four most sensitive indicators for the upstream of RESCR are: C43 balance of trade (0.0071), C32 operating profit (0.0054), C51 research and development investment (0.0038), and C61 sustainable development (0.0037). The four most sensitive indicators for the midstream of RESCR are: C49 import dependency ratio (0.0098), C47 imports (0.0061), C53 research and development investment (0.0049), and C37 operating profit (0.0041). The three most sensitive indicators for the entire supply chain are: C22 political stability (0.0255), C12 employment scale (0.0242), and C23 regulatory quality (0.0214). Compared with Figure 4A, it can be found that important indicators are not necessarily sensitive. Although some important indicators contribute a lot to RESCR, they are difficult to be changed. Although some sensitive indicators are easier to improve, their contribution to RESCR is not high. Therefore, policymakers and supply chain managers should pay special attention to these important and sensitive indicators and prioritize them for optimization. At the same time, significant differences in the importance and sensitivity of upstream and midstream indicators were clearly observed. For example, the import dependence of the midstream smelting and processing sector was found to contribute significantly to resilience, while the import dependence of the upstream mining sector contributed relatively less. Without distinguishing between supply chain segments, the operationalization of the resilience assessment results would be significantly weakened.

In addition to analyzing the resilience curve from the perspective of time, a specific time point can also be selected to analyze the transverse cross-section of RESCR at that time. Here, five time points representing different resilience states are selected as examples, and the radar map (Figure 4C) is drawn according to their resilience values under the five dimensions, so that the weak link of RESCR can be analyzed in a more targeted manner. By comparing the current situation of China’s RESC with the sensitive and important indicators obtained previously, the optimization sequence can be divided.

First, the sensitive indicators with low scores are selected, and then ranked according to their importance (C21 government effectiveness >C24 rule of law >C51 upstream R&D investment >C52 midstream R&D investment >C49 midstream import dependency ratio). These weak indicators are the easiest to adjust and upgrade; so, they should be used as the optimization direction of China’s RESCR in the first stage. Second, the indicators with high scores are removed from all the sensitive indicators, and then ranked according to their importance (C35 upstream price >C63 midstream sustainable development). These indicators, which have room for improvement and are easy to adjust and upgrade, should be used as the optimization direction in the second stage. Finally, indicators that are currently poor and difficult to change (C13 defense expenditure >C48 midstream balance of trade >C36 midstream production >C39 midstream trade competitiveness >C31 upstream production >C41 upstream exports >C34 upstream trade competitiveness >C45 upstream export dependency ratio >C62 upstream resource reserves) are considered.

Effectiveness of export and reserve policies in different scenarios

SD method provides a simulation function for policy makers, and we focused on how policies related to exports and reserves in the RESC would affect RESCR. Figure 5A simulates three export scenarios, with the different types of export policies all starting in January 2018 and not changing midway through. In terms of the overall trend, RESCR is the weakest under the policy of strictly banning the export of RE ores and processed RE products. This proves that it is not feasible to give up the international market if China wants to rely on its current advantages in terms of production capability, as the international supply chain also provides sales channels for Chinese RESC products, which can also increase resilience in the event of a lack of demand in China. In contrast, a policy of completely liberalized exports shows mixed results. While this scenario yields stronger overall RESCR than strict export restrictions, it does not perform well as China’s current policy when facing unexpected events.

Simulated RESCR in different policy scenarios

Specifically, in the absence of unexpected shocks, the current policy of China’s RESCs is close to free trade, and the RESCR is slightly higher than that of the strictly restricted export status, which suggests that a free trade environment is conducive to strengthening the RESCR in normal times. During the first COVID-19 outbreak cycle, the difference in the RESCR between the two policies of restricting export and free export was close to 0.05. The impact of the first COVID-19 outbreak was more confined to China, and the international RESCs were less affected; so, the policy of allowing free export was favorable to China’s RESCs to meet the supply with the help of the international environment, which led to a strong recovery of RESCR. In contrast, the second COVID-19 outbreak was a very different story, as foreign governments treated the COVID-19 medical policy more negatively than China did, and the international supply chain was far more affected than the Chinese domestic ones. During the simulation period from July 2021 to July 2022, compared with the restricted-export policy, the decline in RESCR under the free-export policy was 0.03 points higher. This suggests that during the second outbreak, relying exclusively on international markets to absorb domestic production capacity would result in a much larger shock to supply chain resilience than enforcing a strict export ban. Therefore, based on the simulation results of the SD, policymakers can judge not only the adaptability of different policies in different periods but also the state of the international supply chain based on their own national data.

Figure 5B simulates three reserve scenarios, with the different types of reserve policies all starting in January 2018 and not changing midway through. In the two years without COVID-19 shocks, the highest RESCR is for the policy scenario that allows depletion of RE reserves within reasonable limits and timely replenishment of the reserves. This suggests that depleting a certain amount of RE reserves will lead to an increase in the production of RE products in the absence of an unexpected shock, but the implementation of this type of policy is conditional on the ability to simultaneously enhance the development capability of RE mines and replenish reserves in a timely manner. The next policy scenario is to increase reserves on top of the status quo, but RESCR is only about 0.002 higher than the status quo due to the current difficulty in developing RE mines globally. The policy of increasing reserves performed the best in both cycles of the COVID-19 shock, which clearly proves the importance of RE reserves. Conversely, under the policy of depleting reserves, once the system is hit by an unexpected shock, the RESCR will drop significantly. Just as shown in Figure 5B simulation, RESCR experiences a substantial drop of nearly 0.025 in the second cycle.

Discussion

Adaptive learning and macro-resilience memory

By combining MADM and SD, our analytical results help fill the gap of the lack of tools to quantify the resilience and policy effects of CMSCs. Overall, we reveal, for the first time, the non-linear resilience recovery mechanism of China’s RESCs under multiple contingency shocks represented by COVID-19. RESCR exhibits a greater slope of recovery under the second round of multiple shocks (Figure 3), suggesting the effectiveness of China’s coordinated policy responses and institutionalized emergency safeguard measures. Moreover, RESCR, as a whole, shows a step-up result, reflecting that the supply chain resilience system possesses a significant learning capability. We call the enhancement of this learning ability macro-resilience memory. This refers to the accumulative effect where a supply chain system learns from external shocks through policy synergies and the institutionalization of experience across multiple actors, such as governments and enterprises. Different from the traditional micro-perspective of a single enterprise through the increase in inventory and supplier diversification initiatives to enhance immunity, macro-resilience memory is not a simple summation of the immunity of each enterprise within the system, which accounts for the resilience of the non-linear recovery mechanism. This macro-resilience memory has a much longer time lag, and its effects tend to show up in the next shock cycle, unlike the immediate impact of micro-firms’ enhancement measures. These findings complement the missing perspective on the portrayal of learning capability in traditional resilience studies. They further corroborate the need to introduce a time dimension in resilience assessment to measure the historical shock memory effect, which may otherwise underestimate the system’s long-term resilience adaptability and resistance.

Diagnostic and predictive value of the framework

Combining the resilience curves and the cross-section analysis of the indicators, our results not only proved that the indicator system and calculation method constructed in this study have a prospective early warning function but also provided guidance for the optimization of the sequence of “high sensitivity + high significance - high sensitivity + low significance - low sensitivity + high significance.” Specifically, the mutation time points of the RESCR curves (November 2019 and November 2021) are 1–2 months before the peak of COVID19 diagnosis. Based on the sensitivity, importance, and contribution results of the indicators, it is recommended that China’s RESCs focus on monitoring and optimizing R&D investment and midstream product import dependence, as they are the most desirable points of resilience leverage during a crisis. Furthermore, upstream RE mineral prices and employment size can be used as leading indicators of supply chain disruptions, i.e., fluctuations above a certain threshold should trigger a certain start-up reserve release program or other policy actions to address the risk of potential disruptions. Afterward, policymakers can judge the effectiveness of the decision in real time and adjust iteratively based on the feedback of the resilience curve. After the assessment indicators incorporated the consideration of macro-environmental factors, we found that their importance and sensitivity are higher than those related to upstream and midstream links. This suggests that it is not enough to improve resilience indicators for a particular link in the supply chain, but that attention should also be paid to the stabilization of macro-environmental factors. For RESCs in particular, employment size, geopolitical risk, and government efficiency are important for resilience.

Therefore, the empirical results validate the applicability of the research methodology for early warning in the field of RE resources and provide a relocatable framework for risk management in the global supply chain of critical minerals. Supply chain managers can establish a prioritized optimization framework by identifying key performance indicators, which differentiate between important, sensitive, and synergistic metrics for both short-term and long-term improvements. The framework allows policymakers and practitioners to take preemptive measures against vulnerabilities and improve supply chain effectiveness with restricted resources by focusing on performance indicators, which show significant impact and respond well to interventions. This research offers solutions to urgent needs while creating an evidence-based foundation for policy decisions amid growing global supply chain instabilities. Furthermore, we emphasize that a primary contribution of this study is the development of a transferable methodological paradigm. Access to reliable, granular data on critical strategic minerals is notoriously difficult for researchers outside a host country. By integrating SD with MADM, we provide a transparent, replicable framework that enables the dynamic tracking and scenario simulation of resilience. By presenting China as a comprehensive case study and making our assessment model publicly available, we offer a robust paradigm that scholars in other nations can adapt to assess the resilience of their own critical supply chains, thereby fostering a global research community dedicated to this challenge.

Global implications: Policy simulation as a strategic tool

The resilience assessment framework proposed in this study enables decision-makers to conduct near-real-time monitoring and predictive scenario planning. We have, therefore, provided actionable recommendations for policymakers and supply chain managers regarding export control measures and reserve stock strategies. Our simulations confirmed that the efficacy of these policies is highly context dependent. Specifically, our studies showed that export restrictions work to lessen shock impacts during worldwide disruptions, while liberalized export approaches help build resilience in stable times.

This insight is particularly evident in China’s post-pandemic reserve strategy. Our simulation results show that the resilience level under the “increasing reserves” policy scenario is significantly higher than that under the “depleting reserves” scenario. This clearly indicates that China, having experienced the supply shocks of COVID-19, recognizes the potential status quo of insufficient RE reserves and is likely to maintain its strategic plan to both explore resources and build stockpiles. These findings affirm that flexible, context-driven resilience management, which aligns specific policies with external conditions, is paramount.

This finding of context-dependent efficacy provides a direct, data-driven lens for viewing the fierce strategic debates currently underway globally. The resilience challenge is not monolithic; optimal strategies fluctuate dramatically based on a nation’s specific position in the global supply chain. For example, import-dependent nations like the US and the EU face high vulnerability in the “free-exporting” scenario, which relies on unstable foreign supplies. Unlike Chen et al., who argue from a trade-network perspective that US-China interdependence inherently stabilizes global supply chains, our SD simulations underscore that during acute exogenous shocks, relying on foreign supply networks without robust domestic reserve mechanisms leads to severe resilience degradation.27 Consequently, their recent sweeping industrial policies, such as the US Inflation Reduction Act and the EU’s Critical Raw Materials Act, represent a massive strategic pivot to mitigate these exact risks through “friend-shoring,” domestic subsidies, and decoupling.

Furthermore, an explicit comparison with Western mitigation strategies highlights the uniqueness of the Chinese context. While Vivoda notes that Western nations prioritize “friend-shoring” through international alliances to bypass concentrated hubs,8 our findings reveal that network diversification alone may lack the rapid “macro-resilience memory” generated by a centralized, state-regulated system. In contrast to Gholz and Hughes, who posited that supply disruptions are largely neutralized by passive global market adjustments, our 2018–2023 evaluation proves that contemporary RESC stability requires proactive, policy-mediated intervention.32 China, as the dominant supplier, strategically utilizes regulated exports and national reserves. Our simulations align with this approach, confirming that such controls buffer against high-frequency shocks more effectively than relying on fragmented international markets. Meanwhile, emerging upstream producers, such as Australia and Thailand, face a different strategic calculus: how to leverage their resources without succumbing to global market volatility or external processing dependence.31

This divergence proves that there is no universal “best policy.” The central strategic question is which policy mix optimizes resilience given a nation’s specific vulnerabilities. This is precisely where the core contribution of our study lies: the provision of a transferable methodological paradigm. While other nations cannot replicate China’s specific data, they can and should adopt our integrated MADM-SD simulation framework. By inputting their own national data, policymakers in the US, EU, or Australia can rigorously test these exact trade-offs. Our framework provides the actionable, evidence-based simulation tool necessary for any nation to navigate these complex strategic decisions and enhance its own supply chain security.

In conclusion, our analysis highlights the necessity of linking indicator-based static resilience assessments with policy scenario simulations and building dynamic models based on feedback mechanisms to capture the resilience performance of CMSCs and adjust policies in real time. This actionable tool quantifies the resilience curve, analyzes the ideal optimization path, and simulates policy effects. Consequently, it can help nations enhance control over their critical mineral resources. Furthermore, it can reduce strategic miscalculations amid geopolitical tensions, thereby promoting global strategic mutual trust and global supply chain cooperation.

Limitations of the study

This research is not without limitations. Considering the focus of international interest in the RESCs, the study examined only different types of policy scenarios for both exports and reserves, but it can produce new understandings through the use of more policy instruments in analysis. In addition, our model must focus on a specific set of metrics explored in the literature. Future research could broaden the scope to investigate the impact of other factors on supply chain resilience, such as evolving ESG pressures or granular logistics vulnerabilities. Moreover, to address the challenge of obtaining high-frequency macro-economic data, we rely not only on meticulously integrating multiple official data sources but also on highly validated authoritative proxy indicators. Specifically, we operationalize the geopolitical risk, using the World Bank’s “political stability” indicator, which primarily reflects internal domestic stability rather than external geopolitical friction. While alternative macro-indices like the “global peace index” exist, they similarly suffer from temporal lag and conceptually focus on societal violence or militarization, rather than the non-violent economic statecraft most relevant to CMSCs. Therefore, developing high-frequency, strictly external metrics to better isolate geopolitical shocks remains an important direction for future research. While the use of such alternative indicators is a standard and necessary practice in macro-economic research, we acknowledge that they remain indirect measures of the underlying phenomena, constituting a limitation of this study. Limited by the availability of data collection, we also encourage scholars from different countries, especially critical mineral-holding countries, to conduct comparative studies based on actual data from their own countries.

Method details

In order to achieve quantitative evaluation and policy simulation functions for supply chain resilience, we integrated multi-attribute decision-making and system dynamics approaches to develop a methodological framework and usage guidelines as demonstrated in Figure 1.

Establishment of the resilience indicators framework

Our framework’s starting point is to operationalize our definition of resilience. The system’s comprehensive ability to adapt, resist, react, recover, and learn.18,19,20 To quantify this multifaceted concept, we first summarize the common concerns of researchers from relevant theoretical and empirical literature (Table 1): (1) what capability resilience has, (2) what are the characteristics of this capability, and (3) what are the features of indicators to measure this characteristic.

It can be found that the various abilities included in the resilience theory are also divided into five categories like the DPSIR model, but previous studies often focused only on certain abilities of resilience. To overcome this gap, our study required a framework that is both comprehensive and macro-level, moving beyond the firm-centric focus common in the literature.38,39 We therefore selected the DPSIR model, a mature framework widely applied for complex socio-ecological and sustainability assessments.40,41 The DPSIR model offers two unique advantages for this research: first, it ensures a systematic, comprehensive selection of indicators that captures the critical macro-environmental factors and policy interventions that micro-level models omit. Second, its inherent causal-chain logic (D→P→S→I→R) provides the essential theoretical foundation for mapping the feedback relationships in our subsequent System Dynamics model.

In addition to measuring resilience more comprehensively across a range of capabilities, we should also consider the unique challenges and risk factors that critical minerals need to face compared to other supply chains. Specifically, Gholz and Hughes analyzed the impact of geopolitics on market in the case of China’s embargo of REs against Japan in 2010.32 Kirchmaier et al. studied the relationship among price, security and policy through metal crime cases in the United Kingdom.42 In addition, Boer et al. identified metal-specific demand shocks, estimated supply resilience, and study the price impact of the transition in a structural scenario analysis.31 Hsu and Wu demonstrate the role of inventories in matching supply and demand using Chinese customs data on metal imports and firm-level inventories in the metal processing industry.43 Besides, Li et al. through text mining of USPTO patents, found that an increase in the supply of rare metals was positively correlated with patent output.44 It can be found that the international community generally focuses on the impact of security, social administration, market, trade, innovation and sustainable development on the risk of the critical minerals. Therefore, evaluation indicators for critical minerals should cover the six dimensions of political environment and security, efficiency of social functioning, state of the market, international trade, innovative technologies and sustainability.

Meanwhile, previous studies have also summarized some attributes of resilience and accumulated a large number of indicators to measure these attributes. We construct the indicator pool with reference to the literature mentioned in Table 1 as shown in Table 2. Detailed definition descriptions and data acquisition sources for each variable can be found in Table S1.

Data collection

This study takes China’s RESC under the background of COVID-19 as the research object. It is worth emphasizing that the study used a unique data set, which we manually reviewed and integrated publicly available data from official agencies such as the Association of China Rare Earth Industry, the National Bureau of Statistics, the General Administration of Customs of China, the Ministry of Natural Resources of China, and the United States Geological Survey. Information on data sources for specific indicators can be found in Table S1. The unique data set has apparent advantages in coverage breadth; it contains indicators in traditional resilience studies and breaks explicitly down different supply chain links, providing a more comprehensive and detailed analysis perspective. In addition, our unique data set is the most up-to-date data on this particular sector of China’s rare earth supply chain for 2018-2023, and is highly time-sensitive and scarce. Specifically, the time frame is from January 2018 to December 2023, and the historical data includes 72 months. According to the indicator system of the RESCR established, the data is selected for a total of 34 indicators. Thus, the initial evaluation matrix can be constructed with m(m=72) objects and n(n=34) indicators.

To provide a comprehensive overview of our input data, we conducted a full descriptive statistical analysis of all 34 indicators. The summary statistics for the entire 72-month dataset are presented in Table 3. Resilience assessment and sensitivity analysis

We argue that resilience has both static and dynamic properties.

•Static property: At any point in time, resilience can be quantified as a specific value that represents the performance of the subject at that time.

•Dynamic property: The ability of the subject to perform a dimension of the evaluation at different times by comparing the trend of resilience values over a period of time.

Dynamic property: The ability of the subject to perform a dimension of the evaluation at different times by comparing the trend of resilience values over a period of time.

Based on these static attributes, we can plot resilience values over time to form a resilience curve. This curve, in turn, allows us to visualize the dynamic attributes of resilience as they evolve. Therefore, the calculation method of resilience should be able to make full use of the information of the original data and accurately reflect the gap between the calculation results, and at the same time should be able to reflect the level of multiple dimensions of resilience. Therefore, the TOPSIS method, which is widely recognized for its robustness in dynamic performance evaluation over time, is the most appropriate for evaluating resilience.45

Since the RESCR system we designed should be real-time, objective and fully automated. Therefore, the traditional Delphi method of obtaining indicator weights cannot achieve the purpose of timely calculation. And the CRITIC method can completely use the objective attributes of the data itself to comprehensively calculate the weights through the variability and conflict of the indicators.45 As a result, this study chooses to use the CRITIC method to objectively assign weights to the indicators.

For an indicator that is sensitive and has a large effect in a certain stage, targeted suggestions on the promotion path can be obtained according to the theoretical connotation analysis of the indicator. Since the relationship between all indexes and all evaluation objects is established by TOPSIS, the change of each index value may have an impact on the ranking of the global index value, which cannot be quantitatively described by the formula. So the sensitivity interval cannot be deduced backward according to the formula. As a result, we define the sensitivity of an indicator as the absolute value of the difference between the resilience value after a 10 percent rise and the resilience value after a 10 percent fall in the historical average of this indicator.

Resilience simulation and policy scenario modelling

System dynamics (SD) modeling is essential for overcoming the static limitations of DPSIR-MADM assessments. While the MADM assessment framework can quantify past resilience, it cannot model the complex feedback loops driving system performance. Furthermore, it cannot be used for policy testing simulations, which form the core of this study. Therefore, we adopted SD model, the authoritative method for modeling complex feedback systems.46 As recent literature on critical supply chains confirms,47 the primary value of SD models lies in their ability to create policy simulation models for risk assessment and testing alternative solutions.

The process of building the SD model is divided into a commissioning stage and an analysing stage. The SD can be used for prediction and simulation of RESCR only when the SD in the commissioning stage passes the error test. The SD simulations and tests we develop are done in Vensim PLE. In the analysis stage, SD can be used to implement the prediction and simulation functions for RESCR. To realise the prediction of the future condition of RESCR, the time scale of SD needs to be adjusted to 72+x (x is the length of time we want to predict), and then the simulation data of SD will be inputted to the CRITIC-TOPSIS method, and finally the prediction result of RESCR will be obtained.

To realise the simulation of RESCR under different policy scenarios, we need to adjust the parameter equations of the relevant variables in the SD according to the policy content first. Then the simulation data of SD is input to CRITIC-TOPSIS method, and finally the simulation results of RESCR under different policy scenarios are obtained. By analysing the simulation results, it will be beneficial for policy makers to understand the real impact of policies on the rare earth supply chain. We found that the current policy research on the RESC mainly focuses on two aspects: (1) export policy; and (2) reserve policy. Therefore, we have modelled the impact of different policies on RESCR separately, and the assumptions of the relevant variables can be found in the Table S6. Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses, data preprocessing, and visualizations were primarily performed using Python (version 3.11.7). The System Dynamics modeling and policy simulations were conducted using Vensim PLE (version 10.1.3).

In our quantitative analysis, the exact sample size is n = 72, which represents the 72 consecutive months of data collected from January 2018 to December 2023 for each indicator.

Basic descriptive statistics were calculated for all 34 evaluation indicators, including the definitions of the center (mean) and measures of dispersion (standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values). The statistical summaries are located in Table 3 of the main text and Table S2 of the Supplemental Information. To validate the relationships and potential multicollinearity among the indicators, we conducted both Pearson linear correlation and Spearman rank correlation analyses. The comprehensive statistical details, including the correlation coefficients and the corresponding heatmap, can be found in the Supplemental Information (Tables S3 and S4; Figure S1).

For the objective weighting of indicators, the CRITIC method was employed, which strictly quantifies both the contrast intensity (measured by SD) and the conflict (measured by correlation) of the data. Furthermore, to evaluate the statistical significance of resilience differences across various simulated policy scenarios, we utilized a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test. In all statistical tests, a p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The specific F-statistics and p-values are detailed in the results section and the corresponding figure legends.

Finally, the historical fit and predictive reliability of the System Dynamics model were quantitatively validated using the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) and the Coefficient of Variation of the Root Mean Square Error (CV_RMSE). The specific validation error limits and statistical results are reported in the method details section.
