Lived experiences of personal recovery among individuals with severe mental illnesses: an interpretative phenomenology approach
Authors: Meng-Meng Lyu, Yi-Jun Fan, Cong-Hong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersonal recovery is a self-broadening process aimed at living a meaningful life beyond severe mental illnesses. Although personal recovery has become a well-known concept in most Western countries, it remains under-recognized in non-Western countries. In China, personal recovery is situated within a heterogeneously developed community mental health system in which family-based care and neighborhood governance jointly structure access to resources and opportunities for social participation, limiting the transferability of recovery frameworks derived from Western or diasporic Chinese settings. Therefore, the current study aimed to explore the experiences and meanings of personal recovery for individuals living with severe mental illnesses in China.MethodsTwelve participants were selected by purposive sampling from four community mental health centers for face-to-face interviews. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with these participants. Data were analyzed using interpretative phenomenological analysis.ResultsFive themes were identified: (1) Reintegration within Society, (2) Personal Responsibility within Family, (3) Engagement with Reality, (4) Regaining a Sense of Control, and (5) Reconstructing Meaning in Life.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that established personal recovery frameworks are applicable to adults living with severe mental illness in China, while identifying context-specific themes. In the context of China, the recovery of individuals living with severe mental illness has been shown to priorities the significance of social reintegration and the role of family responsibility.

Background

Severe mental illnesses (SMIs), including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder, represent a significant public health concern worldwide, posing burdens on individuals, families, and healthcare systems [1]. Although 13.5%- 58% of individuals achieve recovery with advances in clinical care, quantifying “recovery” remains difficult because estimates vary by diagnosis and outcome definition [2, 3]. Traditionally, clinical recovery in mental health has referred to the reduction or remission of psychiatric symptoms, improvement in social and occupational functioning, and the restoration of capacities often targeted by medical interventions and standardized outcome measures. However, the concept of personal recovery, which reflects a subjective, lived experience of transformation rather than symptom resolution alone, has gained importance in both research and practice [4, 5]. In the recovery paradigm introduced by Anthony, personal recovery is understood as “a deeply personal, unique process of changing one’s attitudes, values, feelings, goals, skills, and/or roles” and entails “living a satisfying, hopeful, and contributing life even with limitations caused by the illness,” including experiences such as overcoming stigma and redefining one’s sense of purpose despite ongoing symptoms [6].

The shift toward personal recovery has driven policy and service reforms in Western countries [7, 8]. Countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia have integrated the concept of personal recovery into their mental health care frameworks, promoting a more holistic approach to recovery [9–11]. Community rehabilitation services guided by the concept of personal recovery, including symptom management, social skills training, and vocational rehabilitation training, have improved participants’ self-care, social adaptation, and employment abilities [12–14]. However, the application of personal recovery frameworks is less evident in non-Western countries, where mental health services are dominated by clinician-led or family-driven approaches, with limited attention being paid to the perspectives, needs, and lived experiences of individuals with SMIs, resulting in a gap between clinical treatment and the holistic recovery process [15].

A transition toward recovery-oriented mental health care frameworks in non-Western contexts is only possible if a consensus on the conceptualization of personal recovery is achieved. However, the conceptualization of personal recovery has relied heavily on Western narratives of experiences with mental illness, and there is a lack of evidence regarding how people experience personal recovery in Asia [16]. The process of personal recovery is intertwined with culture, which shapes individuals’ attitudes, behaviors, and perceptions [17]. The concept of personal recovery is derived from Western contexts and emphasizes individual autonomy and self-determination, may not fully capture the nuances of personal recovery in non-Western cultural settings, where collectivism, family obligations, and social harmony may impact how mental illness and recovery are interpreted [18]. For example, in Asian cultures, family involvement is considered a key component of recovery. Filial piety, a cultural norm that expects children to demonstrate respect, obedience, and care towards parents, and to uphold family obligations, can influence how families support individuals living with mental illness [19]. These cultural differences complicate the application of Western mental health care frameworks in non-Western contexts, emphasizing the importance of developing culturally sensitive approaches to recovery.

An increasing number of qualitative studies have underscored that recovery from SMI is a socially embedded and community-mediated process, shaped by cultural norms, family relationships, and local service environments rather than clinical improvement alone. Studies conducted in Hong Kong and other Chinese diasporic contexts suggest that community-based recovery is often understood in terms of regaining everyday functioning, taking family responsibility, and restoring socially valued roles within one’s immediate social environment [20, 21]. A study conducted in Chinese immigrants further highlights how recovery in the community is negotiated through relational obligations and culturally informed moral expectations [22]. In addition, evidence from other Asian settings further emphasizes the central role of families, community acceptance, and social participation in shaping recovery trajectories for people living with SMI [23–25].

Despite these insights, most of the existing Chinese-language evidence is drawn from Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia, or immigrant communities. In contrast to Hong Kong, Taiwan, or other diasporic settings, community-based mental health services in Mainland China remain unevenly developed, with families assuming primary responsibility for long-term care and recovery unfolding within tightly structured neighborhood and grassroots governance systems [26, 27]. Within this context, recovery is shaped not only by individual efforts and family caregiving but also by everyday forms of community surveillance, administrative classification, and informal social regulation, through which access to resources, participation in community life, and the legitimacy of recovery claims are negotiated [28]. Individuals living with SMI therefore navigate recovery at the intersection of familial obligation, constrained service availability, and community-level governance, a configuration that differs qualitatively from settings where community mental health services are more institutionalized [29]. These structural and relational conditions suggest that recovery processes in Mainland China cannot be adequately captured by frameworks derived from other Chinese or Western contexts, underscoring the need for empirically grounded research that examines how recovery is understood, enacted, and sustained in everyday community life within the Mainland Chinese sociocultural and institutional landscape [16].

Therefore, the present study aims to explore how people with SMIs experience personal recovery in Mainland China, which might advance culturally and structurally grounded understandings of community-based recovery and facilitate the development of recovery-oriented interventions that are both effective and respectful of their beliefs and values.

Methods

Design

The present study adopted an interpretive phenomenological approach and semi-structured interviews in order to examine how adults living with SMI experience and interpret personal recovery in everyday community life. The present study elected to adopt a phenomenological approach, as the objective thereof was not to formulate a systematic theoretical framework concerning recovery processes, but rather to furnish a comprehensive exposition of the significations, embodied practices, and relational contexts through which recovery is conceptualized and enacted by participants. This methodological approach is well suited to capturing the subjective and context-dependent nature of personal recovery, including how recovery is negotiated within family roles and community settings [30]. For this study, the Consolidated criteria on reporting qualitative research (COREQ) were followed [31].

Setting

Participants were recruited from January to May 2024 in Shanghai, China, through the “Sunshine Soul Park” (Yangguang Xin Yuan) program operating across four community mental health centers. These facilities, administered by the Shanghai Disabled Persons’ Federation, function as community-based psychosocial rehabilitation centers designed for individuals with SMI who are clinically stable and residing in the community, often under formal guardianship arrangements consistent with Mainland China’s civil welfare framework. The centers offer structured daytime services, including psychosocial support, social skills training, recreational activities, and occupational rehabilitation. These services are explicitly aimed at enhancing social functioning and community integration, while actively countering pervasive social stigma and self-stigma associated with mental illness. Participants were typically referred via community health stations or social welfare channels and resided in adjacent communities. The composition of the workforce is dictated by localized administrative and funding protocols, with social workers constituting the primary personnel and receiving compensation that is aligned with municipal public welfare wage standards.

Participants

Participants were recruited according to the following inclusion criteria: (i) aged 18–65 years; (ii) meeting the study definition of SMI, operationalized as: a clinician-confirmed ICD-11 diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and other nonorganic psychotic illness; concurrent marked functional impairment in one or more major life domains (work/education, family/social functioning, or self-care); and a course requiring sustained treatment or management, with an illness duration of ≥ 1 year; and (iii) self-identification as being in recovery or having recovered from their mental health condition. Individuals with severe physical disability, current substance use disorder, or intellectual disability (clinically assessed) were excluded.

Procedures

The study was initiated after the ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the authors’ affiliation (SJUPN-202126). Before the interview, the interviewees were informed of the purpose of the study and were assured that the interview would be recorded but their personal information would not be used for data analysis, and that code ID would be used in the data analysis process instead of their real names. After obtaining consent, the informed consent form was signed by all of the interviewees.

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants with diverse characteristics that may shape recovery narratives, including diagnosis, gender, age, illness duration, and level of engagement in community mental health services. The first author purposively approached individuals receiving services from collaborating community mental health rehabilitation centers (n = 19). Personal recovery was briefly explained as a person-defined process of living a meaningful life with agency and hope despite ongoing symptoms, to ensure a shared understanding of the concept. Each individual was then asked, “Do you think you have recovered from your mental illness?” Of those approached, 2 declined participation, and 17 consented, self-identified as being in recovery, and were interviewed. During data collection, five interviews were discontinued and excluded from analysis due to participants’ limited ability to engage meaningfully, including symptom instability or cognitive difficulties. Recruitment and data collection proceeded iteratively, with participants approached sequentially using the same procedures until thematic saturation was achieved.

The initial interview guide was developed based on the research team’s familiarity with previous studies on personal recovery, and was further informed by one pertinent qualitative study to guide the identification of core interview domains [32]. The initial interview guide was then subjected to validation through group discussion with a multidisciplinary panel of five mental health experts: two board-certified psychiatrists with over 10 years of clinical experience in SMI, two licensed social workers with master’s-level training and specialization in psychiatric rehabilitation, and one clinical psychologist with expertise in psychological intervention design. Experts were recruited through the professional network of the Shanghai Mental Health Center and had no conflicts of interest or direct clinical relationships with the participants. Interview questions for recovery experience

One-to-one, semi-structured interviews were conducted by the corresponding author, a registered nurse with > 10 years of psychiatric clinical experience and doctoral training. The interviewer had > 5 years of research experience with people living with mental illness, formal training in qualitative interviewing, and a track record of peer-reviewed qualitative publications. The areas explored in the interview were the meaning of personal recovery from each participant’s perspective, factors that have influenced each participant’s recovery process, and how they perceive their own recovery process. The interviews took place in the consultation rooms within the center to ensure privacy and comfort. Participants were encouraged to lead the conversation and share their experiences and insights. The interview was audio recorded, and the interviewee’s non-verbal movements were recorded with interview notes. Interviews were done in Chinese and ranged between 37 and 61 min, averaging 48 min.

Data analysis

The audio-recorded interviews were transcribed into verbatim transcripts manually by the corresponding author (LCH) and were then analyzed following the steps of Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) given by Smith and Osborn [33]. Data analysis was conducted in Chinese to retain participants’ original meanings. Initial annotations relevant to recovery were developed and transformed into emergent themes at the individual case level. Connections among emergent themes were examined and clustered into higher-order sub-themes. This procedure was applied across cases to identify shared and differing patterns. Themes were iteratively reviewed and refined, resulting in a master table of themes.

Researcher reflexivity and positionality were considered throughout data collection and analysis. All of the authors have academic training in social psychology or working experience in mental health hospitals, which informed sensitivity to participants’ narratives. To reduce potential bias, prior assumptions related to recovery were documented and revisited during the analytic process. Bracketing was treated as an ongoing analytic stance rather than a one-time procedure, allowing interpretations to remain grounded in participants’ accounts while remaining open to unexpected meanings. Three research team members (LMM, FYJ and LCH) independently reviewed transcripts, codes, and themes, followed by discussion to reach consensus. In addition, a focus group with eight study participants was conducted in the final stages of the analysis to validate the key findings. The group’s feedback helped confirm the interpretation of major themes, including the role of ‘solitude’ in early recovery and the process of reintegration through neighborhood relationships.

For reporting purposes, selected quotations were translated into English by bilingual members of the research team. Culturally specific terms without direct English equivalents were discussed among the authors, and translations were agreed upon to reflect participants’ intended meanings. All interpretations were based on the original Chinese data.

Results

This study included 12 participants (6 males and 6 females), with a mean age of 43.4 years (SD = 6.23; range = 35–55 years) (Table 2). Only one participant was married, and the remaining 11 participants were not currently married (91.7%). Among the participants, two females (P1 and P3) had parenting experience, with their children now adults. One male participant (P5) had a child but lost contact after a divorce, with the child being raised by his ex-wife. Additionally, nine participants had no siblings in their families. Five themes and twelve sub-themes emerged from the interviews, providing a comprehensive understanding of personal recovery in China: Reintegration within society, Personal responsibility within family, Engagement with reality, Regaining a sense of control, and Reconstruction of the sense of meaning in life (Table 3).

Themes and sub-themes

Reintegration within society

This theme captures reintegration as a staged process in which participants first withdrew into solitude and later re-entered social life through community-based supports. Solitude functioned as a temporary refuge for emotional stabilization; subsequent reconnection was facilitated by familiar neighborhood ties, emerging institutional resources, and peer relationships.

Self-healing through solitude

Many participants described an early recovery period marked by deliberate reduction of social contact to regain stability and rebuild psychological resources. Solitude was framed less as shame-driven avoidance than as a protected interval for restoring control and readiness. As one participant stated:

‘I stayed at home with my elderly father for over a year. During that time, I had no motivation to go out. I only took my medication and handled basic life needs, but I learned to play the guitar. Music and the guitar helped me get through those dark days. Looking back now, I’m grateful for the guitar.’ (P5, schizophrenia, male).

‘I stayed at home with my elderly father for over a year. During that time, I had no motivation to go out. I only took my medication and handled basic life needs, but I learned to play the guitar. Music and the guitar helped me get through those dark days. Looking back now, I’m grateful for the guitar.’ (P5, schizophrenia, male).

For participants, solitude was described less as retreat and more as a protected interval for regaining emotional footing. Through this period of solitude, the participant can take a break from social pressures. Another participant emphasized that social reconnection followed, rather than preceded, a basic sense of safety:

‘There is no need to push yourself beyond your capabilities. The desire for social interaction arises only after you have established a sense of security.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

‘There is no need to push yourself beyond your capabilities. The desire for social interaction arises only after you have established a sense of security.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

These narratives suggest that early isolation was experienced as protective and preparatory, enabling participants to re-enter social contexts with greater psychological footing.

Supportive network within the community

The transition from self-seclusion to community integration was supported by traditional neighborhood ties as well as institutional resources. In Shanghai’s Lilong (lane) neighborhoods, long-standing ties with neighbors supported re-entry by buffering anticipated stigma and affirming social belonging. The stigmatizing label “crazy” captured fear of being reduced to illness in public. Recognition by familiar neighbors, however, restored continuity of identity and reduced isolation, enabling re-engagement. As one participant shared:

‘At first, I was scared to go out and felt like people saw me as ‘crazy’. But when I met neighbors who had watched me grow up, they didn’t change their attitudes toward me. They welcomed me, invited me over to their homes—it made me feel comforted.’ (P8, schizophrenia, female).

‘At first, I was scared to go out and felt like people saw me as ‘crazy’. But when I met neighbors who had watched me grow up, they didn’t change their attitudes toward me. They welcomed me, invited me over to their homes—it made me feel comforted.’ (P8, schizophrenia, female).

However, as urban development reshapes the landscape, the close-knit, family-like support traditionally offered by Lilong communities is diminishing. Institutional support has emerged as a key element in addressing this gap. Social workers and community rehabilitation centers (“Sunshine Soul Park”) are becoming more involved in guiding individuals through structured reintegration. One participant recalled how a local social worker helped him take the first step outward:

‘After I was discharged from the hospital, I lived the same life every day. Media reports about people with mental illness dragged me into despair again. But then a social worker invited me to attend some community activities. Despite my hesitation, I took the first step, and that made a big difference.’ (P12, schizophrenia, male).

‘After I was discharged from the hospital, I lived the same life every day. Media reports about people with mental illness dragged me into despair again. But then a social worker invited me to attend some community activities. Despite my hesitation, I took the first step, and that made a big difference.’ (P12, schizophrenia, male).

Peer support was identified as an important resource in the recovery process. Participants who attended Sunshine Soul Park described peer relationships as providing mutual recognition, opportunities to practice social skills, and a shared language for recovery struggles. As one participant described:

‘I am bipolar, and sometimes it’s challenging to understand people with schizophrenia. But the way our personalities come together makes our time here less boring. We have a lot of discussions, which sparks thought. Even when communication is difficult, our hearts are connected, revealing shared needs and confusion.’ (P7, bipolar disorder, male).

‘I am bipolar, and sometimes it’s challenging to understand people with schizophrenia. But the way our personalities come together makes our time here less boring. We have a lot of discussions, which sparks thought. Even when communication is difficult, our hearts are connected, revealing shared needs and confusion.’ (P7, bipolar disorder, male).

Active and selective self-disclosure of mental illness

As the participants adapted to the supportive contexts, many of them described their return to more general social settings through selective disclosure. This entailed the decision of whether or not to disclose information, when to do so, and to whom to disclose it, based on perceived safety and relational trust. Disclosure was depicted as a process of continuous negotiation, as opposed to being perceived as a binary event. One participant described disclosure to a close friend as enabling social activity:

‘My friend knows I’m mentally ill, but he respects me and encourages me to go out. We often have dinner together, which motivates me a lot.’ (P5, schizophrenia, male).

‘My friend knows I’m mentally ill, but he respects me and encourages me to go out. We often have dinner together, which motivates me a lot.’ (P5, schizophrenia, male).

Another participant described a gradual disclosure process within a book club, initially concealing information from the organizer and subsequently disclosing it after trust had been established:

‘At first, I was nervous and hid my condition, but later I told the organizer, and he didn’t change his attitude toward me. That made me feel at ease.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

‘At first, I was nervous and hid my condition, but later I told the organizer, and he didn’t change his attitude toward me. That made me feel at ease.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

Selective disclosure was employed as a risk management and agency strategy. Participants retained autonomy over interactional boundaries while assessing the viability of acceptance. The process of reintegration was characterized by a transition from a state of protected solitude to one of supported reconnection. Selective disclosure emerged as an aspect of this transition, and was observed in instances where confidence levels increased.

Personal responsibility within family

This theme emphasizes the role of parental responsibilities and concerns about the future in motivating recovery. In the participants’ narratives, the assumption of familial roles functioned as an organizing motive, facilitating a transition from dependence to self-management.

Parental responsibility

The transition to parenthood has been identified as a key factor in the process of recovery, requiring individuals to assume functional roles and demonstrate independence. One participant described how their role as a parent had led to alterations in self-presentation, daily routines, and responsibilities:

‘Having a child changes everything. You can’t be as unkempt as you used to be; you must be a role model. Before, others took care of me. But now, when my daughter says, ‘Mom, I’m hungry,’ I can’t ignore it—I have to get something for her to eat. It has forced me to mature.’ (P4, schizophrenia, female).

‘Having a child changes everything. You can’t be as unkempt as you used to be; you must be a role model. Before, others took care of me. But now, when my daughter says, ‘Mom, I’m hungry,’ I can’t ignore it—I have to get something for her to eat. It has forced me to mature.’ (P4, schizophrenia, female).

In addition, some of the participants mentioned that caregiving was sustained even through periods of symptom relapse and articulated as a central form of familial responsibility. Relapse was not described as a disruption of everyday roles, but as a condition managed through continued parenting, self-monitoring, and timely family-facilitated care-seeking. One participant stated:

‘Even when you are ill, you must still fulfill the responsibilities that are yours, such as caring for your children and family. By continuing to engage in the responsibilities and tasks that are part of normal life, recovery will naturally follow.’ (P2, schizophrenia, female).

‘Even when you are ill, you must still fulfill the responsibilities that are yours, such as caring for your children and family. By continuing to engage in the responsibilities and tasks that are part of normal life, recovery will naturally follow.’ (P2, schizophrenia, female).

Caring for aging parents and independence

Participants also linked recovery to anticipating parents’ ageing and avoiding becoming a burden to others. This responsibility subsequently influenced the development of commitments to independent living and health maintenance. One participant is quoted as stating:

‘Our parents are getting older, and I don’t want to be a burden to them. It’s important for me to live independently now so I can care for them in their old age. Thinking about this responsibility, I can’t slip back into old habits. I have to take good care of myself.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

‘Our parents are getting older, and I don’t want to be a burden to them. It’s important for me to live independently now so I can care for them in their old age. Thinking about this responsibility, I can’t slip back into old habits. I have to take good care of myself.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

Another participant emphasized planning for both parents’ care and personal aging, translating responsibility into practical skill acquisition:

‘We must consider both our parents’ care and our own future needs. I prefer not to go to a nursing home, so it’s important to learn to live independently. I’ve started learning practical skills like changing light bulbs and fixing plumbing.’ (P2, schizophrenia, female).

‘We must consider both our parents’ care and our own future needs. I prefer not to go to a nursing home, so it’s important to learn to live independently. I’ve started learning practical skills like changing light bulbs and fixing plumbing.’ (P2, schizophrenia, female).

Family letting go and fostering independence

Several participants described a turning point in personal recovery when guardians or family members reduced supervision and transferred responsibility for everyday life. This experience of “letting go” was lived in contrasting ways but consistently marked a shift in how participants related to themselves as capable actors.

For some, “letting go” occurred abruptly and was experienced through threat or withdrawal of protection. One participant recalled how her guardian’s response during a suicidal crisis forced her to confront life independently:

‘The biggest turning point in my recovery was when my aunt (my guardian) let go and made me handle everything myself. After I attempted suicide in 2016, she threatened to have me committed for life. That’s when I thought, since I can’t die, I might as well live properly.’ (P3, schizophrenia, female).

‘The biggest turning point in my recovery was when my aunt (my guardian) let go and made me handle everything myself. After I attempted suicide in 2016, she threatened to have me committed for life. That’s when I thought, since I can’t die, I might as well live properly.’ (P3, schizophrenia, female).

By contrast, other participants described family “letting go” as a gradual process through which personal recovery unfolded via learning everyday competence. In these accounts, reduced caregiving enabled a growing sense of self-reliance:

‘I grew up relying on my father, but after I became ill, he made me learn to cook and manage the household. Now, I can handle everything and live frugally. Looking back, I’m grateful he pushed me to learn these skills.’ (P2, schizophrenia, female).

‘I grew up relying on my father, but after I became ill, he made me learn to cook and manage the household. Now, I can handle everything and live frugally. Looking back, I’m grateful he pushed me to learn these skills.’ (P2, schizophrenia, female).

For the participants, the concept of “letting go” within the family unit functioned as a relational condition through which personal recovery was promoted, whether through abrupt rupture or gradual cultivation, by repositioning participants as responsible for their own daily lives.

However, some participants mentioned that parental overprotection could limit their recovery, as their parents consistently viewed them as patients. This, in turn, stimulated participants’ autonomy, which manifested as proactive engagement in activities of daily living to relieve the burden of care and pressure from their parents.

‘She (my mother) feels compelled to monitor everything I do, often assuming I can’t manage independently. I handle my tasks well—she could take a break and enjoy some TV or something, right? I believe parents should learn to let go at the appropriate time.’ (P10, bipolar disorder, male).

‘She (my mother) feels compelled to monitor everything I do, often assuming I can’t manage independently. I handle my tasks well—she could take a break and enjoy some TV or something, right? I believe parents should learn to let go at the appropriate time.’ (P10, bipolar disorder, male).

Engagement with reality

This theme delineates personal recovery as sustained engagement with reality through embodied routines and meaningful activities. Participants conceptualized recovery not as the resolution of symptoms, but rather as the capacity to maintain presence and stability through tangible actions in daily life.

Embodied engagement and grounding

Participants described bodily routines, such as doing housework and exercise, as grounding practices that helped interrupt intrusive thoughts and foster a sense of steadiness in daily life. Embodied activity supported personal recovery by stabilizing attention, restoring agency, and enabling participants to remain engaged with daily life despite ongoing vulnerability. One participant explained:

‘I need to keep doing things, otherwise my mind starts to spiral—even watching TV can trigger it. Medication helped, but it didn’t stop the intrusive thoughts completely. Exercise and housework still help me regulate myself.’ (P5, schizophrenia, male).

‘I need to keep doing things, otherwise my mind starts to spiral—even watching TV can trigger it. Medication helped, but it didn’t stop the intrusive thoughts completely. Exercise and housework still help me regulate myself.’ (P5, schizophrenia, male).

Another participant linked routine activities to the process of regaining autonomy and resuming a perceived sense of normality:

‘Whether I am a patient or not, I believe it’s important to live my life well. At least I should be able to buy things, do laundry, and cook independently. I want to focus on returning to life and living in the present rather than dwelling on the past.’ (P2, schizophrenia, female).

‘Whether I am a patient or not, I believe it’s important to live my life well. At least I should be able to buy things, do laundry, and cook independently. I want to focus on returning to life and living in the present rather than dwelling on the past.’ (P2, schizophrenia, female).

Immersion in meaningful activities

In addition to the completion of daily tasks, participants described the importance of engaging in personally meaningful activities, including artistic creation, musical activities, and written expression, in promoting recovery. These activities provided participants with a sense of structure, purpose, and a mean to organize their time. This was beneficial for those who were unemployed and had a significant amount of leisure time, as the absence of structured activities increased their risk of overthinking. One participant offered the following insight:

‘When I was sick, my mind was in turmoil. Now, my thoughts are clearer. I’ve made it a point to attend ‘Sunshine Soul Park’ every day, where I learn to embroider, play the Gu-Qin, and read books. However, when I run out of things to do, it’s easy to slip back into overthinking.’ (P3, schizophrenia, female).

‘When I was sick, my mind was in turmoil. Now, my thoughts are clearer. I’ve made it a point to attend ‘Sunshine Soul Park’ every day, where I learn to embroider, play the Gu-Qin, and read books. However, when I run out of things to do, it’s easy to slip back into overthinking.’ (P3, schizophrenia, female).

Regaining a sense of control

Participants described recovery as increasing capacity to live with illness without being dominated by it. Control was rebuilt through understanding the illness in context, adjusting everyday practices, and developing reflective self-knowledge.

Understanding illness patterns and learning to live with them

Participants’ narratives suggested that regaining control began with learning how the illness operated in their own lives and reappraising what everyday living required. Through trial and error, they revised assumptions, adopted workable strategies, and increasingly treated treatment adherence and professional input as tools for sustaining agency. They learned to refrain from making independent changes to medication and to seek clinical advice when side effects disrupted their daily lives. For some, this transition was facilitated by others before becoming internalized, moving from externally monitored adherence to self-management:

‘In the beginning I resisted taking medication, and my father had to push me hard… I can take my medication independently now.’ (P11, schizophrenia, male).

‘In the beginning I resisted taking medication, and my father had to push me hard… I can take my medication independently now.’ (P11, schizophrenia, male).

Participants also identified personal relapse triggers and reorganized routines accordingly, reframing “slowing down” as an active choice to protect stability:

‘When I worked at a logistics company, I had three jobs at once… the stress was overwhelming, and I couldn’t sleep well… I chose to quit that job… because I knew for my condition, sleep is the first priority.’ (P1, bipolar disorder, female).

‘When I worked at a logistics company, I had three jobs at once… the stress was overwhelming, and I couldn’t sleep well… I chose to quit that job… because I knew for my condition, sleep is the first priority.’ (P1, bipolar disorder, female).

Participants also described a process of recalibration of their relationship to stigma and past setbacks, involving a filtration of external judgement and reorientation to the present, often through visible everyday practices. Some adjustments have been made to the appearance and conduct in order to support confidence and social positioning. This should be framed as adaptation rather than concealment:

‘Before I got sick, I was very sloppy… I wanted to show others a better mental image. If you say I’m mentally ill, I would behave as if I’m not… presenting a better exterior while also strengthening my inner self-reliance… Now… they say I’ve changed a lot.’ (P5, schizophrenia, male).

Rediscovering Oneself

Alongside illness management, participants described regaining control as rediscovering the self beyond diagnosis through sustained self-observation and a personalized way of governing everyday life. For some, this took a “case-based” form, involving systematic monitoring of mood, conduct, and life satisfaction to guide iterative change:

‘I see myself as a case to study… now I can look at myself more objectively… I understand myself better.’ (P7, bipolar disorder, male).

‘I see myself as a case to study… now I can look at myself more objectively… I understand myself better.’ (P7, bipolar disorder, male).

The rediscovery process also had a positive impact on relationships. Participants described using written notes and post-hoc reflection to analyze interactional patterns, identify sources of misunderstanding, and adjust future engagement:

‘I write notes every day… comparing my values with others’… so that I can get along better with this person in the future.’ (P3, schizophrenia, female).

‘I write notes every day… comparing my values with others’… so that I can get along better with this person in the future.’ (P3, schizophrenia, female).

Reconstruction of the sense of meaning in life

This theme explores how individuals have navigated the loss and challenges posed by their conditions to reconstruct a renewed sense of meaning and purpose in life. Central to this process are two key practices: engaging in creative activities and taking social responsibility. Both avenues offer opportunities for participants to reconnect with their intrinsic potential, provide them with spiritual sustenance, and foster a sense of fulfillment. While the activities in Theme 3 primarily anchored participants in the present through embodied engagement with daily life, the practices described here were narrated as more enduring sources of purpose.

Engaging in creative activities

Participants described how discovering and cultivating personal hobbies, such as painting or calligraphy, and engaging with Buddhist teachings became significant sources of spiritual support. These activities not only helped them reconnect with their potential but also provided a structured way to create meaning and establish a new life order. One participant described how learning to paint gradually became a lifelong orientation rather than a pastime. The discovery of competence in painting engendered a surge of accomplishment, and the act of ongoing exploration was conceptualized as a means of living with loss and mortality:

‘I began painting to fill my time as I grew older. When I realized I was quite good at it, it gave me a profound sense of accomplishment. I believe that in life, experiences are what matter most. Ultimately, we all need something to pursue, which helps us cope with both loss and the inevitability of death.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

Ultimately, we all need something to pursue, which helps us cope with both loss and the inevitability of death.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

For others, calligraphy and scriptural copying were narrated in explicitly spiritual terms, as practices that restored inner orientation and reshaped how they related to themselves and others.

‘Calligraphy and the practice of copying Buddhist scriptures have given me a renewed sense of purpose. I do this whenever I need to find calmness or clarity, or when my mind feels restless.’ (P7, bipolar disorder, male).

‘Calligraphy and the practice of copying Buddhist scriptures have given me a renewed sense of purpose. I do this whenever I need to find calmness or clarity, or when my mind feels restless.’ (P7, bipolar disorder, male).

Taking social responsibility

A common thread in the participants’ narratives was the importance of peer relationships and mutual support. Participants noted that, through engaging in peer-based activities and volunteer work, they not only contributed to others but also found validation and meaning for their own existence. One participant noted:

‘Initially, I was unsure whether teaching others would be a path to self-realization. Now, I’ve found my answer: my main focus is to help others, and through that, I also realize my dream of making a positive impact.’ (P3, schizophrenia, female).

‘Initially, I was unsure whether teaching others would be a path to self-realization. Now, I’ve found my answer: my main focus is to help others, and through that, I also realize my dream of making a positive impact.’ (P3, schizophrenia, female).

Others described volunteering as a concrete route to competence, confidence, and renewed purpose. One participant recalled becoming a museum docent as an unforgettable experience in which he gradually mastered the skills of guiding and explaining exhibits:

‘As a volunteer, I had the chance to work as a docent at a museum. It was an unforgettable experience where I gradually mastered the art of guiding and explaining exhibits.’ (P12, schizophrenia, male).

‘As a volunteer, I had the chance to work as a docent at a museum. It was an unforgettable experience where I gradually mastered the art of guiding and explaining exhibits.’ (P12, schizophrenia, male).

Half of the participants also engaged in advocacy efforts for mental health awareness. By sharing their recovery stories, they not only inspired their peers but also contributed to wider discussions about the rights of individuals with mental illness. These efforts emphasized the need for increased support, particularly in areas such as employment and social services. One participant reflected on his experience:

‘I was nervous at first, but after sharing my story with nursing students and seeing their respectful interest, I realized that my journey could offer value to others.’ (P6, schizophrenia, male).

Discussion

The present study explored the lived experiences of personal recovery among individuals with SMI in mainland China. The findings show strong thematic convergence with the CHIME framework while also identifying culturally patterned recovery processes in the context of SMI [34]. The five core themes map onto CHIME’s domains. “Reintegration within Society” corresponds to Connectedness, as participants emphasized neighborhood ties, peer support, and community-based resources. “Engagement with Reality” and “Reconstructing Meaning in Life” relate to Meaning, as participants described the importance of daily routines and meaningful activities in fostering stability and purpose. “Regaining a Sense of Control” and “Personal Responsibility within Family” resonate with Identity and Empowerment, reflecting efforts to maintain a coherent sense of self and to exercise agency in symptom management and family roles. However, “Family Letting Go and Fostering Independence” highlights a culturally salient extension of Empowerment that foregrounds negotiated autonomy within interdependent family structures. These findings suggest both convergence with and contextual expansion of the CHIME framework in the mainland Chinese context.

The present study extend CHIME in several respects as well. “Self-healing through Solitude” and “Caregiving for Aging Parents with Autonomy” were emphasized in the present study. These findings suggest culturally nuanced recovery strategies in non-Western contexts, where familial obligations and introspective practices may hold greater significance than the individualistic orientation of CHIME framework. In addition, a more explicit somatic register of recovery (“Body Engagement and Grounding”) was prominent in participants’ accounts, complementing CHIME’s primarily psychosocial emphasis. Furthermore, comparison with Jaiswal’s SMI recovery framework indicates both convergence and divergence [35]. Consistent with the SMI recovery framework, participants’ narratives foregrounded identity work and empowerment; however, findings of this study position family caregiving and intergenerational responsibility as central pathways through which recovery were achieved. Lats but not least, scarcity of Hope-related narratives in our findings may reflect participants’ advanced recovery stages, which is consistent with Yarborough’s model of recovery where individuals progress from “getting by” to “getting on” phases, with less emphasis on hopefulness as recovery becomes routinised in everyday life [36].

The findings of this study help explain the avoidance behaviors seen in early-stage of recovery. Most participants reported that their recovery began with a period of seclusion, aligning with prior research that individuals often experience social isolation early in their illness due to symptoms and medication side effects [37]. The “solitude” described here was typically self-chosen and time-limited, and was narrated as a transitional practice for restoring psychological capacity before re-engagement, which closely align with the notion of positive solitude, defined as a voluntary, bounded strategy for affect regulation that supports subsequent social interaction [38]. Participants framed this interval as necessary for “self-healing” and for rebuilding the courage to socialize, suggesting that externally imposed or premature demands for social participation may be experienced as mismatched to readiness and may help explain why early re-entry efforts can fail when pressure is applied [39]. However, this account should not be conflated with chronic withdrawal. Therefore, recognizing the importance of this phase allows healthcare professionals and family members to better support the gradual rebuilding of confidence essential for recovery [40]. Interventions during this stage should focus on support, reassurance, and fostering courage, as premature demands can hinder recovery.

In addition to intrinsic self-healing, the extrinsic manifestation of recovery is social reintegration, which align with the findings of previous studies [41, 42]. It is of noted that community integration manifest as neighborhood attachment and peer support networks within the Chinese context. For individuals with mental illness in Shanghai, attachments and interactions within neighborhood lanes foster identity and safety, while community mental health infrastructure provides support for the social integration, social participation and identity reconstruction. This is consistent with contemporary global frameworks advocating for geographically accessible, person-centered care models that integrate clinical and psychosocial support to foster meaningful community inclusion [43]. Participation in community-based rehabilitation programs can provide participants with structured routines and tangible transitional pathways, such as facilitated engagement opportunities, informational resources, and linkages to social supports. Peer support emerged as a pivotal mechanism within these ecosystems. Participants described peer-informed spaces as uniquely mitigating perceived social threat, validating lived experience through mutual understanding, and offering a non-stigmatizing environment for rehearsing interpersonal skills and rebuilding relational confidence, which are functions inherently difficult to replicate within professionally mediated support alone [44]. These dynamics reflect peer support’s distinct epistemic value, including lived experience as a catalyst for hope, reciprocity, and identity reclamation. Consequently, findings underscore the imperative to formally integrate certified peer support specialists as a core, reimbursable component of community mental health systems.

As participants rebuild courage, they gradually seek broader interpersonal interactions and consider disclosing their condition to acquaintances. However, disclosure remains a complex challenge with both individual and social implication [42, 45]. In Chinese cultural contexts, decisions about disclosure are often shaped by concerns related to mianzi (face), relational harmony, and role obligations, rendering disclosure less an individual choice than a negotiated social practice. As shown in studies of Chinese and Chinese immigrant communities, disclosure of mental illness is selective and strategic, aimed at preserving social standing and protecting family members from stigma rather than asserting personal authenticity. While disclosure may facilitate emotional support and social connection, it also carries risks of social distancing, moral judgment, or discrimination in work and community settings [46]. Therefore, mental health professionals are encouraged to facilitate activities that reduce discrimination of mental illness and help individuals establish and maintain healthy social relationships through community organizations and social services.

Participants emphasized that achieving self-sufficiency were crucial to recovery, aligning with previous research. For instance, a study on schizophrenia identified autonomy in self-care as key motivators of recovery [47]. However, participants noted that parental overprotection hindered their ability to take responsibility. In many Asian cultural contexts, parent-child relationships are commonly organized around relational interdependence rather than early individuation, with parental responsibility extending into children’s adult lives [48]. This sustained involvement is culturally embedded in moral expectations of care, family obligation, and risk avoidance, and may be further reinforced when an adult child is diagnosed with mental illness [49]. Within this framework, parental overprotection is driven by concerns about relapse, social stigma, and long-term security, reflecting an emphasis on protection and stability over individual autonomy [50]. From an Asian cultural perspective, navigating overprotection therefore requires not simply reducing parental involvement, but recalibrating relational boundaries so that care can coexist with autonomy-supportive practices that recognize adults with mental illness as capable moral agents within the family [51].

The participants reported that parenting responsibilities was also one of the key elements of recovery among people living with SMI in China. This finding is consistent with previous studies in which several parents with mental illness reported experiencing hope and pride in parenting roles [52, 53]. These findings suggest that the integrity of the parent-child relationship for people with mental illness should be given high priority. Nevertheless, psychiatric symptoms and the lack of efficacious support structures may precipitate challenges in parenting for individuals with mental illness [52, 54]. It is therefore crucial for psychiatric professionals to adopt a strengths-based, system-wide approach to practice that is family-centered and places parenting support at the core of decision-making processes [55], facilitating the creation of a multi-layered support system around the parent through the parent’s advocacy role.

Perceived control is a key element of recovery for individuals with mental illness. In this study, regaining a sense of control emerged as a central recovery process, characterized by the gradual reconstruction of agency through illness understanding, self-management, and everyday decision-making, rather than symptom elimination, highlighting control as a relational and practice-based achievement within personal recovery. This is consistent with the findings of previous study. For example, a recent study demonstrated that recovery involves developing control over behaviors and coping strategies rather than managing the illness [56]. The findings further suggest that control is facilitated by supportive family dynamics, peer modeling, and community-based structures that allow graded autonomy, while it is hindered by overprotection, stigma, and management-oriented service practices. Accordingly, cognitive interventions and social skills training (SST) could benefit participants. Cognitive-based interventions, such as health education programs [57], help participants understand their illness and share their experiences, while SST teaches interpersonal skills for social interactions, including friendships and healthcare communication [58, 59]. However, further research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of SST and self-directed research in enhancing perceived control and supporting mental health recovery.

Embodied Engagement and Grounding was another prominent sub-theme in the present study, identified as an essential process in personal recovery. Participants emphasized that engaging in activities of daily living (ADLs) and creative hobbies allowed them to live in the moment and reduce intrusive thoughts. Studies focused on the role of ADLs and creative hobbies in personal recovery report that engaging in ADLs and creative hobbies is an important stepping stone associated with mental health recovery [60, 61]. Engaging in ADLs and creative activities provides a sense of reality, which was also mentioned as a way of coping with intrusive thoughts and self-harming behaviors [62, 63], thereby facilitating recovery. These findings highlight the importance of occupational therapy and art therapy in promoting recovery.

Meaning in life is a key aspect of personal recovery, as highlighted by Leamy et al. [34]. In this study, participants’ sense of meaning was rekindled through hobbies and volunteering [64]. Employment can be a meaningful source of role identity and social participation in personal recovery [2]. When access to work is constrained, some individuals may seek purpose through other valued roles and activities, including creative pursuits, which can support meaning-making in everyday life. In Chinese cultural contexts, work may also carry moral and relational significance linked to social contribution and role fulfilment, which may shape how employment disruptions are experienced [65]. This suggests that art-based practices could play a role in future recovery efforts, aiding in restoring meaning. Additionally, participants emphasized the importance of volunteering and advocacy, reflecting the collectivist culture in China, which values individual contributions to the group [66, 67]. These findings suggest that, within Chinese culture, meaning in life is often tied to a commitment to community welfare and collective interests.

Limitations

While this study adhered to COREQ reporting standards and integrated Yardley’s four principles for qualitative research quality, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, participants were recruited from urban settings and were engaged in community mental health services; recovery experiences may differ in rural contexts where service availability, peer programs, and opportunities for structured community participation are more limited, and where reliance on family care and local stigma dynamics may be different. Second, data were generated through cross-sectional semi-structured interviews, which limits inferences about how personal recovery processes evolve over time. Third, while analytical procedures were documented per COREQ guidelines, the interpretive nature of thematic analysis inherently involves researcher subjectivity. We acknowledge that researcher perspectives may have shaped interpretative emphasis despite reflexive documentation, team-based review, and consensus discussions. A further limitation is that the interview guide was not validated with experts by experience. Future studies should consider involving individuals with lived experience of SMI in the development and validation of interview materials to enhance their contextual relevance, sensitivity, and ecological validity. Finally, as data were derived from cross-sectional, semi-structured interviews, changes in recovery over time could not be examined. Future research should include diverse geographic and service contexts and adopt longitudinal designs.

Conclusions

Findings of the present study align with international personal recovery literature in highlighting meaning-making, connectedness, and responsibility. In contrast, in mainland China these processes are shaped by a range of social and cultural factors, including strong family involvement, overprotective caregiving practices, and intergenerational obligations. These factors have influenced the community reintegration of individuals with mental illness. Based on this, we advocate for community-based, recovery-oriented interventions, including peer support, health education, and social skills training, tailored to the Chinese cultural context.
