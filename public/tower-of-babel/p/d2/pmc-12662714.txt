Ethical, legal, and social issues related to ingestible, implantable electronic devices and mRNA delivery devices
Authors: Lisa Bero, Natalie Banacos, Christine M Baugh, Peter R Chai, Tiffany R Glynn, Matthew DeCamp, Ben Harnke, Hannah Scotch, Jelena Durdic, Matthew Wynia, Eric G Campbell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYOral delivery systems are being developed to treat disease, including ingestible devices that implant in the gut and deliver electrical stimulation or mRNA therapeutics. The implementation of these devices raises questions about the associated ethical, legal, and social implications (ELSIs). Our scoping review examined 83 scholarly publications published between 1946 and 2024 that investigate ELSI topics around the development and use of these technologies. We highlight the characteristics of the publications, the ethics topics mentioned, and a content analysis to describe ELSI themes. Themes particularly relevant to ingestible, implantable electronics and mRNA delivery devices were data use, invasiveness, and possible impacts on patient-clinician trust. This review suggests solutions to better protect patients from ethical and clinical risks resulting from data misuse and future empirical research engaging clinicians, patients, and researchers in this field.

INTRODUCTION

The oral route is one of the most common strategies for delivering medications and health monitoring systems. Innovations in oral delivery of drugs, capsules, and devices have addressed medication adherence (e.g., extended-release formulations), targeted delivery (e.g., gated-release formulations), diagnostics (e.g., environmental assessment), and localized monitoring (e.g., gastric residence vs. transitory devices). One such innovation is ingestible electronics, with the development of the first pressure-sensing pill in 1957, core temperature sensors in the 1980s, capsule endoscopy and SmartPills in the 2000s, and digital pills in the 2010s.1 Currently, the convergence of ingestible and implantable technologies is facilitating the development of devices that implant in the gut post-ingestion. The goals of these devices are to generate electrical stimulation aimed at inducing hormonal changes2,3 and/or to deliver small molecules, peptides, and mRNA therapeutics through the tissue of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.4–6 The current research direction in ingestible electronic systems includes a pathway to address a wide variety of population health endemics, including metabolic disease (e.g., diabetes and obesity).

Given the novelty of ingestible, implantable electronic systems that share characteristics of both drugs and devices and their abilities for physiological modification, questions arise about the ethics, regulatory oversight, and social considerations of these products. For example, these devices could produce enduring or irreversible hormonal/other physiological changes in the body. Although not permanent, they are designed to reside in the GI tract for at least 30 days. Ingestible, implantable electronic devices collect and transmit data and may be controlled from outside of the person with the implant. Malfunctioning devices could create risks of physical or technological harm. There are also unknown potential repercussions of these devices, given emerging literature on the role the GI tract may play in neuroendocrine and other diseases.7,8 Additionally, populations that could potentially benefit from or be harmed by these devices (i.e., marginalized and minoritized individuals) are at higher risk for suboptimal health issues, including metabolic disorders,9 and have experienced historical discrimination in healthcare and health technology spaces that need to be considered.10–12

The goal of our research is to develop an empirically informed conceptual framework to understand the ethical, legal, and social implications (ELSIs) of the research, development, and future clinical use of ingestible implantable electronic systems and mRNA delivery devices. This scoping review is a step in developing the conceptual framework, as the extent of scholarship focused specifically on these types of devices is unknown. Empirical and normative ethics research related to medical devices spans a range of topics, including protection and perceptions of human subjects during product development, a lack of inclusivity in the research, data sharing and privacy, and the approval process for implantable devices.13–16 Physicians’ views on and the ethics of implantable device deactivation have also been explored, particularly regarding cardiac devices like pacemakers, left ventricular assist devices (LVADs), and implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs).17,18 However, reviews have found little research on social implications.15,19

To fill this knowledge gap, we conducted a scoping review to understand ethical, regulatory, and social perspectives in the research, clinical, and patient communities around the development, testing, and therapeutic uses of ingestible implantable electroceuticals and mRNA delivery devices.

RESULTS

As shown in Figure 1, database and citation-chasing searches identified 5,992 titles and abstracts for screening, resulting in 152 eligible full-text reports. Following the screening of full texts, 83 papers were included in the scoping review (18 empirical and 65 other scholarship), and 82 papers included sufficient information for and were included in the content analysis. Note S1 lists the 83 included papers. Of the 83 publications included, 52 (63%) focused on electroceuticals, defined as devices designed to be ingested and containing electronics that deliver electrical stimulation to areas of the GI tract. No papers focused on ingestible, implantable mRNA delivery devices. Most of the included papers about electroceuticals (67%, 35/52) were published between 2020 and April 2024. The articles were published in a wide variety of journals, with foci ranging from medicine and surgery to bioengineering, law, and ethics. Advanced Drug Delivery Reviews and the American Journal of Bioethics contributed the most papers per journal, at three each. The majority of papers focused on the Americas and Europe, including most of the empirical studies (94%, 17/18). Almost half the articles did not report a funding source (45%, 37/83), but the majority of those that did were government funded (Table 1). Seven papers declared funding from the pharmaceutical/biotech industry. Authors declared conflicts of interest in 15 papers, and 10 papers included industry-affiliated authors.

A variety of groups who may be involved in the development, prescribing, and use of these technologies were discussed, including patients (n = 59), healthcare workers (n = 54), and scientists and engineers (n = 19). While more than half of the papers discussed equity issues in some way, only 3 of the 18 empirical studies included any equity-related covariates in their analyses (e.g., race, ethnicity, income, education, employment, and geographic indicators) (Table 1).

In our sample, 31 papers discussed diabetes, nine discussed obesity, and seven discussed other endocrine or metabolic diseases. Psychiatric disease (57%, 47/83), infectious disease (42%, 35/83), and neurological disease (41%, 34/83) were covered more frequently than the metabolic conditions that are current targets for developing ingestible, implantable electronic devices.

Of the 52 papers discussing electroceuticals, 41 (79%) mentioned ingestible electroceuticals, 23 (44%) mentioned non-ingestible electroceuticals, and 11 (21%) discussed both. Non-ingestible electroceutical devices and procedures included vagus nerve stimulators, deep brain stimulation, electroconvulsive therapy, and brain-machine interfaces. The most frequently discussed electroceutical interventions were “smart pills,” digital pill systems designed to monitor medication adherence (98%, 51/52). Many of these papers focused on the ethical implications of electroceuticals that monitor adherence to pharmacological treatment for mental health conditions and applications for patients to monitor their own adherence to treatment regimens for tuberculosis, HIV prevention, and opioid intake after fractures. The 31 publications discussing interventions that were not ingestible electroceuticals included ingestible drug delivery devices that are not electronic, capsule endoscopy devices, ingestible intragastric balloons, and various “smart” versions of devices, including insulin pens, inhalers, and autoinjectors.

Of the 83 papers, 57 (31 with a focus on electroceuticals) mentioned at least one or more of the fundamental principles of bioethics as laid out by Beauchamp and Childress, but most of these were cursory.21 Autonomy (59%, 49/83), related to both patients and clinicians, was the most frequently mentioned principle of bioethics. Beneficence (42%, 35/83) and non-maleficence (23%, 19/83) were also mentioned. Justice was mentioned (41%, 34/83), most often in the context of socioeconomic barriers to access to the technologies.

Ethics of conflicts of interest were infrequently mentioned (16%, 13/83). Most papers merely noted that due to the small community of researchers working on new technology development and the investment from industry, conflicts of interest are likely to exist. One paper provided detailed guidance for managing the conflicts of interest of researchers developing new technologies.22 Four publications presented ethical frameworks, 3 of which were principle based, while one was based on five dimensions of ethical judgment (Table 2).

Table 3 also shows the subcategories of ELSI topic mentions. The most frequently mentioned security and privacy subtopics were privacy (72%, 60/83), data sharing (53%, 44/83), and hacking (51%, 42/83). Note S2 describes these data collection and use concepts in greater detail.

Access to (55%, 46/83) and the cost of (52%, 43/83) these emerging technologies were often tied to discussions of inequity (41% 34/83) and insurance coverage issues (28%, 23/83). Clinical trial participants are the first users of new technologies. Hendricks et al. have described how trial participants may face ongoing financial burdens for device maintenance and potential complications after trial completion, especially when healthcare budgets do not cover these costs.29

A wide variety of issues related to decision-making were described, including patient willingness to use the technology (47%, 39/83), the need for shared decision-making (30%, 25/83), and the availability or lack of evidence to inform clinicians’ choices (27%, 22/83). The literature assumed that patients should consent to use the devices (either as part of a trial or for clinical use). Discussion of consent issues ranged from the obvious need for informed consent (41%, 34/83) to considerations of software user agreements and how their complexity adds challenges to patients’ thorough understanding of informed consent (12%, 10/83). Most of the articles that discussed device control described patient control of the device (93%, 14/15), but nine of these discussed how physician control of the device might lead to unwanted monitoring of patients (60%). Control of the device was seen as distinct from control of the data, as discussed in the next section. Relatedly, much of the discussion around stigma (39%, 32/83) was related to electroceuticals for medication adherence monitoring in patients with mental health issues. Electroceuticals that monitor patients’ adherence to medication for mental health conditions could stigmatize patients by revealing their diagnosis or implying an inability of the patient to manage their own health.

Responsibility for adverse health events was an additional area of discussion (29%, 24/83). Topics included whether liability for adverse health outcomes falls with the user, prescriber, manufacturer, or regulator. The locus of responsibility could vary depending on the reason for the adverse event, if known. Concerns were raised about both patients’ and physicians’ ability to effectively monitor the vast amounts of data collected by devices. In addition, adverse events could be due to device failures, software failures, software breaches, data breaches, or failure to update a device or software. Automated messages about needed updates could be particularly problematic, as physicians and patients could suffer from “alert fatigue” and not take action even if they receive notice. Hegde et al. have argued that patients may even seek to shift responsibility for their actions to the manufacturers of the devices if the use of the device leads the patient to engage in risky activities that they avoided before having the device.30

Environmental concerns included battery life and type (20%, 17/83, and 5%, 4/83, respectively) related to battery disposal, as well as device disposal (6%, 5/83). The device needs to be able to collect data, deliver therapy, and maintain security features on a power supply constrained by the device size, which, for ingestibles, is limited by the size of the GI tract.

Overall, the existing literature on the ethics of development and use of electroceuticals is cautious about these new technologies (62%, 51/82, are cautious about the ethical issues raised). Caution about the ethics aligned with neutral or positive views of the devices. Most articles expressed favorable (44%, 36/82) or neutral (46%, 38/82) views of the devices being discussed. Most articles (73%, 60/82) did not express a view about industry involvement in the development or testing of the devices. Major areas of caution were threats to patient autonomy, complexity of informed consent, data sharing, privacy, and security. The literature was also cautious about whether beneficence has been achieved due to unknown risk-benefit profiles for the devices and their unknown societal value. Concerns about scientific “spin” that overemphasizes efficacy and underemphasizes harm were also raised.

Qualitative data

Two major themes relevant to ingestible, implantable electronic or mRNA delivery devices that emerged were related to the collection and management of the data and the implantable, ingestible nature of the device.

CONCERNS REGARDING DATA

Ingestible electroceuticals can collect behavioral and biometric data, and much of the ethics discussion has focused on the risks presented by the accessibility of that data. Some have raised concerns that the risks posed by data collection may be greater than the therapeutic benefit.

The literature describes a variety of concerns related to data ownership, data sharing, security, privacy, and confidentiality (Note S2). However, these concepts are not clearly delineated, and the terms are used differently across disciplines. For the purposes of this review, we define data ownership as legal ownership of the data collected by a device; data sharing as sharing of and access to data collected by a device; security as the process of protecting digital information from unauthorized use throughout its life cycle; privacy as the control of how, when, and to what extent information is shared; and confidentiality as the ethical and legal obligation to protect personal information from unauthorized disclosure, modification, or loss. In the literature identified in this review, the boundaries between these categories are often blurred and may overlap. For example, the concern that the unauthorized use of data from a device by a patient’s relative could stigmatize the patient could be related to a lack of clarity about data sharing, inadequate security, and inadequate protections of privacy or confidentiality.

Note S2 describes ELSI concerns related to device data identified in this review. Overall, there are ethical concerns about the risks of data misuse, how the complexity of data ownership and use is communicated, and the effects of data sharing on patient autonomy. Concerns about the monetization of health data and their use for marketing purposes were also expressed.31 Extreme examples of data misuse include patient coercion, surveillance, and using data for police or judicial proceedings.30 The authors suggested that insurance companies could use these health data to deny coverage, and on the other hand, insurers could tie insurance benefits or reduced premiums to the adoption of certain technologies or make coverage conditional upon their use.32,33 Data use issues extended to the possible stigmatization or criminalization of patients if data on their diagnosis or behaviors were revealed to their physicians or individuals outside the health system or implied by using the device.

Although many stakeholders are involved in the collection and use of data from ingestible electroceuticals, responsibility for ethical use is not clearly delineated. Flore describes a custodial model where a developer believes that data are owned by patients, with the company as the custodian, raising questions of agency, consent, and privacy.34 There is no clear consensus on the extent of data access or who should have access to data, with patients, clinicians, family members, friends, researchers, health systems, insurance companies, the device manufacturer, and other third parties all considered potential candidates for data access. Security and privacy concerns are mentioned as the responsibility of developers who can provide technological solutions. In addition, despite federal regulations around health-related data privacy and security among “covered entities” and their “business associates,”35 it is often unclear how responsibility for data use is maintained among non-covered entities or if circumstances change, such as a developer or an insurance company that holds data going out of business or being sold.

Uncertainty about which stakeholders can have access to and control data highlights concerns about data confidentiality. Stakeholders who often have access to data from devices, such as developers or family, are not typically held to the same standards of confidentiality as healthcare providers when dealing with health data. While user agreements cover ownership and requirements to maintain and update a device, they do not typically cover the confidentiality of the data.

Risks related to data are not sufficiently presented during informed consent.24,29,32,36 User or privacy agreements that cover data are often hundreds of pages written in technical language and are separate from the informed consent documents. There is no standard opportunity for patients to discuss these agreements with their provider or the manufacturer or to negotiate specific aspects of the agreement. The agreements often include provisions that patients are unaware of that allow the use of the data for a variety of purposes. Patients may also be unaware of updates and changes in user agreements.37

CONCERNS REGARDING DEVICE INVASIVENESS

The ingestible nature of electroceuticals has sparked an investigation of patient perceptions related to the invasiveness of a device. Ingestible devices are sometimes seen as more invasive than standard-of-care treatments due to their physical characteristics (e.g., ease of swallowing, duration of time retained in the GI tract, removal of the device, etc.). However, the concept of invasiveness extends beyond these physical properties to include interference with the patient’s daily routine, considerations of the potential of a therapy to cause harm, how localized or diffuse its effects are, the amount of control people have over the intervention and its effects, how permanent its effects are perceived to be, and how familiar the intervention seems to the user.36,38

Because of their easy administration and low-maintenance nature, ingestible devices were often seen as promoting access to care since patients may be treated with fewer trips to the doctor or pharmacy, which is also helpful for those without reliable access to transit or those living in some rural areas. Ingestible devices may be preferred over surgery for elderly or other high-risk surgical patients. However, the high costs of ingestible devices were seen as a potential barrier to access. Risks related to the ingestible nature of the device have been discussed above and relate primarily to loss of autonomy, privacy, data security, and stigmatization.39 The literature does not provide a detailed discussion of differences in ethical concerns, if any, between transitory ingestible electronics and those that are implantable in the gut, like the ingestible, implantable electronic and mRNA delivery devices that are the subject of this review.

CONCERNS REGARDING LEVEL OF TRUST IN PATIENT-CLINICIAN RELATIONSHIPS

Though it is not often discussed explicitly in scholarship around ingestible and implantable electroceuticals, many of the ethical issues raised revolve around the idea of trust. For a patient, it is imperative not only that they trust the device itself but also that they trust their clinician to appropriately inform them about the device and the use of data resulting from the device (even in areas in which the clinicians themselves may not be comfortable, such as user agreements). Patients additionally need to trust that developers have designed devices that can be controlled ethically and have properly secured their data. Patients’ level of trust can affect how they use the device. For example, the willingness of patients to share data is related to medical mistrust.14

The literature describes how devices may negatively impact trust in the patient-clinician relationship. Devices may increase clinicians’ access to formerly private information, allowing them to have more data on details of their patients’ lives, and could become a kind of “Big Brother,” continuously capturing data about a patient’s health and activities, even when they may be unwilling to share such information.36 Discrepancies between patient self-reports and recorded data may cause clinicians to mistrust patients, risking stigmatization and dissolution of two-way trust.36,40,41 Ingestible devices might also disrupt clinician-patient relations by supplanting in-person visits, or they could give insurance companies a larger role in deciding care where they can intervene based on patient data, thus eroding the patient-doctor relationship and trust. Additionally, all of these pathways to harming trust might be especially likely to arise among those who already mistrust the medical system or among minoritized or marginalized populations that have experienced healthcare and technology discrimination.42

There is less discussion in the literature on potential improvements in patient-clinician trust, although some postulated that these devices could enhance trust. Trust may be improved by the enhanced transparency and data provided by devices. When physicians receive patient information without the patient telling them, patients may feel more comfortable and assume they are being followed more closely.37,43 The potential for wider access to healthcare offered by ingestible devices could also enhance trust in the medical system.44

PERSPECTIVES

This scoping review provides an overview of scholarship examining ethical, legal, and social issues relevant to the development and use of ingestible, implantable electronics and mRNA delivery devices. Many of the included papers provided minimal discussions of the ethical issues mentioned. Ethical frameworks were rarely cited as foundations for the papers, and principles of ethics were not defined or used consistently. Devices’ impact on patient autonomy was a major area of bioethics inquiry. The ethical concerns that were described in the most detail related to the data generated by the device, most specifically with regard to privacy, security, and confidentiality. Concerns about access to the technologies due to their high cost were often mentioned, but issues of justice and equity were not explored in depth. Risks of patient stigmatization were mentioned in several contexts, including resulting from unwanted release of data about a patient’s condition or lifestyle, monitoring from health professionals, or implying a patient’s inability to manage their own health. Although most of the articles mentioning stigma did so in the context of mental health issues, these concerns could apply to metabolic diseases that are often stigmatized as “lifestyle” diseases.

GAPS IN LITERATURE

We identified major gaps in the types of devices, the stakeholders mentioned, and the ELSI issues raised. All of these gaps merit further investigation. First, no papers focusing on ingestible, implantable mRNA delivery devices were identified. Although approximately two-thirds of the identified papers specifically discussed ingestible electroceuticals, these were primarily smart pills (drug-adherence monitoring device combinations) and not ingestible, implantable electronic devices (or systems) aimed at modifying hormonal pathways through electrical stimulation of the gut. These types of ingestible, implantable systems could generate distinct ethical concerns. Second, investigations that include minoritized/marginalized populations and examine equity issues are a gap. Third, principle-based bioethics approaches were predominant in the literature, offering an opportunity to apply other frameworks to examine ELSI issues related to these devices. Although industry-academic partnerships are critical to the development of new technologies, conflict of interest issues resulting from these partnerships were rarely mentioned or examined. Compared to battery life and disposal, broader ethical issues around sustainability and the environmental impact of technology production and device disposal were rarely considered. Lastly, the empirical research identified was too heterogeneous and insufficient to suggest topics for systematic reviews. We may have missed relevant publications that were not published in English.

Very little scholarship was focused specifically on the types of devices currently being developed by the ARPA-H project that funds this work, that is, ingestible, implantable electronic systems and mRNA delivery devices aimed at modifying hormonal pathways to treat metabolic disease. This raises the question of whether ethical issues raised in the broader literature on ingestible devices are relevant and whether there are additional issues that should be considered. None of the ethical issues described in this scoping review appear to be unique to ingestible electroceuticals or mRNA delivery devices, although those related to the implantable nature of the device or data collection and use have particular saliency.

ETHICAL ISSUES RELATED TO IMPLANTABLE NATURE OF THE DEVICE

These devices raise ethical issues related to their potential to produce long-lasting changes in a person by modifying their hormonal composition or protein synthesis, with unknown effects. After an electroceutical or mRNA delivery device is implanted in the body, it emits electrical signals that impact the nervous and endocrine systems or delivers mRNA, which may result in the ongoing production of encoded proteins. Changes in persons have been considered in the context of devices used for mental health treatment, with concerns raised about unintended changes to an individual’s personality or feelings.45 None of the papers in this scoping review addressed risks associated with long-term changes in bodily functions that are the aim of these devices. Ethical issues related to how these changes might affect a patient’s desire to be treated, with possible requests for device removal or treatment discontinuation, need to be explored.

By examining features of ingestible, implantable devices, we contribute to an ongoing debate around the definition of medical intervention invasiveness. De Marco et al. suggest that perceptions of invasiveness may vary along the following dimensions: (1) interventions have degrees of invasiveness, (2) invasiveness for a given intervention is relative to its alternatives (in the case of obesity and diabetes described in our reporting, an ingestible device vs. self-injection or surgery), and (3) there are nonphysical factors that people feel invade personal boundaries.46 Bluhm et al. found that the invasiveness of a device depended on which effects remained localized, the amount of control the patient had over those effects, how permanent the effects seemed to be, and familiarity with the intervention.38 Refining perceptions of invasiveness related to the ingestible, implantable devices discussed in this paper opens avenues for further research. For example, invasiveness may be heightened because these devices are swallowed and can be controlled externally, both crossing a physical barrier and lessening a patient’s feeling of autonomy. Data-sharing capabilities may also make patients feel their privacy is invaded by such a device. On the other hand, these ingestible devices may decrease “lifestyle invasiveness,” making it easier for patients who lack access to transit or live far away from their physician to maintain their treatment regimen.

ETHICAL ISSUES RELATED TO DATA COLLECTION BY THE DEVICE

As implantable electroceuticals and mRNA delivery devices are being designed to collect patient data and distribute them wirelessly, ethical issues surrounding privacy, security, and data sharing are especially relevant. It is well established that patients are not aware of the extent to which health applications share data with third parties. For example, wearable devices and health applications share data with other apps that collect consumer data.47 These data, in turn, can be used to deliver highly targeted promotional messages from developers and commercial sponsors, including the pharmaceutical industry.48 Papers in the scoping review raised a variety of concerns related to data use, including surveillance, invasion of privacy, violations of confidentiality, coercion, application in litigation and judicial cases, denying healthcare, and nefarious uses, including perpetrating harm.

Patients may not be adequately informed of the possible harms that can result from the sharing or misuse of data collected by devices. The harms that can result from data risks are not adequately covered by user agreements, informed consent, or shared decision-making procedures. The requirement of a user agreement in addition to the traditional consent process for patients receiving a device creates another level of complexity in understanding the possible harms and benefits from a device, especially since user agreements are generally known to be infrequently read and poorly understood. Such complexity can threaten a patient’s autonomy to make informed decisions.

Sufficient protections from harm related to data collection and use do not exist. Technological solutions, such as cybersecurity “fixes,” are most commonly proposed as the best method to protect the privacy and confidentiality of patient data collected by devices. There are physical and engineering limitations to creating cybersecurity solutions for novel implantable technologies, as well as user fatigue in keeping the security software updated. Importantly, the scoping review revealed that responsibility for adverse events related to data misuse is not clearly delineated. Developers, clinicians, patients, health systems, family, friends, and regulators were all mentioned as having responsibility for adverse events related to data sharing. This diffusion of responsibility allows people to dodge the ethical predicament presented by creating or using products that produce harm, thus making it easier to fail to act to prevent harm.49

CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK

More empirical research involving researcher, patient, and clinician populations is needed to elucidate ethical issues that may be unique to ingestible, implantable electronic devices and mRNA delivery devices. We also suggest several areas where policy development could be considered based on the identification of existing gaps. First, informed consent documentation and processes should adequately convey the full range of risks of harm related to data use and the potential long-lasting physiological effects of these technologies. For instance, tools being developed to enhance shared decision-making around complex clinical decisions50 might also be used to enhance the informed consent procedures as these unique devices are developed and tested in a research setting. Second, devices are currently approved for clinical use by regulatory authorities based on an assessment of clinical harms and benefits that does not include an evaluation of the privacy and security of data collected by a device. Data security and privacy are typically addressed in legal user agreements and are overseen by separate regulatory agencies that are often related to communication and not health. Given the numerous ethical issues raised concerning harms from data collected and shared by ingestible devices, adequate protection of data, including safeguards against malicious manipulation of data, should be required as part of the regulatory approval process for such devices. Third, adverse-event reporting on novel technologies should include adverse events associated with data use or misuse. Fourth, policymakers and regulators should note the diffusion of responsibility that is evident in the literature on ethical risks related to the collection and use of data from these devices and develop clear pathways for assigning accountability for adverse effects related to data use.

METHODS

Protocol and registration

Our scoping review protocol, which contains a detailed description of our search strategy and methods, has been published on the Open Science Framework here: https://osf.io/tf4ec.51 We report the review according to PRISMA-ScR.52 We followed JBI and Cochrane methods for conducting systematic scoping reviews, summarized briefly below.53–55

Given that the research and development of ingestible, implantable electronic devices and mRNA therapeutics are rapidly evolving, we took a broad approach to identifying and analyzing any relevant ethics or legal scholarship related to the research or use of these products. We searched for empirical research, relevant ethical frameworks, and normative and theoretical scholarship that help define the range of issues. We describe the types and characteristics of available evidence or scholarly writing, key concepts, gaps in the evidence, and areas with sufficient evidence for systematic reviews, if any.

Information sources and search strategy

Under the guidance of a health sciences librarian (B.H.), we developed a broad and comprehensive search strategy that included the identification of papers described under the objective and eligibility criteria above. Search terms were identified using controlled vocabulary (subject headings), by consulting with content experts, and by text analysis of articles previously identified as meeting selection criteria. B.H. conducted searches on March 22, 2024, and April 8, 2024. The search strategy is described in detail in our protocol. The search strategy is described in detail in our protocol, and the search strategy for one database is shown in Table S1.51 We built the search in Ovid Medline (PubMed equivalent) and then translated the syntax to the other databases.

The following databases were queried in March: Ovid MEDLINE ALL (1946 to March 21, 2024); Embase (Embase.com, 1974 to March 21, 2024); Web of Science; American Psychological Association PsycINFO (Ovid, 1806 to present); ProQuest Dissertations & Theses A&I (1743 to present); and Google Scholar (first 100 citations) retrieved via Publish or Perish (available from https://harzing.com/resources/publish-or-perish). The following databases were searched in April: IEEE Xplore, BELIT Bioethics Literature Database, JSTOR, ACM Digital Library, Nexis Uni, and Westlaw International.

Conference abstracts/papers were excluded in Embase. Searches were limited to English language citations. Animal-only studies were excluded. No other limits were applied. Search strategies were reviewed by another health sciences librarian, Ellie Svoboda, using the PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies) checklist.56 Search terms were identified using controlled vocabulary (subject headings), by consulting with content experts, and by text analysis of gold-standard articles.

Search results were manually deduplicated in Endnote. All unique results were imported into Covidence, where the remaining duplications were automatically identified and removed.

On July 26, 2024, we used citation chaser, an application for backward citation chaining, to search the references cited in our final sample of included studies for any additional relevant works.57

Eligibility criteria

Setting

We included research and scholarship conducted or based in any country or written by authors from any country.

Types of publications/study designs

We included (1) empirical research of any design (qualitative, quantitative, and reviews of qualitative or quantitative research); (2) opinions, editorials, and letters; (3) frameworks (a set of theoretical assumptions that guide the structure and interpretation of findings); (4) normative scholarship; (5) theoretical scholarship; (6) legal and other scholarly analysis published in indexed biomedical, legal, or ethics literature; (7) reviews (any kind); and (8) full-paper conference proceedings. We excluded conference proceedings without full papers, preprints, blogs, social media, media reports, abstract-only pieces, and full books. We excluded publications published in languages other than English.

Population

We included studies conducted in or discussing humans of any age, gender, race, or ethnicity. We excluded studies focused solely on animals (e.g., animal ethics, regulation of animal studies, and studies in laboratory animals).

Intervention

As our preliminary searches identified no ELSI papers on ingestible, implantable electronic devices (or systems) and ingestible, implantable mRNA delivery devices, we broadened the focus of this review to include (1) ingestible electroceuticals and (2) ingestible mRNA therapeutics. We defined ingestible electroceuticals as devices designed to be ingested and containing electronics that deliver electrical stimulation to areas of the GI tract. We defined ingestible mRNA pills as those designed to inject mRNA into the GI tract and function to encode therapeutic proteins. We included literature discussing relevant technologies either generally or by proprietary name. We excluded literature focused solely on wearable electronics, pacemakers and other cardiac devices, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) units, cochlear implants, and mRNA vaccines, as the ethical, legal, and social issues regarding these technologies have been well defined and documented.

Comparison

For empirical studies, when relevant, we included any comparisons to (1) ingestible electroceuticals and (2) ingestible mRNA therapeutics.

Outcomes

As this is a scoping review, we included any outcomes related to ethical, legal, or social issues related to (1) ingestible electroceuticals, (2) ingestible mRNA therapeutics, or (3) related devices.

Selection process

Title/abstract screening

After deduplication, 5,992 articles remained in Covidence and were included in the first screening phase. Six screeners (E.G.C., N.B., T.R.G., P.R.C., J.D., and L.B.) evaluated the titles and abstracts in Covidence against the criteria described in our protocol.51 Two screeners independently reviewed each article, with conflicts adjudicated by a senior screener (L.B.). Quality assurance checks were performed on a 10% sample of included and excluded articles when screening was approximately 33% complete and again when screening was about 75% complete. At the end of this phase, 5,840 articles were excluded. All PDFs of included articles not automatically acquired by Covidence were retrieved and uploaded.

Full-text screening

Five screeners (N.B., J.D., L.B., T.R.G., and P.R.C.) evaluated 152 full-text articles. Two screeners independently reviewed the full text against the eligibility criteria, with disagreements decided by a senior screener (L.B. or N.B.). This means that 2 screeners read the full text of 152 articles to determine if the articles met the selection criteria, resulting in 83 articles for inclusion and analysis. Reasons for excluding full-text reports were selected from a dropdown list and recorded. There was no need to contact any of the study authors for clarification to make screening decisions. Articles identified through citation chasing were screened at the title/abstract and full-text levels by a single coder (N.B.).

Data extraction and verification

Quantitative data charting

We developed and pilot tested a data extraction and coding form in the web-based platform Qualtrics. One reviewer (either N.B., J.D., or H.S.) extracted and coded data, which were verified by another reviewer (L.B. or N.B.).54,58 We extracted publication type, publication information (author, title, and date), funding sources, disclosed conflicts of interest, and geographic regions relevant to the article. We coded the specific health conditions and interventions (implantable electroceuticals, mRNA devices, and other) discussed in each article. We coded an initial list of ELSI topics according to our protocol.21 Similarly to Fleming et al., we coded “mentions” of any ELSI topic expressed by a specific passage in the text of the included paper.59 Thus, in this initial phase of coding, we did not capture the context of the ELSI issue or how it was described. We coded whether each paper mentioned issues of equity or collected data for an equity analysis as outlined in the PROGRESS categories.60 We planned to stratify outcomes by disadvantaged population type, if reported (e.g., pregnant women, older adults, people with lower incomes, and people living with mental illness) but were unable to do this because there were insufficient data in the included studies.35 For empirical research, we also extracted the study design, study population, and interventions evaluated.

Qualitative content analysis

As recommended by Pollock, we conducted a basic content analysis to map and describe the ELSI concepts in more depth.54 Articles that contained sufficient text related to the coded ELSI issues (i.e., more than a “mention”) were coded line by line to capture the full scope and context of the ELSI content discussed. Codes and subcodes were developed both deductively (from the codes used for the quantitative data extraction portion of analysis) and inductively (based on the ideas mentioned in the texts themselves). Examples of deductively generated subcodes include privacy, confidentiality, and data sharing (subcodes of the privacy and security parent code) and agency, coercion, and shared decision-making (subcodes of the autonomy parent code). Codes/subcodes generated inductively included invasiveness, trust, enhancement, unique features of regulatory processes, social influence, and sustainability. To further understand the context in which an ELSI issue was discussed, each article was also assigned a tag denoting the authors’ sentiments toward industry involvement in device development (favorable or not favorable), their view of the device itself (favorable, neutral, or not favorable), and the magnitude of their concerns regarding ELSI topics (cautious regarding the issue or not cautious). Articles that did not contain enough information to ascertain the authors’ sentiments toward one or more of these issues were not given a tag for those issues. Each article was independently coded by one of three members of the study team (N.B., J.D., or H.S.). This subset of the team met weekly to review and reconcile coding questions. Coding frequencies and reports were generated using Atlas.ti (Atlas.ti v.24.2.0; Berlin, Germany) and were reviewed by a larger subset of the study team (N.B., J.D., H.S., and L.B.). Based on these coding reports, analytic memos were written for each code and subcode to describe the data more holistically.

Evaluation of included studies

Per standard scoping review methods, we did not assess the risk of bias for primary studies due to the heterogeneity of study designs included.20

Data analysis

Due to the large amount of heterogeneity of publication types and breadth of ELSI topics included in the review, a quantitative summary of the findings was not feasible. We provide frequency counts, text, and graphical presentations of the data. We present the synthesis according to recommendations for scoping reviews.53 Quantitative data were generated in Qualtrics (Qualtrics; Provo, USA) and analyzed in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; Vienna, Austria). Coding frequencies and coding reports were generated by Atlas.ti to facilitate qualitative data analysis. Review authors who were authors of studies eligible for inclusion were not involved in the screening, data extraction, or evaluation of those studies.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

There is little research on the ethical, legal, and social implications (ELSIs) of the development and use of ingestible implantable electronics and mRNA delivery devices. Among existing literature, researchers have identified concerns about privacy, security, and data use; invasiveness, both in the physical and psychological sense; and impacts on trust in patient-clinician relationships. Our analysis of the literature found that informed consent documentation and processes do not adequately convey the risks of harm related to data use and the health-related effects of these technologies. Patient risks resulting from potential data misuse are not considered for adverse event reporting or regulatory approval. Examining participants’ perceptions of privacy and security issues, invasiveness, and implications for the physician-patient relationship will be needed in future studies. Future improvements in data protection, communication of risk, and accountability are needed alongside the development of these technologies.
