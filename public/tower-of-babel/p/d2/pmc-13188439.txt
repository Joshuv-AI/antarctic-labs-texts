Familial complex regional pain syndrome in pediatric siblings: a diagnostic challenge and therapeutic success—a case report
Authors: Faten Ahmad Al-Kateb, Duaa Dyab, Rola Alsalamah, Basher Almadani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) is exceptionally rare in pediatric populations (Weissmann and Uziel in Pediatr Rheumatol Online J 14(1):29, 2016), with familial clustering being previously unreported in children. The diagnostic challenge is compounded by the extensive differential diagnosis of severe extremity pain in this age group (Harden et al. in Pain Med 23(Suppl 1):S1–S53, 2022; Marinus et al. in Lancet Neurol 10(7):637–648, 2011).Case presentationWe report two pediatric siblings of Arab ethnicity who developed sequential severe extremity pain:A 3-year-old girl of Arab ethnicity with a 2-month history of progressive pain starting in the lower extremities and extending to the upper extremities, followed by her 5-year-old brother with a similar presentation 20 days later. Both children presented with severe functional impairment, autonomic dysfunction, and met the Budapest Criteria for CRPS Type 1 (Harden et al. in Pain 150(2):268–274, 2010). Comprehensive investigations systematically excluded infectious, inflammatory, neoplastic, genetic, and metabolic causes through a structured diagnostic approach. The tight temporal clustering (within a 20-day interval) suggests shared environmental triggers in genetically susceptible individuals, rather than purely hereditary factors. Both patients demonstrated remarkable improvement with Gabapentin and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy (Mangnus et al. in Drugs 82(5):511531, 2022; Goebel et al. in Ann Intern Med 152(3):152–158, 2010).ConclusionsThis represents the first reported case of familial CRPS in pediatric siblings, demonstrating the complex interplay between environmental triggers and genetic susceptibility in CRPS pathogenesis. The case highlights the importance of systematic differential diagnosis in pediatric extremity pain and demonstrates the efficacy of early aggressive treatment with Gabapentin and IVIG in pediatric CRPS (Mangnus et al. in Drugs 82(5):511531, 2022; Goebel et al. in Ann Intern Med 152(3):152–158, 2010).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-026-06006-x.Keywords: Complex regional pain syndrome, Pediatric, Familial, Differential diagnosis, Gabapentin, Intravenous immunoglobulin, Budapest criteria, Environmental triggers

Introduction

Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) is a debilitating chronic neuropathic pain condition characterized by severe pain disproportionate to the inciting event, accompanied by sensory, autonomic, motor, and trophic changes [2, 3]. While affecting approximately 200,000 individuals annually in the United States, pediatric cases represent less than 5% of all CRPS diagnoses, with peak incidence in adolescence rather than early childhood [1].

The condition is classified into Type 1 (without definable nerve injury, formerly known as reflex sympathetic dystrophy) and Type 2 (with definable nerve injury, formerly known as causalgia) [2]. Type 1 CRPS typically develops following minor trauma, surgery, or immobilization, while Type 2 CRPS occurs after major nerve injury. Both types can present with similar clinical features, including severe pain, autonomic dysfunction, and trophic changes [2]. Diagnosis relies on the Budapest Criteria, which require the presence of symptoms and signs across multiple categories: sensory (hyperalgesia/allodynia), vasomotor (temperature asymmetry/skin color changes), sudomotor/edema (edema/sweating changes), and motor/trophic (decreased range of motion/motor dysfunction/trophic changes) (Table 1) [4].

Familial clustering of CRPS has been documented in fewer than 10 cases worldwide, all of which have been exclusively in adults [7]. Recent evidence suggests genetic polymorphisms in HLA genes, inflammatory mediators, and ion channels may confer susceptibility [7]. However, the pathogenesis likely involves complex interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental triggers, including infections, trauma, and immune system activation [7, 8].

The occurrence of CRPS in pediatric siblings has never been reported, making this case significant for understanding the interplay between genetic and environmental contributions to CRPS pathogenesis.

The differential diagnosis of severe extremity pain in children is extensive, encompassing neurological, infectious, rheumatological, hematological, genetic, and metabolic conditions. This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges and systematic approach required to reach the correct diagnosis while excluding potentially life-threatening conditions.

Discussion

Significance of environmental–genetic interaction

This case represents the first reported occurrence of CRPS in pediatric siblings, demonstrating the complex interplay between environmental triggers and genetic susceptibility. Critically, the tight temporal clustering (within a 20-day interval) strongly suggests shared environmental exposure in genetically predisposed individuals, rather than purely hereditary factors [7, 8].

Environmental vs. genetic factors: The 20-day interval between symptom onset in siblings is highly suggestive of a shared environmental trigger acting upon genetically susceptible individuals. This temporal pattern argues against pure genetic inheritance and supports a "two-hit" hypothesis where:

Genetic predisposition provides susceptibility

Environmental trigger precipitates disease onset

This model is consistent with other autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, where genetic susceptibility alone is insufficient for disease development [7].
