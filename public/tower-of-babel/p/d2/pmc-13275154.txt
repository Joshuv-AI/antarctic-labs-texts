Exploring the Interface Between International Nongovernmental Organizations and Local Vietnamese Hospitals in Cleft Care
Authors: Thalia Le, Lan N. Nguyen, Charlotte E. Berry, Anh M. Dinh, Julien Klaudt-Moreau, Ton C. Doan, Christopher M. Runyan, Phuong D. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Despite recent advances in cleft care, Vietnam continues to have extensive involvement from international nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), creating a fragmented system with limited coordination. This project seeks to establish a baseline understanding of Vietnam’s cleft care landscape, including major international NGOs and their collaborations with local partners, costs and insurance coverage, and patterns applicable to other low- and middle-income countries with NGO-supported cleft care.Methods:We conducted a mixed-methods study using literature review and semi-structured interviews with surgeons, hospital administrators, and NGO representatives (n = 7). Sources included PubMed, targeted Google searches, and government regulations on NGO involvement, cleft care costs, and insurance policies.Results:Vietnam partners with 17 international NGOs that operate through 3 distinct but complementary models: vertical, horizontal, and diagonal models. These collaborations offer financial support, infrastructure, technology investment, and research development, leading to higher surgical volume, more timely access to care, and implementation of standardized multidisciplinary cleft services in Vietnam. National insurance provides broad coverage but requires a complex referral process with limited benefits for secondary cleft services. Limited awareness of insurance benefits and mistrust of local care further discourage the use of national health insurance.Conclusions:Improving access to comprehensive cleft services in Vietnam requires streamlining insurance referral pathways and decentralizing advanced cleft services to regional centers to ensure continuity of care. Aligning NGO activities with national policies is essential to building a coordinated and sustainable cleft care system in Vietnam and in other low- and middle-income countries with strong insurance frameworks and significant NGO involvement.

Takeaways

Question: How do international nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) collaborate with Vietnam’s local cleft care sectors?

Findings: Vietnam partners with 17 international NGOs that operate through 3 distinct but complementary models, collectively enhancing surgical capacity and comprehensive cleft protocols. However, advanced secondary cleft care services remain concentrated in major tertiary hospitals. Barriers to receiving health insurance reimbursement include complex referral requirements, limited awareness of benefits, mistrust in local facilities, and limited coverage for secondary cleft care.

Meaning: Aligning NGO activities with national policies is essential to building a coordinated and sustainable cleft care system in Vietnam and other LMICs with strong insurance frameworks and NGO presence.

INTRODUCTION

Cleft lip and palate (CLP) is among the most prevalent congenital defects globally, with more than 90% of the burden occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1,2 In Southeast Asia, an estimated 576 million individuals lack access to surgical care.3 Vietnam accounts for 83 million of these cases3 and has the second-highest incidence of severe CLP deformities across Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Northern Africa, and the Middle East.4 With 1.5 million annual births and a cleft incidence of 1 in every 500, the country is estimated to have 3000 new CLP cases annually.5

Vietnam’s public health insurance covers more than 89% of the population and heavily subsidizes pediatric care.6 However, many families depend on nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) for comprehensive cleft treatments.7,8 Despite decades of extensive international NGO engagement, there has been no comprehensive review of their activities and impact on Vietnam’s cleft care. These NGOs operate with different models, patient demographics, and coverage for CLP services.9,10 This variability raises concerns about a fragmented system in which local hospitals, insurance systems, and NGOs operate without sufficient coordination.

Using a mixed-methods approach, this project seeks to establish a baseline understanding of (1) Vietnam’s current cleft care landscape, (2) who the major international NGOs are and how they collaborate with local partners to impact primary and secondary cleft care, (3) costs and coverage of care, (4) barriers to national insurance use, and (5) patterns that can be recapitulated in other LMICs where NGOs support cleft care.

METHODS

Literature Review

We used a mixed-methods design combining interviews and a scoping review of NGO activity and Vietnamese health policy. A scoping review was conducted using PubMed and targeted Google searches with a detailed review of the first 10 pages of results. (See appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays identification of NGOs by databases, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E964.) Data on NGO names, local partners, activities, and sites were extracted. Key regulations, including Decrees 22/2023/TT-BYT, 25/2008/QH12, and 46/2014/QH13, were examined via the Ministry of Health’s database to assess cleft care coverage across hospital tiers.11–13

Interview Design and Participants

T.L. and A.M.D. conducted in-person and virtual interviews with 5 surgeons and 2 administrators from Vietnam National Children’s Hospital (VNCH) in Hanoi and Children’s Hospital 1 (CH1) in Ho Chi Minh City. Three surgeons also serve as NGO representatives at their respective institutions.

Data Collection

Interviews were conducted between March 2023 and April 2024 either in person or virtually, before which informed consent was obtained. Questions explored advancements in primary cleft care capacity, integration of comprehensive cleft care protocols at participating hospitals, and barriers to using national insurance for cleft treatment. (See appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the interview questions [translated from Vietnamese to English], https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E965.)

Analysis

T.L. and A.M.D. independently transcribed and translated interviews from Vietnamese to English and analyzed the data via inductive thematic analysis. An initial coding framework was refined to identify key themes. Recurring topics were grouped into thematic categories. Findings were compared with data from the literature and policy documents to identify areas of overlap and divergence. Quotations and excerpts were lightly edited for clarity.

Cleft Care Coverage in Vietnam

Vietnam’s tax-funded social health insurance covered more than 89% of the population in 2021 and was projected to reach 95% by 2025.6,14,15 Hospitals are tiered into primary (district), secondary (provincial), and tertiary (central) levels.16 Patients are incentivized to follow a referral pathway from lower to higher tier hospitals (health administrators 3 and 4, oral communication) (Table 1). Cost of Surgical Cleft Services in Vietnam

In 2023, Vietnam’s gross domestic product per capita was $4346,17,18 but households with cleft patients reported a much lower average annual income of $2400, often in unskilled labor and located far from care centers.2,7 Under Decree 22/2023/TT-BYT, tertiary hospitals charge $103–$169 per primary cleft surgery, with nonsurgical care costs adding $75–$150—bringing the total to approximately $200–$300 (Table 3).13,19 NGO-reported costs per surgery were notably higher at $511–$850 (Operation Smile [OS]) and $367–$426 (Smile for Children) in 2020-adjusted dollars (Table 4).20,21 These higher costs reflected a period when NGOs relied on international surgical teams, prompting a later shift toward locally staffed missions for primary CLP surgery.

Current Landscape of Cleft Care in Vietnam

Surgical Volume and Cleft Care Capacity

In Southern Vietnam, CH1, Children’s Hospital 2, and Odonto-Maxillo-Facial Hospital lead pediatric care, whereas in the North, VNCH is the largest tertiary children’s hospital in the country. A study by ReSurge International (Resurge)23 reported that cleft palate repairs at VNCH more than doubled from 2014 to 2019.3 The median age for lip and palate repair also decreased significantly between 1993 and 2003, from 14.0 to 1.3 years and 13.5 to 5.0 years, respectively.24

“Since partnering with Smile Train and Noordhoff Craniofacial Foundation (NCF) in 2019,25 VNCH has increased capacity to nearly 1,500 cleft cases annually, expanded dedicated surgical beds from 1 to 4, and expanded its cleft surgical team from 2 to 11 surgeons” (surgeon 2 and 5, oral communication). ReSurge collaborations introduced many techniques for secondary cleft procedures to VNCH, including mandibular distraction and VPI surgery using Furlow double-opposing Z-plasty, posterior pharyngeal flap, and Orticochea pharyngoplasty.3 ReSurge’s training curriculum also emphasized patient education on oral hygiene, nutrition, and follow-up adherence and helped reduce palatal fistula rates at VNCH from 37.5% to 14.3%.26

Comprehensive Cleft Care Protocols

Historically, cleft treatment in Vietnam focused on primary CLP repairs, overlooking long-term needs such as aesthetics, speech, and psychological support. In collaboration with Smile Train in 2009, CH1 in the South pioneered the implementation of a 14-step comprehensive protocol to provide longitudinal and multidisciplinary CLP care in Vietnam (surgeons 1 and 3, oral communication). “The comprehensive protocol spans from prenatal ultrasound to assess congenital malformations until adulthood (Fig. 1). We operate on 8–10 CLP patients daily, most of whom are referred from provinces without cleft facilities or cannot afford local treatment” (surgeon 1, oral communication). Odonto-Maxillo-Facial Hospital, a partner of the University of British Columbia and British Columbia Children’s Hospital, has also provided comprehensive CLP care for more than 20,000 Vietnamese patients from 32 southern provinces since 2018.27 Similarly, in the north, “VNCH has adopted NCF’s comprehensive cleft care protocol since 2019” (surgeon 2, oral communication).

NGOs running horizontal models, such as Smile Train and Noordhoff Craniofacial Foundation (NCF), build local capacity through direct hospital funding, travel subsidies for socioeconomically disadvantaged patients, and reimbursement for services not covered by national health insurance.30–33 The scale of their partnerships is immense. A 2024 Smile Train report noted that they supported 23,189 primary cleft procedures in Vietnam from 2001 to 2023.22 These collaborations enhance infrastructure, standardize protocols, and build long-term local expertise. For instance, the development of permanent cleft centers, such as NCF’s partnership with VNCH and Smile Train’s partnership with CH1, has enabled these local hospitals to adopt internationally recognized comprehensive cleft protocols and independently provide multidisciplinary cleft care in Vietnam. Smile Train also established a Cleft Leadership Center in Vietnam in 2021 as a permanent hub for advanced treatment, education, and research.34 However, the horizontal model depends on large tertiary hospitals as partners, with limited impact in rural areas.30–33 Heavy reliance on NGO funding also concentrates and overloads patients to major urban tertiary hospitals, disrupting referral pathways and sidelining provincial and district facilities (Fig. 2).35,36 The centralization of multidisciplinary cleft care to tertiary hospitals with NGO partnerships weakens incentives to expand secondary cleft services to other regional hospitals.

Geographic distribution of major NGOs with multiple local hospital partners in Vietnam.

The vertical model delivers short-term relief through mission-based care by deploying foreign or local teams to perform surgery locally.37 Organizations, including OS, Facing the World (FTW), Smile for Children, ReSurge, and Nuoy Reconstructive International (formerly Reconstructive International Cooperation Exchange), initially relied on this model in Vietnam. Government collaboration, such as OS’s partnership with the Ministry of Health, is essential to prioritize resources in areas of unmet need and to plan future mission trips. Such partnerships also ensure program adaptability and support the establishment of regional cleft registries jointly managed by NGOs and health ministries. Although effective in expanding geographic reach and addressing urgent needs, these episodic interventions often lack robust follow-up and systematic patient record review. Visiting professionals with limited cleft training who join missions mainly to gain experience may increase the risk of complications that local teams must manage without adequate documentation.38–41 Mission-based care can also inadvertently discredit local surgeons, as patients may postpone treatment to wait for visiting international teams.42

Recently, many of these NGOs have transitioned towards the more sustainable strategy of the diagonal model, leveraging the strengths of both vertical and horizontal care models (Fig. 3). A 2019 review found that 50% of NGOs providing cleft care used a diagonal model, compared with 40% using a vertical model and only 10% using a horizontal model.43 This hybrid needs-based mission approach allows NGOs to respond to urgent surgical needs while investing in sustainable improvements to local healthcare infrastructure for interdisciplinary care.44,45 For instance, FTW, ReSurge, and Nuoy Reconstructive International continue to provide mission-based educational services but increasingly focus on multidisciplinary care, needs-driven training for local surgeons, and research capacity development. Their programs now extend beyond primary cleft repair to include complex procedures such as VPI surgery, ABG, and orthognathic surgery. Equipment donation and advanced technology training support long-term sustainability by enabling digital monitoring of cleft-related procedure outcomes, such as 3-dimensional volumetric assessment of ABG, virtual surgical planning, and 3-dimensional printing of customized splints for orthognathic surgery.46 FTW has also supported Vietnamese hospitals in achieving international accreditation through the United Kingdom’s Royal College of Surgeons to standardize care protocols.47 Operation Smile Academy, launched during COVID-19, is a virtual platform designed to strengthen cleft care training in LMICs. It quickly became a valuable resource for Vietnamese trainees through competency-based e-learning and mentorship.48 These NGOs also offer structured training programs, including simulation-based workshops and international clinical fellowships, though many remain limited to observerships for Vietnamese surgeons at partnered international sites. Public engagement campaigns with local NGOs and partner hospitals, delivered through social media, television news, and hospital websites, help raise awareness of NGO partnerships and support follow-up scheduling.

Barriers to Insurance Use for Cleft Care

The most frequently cited barrier to maximizing insurance reimbursement for cleft care services at tertiary hospitals is the difficulty in obtaining referrals from lower tier health facilities (surgeons 1, 2, 4, 5; health administrator 1, oral communication). This disproportionately affects rural patients who travel long distances to obtain referrals, resulting in delayed care or increased out-of-pocket expenses. Second, although provincial hospitals are mandated to provide or refer cleft cases to higher tier hospitals, in practice, some underresourced provincial hospitals may avoid issuing referrals to sidestep penalties for failing to offer the required services (surgeon 2, oral communication). Third, many families are unaware of their insurance benefits or are referred to unwanted in-network hospitals, leading them to bypass the referral system and pay higher out-of-pocket costs at preferred facilities (surgeon 2, oral communication). Additional barriers include patient mistrust in the quality of care at provincial hospitals,2,7 and stricter surgical eligibility criteria at nonspecialized secondary or tertiary hospitals.

DISCUSSION

Our study is the first of its kind to shed light on the intricate, multilayered relationship between how international NGOs interact with local healthcare systems and governmental policies in Vietnam. National health policies are often overlooked in discussions of NGO activity, yet they shape how services are coordinated, reimbursed, and sustained. International NGOs often have the responsibility to respond to their donor and stakeholder expectations. The need to demonstrate exclusive, program-specific impact and metrics to sustain financial support can sometimes inadvertently create competitive pressure among NGOs, especially when programs compete for overlapping funding sources, operate in the same regions, or partner with the same local institutions. Such dynamics can disincentivize data sharing and cross-organizational collaboration, resulting in variable integration with existing governmental national policies and local insurance frameworks.

Vietnam’s cleft care landscape is uniquely shaped by its cultural, sociopolitical, and economic context. However, the recurring challenges of fragmented communication among multiple care stakeholders and limited alignment with local policies seen in Vietnam’s cleft care have also been observed in other LMICs with strong health insurance frameworks and significant NGO presence. Therefore, our findings offer a granular yet transferable cleft care framework to explore corollaries across these complex LMIC ecosystems without prescribing universal solutions.

Recommendations for Strengthening Local National Policy Impact

For local stakeholders, our findings suggest several strategies to enhance the accessibility and coordination of cleft care in Vietnam. First, our findings inform national policymakers that the availability of health insurance coverage alone does not ensure access to cleft services. The Vietnamese referral-based insurance structure, which requires patients to progress from district to provincial to central hospitals, often leads to delayed care, increased travel costs, or reduced reimbursements when referral pathways are bypassed. Similar policy-related barriers to accessing care have been reported in other LMICs. In Colombia, CLP care is also not formally regulated at the national level, and treatment locations are determined by insurer-specific provider networks rather than standardized care pathways,44 resulting in fragmented longitudinal care, inconsistent access to secondary services, and increased reliance on international NGOs.

High out-of-pocket costs for medical and dental devices not covered by national insurance plans also reinforce NGO dependence. In the Philippines, despite national insurance coverage of up to $642 for primary CLP repair, limited support for secondary services has resulted in NGOs independently financing and delivering follow-up care, often provided outside formal referral pathways and with limited systematic integration into public health planning.49 Similarly, in India, even with widespread public and private coverage through schemes such as Ayushman Bharat, external anomalies such as CLP are often excluded, causing increased out-of-pocket expenses.50,51

Collectively, these examples reflect how systemic gaps in government regulations, insurance referral pathways, and coverage for secondary cleft services drive reliance on NGOs, resulting in parallel service delivery that is insufficiently integrated with national data systems, referral pathways, and reimbursement structures. These challenges highlight the need for LMICs to expand insurance coverage to secondary cleft care services and streamline complex health insurance pathways to improve care accessibility. In Vietnam, potential solutions include allowing tertiary hospitals to directly register insured patients and implementing electronic referral systems to guide patients through appropriate referral steps and maximize insurance reimbursement. National insurance coverage should be expanded to encompass the full continuum of cleft treatment. Finally, disseminating the comprehensive cleft care protocols successfully implemented at major centers such as CH1 and VNCH may support broader adoption of multidisciplinary care models nationwide and strengthen collaboration across regional hospitals.

Recommendations for Enhancing International NGO Impact and Sustainability in Local Cleft Care

The most effective model for cleft service delivery is one in which NGO efforts are coordinated within a country-wide strategic framework, are responsive to the priorities of local health leadership, and complement identified gaps in the public health system to support sustainable, longitudinal care. Each NGO model offers unique strengths and limitations but should function in complementary ways. In Vietnam, vertical models reach underserved areas but lack sustainability. Horizontal models support larger patient volumes and long-term infrastructure at tertiary hospitals but are geographically limited. NGOs with vertical models should work with the Ministry of Health to improve patient mapping and long-term tracking systems to ensure timely follow-up and management of complications.

Horizontal organizations such as Smile Train and NCF can continue to provide financial and infrastructural support for local tertiary centers while helping standardize comprehensive cleft care protocols nationwide. Future efforts could include developing regional comprehensive cleft centers and research hubs to decentralize cleft services and promote long-term sustainability. Permanent comprehensive cleft centers exist in many LMICs, such as OS’s Guwahati Comprehensive Cleft Care Center in India52 and the Cebu Comprehensive Cleft Care Center of Excellence in the Philippines.53 Diagonal organizations should continue to focus on needs-based training for secondary cleft procedures,31,44 adapting their educational missions to Vietnam’s evolving cleft care landscape, as well as supporting bidirectional clinical exchanges. Leveraging the strengths of each model and aligning them under shared national priorities will support a coordinated and sustainable cleft care network that integrates both domestic and international strengths. Improved inter-NGOs cooperation is essential to maximize collective impact, avoid duplication of efforts, and ensure efficient resource allocation across different geographic regions.

LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations, including potential bias from only interviewing surgeons at NGO-affiliated hospitals and not involving patient perspectives. The validity of our interview findings may also be limited by translation from Vietnamese to English and the small sample size, which may not capture the full diversity of stakeholder perspectives. Finally, the semistructured interview guide may have influenced responses by emphasizing certain aspects of NGO–hospital collaboration while limiting exploration of other themes.

CONCLUSIONS

Partnerships with 17 international NGOs have strengthened Vietnam’s surgical capacity and expanded comprehensive cleft protocols, but advanced secondary cleft services remain concentrated in major tertiary hospitals. The 3 NGO operating models offer distinct strengths and limitations but function in complementary ways across the Vietnamese cleft landscape. Barriers to the use of national health insurance include a complex referral system, mistrust in local healthcare facilities, lack of awareness about insurance benefits, and high out-of-pocket costs for travel to access care. Aligning international NGO activities with national policies is essential to ensure a coordinated and sustainable cleft care network in Vietnam and other LMICs with substantial NGO presence.
