Magnetic resonance T2* mapping sequence for imaging of talar cartilage injury under traction with the small field of view coil
Authors: Yan Sun, Qixiang Zhuang, Xueping Gu, Yuefeng Hao, Kefu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe accurate diagnosis of talus cartilage injury is difficult to achieve on conventional sequences. This study aimed to assess the application values of axial traction during high resolution 3.0-T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the ankle on T2* mapping quantitative assessment of talar cartilage imaging with a small field of view (FOV) coil.MethodsA prospective study of 18 volunteers and 31 patients undergoing high-resolution MRI of the ankle with an FOV coil with and without axial traction (5 kg) was performed. Quantitative cartilage assessment included three T2* mapping sequences: (I) dedicated ankle-foot coil without traction, (II) small FOV coil without traction, and (III) small FOV coil with axial traction. Noise and motion artifacts, surface contour, and layer-by-layer display of the talar cartilage on three kinds of T2* mapping images were evaluated on a four-point scale. Subsequently, the T2* values of the different layers of cartilage in the medial, middle, and lateral zones of the talus in the volunteer group and different types of cartilage injuries and noninjured regions in the patient group were compared. Coronal proton density (PD)-weighted turbo spin echo (TSE) BLADE sequences were acquired for morphological evaluation under ankle axial traction, and it was determined whether there was a difference between the BLADE sequence and the T2* mapping sequence in detecting the number of talar cartilage injuries. Statistical analysis included analysis of variance tests with Bonferroni correction.ResultsThe T2* mapping sequence with axial traction in noise and motion artifacts, surface contour, and layer-by-layer display of the talar cartilage were rated superior (P<0.05) as compared to the BLADE sequence, while noise and artifacts before and after the small FOV coil traction were not significantly different (P=1).Significant differences in T2* values were present between cartilage layers across the medial, middle, and lateral talar dome zones in the volunteer group (P<0.001) but not between the superficial and radial layers in the middle zone (P=0.145). In the patient group, T2* mapping with traction detected 11 more injuries than did the BLADE sequence. The mean T2* values of different types of cartilage injuries differed significantly from those of noninjured regions (P<0.001). Pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences between noninjured cartilage and each type of cartilage injury (P<0.05). Significant differences were also observed in all comparisons between different cartilage injury subgroups (P<0.05), except between cartilage fractures and signal alterations (P=1.0).ConclusionsQuantitative T2* mapping sequence for the imaging of talar cartilage injury under axial traction with the small FOV coil increased the joint space width, allowed for better visualization of the surface contour and layers of talar cartilage, reduced noise and motion artifacts, and improved the display discrimination ability of T2* mapping sequence for different injuries to the talar cartilage.

Introduction

Patients with ankle-joint sports injuries often have cartilage damage, with talar cartilage injury being the most common clinical finding. Sports medicine focuses on accurately assessing the degree of early injury, which is crucial for determining different clinical treatment plans and eventual prognosis. Therefore, early and accurate assessment of ankle-joint cartilage injury enables timely clinical intervention, thus preventing further injury and maximizing patient benefit. The use of magnetic resonance (MR) examination for evaluating ankle ligaments and cartilage before treatment is being increasing used among physicians (1,2). However, because the thickness of the talar cartilage is very thin and the imaging features of early cartilage injury are subtle and unclear, routine MR imaging (MRI) has poor accuracy in displaying early injuries. The use of a small field of view (FOV) coil and BLADE sequence under traction conditions has better imaging effects in accurately displaying talar cartilage injury changes than does routine MRI (3), but it only shows cartilage morphology. With the continuous advancement of MR quantitative technology, earlier prediction and detection of cartilage injury before morphological changes have become possible, and MRI quantitative techniques such as T2 mapping have been applied to the early evaluation of bone and joint cartilage physiopathology (4). Gradient echo sequence T2* mapping, has a relatively shorter scanning time in demonstrating the physiological structure of cartilage and demonstrates good potential for application (5). Therefore, this study innovatively used a 3.0-T MR device with a small FOV coil under traction to perform T2* mapping-based quantitative imaging of the talar cartilage in the ankle joint and compared its performance with that of high-resolution BLADE sequence talar cartilage morphological imaging. The aim is to develop a more effective method that can accurately predict and detect cartilage injury before—or in the early stages of—morphological change and thus guide clinical treatment. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-24-1771/rc).

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (No. KL901123) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All participants provided informed consent. This prospective study included a volunteer group and a patient group. The volunteer group comprised 18 participants, including 10 males and 8 females, with an average age of 32±8 years, who had no history of ankle joint degeneration, trauma, or surgical procedures. The patient group included patients who were advised to undergo MRI examination for ankle cartilage injury by clinical doctors in the Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University from July 2020 to July 2024. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (I) routine MRI and T2* mapping sequence scanning of dedicated ankle-foot coils completed on 3.0-T MRI; (II) T2* mapping sequence imaging of a small FOV single-channel surface coil with a diameter of four centimeters at the suspected cartilage injury site on the target side; (III) repeat T2* mapping sequence scanning with simultaneous hanging of a sandbag of approximately 5 kg horizontally at the distal end of the ankle joint, with the position of the surface coil of the small FOV being maintained; and (IV) small-field high-resolution BLADE sequence morphological imaging under the traction state. The exclusion criterion was poor image quality due to motion artifacts or improper positioning. After 2 cases were excluded due to poor image quality, the final patient group included 31 patients with unilateral ankle joints, aged 15–69 years, and with a median age of 45.6±17.83 years.

Imaging examination methods

All participants underwent examinations in the supine position, feet first in a 3.0-T MR scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Initially, a coronal scan was performed with a 16-channel phased array dedicated ankle-foot coil (Medcoil Healthcare, Suzhou, China) for the ankle joint to obtain routine turbo spin echo-proton density (TSE-PD) and T2* mapping. Subsequently, a 4-cm-diameter single-loop small FOV surface polarization coil (Siemens Healthineers) was placed near the talar dome level on the examined side ankle joint after being secured by a strap. A repeated T2* mapping scan was performed under the same parameters as those of the aforementioned dedicated ankle-foot coil. Finally, a sandbag weighing 5 kg was hung on the distal foot side of the ankle joint, and horizontal traction was applied (Figure 1). Under this condition, the same parameter T2* mapping scan was repeated, and the patient group was scanned with the BLADE sequence to increase the display of cartilage morphology. All participants underwent routine TSE-PD and T2* mapping sequences with the ankle-foot coil before the small FOV coil T2* mapping sequence was performed before and after traction. Finally, imaging of the patient group was completed with BLADE sequence and traction, with the parameters of the T2* mapping sequences being completely the same for all three states (Figure 2). The sequence parameters were as follows: (I) Routine MR scanning was performed, including oblique coronal plane frequency-saturated fat TSE-PD imaging along the direction of the tibial internal and external ankle [repetition time (TR) =2,140 ms, echo time (TE) =22 ms, FOV =180 mm × 180 mm, pixel size =0.5×0.5 mm2, matrix =320×256, slice thickness =3.0 mm, time acquisition (TA) =1 min and 48 sec], followed by T2* mapping (TR =445 ms; TE =4.36, 11.90, 19.44, 26.98, and 34.52 ms; FOV =120 mm × 120 mm; pixel size =0.2×0.2 mm2; matrix =320×320; slice thickness =2.0 mm; and TA =4 min and 46 sec). (II) After the small FOV surface coil was replaced, a T2* mapping sequence scan was performed under the same parameters as those described above. (III) A sandbag weighing approximately 5 kg was hung on the plantar side of the examined ankle joint for horizontal traction, and the T2* mapping scan was repeated under this condition with the same parameters. Meanwhile, under this traction state, a BLADE sequence with oblique coronal plane frequency-saturated fat-suppression was performed (TR =3,000 ms, TE =49 ms, FOV =100 mm × 100 mm, pixel size 0.3×0.3 mm2, matrix 320×320, slice thickness =2.0 mm, BLADE coverage =100%, TA =5 mins and 20 sec) (Table 1).

Semiquantitative evaluation of the volunteer group

Semiquantitative morphological MR analysis of ankle talar cartilage was performed on the Syngovia postprocessing workstation (Siemens Healthineers), and blind evaluations were conducted by two radiologists (with 10 and 11 years of bone and muscle diagnosis experience, respectively). In case of a difference in opinions, a consensus was reached after discussion. The image quality of the three T2* mapping sequences were compared in terms (I) noise and motion artifacts, (II) upper surface contour of the talus cartilage, and (III) layer-by-layer display of the talus cartilage. (I) Noise and motion artifacts were evaluated on a four-point scale as follows: 1= severe noise and artifacts resulting in unacceptable image quality, 2= obvious noise and artifacts that may limit diagnostic performance, 3= less noise and artifacts with good image quality, and 4= almost no noise and artifacts with the best image quality. (II) The upper surface contour of the talus cartilage was evaluated as follows: 1= contour cannot be displayed (entire cartilage surface is blurry and cannot be distinguished from the opposite tibia distal cartilage surface or synovial area), 2= some contours are displayed, 3= most contours are displayed, and 4= all contours are displayed (the entire cartilage surface can be clearly distinguished from the synovium and opposite tibia distal cartilage surface. (III) Layer-by-layer display of the talus cartilage was evaluated as follows: 1= display of one-layer structure, 2= display of two-layer structure, 3= display of three-layer structure, and 4= display of four-layer structure.

Quantitative evaluation of the volunteer group

Quantitative measurements of T2* values were performed on the T2* mapping images in different layers of the talar cartilage under plantar traction. The articular surface of the talar dome was divided with a three-column grid into three zones equal in area in the axial plane. The three equal zones were delineated as the medial, middle, and lateral zones, respectively. The surface areas of each zone of the articular talar dome were identical. In the coronal T2* mapping image, three different cartilage zones (medial, middle, and lateral zones) were selected for measurement. In this study, we assumed that the different layers from the top down on the talar cartilage T2* mapping image corresponded to the multilayer physiological structure of transparent cartilage (i.e., the superficial layer → the transitional layer → the radial layer → the calcification layer) (6). The measuring range for each layer of the cartilage was ≥20 pixels. The measurements were performed on the Syngovia postprocessing workstation, and the window width and level were adjusted to provide sufficient grayscale display for the different layers of the cartilage. Due to the small FOV coil’s signal acquisition range of approximately 4 cm, the measurement of T2* values for each talar cartilage was only performed on the injured side and the connected middle region. The area opposite the injured side was not measured because it fell beyond the small coil acquisition range. The potential differences in T2* values between different layers of talar cartilage in the medial, middle, and lateral zones were compared.

Quantitative evaluation of the patient group

A based on the cartilage injury grading of high-resolution MRI of the ankle joint, talar cartilage injury was defined as having abnormal cartilage thickness, abnormal cartilage surface (cartilage fissure, fracture, or cartilage detachment), and internal signal changes (7). First, the two senior radiologists counted the number of injured cartilage regions in both T2* mapping under traction and BLADE sequence images, respectively (and reached a consensus through discussion if there were differences in opinions), to determine if there were differences in the number of talar cartilage injuries identified between the two methods. Second, the window width and level of the T2* mapping images were adjusted on the Syngovia postprocessing workstation, and the T2* values of the entire layer of the abnormal cartilage injury regions and those of the adjacent noninjured cartilage regions were measured. The region of talar cartilage that had lost four layers of color for the normal cartilage on T2* mapping image was defined as an abnormal color gradation. The measurement values of all abnormal cartilaginous regions on the T2* mapping were classified according to the types of cartilage injuries as follows: type I, abnormal cartilage thickness; type II, cartilage fracture (cartilage fissure without separation); type III, cartilage detachment, including (I) blistered cartilage with intact continuity but partial detachment below, (II) partial detachment with only one end continuous, and (III) free detachment with no connection at either end; and type IV, internal signal change of talar cartilage. These values were compared with the T2* measurements of the entire layer of noninjured talar cartilage (type V).

Statistical methods

SPSS 24.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis, with frequency being used to describe the count data. Analysis of variance analysis (ANOVA) was applied for the subjective scores for noise and motion artifacts, surface contour of talar cartilage, and layer-by-layer display of talar cartilage in the three T2* mapping sequences; the T2* values of different layers of cartilage in the medial, middle, and lateral zones of talar cartilage in the volunteer group; and the comparison of T2* measurements between different types of cartilage injuries and noninjured regions in the patient group. Bonferroni correction analysis was used for pairwise comparisons, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The semiquantitative evaluation showed that there were statistically significant differences between the three T2* mapping sequences in noise and motion artifacts, surface contour, and layer-by-layer display of the talar cartilage. Only the comparison between noise and artifact before and after small coil traction did not show a statistical difference (P=1), while pairwise comparisons of the other two parameters showed statistical differences (P<0.05). Discussion

The talar cartilage in the ankle joint is composed of multiple layers of chondrocytes and a large amount of extracellular matrix, mainly consisting of water and electrolytes, type II collagen fibers, and highly negatively charged proteoglycan aggregates. The irregular contour at the bottom of the cartilage is a highly vascularized subchondral bone plate tightly bound to the calcification layer at the deepest part of the transparent cartilage (6). However, the deep MR signal of the transparent cartilage has the characteristic of rapid transverse magnetization decay. Therefore, in this study, T2* mapping was used with the shortest ultrashort echo time of 4.36 ms in order to achieve higher sensitivity to collagen structure and deep cartilage regions (7-9) as compared to high-resolution cartilage imaging using the small FOV BLADE sequence, providing better display of cartilage morphology (3). These sequences were acquired with a small FOV coil, which has been demonstrated to enhance visualization of talar osteochondral lesions (10). Additionally, T2* mapping offers shorter scan times and superior three-dimensional acquisition as compared to traditional T2 mapping. Early treatment of talar cartilage injuries can delay progression, especially for injuries smaller than 1 cm (11). Biochemical changes in the early degeneration of cartilage, such as a significant loss of proteoglycans and collagen proteins and an increase in water content and flow of free water molecules in the extracellular matrix, are limited (12,13). These injuries are potentially reversible but can only be detected by biochemical quantitative techniques such as T2* imaging (14). However, the talar cartilage is very thin, which presents a significant challenge in practical applications. Thus, traction can alleviate surface pressure on the cartilage and make the cartilage stretch to a certain extent, allowing for better display of cartilage injury characteristics.

This study demonstrated the feasibility of using T2* mapping with axial traction in 3.0-T MR imaging to evaluate talar cartilage in the ankle joint. Combined application of axial traction and the small-FOV coil T2* mapping sequence significantly improved image quality by reducing noise and enhancing the display of the talar cartilage surface contour, allowing for the display of four-layer structural features of the normal cartilage. We measured the T2* relaxation times of the superficial layer, transitional layer, radial layer, and calcification layer of normal talar cartilage in volunteer and compared the differences among the layers. Only the superficial layer and radial layer in the middle zone showed no statistical difference, possibly due to the greater pressure on the middle zone resulting in less water content differences between different layers. However, the high-intensity transitional layer between the two layers effectively helped with visual discrimination, and talar cartilage injuries were less likely to occur in the middle zone. These findings may help clinicians to differentiate normal and abnormal cartilage on T2* mapping imaging. We further found that the T2* mapping sequence could detect more cartilage injuries than could high-resolution imaging with a small-FOV coil BLADE sequence because quantitative MRI of cartilage has an advantage in detecting subtle or preexisting cartilage morphological changes. Early detection before irreversible cartilage loss can delay progression to late osteoarthritis and potential joint replacement (15), and thus we performed comprehensive comparisons of T2* relaxation times of different types of cartilage injuries with noninjured cartilage regions. The results indicated that only cartilage fractures (cartilage fissure without separation) were not significantly different from internal signal changes. However, noninjured cartilage exhibited the unique four-layer structural features, making it easy to distinguish cartilage fracture and internal signal change lesions with completely low T2* values. Additionally, internal signal changes mostly appeared in the patchy low T2* value at the junction of partially detached cartilage (one end attached to normal cartilage and the other end detached from subchondral bone) or swollen cartilage (without cartilage fracture or bone fracture, only separated from subchondral bone) with noninjured cartilage. Cartilage fractures mostly exhibited a band-like low signal that traversed the entire cartilage layer. These differences can help distinguish between the two types of injuries. Therefore, T2* quantitative values of different types of cartilage injuries can effectively support the early diagnosis of cartilage status and may have important clinical application value for talar cartilage evaluation.

Quantification of T2* relaxation times is technically challenging. The T2* values measured in noninjured regions in this study (18.07±2.37 ms) were comparable to and fell within the range of those in a control group (16.86–18.46 ms) reported for talar cartilage in patients with ankle instability in a study by Yiwen et al. (16). Moreover, in the patient group in our study, the talar cartilage T2* values at the injury region caused by ankle instability were significantly lower (in the range of 16.92–22.18 ms) compared to the abnormal thickness of 33.08±11.05 ms and cartilage detachment of 24.00±5.20 ms but higher than the 9.89±2.34 ms for cartilage fractures and the cartilage signal change of 12.19±3.26 ms. The reason for this may be the milder degree of cartilage damage caused by ankle instability or the difference in imaging status caused by the compression of the talar cartilage surface and tibial distal cartilage without traction, but this speculation required further verification.

The limitations of this study include a relatively small sample size, especially for the patient group, and thus further expansion in future research is needed. Second, the manual measurement of the talar cartilage T2* values may inevitably entail human operation error. Furthermore, this study used a small FOV focused on the lesion area for patients, but in volunteers, the small FOV was centered on the talar dome. This difference could have affected group comparisons, as the FOV in the volunteer group allowed for consistent imaging of all cartilage layers (superficial layer, transitional layer, radial layer, and calcification layer) across the same anatomical area. In contrast, if the injury is located peripherally or atypically in patients, certain cartilage layers might not be as clearly visualized, and the cartilage thickness and morphology may differ from the talar dome, thus complicating direct comparison. An additional limitation is the lack of arthroscopic findings as a reference standard. This study’s use of axial traction increased setup time and could be uncomfortable or unfeasible for patients with acute pain, potentially limiting its use, especially in patients who already have limited joint mobility or during routine screening for less symptomatic individuals. Whether this additional burden is justified requires a comparison with other modalities used for cartilage imaging. Although T2* with traction improved injury site detection as compared to BLADE, further investigation is needed to determine whether this addition leads to better patient outcomes as compared to traditional imaging techniques.

Conclusions

Weight-bearing traction T2* mapping imaging of the ankle joint is technically feasible. Applying T2* mapping imaging sequences under this state can provide earlier and more comprehensive quantitative features of talar cartilage injuries than can routine high-resolution morphological imaging alone, thereby providing earlier and more accurate MR diagnosis of talar cartilage injuries.
