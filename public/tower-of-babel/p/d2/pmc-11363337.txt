Supramolecular assembly of polycation/mRNA nanoparticles and in vivo monocyte programming
Authors: Yizong Hu, Stephany Y. Tzeng, Leonardo Cheng, Jinghan Lin, Andres Villabona-Rueda, Shuai Yu, Sixuan Li, Zachary Schneiderman, Yining Zhu, Jingyao Ma, David R. Wilson, Sydney R. Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceConventional drug delivery vehicles employ nanoparticle sizes below 200 nm to reduce loss to phagocytic cells. This work, instead, takes advantage of this size effect to achieve preferential messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) delivery to monocytes by engineering colloidally stable particles larger than 200 nm. Utilizing surface charge modulation as a generalizable strategy, this work reports assembly of poly(β-amino ester)/mRNA) nanoparticles into supramolecular assemblies that are kinetically tunable between 200 and 1,000 nm and reveals an optimal size of 400 nm for safe and efficient transfection of monocytes following intravenous administration. Efficient transfection of monocytes by tumor antigen mRNA offers opportunities for cancer vaccines due to the ability of monocytes to differentiate into mature antigen-presenting cells that can mediate an antitumor responses.

Macrophages are a valuable therapeutic target in diverse applications because of their effector roles in innate immunity and their involvement in pathological processes such as cancer (1, 2). Direct programming of macrophages in vivo has important clinical utility, such as reversal of the immune-suppressive state caused by tumor-associated macrophages (3) and generation of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-macrophages (4, 5). Despite a strong focus on macrophages, there has been little exploration of targeting their primary precursor, circulating monocytes, for therapeutic purposes. Circulating monocytes comprise inflammatory monocytes (Ly6Chigh in mice, CD14high CD16– in humans) and patrolling monocytes (Ly6Clow/– in mice, CD14low CD16+ in humans) (6, 7). Both subsets are naturally involved in first-line responses to contain pathogens upon infection. Following phagocytosis and sensing of pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns, Ly6Chigh monocytes differentiate into monocyte-derived dendritic cells (Mo-DCs, CD11c+ MHC-II+) and subsequently migrate to adjacent lymph nodes (8). These cells secrete proinflammatory chemokines and cytokines (9) and effectively prime the adaptive immune response either by direct interactions with T cells (10–12) or by antigen transfer to resident DCs (13) in the lymph node. While conventional biomaterials-based vaccine design focuses on antigen delivery to mature DCs (14), the monocyte behaviors seen in natural infection processes present an interesting yet underexplored venue to examine whether monocytes can be programmed as inducible antigen-presenting cells (APCs), with the advantage of their great abundance, estimated to be nearly 20-fold higher than conventional DCs (8), more so in humans than in mice (15).

Current approaches have achieved delivery to monocytes and macrophages through active targeting, such as by nanoparticles decorated with mannose (3, 4), galactose (16), CD163 (17), RGD (18), and apoptotic bodies (19). It is interesting to note that these phagocytic cells naturally survey the blood and clear foreign particles that enter the circulation with a wide range of chemical and physical properties (i.e., lacking specificity) to achieve adequate protection (20, 21). It is well documented that such clearance is mediated by size-dependent phagocytosis of the particles following intravenous (i.v.) injection and exhibits preferential uptake of larger particulates particularly in the larger submicron to micron range (22). The clodronate liposome is an example that takes advantage of such preferential uptake, being widely used to temporarily deplete monocytes and macrophages in mice (23) because its 1.5 to 2 μm size enables selective phagocytosis. Another example is a 400 to 800-nm antigen-carrying poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) particle formulation that achieved tolerance induction upon delivery to monocytes and macropahges (24). While both cases suggest that a process intended for clearance can also be utilized for delivery purposes, neither has the capability to enable intracellular gene delivery, which is increasingly attractive for programming cells with small interfering ribonucleic acid (siRNA), messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA), and gene editing machinery (25). Here, the major goal is to engineer large submicron (>200 nm) mRNA vehicles for passively targeted delivery to monocytes via preferential phagocytosis without relying on cell-specific ligands.

Common approaches to stabilize gene delivery vehicles through surface PEGylation (26) and charge repulsion (27) kinetically arrest particle sizes below 200 nm. However, electrostatically complexed nanoparticles are extremely difficult to assemble into larger-sized particles, as Coulombic interactions alone cannot construct superstructures in media with physiological ionic strength without effective methods to impart colloidal stability and avoid uncontrollable aggregation (28). Recognizing that the kinetics perspective has largely been missing from the existing body of work on nanoparticle self-assembly (29, 30), we addressed this challenge by selecting a polymeric carrier structure that enables modulation of both Coulombic and van der Waals interactions in solution, enabling switchable induction of a controlled assembly process by simply tuning the solution conditions. Specifically, we selected a particular structure of poly(β-amino ester) (PBAE), a type of synthetic biodegradable cationic polymer carrier that has been extensively studied for gene delivery (31), and developed a scalable strategy to assemble and stabilize PBAE/mRNA particles with any target size between 100 and 1,000 nm using preformed, sub-100-nm nanoparticles. We first demonstrated that 400-nm particles mediated the highest transfection efficiency in macrophages in vitro and in monocytes in vivo, and then showed the ability of the transfected monocytes to differentiate into functional APCs and elicit a significant tumor-specific immune response using the assembled particles carrying ovalbumin (OVA)-encoding mRNA and a CpG adjuvant.

Results

Controllable Supramolecular Assembly of PBAE/mRNA Particles.

We selected a PBAE structure (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1) based on a screening of a series of PBAE carriers for in vivo mRNA delivery (32), featuring a 2-aminoethyl morpholine pendent group and substantial hydrophobicity along the backbone and side chains. When mixing the ethanol-dissolved PBAE with mRNA in a mildly acidic (pH 5.0) aqueous solution, PBAE/mRNA nanoparticles formed as a result of polyelectrolyte charge neutralization and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 1B). The colloidal stability of these nanoparticles depends on competition between long-range electrostatic repulsion of residual positive charges on the surface and short-range van der Waals attractions among hydrophobic groups on the PBAE chains (27, 33). To allow further assembly of these initially stable nanoparticles (Fig. 1C-1), the excess positive charges of PBAE amines on the nanoparticle surface can be reduced by an increase in pH that deprotonates amine groups and/or an increase in solution ionic strength (I) that decreases the range of the electrostatic forces via Debye screening. Experiments showed that an increase in I alone caused PBAE/mRNA nanoparticles to further assemble (Fig. 1C-2), whereas an increase of pH to 7.4 alone was insufficient (Fig. 1C-3). Combining both approaches by buffer replacement to pH 7.4 and I of 0.205 M (Fig. 1D) mediated a faster assembly to form submicron- and micron-sized particles (Fig. 1C-4). Importantly, this procedure did not lead to dissociation of mRNA and PBAE from assembled particles (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A).

Identifying an approach to effectively arrest the size growth of the assembled nanoparticles is critical to controlling particle size. Addition of 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxy poly(ethylene glycol)-2000 (DMG-PEG2000), a PEGylated lipid, enabled hydrophobic insertion of the lipid tail into the growing particle surface and imparted steric hindrance to effectively stop particle growth and confer sufficient colloidal stability. More importantly, this growth arrest can be achieved at any point during the assembly process if the DMG-PEG2000 concentration is higher than 5% w/w in reference to PBAE (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Following stabilization of the particles, the average zeta potential was drastically reduced from the initial 52.3 ± 0.7 mV for the PBAE/mRNA nanoparticles to nearly neutral (3.1 ± 1.1 mV), regardless of size (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). The resulting particles remained stable after purification via dialysis and could be produced at any target size between 100 and 1,000 nm in physiologically compatible buffers in a scalable and reproducible manner. In addition, the particle assembly kinetics could be fine-tuned by varying the ionic strength of the buffer (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D). The purified particles were stored at –80 °C in suspended form for at least 1 mo and remained unchanged after thawing at ambient temperature for at least 6 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed the morphology of the assembled particles and showed a population size consistent with DLS measurements (Fig. 1G). Crystal-like structures were observed in 300 to 400 nm particles, similar to morphological patterns observed in superstructures of charged gold nanoparticles (29). The mRNA payload levels in a series of particles with different sizes assembled using Cy5-labeled mRNA were measured using the cylindrical illumination confocal spectroscopy (CICS) platform (34) (Fig. 1H). A deconvolution algorithm (Methods) was applied to correlate the Cy5 fluorescent intensity distributions of assembled particles with the unencapsulated single mRNA molecules. As the z-average diameter, Dz, characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS) increased from 100 to 400 nm, the weight average number of mRNA molecules loaded per particle increased, as depicted by the right-shifting fluorescence profiles (Fig. 1 I and J). 400-nm particles carried a weight average of 207 mRNA copies per particle, 53.1-fold more than the 100-nm particles (3.9 copies, Fig. 1K). These results confirmed that the assembled larger submicron particles contained a drastically increased payload capacity compared to nanoparticles with a Dz below 200 nm (35, 36).

Understanding the Supramolecular Assembly Process.

To understand the effect of pH and ionic strength on particle growth, molecular dynamics simulations of the nanoparticle assembly process were conducted using a coarse-grained (CG) model in an implicit solvent (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), with ions and charges on mRNA and PBAE modeled explicitly (37). Unlike most other simulation approaches to complexation phenomena, dynamic (de)protonation of PBAE amines was incorporated to account for responses to variation in pH and the local electrostatic environment (i.e., salt, counterions, and polyanions), achieved via a hybrid molecular dynamics charge regulation Monte Carlo (MD/CR-MC) method (38, 39). At pH 5 and low ionic strength, the anionic mRNA was observed to readily bind to highly protonated PBAE to form compact spherical complexes, consisting of a core rich in mRNA and charge-neutralizing PBAE with the alkyl side chains preferentially distributed in the outer shell (Fig. 2A). As expected, the outermost amines (r > 10σ, where σ is the size of monomers and ions; see Methods) showed a protonation fraction α of ~85% (Fig. 2B), similar to free PBAE in solution. Deeper within the shell, 8σ < r < 10σ, where protons were less likely to penetrate and the local electric field was controlled by like-charged amines, α decreased to ~80%. In the core region (r < 8σ), the α value reverted to ~85%, despite the high density of amine groups, due to the higher mRNA concentration. The complex as a whole was overcharged by about 30% (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). This excess positive charge, in combination with the low ionic strength, contributed to the high colloidal stability of the nanoparticle. Upon changing the conditions to pH 7 and high ionic strength, we observed a strong overall decrease in α (Fig. 2B), attachment of additional PBAE to the complex (Fig. 2C), and amplified radial dependence of α throughout the complex (Fig. 2B). However, the increasing number of available amines in the complex compensated the decrease in α so that the number of charged amines in contact with the RNA only slightly decreased (Fig. 2 A and C, Insets), lowering the overcharging degree from 30 to 22% (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). This, along with the increased surface hydrophobicity from the additional PBAE, promoted nanoparticle assembly without jeopardizing structural integrity. This explains the experimental observation that a consistent mRNA encapsulation efficiency of >80% was maintained for all assembled particles (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A).

To better capture the nanoparticle assembly process in situ, we obtained cryogenic TEM images of snap-frozen particles upon solution challenge to pH 7.4 at either low (Fig. 2D, slow growth) or high (Fig. 2E, fast growth) ionic strength, before addition of DMG-PEG2000 for stabilization. Ongoing merging events between particles during assembly were readily captured and were more frequent in the group with the faster growth kinetics, consistent with the nanoparticle assembly mechanism. Given the high payload capacity of larger particles, it is worth noting that this mode of nanoparticle assembly can facilitate codelivery of multiple types of nucleic acid cargos, as demonstrated in the preparation of 250 to 900-nm particles, which showed improved coencapsulation of short Cy3-siRNA and long Cy5-mRNA as the size increased (Fig. 2 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Assembled 400- to 900-nm PBAE/mRNA Particles, but Not Smaller Nanoparticles, Enabled Efficient Transfection of Primary Murine Macrophages In Vitro.

A series of particles with Dz of 100, 250, 400, and 900 nm were prepared using the same method as in Fig. 1D to ensure that they had the same composition. The 100-nm particles carrying green fluorescent protein mRNA (mGFP) achieved over 90% transfection efficiency in HeLa cells in vitro (Fig. 3A), but were completely ineffective in transfecting primary murine bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs, CD115+ F4/80+ Ly6C–, SI Appendix, Fig. S6A and Fig. 3B). In contrast, high transfection efficiency was achieved by the 400- and 900-nm particles in the 0.04 to 1 µg/mL mRNA dose range, and the size threshold for effective transfection was between 250 and 400 nm (Fig. 3 C and D). The average mRNA expression level per cell increased with Dz (Fig. 3D). Cellular uptake measured using Cy5-mRNA as the payload (Fig. 3 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C) significantly increased with Dz from 100 to 900 nm due to the unique phagocytic activity of BMDMs. Cell viability decreased when the 900-nm particles were tested in the 0.4 to 1 µg/mL mRNA dose range (Fig. 3G), partially explaining their decreased transfection efficiency under these conditions. Under confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) with stained lysosomes, only minimal Cy5-tagged particles, either 100 or 400 nm, colocalized with lysosomes at 8 h postdosing (Fig. 3H, white arrows), confirming the high endo-lysosomal escape capability of this PBAE structure (32). In commonly referenced phagocytic cell lines, the dependency of transfection and cellular uptake efficiency on particle size was far less evident (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), which suggests specificity of the size effects to primary cells. Collectively, these results suggest that the transfection of BMDMs mediated by the assembled PBAE/mRNA particles was mostly enabled by particle size-dependent uptake, with the 400-nm particles exhibiting sufficiently high cell uptake while maintaining high cell viability.

The transfected BMDMs were also activated at a more pronounced level by the 400-nm particles than by the 100-nm particles (Fig. 3I and SI Appendix, Fig. S8) with coencapsulated ovalbumin mRNA (mOVA) or mGFP, and CpG oligonucleotide (ODN) 1826, a toll-like-receptor-9 agonist (40). Revealed through a flow cytometry-based BMDM functional phenotyping panel, upregulation of proinflammatory markers, including B7H1 (PD-L1), CD86, CD80, CD64, CD40, and CD36, was most pronounced in BMDMs transfected by the 400-nm particles co-loaded with ODN. While different degrees of activation were caused in BMDMs by treatment with mOVA or mGFP particles, presumably due to intrinsic differences in the immunogenicity of each mRNA construct and each expressed transgene protein (41), these results highlighted that successful transfection mediated by the assembled particles, especially when combined with an adjuvant, can provide immunological stimulation to the target cells.

Biocompatibility, Biodistribution, and Delivery Profiles of the Assembled PBAE/mRNA Particles in Mice Following Intravenous Administration.

The same series of 100-, 400-, and 900-nm particles was i.v. administered to CD-1 mice at a single dose equivalent to 0.5 mg/kg of mRNA or 3 doses (4 d apart) equivalent to 0.25 mg/kg of mRNA per dose, using PBS as a control. Following assessments of tissue and blood samples collected on day 3 and 14 after injection, no test particle-related pathological indicators were observed. All three particles showed similar findings (see SI Appendix, Fig. S9 for representative images from a mouse injected by 400-nm particles compared to control). Previous reports indicated that larger submicron (>200 nm) particles may have a distribution biased to high-perfusion areas, such as lungs (42) and kidney glomeruli (43). We did not observe deposition in such tissues in mice that received either one or three doses of the assembled particles (Fig. 4A). On day 3 or 14 postinjection, blood chemistry (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) and complete blood count (CBC, SI Appendix, Fig. S11) panels did not reveal any clinically concerning indicators. Intravenously injected 400-nm particles were quickly (<15 min) cleared from circulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B) and were primarily deposited in the liver and spleen (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S12C).

Following i.v. injection of the 100- or 400-nm particles carrying Cy5-mRNA in C57BL/6J mice, they were rapidly taken up by circulating Ly6Chigh inflammatory monocytes, but the 400-nm particles resulted in a 10-fold higher uptake than the 100-nm particles (Fig. 4 C and D). No significant uptake of these particles by other circulating immune cells was detected within 1 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 A and B). After 1 h, the inflammatory monocytes presumably carrying phagocytosed particles had emigrated from circulation (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S13C). Between 1 and 2 h, neutrophils (Fig. 4F) and Ly6ClowCD11b+ patrolling monocytes (Fig. 4G) with a more definitive phenotype of CX3CR1+ (Fig. 4H) were found to extravasate into the liver. Simultaneously, a high influx of Ly6Chigh inflammatory monocytes was detected in the liver (Fig. 4G). Because Ly6Chigh monocytes had been depleted from circulation earlier (Fig. 4E), this influx may have resulted from an emergency release of Ly6Chigh monocytes by the bone marrow (6), which then migrated to the liver, where the PBAE/mRNA particles were deposited, though this hypothesis needs further verification. Such monocyte responses to the particles resemble monocyte responses to microbial infection (7). Both the 100-nm and 400-nm particles triggered similar levels of neutrophil and monocyte deposition in the liver (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A), but not to the spleen (SI Appendix, Fig. S14B). When cellular uptake was assessed at 1 h postinjection in the liver (Fig. 4 I and J and SI Appendix, Fig. S15A) and spleen (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 B and C), myeloid cells were found to have preferentially taken up PBAE/mRNA particles compared to other cell types. The 400-nm particles yielded on average 2.9-fold higher level of uptake in all cell types than 100-nm particles (Fig. 4J), consistent with cellular uptake results with BMDMs in vitro.

Gene Expression Profiles Mediated by Assembled PBAE/mRNA Particles Following i.v. Administration in Mice.

To analyze the gene expression profile, the assembled particles carrying mRNA encoding Cre recombinase (mCre) were i.v. injected in Ai-9 mice (Methods) at an mRNA dose of 0.5 mg/kg, and the liver and spleen cells were analyzed by flow cytometry at 24 and 72 h postinjection. At 24 h, the total number of neutrophils returned to its normal level (Fig. 4F), whereas the increase in the presence of monocytes persisted until 72 h post injection (Fig. 4G). At 72 h, a higher abundance of mature macrophages (F4/80+CD11b+) was detected in the liver and spleen compared to the untreated control mouse (Fig. 4K). Because monocytes were found to migrate to the liver at earlier time points (Fig. 4G), while no local hepatic macrophages (Kupffer cells) (44) or splenic macrophages (45, 46) possess this F4/80+CD11b+ phenotype, we postulate that a fraction of the monocytes had differentiated to macrophages following deposition and uptake of the assembled PBAE/mRNA particles. Around half of these newly populated macrophages had negative Ly6C expression, but the other half still carried an intermediate level of Ly6C marker (Fig. 4L), which is a hallmark for the transition stage of monocyte differentiation (47). While Ly6Chigh monocytes also carry F4/80 expression, we found in an independent experiment that the induced F4/80+ macrophages at 72 h were distinguishable from the homed monocytes at 24 h post injection by a lower CD11b level, besides negative or lower Ly6C level (see detailed backgating analysis in SI Appendix, Fig. S16A), which further supported our hypothesis of monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation. Newly populated CD11b+CD11c+ cells, presumably Mo-DCs, were also observed in the liver (Fig. 4M), but not in the spleen (SI Appendix, Fig. S16B). We deduced that the differentiation of monocytes into F4/80+ or CD11c+ cells was initiated by both uptake of and stimulation by the assembled PBAE/mRNA particles, as well as by their extravasation into peripheral tissues that provided them physical (48, 49) and cytokine (50) cues, although elucidation of the detailed mechanism would need further examination in future studies.

Interestingly, assessment of transfection efficiency in the Ai-9 model (SI Appendix, Fig. S17A) showed that particle uptake by monocytes and their subsequent differentiation was coupled with successful transfection of the resultant macrophages. Among all tested particles, the 400-nm particles yielded the highest tdTomato expression level in induced monocytes at 24 h, and in induced F4/80+ macrophages and induced Mo-DCs at 72 h (Fig. 5A). The induced macrophages were the population with the highest transfection efficiency in both the liver and the spleen (Fig. 5 B and C), constituting 40.4% of all transfected immune cells in the liver (Fig. 5D). Of note, 69.6% of the transfected immune cells in the liver and 39.5% of the transfected immune cells in the spleen were of myeloid origin (Fig. 5 D and E). The 400-nm particles resulted in a significantly higher ratio of transfected immune cells vs. nonimmune cells in the liver than the 100-nm nanoparticles (1.53 vs. 0.11, SI Appendix, Fig. S17B), whereas nearly all transfected cells in the spleen were immune cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S17C).

When BALB/cJ mice were i.v. injected with a series of assembled particles carrying luciferase mRNA (mLuc), the transgene expression characterized in vivo and ex vivo increased with Dz (Fig. 5 F and G). Notably, the 400-nm particles resulted in transgene expression predominantly in the liver, 3.7-fold of that in the spleen and 39.8-fold of that in the lungs. The luciferase level mediated by the 400-nm particles was not only 24.4-fold higher than that of the 100-nm particles in the liver (Fig. 5G) but also more durable upon a single injection or multiple injections (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). The difference in size-dependent transfection between particles loaded with mCre and those loaded with mLuc was likely due to the difference in the reporter systems, as the mCre-induced tdTomato+ expression captures the number of transfected cells, whereas the luciferase assay measures the transient luciferase concentration among all transfected cells. Collectively, these results highlighted that 400-nm particles displayed the greatest capability to enter monocytes, subsequently mediate their differentiation into macrophages and Mo-DCs, and functionally deliver the mRNA cargo that resulted in transgene expression in these cells. By correlating in vitro (Fig. 3E) and in vivo (Fig. 4J) cellular uptake experiments, we concluded that the superior transfection activity and cell programming function of the 400-nm particles over the 100-nm nanoparticles was primarily due to enhanced cellular uptake.

Monocyte-Targeted Assembled PBAE/mRNA Particles Mediate a Significant Antitumor Response in the B16-OVA Melanoma Mouse Model.

With the aim of maximizing transfection efficiency and minimizing the risk of cytotoxicity, we chose to test the assembled 400-nm particles in a proof-of-concept therapeutic vaccine model for treating subcutaneously (s.c.) implanted ovalbumin (OVA)–expressing melanoma (B16-OVA), against the 100-nm nanoparticles. To boost the potential vaccine efficacy while considering the monocyte turnover time of at least 3 d following a single injection (Fig. 4G), three consecutive doses at 0.25 mg mRNA/kg separated by 4 d were given i.v. starting at 8 d following implantation, and tumor growth was tracked (Fig. 6A). Statistically significant extension of survival (Fig. 6B) and delay of tumor growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S19) were observed in the group that received the 400-nm particles loaded with mOVA or ODN as the sole functional payload but not in the group treated with the 100-nm particles. T cell responses were assessed by flow cytometry, and the 400-nm particles were found to induce a significantly higher number of OVA-specific T cells in the tumor (Fig. 6C). We next co-loaded ODN as the adjuvant with mRNA at half the mRNA dose and first confirmed that ODN did not influence mRNA expression or cause acute toxicity following i.v. injection of mLuc-loaded particles (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). With mOVA plus ODN as payloads, both the 100 and 400-nm particles mediated potent antitumor effects (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S21). While the tumor inhibition rates appeared to be similar, the 400-nm particles induced significantly more OVA-specific CD8+ T cells in both the tumor and the tumor-draining lymph node (dLN) than the 100-nm nanoparticles (Fig. 6C), although comparable numbers of OVA-specific CD8+ T cells were found in the blood, spleen, and a distal lymph node (SI Appendix, Fig. S22). It is worth noting that the 400-nm particles carrying luciferase mRNA (mCtrl) and ODN significantly extended the median survival from 20 d in the control group to 26 d. However, the number of OVA-specific CD8+ T cells induced by these particles was barely detectable (Fig. 6C). In addition, a relatively low degree of CD8+ T cell infiltration was observed in the tumor via immunohistochemistry (SI Appendix, Fig. S23). These collectively indicate that monocytes and macrophages may play a role directly as effector cells in mediating tumor killing as a result of adjuvant stimulation (51), in addition to potentiating T cell-mediated antitumor response.

Monocytes home to the adjacent lymph node in response to infection in order to mediate immune response through antigen presentation (12). We next examined whether the transfected monocytes can home to the dLN. A significantly higher fraction of F4/80–CD11b+CD11c+ DCs was detected in the dLN of the mice treated with the 400-nm particles carrying mOVA and ODN, compared to the untreated mice (Fig. 6 D and E). These cells feature intermediate or low levels of MHC-II in comparison to the same subpopulation of DCs in untreated mice. The majority (75.2%) of the MHC-IIint subpopulation (Fig. 6F, Population 1) was found positive for expression of CD209a (DC-SIGN), indicating their monocyte origin (8), while 18.3% were found to be positive for TNF-α as examined by intracellular staining, indicating their inflammatory nature (9). From the phenotypic visualization (Fig. 6D) and quantification (Fig. 6G and SI Appendix, Fig. S24) of MHC-II and Ly6C levels, we observed a pattern of upregulation of MHC-II (from Population 5 to 4 to 3) and a pattern of downregulation of Ly6C (from Population 6 to 7 to 8) in the group treated by 400-nm particles carrying mOVA and ODN, highly indicative of a differentiation process from monocytes into Mo-DCs (9, 12). These data collectively suggest that codelivery of mOVA and ODN by 400-nm particles effectively generates inflammatory Mo-DCs in the dLN.

Characterization of both the lymphatic and myeloid responses induced by treatment groups in the tumor, blood, spleen, dLN, and distal LN showed that 400-nm particles triggered a more comprehensive immune response (Fig. 6H and SI Appendix, Table S1). Examples of relevant metrics included a decrease in abundance of tumor-associated macrophages (lane 61) (52) and an increase in MHC-II expression on tumor cells (lane 60); the latter was driven by increased IFN-γ signaling and correlated with positive responses to immunotherapy (53). The ability of this treatment to affect tumors at other sites was demonstrated in a B16-OVA lung colonization model, a frequently used model of metastasis (54, 55), in which assembled particles, both 100 nm and 400 nm in size, carrying mOVA and ODN were found to reduce growth of tumor cells in the lungs with no additional benefits observed from anti-PD1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S25). The lack of difference between the two sizes of the assembled particles in this model, despite drastic transfection efficiency improvement by 400 nm over 100 nm (Fig. 5) or observed antitumor efficacy exclusively for 400-nm particles with mOVA or ODN as the sole functional payload (Fig. 6B), may be attributed to the strong adjuvant effect from ODN, which can initiate potent immune responses even with a lower level of antigen expression in these mice.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive investigation of an unusual submicron vehicle size range (200 to 1,000 nm) for in vivo mRNA delivery vehicles. Combining experimental and simulation methods, we design a supramolecular assembly strategy that produces PBAE/mRNA particles at any target size from 100 to 1,000 nm in a controlled, reproducible, and scalable manner and affords colloidal stability and structural integrity of these particles to maintain protection of their functional payloads in the complexed form. Even though a specific PBAE carrier was used, this strategy may be generalizable to other nanoparticle delivery systems due to the ubiquitous nature of the control mechanism. While the literature has generally described nanoparticles smaller than 200 nm for systemic gene delivery, the larger assembled particles reported here are not only effective in transfecting monocytes and macrophages, but also safe for systemic delivery due to the biodegradability of PBAE, and presumably rapid sequestration of the assembled 400-nm particles by monocytes, which limits the circulation time of the particles.

While most prior studies demonstrate undesired particle clearance due to the phagocytic activity of monocytes and macrophages, our study showcases a unique strategy of utilizing this feature for targeted mRNA delivery of larger submicron PBAE/mRNA particles to generate transfected macrophages and inflammatory Mo-DCs in vivo (Fig. 6 I and J). This particle-size-based strategy may be applicable to other types of APCs, as it is interesting to note that a lipid nanoparticle formulation reported by Okuda et al. (56) and a lipoplex cancer vaccine developed by BioNTech (57, 58), both of which efficiently deliver antigen mRNA to splenic DCs, possess average sizes of ca. 360 and 400 nm, respectively. Through the proof-of-concept OVA-expressing melanoma model, we highlight the potential of circulating monocytes as a vaccine target, as suggested previously (8). Our findings also demonstrate that transfected monocytes/macrophages can be programmed for activation, particularly with codelivered ODN adjuvant. It is therefore worthwhile to investigate their potential to become effector cells, such as CAR macrophages, through mRNA payload design. Given the safety and systemic efficacy revealed in this study, this assembled particle platform will find further application in cancer immunotherapy and, more broadly, in other immunotherapy strategies.

Methods

We refer the readers to SI Appendix for full, detailed description of all the methods used in this study, including catalog numbers of chemicals and reagents. The experimental procedures of key experiments are described below:

Synthesis of PBAE.

PBAE was synthesized following previously reported methods (32). The monomers bisphenol A glycerolate (1 glycerol/phenol) diacrylate, 4-(2-aminoethyl)morpholine and 1-dodecylamine were mixed at a molar ratio of 1.15:0.5:0.5 in anhydrous dimethylformamide to be polymerized at 85 °C for 48 h. Diethylenetriamine in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran was added for end capping at room temperature for 2 h. The PBAE was precipitated in anhydrous diethyl ether, washed, and dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide before use.

Supramolecular Assembly of PBAE/mRNA Nanoparticles into Larger Submicron Particles.

The mRNA was dissolved in 25 mM magnesium acetate (MgAc2) buffer at pH 5.0. The PBAE stock solution was diluted by a mixture of 90 v/v% EtOH and 10 v/v% of the same MgAc2 buffer to protonate amine groups. The PBAE-to-mRNA mass ratio was maintained at 30:1 throughout this study. The mRNA solution and the PBAE solution were mixed at a volumetric ratio of 3:1, forming stable nanoparticles around 100 nm, which were dialyzed against 1 mM MgAc2 buffer at pH 5.0. To induce supramolecular assembly, the nanoparticle suspension was mixed with an equal volume of solution containing buffering salt (Na2HPO4 and NaH2PO4) to adjust the pH to 7.4 and nonbuffering salt (NaCl and KCl) to increase ionic strength. When the particles reached the desired size, DMG-PEG2000 (in EtOH) was added at 1 to 20% of the mass of PBAE to stabilize the particles. For in vivo experiments, a second dialysis was applied against 10 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7.4 with 9 w/v% trehalose as a cryoprotectant.

Particle Characterization.

The assembled PBAE/mRNA particles were subjected to the following characterizations: dynamic light scattering (DLS, ZS-90, Malvern) with a 90° detection angle to measure particle size; TEM with negative staining by 2 w/v% uranyl acetate as well as Cryogenic TEM (cryo-TEM) analysis of vitrified samples during assembly using an F200C Talos TEM scope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 200 kV to assess morphology; zeta potential by phase analysis light scattering (PALS) using the same DLS instrument; encapsulation efficiency using a modified Quant-it™ RiboGreen (Thermo Fisher Scientific) protocol; coencapsulation efficiency of Cy5-mRNA and Cy3-siRNA and particle integrity after particle assembly and dosage to HeLa or BMDMs using CLSM; mRNA loading level by an in-house built cylindrical illumination confocal spectroscopy (CICS) platform. Detailed sample preparation and data analysis procedures for all these characterizations are fully described in SI Appendix.

Hybrid MD/CR-MC Simulation of Assembly of PBAE/mRNA Particles.

All simulations were carried out with the LAMMPS MD package (59). Both mRNA and PBAE were represented by bead–spring models. All beads had a diameter σ = lB, where lB = 0.71 nm is the Bjerrum length in water. The length σ was used as the unit length throughout the data presentation. The mRNA was a flexible N = 200 chain with each bead carrying a permanent charge of –1. Each PBAE polymer was represented as an N = 50 chain consisting of a linear backbone of neutral beads and protonatable amine beads and seven short hydrophobic side chains (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). We applied a recently developed MD/CR-MC method to incorporate the PBAE amine protonation equilibrium (31, 38). The full simulation methods are described in SI Appendix.

In Vitro Transfection and Activation of BMDMs.

The BMDMs were harvested from the bone marrow of C57BL/6 mice (Jackson Laboratory) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 20 ng/mL macrophage-colony stimulatory factor (M-CSF) for 6 d before use. For transfection studies, BMDMs were seeded 24 h prior to transfection in 24-well ultralow attachment cell culture plates at 400,000 cells/well in 500 μL. PBAE/mRNA particles were pipetted into prewarmed aliquots of the full culture medium, briefly vortexed, and the dose-containing medium was added into the cell culture and mixed by vigorous pipetting. After 4 h of incubation, the particles were removed by pelleting the cells by centrifugation at 300 ×g for 5 min and replacing the supernatant with fresh medium. The cells were then subjected to additional incubation, flow cytometry, and CLSM analysis (SI Appendix).

Animal Studies.

All the animal procedures described in this study were approved by the Johns Hopkins University Animal Care and Use Committee (JHU ACUC, protocol #MO20E63 and #MO22E239 to Hai-Quan Mao, and #MO21M384 to Stephany Y. Tzeng and Jordan. J. Green). The following animal studies were performed: Biocompatibility of assembled particles following intravenous injection. CD-1 IGS mice (Charles River Laboratories) were i.v. injected via the retro-orbital sinus with 0.5 mg mRNA/kg (equivalent to 15 mg PBAE/kg) in a single injection, or with 0.25 mg mRNA/kg (i.e., 7.5 mg PBAE/kg) in each of three consecutive injections separated by 96 h. In vivo biodistribution of assembled particles. C57BL/6 mice were i.v. injected 100 μL of particles formulated with Cy5-labeled mRNA (ApexBio Technology) and Cy7-labeled plasmid DNA (Cy7-pDNA) at a weight ratio of 4:1, and with a dose of 0.25 mg mRNA/kg. Assessment of in vivo cellular uptake by, and responses from immune cells within 2 h in C57BL/6 mice. C57BL/6 mice were i.v. injected 100 μL of particles formulated with Cy5-mRNA (ApexBio Technology). Assessment of transfected cell types and responses from immune cells at 24 and 72 h in Ai-9 mice. Ai-9 mice (in-house bred, originally obtained from Jackson Laboratory as B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm9(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J) were i.v. injected 200 μL of particles formulated by mRNA encoding Cre-recombinase (mCre, TriLink BioTechnologies). Proof-of-concept efficacy of PBAE/mRNA particles in B16-OVA tumor model. A s.c. B16-F10-OVA tumor model was established in 8-wk C57BL/6 J mice by s.c. injection of 100 μL of serum-free RPMI medium containing 2 × 105 B16-F10-OVA cells into the left flank. On day 7 following inoculation, the tumor size was assessed, and mice were randomly assigned to different treatment groups. On day 8, mice were i.v. injected with 100 μL of particles with different payloads. For the survival study, all treatment groups received three consecutive doses, separated by 96 h, and the tumor growth was measured every 2 d in a blinded manner. Mice were euthanized according to JHU ACUC guidelines if tumor area reached 200 mm2, or severe sign of morbidity was observed. For the phenotyping study, all treatment groups received 2 doses. We refer readers to SI Appendix for detailed procedures for all these animal studies, including tissue harvest time points and methods, grouping, and flow cytometry panels.
