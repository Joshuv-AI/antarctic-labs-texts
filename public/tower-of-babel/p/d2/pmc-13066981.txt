UV1 plus pembrolizumab in recurrent and metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma: safe, immunogenic, but lacking efficacy
Authors: Guillermo Flores, Marisa R Buchakjian, Molly Heft Neal, J Chad Brenner
Published: 2026

Few therapies have redefined the therapeutic armamentarium of cancer in modern times as much as immunotherapy. For patients with incurable recurrent or metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (R/M HNSCC), checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab are now considered standard of care. Published in 2019, KEYNOTE-048 was a randomized, phase 3 study of patients with untreated R/M HNSCC which showed that only 6–20% of patients have an objective response to pembrolizumab depending on programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression level (1). Furthermore, the KEYNOTE-040 study showed that while pembrolizumab was superior to investigator’s choice standard-of-care chemotherapy (methotrexate, docetaxel, or cetuximab) in patients with cancers that progressed while on platinum chemotherapy, the median overall survival (OS) increased by less than 2 months (2). Clearly, R/M HNSCC is a cancer in desperate need of new modalities and strategies due to both its high prevalence and poor prognosis. An appealing strategy is to identify synergistic adjuncts to immunotherapy that might amplify immune activation and promote significant and durable responses. One such strategy is therapeutic vaccination.

UV1 is a peptide vaccine that includes three epitopes for maximal immune reactivity of human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT), an enzyme upregulated in approximately 90% of cancers, including the majority of HNSCC (3). Administered with granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF), UV1 is designed to stimulate a sustained CD4+ Th1 T-cell response (4) specific against cancer cells as most normal cells do not produce hTERT. Prior early-phase studies in melanoma (5), non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (4), and prostate cancer (6) showed persistent vaccine-induced immune responses. Furthermore, follow-up on trials of melanoma, NSCLC, and prostate cancer showed significant signs of clinical benefit such as improved OS that correlated with the level of immune response (7). These encouraging data provided the rationale for the FOCUS trial, the first test of UV1 peptide vaccine as an adjunct to pembrolizumab in R/M HNSCC. A summary of UV1 trials is seen in Figure 1.

The FOCUS trial was a randomized, multicenter phase 2 study conducted across 10 sites in Germany (8). Patients with PD-L1+ R/M HNSCC and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2 were randomized to receive pembrolizumab alone or pembrolizumab plus UV1 (400 µg). The primary endpoint was 6-month progression-free survival (PFS) and a total of 75 patients were recruited with 67 reaching the endpoint. Secondary endpoints included objective response rate (ORR), OS, immune responses, and safety.

In R/M HNSCC, the addition of UV1 was well tolerated. Adverse event rates were comparable between arms, though one case of severe anaphylaxis occurred in the UV1 group. A positive immune response was defined as an increase in interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion by peripheral blood mononuclear cells after UV1 peptide antigen stimulation compared to baseline as determined by Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSpot (ELISPOT). Unfortunately, despite generating strong hTERT-specific T cell responses, the addition of UV1 to pembrolizumab did not improve outcomes. When comparing the pembrolizumab arm to the pembrolizumab + UV1 arm, ORR (defined as complete remission + partial remission according to iRECIST criteria) was 45% versus 22%, median PFS was 3.9 versus 3.3 months, and median OS was 13.1 versus 12.6 months, though none of these metrics met statistical significance. The numerical difference in ORR likely reflects baseline patient heterogeneity and limited statistical power rather than a deleterious effect of UV1, particularly in a disease where pembrolizumab monotherapy achieves objective responses in only a small subset of patients with R/M HNSCC (1). Biologically, in this low base response setting, the absence of a strong additive effect from UV1, even if able to elicit a robust immune response, would magnify the influence of any stochastic inter-patient variability. In short, UV1 induced immune reactivity in patients receiving UV1 and pembrolizumab, but this response failed to translate into measurable clinical benefit in patients with R/M HNSCC.

Several explanations may account for the lack of benefit for R/M HNSCC observed in the FOCUS trial. One possibility is the dosing of the UV1 peptide vaccine. In NSCLC, there was observed to be a dose-dependent association with immune response and OS with 700 µg providing the most benefit (4). The FOCUS trial used 400 µg, raising the possibility that this dose might have the ability to generate an objective hTERT-specific T cell response but not to show clinical efficacy. Another possible explanation is that the study population of R/M HNSCC is inherently heterogeneous, and different cancer subsites and inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity might make large-scale R/M HNSCC studies difficult to interpret. Approximately one-third of participants had oropharyngeal cancer, which (especially when human papilloma virus-mediated) can behave biologically very different from oral cavity, laryngeal, or hypopharyngeal disease, potentially obscuring subgroup-specific benefit (9). Additionally, although hTERT is broadly expressed in HNSCC, the immune microenvironment in this cancer may be less permissive to vaccine-induced responses compared to other cancers where UV1 has been trialed in conjunction with immunotherapy like melanoma or NSCLC (10). In the absence of empiric validation in the tumor microenvironment, it is unclear that success in the periphery translates to success in the tumor. Moreover, while PD-L1 positivity was used to select patients, PD-L1 alone provides only a limited snapshot of tumor immune biology; tumors with similar PD-L1 levels can differ markedly in T-cell infiltration, antigen-presentation capacity, stromal exclusion, and other barriers to effective checkpoint-mediated immunity. It is plausible that only certain subsets of tumors, particularly immune “hot” tumors, can mount meaningful responses to UV1, whereas immune “cold” tumors are resistant (11-13). Without stratification by immune subtype, potential clinical benefit could have been obscured.

Finally, the FOCUS trial used IFN-γ as the sole biomarker of vaccine-induced CD4 T-cell response which may not be the most robust measure of immune recruitment in R/M HNSCC. Furthermore, less than a third of patients in either arm were selected to quantify IFN-γ and determine immune response, possibly further limiting generalizability. Complementary biomarkers such as CD8+ T-cell tumor infiltration, spatial positioning of effector T cells in the tumor microenvironment, or expression of immune exhaustion markers [e.g., programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)] could provide additional insight into whether vaccine-induced immunity translates into a biologically meaningful response. The relatively small sample size of 75 evaluable patients further increases the risk that true differences were hidden by variability. The inclusion of ECOG 2 patients, while commendable for reflecting real-world practice in R/M HNSCC where many patients are functionally limited, may have diluted efficacy signals, as frailer patients are often less capable of sustaining durable immune responses even when vaccines successfully prime T-cells. This stands in contrast to immunotherapy administered in peri-operative settings such as in the recently published KEYNOTE-689 trial, where immunotherapy was delivered initially in the neoadjuvant setting and within a more controlled curative-intent clinical context. Such earlier intervention may reduce cumulative immune exhaustion, promote more robust antigen presentation, and allow immunotherapy to more effectively amplify antitumor immunity, potentially contributing to the stronger clinical signals observed (14).

The equivocal results of the FOCUS trial stand in contrast to other UV1 studies. In melanoma, UV1 combined with ipilimumab led to rapid expansion of vaccine-specific T cells and durable clinical benefit (5). In mesothelioma, the NIPU trial did not meet its primary endpoint but showed a potential OS signal with UV1 plus nivolumab and ipilimumab (15). In NSCLC and prostate cancer, early-phase studies demonstrated robust immune activation and clinical benefit with longer follow-up (4,6). These mixed outcomes underscore that tumor context, immune microenvironment, and combination immunotherapy partner likely matter greatly for vaccine efficacy. For all the rapid advancements, there is still no one-size-fits-all immunotherapy for cancer.

In conclusion, the FOCUS trial demonstrates that UV1, at the dose tested, does not improve outcomes when added to pembrolizumab in R/M HNSCC. While safe and immunogenic in the periphery, the trial design and the lack of substantial biomarker evaluation may have concealed potential clinical benefit for sub-groups in the trial. Future research should continue to refine vaccine-based strategies but should also incorporate more comprehensive translational studies to support evaluation of outcomes. Moving forward, the immune context of R/M HNSCC may warrant consideration when designing future combination immunotherapy trials.
