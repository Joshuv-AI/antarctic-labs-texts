The network pharmacology prediction and experiment validation of Astragalus membranaceus for alleviating silicosis fibrosis via decreasing MMP9 and EGFR expression
Authors: Anning Yang, Xiaoyu Luo, Yi Guo, Huning Zhang, Wenyue Zhang, Sirong Chang, Shengpeng Wen, Wenyan Yang, Yue Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the molecular mechanism of Astragalus membranaceus (AM) in alleviating silicosis fibrosis using bioinformatics and in vitro experiments. Active ingredients of AM and their corresponding targets were retrieved from the TCMSP and SwissTargetPrediction databases. These ingredients were screened based on oral bioavailability (OB ≥ 30%), drug-likeness (DL ≥ 0.18), and Lipinski’s Rule of Five, which serves as a standard for identifying drug-like molecules with favorable pharmacokinetic properties in network pharmacology. Silicosis-related targets were collected from DisGENET, GeneCards, and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD). Common targets were identified for protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses. Core ingredients and targets were selected through topological analysis in Cytoscape and subsequently validated using molecular docking (AutoDock), immunofluorescence and RT-qPCR assays. Eight main active ingredients of AM were identified, along with 236 AM targets and 7,706 silicosis targets, resulting in 113 shared targets. Further screening revealed three key ingredients: Quercetin, isorhamnetin, and kaempferol, as well as seven core targets: AKT1, CASP3, PTGS2, MMP9, ESR1, JUN and EGFR. The primary pathways involved included PI3K-AKT, IL-17, and MAPK. Molecular docking analyses indicated that MMP9 and EGFR exhibited higher binding energies with Quercetin. In vitro immunofluorescence results demonstrated that Quercetin downregulated the expression of MMP9, EGFR, and α-SMA in silicotic fibroblasts. Additionally, qRT-PCR assays confirmed that the Quercetin alleviated SiO2-induced fibrotic process by suppressing PI3K-AKT pathway in the anti-fibrotic process. Quercetin, the key active ingredient of AM, exerts its anti-silicosis effect by inhibiting the expression of EGFR, MMP9 and the PI3K/Akt pathway.Keywords: Astragalus membranaceus, Silicosis, Quercetin, Network pharmacology, Molecular dockingSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Diseases, Health occupations, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Silicosis is a progressive occupational pneumoconiosis resulting from chronic inhalation of crystalline silica dust. It is globally recognized as one of the most severe subtypes of pulmonary fibrosis, characterized by persistent inflammation, the formation of silicosis nodules, and irreversibly progressive fibrosis1,2. Between 1990 and 2019, incident cases (new diagnoses) of silicosis increased by 64.6%, rising from 84,821 to 138,965. During the same period, prevalent cases (existing disease burden) grew even more significantly, escalating from 1,383,913 to 2,648,973, which represents a 91.4% increase in the total number of affected individuals3. Currently, no effective treatment strategies exist for silicosis4. Clinical interventions for silicosis-induced pulmonary fibrosis remain limited. Although lung transplantation can significantly improve survival, donor scarcity and prohibitive costs severely restrict patient access5. Consequently, identifying more effective therapeutic strategies for silicosis is an urgent clinical priority.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has emerged as a recognized complementary approach for managing silicosis6. Astragalus membranaceus (AM), first documented in Shennong’s Classic of Materia Medica over two millennia ago, exhibits established pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiviral activities7. As a qi-tonifying agent, it demonstrates significant immunomodulatory potential8,9. Thus, AM shows considerable promise as a therapeutic agent for fibrotic disorders. However, the precise mechanism by which it alleviates silicosis remains incompletely characterized.

Network pharmacology (NP) provides a methodological framework for the systems-level elucidation and visualization of the complex interactions between TCM constituents and disease pathogenesis10. As a core computational technique, molecular docking facilitates therapeutic discovery by predicting ligand-target binding conformations and interaction affinities11,12.

This study integrates network pharmacology with molecular docking to systematically elucidate the mechanisms by which AM attenuates silicosis fibrosis. Core targets identified through computational methods underwent experimental validation via in vitro assays, confirming their functional roles in AM’s anti-fibrotic effects. The integrated approach is illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, a systematic screening process identifies the bioactive ingredients of AM. This is followed by the detection of potential targets that align with the fibrotic mechanisms underlying silicosis. Subsequently, protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks are constructed using the STRING database. Based on centrality metrics derived from topological analysis, core targets are selected. With functional enrichment analysis, we then explore Gene Ontology terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Finally, computational predictions are validated through molecular docking simulations and in vitro cellular assays.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The human embryonic lung fibroblast cell line MRC-5 was obtained from Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). MRC-5 cells are maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (WHB825E191, Pricella Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Pricella Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin mixture (100X, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Beijing Biocode Co., Ltd. (L205-500, Beijing, China). Silicon dioxide (SiO2), Quercetin, and TGF-β1 were acquired from Solarbio (Beijing, China). MMP9 (10375-2-AP, Proteintech Group Inc., Wuhan, China), EGFR (66455-1-Ig, Proteintech Group Inc., Wuhan, China), and α-SMA (AF1032, Affinity Biosciences, Jiangsu, China) were utilized for immunofluorescence assay. The PI3K agonist 740 Y-P was purchased from MedChemExpress (HY-101084, Shanghai, China). A 4% paraformaldehyde solution was obtained from Guangzhou Hucheng Technology (HC0892, Guangzhou, China). DAPI was sourced from Solarbio (C0065, Beijing, China).

Screening of effective ingredients and prediction of therapeutic targets of AM

The TCMSP database (https://old.tcmsp.com/tcmsp.php) was employed to screen key bioactive ingredients. Potential therapeutic targets for these identified ingredients are retrieved from the same database through batch target prediction analysis. The SMILE name of the primary ingredient is then obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and imported into the SWISS Target Prediction database (http://SwissTargetPrediction.ch) to retrieve the gene names associated with the individual primary ingredient. The collected data are integrated from both databases to identify relevant therapeutic targets.

Prediction of silicosis fibrosis targets

We searched for and collected silicosis-related target genes from the DisGENET database (https://www.disgenet.org/), the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), and the CTD database (https://ctdbase.org). A higher Score value indicates a stronger association between a target and the disease. Initially, we sourced 15,256 disease-related target genes from the CTD database. To prioritize biologically relevant interactions, we classified targets exceeding the median Score threshold as potential disease targets. We subsequently eliminated duplicates to derive the final set of disease-associated targets.

Screening of anti-silicosis targets of AM

The Venny 2.1.0 online drawing tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) was utilized to elucidate the molecular interaction targets between AM and silicosis, resulting in the creation of a Venn diagram.

Protein‒protein interaction network analysis

The STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was utilized to identify potential target genes of AM for silicosis treatment. Data were saved in Protein Data Bank (PDB) format with a confidence level ≥ 0.4, and free points were concealed. The initial data were downloaded in TSV format, then imported into Cytoscape 3.10.2 (https://cytoscape.org/) for calculating network topology parameters. Network module analysis was conducted using the CytoNSA plug-in within Cytoscape 3.10.2 software. The top seven proteins were selected based on their degree value to construct the network module.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses

The core molecular targets were uploaded to the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) for Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, with the species set to 'Homo sapiens.' The KEGG pathway analysis revealed the distribution of targets across various pathways, while the GO function analysis identified the primary biological processes, including cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), and biological processes (BP)13,14. For visualization analysis, the top 20 biological functions with the smallest p-values and the top 14 signaling pathways most strongly associated with silicosis were selected using the Microbiota Informatics Platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Molecular docking

AutoDockTools 1.5.7 graphical visualization software (http://autodock.scripps.edu/) evaluated the binding energy and interaction mode between the candidate drug AM and its target. First, the key active ingredients of AM are screened based on their degree values, and the Mol2 file format for each key active ingredient is obtained from the PubChem database. After exporting the SDF file format, OpenBabel 3.1.1 software (https://openbabel.org/) was utilized for 3D format conversion. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) file format was saved as a ligand file, and the corresponding protein structure PDB file is downloaded from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org). After the receptor and ligand files are prepared, hydro-dehydration was performed, and the files are saved in PDBQT format. The grid box module of AutoDockTools was employed to set the docking box parameters for the receptor and ligand, which are then exported as a .gpf file. Autogrid 4 was run to set docking parameters and calculation methods, followed by running AutoDock 4 to view the results. The conformation with the lowest binding energy was saved, and the results were visualized using PyMol 2.4.0 software (https://pymol.org/2/).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability in MRC-5 cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay to identify the optimal concentrations of Quercetin for interventions. MRC-5 cells (8 × 103 cells/well) were plated in 96-well plates with six wells per treatment group and allowed to attach for 24 h. Subsequently, Quercetin was added at concentrations of 0, 20, 22.5, 25, 27.5, and 30 μg/mL for a 24-h treatment. After treatment, each received 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent and 90 μL of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and then underwent 2 to 4 h of incubation. Cell viability was determined by measuring the optical density (OD) at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Silica was used to stimulate MRC-5 cells to establish an in vitro model15,16. Medical Research Council cell strain 5 (MRC-5) was used throughout the in vitro experiments. Five experimental groups were designed: 1. Negative control: Cells received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) equivalent to the SiO2 suspension volume for 24 h; 2. Model group: Cells were treated with 25 μg/mL SiO2 for 24 h to induce fibrosis; 3. Treatment group: Cells were exposed to 25 μg/mL SiO2 for 24 h, followed by 20 μg/mL Quercetin (dissolved in 0.1% DMSO) for an additional 24 h; 4. Positive control: Treated with 5 ng/mL recombinant human TGF-β1 (in PBS with 0.1% BSA) for 24 h , as TGF-β1 is a well-characterized inducer of fibrotic markers in lung fibroblasts17,18; 5. Solvent control: Cells were treated with 0.1% DMSO in PBS to eliminate potential vehicle effects. Tests confirmed that 0.1% DMSO did not significantly affect cell viability. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked with 5% BSA. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies (anti-α-SMA, anti-MMP9, and anti-EGFR), followed by a 1-h incubation at room temperature with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:300 dilution). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT qPCR)

The samples were lysed in RZ lysis buffer. Total RNA was extracted from the MRC-5 cells according to the protocol provided with the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized through reverse transcription using PrimeScript RT Premix (TaKaRa, Japan). Subsequently, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was conducted using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa, Japan). The specific primer sequences utilized are listed in Table 1. GAPDH was obtained from Sangon Biotech (B661104, Shanghai, China). The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔct method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses utilized GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com), which recorded exact p-values. Inter-group comparisons employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from a minimum of three independent experiments. A p-value of less than 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Results

Screening of effective ingredients and prediction of therapeutic targets of AM

AM, officially documented in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, is derived from the dried radix of Astragalus mongholicus Bunge (Fabaceae family)19. We utilized specific criteria from the TCMSP database, including oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30%, drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18, relative molecular mass (Mw) ≤ 500, lipid-water partition coefficient (LogP) ≤ 5, number of hydrogen bond donors (nOHNH) ≤ 5, and number of hydrogen bond acceptors (nOH) ≤ 10, to identify the active ingredients of AM20. Through these screening criteria, we identified eight primary bioactive compounds (Table 2), which include Bifendate, Calycosin, Formononetin, Isorhamnetin, Jaranol, Kaempferol, Quercetin, and Kaempferol. Subsequent target prediction using SwissTargetPrediction identified 236 putative therapeutic targets, which were further analyzed through NP analysis.

Prediction of silicosis fibrosis targets

The pathogenesis of silicosis fibrosis results from multiple interrelated pathological processes, including sustained inflammation, fibroblast activation, and aberrant extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition21. We utilized three established databases—DisGeNET, GeneCards, and CTD—to identify key regulatory genes associated with silicosis. Initial screening produced 121 high-confidence targets in DisGeNET, 259 disease-associated genes in GeneCards, and 7,589 potential targets in CTD, which were filtered by median score. The CTD targets underwent pre-filtering using median score thresholds (range: 3.81–18.38), with these values reflecting gene-disease association confidence levels. Following integration and removal of duplicates, we retained 7,706 unique genes for further analysis of silicosis pathogenesis (Fig. 2).

Screening of anti-silicosis targets of AM

We performed Venn analysis using Venny 2.1 to visualize overlapping targets. The intersection analysis reveals 113 common targets, which represent 1.4% of the total predicted targets, suggesting their potential as key mediators of AM’s anti-silicosis effects (Fig. 2).

The top seven hub proteins were selected based on degree centrality: AKT1, CASP3, EGFR, ESR1, PTGS2, JUN, and MMP9 (Fig. 3B; Table 3). Detailed degree centrality values are presented in Table 3. GO and KEGG analyses

Functional enrichment analysis of the 113 shared targets was performed using the DAVID database, which included GO annotation and KEGG pathway analysis. The GO analysis comprised three functional categories: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). In the BP analysis, target genes were predominantly enriched in signal transduction, inflammatory response, response to hypoxia, and immune response. The CC analysis primarily identified ingredients such as the cell surface, cytoplasm, cytosol, endosome, and nucleus. The MF analysis revealed activities including protein serine/threonine kinase activity, heme binding, ATP binding, and endopeptidase activity. GO terms were ranked by statistical significance (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05), with the top 20 from each category visualized in a bubble chart (Fig. 4A). The KEGG analysis identified 122 significantly enriched pathways (P < 0.05), primarily involving the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 4B)23–25. The depth of color in the chart represents the magnitude of the P-value, with deeper shades of red indicating smaller P-values, which signify greater statistical significance of pathway enrichment. Network topology analysis conducted using Cytoscape 3.10.2 and Network Analyzer identified Quercetin, isorhamnetin, and kaempferol as the three AM compounds with the highest degree centrality values, designating them as core bioactive ingredients (Table 4). Structural representations, including 2D chemical structures and 3D conformations of these key compounds, were generated using ChemDraw Professional 20.0 (https://www.graphpad.com) (Fig. 5A–C). Molecular docking evaluated the binding affinities of three top-ranked AM compounds-Quercetin, isorhamnetin, and kaempferol-with their respective targets by calculating binding energy (ΔG, kcal·mol−1) and interaction patterns. More negative binding energy values (ΔG) indicate a stronger ligand-target affinity, reflecting lower energy requirements for complex formation and greater thermodynamic stability of the interaction under physiological conditions.

Quercetin formed highly stable complexes with MMP9 and EGFR. Quercetin established two hydrogen bonds with MMP9 at Pro246 and Arg249, respectively. It also engaged in multiple hydrophobic interactions and π-π stacking with residues surrounding the Ala242-Pro246-Arg249 pocket. Quercetin formed hydrogen bonds with EGFR at Ile732, Glu709, and Leu730, while extensive hydrophobic interactions and π-cation interactions occur within the Ile732-Leu730 hydrophobic cluster. These strong non-covalent interactions, along with the low binding energy values, indicate a high affinity and potential regulatory effect on the functions of the target proteins. To visually represent these interactions, detailed molecular interaction diagrams were generated (Fig. 5D). These figures clearly illustrate the spatial arrangement of Quercetin within the active sites of MMP9 and EGFR, emphasizing the specific residues involved in hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and π-π/π-cation stacking.

The nine strongest binding pairs, ranked by ΔG values, are as follows: Quercetin-MMP9 (− 8.75 kcal·mol⁻1), kaempferol-MMP9 (− 8.68 kcal·mol⁻1), kaempferol-JUN (− 8.31 kcal·mol−1), isorhamnetin-MMP9 (− 7.90 kcal·mol⁻1), isorhamnetin-JUN (− 7.40 kcal·mol−1), Quercetin-JUN (− 7.17 kcal·mol⁻1), Quercetin-AKT1 (− 6.95 kcal·mol⁻1), kaempferol-AKT1 (− 6.88 kcal·mol⁻1), and Quercetin-EGFR (− 6.63 kcal·mol⁻1). Notably, Quercetin did not form stable complexes with PTGS2 (ΔG > − 5.0 kcal·mol⁻1). This lack of stability may arise from two primary factors. First, the inherent limitations of rigid-docking algorithms in modeling membrane protein dynamics, as PTGS2 is membrane-associated and possesses allosteric binding pockets27. Second, the regulatory mechanisms of PTGS2 involve multiple factors, including transcriptional activation, mRNA stability regulation, and feedback regulatory mechanisms28,29.

Key pathway screened and validation in vitro

Quercetin, isorhamnetin, and kaempferol exhibit anti-fibrotic properties in silicosis by targeting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways30–32. Topological analysis further revealed that all three compounds share identical network centrality (Degree = 53), indicating their potential to regulate similar molecular mechanisms underlying fibrosis. However, molecular docking studies provided a critical basis for prioritization: Quercetin demonstrated significantly higher binding affinity to core fibrotic targets compared to isorhamnetin and kaempferol. Specifically, Quercetin had a mean binding free energy (ΔG) of − 7.02 ± 0.93 kcal·mol⁻1, surpassing isorhamnetin (− 6.69 ± 0.87 kcal·mol⁻1) and kaempferol (− 6.94 ± 1.31 kcal·mol⁻1). This lower ΔG value indicates a more stable complex formation, suggesting enhanced biological activity. Complemented by its well-documented efficacy and mechanistic characterization in silicosis models33, Quercetin was identified as the optimal candidate for in vitro validation, representing the lead anti-fibrotic ingredient of AM.

Network analysis pinpointed MMP9 and EGFR as the top-ranked targets, with the PI3K-AKT pathway significantly implicated in the anti-fibrotic effects of AM against silicosis. Notably, among the six validated targets, MMP9 and EGFR exhibited the strongest binding affinities to the bioactive components of AM. As a well-recognized downstream target of the PI3K-AKT pathway, MMP9 facilitates ECM degradation and is pathologically upregulated in fibrotic lung tissues34. EGFR, by contrast, promotes fibrogenesis through enhancing fibroblast proliferation and ECM deposition35.

To confirm these in silico findings, we validated the in vitro functional relevance of key molecules (α-SMA, MMP9, and EGFR) identified via network pharmacology and molecular docking. Immunofluorescence staining showed that compared with the control group (7.75 ± 0.94), SiO2 notably elevated α-SMA expression in MRC-5 cells (19.91 ± 0.06, P < 0.01), whereas Quercetin diminished this upregulation (8.77 ± 0.47, P < 0.01). Consistently, TGF-β1 (a classic pro-fibrotic cytokine) also significantly increased α-SMA expression (22.15 ± 2.13, P < 0.01). In parallel, Quercetin suppressed MMP9 and EGFR expression in SiO2-induced MRC-5 cells (Fig. 7).

Collectively, these results illustrate that Quercetin exerts its anti-fibrotic activity by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, as well as downregulating the expression of MMP9 and EGFR—key pro-fibrotic molecules implicated in silicosis progression.

Discussion

Silicosis fibrosis is characterized by excessive accumulation of lung fibroblasts, uncontrolled secretion of ECM, and collagen deposition36,37. Current pharmacotherapies for silicosis are limited and associated with severe adverse effects, highlighting the urgent need for the development of safer novel treatments and elucidation of underlying mechanisms. AM has gained global recognition for its anti-fibrotic effects across various organs. Preclinical evidence demonstrates that AM extract injection attenuates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in rodent models38, providing proof-of-concept for efficacy and preliminary safety data for clinical translation. Through integrated network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro validation, we identified six core targets (MMP9, EGFR, AKT1, CASP3, ESR1, JUN) that mediate AM’s anti-silicosis effects, regulating inflammation and apoptosis.

AM operates through multiple pathways, including PI3K-Akt, IL-17, TNF, EGFR, and MAPK. Its active ingredients, such as quercetin, isorhamnetin, and kaempferol, exhibit stable binding to these core targets, resulting in multi-target effects. Isorhamnetin inhibits fibrosis via the TGF-β1/Smad3/p38 MAPK pathway, regulating autophagy and ECM, while also suppressing hepatic stellate cell activation. Kaempferol exerts anti-fibrotic effects through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions, which include blocking macrophage M1 polarization, inhibiting MAPK/NF-κB signaling, and counteracting TGF-β1-induced upregulation of αSMA and collagen 39–41. In this study, quercetin was selected for in-depth experimental validation based on the following integrated considerations. First, molecular docking analysis revealed that quercetin exhibited the strongest overall binding affinity (most negative mean ΔG) to the six core targets compared with isorhamnetin and kaempferol. Specifically, it formed the most stable complex with MMP9 (ΔG = − 8.75 kcal·mol⁻1) among all tested pairs and displayed strong binding to EGFR. As MMP9 and EGFR were identified as top hub genes in the PPI network and are central to the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, this high-affinity interaction provided a robust mechanistic basis for prioritizing quercetin. Second, mounting preclinical evidence confirms that quercetin exerts consistent anti-fibrotic effects across multiple organs, including the lung, kidney, and liver 42–45. In addition, its well-documented safety profile and widespread dietary exposure further support its translational potential45,46. Although isorhamnetin and kaempferol are also promising flavonoids, quercetin possesses more comprehensive mechanistic evidence and safety data relevant to silicosis. Therefore, quercetin was chosen as the representative active ingredient to experimentally validate the anti-silicotic mechanism of AM.

Fibroblasts drive pathological ECM remodeling through excessive synthesis of type I and III collagen and differentiate into myofibroblasts, as evidenced by α-SMA expression, enhanced ECM production, and tissue disruption47–49. Our findings demonstrate that quercetin directly suppresses the core drivers of silica-induced fibrosis, and its mechanistic hierarchy and pathological relevance to silicosis are further clarified by functional experiments. Specifically, SiO2 exposure upregulates the expression of EGFR, MMP9, and α-SMA proteins in lung fibroblasts, as well as PI3K and AKT mRNA, while quercetin significantly downregulates their expression. Notably, quercetin acts as an upstream modulator of the EGFR-PI3K/AKT axis in silicosis fibrosis: our rescue experiments with the PI3K agonist 740 Y-P confirmed that artificial activation of PI3K reversed the inhibitory effects of quercetin on PI3K and AKT mRNA expression, which directly verifies that quercetin exerts its anti-fibrotic effect by targeting EGFR-the upstream initiator of this axis that is specifically activated by silica dust-rather than regulating downstream components of the PI3K-AKT pathway. This upstream regulation further leads to the downregulation of MMP9 and α-SMA, the key downstream effectors of fibrotic remodeling.

EGFR and MMP9 are selected as the core targets for validation not only due to their high binding affinity with quercetin, but also their silicosis-specific pathological significance that is distinct from other core targets (e.g., AKT1, JUN, CASP3). EGFR activation is a direct trigger of silica-induced fibroblast proliferation and myofibroblast transformation50, while MMP9—a downstream effector of the PI3K-AKT pathway—is pathologically upregulated in silicotic lung tissues and drives the formation of silicotic nodules via promoting fibroblast migration and abnormal collagen secretion51,52. In contrast, other targets regulate general cellular processes such as cell survival, apoptosis, and inflammation, with no specific association with silica dust-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Fei et al53 confirmed that quercetin lowers lung MMP9 levels and inhibits the senescence-associated secretory phenotype in silica-induced mouse silicosis, indicating that our fibroblast-based in vitro results may partially predict the in vivo ECM-modulating efficacy of quercetin in silicosis. We will further establish silica-induced mice silicosis models to assess MMP9 and EGFR expressions following AM intervention, exploring the role of AM in multicellular crosstalk between macrophages and fibroblasts in silicosis.

The PI3K/AKT pathway regulates cell differentiation and directly participates in fibrogenesis54. Inhibiting its upstream regulators reduces fibroblast activation and ECM deposition55, establishing it as a critical target for anti-fibrotic therapy. Activation of the IL-17 pathway triggers the release of chemokines, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and tissue-remodeling factors, which amplify inflammation56. Therefore, inhibiting this pathway may provide a therapeutic approach for silicosis by suppressing neutrophil recruitment and pro-fibrotic cytokines. The MAPK pathway regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and stress responses57. Inhibition of the TLR4-NF-κB/MAPK pathway and reduction of macrophage pyroptosis alleviates silica-induced pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis in mice58. MMP9, a downstream effector of the PI3K/AKT pathway, promotes fibroblast migration51, driving pathological collagen secretion52. EGFR, a transmembrane tyrosine kinase, activates the RAS/RAF/MAPK, STAT3, and PI3K/AKT pathways in silicosis59. The EGFR inhibitor gefitinib slows the progression of silicosis by blocking p-EGFR-driven fibronectin synthesis50. AKT1 upregulates macrophage IL-13, which regulates pulmonary fibrosis60. JUN modulates lung cell survival and apoptosis to mitigate fibrosis61,62. Additionally, CASP3 inhibition reduces renal and pulmonary fibrosis63,64. Collectively, these targets support the multi-target therapeutic approach of anti-fibrotic treatment.

This polypharmacological strategy of AM is concretely exemplified by its core ingredient, quercetin. As our study demonstrates, quercetin itself exerts multi-target and multi-pathway regulatory effects by concurrently inhibiting EGFR, MMP9, and the PI3K/AKT pathway. This intrinsic polypharmacology endows quercetin with notable clinical and translational significance for silicosis treatment, which is further supported by its well-characterized safety profile and extensive human exposure background. Quercetin is a naturally occurring flavonoid widely present in dietary fruits, vegetables, and medicinal herbs (including AM), with a long history of human consumption and no obvious toxic effects on the respiratory, hepatic or renal systems at therapeutic doses45,46—a critical advantage for silicosis patients with chronic lung damage who may require long-term adjuvant treatment. Although quercetin has low oral bioavailability (2–5%), this limitation can be effectively addressed by advanced drug delivery strategies (e.g., lung-targeted lipid nanoparticles) to enhance its lung accumulation and local bioavailability in silicotic lesions. Moreover, quercetin-enriched AM preparations represent a more feasible adjunctive therapeutic strategy for silicosis: the synergistic effects of other active anti-fibrotic ingredients in AM (e.g., isorhamnetin, kaempferol) can compensate for the single-component limitation of quercetin, and the well-established safety and clinical application history of AM as a traditional Chinese medicine further lay a solid foundation for its clinical translation.

Quercetin’s multi-target regulatory properties also confer a pivotal translational advantage over single-target anti-fibrotic agents such as gefitinib. Silicosis fibrosis is driven by a complex network of multiple intertwined signaling pathways, and single-target agents only block a single molecular node in this network, which leads to limited therapeutic efficacy and a high risk of drug resistance in clinical practice. In contrast, quercetin achieves synergistic anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects by simultaneously inhibiting the upstream initiator (EGFR) and downstream effector (MMP9) of the PI3K-AKT pathway, combined with its reported anti-inflammatory effects on the MAPK/NF-κB pathway30. This multi-target regulation is highly aligned with the complex pathological characteristics of silicosis (persistent inflammation accompanied by progressive fibrosis), which is the key advantage of quercetin over single-target agents. Future studies may investigate the combination therapy of quercetin-enriched AM preparations and pirfenidone to simultaneously target the PI3K-AKT and TGF-β pathways, thereby amplifying anti-fibrotic effects while minimizing systemic toxicity for silicosis patients.

Our study offers insights into the anti-silicosis mechanisms of AM but has several limitations: 1) the absence of alveolar epithelial cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells in the models, which leaves intercellular crosstalk uncharacterized; 2) limited validation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, focusing primarily on MMP9/EGFR, while the IL-17/TNF/MAPK pathways remain uninvestigated; and 3) the lack of co-culture systems to examine macrophage-fibroblast interactions. Subsequent research will address these gaps by exploring multicellular crosstalk and the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that quercetin, the core active ingredient of AM, alleviates silicosis fibrosis by suppressing the expression of EGFR and MMP9, as well as inhibiting the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. This anti-fibrotic mechanism was systematically validated using an integrated approach that combines bioinformatics prediction and in vitro experimental validation. Collectively, our findings provide a comprehensive and robust theoretical and experimental basis for future investigations and the potential clinical translation of AM in the management of silicosis-associated fibrosis.
