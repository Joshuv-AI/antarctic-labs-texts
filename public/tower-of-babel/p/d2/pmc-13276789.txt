Thermal and Photocatalytic Conversion of CO2 to Ethanol: Advances in Catalyst Design, Challenges, and Opportunities
Authors: Bryan Rocano‐Merchan, Alejandro Cañete‐Arché, Asier Agrelo‐Lestón, Jonatan Perez‐Arce, Zoraida Freixa, Ander Centeno‐Pedrazo, Max García‐Melchor, Eduardo J Garcia‐Suarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transition toward a carbon‐neutral energy system requires efficient strategies to convert CO2 into energy‐dense liquid fuels capable of decarbonizing sectors where electrification remains impractical. Among emerging renewable synthetic fuels, ethanol is particularly attractive due to its high energy density, favorable handling properties, chemical versatility, and compatibility with existing infrastructure. Thermocatalytic and photocatalytic CO2 conversion routes have advanced rapidly, yet challenges in activity, selectivity, and catalytic durability continue to limit large‐scale deployment. This review surveys catalytic systems developed for CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion, covering both thermocatalytic and light‐driven approaches. Proposed reaction mechanisms are discussed with emphasis on how C—C coupling pathways depend on alloying, promoter effects, support properties, tandem configurations, and semiconductor design. Catalyst performance is compared across studies, and systems are grouped according to metal abundance and prevalence, highlighting strategies that enhance ethanol selectivity and productivity. Overall, this review integrates experimental performance with mechanistic insights from spectroscopy and theory, provides a comprehensive overview of the state of the art, and identifies key challenges and future directions for achieving higher yields, improved selectivity, and long‐term stability in renewable ethanol production from captured CO2.Keywords: alloys, bimetallics, CO2 conversion, DFT, heterogeneous catalysis, homogeneous catalysis, photocatalysis, reaction mechanisms, renewable synthetic ethanol production, renewable synthetic fuels, single‐atom catalysts, thermal catalysis

Introduction

Global warming remains an urgent challenge as human activities continue to accelerate climate change through the sustained release of greenhouse gases (GHGs). According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), CO2 emissions from the energy sector increased by 0.8% in 2024 relative to 2023, reaching 37.6 Gt CO2 [1]. Although natural systems exchange large amounts of CO2, these fluxes were approximately balanced over preindustrial timescales. As a result of sustained fossil fuel use, anthropogenic emissions now exceed the capacity of the oceans and biosphere to absorb CO2, causing a persistent rise in atmospheric concentrations. This accumulation contributes to rising global mean temperatures and associated environmental impacts, including sea level rise, more frequent extreme heat events, and increased risks of natural disasters such as wildfires and flooding [2].

In recognition of the need for coordinated action, the Paris Agreement, established in 2015 under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) [3], seeks to limit global warming to well below 2.0°C while pursuing efforts to restrict it to 1.5°C above preindustrial levels. To evaluate what would be required to meet these objectives, van Soest et al. modeled the emission reductions necessary to remain below 2°C [4]. Their analysis indicated that global GHG emissions in 2030 would need to be roughly 20% lower than in 2010. However, current policy trajectories suggest that emissions in 2030 will be slightly above 2010 levels, implying that substantially deeper annual reductions will be required after 2030.

Meeting these requirements depends not only on efficiency and electrification but also on technologies that directly manage CO2. For this reason, both the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) and the IEA identify carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) as an essential option for sectors where direct decarbonization remains difficult, including cement, steel, and several chemical and transport applications [5]. Within CCUS, carbon capture and storage (CCS) typically involves the long‐term containment of CO2 in geological formations, while CO2 utilization converts captured CO2 into fuels and chemicals. If powered by low‐emission energy sources, these conversion pathways can contribute to net CO2 reduction while supplying carbon‐based products [6].

A significant portion of global emissions arises from hard‐to‐abate sectors where electrification is limited. Transport is central among them, accounting for roughly 21% of global fossil CO2 emissions in 2023 and remaining heavily dependent on petroleum‐based liquid fuels [7]. In particular, decarbonizing high‐energy‐density modes of transport such as aviation and shipping is difficult because their performance requirements are currently met almost exclusively by fossil‐derived fuels.

As a result, alternative liquid fuels are needed alongside electrification. Renewable fuels derived from biomass offer one option, but renewable synthetic fuels are becoming increasingly important. Produced by combining captured CO2 with hydrogen and low‐emission electricity, these fuels can deliver drop‐in liquid products that lower the carbon intensity of sectors reliant on high‐energy‐density carriers [8].

Renewable Synthetic Fuels from Captured CO2

Renewable synthetic fuels produced from captured CO2 are emerging as key options for hard‐to‐abate sectors [9]. Within this category, renewable synthetic alcohols have received particular attention because they can be used as fuel additives or, in some cases, as standalone fuels with high energy density, stability under ambient conditions, and comparatively easy storage and transport [10, 11]. Alongside their role in circular carbon strategies, these alcohols can also reduce pollutant emissions, including particulate matter and nitrogen oxides, when blended or used directly [12].

Reflecting this growing interest, market projections anticipate continued growth in renewable synthetic alcohol‐based fuels, with a compound annual growth rate of about 4.1% between 2025 and 2033 [13]. Substantial investment in commercial‐scale projects highlights this momentum [14, 15, 16]. Although renewable synthetic methanol dominates current development due to more mature production routes, there is an increasing interest in technologies capable of converting CO2 into renewable synthetic ethanol, particularly for transportation applications [17].

Ethanol as a Renewable Synthetic Fuel

Methanol and ethanol are relevant low‐carbon fuels and versatile chemical feedstocks, although methanol has traditionally received more attention because its production pathways are more technologically established. Ethanol, however, is less toxic, less corrosive, and easier to handle in ground transportation [12]. It is also an efficient liquid energy carrier compatible with existing distribution and storage infrastructure, which facilitates integration into fuel systems with minimal modification [12].

Currently, ethanol is produced mainly by biomass fermentation or by petrochemical synthesis via ethylene hydration [18]. Fossil‐derived pathways dominate global supply, accounting for roughly 71.0% of the production due to their lower cost and established industrial infrastructure. Demand is expected to continue rising, with projections indicating a compound annual growth rate of approximately 6.0% between 2025 and 2034, reflecting ethanol's expanding role in global biofuel markets.

In parallel, several emerging strategies aim to produce ethanol directly from CO2 using renewable energy inputs, thereby enabling carbon‐neutral or carbon‐negative supply chains and linking renewable hydrogen production with chemical manufacturing [19, 20, 21]. These strategies include thermocatalytic, photocatalytic, and (photo)electrocatalytic routes. Despite their potential, current technologies face significant challenges in terms of activity, selectivity, and durability, which limit their scalability [19].

Among these approaches, thermocatalysis is currently closest to industrial applicability, supported by advances in heterogeneous catalyst design and expanding mechanistic insight into CO2 hydrogenation and C—C coupling pathways leading to C2+ alcohols [22, 23, 24, 25]. Homogeneous systems have also been investigated, although to a much lesser extent and at a significantly lower technological readiness level. Building on this foundation, photocatalysis offers a complementary, potentially solar‐driven route in which light‐generated charge carriers drive CO2 reduction. Although efficiencies remain modest, continued advances in materials engineering, heterojunction design, plasmonic enhancement, and selectivity‐control strategies highlight its growing promise for CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion. Electrocatalytic CO2 reduction has likewise become a very active research area with substantial progress toward C2 products [6, 26]; however, because this topic has been extensively reviewed elsewhere, it is not covered in the present work.

Scope and Structure of This Review

In this review, we focus on recent advances in thermo‐ and photocatalytic CO2 conversion to ethanol, with emphasis on catalyst development, mechanistic understanding, and the limitations that shape prospects for practical implementation. Our aim is to provide a comprehensive and structured overview of the state of the art by organizing the available literature, identifying key performance trends and mechanistic insights, and highlighting the most relevant challenges and opportunities in the field.

The thermocatalytic section (Section 2) begins with reaction mechanisms (Section 2.1), followed by an overview of catalyst types (Section 2.2). Catalysts are then grouped into precious‐metal (Section 2.3) and nonprecious‐metal systems (Section 2.4), reflecting differences in cost, abundance, and long‐term resource sustainability [27, 28]. The precious‐metal section is further divided between heterogeneous (Rh, Pd, Ir, Au, Ru) and homogeneous catalysts, while the nonprecious‐metal section covers catalysts based on Cu, Fe, and Co, as well as bimetallic and tandem systems.

The photocatalytic section (Section 3) follows a parallel structure but is organized by semiconductor class because the active phase in these systems is the photoactive material itself. Bi‐based materials (Section 3.2), g‐C3N4 (Section 3.3), copper oxides (Section 3.4), SrTiO3 (Section 3.5), TiO2 (Section 3.6), and In‐based (Section 3.7) semiconductors are discussed individually, followed by a subsection on emerging and less conventional photocatalysts.

Overall, this review synthesizes key advances and outstanding challenges across thermocatalytic and photocatalytic CO2‐to‐ethanol pathways, with particular attention to selectivity, durability, and mechanistic proposals for C—C coupling. In doing so, it aims to support the rational design of next‐generation catalysts and provide a coherent foundation for future research.

Methodology

Comparison of catalytic performance across the literature requires caution, as selectivity and productivity are not reported on a fully uniform basis. In this review, selectivity values reported on a total carbon basis are referred to as ethanol, higher‐alcohol, or alcohol selectivity. Higher‐alcohol selectivity excludes methanol. However, some studies report selectivity on alternative bases, such as CO‐free selectivity or selectivity within the alcohol fraction. These definitions are retained as originally reported, as they cannot always be rigorously converted from the available data. Accordingly, the reporting basis is explicitly identified throughout the text and tables, and values reported on different bases should be considered only approximately comparable unless otherwise noted.

For thermocatalysis, ethanol space–time yield (STY) values are standardized to mmol g–1 h–1 whenever the information provided allows a rigorous conversion. Full standardization is not always possible, as some studies report productivity using alternative metrics (e.g., mg gcat –1 h–1 for alcohols or higher alcohols) or lack sufficient compositional detail to enable unambiguous conversion to an ethanol‐based, mass‐normalized rate. In such cases, the original units are retained and clearly annotated. For photocatalytic systems, STY is expressed in µmol g–1 h–1, consistent with the lower productivities typically reported relative to thermocatalytic systems. These criteria are adopted to maximize transparency while avoiding artificial precision in cross‐study comparisons.

The H2/CO2 ratio is another key reaction parameter in CO2 reduction. In most thermocatalytic studies discussed here, this ratio is 3 and is therefore not reported systematically. It is specified only when it differs from this value or when it is particularly relevant to the interpretation of catalytic performance.

In addition, three summary tables are included (Tables 1–3). Table 1 compiles data from precious‐metal catalysts, including CO2 conversion, ethanol STY, ethanol selectivity, and related performance indicators. Table 2 summarizes nonprecious‐metal catalysts and includes additional columns for higher‐alcohol selectivity and STY, reflecting the broader product distribution typically reported for these systems. Table 3 covers photocatalytic systems and compiles key performance metrics, including ethanol productivity, selectivity, and, when available, apparent quantum efficiency (AQE), solar‐to‐fuel (STF) conversion metrics, and stability data.

Thermocatalytic CO2 Conversion to Ethanol

Reaction Mechanisms

The thermocatalytic hydrogenation of CO2 to ethanol is intrinsically complex because ethanol is a C2 product and therefore requires one C—C coupling step between C1‐derived intermediates. To date, no single mechanism has been universally accepted, and several parallel or competing pathways may operate simultaneously on a given catalyst [153, 154, 155]. Even so, most mechanistic proposals share a common element, namely, that C—C coupling occurs when an adsorbed alkyl fragment (*CH x ; * denotes an active site) reacts with an adsorbed C1 intermediate.

The differences among the proposed mechanisms mainly concern how the *CH x fragment is generated and which C1 species participates in the coupling. A practical way to organize them is to classify each mechanism according to the origin of the C1 intermediate involved in C—C coupling. Scheme 1 summarizes the three most widely discussed mechanisms: the CO‐insertion pathway (in green), the formate‐mediated pathway (in orange), and the methanol‐mediated pathway (in blue).

Conceptual reaction network for thermal CO2 conversion to ethanol, highlighting CO‐insertion, formate‐mediated, and methanol‐mediated pathways. Adsorbed intermediates are denoted with “*”. Solid arrows indicate single elementary steps, gray arrows mark C—C coupling steps, and dashed arrows represent sequences of multiple elementary steps.

In the CO‐insertion mechanism, CO2 is reduced to CO, most commonly through the reverse water–gas shift (RWGS) reaction (Equation (1)) [156].

The CO formed in this step adsorbs on the surface as *CO. Under reducing conditions, *CO may remain intact or undergo stepwise hydrogenation or dissociation to form *CH x (x = 0–3) and partially oxygenated intermediates such as *CH x O. C—C coupling then occurs when *CO reacts with a reduced C1 fragment (*CH x or *CH x O), forming an acyl‐type intermediate (*CH x CO) that is hydrogenated to a surface ethoxy species (*CH3CH2O) and eventually to ethanol, which then desorbs. In broad terms, this route resembles classical Fischer–Tropsch chemistry, with CO hydrogenation and C—C bond formation occurring on metallic surfaces under conditions that favor C2 oxygenates rather than hydrocarbons.

In the formate‐mediated mechanism, the key C1 intermediates form directly through CO2 hydrogenation to formate (HCOO*) and formyl (HCO*) species. These species are subsequently reduced to *CH x O and *CH x fragments, which can combine through reactions such as *CH x with HCOO* or HCO*, producing acetate‐ or acetyl‐like intermediates. Further hydrogenation yields ethoxy species and ultimately to ethanol. A closely related variant, the methanol‐mediated route, involves continued hydrogenation to methoxy or methanol. These species can decompose to adsorbed *CH3, which then couples with *CO or formate‐derived fragments, feeding into the same C—C forming step as in the CO‐insertion mechanism.

Some studies have also proposed an Eley–Rideal‐type pathway, where an adsorbed *CH x species reacts directly with CO2 in the gas phase. Although the initial step differs from the routes above, the downstream intermediates converge to the same acetyl or acetate species. Similarly, tandem mechanisms naturally fit into this classification: the reduced C1 fragment and the C1 partner for coupling may arise from different activation routes, yet the C—C bond still forms through the combination of a reduced C1 species with *CO or formate‐derived fragments.

Viewed collectively, these pathways represent variations on a common mechanistic theme in which a reduced C1 fragment (*CH x ) reacts with a C1 species derived from CO, formate, or methanol, and the resulting C2 intermediate is hydrogenated stepwise to ethanol. Nevertheless, no single C—C coupling pathway can be generalized across catalyst classes; instead, the preferred route is catalyst‐dependent rather than intrinsically linked to a specific metal identity [156].

Implications for Catalyst Design: Descriptor‐Based View

Among the proposed pathways, the CO‐insertion route is the most frequently discussed. Its efficiency depends on the ability of the catalyst to supply *CO via the RWGS reaction and on the balance between *CO coverage, *CO hydrogenation, and C—O bond cleavage. Medford and co‐workers analyzed these trends by combining linear scaling relations and mean‐field microkinetic modeling, projecting catalyst performance onto a two‐dimensional descriptor space defined by carbon and oxygen binding energies [157]. This approach condenses the extensive network of elementary steps into a simple map that captures the essential reactivity patterns.

The resulting volcano plot reveals distinct regimes (Scheme 2). When both carbon and oxygen bind strongly to the catalyst surface, C—O bond scission becomes facile, and methanation predominates. At the opposite extreme, when carbon and oxygen bind only weakly, CO activation is difficult, and methanol becomes the favored product. Between these two regimes lies a narrow zone in which the carbon and oxygen bind strongly enough to permit C—O activation yet weakly enough to allow *CH x —*CO coupling without excessive overhydrogenation. Only catalysts that fall within this intermediate region show the potential for meaningful ethanol selectivity.

Strongly hydrogenating metals (Ru, Rh, Ni, Ir, Pd, Pt) lie in the methane‐selective domain, while weakly binding late noble metals (Cu, Ag, Au) in the methanol‐selective domain. This figure qualitatively illustrates the results from Medford et al. [157], in which points represent adsorption energies to (211) surfaces calculated with the RPBE functional.

Screening of metal catalysts within this framework places transition metals such as Ni, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ir, and Pt in the strong‐binding region, where methane formation dominates, whereas coinage metals such as Cu, Ag, and Au lie in the weak‐binding region, where methanol is favored. Notably, none of the pure transition metals fall naturally within the narrow window associated with selective ethanol formation.

This mechanistic picture clarifies why highly active and selective ethanol catalysts based solely on monometallic surfaces have been difficult to develop. It also highlights how tailored modifications can shift a catalyst toward the desired descriptor region. For relatively noble metals, design strategies that enhance C—O bond activation can bring their performance closer to the ethanol‐selective domain. Conversely, for overly reactive metals, approaches that moderate C—O bond cleavage can suppress methanation. Alloying, promoter effects, and metal–support interactions all offer ways to tune carbon and oxygen binding energies so that both C—O activation and C—C coupling occur at comparable rates, which is an essential requirement for selective ethanol formation.

Overview of Thermocatalysts

The preceding discussion provides a mechanistic and descriptor‐based context for understanding how CO2 activation, C—O bond scission, and CH x —C1 coupling govern ethanol formation. In this section, we survey the thermocatalytic systems that have been reported for CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion and highlight how their design principles reflect the broader mechanistic rationale outlined above.

Thermocatalytic CO2 hydrogenation to ethanol typically requires elevated temperatures, often up to about 900°C, because the RWGS is endothermic [158]. Although some recent studies have demonstrated RWGS at considerably milder temperatures (near 150°C) [159], most reported systems still rely on high‐temperature, high‐pressure, and H2‐rich conditions. Under these conditions, heterogenous catalysts are generally preferred because they remain stable and can be more readily separated from reaction products than homogeneous catalysts. The most widely investigated metals for heterogeneous CO2‐to‐ethanol thermocatalysis include Fe, Co, Cu, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ir, and Au (groups 8–11). In the sections that follow, these catalysts are discussed individually, organized according to their classification as precious or nonprecious metals.

Precious metals (Ru, Rh, Pd, Ir, Au) often exhibit higher intrinsic activity and selectivity toward ethanol but are limited by their cost and supply constraints. In contrast, nonprecious metals (Fe, Co, Cu) are more abundant and attractive for scale‐up, though they generally show lower performance. This contrast mirrors broader concerns regarding long‐term resource sustainability as precious metals face greater supply risks [160]. For completeness, we also discuss the comparatively few homogeneous thermocatalysts reported to date, all of which rely on precious‐metal centers.

Across catalysts families, alloying, promoters, and support interactions are frequently used to tune ethanol selectivity. Within the descriptor framework, alloying alters the electronic structure of the metal surface and thereby shifts carbon and oxygen binding energies. For example, Cu–Co alloys have been predicted to lie within the ethanol‐selective region and have been experimentally shown to enhance higher‐alcohol formation [157, 161]. Alkali promoters provide another way of tuning reactivity [38, 45]; potassium, for instance, strengthens both carbon and oxygen binding on Cu, shifting it toward a regime associated with higher oxygenate selectivity. Because CH x —CO transition states likely possess substantial dipole moments, alkali species may also lower CH x —CO coupling barriers and thus promote C2 formation [157].

In addition, alkali promoters are often associated with the suppression of excessive hydrogenation, which can favor oxygenate formation over hydrocarbons. This effect, however, is frequently accompanied by lower CO2 conversion, reflecting the trade‐off between limiting overhydrogenation and maintaining sufficient hydrogenation activity. Transition‐metal additives can also modify the catalytic environment, although they should not always be regarded as simple promoters. Fe‐containing species, for example, may form catalytically active oxide, carbide, or metal–carbide interfacial phases under reaction conditions, participating directly in RWGS, Fischer–Tropsch‐type chain‐growth steps, and C2 oxygenate formation.

Reactivity can also be tuned through geometric modifications. Reducing the number of step or defect sites on metals that reside in the methane‐selective regime, or alternatively increasing the density of low‐coordination sites on relatively noble metals, can shift selectivity toward ethanol. Such geometric effects depend sensitively on particle size, metal–support interactions, and synthesis method, which makes them challenging to control consistently.

Importantly, multicomponent catalysts, whether alloyed, promoted, or supported, necessarily possess a distribution of site types rather than a single, uniform active site. As a result, macroscopic selectivity reflects the collective behavior of all accessible sites. An ethanol‐selective site is therefore essential but not sufficient; the catalyst must also avoid exposing sites that favor methane or methanol at higher rates. In this context, catalyst supports play an important role. Beyond stabilizing and dispersing metal nanoparticles and promoters [33], supports containing oxygen vacancies can enhance CO2 activation and, in some cases, contribute directly to RWGS activity [126, 162]. Their influence must therefore be considered as part of the overall catalytic environment since metal–support interactions can modify reactant adsorption strength, hydrogen availability, and the competition between methane, methanol, and C2+ alcohol formation [163]. For this reason, the presence of oxygen vacancies should not be interpreted as establishing a direct correlation with ethanol selectivity. While they may improve CO2 adsorption, activate intermediates, stabilize isolated metal sites, or enhance RWGS activity, ethanol and higher‐alcohol formation additionally require suitable sites for CO stabilization, controlled hydrogenation, and C—C coupling while suppressing competing methane and methanol pathways [164].

A similar consideration applies to specific oxidation states. Mixed oxidation states such as Rh+/Rh0, Cu+/Cu0, Co0/CoO, or Co0/Co2+ have been proposed to promote ethanol‐selective behavior, as dual‐site environments (M0/Mδ+) can enable complementary adsorption and activation of key intermediates involved in CO insertion and C—C coupling [45]. However, such correlations remain catalyst‐specific rather than universal, as changes in oxidation state are often coupled with variations in particle size, dispersion, support interaction, vacancy concentration, and promoter distribution.

Overall, the literature indicates that ethanol selectivity is best understood as the outcome of a multifunctional catalytic environment, rather than as the consequence of any single structural descriptor.

Precious‐Metal Catalysts

This section covers precious metal catalysts reported for CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion. We first discuss heterogeneous systems based on Rh, Pd, Ir, and Au, followed by homogeneous catalysts, which mainly contain Ru and Rh centers [155, 165, 166]. Within each category, metals are presented in order of their prevalence in the literature.

Heterogeneous Catalysts

This section focuses on metals that lie in the strong‐binding, methane‐selective region of the descriptor map (Scheme 2) described in Section 2.1.1. For such systems, achieving ethanol formation generally requires strategies that moderate C—O bond scission and favor controlled CH x —CO coupling over excessive hydrogenation.

Rh‐Based Catalysts

Rh has been widely studied across several catalytic applications, including fine chemical synthesis, methane conversion, and hydrogen generation. It is also highly active for syngas (CO and H2) conversion to value‐added products. Rh sits deeply within the methane‐selective region (Scheme 2), where C—O cleavage is favored and CH x fragments hydrogenate readily [167]. As a result, most Rh‐based catalysts for ethanol production rely on promoters such as Fe, Mn, or alkali metals to shift selectivity toward oxygenates [168, 169].

One of the earliest contributions to renewable synthetic ethanol synthesis in batch reactors examined TiO2‐supported Rh10Se clusters [29, 30, 31]. Catalyst pretreatment strongly influenced performance, with decarbonylation followed by H2 reduction giving the highest ethanol selectivity (83.0% among all products) and a STY of 1.90 mmol g–1 h–1 at 250°C, 0.47 bar, and a H2/CO2 ratio of 2:1. The study attributed the promotional effect of Se to its influence on the electronic structure of Rh. Extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) data indicated a modified Rh coordination consistent with a more electron‐deficient cluster, which was associated with a shift in reactivity away from CO dissociation and toward acetyl formation. The proposed pathway followed an Eley–Rideal‐type route in which *CH x fragments on Rh couple directly with CO2‐derived carbonate species to form acetyl or acetate intermediates that subsequently hydrogenate to ethanol. This mechanism was supported by in situ Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy bands assigned to carbonate and acetate species, together with EXAFS evidence for a “rhodium selenide”‐like electronic state.

In a more recent study, Ye et al. reported a bifunctional Rh single‐atom catalyst supported on crystalline β‐Mo2C nanowires, with K as a promoter to further enhance selectivity [32]. The optimized composition, K0.2Rh0.2/β‐Mo2C (0.2 wt% Rh and 0.2 wt% K), achieved 72.1% ethanol selectivity and an ethanol STY of 0.03 mmol g–1 h–1 at 150°C and 60 bar in 1,4‐dioxane. In this system, K was proposed to enhance CO2 adsorption and activation on β‐Mo2C, while Rh catalyzed the RWGS reaction to provide *CO. In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) showed Rh‐bound *CO together with strongly adsorbed carbonate species on Mo sites, suggesting that these CO2‐derived intermediates participate directly in the C—C coupling step leading to ethanol.

Zheng et al. reported notable improvements in CO2 conversion and ethanol yield using a Rh1/CeTiO x catalyst, in which Rh exists as isolated Rhδ+ single atoms [33]. Incorporation of Ti into CeO2 increased the density of oxygen vacancies, improving metal dispersion and stability. At 250°C and 30 bar, this catalyst achieved 6.3% CO2 conversion, 99.1% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 0.01 mmol g–1 h–1. The authors attributed the enhanced performance to higher oxygen vacancy concentration, which suppressed Rh aggregation and provided interfacial sites active for C1 species. Based on DRIFTS data, they proposed a CO‐insertion pathway in which CO2 is first reduced to *CO and *CH x at the Rh–oxygen‐vacancy interfacial sites on CeTiO x . Stabilization of *CO on Rhδ+ then facilitates C—C coupling between *CO and reduced C1 fragments.

The same group also investigated the influence of Rh loading on Rh/CeO2 at 250°C and 30 bar [34]. The lowest Rh loading (0.1 wt%) gave the best ethanol performance, with 3.2% CO2 conversion, 74.3% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 2.16 mmol g–1 h–1. This superior behavior was attributed to higher dispersion of Rh on CeO2 and the predominance of Rhδ+ species over metallic Rh. Under these conditions, hydrogenation was sufficiently moderated to maintain an appropriate balance between dissociated and nondissociated *CO species, favoring ethanol formation. In contrast, increasing the Rh loading to 2.0 wt% led to a predominance of metallic Rh, which is more prone to overhydrogenation and thus less favorable for ethanol generation. Overall, these results suggest that ethanol formation on Rh/CeO2 requires a finely tuned hydrogenation activity since excessive hydrogenation depletes the *CO‐derived intermediates needed for C—C coupling.

In a subsequent study, Zheng et al. evaluated CO2 hydrogenation in aqueous medium to probe the effect of promoters and identified a Rh/CeTiO2 catalyst combined with dissolved LiI as an effective system (Scheme 3) [35]. In a slurry reactor at 250°C and 30 bar, they reported 9.2% CO2 conversion, 99.5% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 8.42 mmol g–1 h–1, whereas operation in a fixed‐bed reactor under similar conditions gave only 1.6% CO2 conversion with no detectable ethanol. Replacing water with methanol dramatically increased the ethanol STY to 223.10 mmol g–1 h–1, which the authors attributed to water formation during methanol conversion, consistent with a continued increase in ethanol yield between 5 and 20 h on stream.

Route A (blue) represents the heterogeneous pathway on Rh1/CeTiO x , proceeding through formate‐ and CO‐derived intermediates to yield ethanol. Route B (green) represents the LiI‐mediated pathway, in which dissolved Li+/I– promotes methanol activation and methyl generation that subsequently couples with *CO on single Rh sites. Spin‐polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations at the PBE + U level provided mechanistic insight into this LiI‐promoted pathway. The surface was modeled as a Rh single atom anchored at an oxygen‐vacancy site on a Ce0.7Ti0.3O x slab, while Li+ and I– species were introduced to represent the dissolved promoter. DFT calculations showed that LiI acts as a biphasic Lewis acid–base pair: Li+ coordinates the O atom of adsorbed methanol, whereas I– interacts with the methyl group, thereby polarizing and elongating the C—O bond. This polarization facilitates the heterolytic cleavage of methanol to form *CH3I and solvated LiOH in a barrierless process. The subsequent dissociation of *CH3I into *CH3 and I– proceeds with a significantly lower barrier than direct C—O scission in *CH3OH. Charge‐density difference maps and Bader analyses confirmed this electron polarization. The resulting *CH3 species then couples with *CO adsorbed on single Rh sites to ultimately form ethanol, establishing a homogeneous–heterogeneous cooperative mechanism in which LiI mediates methyl generation in the liquid phase while Rh1/CeTiO x activates *CO and catalyzes the C—C coupling step (Scheme 3).

Zheng et al. next examined whether a similar electronic modulation could be embedded directly into the catalyst by introducing phosphorus into the coordination environment of Rh single atom catalysts [36]. To this end, they employed a nitrogen‐doped carbon (CN) support and further incorporated P, comparing Rh/CN with P‐modified Rh/CNP catalysts. Rh/CNP delivered the highest performance, achieving 4.9% CO2 conversion, 81.8% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 3.68 mmol g–1 h–1 at 300°C and 1 bar. DFT + U calculations showed that Rh–N4 sites on undoped CN favor methanol formation because *CH3OH is strongly stabilized and its C—O bond cleavage is kinetically hindered. In contrast, on Rh–N3P1, electron donation from P to Rh polarizes the C—O bond of *CH3OH, lowering the barrier for its dissociation to *CH3. Bader charge analysis confirmed the charge transfer from P to Rh and from Rh to the adsorbate, which elongates the C—O bond. The computed Gibbs energy profiles revealed a C—O cleavage barrier of only 0.27 eV on Rh–N3P1, compared with 2.33 eV on Rh–N4. The subsequent coupling of *CH3 with *CO proceeds with a barrier of 0.48 eV, which is lower than that for CO desorption or further hydrogenation. Mechanistically, ethanol formation again follows a methanol‐mediated pathway in which *CH3 generated via P‐induced C—O bond polarization couples with *CO (Scheme 4), whereas methanol remains favored on Rh–N4 due to the much higher C—O cleavage barrier.

CO2 is stepwise hydrogenated to *CH3OH, which undergoes P‐induced C—O bond polarization and dissociation to *CH3, followed by coupling between *CH3 and *CO and final hydrogenation/desorption to form ethanol. Reproduced with permission [36] 2024, Elsevier.

Gong et al. studied FeO x as a promoter in Rh nanoparticles catalysts supported on TiO2 P25 (a mixed anatase–rutile material), forming RhFeO x /P25 [37]. At 220°C and 50 bar, this system reached 15.6% CO2 conversion, 90.5% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 18.70 mmol g–1 h–1. The addition of FeO x and the use of water as solvent both enhanced conversion and ethanol selectivity. Consistent with earlier Rh‐based work, the balance between Rhδ+ and Rh0 species played a central role. CO2 Temperature‐programmed desorption (TPD) and H2/D2 exchange experiments probed adsorption and activation, while DRIFTS was used to elucidate the mechanism. Ethanol formation was attributed to a formate‐mediated pathway in which Rh0 facilitates CO2 and H2 activation, whereas the interfacial Rh0–Rhδ+–OV–Ti3+ ensemble drove the coupling between *CH x O and *CH x intermediates.

Kusama et al. investigated Rh/SiO2 in a continuous reactor, examining the effects of promoter addition, metal particle size, support, and precursor choice, and proposed a reaction mechanism based on in situ FTIR spectroscopy [38]. Li promotion increased ethanol selectivity to 15.5% while slightly lowering CO2 conversion to 7.% at 240°C and 50 bar. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data indicated that Li modified the electronic state of Rh and altered CO adsorption: on 5.0 wt% Rh‐Li/SiO2, bridged *CO species were more abundant than linear *CO. The authors claimed that because bridged *CO interacts with multiple Rh atoms, it occupies larger surface areas, suppresses hydrogenation, and thereby favors *CO insertion into *CH x fragments. Bando et al. later reported a similar promotional effect on zeolite‐supported Rh [39]. At 250°C and 30 bar, Li increased ethanol formation by improving CO2 adsorption and facilitating nondissociative conversion into *CO on Rh sites, while Li‐independent sites generated *CH3 that subsequently coupled with *CO. In situ FTIR observations of adsorbed species supported this mechanism.

The same group also examined Fe incorporation by preparing a 5.0 wt% Rh–Fe (1:2)/SiO2 catalyst [40]. At 240°C and 50 bar, this system achieved 26.7% CO2 conversion with an ethanol yield of 4.3% and an ethanol selectivity of 16.0%. Fe addition played a decisive role because it altered the electronic environment of Rh, thereby enhancing both conversion and selectivity. The reaction was also proposed to follow a CO‐insertion mechanism in which bridged *CO acted as the key intermediate.

Kusama et al. further evaluated particle‐size effects on ethanol formation. Rh nanoparticles gave an ethanol selectivity of around 2.0% at 260°C and 50 bar [41]. In contrast to their promoter studies, they concluded that linear *CO was more reactive toward ethanol formation than bridged *CO under these conditions, suggesting the bridged *CO does not directly participate in C—C coupling in this regime. They also showed that the choice of the Rh precursor strongly influences particle size [42]. Among Rh(III) acetate, nitrate, and chloride, Rh(III) acetate produced the smallest particles (2.8 nm), which corresponded to the highest ethanol selectivity at 260°C and 50 bar.

In related work, Kusama et al. found that Rh/CeO2–SiO2 with a Ce:Rh atomic ratio of 1 exhibited the highest ethanol selectivity (6.1%), achieving 8.3% CO2 conversion at 240°C and 50 bar [43]. In this system, CeO2 improved CO2 activation and contributed to the overall activity. An empirical correlation between ethanol selectivity and the formation of methanol, CO, and CH4 suggested that methanol and CO act as intermediates in ethanol formation, whereas CH4 selectivity reflects the extent of hydrogenation.

Building on these ideas, Yang et al. developed a RhFeLi/TiO2 nanorod catalyst with an optimal metal loading of 2.5 wt% (Rh:Fe:Li = 1), which achieved 15.0% CO2 conversion, 4.0% ethanol yield, and 32.0% ethanol selectivity at 250°C and 30 bar [44]. The TiO2 nanorod support played an important role, as its high density of surface hydroxyl groups nearly doubled ethanol selectivity compared with commercial TiO2 (∼18.0%), as confirmed by FTIR and DRIFTS. In this system, Fe species catalyzed the RWGS reaction to generate CO, while surface hydroxyl groups stabilized HCOO* intermediates that served as precursors for *CH x species. Based on DRIFTS, the authors proposed that these *CH x fragments subsequently undergo C—C coupling via a formate‐mediated pathway over Rh–Fe sites to produce ethanol. Notably, Li increased CO2 conversion (from <10.0% without Li) but had little influence on ethanol selectivity.

Zhang et al. developed an embedded Na–Rh zeolite silicate‐1 catalyst (Na–Rh@S‐1), which achieved 10.0% CO2 conversion, 24.0% ethanol selectivity, and an STY of 0.53 mmol g–1 h–1 at 250°C and 50 bar [45]. Embedding Na–Rh within the zeolite framework was essential to maintain catalyst stability and ethanol production. The authors emphasized the critical role of Na at a nominal loading of 0.2 wt%, as ethanol was not formed in its absence. H2 temperature‐programmed reduction (H2‐TPR), CO‐TPD, and XPS confirmed that Na moderated the hydrogenation ability of Rh and adjusted its electronic state, preventing full reduction and enabling the coexistence of Rh0 and Rh+ species (Scheme 5). This dual‐site environment is central to the proposed CO‐insertion pathway: Rh+ provides sites for nondissociative CO adsorption, while Rh0 facilitates hydrogenation and C—C coupling, as supported by in situ DRIFTS.

Na incorporation stabilizes a dual Rh+/Rh0 environment, where Rh+ enables nondissociative CO adsorption and Rh0 promotes hydrogenation and C—C coupling. The scheme illustrates the interplay between these sites in forming *CO, *CH x , and oxygenated intermediates that lead to ethanol, methanol, or methane formation. Reproduced with permission [45] 2021, American Chemical Society.

Tran and Kaliaguine showed that the preparation method strongly affects ethanol synthesis in Rh–FeO x –Na systems [46]. At 200°C and 10 bar, the catalyst prepared by incipient wetness impregnation (IWI) outperformed the coprecipitated analog, reaching 7.0% CO2 conversion, 91.0% ethanol selectivity in the liquid phase (11.0% in the gas phase), and an ethanol STY of 0.13 mmol g–1 h–1. Rh enhanced CO adsorption, thereby boosting CO2 conversion, alcohol selectivity, and yield, while Na promoted Fe carburization, as confirmed by X‐ray diffraction (XRD) and XPS. However, Na also reduced overall CO2 conversion and ethanol STY by suppressing H2 adsorption, which limited excessive hydrogenation. This effect was more pronounced when Na formed agglomerates rather than remaining well dispersed, underscoring the importance of the preparation method. The introduction of ZSM‐5 zeolite as a support further increased ethanol selectivity and raised the STY to 0.30 mmol g–1 h–1, with only minor influence on CO2 conversion. In situ DRIFTS confirmed *CO and *CH x as key intermediates for C2 formation, suggesting that the reaction followed a CO‐insertion pathway.

Ji et al. also identified Na and Fe as effective promoters by screening Rh‐based catalysts supported on CeO2[47]. Among the formulations tested, 2Rh0.5Fe0.5Na/CeO2 showed the best performance, achieving 13.0% CO2 conversion and 29.8% ethanol selectivity, corresponding to an ethanol STY of 2.33 mmol g–1 h–1 at 250°C and 30 bar. H2‐TPR indicated that Fe improved Rh dispersion and increased the number of available active sites, while CO‐TPD showed that Na enhanced Rh reduction and CO adsorption. In addition, Na increased the basicity of the system, improving CO2 adsorption and likely facilitating its dissociation. Based on in situ DRIFTS, the authors proposed that either *CO or HCOO* intermediates may couple with *CH x to form C2 species.

Beyond Na, the combined use of K and Fe promoters was studied by Goryachev et al. [162] The optimal K2Fe20Rh5/SiO2 catalyst achieved 18.4% CO2 conversion, 15.9% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 367.06 mmol g–1 h–1 at 250°C and 75 bar. The presence of both Fe and K was necessary, as Rh alone favored methanation. According to the authors, Rh activated H2, Fe prevented excessive methane formation and suppressed excessive CO2 dissociation by modifying the electronic state of Rh, while K stabilized *CO and limited overhydrogenation. The authors also proposed a CO‐insertion mechanism based mainly on product distribution trends and promoter effects, although direct spectroscopic or computational evidence for the key intermediates was not reported.

Chen et al. later prepared a series of Rh–Fe–K catalysts supported on TiO2 or CeO2 by deposition–precipitation (DP) or IWI and evaluated their performance under CO2 hydrogenation conditions at 200°C and 30 bar [48]. The TiO2‐supported catalyst prepared by DP was the most effective, reaching 8.6% CO2 conversion, 49.1% CO‐free ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 0.31 mmol g–1 h–1. In contrast, the IWI‐prepared catalyst showed higher methane selectivity, and the CeO2‐supported catalysts produced mainly methanol and methane. In situ DRIFTS helped rationalize these trends. Ethanol‐related bands were observed only for the TiO2‐DP catalyst, whereas no ethanol signals appeared on the CeO2‐supported system. On TiO2‐DP, ethanol features coexisted with formate and CO intermediates and only weak methoxy signals, leading the authors to propose a formate‐mediated pathway in which CO2 is hydrogenated to HCOO*, reduced to *CH2, and then combined with *CO to form a *CH2CO intermediate that further hydrogenates to ethanol. In contrast, the absence of ethanol bands on CeO2, together with intense methoxy and geminal dicarbonyl *CO features (two CO molecules bound to the same Rh atom), was interpreted as evidence that *CO is stabilized in a geometry that disfavors coupling with *CH2 and instead promotes further hydrogenation toward methanol and methane.

To support this picture, spin‐polarized DFT–PBE(D3) calculations were carried out on a Rh3Fe17 cluster supported on TiO2(110), systematically evaluating reaction energies and activation barriers for several hydrogenation and C—O cleavage steps in order to find the lowest‐energy pathway. The preferred route proceeds through HCOO*, HCOOH*, CH2O*, and CH2CO* as key intermediates. CO* hydrogenates more readily to HCOO* than to *COOH, and CH2O* is more likely to form CH2OH* than CH3O*, making C—O scission of CH2O‐derived species the most favorable route to a reduced C1 fragment. The key C—C coupling step involves *CH2 combining with *CO to generate CH2CO*, identifying *CH2 and *CO as the most favorable coupling pair on Rh–Fe alloy sites and indicating that methoxy‐derived *CH3 is not required as a precursor to ethanol. Each elementary hydrogenation step was treated as the transfer of an adsorbed H* formed by H2 dissociation on the bare cluster. However, this approach does not explicitly consider the hydrogen‐covered resting state of the catalyst, which may influence adsorption energies and barriers [170].

Pd‐Based Catalysts

Pd lies within the methanation‐selective region of the descriptor map (Scheme 2), where strong carbon and oxygen binding favors C—O scission. As a result, Pd‐based catalysts generally require alloying or support‐induced electronic modifications that moderate Pd's reactivity and stabilize *CO and *CH x species sufficiently to enable C—C coupling and compete with overhydrogenation [171].

Bai et al. reported ordered Pd2Cu nanoparticles supported on TiO2 P25, evaluated in a batch reactor at 200°C and 32 bar using water as solvent [49]. The catalyst achieved 92.0% ethanol selectivity with an ethanol STY of 41.50 mmol g–1 h–1. XPS and DRIFTS analyses showed that oxygen vacancies on TiO2 enhanced CO2 activation and adsorption of intermediates, while electronic interactions between Pd and Cu modulated the hydrogenation of *CO and tuned adsorption energies. Together, these effects shifted the reaction network toward oxygenate formation during CO2 hydrogenation.

Caparrós et al. examined a Pd single‐atom catalyst supported on Fe3O4 nanoparticles (0.1Pd/Fe3O4) in a fixed‐bed reactor at atmospheric pressure [50]. Although the CO2 conversion at 300°C was modest (0.3%), the catalyst delivered exceptionally high ethanol selectivity (97.5%) with an ethanol STY of 0.41 mmol g–1 h–1, producing only ethanol and methanol. The results indicated that Fe3O4 catalyzed the RWGS to generate *CO, while the Pd–Fe3O4 interface provided the environment required for C—C coupling, thereby enabling ethanol formation despite the low overall conversion.

Zhou et al. developed a potassium‐promoted Pd catalyst supported on iron oxide (0.3K–1Pd/Fe2O3), where Pd existed as PdO x and Pd–Fe alloy species [51]. At 320°C and 50 bar, the system achieved ∼28.0% CO2 conversion and 16.0% CO‐free higher‐alcohol selectivity, with ethanol as the primary alcohol. K promotion increased alcohol formation and suppressed methanation relative to unpromoted Pd/Fe2O3. In situ CO‐DRIFTS revealed that Pd–Fe alloy sites stabilized nondissociative *CO, while CO temperature‐programmed surface reaction equipped with a quadrupole mass spectrometer (CO‐TPSR‐MS) demonstrated that in situ‐formed iron carbide was responsible for dissociative *CO activation to yield *CH x intermediates.

Building on this approach, Wang et al. investigated Pd/Fe2O3 catalysts with higher Pd loadings, increasing it to 6.9 wt% [52]. After reduction, the catalyst contained Pd–Fe alloy particles coexisting with Fe3O4. At 300°C and 50 bar, it reached 33.3% CO2 conversion and 26.5% CO‐free higher‐alcohol selectivity, again with ethanol as the dominant product. The higher Pd loading substantially enhanced higher‐alcohol formation. RWGS activity was attributed to Pd–Fe alloy sites, while in situ DRIFTS suggested that C—C coupling occurred at interfaces between the alloy and in situ‐generated Fe5C2, which the authors identified as crucial for ethanol formation under these conditions.

Lou et al. prepared CeO2‐supported Pd dimers (Pd2/CeO2) featuring a Pd2O4 configuration (Figure 1a) and evaluated them at 240°C and 30 bar [53]. The catalyst achieved 9.2% CO2 conversion, 99.2% CO‐free ethanol selectivity, and a STY of 1.29 mmol g–1 h–1. Despite containing only 0.1 wt% Pd, the dimer catalyst substantially outperformed Pd nanoparticles (1.9 wt% Pd). In this system, CeO2 nanorods supplied oxygen vacancies that enhanced CO2 adsorption, while the Pd2O4 sites facilitated CO2 dissociation to *CO. The resulting *CO species bound strongly to the dimer sites, which prevented premature desorption and ensured that hydrogenation proceeded on the same surface ensemble. This tight coupling of adsorption, activation, and hydrogenation steps enabled *CO to undergo stepwise hydrogenation to *CH3, which then reacted with retained *CO to form the C—C bond leading to ethanol.

DFT + U calculations (PBE) elucidated the reaction mechanism. Two models were constructed: Pd2 anchored at an oxygen vacancy on CeO2(110), consistent with the experimentally observed Pd2O4 configuration, and Pd(111) as a reference for nanoparticle‐like behavior. The Pd2/CeO2(110) model reproduced EXAFS‐derived bond distances, confirming the model's reliability. On the dimer, CO2 activation proceeded through direct dissociation to CO with a barrier of 0.70 eV, which was considerably lower than the formate‐mediated route (>1.70 eV). The strongly bound *CO (desorption energy ∼ 2.9 eV) then hydrogenated to *CHO and *COH, ultimately generating *CH3 species that coupled with retained *CO to form ethanol. Theoretical calculations therefore identified a methanol‐mediated route (Figure 1b) in which *CH3 produced on the dimer couples directly with *CO, consistent with the experimental interpretation.

Chen et al. extended this concept by encapsulating Pd dimers supported on CeO2 within a hydrophobic silica shell (Pd2Ce@Si16), which maintained dimer stability by retaining water near the active sites (Scheme 6) [54]. At 240°C and 30 bar, the catalyst achieved 6.9% CO2 conversion, 97.8% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 0.09 mmol g–1 h–1. Although the STY decreased relative to the unencapsulated system, Pd2Ce@Si16 maintained stable performance for 60 h. The hydrophobic shell played a stabilizing role by trapping the water coproduct formed during the reaction, which inhibited Pd migration and preserved the dimer architecture under operating conditions.

Illustration of in situ‐generated water confined within a hydrophobic silica–shell nanoreactor, which stabilizes the Pd dimer sites. Reproduced with permission [54] 2023, American Chemical Society.

DFT calculations (same methodology as Ref. [53]) were performed on Pd2/CeO2(111) and Pd1/CeO2(111) models. In contrast to CeO2(110), where direct *CO2 dissociation was facile, the CeO2(111) surface required an H‐assisted *COOH pathway because direct dissociation had a significantly higher energy barrier (1.46 eV). Once *CO formed, its hydrogenation produced *CH3OH, which dissociated into *CH3 and *OH rather than desorbing, maintaining consistency with the C—C coupling mechanism previously proposed. Including adsorbed H2O and OH at the dimer site showed that water had little effect on the elementary surface steps but substantially increased the barrier for Pd migration. This result explained why confining water within the hydrophobic shell stabilized the Pd dimer and preserved ethanol selectivity during extended operation.

Ir‐Based Catalysts

Ir behaves similarly to Rh and Pd in the strong‐binding regime (Scheme 2), where C—O bond scission readily occurs and methane tends to dominate. Consequently, Ir‐based catalysts generally follow strategies that attenuate Ir's intrinsic reactivity and steer the surface chemistry toward controlled formation of C2 oxygenates rather than overhydrogenated products.

Nagata et al. investigated a bimetallic Ir–Mo/SiO2 catalyst and found that combining Ir with Mo significantly increased activity relative to either monometallic component under 220°C, 50 bar, and a H2/CO2 ratio of 2:1 [55]. The catalyst reached ∼15.0% CO2 conversion and 5.0% higher‐alcohol selectivity, with the best performance obtained at a Mo/Ir molar ratio of 0.3. In this system, Ir facilitated the reduction of MoO2 to Mo, which the authors associated with the observed activity enhancement. Introducing Li as an alkali promoter further raised higher‐alcohol selectivity to ∼8.0%, although CO2 conversion decreased slightly to ∼8.0%, and ethanol appeared as part of the higher‐alcohol fraction.

Ye et al. developed a bifunctional single‐atom Ir1–In2O3 catalyst in which isolated Ir atoms are anchored at oxygen vacancies of In2O3 [56]. The vacancy bound Irδ+ sites enhanced CO2 activation and stabilized *CO intermediates, as confirmed by in situ DRIFTS and Ar‐flushing experiments. Under 200°C and 60 bar in water, the catalyst achieved an extraordinary ethanol selectivity of 99.7% within the alcohol distribution and an ethanol STY of 0.99 mmol g–1 h–1. Meanwhile, In2O3 contributed by promoting methanol synthesis, which subsequently reacted with Ir‐bound *CO to form C—C coupled intermediates. Although reported without methodological details, DFT calculations indicated that CO2 binds more strongly on Ir1–In2O3 than on bare In2O3 and that the isolated Ir atom adjacent to an oxygen vacancy reduces the CO2 dissociation barrier from 0.77 to 0.48 eV. This stabilization of Ir–CO species provides the link between enhanced CO2 activation and efficient C—C coupling.

Au‐Based Catalysts

Au lies at the opposite end of the descriptor map from Ir, Rh, and Pd (Scheme 2). It is positioned in the weak‐binding, methanol‐selective region, where *CO adsorption is too weak for C—O cleavage. As a result, ethanol‐forming Au catalysts typically rely on supports or cluster architectures that introduce more reactive sites capable of stabilizing *CO and *CH x intermediates sufficiently to promote C—C coupling.

Wang et al. evaluated Au nanoclusters supported on anatase TiO2 for ethanol formation under 200°C and 60 bar using DMF as solvent [57]. The system delivered 99.0% ethanol selectivity in the alcohol fraction with an ethanol STY of 2.83 mmol g–1 h–1. TiO2 contributed through its oxygen vacancies, which improved CO2 adsorption and activation, as evidenced by ultraviolet–visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS) and DRIFTS. Au was proposed to catalyze the C—C coupling step between *CO and *CH3, not directly demonstrated in this study but consistent with previously reported Au–TiO2 mechanisms.

A second strategy for enhancing Au reactivity is to exploit atomically precise Au clusters. These clusters exhibit a nonmetallic, molecular‐like behavior, with discrete energy levels that can shift the Au d‐band center upward and strengthen adsorption of *CO and *CH x species. Yang et al. demonstrated this concept using Au11 clusters, which catalyzed CO2 hydrogenation to ethanol with >80.0% CO‐free selectivity at 120°C and 30 bar [58]. Under reaction conditions, partial ligand removal exposed active Au sites that stabilized *COOH, *CO, and *CH2. In situ IR spectroscopy identified *CO as the central intermediate that couples with *CH2 to form a C2 species, which then hydrogenates to form ethanol.

DFT–PBE(D3) calculations on Au11/ZrO2(111) supported this mechanism: CO2 is sequentially reduced to *COOH and *CO, followed by a nearly barrierless coupling between *CO and *CH2 (0.24 eV). The subsequent hydrogenation step via *CH2OH formation was identified as the rate‐limiting step (0.60 eV), while pathways to methane or methanol required higher barriers (>0.80 eV). Importantly, the high selectivity of Au11 was not shared by other clusters. Au9, despite also being nonmetallic, bound intermediates too strongly, shifting hydrogenation toward CH4. Au36, in contrast, bound species too weakly and released formate prematurely, favoring HCOOH formation. These comparisons show that Au11 occupies a narrow electronic–geometric window where nonmetallic character, intermediate binding, and accessible coupling geometry coincide to make C—C coupling thermodynamically and kinetically favorable.

Ru‐Based Catalysts

Ru occupies the same strong‐binding, methane‐selective region as Rh and Ir, meaning that ethanol formation requires strategies that avoid deep hydrogenation while preserving pathways to *CO and *CH x intermediates. Zhou et al. [59] addressed this challenge by developing a Ru/In2O3–ZnO2 catalyst in which steam was introduced as a mild oxidant to counteract the strong reducing effect of Ru. Without steam, Ru readily reduces In2O3–x to inactive Ru–In alloys; maintaining an oxidized In2O3–x phase is therefore essential. Operando X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) confirmed that, under stabilized conditions, In2O3 remained largely unreduced, Ru stayed metallic, and no Ru–In alloy was formed. This preservation of the Ru–In2O3–x interface was proposed to be crucial for ethanol formation.

To further enhance selectivity, the authors encapsulated the catalyst within an imine‐based porous organic polymer (IPOP), creating a final IPOP/Ru/In2O3–ZnO system. Under 250°C, 6 bar, and 10.0% steam, the catalyst achieved ∼1.0% CO2 conversion, 70.0% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 2.83 mmol gRu –1 h–1. The porous polymer shell was proposed to exert a confinement effect, increasing the residence time of intermediates and thereby enhancing the probability of C—C coupling between *CO and *CH x species. The improved ethanol selectivity, combined with the spectroscopic evidence confirming preservation of the active interface, supports the conclusion that both interface chemistry and nanoscale confinement are key contributors to the observed performance.

Homogeneous Catalysts

Ru‐based homogeneous catalysts are widely used across organic transformations because Ru can access up to eleven oxidation states, giving it notable electronic flexibility [172]. This versatility allows Ru complexes to accommodate diverse ligand environments, including phosphines and carbonyls, and to adapt their electronic structure under reaction conditions [173]. As a result, Ru complexes have been applied to C—C coupling, CO2 activation, and numerous hydrogenation reactions.

Tominaga et al. reported the first homogeneous catalytic hydrogenation of CO2 to ethanol using Ru3(CO)12–KI as the main catalyst and Co2(CO)12 as a cocatalyst at 200°C and 117.6 bar, with a H2CO2 ratio of 5:1 in 1,3‐dimethyl‐2‐imidazolidinone (DMI) [60]. This system delivered an ethanol selectivity of 18.8% and a yield of 12.33 mmol L–1 h–1. The cobalt cocatalyst significantly increased ethanol formation, whereas Ru3(CO)12 alone was primarily active for methanol synthesis. From product distributions and time‐course measurements, the authors proposed that CO2 is first reduced to methanol on the Ru complex and that the Co complex then promotes methanol homologation to ethanol.

In a related study, Tominaga et al. introduced one of the earliest examples of ethanol synthesis via direct methanol homologation using CO2 and H2 [61]. A Ru–Co bimetallic system composed of Ru3(CO)12 and Co2(CO)8 with LiI in DMI at 180°C, 121.6 bar, and a H2:CO2 ratio of 5:1, reached ethanol yields up to 32.0% based on methanol. Reaction‐profile analysis and the absence of acetate species indicated that CO2 is first reduced to CO, methanol reacts with iodide to generate CH3I, and CO inserts into the Co—CH3 bond to form an acetyl species that hydrogenates to acetaldehyde and subsequently to ethanol. These findings reinforced the complementary roles of Ru in facilitating the RWGS step and Co in driving the homologation sequence.

Building on this foundation, Qian et al. developed a homogeneous system based on Ru3(CO)12/Rh2(CO)4Cl2 (with a Ru:Rh ratio of 28.2:51.5, in mmol of metal), promoted by LiI in DMI at 200°C and 80 bar with a H2:CO2 ratio of 1:1 [62]. The system achieved an ethanol selectivity within the alcohol fraction of 47.3% and an ethanol STY of 3.05 mmol L–1 h–1. LiI promoted both alcohol formation and catalyst stability, while DMI further stabilized the active species. Tracer experiments indicated that methanol functioned as an intermediate leading to ethanol and that ethanol itself continued to homologate under reaction conditions.

The same group replaced Rh with Co, using Ru3(CO)12/Co4(CO)12 (Ru:Co = 2:1) combined with bis(triphenylphosphine)iminium chloride (PPNCl) as cocatalyst, LiBr as promoter, and DMI as solvent [63]. Conducted at 200°C and 90 bar with a H2:CO2 ratio of 2:1, this system achieved an ethanol selectivity of 87.5% within the alcohol fraction and an ethanol STY of 14.75 mmol L–1 h–1, markedly higher than the Ru/Rh system. Screening of halides showed that LiBr substantially accelerated alcohol formation. The authors proposed that chloride (supplied by PPNCI) coordination to Ru3(CO)12 increases electron density at Ru, facilitating oxidative addition of the alkyl halide. According to the mechanism outlined in Scheme 7, CO2 is first reduced to CO and methanol (step 1); methanol then forms CH3Br via substitution with LiBr (step 2); oxidative addition of CH3Br to Ru produces a CH3Br–Ru species (step 3); carbonyl insertion gives an acetyl intermediate (step 4); and stepwise hydrogenation yields acetaldehyde and ethanol (step 5). Co was suggested to accelerate the overall process, although its precise role remains less well defined.

Proposed reaction mechanism for ethanol synthesis catalyzed by Ru3(CO)12/Co4(Co)12. Reproduced and adapted with permission [63] 2016, Royal Society of Chemistry.

Wang et al. expanded these concepts by performing direct methanol homologation using methanol, CO2, and H2 [64]. Using [RuCl2(CO)3]2/Co2(CO)12 with LiI in N‐ethyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NEP), the reaction at 160°C and 80 bar (H2:CO2 = 3:1) gave an ethanol selectivity of 65%. Isotope labeling and comparisons against single‐metal controls confirmed a tandem sequence in which Ru catalyzes the RWGS step to generate CO, methanol reacts with iodide to form CH3I, and CO inserts into a CH3—Co bond to yield acetaldehyde and then ethanol.

In parallel, Bediako et al. performed methanol homologation using methanol, CO2, and H2 at 160°C and 90 bar (H2:CO2 = 2:1) in 1‐butyl‐3‐methylimidazolium chloride ([bmim][Cl]) [65]. A catalytic system based on Ru3(CO)12 with LiI and LiCl reached an ethanol selectivity of 51.5% and an ethanol yield of 32.0% based on methanol conversion. Methanol was essential because only trace ethanol formed when CO2 and H2 were used without it. The ionic liquid also played a central role; the [bmim]+ cation stabilized the Ru complex, while Cl– and I– promoted methyl‐halide formation. The Ru catalyst, together with Li salts, facilitated RWGS and supplied CO; the methyl halides underwent oxidative addition, followed by carbonyl insertion and hydrogenation to generate ethanol (Scheme 8).

Steps 2–3: Methanol reacts with LiI/LiCl to generate methyl halides (CH3X). Step 4: Oxidative addition of CH3X to Ru gives Ru—CH3 species. Step 5: CO insertion into the Ru—CH3 bond forms an acyl (CH3CO–Ru) intermediate. Step 6: Successive hydrogenation of the acyl intermediate forms acetaldehyde and then ethanol. Taken together, these studies demonstrate complementary strategies to promote methanol‐mediated ethanol synthesis. Wang et al. relied on Ru–Co synergy to divide the RWGS and homologation functions between two metals, whereas Bediako et al. showed that ionic liquids can stabilize Ru species and generate the reactive methyl‐halide intermediates needed for C—C coupling in the absence of a second metal. Both approaches converge on the same fundamental sequence and highlight how ligand effects, solvent environments, and metal–metal cooperation can shape homogeneous CO2‐to‐ethanol catalysis.

Nonprecious‐Metal Catalysts

This section first examines monometallic Cu‐, Fe‐, and Co‐based systems, followed by bimetallic variants such as Cu–Fe and Cu–Co alloys, and finally tandem catalysts. Within each category, metals are presented in order of their prevalence in the literature.

Cu‐Based Catalysts

Cu is a 3d transition metal with versatile catalytic behavior due to its accessible oxidation states (0, 1, and 2), which enable both one‐ and two‐electron pathways across a wide range of industrial reactions [174, 175]. Within the descriptor map, Cu falls in the weak‐binding, methanol‐selective region (Scheme 2), where C—O bond cleavage is difficult and methanol formation is typically favored. As a result, ethanol‐selective Cu catalysts are designed to increase Cu's reactivity so that C—O activation and CH x —CO coupling can compete successfully with methanol formation [176, 177].

A notable illustration of this design strategy is the Cu+ center embedded in a Zr12‐based metal–organic framework (MOF) (Zr12‐bpdc) reported by An et al. [98], with the Cs‐modified material (Zr12‐bpdc‐Cu) providing the best performance. In a batch reactor at 100°C and 20 bar using anhydrous THF, this catalyst achieved 96.0% CO2 conversion and 99.0% ethanol selectivity across all phases, corresponding to an ethanol yield of 87.90 mmol g–1 h–1. Methanol and other oxygenates appeared only in trace amounts (<1.0%), and selectivity depended strongly on the alkali promoter, with Cs giving the highest activity. In this system, Cu sites enabled H2 activation and CO2 hydrogenation to CHO* and *CH3 intermediates, which subsequently underwent C—C coupling. The authors proposed that Cu+ sites promote direct C—C coupling, Cs+ stabilizes *CO and *CH3, and the MOF structure prevents Cu aggregation. Their mechanistic proposal, shown in Scheme 9, involves bimetallic H2 activation at a Cu+ dimer, CO2 hydrogenation to methanol and to a Cu‐bound formyl species, and formyl–methanol coupling, with Cs+ stabilizing the formyl intermediate. DFT–B3LYP calculations on a truncated Zr12 cluster supported this view, showing that Cs+ enhances electron density at the Cu+ dimer and lowers the barrier for formyl formation. Notably, this mechanism differs from classical CO‐insertion routes and instead relies on coupling between formyl and methanol.

Proposed reaction pathway for methanol and ethanol formation over the Zr12‐bpdc‐Cu catalyst. Reproduced with permission [98] 2019, Springer Nature.

A different strategy was pursued by Ding et al., who developed the Cu@Na‐Beta system, in which 2–5 nm Cu nanoparticles were embedded within a crystalline Na‐Beta zeolite [99]. At 300°C and 21 bar, the catalyst achieved 7.9% CO2 conversion, 100% CO‐free ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 8.65 mmol g–1 h–1. Here, Cu sites converted CO2 and surface methyl species into CH3COO*, which then hydrogenated to ethanol. At the same time, the confinement within the zeolite suppressed side reactions, preventing methanol and acetic acid formation and allowing the catalyst to remain stable for 100 h.

Iltsiou et al. investigated Cu nanoparticles embedded in mesoporous zeolites under aqueous conditions at 200°C and 40 bar [100]. Among the materials tested, Cu@m‐Beta delivered the highest performance, reaching 17.0% CO2 conversion, 68.0% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 0.39 mmol g–1 h–1. Isotope‐labeling with methanol‐d 4 confirmed that methanol acted as an intermediate that subsequently homologated to ethanol. In this context, mesoporosity improved mass transport, while encapsulation stabilized Cu nanoparticles and suppressed secondary pathways. Water further assisted this methanol‐mediated route, reinforcing the overall contribution of the zeolite environment to ethanol formation.

Alloying strategies have also expanded the landscape of Cu‐based systems. Zhang et al. [101] developed a Ga‐promoted Cu–Co catalyst in which a Ga/Co ratio of 0.4 yielded the optimal composition. Operated at 220°C and 30 bar, the catalyst achieved 17.8% CO2 conversion, 23.8% ethanol selectivity, and an STY of 1.35 mmol g–1 h–1. Structural analyses showed that Ga incorporation strengthened Cu–Co electronic interactions, generating Cu0/Cu+–CoGaO x interfacial sites. These CoGaO x species introduced oxygen vacancies that enhanced CO2 activation (as confirmed by CO2‐TPD and CO2‐DRIFTS), while Cu+ sites stabilized *CO (supported by CO‐TPD). In situ DRIFTS detected *CH x and *C2H5O species, consistent with a CO‐insertion pathway (Scheme 10).

Proposed reaction pathway for methanol and ethanol formation over the Cu–Co catalyst. Reproduced with permission [101] 2022, American Chemical Society.

Shao et al. explored Mn as an alternative promoter in a CuCoMn/Al2O3 catalyst [102]. Although this system performed well for CO hydrogenation at 260°C and 34 bar with a H2/CO2 ratio of 2:1, its activity and selectivity declined sharply when CO2 was used as the sole carbon source. This contrast indicated that the catalyst favored CO insertion over direct CO2 activation.

Potassium promotion provides another route for tuning Cu‐based systems. Zhang et al. [103] reported that introducing 3.0 wt% into a Cu/CoO x catalyst increased CO2 conversion to 4.9%, ethanol selectivity to 38.8%, and the ethanol STY to 0.61 mmol g–1 h–1 at 200°C and 30 bar. DRIFTS and TPSR‐MS showed that K promotion generated Co2+–OV–Co2+, Co–O–K, and Cu+–O–K surface motifs, which enhanced CO2 adsorption and stabilized HCOO* and *CH x intermediates. Complementary DFT–PBE(D3) calculations on two interfacial heterostructures, Cu2O(111)/Co3O4(311) and K–Cu2O(111)/Co3O4(311), showed that K+ strengthens formate binding by allowing HCOO to bridge Cu+ and K+, lowering the barrier for hydrogenation and enabling its coupling with *CH x to form acetate, which subsequently hydrogenates to ethanol.

Although CO2‐to‐ethanol typically requires pressures of 10–90 bar, several recent studies have explored Cu‐based systems capable of operating at atmospheric pressure [178]. These efforts have focused on how water, Cu oxidation states, and support interactions can compensate for the lower driving force and promote ethanol formation under mild conditions. For instance, da Silva et al. demonstrated that adding water to the feed (H2:CO2:H2O = 1:1:1) enabled ethanol production even under atmospheric pressure [104]. At 190°C, the system generated approximately 2.00 × 10–3 mmol g–1 h–1 ethanol with 84.0% selectivity, although CO2 conversion remained below 0.1%. According to the authors, water played several roles (Scheme 11): it stabilized *CO, promoted C—C coupling, and acted as a hydrogen donor, while its presence gradually oxidized Cu over time [104, 178].

Proposed reaction pathway illustrating (i) water adsorption and (ii) CO2 hydrogenation on Cu0/Cu+ sites. Reproduced with permission [104] 2023, Elsevier.

Caldeira et al. extended this concept by examining CO2 hydrogenation with water steam (CO2:H2O = 1:1) at 200°C and atmospheric pressure [105]. This system achieved an ethanol STY of 0.78 × 10–3 mmol g–1 h–1, producing ethanol as the exclusive carbon‐containing product. In contrast, performing CO2 hydrogenation with H2 alone (H2:CO2 = 1:1) led primarily to methanol. The authors attributed the lower STY relative to da Silva et al. (2.00 × 10–3 mmol g–1 h–1) to their catalyst's smaller surface area (12 vs. 21 m2 g–1). Ethanol formation again correlated with Cu oxidation, as steam converted Cu0 to Cu2O and CuO. CO‐DRIFTS confirmed stronger CO binding on oxidized Cu species, supporting their role in stabilizing key intermediates, while redox cycling likely contributed to H2 activation.

Complementing these findings, de Paula et al. [106] investigated Cu supported on the zirconium‐based MOF UiO‐67, leveraging its high surface area to disperse up to 20.0 wt% Cu. At 260°C, atmospheric pressure, and an H2:CO2 ratio of 3:1, the catalyst achieved ∼4.0% CO2 conversion, <1.0% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of ∼1.50 × 10–2 mmol g–1 h–1. Despite the higher STY compared with steam‐assisted systems, the catalyst primarily promoted the RWGS reaction and showed limited intrinsic ethanol‐forming ability. Even so, the study highlighted that maintaining Cu loadings below 20.0 wt% is important for preserving dispersion and activity at low pressure.

Collectively, these studies show that Cu‐based catalysts are capable of operating under both conventional and mild conditions, though ethanol selectivity at low pressure remains modest. Nonetheless, the strong sensitivity to promoters, oxidized Cu species, and solvent or steam environments points to promising design strategies for developing Cu catalysts capable of ethanol formation under lower energy conditions.

Fe‐Based Catalysts

Iron is well known for its central role in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, where its strong affinity for carbon and oxygen favors deep reduction of C1 intermediates and typically leads to CH4 or higher hydrocarbons. Consistent with this behavior, Fe also displays high activity in the direct hydrogenation of CO2, often producing alkenes or methane through pathways dominated by the RWGS reaction [179, 180]. Ethanol‐selective Fe catalysts therefore require strategies that moderate this intrinsic tendency toward over‐reduction and promote controlled C—C coupling.

The most relevant Fe‐based systems for CO2 hydrogenation to ethanol have been developed in continuous‐flow reactors. Huang et al. demonstrated that Mn–K copromotion on iron carbide can redirect Fe reactivity toward alcohol formation [107]. At 300°C and 30 bar, the optimized 10Mn1K–FeC catalyst achieved 40.5% CO2 conversion with 7.0% ethanol selectivity in a fixed‐bed reactor. Mechanistically, the reaction was proposed to begin with RWGS, and in situ DRIFTS revealed CH x O* species on the Mn‐K‐modified carbide surface, while CO2‐TPD showed that K improved CO2 adsorption. DRIFTS further detected *CH x , indicating that *CH x fragments couple with CH x O* intermediates to form C2 products.

Wang et al. advanced these ideas by developing Na–ZnFe@C, a Na–ZnO x –Fe5C2–Fe3O4 catalyst coated with a graphene‐like carbon layer [108]. Operated at 320°C and 50 bar, this system delivered 38.4% CO2 conversion, 20.3% CO‐free ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 3.44 mmol g–1 h–1, substantially outperforming analogous catalysts without the carbon layer (9.0% of CO‐free ethanol selectivity and an STY of 1.47 mmol g–1 h–1). The carbon layer played a central electronic role: by functioning as an electron buffer, it prevented excessive charge transfer from ZnO to the Fe phases, a process that would otherwise strengthen *CO binding and increase overhydrogenation. This moderated *CO adsorption and helped maintain a favorable balance between *CO and *CH x intermediates. In situ DRIFTS detected formate, *CH x , and *CO species, supporting a CO‐insertion mechanism in which *CO generated on Fe3O4 couples with *CH2 produced on Fe5C2 to form *CH2CO, which then hydrogenates to ethanol. DFT–PBE calculations comparing Fe5C2(510)/C, ZnO–Fe5C2(510), and ZnO–Fe5C2(510)/C models corroborated this mechanistic proposal. In particular, Bader analysis showed that ZnO–Fe5C2 alone accumulates excess charge and binds *CO too strongly, whereas Fe5C2(510)/C binds *CO too weakly. Only ZnO–Fe5C2(510)/C provided intermediate *CO binding and a very low barrier (0.29 eV) for *CH2–*CO coupling, consistent with the experimentally observed ethanol selectivity trends.

Yao et al. further examined how alkali and sulfate promoters can tune Fe‐based catalysts and identified Na–S–Fe as the optimal system [109, 110]. Prior to reaction, the dominant phase was Fe2O3, while Fe3O4 and Fe5C2 emerged under reaction conditions. At 320°C and 30 bar, this catalyst achieved 32.0% CO2 conversion, 15.9% CO‐free higher‐alcohol selectivity, and an alcohol STY of 78.50 mg g–1 h–1, with ethanol comprising more than 70.0% of the alcohol fraction. Na and S acted cooperatively, balancing CO dissociation and nondissociative CO activation while suppressing excessive hydrogenation. Interestingly, the identity of the alkali cation strongly influenced the spent‐catalyst phase distribution: heavier alkali metals favored Fe5C2, whereas lighter ones stabilized Fe3O4. Because Fe3O4 is active for RWGS while Fe5C2 promotes C—C coupling, this promoter‐dependent phase composition played a decisive role in alcohol formation.

To rationalize the effect of sulfate, DFT–PBE(D3) calculations were performed on Fe5C2(510) slabs modified with Na alone or with both Na and S. Adsorbed CO (representing coverage generated by RWGS) was compared on the two surfaces. The Gibbs adsorption energy of *CO was 0.8 eV stronger on FeNaS than on FeNa, indicating that sulfate stabilizes *CO and increases its surface coverage, thereby improving the likelihood of coupling with *CH x fragments. Examination of the hydrogenation sequence from *CO to ethanol (via *COH, *HCOH, and *H2COH) revealed that the first hydrogenation step was endothermic on FeNa but became exothermic with sulfate comodification. As a result, the overall reaction for forming ethanol from *CO and *CH2 decreased from 1.60 eV on FeNa to 0.73 eV on FeNaS. This substantial thermodynamic improvement shows that Na–S co‐modification not only stabilizes *CO but also lowers the energy penalties associated with its stepwise hydrogenation and subsequent C—C coupling, consistent with the experimentally observed enhancement in alcohol selectivity.

Bimetallic Cu–Fe Catalysts

Cu and Fe display complementary behavior in CO2 hydrogenation. Cu binds carbon and oxygen relatively weakly and typically favors methanol formation, whereas Fe binds them much more strongly and drives CH4 or longer‐chain hydrocarbons through Fischer–Tropsch‐like chemistry. Cu–Fe bimetallic catalysts are therefore designed to combine these tendencies, using Fe‐rich domains to activate CO2 and Cu‐rich environments to moderate overhydrogenation and promote oxygenate formation. All systems discussed in this section have been evaluated in continuous reactors.

Li et al. [111] investigated a Cu–Zn–Fe–K catalyst for higher‐alcohol synthesis. The optimal composition, CuZnFe0.5K0.15, achieved 42.3% CO2 conversion and 36.7% higher‐alcohol selectivity at 300°C and 60 bar in a fixed‐bed reactor, with ethanol as the dominant alcohol. In this system, Fe and K both played key roles. K increased surface basicity, improving CO adsorption and facilitating C—C coupling, while Fe enhanced Cu dispersion and formed iron carbides under reaction conditions, as confirmed by XRD. These carbides, known active phases for chain growth via CO‐insertion, were proposed to contribute directly to higher‐alcohol formation. Their importance was evident from comparison with a Fe‐free catalyst, where CO2 conversion dropped from 42.3% to 25.1% and alcohol selectivity from 36.7% to 10.9%.

Using a related design approach, Xu et al. developed a Cs‐promoted Cu–Fe–Zn catalyst (Cs–C0.8F1.0Z1.0) to probe the role of Cs on Cu–Fe systems [112]. Cs weakened H2 adsorption, which did not significantly change CO2 conversion but did enhance alcohol formation. In a fixed‐bed reactor at 330°C and 50 bar, this catalyst reached 36.6% CO2 conversion and 19.8% higher‐alcohol selectivity, again with ethanol as the main alcohol. In situ DRIFTS indicated that Cu–ZnO catalyzes RWGS to generate CO, while Cu–Fe7C3 interfacial sites (identified by XRD and TPSR) activated such CO and produced C2 intermediates detected in DRIFTS. On this basis, and supported by previous studies, the authors proposed a CO‐mediated pathway (Scheme 12).

Proposed mechanisms of CO2 hydrogenation over the Cs‐promoted Cu–Fe–Zn catalyst. Reproduced with permission [112] 2020, American Chemical Society.

In a subsequent study, Xu et al. developed a K‐promoted Cu–Mg–Zn–Fe multifunctional catalyst (4.6K–CuMgZnFe). At 320°C and 50 bar, this material achieved 30.4% CO2 conversion, 22.8% CO‐free higher‐alcohol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 1.32 mmol g–1 h–1[113]. In situ DRIFTS and TPSR showed that K promoted the RWGS reaction, strengthened linearly adsorbed *CO on Cu sites, and suppressed excessive hydrogenation. These effects helped balance *CH x and *CO coverages, which favored their coupling toward higher alcohols.

In line with this, Liu et al. prepared a K‐promoted FeCu/a‐ZrO2 catalyst that achieved 32.5% CO2 conversion, 29.0% CO‐free higher‐alcohol selectivity, and a higher‐alcohol STY of 125.00 mg gcat –1 h–1 at 320°C and 40 bar, with ethanol representing ∼60.0% of the alcohol distribution [114]. Here, K generated oxygen vacancies in ZrO2 (as indicated by O 1s XPS) and enhanced CO activation, as shown by in situ CO‐DRIFTS. K also suppressed overhydrogenation and stabilized Cu–Fe5C2 interfaces, at which DRIFTS detected CO‐derived and C2 oxygenated intermediates consistent with a CO‐insertion pathway.

Du et al. further extended this system by incorporating Zn, yielding a KFeCuZn/ZrO2 catalyst that reached 52.5% CO2 conversion and 16.5% ethanol selectivity at 360°C and 40 bar [115]. In this catalyst, each metal contributed a distinct function that collectively shaped the reaction pathway. K promoted CO2 activation, as evidenced by DRIFTS, while Cu facilitated nondissociative *CO activation. Fe carbide species, identified by XRD and linked to C—C coupling through C2 intermediates detected by in situ DRIFTS, provided the chain‐growth capability. Zn, in turn, enhanced thermal stability and mitigated Cu sintering, as shown by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Together, these effects created a balanced catalytic environment that steered the reaction toward higher‐alcohol formation.

Beyond alkali and Zn promotion, Wang et al. investigated sulfate promotion in a K‐promoted CuFeZn catalyst [116]. The resulting 0.6S‐KCFZ formulation delivered 36.1% CO2 conversion, 20.2% CO‐free higher‐alcohol selectivity, and a higher‐alcohol STY of 50.70 mg gcat –1 h–1 at 320°C and 50 bar, with ethanol again the most abundant alcohol. Sulfate enhanced higher‐alcohol synthesis by balancing dissociative and nondissociative CO activation (as evidenced by CO‐TPSR) and by increasing H2 adsorption, as shown by H2‐TPR. When combined with a CuZnAlZr promoter phase, the higher‐alcohol STY increased further, indicating a synergistic interaction between sulfate and the mixed‐oxide support.

For the same CuFeZn core, Yang et al. examined the influence of N‐doping via 2,6‐pyridine dicarboxylic acid (PDA). The optimized CuFeZn–0.7PDA catalyst attained a CO2 conversion of 10.6% and an ethanol selectivity of 27.0% at 310°C and 40 bar, with ethanol as the dominant product [117]. XPS and XRD showed that PDA increased oxygen‐vacancy concentrations, which, in turn, facilitated CO2 activation. Based on these results and previous studies on Cu‐ZnO and Fe5C2, the authors proposed that CO forms and dissociates at Cu–Zn–Fe interfacial sites and inserts into *CH x species, giving rise to C2 products.

Building on this approach, Jia et al. developed a related N‐doped CuFeZn catalyst, PDA/CFZ–N450 (calcined at 450°C) [118]. Tested at 320°C and 40 bar, the material delivered ∼7.0% CO2 conversion, 39.8% higher‐alcohol selectivity, and a higher‐alcohol STY of 55.93 mg mLcat –1 h–1, with ethanol accounting for 80.0% of the alcohol fraction. The authors attributed this performance to the stabilizing influence of N‐doping. In particular, oxygen vacancies in ZrO2 stabilized N species, which, in turn, stabilized both the active metals and key reaction intermediates under reaction conditions. This stabilization effect was consistent with the observed linear correlation between nitrogen content and C2+ selectivity. According to the authors, Cu activates and dissociates H2, the Cu–ZnO interface promotes CO insertion, and Fe sites mediate C—C coupling, together leading to an enhanced formation of higher alcohols.

Zhang et al. investigated chromium promotion in a CuFe catalyst for CO2 hydrogenation to higher alcohols [119]. The Cr(1%)–CuFe catalyst achieved 38.4% CO2 conversion, 29.2% CO‐free higher‐alcohol selectivity, and a higher‐alcohol STY of 104.10 mg gcat –1 h–1 at 320°C and 40 bar, with ethanol constituting nearly 80.0% of the alcohol fraction. To clarify how Cr modifies the Cu–Fe interface, DFT–PBE calculations were performed on Fe5C2 clusters supported on Cu(111) (CuFe) and on the same interface decorated with CrO3 motifs (Cr–CuFe). The analysis considered CO2 and H2 adsorption, H2 dissociation, *CO dissociation, and *CH x —*CO coupling. CO2 adsorption was slightly weaker on Cr–CuFe (–0.44 eV) than on CuFe (–0.58 eV), in line with TPD measurements, while H2 adsorption strengthened and its dissociation barrier decreased (0.24 vs. 0.37 eV). Moreover, *CO dissociation became less favorable on Cr–CuFe (2.34 eV) relative to CuFe (1.22 eV), which suppressed carbide formation. In contrast, *CH x —*CO coupling to form acetate intermediates proceeded more readily on Cr–CuFe (1.54 eV vs. 1.99 eV). These trends indicate that trace Cr shifts the mechanism away from CO dissociation and toward nondissociative CO pathways that favor oxygenates. In situ DRIFTS supported this interpretation by showing acetate and acetaldehyde species on Cr–CuFe, whereas CH x bands dominated on the undoped catalyst.

He et al. examined La promotion in CuFeO x and found that La significantly enhanced ethanol synthesis at 320°C and 30 bar [120]. The La(5%)–CuFeO x catalyst achieved 39.8% CO2 conversion, 33.8% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol yield of 13.5%. Energy‐dispersive X‐ray elemental mapping of high‐angle annular dark‐field (HAADF) STEM images showed that La improved the dispersion of Cu and Fe, correlating with enhanced chain‐growth capacity. In situ XRD and TPR indicated that La lowered the CuO reduction temperature, while XPS indicated a higher Cu0 fraction, consistent with improved H2 activation. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and XPS also showed increased oxygen‐vacancy concentrations with La addition, and CO2 conversion scaled linearly with vacancy density, in agreement with stronger CO2 adsorption observed by TPD and FTIR. In situ FTIR under CO2/H2 and CO‐TPSR‐MS identified HCOO*, *CH x , and acetyl intermediates characteristic of a CO‐insertion mechanism. DFT–PBE calculations on Cu/Fe5C2(510) and La2O3–Cu/Fe5C2(510) slabs supported this picture. Bader charge analysis indicated electron transfer from Fe to La, which lowered the electron density on Fe and shifted *CO binding from bridged to linear configurations at Cu sites. This redistribution balanced dissociative and nondissociative CO activation, leading to compatible rates of *CH x and CHO* formation and helping explain the ethanol selectivity observed for the catalyst.

Ma et al. investigated Ga promotion (1.1 wt% Ga) in an FeCuGaZn catalyst [121]. At 340°C and 50 bar, the material achieved 26.0% CO2 conversion, 23.1% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 1.93 mmol g–1 h–1. Under reaction conditions, Fe3O4 partially carburized to Fe5C2, as confirmed ex situ by XRD and XPS. DFT–PBE calculations compared Cu–FeGaO x and Cu–Fe3O4 interfaces and found a 0.21 eV difference in *CO adsorption energies, indicating that Ga modifies interfacial CO activation. Mechanistically, RWGS was assigned to Cu–MO x /Fe3O4 sites, while nondissociative *CO activation occurred at Cu–FeGaO x and *CO dissociation to *CH x proceeded on Fe5C2. DRIFTS detected sequential formation of *CO, *CH x , *CH3COO, and *CH3CHO species on Ga‐promoted catalysts, consistent with ethanol formation via *CO insertion into *CH x . In contrast, the Ga‐free FeCuZn catalyst favored deeper *CO dissociation and *CH x coupling, reducing ethanol selectivity.

Kostyniuk and Likozar developed a 4%Cs/25%Cu–25%Zn/50%Fe catalyst that achieved 17.0% CO2 conversion, 30.0% CO‐free ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 5.00 mmol g–1 h–1 in a parallel packed‐bed reactor operated between 200°C and 300°C at 20 bar [25]. Rather than assigning distinct roles to each metal, the authors attributed the performance to composite interfaces. In situ DRIFTS indicated that formate‐related intermediates formed predominantly on Cu–Zn domains, while CO‐derived intermediates accumulated on Cu–Fe interfaces. On this basis, they proposed a tandem mechanism in which formate‐mediated and CO‐insertion pathways operate simultaneously, with Cs tuning interfacial properties and stabilizing key intermediates.

Si et al. used physical sputtering to deposit Cu nanoparticles onto Na‐modified Fe3O4[122]. At 310°C and 30 bar, this material achieved 32.3% CO2 conversion, ∼10.0% CO‐free ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 3.32 mmol g–1 h–1. In situ DRIFTS and XPS showed that CO2 was first reduced to CO on Fe oxides, followed by C—C coupling on Fe5C2 sites and CO insertion at adjacent Cu interfaces to form ethanol. Na promotion enhanced Fe carburization and stabilized oxygen vacancies, whereas sputtered Cu nanoparticles improved CO activation and CO insertion.

Liu et al. studied the influence of supports on Na‐promoted Cu–Fe catalysts by comparing ZnO, ZrO2, In2O3, Al2O3, and CeO2[123]. ZnO emerged as the optimal support, with NaCuFeZn attaining 28.3% CO2 conversion and 17.2% ethanol selectivity at 360°C and 50 bar. CO2‐TPD showed that Na increased basicity and CO2 adsorption, while O 1s XPS, XRD, and TEM indicated that ZnO supplied oxygen vacancies, dispersed Cu and Fe species, and promoted FeC x formation. In situ DRIFTS signals corresponding to C2 intermediates, together with earlier mechanistic studies, pointed to such FeC x phases as key contributors to C—C coupling and selective ethanol synthesis.

Xiang et al. examined K promotion in FeCuAl catalysts and identified 1.6 wt% K as the optimal loading for ethanol synthesis [124]. Interestingly, higher K contents shifted selectivity toward C5+ products. The K/FeCuAl system achieved 41.2% CO2 conversion and 28.7% CO‐free ethanol selectivity. The authors reported that Fe3O4 mediated the RWGS reaction and that a Fe3O4/χ‐Fe5C2 ratio of 1.58 was optimal for ethanol production, reinforcing the recurring theme that balancing oxide and carbide phases is crucial for steering Cu–Fe‐based catalysts toward C2 oxygenates.

Co‐Based Catalysts

One of the most established uses of Co‐based catalysts is Fischer–Tropsch synthesis [181]. Beyond that, Co catalysts have also shown activity in hydrogen generation and oxygenate synthesis [182, 183], and this broader reactivity has motivated interest in adapting cobalt systems for selective CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion.

Wang et al. introduced the CoAlO x −600 catalyst (Co/Al = 2.72), which was tested at 140°C and 40 bar in water for 15 h [125]. Remarkably, the catalyst achieved 92.1% ethanol selectivity with an ethanol STY of 0.44 mmol g–1 h–1, and no methane, hydrocarbons, or CO were detected, an outcome that contrasts sharply with typical Co‐based Fischer–Tropsch behavior. The reduction temperature proved critical: temperatures below 600°C decreased ethanol selectivity and shifted the distribution toward methanol and CO. In this system, maintaining a balanced Co0/CoO ratio was essential. Co0 activated H2 and generated *CH x species, while CoO provided sites for CO2 adsorption and acetate formation, which then underwent C—C coupling, as confirmed by XANES and in situ FTIR.

In continuous‐flow reactors, several studies have further demonstrated the promise of Co catalysts, with most efforts focusing on controlling cobalt oxidation states, incorporating promoters, and tuning support interactions to optimize ethanol formation. For example, Zheng et al. evaluated Co nanoparticles supported on La2O3–La4Ga2O9 (Co:Ga = 7:3) at 240°C and 30 bar [184]. The catalyst achieved 9.8% CO2 conversion and 65.8% ethanol selectivity. Ga played a dual role: it suppressed excessive hydrogenation and stabilized Coδ+ species (consistent with reduced CH4 formation and the measured Co0/ Coδ+ ratio) while enhancing CO adsorption. In line with earlier studies [185, 186], CO2‐TPD showed that Co0 generated *CH x intermediates, and Coδ+ enabled nondissociative CO2 adsorption, together providing the C1 fragments required for C—C coupling.

An et al. later replaced the mixed oxide support with La4Ga2O9 alone and adjusted the Co:Ga ratio to 1:1, testing the system at 270°C and 35 bar [126]. Under these conditions, CO2 conversion decreased to 4.6% and ethanol selectivity to 34.7%. The shift in performance was attributed to La4Ga2O9, which in this formulation catalyzed the RWGS reaction, a role rarely assigned to this support but experimentally verified here. As before, Co0 sites facilitated *CH x formation (as supported by XAFS/XPS), while Co2+ stabilized C—O surface species. The observed catalytic trends showed that the Co0/Co2+ ratio governed the product distribution, with higher fractions of Co0 favoring methanol formation at the expense of ethanol.

Continuing this line of investigation, An et al. examined Ga‐promoted Co0–Coδ+ pairs supported on Al‐ and SiO2‐containing materials [127]. The optimal CoGaAlO4/SiO2 catalyst delivered 4.4% CO2 conversion, 20.1% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 0.30 mmol g–1 h–1 at 270°C and 30 bar. A Ga:Al ratio of 1:1 enabled fine control over the oxidation state of cobalt, yielding an optimal Co0/Coδ+ ratio of 0.34 (extracted from XPS), which correlated directly with ethanol formation. CO2‐TPD showed that Ga‐ and Al‐containing spinels enhanced CO2 adsorption and activation, while catalytic tests confirmed that these spinels also promoted RWGS activity (Scheme 13), supplying the *CO species needed for the subsequent C—C coupling step.

Zhang et al. assessed Na‐promoted cobalt carbide (Co2C) catalysts supported on various materials, identifying Na–Co2C/SiO2 and Na–Co2C/Si3N4 (20.0 wt% Co, 2.0 wt% Na) as optimal for alcohol synthesis [128]. At 250°C and 50 bar, these catalysts reached 18.8% CO2 conversion and ∼6.0% ethanol selectivity. The support played a decisive role because both SiO2 and Si3N4 stabilized Co2C, which was identified as the active phase for higher‐alcohol formation. Ethanol formed via a CO‐insertion mechanism on Co2C, while Co0 sites promoted CH4 production. In a follow‐up study on 2% Na–Co2C/SiO2 at 220°C–310°C at 50 bar [129], CO2 conversion reached 53.2% at 310°C, 6.8% of ethanol selectivity, and ethanol STY of 1.10 mmol g–1 h–1. Na stabilized Co2C through strong metal–support interactions and enhanced CO selectivity by promoting RWGS activity. A combined set of in situ DRIFTS, TPD experiments, and spin‐polarized DFT–PBE(D3) calculations on Co2C(111) and Na–Co2C(111) slabs clarified the underlying mechanism. Bader charge analysis indicated electron transfer from Na to Co, increasing the electron density of the carbide phase and stabilizing its structure. This charge redistribution suppressed *CO dissociation while enhancing nondissociative *CO adsorption, thereby maintaining a favorable *CO/*CH x ratio for C—C coupling. Na also raised *CO‐scission barriers (by 0.6 eV for direct cleavage and 1.2 eV for H‐assisted cleavage) while strengthening CO2 and CO adsorption. Together, these effects promoted ethanol formation via *CO insertion into *CH x , and they rationalized the experimentally observed volcano‐type dependence of ethanol STY on Na loading.

Ding et al. further emphasized the importance of balancing cobalt oxidation states and selecting an appropriate support [130]. Among the materials screened, 5Co/S‐1 provided the best performance, achieving 13.9% CO2 conversion, 27.0% ethanol selectivity, and an STY of 0.83 mmol g–1 h–1 at 250°C and 30 bar. The microporous S‐1 silicate support promoted Si–O–Co interactions, minimized Co agglomeration, and maintained an optimal Co0/CoO ratio. These are properties that could be tuned by adjusting the reduction temperature. In situ DRIFTS showed that Co0 formed *CH x species, while CoO stabilized HCOO*, and ethanol arose from the coupling and subsequent hydrogenation of these two intermediates (formate‐mediated pathway, Scheme 1).

Bimetallic Co Catalysts

Wang et al. explored combining cobalt with other active metals by developing CoNi catalysts supported on Al2O3[131]. Their optimal composition, Co0.52Ni0.48AlO x , contained CoNi alloys together with CoO phases and achieved an ethanol selectivity of 85.7% and an ethanol STY of 1.32 mmol g–1 h–1 at 200°C and 40 bar in water. In this system, Ni incorporation promoted the formation of *CH x species, whereas CoO stabilized HCOO* intermediates, as evidenced by in situ FTIR. The subsequent detection of *CH3COO and C2H5O species in DRIFTS supported a formate‐mediated pathway in which ethanol forms through coupling between HCOO* and *CH x species.

Building on this bimetallic design strategy, the same group developed a K‐promoted Ni–Zn catalyst (4K‐Ni1Zn3‐300) [132]. This catalyst reached a CO2 conversion of 29.7%, a 6.7% higher‐alcohol selectivity and alcohol STY of 62.70 mg g–1 h–1 at 350°C and 30 bar. K promotion enhanced CO2 adsorption, suppressed overhydrogenation, and stabilized the in situ‐formed Ni3Zn1C0.7 phase, which the authors identified as active for CO2 adsorption and C—C coupling. In situ DRIFTS detected carboxylate (*CH3COO) species, consistent with a CO2‐insertion mechanism in which CO2 inserts into *CH x fragments to form surface acetate that then hydrogenates to ethanol. DFT–PBE calculations on Ni3Zn3C0.7(111) reinforced this interpretation: K strengthened CO2 adsorption (–1.20 eV vs. –0.51 eV), raised the barrier for *CH2 hydrogenation to methane (1.56 eV vs. 0.45 eV), and lowered the barrier for the most favorable C—C coupling step between CO2 and *CH2 (0.24 eV vs. 0.63 eV). The hydrogenation of acetate to ethanol was identified as the rate‐determining step (1.35 eV with K vs. 1.93 eV without K). Comparisons with K‐modified Ni(111) and Ni1Zn1(101) further showed that carburization of Ni–Zn is essential for allowing the C—C coupling step.

Liu et al. then demonstrated that ethanol selectivity over Co–Cu alloys strongly depends on surface segregation [133]. DFT‐calculated (BEEF‐vdW) surface phase diagrams revealed that, under reaction conditions, three surface states may form: Cu‐rich, moderately Co‐segregated, and highly Co‐segregated. Cu‐rich surfaces favor methanol because *CH2O hydrogenates readily and C—O cleavage is kinetically inaccessible (>2 eV). Highly Co‐segregated surfaces accumulate dense *CO coverages and favor methane. In contrast, moderately Co‐segregated surfaces lower the barrier for C—O cleavage in *CH2O relative to hydrogenation, promoting *CH2 formation and its subsequent coupling with *CO to produce *CH2CO, the precursor to ethanol. Guided by these predictions, the authors used controlled CO pretreatments to stabilize the moderately segregated state, attaining 61.0% ethanol selectivity at 200°C and 40 bar in water, although the basis for this selectivity (whether calculated among all carbon‐containing products or excluding CO) was not explicitly reported.

To further probe how supports influence this behavior, the same group examined a CoCu/MCM‐41 catalyst [134]. Supported on mesoporous silica, this material achieved 85.3% ethanol selectivity and a STY of 0.01 mmol g–1 h–1 at 200°C and 40 bar in water. Under these conditions, CO2 hydrogenation proceeded via RWGS to form CO, and the Co–Cu alloy then modulated CO2 and CO activation. DRIFTS, TPD, and XPS indicated that Cu suppressed excessive oxygen coverage on Co sites, while Co enabled nondissociative *CO adsorption, thereby facilitating coupling with *CH x species.

Beyond Co–Ni and Co–Cu systems, several studies focused on Pt–Co combinations and the role of water. For example, He et al. evaluated the effect of water in a 1.0 wt% Pt/Co3O4 catalyst for higher‐alcohol synthesis [135]. At 200°C and 80 bar, using a water/DMI (15:85 v/v) mixture, the catalyst reached a higher‐alcohol selectivity of 33.1% among all products and an alcohol mix STY of 0.51 mmol g–1 h–1, with ethanol as the major component. Pt, present mainly as Pt0 according to XPS, promoted the RWGS reaction. Isotopic labeling with D2O and 13CH3OH showed that water facilitated methanol dissociation into *CH3 and OH*, which, in turn, promoted *CO insertion into *CH3 and its subsequent hydrogenation (methanol‐mediated pathway, Scheme 1).

In a follow‐up study, Huang et al. employed the same Pt–Co3O4 combination but targeted direct ethanol synthesis [136]. At 240°C and 32 bar in water, the system reached ∼88.0% ethanol selectivity and an ethanol yield of 0.27 mmol g–1 h–1, although Pt loadings below 1.0% reduced ethanol selectivity. The introduction of Pt generated interfacial sites with oxygen vacancies, and in situ DRIFTS showed that these interfacial sites produced *OCH3 via RWGS, while Co3O4 supplied *CH3. Therefore, the authors suggested that coupling between these intermediates ultimately yielded ethanol, highlighting the importance of the interfacial sites generated in the Pt–Co3O4 catalyst.

Liu et al. then examined how mesoporous supports further affect this chemistry [137]. Co3O4‐m‐300/200 (a mesoporous material reduced at 300°C and tested at 200°C) outperformed its nanoparticle analog (Co3O4‐np‐300/200) owing to a higher density of active sites and improved Co stability. Introducing Pt further enhanced alcohol selectivity: Pt/Co3O4‐m‐200/200 achieved 10.7% CO2 conversion and 47.2% alcohol selectivity at 200°C and 20 bar in continuous flow. Although Pt lowered overall CO2 conversion and slightly decreased higher‐alcohol STY, it increased selectivity by confining intermediates within the mesopores and suppressing overhydrogenation.

Other lines of research have focused on the combined effects of noble metal and alkali promoters. Okabe et al. investigated how the Co precursor (nitrate vs. acetate) and promoters (Ir and Na) affect Co/SiO2 performance at 220°C and 21 bar [138]. Ir/Co(A)–Na2O/SiO2 (A = acetate‐derived Co precursor) was identified as optimal, achieving 7.6% CO2 conversion and 7.9% ethanol selectivity. Ir improved Co dispersion and facilitated reduction to Co0, with 74.9% Co0 measured by XPS and TPR. Alkali promotion decreased CH4 selectivity and increased CO and ethanol selectivity, consistent with the tendency of alkali metals to promote C—C coupling pathways.

Beyond Co‐based bimetallics, a series of CoMo carbide and sulfide systems further highlighted the role of Mo. Zhang et al. synthesized CoMoC x catalysts and identified an 800°C‐carburized material containing Co6Mo6C2, β‐Mo2C, and metallic Co as optimal [139]. At 180°C and 20 bar in DMF, this catalyst reached 97.4% ethanol selectivity in the alcohol fraction and an ethanol STY of 0.53 mmol g–1 h–1. XRD and XPS confirmed the coexistence of the three phases, while in situ DRIFTS revealed carbonate and CH3O* species, suggesting that Co6Mo6C2 stabilizes both CH3O* and *CO intermediates, enabling their coupling and subsequent hydrogenation to ethanol (CO‐insertion pathway, Scheme 1).

A complementary study by Nieksens et al. evaluated a CoMoS catalyst in continuous flow [140]. They identified an optimal H2:CO2 ratio of 3:1 and observed that lower temperatures favored alcohol selectivity. Under the optimal conditions (340°C and 104 bar), the catalyst achieved 5.5% ethanol selectivity and 32.0% CO2 conversion.

Similarly, Liu et al. developed a Mo–Co–K sulfide catalyst on activated carbon, which enhanced dispersion of the active phase [141]. At 320°C and 50 bar, the optimized Mo1Co0.3K0.9/AC‐15% catalyst achieved 24.2% CO2 conversion and 6.7% CO‐free ethanol selectivity. K promotion increased the Mo4+ fraction (as observed by XPS) and, together with Co, tuned the electronic state of Mo to facilitate C—C coupling via a CO‐insertion pathway, consistent with mechanisms proposed in earlier Mo–Co systems.

These Mo–Co studies collectively stress that Mo itself can function as an active center for CO2 hydrogenation to alcohols. Tatsumi et al. provided early evidence by evaluating SiO2‐supported Mo catalysts promoted with alkali salts at 250°C and 1 bar [142]. The Mo–K/SiO2 catalyst produced C1–C5 alcohols, with ethanol as one of the main higher alcohols, and showed much higher‐alcohol selectivity under CO2/H2 than CO/H2 feeds. The authors suggested that CO2 is first reduced to CO and may partially oxidize Mo to intermediate states, although no spectroscopic or computational evidence was provided. Among the promoters tested, KCl performed best, giving 35.0% ethanol selectivity, while Li and Na salts were less active.

K–Co systems supported on oxides provide an additional perspective on alkali promotion. Witoon et al. developed a K–Co catalyst supported on In2O3 (2.5K5Co–In2O3), which achieved 36.6% CO2 conversion and ∼65.0% higher‐alcohol selectivity at 380°C and 40 bar, with ethanol as the predominant alcohol [143]. K introduction led to the formation of K–O–Co species which suppressed overhydrogenation by weakening H2 adsorption. In2O3 catalyzed the RWGS reaction to form CO, as evidenced by the dominant CO selectivity. XANES, XPS, and H2‐TPD confirmed the formation of K–O–Co species capable of hydrogenating CO to C x H y intermediates. By analogy with prior Co‐based studies, the authors proposed a CO‐insertion route to higher alcohols, although detailed C—C coupling steps were not directly probed.

Finally, Lage et al. investigated a 1.0 wt% K‐promoted Co1.8Cu0.9AlO x catalyst at 250°C, 30 bar, and a H2:CO2 ratio of 3:2 [144]. The system achieved 12.2% CO2 conversion, 20.8% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 3.08 mmol g–1 h–1. The authors highlighted three key factors governing ethanol synthesis: (i) an optimal Co/(Co + Cu) ratio of 0.66 that balanced hydrocarbon, methanol, and higher‐alcohol formation while minimizing CH4 and CO; (ii) an optimal reduction temperature of 400°C; and (iii) the need to maintain surface exposure of the active metals rather than encapsulating them within the support.

Tandem Catalysts

Tandem catalysis refers to the use of two or more catalysts with complementary functions arranged so that the product of one step becomes the reactant of the next [187]. In CO2‐to‐alcohol conversion, this typically means combining a catalyst that activates CO2 with a second catalyst that promotes C—C coupling. These components can be arranged in dual‐bed configurations or physically mixed in a single bed (Figure 2). Among reported systems, Cu‐ and Fe‐based combinations are the most widely explored, and all studies to date have been carried out in continuous reactors.

Different reaction configurations. (a,b) Dual‐bed configurations in which the catalysts are separated by an inert bed (e.g., quartz sand). (c) Mixed‐bed configuration in which both catalysts are powder‐mixed.

Inui et al. laid much of the early groundwork for CO2 hydrogenation to ethanol in continuous reactors using multifunctional tandem catalysts [145, 146]. They systematically examined how different metal‐based components and their combinations affect ethanol formation. Composite catalyst systems were formulated primarily from Fe, Cu, Zn, Al, and K, with small amounts of more expensive metals (Pd, Rh, Ga) used to boost performance. A series of tests at 330°C–350°C, 80 bar, and H2:CO2 = 3:1 led to several general conclusions. Rh‐based catalysts selectively converted CO2 into CO but were not effective for alcohol synthesis on their own. Fe‐based catalysts played a central role in C—C coupling, whereas Cu‐based catalysts lacked this capability and primarily produced methanol. The best‐performing configuration was a mixed system combining an Fe‐based catalyst (Fe, Cu, Al, K) with a Cu‐based catalyst (Cu, Zn, Al, K). Under optimized conditions at 330°C, this composite achieved 31.1% CO2 conversion, 9.3% ethanol selectivity, and an ethanol STY of 18.41 mmol mL–1 h–1.

Building directly on this concept, Guo et al. examined the role of K and Zn promoters in a Cu‐based catalyst combined with a Cu–Fe‐based system [147]. They developed a layered composite consisting of CuZnK0.15(CZK) and Cu25Fe22Co3K3 (CFCK), denoted as CZK(1.5)||CFCK(4.5). At 350°C and 60 bar, this tandem catalyst achieved 32.4% CO2 conversion, 11.8% alcohol selectivity, with higher alcohols comprising 55.3% of the alcohol fraction, and an alcohol STY of 131.00 mg mL–1 h–1. In this configuration, catalytic tests indicated that CZK catalyzes the RWGS reaction, generating CO, while CFCK provides the C—C coupling sites. K2O enhanced alcohol selectivity and suppressed methanation (as shown by CO2‐TPD and product distributions), whereas ZnO improved Cu dispersion according to TPR, so that both promoters contributed to the overall performance.

Wang et al. further advanced tandem systems by combining a Na‐doped Fe‐based catalyst (Na–Fe@C) with a K‐doped CuZnAl catalyst (K–CuZnAl) through granule mixing in a single bed (Figure 2c) [148]. The optimal formulation, 2% Na–Fe@C/5% K–CuZnAl, achieved 39.2% CO2 conversion and 35.0% CO‐free ethanol selectivity at 320°C and 50 bar. In this system, K–CuZnAl catalyzed the RWGS reaction, generating *CO, while Na–Fe@C facilitated C—C coupling between *CH x and *CO/*CHO to form ethanol. In situ DRIFTS identified HCOO* and *CH x O species on K–CuZnAl, *CH x and alkenes on Na–Fe@C, and aldehyde intermediates only when both components were combined, consistent with a genuine tandem sequence. DFT–PBE calculations on Fe5C2(510) and Na2O/Fe5C2(510) slabs compared *CH2—*CHO and *CH2—*CO coupling. Na lowered the barrier for *CH2—*CHO coupling from 0.73 to 0.14 eV and for *CH2—*CO from 1.20 to 0.69 eV. The authors concluded that *CH2—*CHO is kinetically preferred, but that *CH2—*CO coupling remains accessible under the high *CO coverage expected on Fe5C2.

A similar tandem concept was adopted by Xu et al. [149], who combined CuZnAl with K‐CuMgZnFe as a 1:1 powder mixture. At 320°C and 50 bar, this system reached 42.3% CO2 conversion, 17.4% higher‐alcohol selectivity, and an alcohol STY of 106.50 mg gcat –1 h–1, with ethanol accounting for almost 80.0% of the alcohol fraction. In this case, CuZnAl again functioned as the RWGS catalyst, while K‐CuMgZnFe promoted *CO hydrogenation to *CH x and subsequent C—C coupling. In situ DRIFTS and TPSR supported these assignments. Reactor‐configuration experiments and CO‐chemisorption/TPSR studies further showed that the spatial proximity between the two components was crucial, as CO generated on CuZnAl migrated to K‐CuMgZnFe, where higher alcohols were formed (Scheme 14).

Huang et al. [150] applied a related strategy using a MnCuK–FeC/CuZnAlZr tandem catalyst mixed in a 1:1 powder ratio, demonstrating that simple physical mixing of two optimized components can outperform other arrangements. At 300°C and 30 bar, this tandem system achieved 42.1% CO2 conversion and 9.3% ethanol selectivity. Here, CuZnAlZr catalyzed the RWGS reaction, while the MnCuK‐modified iron carbide phase drove chain growth, as inferred from catalytic data. In situ DRIFTS showed that Mn and Cu promoted nondissociative CO2 activation, evidenced by formate, methoxy, and C2 oxygenated intermediates, while K suppressed excessive hydrogenation according to CO2‐TPD and product distributions.

In another example of layered architectures, Zhang et al. developed a K–CuZnAl/Zr–CuFe composite, in which K–CuZnAl was physically mixed with a Zr–CuFe layer [151]. Tested at 320°C and 40 bar, this tandem catalyst attained 40.6% CO2 conversion and ∼18.0% CO‐free ethanol selectivity. In this system, K–CuZnAl again served as the RWGS‐active component, as confirmed by catalytic tests and CO‐TPD, whereas Zr–CuFe promoted C—C coupling, supported by in situ DRIFTS/TPSR signals of C2 oxygenated intermediates. On K–CuZnAl, K enhanced alcohol synthesis by suppressing overhydrogenation, as suggested by CO2‐TPD. On Zr–CuFe, the Cu/Fe ratio‐controlled CO dissociation, inferred from prior studies, which, in turn, balanced *CO and *CH x coverages and helped steer the reaction toward ethanol.

Finally, Zhang et al. [152] proposed a multifunctional tandem catalyst based on Co2C and CuZnAl, configured in a dual‐bed arrangement with Co2C upstream and CuZnAl downstream (Figure 2a,b). In this configuration, Co2C catalyzed the RWGS reaction, and produced CO and olefins, which then diffused to the CuZnAl bed. There, olefins were hydrogenated to *CH2 species and coupled with *CHO intermediates generated via *CO hydrogenation at the Cu/ZnAl2O4 interface. At 250°C and 50 bar, the Co2C||CuZnAl tandem system reached 20.7% CO2 conversion, 18.4% higher‐alcohol selectivity, and a higher‐alcohol STY of 2.20 mmol g–1 h–1, with ethanol comprising ∼65.0% of the higher‐alcohol fraction. DFT–PBE(D3) calculations on a Cu6/ZnAl2O4(110) slab supported this mechanistic picture. On isolated Co2C, *CO insertion into *CH x was identified as the preferred C—C coupling route, but in the tandem configuration this pathway became thermodynamically and kinetically less favorable. Instead, *CH2—*CHO coupling at the Cu/ZnAl2O4 interface emerged as the lowest‐energy route, with an activation barrier of 0.35 eV and a strongly exothermic reaction energy (–1.60 eV), whereas alternative couplings (*CH2—*CH2O, *CH3—*CHO) and CO‐insertion steps were less favorable.

Taken together, these studies show that tandem designs can decouple CO2 activation from C—C coupling, allowing each step to be optimized on a different catalyst. The performance of such systems depends not only on the individual components but also on their spatial arrangement and proximity, which govern how efficiently intermediates such as CO, olefins, and aldehydes are transferred between active phases and converted into ethanol and other higher alcohols.

Photocatalytic CO2 Conversion to Ethanol

Fundamentals of Photocatalysts and Requirements for CO2‐to‐Ethanol Conversion

Photocatalysis has emerged as an attractive strategy for driving chemical transformations by harnessing sunlight as a clean and abundant energy source. Because light absorption can initiate reactions under mild conditions, photocatalytic pathways offer a more sustainable alternative to conventional thermally driven processes [188, 189, 190, 191, 192]. Despite these advantages, their efficiencies remain well below those of industrial technologies, so progress in materials design and mechanistic understanding is essential [193, 194].

At its core, photocatalysis refers to the acceleration or initiation of a chemical reaction through the interaction of light irradiation (ultraviolet, visible, or infrared) with a light‐absorbing material capable of generating excited‐state charges that drive the reaction [195, 196, 197]. In a typical process (Scheme 15), the photocatalyst is a semiconductor that absorbs photons with energies equal to or greater than its bandgap (E g) [190]. This excitation promotes electrons from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), creating an electron–hole pair [194, 198]. The photogenerated electrons can then engage in reduction reactions, while the holes drive oxidation steps [194, 198, 199].

Schematic view of the process of a photocatalytic reaction. CBM refers to the conduction band minimum, VBM refers to the valence band maximum, and A and B refer to the compounds being reduced and oxidized, respectively.

To make these redox processes thermodynamically accessible, the photocatalyst must possess appropriately positioned band edges. More specifically, the conduction band minimum (CBM) must lie above (more negative than) the reduction potential of the desired half‐reaction, whereas the valence band maximum (VBM) must lie below (more positive than) the oxidation potential [200, 201]. This alignment ensures that the photogenerated charges carry sufficient driving force for the targeted chemical steps [202]. For CO2 conversion to ethanol specifically, the CBM must be more negative than the CO2/C2H5OH reduction potential, and the VBM must be more positive than the H2O/O2 oxidation potential (Figure 3) [203].

Band‐edge positioning of various semiconductors relative to the redox potential of the CO2‐to‐ethanol reduction (green dashed line) and H2O/O2 oxidation (blue dashed line) reactions. Values within the arrows indicate the bandgap (in eV) for each semiconductor, while arrows in purple indicate that the absorption range lies within the UV region.

Although this band‐alignment requirement appears straightforward, photocatalytic CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion remains exceptionally challenging because of the complexity of the reaction pathway and the required transfer of 12 electrons. Achieving efficient charge separation and suppressing electron–hole recombination is therefore essential, as recombination is one of the primary limitations of photocatalytic systems and becomes even more problematic in multielectron processes [204]. These issues add to reaction‐specific challenges such as the formation and stabilization of intermediates, the kinetically sluggish C—C coupling step, and competition with side reactions that generate C1 products such as CO, CH4, or methanol [205].

Bismuth‐Based Catalysts

Against this backdrop, bismuth‐based semiconductors have gained significant attention because their visible‐light absorption, favorable band structures, and distinctive physicochemical properties make them promising platforms for CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion [206, 207, 208]. Depending on composition, their bandgaps typically range from 1.0 to 3.0 eV, enabling photocatalytic activity across a broad region of the visible spectrum [208, 209, 210, 211]. These attributes have motivated a series of studies examining how composition, morphology, and heterojunction formation influence activity and selectivity.

Liu et al. [66] provided an early example by synthesizing BiVO4 photocatalysts using a microwave‐assisted hydrothermal method with two different surfactants: CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) and PEG (polyethylene glycol). CTAB yielded monoclinic scheelite BiVO4, whereas PEG promoted the formation of tetragonal zircon BiVO4, as confirmed by XRD. SEM images revealed sheet‐like particles for the monoclinic phase and rod‐like particles for the tetragonal phase, while UV–vis absorption measurements showed bandgaps of 2.24 eV (monoclinic) and 2.56 eV (tetragonal). In CO2 reduction experiments in water at 0°C, ethanol was the dominant product, and the monoclinic BiVO4 exhibited dramatically higher activity. Under UV–vis irradiation, it was ∼19 times more active than under visible light alone (2033.0 vs. 108.0 µmol g–1 h–1). The authors attributed this enhancement to the formation of CO2 3– radical anions under UV–vis excitation and to the more asymmetric Bi3+ coordination in the monoclinic structure, which strengthened interactions with these radicals and promoted C—C coupling.

To further optimize Bi‐based systems, Ribeiro et al. [67] conducted a systematic study of Bi2WO6, varying synthesis time (8–24 h) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) concentration (5.0–50.0 wt%) to tune the morphology and surface characteristics. Characterization by XRD, SEM, UV–vis, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET), and zeta potential showed that moderate PVP loading (10.0 wt%) and shorter synthesis time (8 h) produced highly porous, flower‐like microspheres with a large surface area (45.9 m2 g–1). These structural features correlated with a substantial improvement in ethanol production (68.9 µmol g–1 h–1), a 258% increase over PVP‐free Bi2WO6. Ethanol was again the main product, likely formed through reduction of carbonate/bicarbonate species followed by C—C coupling.

In a complementary study, the same group examined Bi2MoO6 prepared through a solvohydrothermal route, exploring the effects of temperature, solvent, pH, and synthesis time [68]. XRD, SEM, UV–vis, BET, BJH, and zeta potential analyses indicated that solvent and pH were the dominant parameters affecting morphology, crystallinity, and surface area. Samples synthesized in water tended to form nanoplates, whereas those prepared in ethylene glycol/ethanol mixtures exhibited more diverse morphologies, including hierarchical spheres. Ethanol was the only detectable product during photocatalysis, with the highest yield (34.4 µmol g–1 h–1) achieved with the sample synthesized at 140°C and pH 2, which combined favorable morphology with enhanced visible‐light absorption. The authors proposed that ethanol formation proceeded through reduction of (bi)carbonate intermediates followed by radical coupling to produce C2 species.

Zhao et al. [69] extended this study by preparing a hierarchical Bi@Bi2MoO6 composite via a solvothermal route, producing Bi0 nanoparticles deposited on thin Bi2MoO6 nanosheets that contained oxygen vacancies (Scheme 16). Structural and surface analyses (XRD, SEM, TEM, XPS, UV–vis) confirmed successful formation of Bi nanoparticles on the nanosheet surface, which enhanced visible‐light absorption and improved charge separation. Under a CO2‐saturated NaHCO3 solution (0.17 mol L–1), the material produced ethanol at 17.93 μmol g–1 h–1 with 92% selectivity, 2.86 and 1.65 times higher than pristine Bi2MoO6 and oxygen‐deficient Bi2MoO6, respectively. The improved performance arose from the synergetic combination of oxygen‐vacancy‐rich Bi2MoO6 (providing abundant active sites) and Bi nanoparticles (enhancing charge separation). Mechanistic insights from 13C isotope labeling experiments revealed that ethanol originated from coupling between bicarbonate (HCO3 –) and CO2 via carbene‐type intermediates (:CO2H–/:CH2).

Diagram of the photocatalytic reduction of CO2 to ethanol using Bi@Bi2MoO6. Reproduced with permission [69] 2021, Wiley, VCH.

Das and co‐workers [70] introduced an additional design strategy by synthesizing a red phosphorus (RP)–Bi2MoO6 (BMO) heterojunction by combining exfoliated RP nanosheets with Bi2MoO6 nanoparticles through ultrasonication. XRD, SEM, TEM, AFM, UV–vis, and XPS characterization showed that extended sonication (20 h) exfoliated RP into single layers (∼2 nm thick) while enabling uniform deposition of Bi2MoO6. This configuration formed a type‐II heterojunction that promoted charge separation. The RP–BMO(21) (where 21 refers to the RP:BMO weight ratio) sample achieved an ethanol production rate of 51.8 µmol g–1 h–1 under visible light, outperforming both parent materials. In situ DRIFTS and kinetic isotope effect studies using deuterated methanol indicated that ethanol formation proceeded through self‐coupling of in situ generated methanol via *OCH3 intermediates at Bi–Mo sites.

More recently, Mandal et al. [71] reported a composite made of Cu‐doped Bi2MoO6 nanoribbons (Cu‐BMONR) supported on reduced graphene oxide (rGO) in which 2.0 wt% Cu delivered an ethanol yield of 133.1 µmol g–1 h–1 with 100% selectivity, corresponding to a 2.3‐fold enhancement compared with the undoped composite. Spectroscopic evidence (FTIR, XPS) indicated strong bonding between GO and Cu‐BMONR, with shifts in Bi (4f), Mo (3d), and C (1s) binding energies indicating electron donation from Bi and Mo and formation of a robust interfacial junction. Cu incorporation reduced the bandgap, improved visible‐light absorption, and enhanced charge‐transfer dynamics, as evidenced by photoluminescence (PL) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements. BET analysis revealed an increased surface area relative to pristine BMONR, consistent with greater CO2 adsorption capacity. Notably, removing Cu or rGO produced mixed methanol/ethanol products, highlighting the key role of the Cu–rGO junction in stabilizing intermediates and steering C—C coupling toward ethanol.

C3N4‐Based Catalysts

Carbon nitride (C3N4) is considered an intermediate bandgap semiconductor, with a reported bandgap of approximately 2.70 eV (Figure 3) [212, 213]. It has emerged as a promising metal‐free photocatalyst due to its visible‐light absorption capacity and chemical stability [212, 214]. The position of its CB is sufficiently negative to drive the reduction of CO2 to value‐added products such as ethanol [215]. Nevertheless, pristine C3N4 typically exhibits poor charge separation and limited selectivity toward CO2 reduction to ethanol [216]. To overcome these limitations, various strategies, such as heterojunction construction or cocatalyst loading, can be applied to enhance charge‐carrier dynamics and facilitate multielectron transfers [212, 215, 216, 217].

Within this context, Wang et al. [72] developed a composite photocatalyst by combining trimodal porous silica, derived from waste rice husk, with graphitic carbon nitride (g‐C3N4) nanotubes using a two‐step hydrothermal synthesis. SEM, TEM, N2 adsorption–desorption analysis, and CO2 adsorption isotherms confirmed the formation of hollow tubular structures containing meso‐, micro‐, and macropores, which increased both the surface area and CO2 uptake. The best‐performing sample exhibited selective ethanol formation, reaching 196.0 µmol g–1 after 8 h of visible‐light irradiation and clearly outperforming bulk g‐C3N4, which mainly produced methanol. Complementary UV–vis, PL, EIS, and photocurrent measurements showed enhanced light absorption, improved charge separation and transport, and reduced recombination, collectively explaining the enhanced ethanol productivity upon incorporation of trimodal porous silica.

Going beyond textural optimization, Shi et al. [73] introduced atomic‐level control by anchoring In–Cu dual‐metal sites on polymeric carbon nitride (InCu/PCN). AC‐HAADF‐STEM, XAFS, and XPS confirmed atomically dispersed Cu+ and In3+ centers coordinated as Cu–N3 and In–N4 units, interconnected through Cu–N–In bridges with an average In–Cu distance of 0.33 nm. DFT–PBE calculations indicated that this bridging motif drives charge transfer from In to Cu, enriching Cu 3d states near the Fermi level while depleting In states. Time‐resolved PL (tr‐PL) and femtosecond transient absorption measurements corroborated the computational predictions, revealing more efficient charge separation and longer carrier lifetimes on InCu/PCN than on Cu/PCN or In/PCN. Mechanistically, DFT results showed that Cu sites are responsible for CO2 activation to *COOH and *CO, whereas adjacent In atoms stabilize a second *CO through strong chemisorption. The Cu–N–In bridge thereby promotes *CO—*CO coupling, lowering the barrier from 1.31 eV on Cu/PCN to 0.79 eV. Subsequent hydrogenation of *COCO to *CHCH2OH and final protonation to ethanol proceeded exergonically. Crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) analysis further indicated strengthened metal–CO coupling on InCu/PCN relative to single‐metal analogs, consistent with redshifted *CO stretching bands observed by in situ DRIFTS. These structural and electronic features translated directly into improved photocatalytic performance, with InCu/PCN achieving an ethanol production rate of 28.5 µmol g–1 h–1 and an ethanol selectivity of 92.4%.

In a follow‐up study, Shi et al. [74] focused on a single‐metal analog with tailored coordination, namely, a carbon nitride‐supported Cu single‐atom catalyst featuring a defected low‐coordination Cu–N2 motif (Cu–N2–V). This photocatalyst achieved an ethanol production rate of 69.8 µmol g–1 h–1 with an ethanol selectivity of 97.8%, outperforming both the defect‐free system and previously reported InCu dual‐site catalyst [73]. XPS, XAFS, and EPR showed that Cu is predominantly present as Cu+ with a minor Cu2+ fraction. This mixed‐valence state was found to be essential: Cu+ activates CO2 to *COOH and *CO, while Cu2+ strengthens *CO adsorption and promotes C—C coupling via *CO—*CO dimerization. DFT–PBE calculations revealed that the adsorption of a second *CO to form *COCO is exothermic, whereas alternative hydrogenation routes to *COH or *CHO are far less favorable. In contrast, *CO—*CO coupling is uphill on Cu–N3 and Cu–N4 analogs. The subsequent hydrogenation of *COCO to *COCOH was identified as the rate‐determining step, although it remained substantially less energy demanding on Cu–N2–V than on Cu–N3 or Cu–N4. In situ DRIFTS detected stable chemisorbed *CO and a progressive increase in *COCO and *COCOH signals under illumination, experimentally validating the DFT‐predicted *CO—CO coupling and hydrogenation sequence. In addition, the Cu–N2–V system showed broadened visible‐light absorption, longer carrier lifetimes, and more efficient charge separation than conventional single‐atom or nanoparticle catalysts, as evidenced by UV–vis and PL measurements.

To further explore multisite copper architectures on C3N4, Guo et al. [75] prepared a photocatalyst hosting Cu4 clusters adjacent to Cu single atoms on a carbon nitride surface (Cu4/C1@CN), using a two‐step calcination process (Figure 4a). Under CO2 reduction conditions, this material delivered an ethanol production rate of 154.0 µmol g–1 h–1 with 98.0% selectivity. Control experiments showed that isolated Cu1@CN produced almost exclusively CO, whereas Cu4/Cu1@CN generated C2 products selectively, highlighting the importance of the synergy between Cu clusters and neighboring single atoms for C—C coupling. Optical and electrochemical characterizations (UV–vis, tr‐PL, transient photocurrent response, and EIS) revealed a narrower bandgap, stronger light absorption, and more effective charge separation in the dual–site system than in Cu1@CN or pristine CN. HAADF‐STEM, XAFS, and XPS confirmed atomically dispersed Cu4 clusters associated with Cu single atoms in Cu–N2 and Cu–O coordination environments (Figure 4b–d), with Cu present as a mixture of Cu0 and Cu+. In situ attenuated total reflection surface‐enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR‐SEIRAS) identified *CO, *COOH, *COCO, *COCHO, and *OCH2CH3 intermediates, indicating that ethanol forms via *CO—*CO coupling to give *COCO, followed by hydrogenation to *COCHO and further stepwise hydrogenation to ethanol. DFT–PBE(D3) calculations compared three coupling scenarios: (i) *CO—*CO coupling on a Cu4 cluster, (ii) coupling between *CO on the cluster and *CO on a neighboring single atom (Cu4 + 1), and (iii) coupling on an isolated Cu1 site. While CO formation was facile on all sites, *CO—*CO coupling on Cu1 was unstable, consistent with its poor C2 activity. In contrast, the Cu4 + 1 configuration displayed the lowest C—C coupling barrier, demonstrating that interfacial cooperation between Cu4 and Cu1 sites is kinetically optimal for *CO—*CO dimerization.

Wang and co‐workers [76] pushed the idea of multiatom copper motifs further by using a prelocking, nanoconfined polymerization strategy to obtain an ultrahigh‐density Cu multiatom catalyst with a Cu loading of 36.0 wt%. The resulting material featured triangular Cu3 motifs deposited on the carbon nitride surface and achieved an ethanol production rate of 117 µmol g–1 h–1 with 98.0% selectivity. AC‐HAADF‐STEM confirmed both the high Cu dispersion and the triangular Cu3 configuration, showing interatomic distances of 2.5 ± 0.5 Å. EXAFS analysis detected Cu–N coordination, indicating interaction with C3N4 (Figure 5b), and a Cu–Cu peak at 2.2 Å that corroborated the trimeric structure. XPS analysis of the Cu 2p and Cu LMM regions revealed coexisting Cu0/Cu+ and Cu2+ species, with Cu0/Cu+ accounting for 88.0% of the Cu 2p signal. Further examination of the Cu LMM region confirmed Cu+ as the predominant state. Moreover, in situ XPS measurements provided insights into the light‐induced dynamics of copper. Upon illumination, the main Cu 2p 3/2 peak exhibited a downshift of 0.33 eV, indicating electron accumulation at the Cu sites. Concurrently, the Cu+/Cu2+ atomic ratio increased from 1.3 in the dark to 2.9 under illumination, evidencing the photoinduced reduction of Cu species. Photocurrent and tr‐PL measurements demonstrated more effective charge separation and longer carrier lifetimes (Figure 5a), while UV–vis spectroscopy showed enhanced visible‐light absorption. In situ DRIFTS combined with DFT–PBE(D3) calculations revealed that Cu3 motifs stabilize both ontop‐ and bridge‐adsorbed *CO species, lowering the C—C coupling barrier and promoting conversion of *CHCOH to *CHCHOH intermediates. This pathway biases the reaction toward ethanol rather than the thermodynamically more favorable ethylene route (Figure 5c).

Defect engineering offers another avenue to tune C3N4‐based systems. A prominent example is the work by Zhou et al. [77], who integrated carbon vacancies into a Cu‐doped carbon nitride (Cu–CvN) to improve ethanol selectivity. CO2 photoreduction tests yielded 122.6 µmol g–1 h–1 ethanol with 93.7% selectivity, a 4.5‐fold increase compared with the vacancy‐free sample. XRD, FTIR, XPS, and EPR confirmed both Cu incorporation and controlled introduction of carbon defects, whose concentration increased with calcination temperature and was reflected in a decreasing C/N ratio. The same temperature variation also altered Cu+/Cu0 ratios, which correlated with liquid‐product selectivity. Optical and electrochemical characterizations (UV–vis spectroscopy, PL, transient absorption spectroscopy, photocurrent measurements, and EIS) revealed bandgap narrowing, enhanced visible‐light absorption, extended carrier lifetimes, and reduced charge‐transfer resistance, together indicating more efficient charge separation and improved multielectron transfer for C—C coupling. DFT–PBE calculations showed that Cu doping promotes CO2 activation and *CO stabilization by lowering the Gibbs adsorption energy from 0.57 to 0.14 eV, while carbon vacancies reduce the *CO dimerization barrier through formation of a six‐membered ring intermediate, thereby accelerating C—C bond formation.

Finally, Li et al. [78] introduced a nitrogen‐bridged S–N–Cu dual‐site photocatalyst on red polymeric carbon nitride (Cu/S‐RPCN), targeting simultaneous control of charge separation and intermediate binding. The optimized material achieved an ethanol production rate of 50.4 µmol g–1 h–1 with 99.5% selectivity in pure water, representing a 3‐fold activity increase over the Cu‐only analog, while an S‐only sample was inactive. HAADF–STEM and EXAFS showed atomically dispersed Cu in a Cu–N4 environment, with no Cu–Cu coordination at 2.2 Å. XPS and XANES indicated a mixed‐valence Cu state between Cu+ and Cu2+ with an average oxidation state of + 1.3 and revealed that S atoms substitute lattice C atoms in the heptazine units. The superior performance of Cu/S‐RPCN was attributed to a synergistic mechanism in which Cu and S function as electron and hole traps, respectively, as supported by faster charge–transfer dynamics, enhanced photocurrent response, and in situ XPS showing electron accumulation on Cu under illumination. In situ FTIR and DFT–PBE(D3) calculations further demonstrated that S incorporation optimizes *CO adsorption on Cu sites, weakens Cu—O bonding, and lowers the barrier for *CO—COH coupling, directing the reaction through an *OCHCH2OH intermediate that is key to the ethanol formation pathway.

Copper Oxide‐Based Catalysts

Copper oxides have attracted significant attention as photocatalysts because of their relatively narrow bandgaps, typically reported in the range of 1.20–2.20 eV depending on the Cu oxidation state. This allows efficient operation under visible‐light irradiation [218, 219, 220]. In the context of CO2 reduction to multicarbon products, Cu2O offers CB positions suitable for driving CO2 reduction [221, 222]. In addition, the ability of Cu oxides to facilitate C—C coupling and stabilize key reaction intermediates makes them especially appealing for enhancing selectivity toward ethanol [223].

Within this family, Huo et al. [79] reported a superhydrophobic Cu2O hollow structure designed specifically to overcome mass transport limitations associated with CO2 delivery to active sites (Scheme 17). The central feature of this system is the so‐called nanofence effect: surface modification with 1‐dodecanethiol (DDT) produces a superhydrophobic interface that blocks water infiltration while still allowing continuous CO2 transport and accumulation at the catalyst surface (Scheme 17). Under these conditions, the material afforded an ethanol production rate of 996.2 µmol g–1 h–1 with an ethanol selectivity of 59.6%. XRD, XPS, and Raman spectroscopy confirmed the chemisorbed DDT without compromising the intrinsic photocatalytic properties of Cu2O or its efficient charge separation. In situ FTIR detected the same intermediates (*COOH, *CO, *OCCHO) on both DDT‐modified and unmodified Cu2O, but at higher concentrations on the superhydrophobic structure, consistent with more CO2 delivery and *CO buildup. To rationalize this behavior, spin‐polarized DFT–PBE(D3) calculations on Cu2O(111) modeled ethanol formation via a *CO—*CHO coupling pathway at low (0.22 ML) and moderate (0.44 ML) *CO coverages. Although limited to ground‐state thermodynamics, and therefore not explicitly accounting for photoexcitation, theoretical calculations showed that increasing *CO coverage lowers the C—C coupling barrier from 1.75 to 1.23 eV. This trend supports the hypothesis that continuous CO2 supply in the nanofence architecture sustains high *CO surface concentrations, thereby facilitating C—C coupling and enhancing ethanol selectivity.

Building on this concept, Huo et al. [80] next developed a nanoconfined three‐phase tandem system based on a core–shell architecture comprising Ag nanoparticles encapsulated within a hydrophobic mesoporous Cu2O shell (O‐Ag@Cu2O), as shown in Scheme 18. This design delivered an ethanol evolution rate of 450.2 µmol g–1 h–1 with a markedly higher selectivity of 94.2%. As in their earlier work, DDT modification imparted hydrophobicity, confirmed by water contact angles of 132.5° for the modified structure versus 34.8° for the unmodified analog. In this case, however, the combination of hydrophobicity and interconnected mesopores created a nanoconfined CO2 reservoir that extended *CO residence time and shifted selectivity toward *CO—*CO coupling rather than *CHO formation.

In situ FTIR spectroscopy supported this picture: all samples showed *COOH, *CHO, and carbonate species, but the hydrophobic structures exhibited much stronger *COOH and carbonate signals, consistent with CO2 accumulation under nanoconfinement. Crucially, only O‐Ag@Cu2O displayed the –CH2– rocking vibration associated with ethanol formation. Guided by these observations, DFT–PBE(D3) calculations were performed on a bare Cu2O(111) slab and on Cu2O(111) decorated with a four‐atom Ag cluster (three Ag atoms in contact with Cu2O and one ontop). The calculations indicated that Ag promotes CO formation by lowering the *COOH‐to‐*CO barrier, after which *CO desorbs from Ag and readsorbs spontaneously onto nearby Cu sites. At high *CO coverage, the barrier for *CO—*CO coupling becomes the lowest among competing pathways, whereas at low coverage *CHO formation is preferred. Together, these results show that nanoconfinement and hydrophobicity raise *CO coverage into a regime where *CO—*CO coupling outcompetes *CHO hydrogenation, thereby explaining the nearly exclusive ethanol selectivity. Compared with the earlier superhydrophobic hollow structure [79], the core–shell system sacrifices some productivity (996.2 vs. 450.2 µmol g–1 h–1) but gains substantially higher selectivity (59.6 vs. 94.2%), making O‐Ag@Cu2O a more sophisticated architecture that prioritizes selectivity in ethanol production.

Complementary to these nanoconfinement strategies, Wang et al. [81] addressed facet engineering and reported CuO photocatalysts selectively exposing the (010) facet. Under CO2 photoreduction conditions, CuO(010) achieved 96.9% ethanol selectivity and a yield of 30.5 µmol g–1 h–1, outperforming CuO(111) and nanoparticle‐based references. XAFS analysis revealed lower coordination numbers for both Cu (3.1 vs. 4.1) and O (2.2 vs. 2.9) in CuO(010) compared with CuO(111), indicating a higher density of tricoordinated Cu (Cu3c) and dicoordinated O (O2c) sites. DFT–PBE(D3)+U calculations were then used to compare C—C coupling pathways across facets. On CuO(111), the rate‐determining step is the initial hydrogenation of CO2 to *COOH, and subsequent *CO hydrogenation to *CHO or *CO—CO coupling is less favorable than *CO desorption, consistent with CO being the main product. In contrast, on CuO(010), CO2 activation is more favorable and *CO adopts a bridging configuration between adjacent Cu3c sites. This geometry strengthens *CO binding, as confirmed by Bader charge analysis and CO‐TPD measurements showing higher *CO coverages. Importantly, adjacent Cu3c–O2c ensembles stabilize the *COCO intermediate, making the symmetric *CO dimerization the preferred C—C coupling pathway, while the asymmetric *CO—*CHO route is disfavored by the high formation energy of *CHO. Operando DRIFTS corroborated the accumulation of *COCO and its hydrogenated derivatives, consistent with the dominant symmetric pathway. In parallel, optical and electrochemical characterizations (UV–vis spectroscopy, tr‐PL, transient photocurrent responses) showed that CuO(010) exhibits superior charge separation, longer carrier lifetimes, and broader solar‐light absorption than CuO(111), all of which support its higher AQE for ethanol formation.

SrTiO3‐Based Catalysts

SrTiO3 is a well‐established wide‐bandgap semiconductor (∼3.20 eV) known for its chemical and photochemical stability, and for the structural flexibility of its perovskite framework, which enables diverse modification strategies [224, 225]. Its CB lies at a sufficiently negative potential to thermodynamically drive CO2 reduction [226], although its intrinsic activity remains limited by its UV‐only absorption and its poor selectivity toward C2 products [227, 228]. As a result, considerable efforts have focused on modifying SrTiO3 through metal doping, surface decoration, or heterojunction formation to extend its light response and stabilize intermediates involved in C—C coupling [224, 226, 229, 230].

Within this context, Yu et al. [82] developed a SrTiO3 composite incorporating Cu, Ni, and TiN plasmonic nanoparticles to achieve full‐spectrum absorption. Under CO2 photoreduction conditions, the material delivered 79.0% ethanol selectivity with a yield of 21.3 µmol g–1 h–1. The composite exhibited significantly enhanced solar absorption, with pronounced localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) peaks at 450 and 607 nm. PL spectroscopy revealed prolonged charge‐carrier lifetimes and suppressed radiative recombination, both essential for multielectron reduction. DFT–PBE calculations further showed that the Cu@Ni interface strengthened CO2 adsorption and lowered activation barriers, thereby facilitating C—C coupling through CO dimerization. However, cycling tests indicated performance degradation after three runs, which XRD attributed to oxidation of the Cu and TiN nanoparticles, reinforcing the importance of balancing broader light absorption with long‐term structural durability.

A mechanistically distinct strategy was introduced by Narendran et al. [83] who incorporated the Mo2AlB2 transition‐metal aluminum boride phase as cocatalyst in a SrTiO3/Mo2AlB2 hybrid. While originally developed for carbendazim degradation, the system showed remarkably high activity for CO2 photoreduction, achieving an ethanol yield of 9.9 mmol g–1 h–1, over an order of magnitude higher than pristine SrTiO3. XPS analysis revealed opposite binding‐energy shifts in SrTiO3 (positive) and Mo2AlB2 (negative), indicating electron migration from SrTiO3 to the Mo2AlB2 phase and formation of a Schottky junction. DFT–PW91 calculations on a SrTiO3(110) slab model (the facet identified by HRTEM) showed that *CO binds far more strongly than *C2H5OH, disfavoring *CO desorption and channeling reactivity toward *CO retention and C—C coupling. This strong *CO stabilization, combined with Schottky junction‐assisted charge transfer, offers a plausible explanation for the unusually high C2 selectivity. The hydrophobicity of the composite (contact angle > 90°) further minimized competition from aqueous species, creating an interfacial environment more conducive to C—C bond formation.

TiO2‐Based Catalysts

Titanium dioxide is one of the most extensively studied photocatalysts owing to its abundance, nontoxicity, corrosion resistance, and low cost [190, 231, 232]. With a bandgap in the range of 3.003.20 eV, its intrinsic activity is largely confined to the UV region [190, 231]. Although its conduction‐band potential is thermodynamically capable of reducing CO2, the available overpotential is often insufficient to efficiently drive multielectron pathways leading to ethanol [233]. Nevertheless, decades of work have established that TiO2 can be transformed into an effective CO2‐to‐ethanol photocatalyst through bandgap engineering, heterojunction construction, cocatalyst deposition, and surface modification strategies [234, 235].

A clear illustration of such modifications was provided by Pastrana‐Martínez et al. [84], who examined graphene oxide–TiO2 (GOT) composites decorated with Cu. The system showed strong pH‐dependent product selectivity: mild acidic conditions (pH 4) favored methanol formation (47.0 µmol g–1 h–1), whereas alkaline conditions (pH 11) shifted selectivity to ethanol (144.7 µmol g–1 h–1). This shift was attributed to changes in the dominant CO2‐derived intermediates, with proton‐rich environments promoting C1 termination and alkaline environments enabling C—C coupling. Incorporation of 4.0 wt% graphene oxide narrowed the bandgap to 2.95 eV and enhanced visible‐light absorption, while Cu deposition (especially using CuN as precursor) further increased methanol production (56.2 vs. 6.0 µmol g–1 h–1 for CuN/GOT and thermally treated GOT‐500, respectively). However, ethanol production was highest on pristine GOT, likely because thermal treatments used to incorporate Cu also removed oxygen‐rich GO groups essential for efficient heterojunction formation.

Seeharaj et al. [85] extended this approach by developing a three‐component TiO2/rGO/CeO2 photocatalyst by combining ultrasonically treated TiO2 with CeO2 (1.0 wt%) and reduced graphene oxide (rGO, 0.75 wt%). Ultrasonic treatment converted bulk TiO2 into nanosheets with nearly double the surface area (101 vs. 59 m2 g–1). Under CO2 photoreduction, the optimized composite produced methanol and ethanol at rates of 641.0 and 271.0 µmol g–1 h–1, respectively, representing ∼7 times greater activity than pristine TiO2 (Figure 6a). This improvement was attributed to (i) bandgap narrowing to 3.02 eV, enabling absorption of lower energy photons and thereby increasing the number of photogenerated charge carriers (Figure 6b); (ii) more efficient charge separation through the formation of a TiO2/rGO/CeO2 heterojunction; and (iii) synergetic contributions from each component, including the increased surface area of TiO2 nanosheets and its favorable optical properties, the redox flexibility of CeO2 (Ce3+/Ce4+), and the large surface area of rGO. Characterization through BET analysis revealed that, after incorporation of CeO2 and rGO, the surface area of the composite photocatalyst (83 m2 g–1) was slightly lower than that of the bare TiO2 nanosheets (101 m2 g–1) but still significantly higher than that of pristine TiO2. Fluorescence spectroscopy confirmed a reduction in electron–hole recombination, and Raman spectroscopy identified rGO through characteristic D and G bands. The authors proposed the formation of a heterojunction (Figure 6c) in which holes in TiO2 and CeO2 accumulate in the VB of CeO2, while electrons migrate from the CeO2 CB to TiO2 and subsequently to rGO, promoting effective charge separation and transfer.

(a) Ethanol and methanol production rates for the different TiO2‐based systems. (b) Fluorescence spectra of the photocatalysts. (c) Schematic illustration of photocatalytic CO2 reduction over TiO2/rGO/CeO2. Reproduced and adapted with permission [85] 2019, Elsevier.

Building on this architecture, Seeharaj et al. [86] subsequently prepared a CeO2/CuO/TiO2 heterojunction via ball‐milling, identifying 1.0 wt% CeO2 and 3.0 wt% CuO as optimal. The composite achieved an ethanol production rate of 30.5 µmol g–1 h–1, nearly 3 times the activity of pristine TiO2. XRD, TEM, and SEM energy‐dispersive spectroscopy (SEM‐EDS) confirmed crystalline TiO2 and uniform dispersion of both CeO2 and CuO. Bandgap narrowing from 3.11 to 2.88 eV improved visible‐light harvesting, and reduced PL intensity indicated enhanced charge separation. The proposed mechanism mirrors the earlier TiO2/rGO/CeO2 system [85]: holes in the VB of TiO2 migrate to the one of CeO2, where oxidation occurs, while electrons transfer from CeO2 to the CB of TiO2 and ultimately to CuO, which serves both as an electron acceptor and a CO2 adsorption site. The study was limited by incomplete methanol quantification due to experimental constraints, but the ethanol enhancement remained clear.

Lertthanaphol et al. [87] further explored metal‐modified TiO2/GO composites (M‐TiO2/GO; M = Ag, Cu, Pd) prepared through a one‐pot hydrothermal synthesis with 1.0 wt% metal loading. The catalysts followed the activity trend Cu–TiO2/GO > Pd–TiO2/GO > Ag–TiO2/GO, producing ethanol at 233.0, 125.0, and 109.0 µmol g–1 h–1, respectively, all outperforming the metal‐free composites (59.0 µmol g–1 h–1). UV–vis spectroscopy showed that all metal‐decorated systems exhibited bandgap narrowing and improved visible‐light absorption. FTIR and XPS confirmed strong Ti–O–C and Ti–C interactions between TiO2 and GO, while BET analysis indicated significantly increased surface areas, particularly for Pd–TiO2/GO (214 m2 g–1). The activity trend, however, did not correlate with surface area alone, highlighting that efficient charge transfer (not only surface structure) is the primary driver of performance. GO played a crucial role by stabilizing TiO2 nanoparticles, preventing agglomeration, and providing rapid electron‐transport pathways that facilitated multielectron CO2 reduction.

In‐Based Catalysts

Although less explored than other semiconductor families, In‐containing materials have recently gained attention as promising platforms for photocatalytic CO2 reduction. In2O3 is an n‐type semiconductor with a tunable direct bandgap (typically 2.70–3.90 eV, depending on the resulting structure after synthesis) [236, 237, 238] and offers adjustable morphologies, broad doping flexibility, and favorable electronic properties [239]. Its wide bandgap, however, limits its solar‐photon utilization. In parallel, indium tin sulfide (In4SnS8), a ternary metal sulfide commonly obtained as nanosheets, has a narrower bandgap and visible‐light activity, making it a complementary candidate for C2‐selective CO2 photoreduction [240, 241]. Together, these materials outline an emerging class of indium‐based photocatalysts whose performance can be strongly modulated by structural disorder, defect chemistry, and single‐atom engineering.

Within this growing landscape, Pan et al. [88] introduced an amorphization‐induced cation‐exchange strategy that enabled In2O3 nanosheets to incorporate unusually high loadings of metallic single atoms under mild conditions. The amorphous material delivered an ethanol production rate of 798.7 µmol g–1 h–1 with 99.5% selectivity. DFT–PBE calculations showed that amorphization fundamentally alters cation‐exchange thermodynamics, lowering the formation energies of indium vacancies (4.99 eV vs. 9.99 eV) and Cu–In substitution (–0.32 eV vs. 1.82 eV) compared with crystalline In2O3. EXAFS‐derived structural models indicated oxygen aggregation around indium vacancies, creating abundant undercoordinated sites that electrostatically favor Cu incorporation. These predictions were experimentally validated: inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP‐OES) showed 12.2 atom% Cu in the amorphous phase versus only 2.2 atom% in the crystalline analog, while XANES/EXAFS and XPS confirmed atomically dispersed Cu species in mixed Cu+/Cu2+ states with no detectable clustering. Optical and electrochemical measurements (UV–vis, PL, transient photocurrent, EIS) revealed bandgap modulation, enhanced visible‐light harvesting, and improved charge separation, aligning with the superior photocatalytic performance. In situ DRIFTS further identified *COOH, *CO, and *COCO species, with the latter intensifying under illumination, indicating that Cu+ sites stabilize *CO and drive C—C coupling. Signals assigned to *COH and *C2H5O further supported the ethanol pathway.

A complementary approach was reported by Gong et al. [89], who synthesized In2O3 decorated with isolated 3‐coordinated Cu+ sites via ion exchange followed by thermal activation. This catalyst reached an ethanol production rate of 20.7 µmol g–1 h–1 with 85.8% selectivity. HAADF‐STEM, EXAFS, XANES, and XPS consistently showed atomically dispersed Cu+ sites anchored through Cu–O–In linkages, creating Cu—O3 coordination units that facilitated intermediate transfer. In situ DRIFTS and Raman spectroscopy revealed a tandem mechanism in which In2O3 generates abundant *CO that migrates to Cu sites, where it forms *COH and subsequently *OCCOH through coupling with a neighboring *CO. DFT–PBE‐calculated density of states, Bader charge, and COHP analyses revealed that strong Cu–In d–p/d–d hybridization shifts the Cu 3d band center closer to the Fermi level, which enhances charge transfer and Cu–C orbital overlap with *CO/*OCCOH on three‐coordinated Cu sites. This electronic configuration stabilizes C2 species and thermodynamically favors *CO—*COH coupling, thereby promoting C—C coupling.

Huang et al. developed a low S‐vacancy In4SnS8 photocatalyst incorporating Cu sites [90]. Although the ethanol production rate was low (0.7 µmol g–1 h–1), the system achieved 100% selectivity. XRD, XPS, and TEM confirmed incorporation of Cu into the lattice, primarily as Cu+, without generating secondary phases. XPS peak shifts toward lower binding energies for the non‐Cu elements indicated increased local electron density from Cu doping. EPR measurements showed that moderate Cu loading (≤150 μL) suppressed S‐vacancy formation, while excessive Cu reintroduced vacancies. DFT–PBE + U calculations supported these findings, indicating that controlled Cu incorporation increases S‐vacancy formation energies. This defect suppression correlated with longer carrier lifetimes in PL spectra. In situ FTIR detected *COOH, *CO, *OCCO(H), and *OCH2CH3 intermediates, consistent with an ethanol formation pathway. DFT calculations further demonstrated that pristine In4SnS8 suffers from high energy barriers for *CO2 to *COOH and *CO to *COH conversions, which become even more unfavorable in vacancy‐rich samples. In contrast, Cu doping introduces adjacent adsorption sites near In atoms, lowering the Gibbs energies of these steps and promoting *CO—*COH coupling, in agreement with the FTIR bands associated with *OCCO(H).

Other Semiconductor Photocatalysts

Although the preceding sections focus on the most widely studied semiconductor families, several additional materials have made meaningful contributions to CO2‐to‐ethanol photocatalysis. While less extensively explored, these systems offer distinctive properties and mechanistic insights that broaden the landscape of photocatalyst design. The following examples highlight how interfacial engineering, defect modulation, alloy formation, and organic–inorganic hybridization can collectively offer more options for selective C2 formation.

Mishra et al. [91] demonstrated this potential by developing Ti3C2MXene/CeO2 heterostructures via a single‐step hydrothermal synthesis. The optimal composite (5.0 wt% MXene) achieved an ethanol production rate of ∼1225.0 µmol g–1 h–1 with 98.0% selectivity, representing a 6‐fold improvement over pristine CeO2. Structural characterization (XRD peak shifts, Raman band evolution) confirmed the formation of a stable heterojunction, while optical and electrochemical measurements (PL, carrier‐lifetime analysis, and EIS) revealed substantially reduced charge recombination and improved interfacial charge transport into the MXene layers. XPS showed mixed Ce3+/Ce4+ states, consistent with enhanced radical generation and suppressed electron–hole recombination. ATR‐FTIR detected bidentate carbonates, and together with a positive Schryver's test and 1H/13C NMR identification of C2 products, supported a formaldehyde‐meditated ethanol pathway [242].

Expanding beyond oxide systems, Chen et al. [92] examined the effect of chalcogen vacancies in molybdenum sulfide selenide (MoSSe), finding that vacancy concentration, tuned through annealing temperature, plays a decisive role. The sample annealed at 500°C possessed the optimal vacancy density and achieved an ethanol production rate of 1.8 µmol g–1 h–1 with 43.0% selectivity. Vacancies generated Moδ+ sites with high d‐orbital electron density, narrowing the bandgap and enhancing charge transfer, consistent with UV–vis, PL, EIS, and photocurrent measurements. Mechanistic analysis combining DFT–PBE(D3) and in situ FTIR suggested that vacancies preferentially stabilize *COH and *CH2 at Mo4+ sites, steering the reaction through a carbene pathway. The authors proposed that C—C coupling occurs via neighboring *C(H)O species associated with vacancy‐adjacent Mo4+ centers.

A different strategy was pursued by Wang et al. [93], who designed a double Z‐scheme SnO2/SnS2/Cu2SnS3 photocatalyst to exploit multistep charge‐transfer pathways. The optimal sample yielded 28.4 µmol g–1 h–1 ethanol with 92.0% selectivity, nearly tripling the performance of the binary SnO2/SnS2 system. UV–vis spectroscopy showed broad absorption across the UV–vis‐NIR range, while tr‐PL confirmed prolonged carrier lifetimes characteristic of efficient Z‐scheme separation. In situ XPS and ultrafast transient absorption spectroscopy verified sequential electron flow from SnO2 to SnS2 and subsequently to Cu2SnS3. In situ DRIFTS detected *CO, *COOH, and *COCOH intermediates, consistent with a *CO—*COH coupling route. DFT–PBE(D3) calculations further showed that Cu2SnS3 lowers the energy for *COOH to CO* conversion, thereby facilitating subsequent C—C coupling.

Hao et al. [94] extended the design space by introducing asymmetric Ce–O–Zn units capable of coupling CH4 and CO2 to ethanol (Scheme 19a). The optimized Ce‐doped ZnO (10.0 wt% Ce) reached 580.0 µmol g–1 h–1 ethanol, with ethanol as the dominant product. The enhanced activity was in part attributed to the formation of abundant Ce–O–Zn interfacial structures, as indicated by XRD peak shifts consistent with lattice contraction. A redshift of the Zn–O stretching band in FTIR, together with binding‐energy shifts in XPS (Zn 2p to higher energies and Ce 3d to lower energies), further supported strong electronic interactions. The asymmetric Ce–O–Zn sites operated cooperatively: Zn sites oxidized CH4 to CH3OOH intermediates, while adjacent Ce centers reduced CO2 to *CO. HRTEM confirmed their spatial proximity, enabling surface coupling between CH3‐derived and CO2‐derived fragments. Isotope‐labeling experiments (13CH4/12CO2) conclusively showed that the ethanol –CH3 group originates from methane and –CH2OH from CO2.

Schematic view of the reaction pathways over the different site configuration. (a) Symmetric Ce–O–Zn sites and (b) separated Ce–O and Zn–O sites. Reproduced with permission [94] 2024, Wiley, VCH.

Turning to alloy‐based strategies, Wang and co‐workers [95] synthesized a single‐atom alloy catalyst consisting of isolated Au atoms embedded within a Cu host matrix (Au1Cu) supported on carbon sheets. The optimal formulation (0.5 wt% Au and 5.0 wt% Cu) produced ethanol at 4.7 µmol g–1 h–1 with 92.5% selectivity (Figure 7a). Aberration‐corrected STEM, XAFS, and XPS confirmed atomically dispersed Au coordinated by Cu, forming a Cu–Au–Cu environment. Spectroscopic analysis indicated that the incorporation of single Au atoms modifies the electronic structure of the Cu nanoparticles: the Cu 2p and Cu‐LLM Auger signals shift in a way consistent with electronic density flowing from Au into the Cu host. This charge redistribution upshifts the Cu d‐band center toward the Fermi level and increases the density of states near it, which includes metal–metal antibonding states identified by COHP analysis. STEM electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) plasmon mapping and UV–vis spectroscopy showed an enhanced LSPR effect relative to monometallic Cu (Figure 7b,c). DFT–PBE calculations indicated that, compared to Cu, the *CO—*CHO coupling step on Au1Cu is associated with a smaller Gibbs energy increase, consistent with an increased *CO coverage on the alloy surface (Figure 7d).

Tan et al. [96] explored an entirely organic route by designing a melon‐based polymeric semiconductor modified with nitrogen vacancies and oxygen dopants (ON‐MOP) that act as dual charge‐asymmetric active sites. The material achieved an ethanol production rate of 800.0 µmol g–1 h–1 with 97.0% selectivity, far surpassing pristine polymeric carbon nitride, which produced mostly CO and CH4. XPS, EPR, and XANES confirmed successful O doping and N‐vacancy formation, while Bader charge analysis showed asymmetric charge distribution around the active sites. UV–vis spectroscopy revealed enhanced absorption and a reduced bandgap, whereas temperature‐programmed PL showed lower exciton‐binding energies. TPD verified strengthened CO2 adsorption, and ATR‐SEIRAS captured *COCOH signatures, consistent with a C—C coupling pathway facilitated by charge asymmetric active sites.

Finally, Altalbawy et al. [97] demonstrated a strategy to enhance the performance of MOFs in photocatalytic CO2 reduction by incorporating a π‐conjugated perylenediimide (PDI) into UiO‐67 and introducing Cu as the active site. The best‐performing material, Cu@PDI(30%)‐NZU67, achieved an ethanol production rate of 941.3 µmol g–1 h–1 with 100% selectivity, corresponding to a 1.9–223‐fold improvement over previously reported MOF systems. CO2 adsorption isotherms at 298 K showed higher uptake at increased PDI loadings despite the lower BET surface area, which the authors attributed to the greater density of N‐containing heteroatoms providing additional binding sites. UV–vis spectroscopy, PL/tr‐PL, photocurrent, and EIS measurements indicated stronger visible‐light absorption, improved charge separation, longer carrier lifetimes, and larger charge mobility. Based on these results, the authors proposed a ligand‐to‐metal charge‐transfer process in which photoexcited PDI radical anions transfer electrons to Cu2+ centers, generating Cu+ species that are active for CO2 reduction.

Challenges and Opportunities

Thermal and photocatalytic CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion have advanced considerably in recent years, but they remain at very different stages of technological maturity. Thermocatalytic systems can already reach CO2 conversions and STYs that appear promising for scale‐up; however, they still face persistent limitations in ethanol selectivity, long‐term stability, process integration, and their frequent dependence on precious metals. Photocatalytic systems, by contrast, offer a sunlight‐driven route to ethanol but currently suffer from extremely low rates, poor selectivity, and incomplete mechanistic understanding.

Among the challenges common to both routes, selectivity is perhaps the most fundamental. Ethanol is a 12‐electron C2 product that competes with multiple thermodynamically and kinetically favored pathways. Under thermal conditions, deep hydrogenation often leads to methane or higher hydrocarbons, whereas partially hydrogenated intermediates frequently terminate as methanol. Under illumination, CO, formate, methane, and methanol dominate because they require fewer electrons and circumvent the need for C—C coupling. Consequently, ethanol is often obtained only as a minor product and reported “high” ethanol selectivity is frequently achieved at the expense of overall productivity. In contrast to thermocatalytic systems, which reach ethanol productivities of a few mmol g–1 h–1, photocatalytic CO2‐to‐ethanol rates are typically in the µmol g–1 h–1 range. Yet ethanol has a substantially higher market value and broader industrial utility than C1 products, so even incremental improvements in ethanol selectivity can carry considerable economic significance. This highlights the value of catalyst design strategies that explicitly target ethanol rather than simply aiming to maximize total CO2 conversion.

Despite their potential, these technologies still face key barriers to scale‐up, with catalyst cost remaining one of the most important, as it can make a non‐negligible contribution to overall process cost and introduce substantial uncertainty in commercial deployment [243]. This is particularly relevant for Au‐ and platinum‐group‐metal‐based systems, given both the high value of metal inventory and the broader supply‐risk concerns associated with critical metals [244]. Nevertheless, the overall catalyst cost also depends on catalyst loading, synthesis/manufacturing steps, and catalyst lifetime rather than on metal price alone [245].

Stability under reaction conditions represents another major obstacle. In thermocatalysis, sintering, phase segregation, redox cycling, and carbon deposition can progressively deactivate active sites or disrupt the delicate balance between metal and oxide phases needed for selective C—C coupling, while at scale such degradation may translate into premature catalyst replacement rather than a simple loss of activity [246]. Therefore, catalytic deactivation studies must be given special attention since comparison between normal operation and artificial aging highlights how laboratory protocols may not fully reproduce industrial deactivation pathways [247]. In photocatalysis, light‐induced corrosion, ligand degradation in MOFs and molecular systems, and restructuring of plasmonic nanoparticles similarly diminish performance over time. Taken together, these considerations underscore that catalyst cost must be considered explicitly when evaluating the scale‐up potential of CO2‐to‐ethanol technologies, while improvements in catalyst stability will require not only more robust materials, but also stability protocols that link operando, multitechnique diagnostics with reactor‐relevant aging and mitigation strategies across scales [248].

In addition, reactor and process design cannot be neglected, since even when catalytic performance is promising, practical implementation will require continuous processing, consideration of reactor configuration, and integrated recycle and downstream separation processes rather than isolated reactor metrics [249, 250]. This is particularly important because ethanol recovery from dilute water‐containing streams may itself become a major bottleneck, and oversimplified separation assumptions can lead to misleading conclusions about practical viability [251, 252].

The thermocatalytic literature surveyed here demonstrates that ethanol can be produced at meaningful rates under high pressure H2, but often at the cost of either selectivity or process efficiency. High CO2 conversion generally requires elevated temperatures and pressures that favor overhydrogenation and chain growth. From a sustainability standpoint, these conditions imply a substantial demand for renewable H2 and its compression, together with a significant heat load. Unless both the H2 supply and the heat input are fully decarbonized, these energy requirements can offset the climate benefits of converting CO2 to ethanol. Therefore, it is important to note that scale‐up of CO2‐to‐ethanol technologies is strongly conditioned by the H2 source, since most global H2 is still fossil‐derived and low‐emission H2 remains only a very small fraction of the total production [253]. In addition, green H2 also remains substantially more expensive than fossil‐derived H2, so improving its cost‐competitiveness, and therefore its practical use in hydrogen‐intensive routes such as CO2‐to‐ethanol, will require broader access to inexpensive renewable electricity, supportive policy frameworks that incentivize deployment, and continued improvements in electrolyzer technology [253, 254]. This motivates the development of catalysts that remain selective at lower temperatures and pressures while being compatible with renewable H2 sources. Descriptor‐based insights from theory offer one avenue for rational catalyst design: by anticipating how alloying, alkali promoting, or support modification shift a catalyst's position within the selectivity map (Scheme 2), one can predict whether these modifications move the material toward or away from an ethanol‐selective regime [157].

For photocatalysis, a central limitation is the low CO2 conversion rate, driven primarily by inefficient solar‐energy utilization. Many semiconductors have suboptimal bandgaps, absorbing only UV or a narrow portion of the visible spectrum. Photogenerated charge carriers also recombine rapidly, reducing the fraction of electrons available for CO2 reduction. Opportunities therefore lie in designing photocatalysts with broader light absorption and improved charge separation. As discussed, some approaches include bandgap engineering and the construction of heterojunctions that pair semiconductors with complementary band structures, allowing spatial separation of electrons and holes and more efficient delivery of charges to reduction and oxidation sites. Another promising strategy is the use of bimetallic cocatalysts [255, 256, 257, 258, 259]. These often exhibit synergistic effects not accessible to single‐metal systems [255], including Schottky‐barrier tuning [260, 261], hydrogen spillover or inverse spillover [262], and stabilization of small metal domains [263]. Together, these effects strengthen charge separation and facilitate multielectron transfer steps that could improve CO2 conversion efficiency.

A further opportunity involves replacing plasmonic noble metals such as Au and Ag, commonly used for light harvesting or as electron sinks, with more earth‐abundant alternatives like Cu or Al. Achieving this, however, will require strategies that match the optical and electronic advantages of these noble metals while ensuring stability under illumination and in the presence of sacrificial reagents.

In this context, two promising technologies are particularly relevant. On the one hand, tandem photothermal catalytic systems represent an emerging opportunity to enhance the overall efficiency of CO2 conversion processes by combining thermally driven surface chemistry with light‐assisted activation, achieving catalytic activities that exceed the simple arithmetic sum of individual components [264, 265, 266]. For instance, modification of thermocatalyst surfaces with plasmonic metals offers a particularly attractive route, as irradiation can induce localized electromagnetic fields and photothermal heating effects that promote surface reactions and complex processes like C—C coupling [264, 267, 268]. Such hybrid systems could improve the utilization of the solar spectrum while simultaneously benefiting from the robustness and maturity of thermocatalytic platforms [266, 267, 268]. Although this concept remains scarcely explored for ethanol synthesis and faces significant challenges regarding selectivity, this approach opens a promising avenue for future catalyst design by bridging the advantages of thermal and photo‐assisted pathways.

On the other hand, direct air CO2 capture (DAC) integration also represents an emerging direction within the broader CO2‐to‐alcohol field. Proof‐of‐concept studies have already demonstrated the capture of atmospheric CO2 followed by its conversion into methanol, supporting the feasibility of integrated CO2‐to‐alcohol schemes [269]. However, extension to ethanol remains largely unexplored and still faces important challenges related to sorption, regeneration, conversion conditions, product separation, and scalability.

A deeper mechanistic understanding remains essential. The elementary steps leading to ethanol formation under photocatalytic conditions are still not fully established [242], and it is often unclear which intermediates govern selectivity or how ethanol formation competes with pathways to CO, formate, methane, and methanol. Operando spectroscopy and microscopy, combined with computational modeling [270, 271, 272, 273], are therefore critical for establishing reliable structure–property relationships. At the same time, careful interpretation of these techniques is required, as experimental conditions and data analysis can significantly influence mechanistic conclusions. However, operando measurements provide ensemble‐averaged properties that reflect many coexisting microstates whose relative populations depend on many factors such as temperature, illumination, solvent or reaction medium, and dynamic surface restructuring. In contrast, atomistic models resolve individual microstates, making it essential to carefully select the relevant structural degrees of freedom and level of theory. Surface coverages, reconstruction motifs, defect states, and, in bimetallic systems, segregation patterns are particularly important in this context.

In practice, the accessible microstate space is extremely large, and exhaustive sampling is computationally intractable. This gap between experimentally accessible observables and atomistic representations highlights the need for advanced sampling and modeling strategies. Microkinetic modeling enables quantitative analysis of competing pathways, including C1 activation, hydrogenation, C—C coupling, and product‐desorption, while kinetic Monte Carlo approaches can further capture the time evolution of surface configurations and adsorbate populations on explicitly defined lattices. Machine‐learning‐based approaches, including accelerated global optimization, enhanced sampling of adsorbate coverages, active‐learning workflows, and machine learning potentials, can further expand the accessible chemical and configurational space at reduced computational cost [274, 275, 276]. Without such approaches, it remains difficult to connect microstate‐resolved energetics to macroscopic observables such as turnover rates or product selectivities.

Although these modeling challenges are becoming increasingly manageable in thermocatalysis, where descriptor‐based frameworks already help guide alloying, promoter selection, support design, and operating‐window optimization, photocatalytic CO2 reduction still lacks an analogous descriptor landscape [277]. In thermocatalysis, artificial intelligence, machine learning, and high‐throughput screening are already accelerating catalyst development by correlating catalyst descriptors with activity and selectivity, reducing dependence on exhaustive trial‐and‐error screening, and enabling faster identification of promising catalyst formulations and reaction conditions. In photocatalysis, similar approaches are highly attractive, but their development remains more limited because catalyst performance is governed by an even more complex and high‐dimensional parameter space, including band structure, heterojunction architecture, cocatalyst loading, defect density, irradiation conditions, mass transfer, and reactor configuration, in addition to photoexcitation, charge transport, and illumination‐induced surface dynamics. Therefore, once robust descriptor sets are established, they could be integrated into DFT‐ or machine‐learning‐based high‐throughput screening platforms, enabling a more systematic exploration of catalyst compositions, heterojunction combinations, cocatalyst configurations, and reaction conditions. For ethanol synthesis in particular, these data‐driven approaches are especially relevant, since both thermocatalytic and photocatalytic routes must simultaneously optimize CO2 activation, intermediate stabilization, suppression of side reactions, and, critically, C—C coupling. Thus, artificial intelligence is unlikely to replace mechanistic catalysis research, but it can substantially accelerate it by narrowing the chemical search space, prioritizing promising materials, and supporting more rational discovery of efficient non‐noble systems [278, 279, 280].

As global ethanol demand is expected to grow in the coming years, there is increasing pressure to reduce the environmental footprint of its production and to explore complementary alternatives such as CO2‐to‐ethanol routes. A comparison between biomass‐, CO2‐, and fossil‐derived ethanol is therefore necessary to assess the challenges and potential benefits associated with CO2‐based pathways. Such a comparison should not be framed as a competition between biomass and synthetic routes, but rather as an evaluation of their respective roles within a more sustainable fuel system.

From a life‐cycle perspective, fossil‐derived ethanol remains the highest‐emission benchmark. Biomass‐derived ethanol generally exhibits lower, but variable, emissions due to land use, resource requirements, and feedstock variability [281]. By contrast, CO2‐derived ethanol does not inherently guarantee lower environmental impacts, as its performance depends strongly on the carbon intensity of electricity, hydrogen sourcing, and the energy demands associated with CO2 activation, H2 production, and downstream processing [281, 282].

Recent comparative life‐cycle assessments indicate that, under specific conditions, CO2‐derived ethanol can approach or, in some cases, outperform fossil‐derived and certain biomass‐based pathways in terms of greenhouse gas emissions. However, these outcomes are highly scenario‐dependent and sensitive to assumptions regarding system boundaries, coproduct allocation, CO2 source, electricity mix, and end‐of‐life considerations [281, 282, 283]. Overall, biomass‐derived ethanol represents a more mature but resource‐intensive route, whereas CO2‐to‐ethanol offers carbon circularity and reduced reliance on biomass, albeit with a stronger dependence on low‐carbon energy and hydrogen supply [281, 282].

Prospective life‐cycle assessments of photocatalytic CO2 reduction further suggest that, although the production of advanced catalysts can be resource‐intensive, improvements in conversion efficiency and catalyst stability may reduce overall environmental impacts compared to conventional fossil‐based routes. Sensitivity analyses also highlight catalyst recyclability as a critical factor, as increasing catalyst lifetime can significantly reduce greenhouse gas emissions and resource depletion. These findings indicate that, for photocatalytic pathways to become viable, future developments must prioritize not only higher yields but also long‐term durability and the use of renewable energy inputs [284].

Moreover, the lack of long‐term and cyclic stability testing, together with nonstandardized performance indicators, still limits the realistic assessment of many photocatalytic systems. This is evident from the studies compiled in this review that are summarized in Table 3, where the most recurrent gap is the absence of photon‐to‐product efficiency metrics, particularly AQE and STF. Reporting high product yields under short irradiation times is insufficient to demonstrate technological relevance, particularly when catalyst deactivation, product readsorption, photocorrosion of materials, or gradual loss of active sites may occur during extended use. Similarly, without performance metrics such as AQE or STF efficiency, direct comparison between studies becomes difficult and sometimes misleading. Therefore, there is a need to unify methodologies, and future studies should systematically report AQE and, when possible, STF together with long‐term and cyclic stability tests since these indicators are essential to distinguish genuinely promising photocatalytic systems from materials that only perform well under specific short‐term conditions.

Biographies

Bryan Rocano-Merchan obtained his BSc in Chemistry from the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU) in 2021. He subsequently completed a postgraduate course in Tools for Development in the Chemical Industry. In 2023, he was awarded the Araba Talent scholarship, which enabled him to pursue an MSc in Sustainable Energy and Green Technologies at University College Dublin (UCD). Since 2024, he has been pursuing a PhD jointly between CIC energiGUNE and UPV/EHU, focused on the development of multifunctional materials for CO2 capture and valorization into synthetic alcohols.

Alejandro Cañete Arché graduated from Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona in 2023 with a BSc in Nanoscience and Nanotechnology. Soon after, he joined the group of Prof. Max García‐Melchor at Trinity College Dublin, where he is currently pursuing a PhD in chemistry funded by the Marie Skłodowska‐Curie Actions. His research focuses on the development of atomistic machine learning models for the in silico screening of novel CO2 electroreduction catalysts.

Asier Agrelo‐Lestón is a Chemist with a Master's degree in Environmental Engineering and a PhD in Chemical Process Engineering from the Technical University of Catalonia (UPC, 2025). His research experience spans heterogeneous catalysis, photocatalysis, and material characterization, with a particular focus on the development of TiO2‐based photocatalysts for solar‐driven hydrogen production. Since joining CIC energiGUNE in 2025, his work has focused on the design and synthesis of ionic liquid‐based catalytic systems for sustainable fuel production and the advancement of more sustainable catalytic processes.

Jonatan Pérez‐Arce is a Chemical Engineer with a PhD in Renewable Materials Engineering from the University of the Basque Country and Tecnalia (2020) with experience as a R&D project technician. Since 2022, he has been a postdoctoral researcher at CIC energiGUNE, where he combines his previous experience in biomass upgrading for the development of bio‐based products with his work on ionic liquid technologies for the development of novel catalytic systems and materials for thermal energy storage applications with new strategies for plastic chemical recycling and more sustainable thermo‐catalytic processes using conventional and non‐conventional heating methods.

Zoraida Freixa completed her PhD in homogeneous catalysis from the Autonomous University of Barcelona (UAB). After completing a three‐year postdoctoral appointment in Prof. van Leeuwen's group at the University of Amsterdam (UvA), she joined the Institute of Chemical Research of Catalonia (ICIQ) as a Ramón y Cajal Researcher. Since 2010, following a brief appointment as a Lecturer at the University of Barcelona (UB), she has held the position of Ikerbasque Research Professor at the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU), San Sebastián, leading a research group focused on the development of photoswitchable and chiral organometallic compounds, with particular emphasis on their catalytic and luminescent behavior.

Ander Centeno‐Pedrazo earned his B.Sc. in chemistry (2017) and M.Sc. in applied chemistry and polymers (2018) at the University of the Basque Country (EHU). He completed his Ph.D. in 2023 on ionic liquid catalysis for CO2–epoxide cycloaddition and bio‐based nonisocyanate polyurethanes. In 2023, he joined Queen's University Belfast as a postdoctoral researcher, working on heterogeneous catalysts for sustainable aviation fuels. In 2024, he joined CIC energiGUNE as a postdoctoral researcher, where he develops ionic liquid‐based catalysts for sustainable fuels.

Max García‐Melchor is an Ikerbasque Research Professor at CIC energiGUNE, where he leads the Atomistic & Molecular Modelling for Catalysis group. His research combines state‐of‐the‐art computational chemistry with artificial intelligence to accelerate the discovery of catalysts for sustainable chemical and fuel production. He earned his Ph.D. in chemistry at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona and has 15+ years of experience in modeling (photo)(electro)catalytic processes and developing high‐throughput workflows for rational catalyst design.

Eduardo J. Garcia‐ Suárez is an Ikerbasque Research Associate Professor at CIC energiGUNE and has led the “Thermocatalysis & Ionic Liquids” group since October 2022. His research focuses on multifunctional catalytic systems and ionic‐liquid‐based technologies for CO2 capture and conversion, biomass upgrading, and plastic waste chemical recycling. His work is guided by sustainability, circularity, and a strong commitment to applying green chemistry principles to advance modern catalytic science toward sustainable solutions with real‐world applications.
