A hydrogel vascular closure device for hemostasis after transfemoral intervention: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Shuhong Yu, Zhichao Huang, Zhimai Lyu, Min Li, Bo Ye, Guoyong Zeng, Jiaping Xu, Huaishun Wang, Jie Hou, Yuehui Liu, Yage Zhao, Zhiliang Guo, Guodong Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn this randomized controlled trial we aim to validate the efficacy and safety of a hydrogel vascular closure device (VCD) for hemostasis after transfemoral intervention.MethodsBetween January and August 2023, 212 patients were enrolled, including 202 in a non-inferiority randomized controlled trial (1:1 hydrogel vs ExoSeal; prespecified margin −10%) and 10 in a hydrogel-only observational arm (8 F). The primary endpoint was device success rate and secondary endpoints including hemostasis time and procedural blood loss.ResultsAmong 102 hydrogel and 100 ExoSeal recipients, hydrogel had non-inferior success rates (99.02% vs 94.00%; 95% CI −0.27% to 11.74%). Hydrogel achieved significantly faster hemostasis (1.99 vs 3.14 min, P<0.001) and reduced blood loss (0.83 mL vs 8.93 mL, P<0.001). No major access site complications were observed in either group. No secondary complications occurred in patients in the experimental group and secondary complications occurred in one patient in the control group (P=0.497). The supplementary cohort of 10 patients supported the efficacy and safety of hydrogel VCD.ConclusionsHydrogel VCD shows non-inferior efficacy to ExoSeal for transfemoral cerebrovascular interventions, with superior hemostatic speed and reduced blood loss while maintaining comparable safety.Trial registration numberChiCTR2300068029 (https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=178962)

Introduction

Percutaneous femoral access revolutionized by the Seldinger technique underpins over 7 million annual endovascular procedures globally.1 Effective puncture site closure remains critical, as traditional manual compression necessitates prolonged immobilization (12–24 hours of bedrest), clinician labor, and patient discomfort.24

Vascular closure devices (VCDs) have emerged as safer alternatives enabling early ambulation, with passive approximators like ExoSeal minimizing vascular trauma through bioabsorbable plugs.511 However, current iterations face limitations, including extended material absorption timelines (≥60 days) and significant intraoperative blood loss (8–15 mL) during deployment.810

Hydrogel technology offers biomimetic solutions through hydrophilic polymer networks which show exceptional fluid absorption (≥95% water content) and rapid biodegradation (30-day clearance).1215 Preclinical studies in porcine models showed that the hydrogel effectively achieved hemostasis in high pressure bleeding scenarios, including 4–5 mm carotid artery incisions and 6 mm diameter cardiac perforations. These results support its potential clinical utility as an advanced sealant for vascular intervention-related wounds.14 Our certified hydrogel-based VCD offers the following advantages: polyethylene glycol-based plug with optimized absorption kinetics; broad compatibility with 5–8 French (F) sheaths (1F ≈ 0.33 mm diameter); and dual mechanical adhesion (vessel wall) and volumetric plug expansion with reduced intraoperative blood loss. This randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted to verify the effectiveness and safety of the hydrogel VCD for hemostasis after femoral artery puncture, with the comparator being the ExoSeal VCD. We aim to establish evidence for the clinical use of hydrogel VCDs in interventional hemostasis management.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This is a prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled clinical trial with a secondary single-arm observational component. A non-inferiority design was adopted for the randomized controlled trial component of the main cohort, which was conducted at four centers.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged 18–80 years, with no restriction on sex; (2) patients undergoing interventional procedures via femoral artery puncture; and (3) patients undergoing interventional procedures using 5 F–8 F sheaths with an effective length of ≤12 cm (patients using 5 F–7 F sheaths were entered into the randomized controlled component of the study, while patients using 8 F sheaths were entered into the single-group observational component). The major exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with common femoral artery occlusion or a lumen diameter of <5 mm on visual inspection; (2) patients who had previously undergone a surgical procedure, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty, stent placement, or vascular graft placement at the access site; (3) patients with visual calcification at the access site or the presence of clinically significant peripheral vascular disease (requiring interventional procedures) or implantation of a stent ≤1 cm from the puncture point; and (4) patients in whom the puncture point was located above the lowermost edge of the inferior abdominal wall artery and/or above the inguinal ligament according to skeletal landmarks. More details on the enrollment process are provided in onlinesupplemental files 2 3.

Randomization

In the randomized controlled component of the trial (main cohort), 210 patients were required. Randomization was performed using the Interactive Web Response System to automatically assign random numbers to the patients for subsequent allocation to the experimental group or the control group (1:1). Patients in the experimental group were treated with the hydrogel VCD (models 5 F, 6 F, or 7 F) manufactured by Shanghai Yiximei Medical Devices Co. Patients in the control group were treated with the ExoSeal VCD (models 5 F, 6 F, or 7 F) manufactured by Cordis Corporation.

ExoSeal was selected as the comparator because it is a fully extravascular bioabsorbable closure device that has been approved by the National Medical Products Administration and has been widely adopted in clinical practice in China. Its similar mechanism of action and extensive use in routine vascular interventions made it a practical and appropriate benchmark for comparison.6 8 16

Since the control group did not have a product model with an 8 F vascular sheath, it did not meet the requirements of the parallel control. The single-group observational component of the study was performed to ensure complete coverage of the test device models. In this part, 10 patients were treated with the 8 F hydrogel VCD.

Endpoints and definitions

The primary endpoint was the VCD success rate, defined as (1) hemostatic plug placement and successful manipulation; (2) hemostasis time ≤5 min; and (3) complete withdrawal of the delivery system outside the body and no intraoperative complications related to the experimental device. The secondary endpoints were the time to hemostasis and blood loss volume during vascular closure. The time to hemostasis was defined as the time (in minutes) from sheath removal to the first observation of successful hemostasis.8 16 After the sheath was withdrawn from the artery and observed after manual compression according to actual clinical procedures, successful hemostasis was confirmed by arterial hemostasis (no or little subcutaneous exudate, no dilatation or hematoma development). Blood loss volume during vessel closure was defined as the volume of bleeding from the femoral artery puncture site from the time of VCD insertion until sheath withdrawal, calculated according to the following formula: blood loss during vessel closure (mL) = (weight of hemostatic gauze after completion of hemostasis (g) − weight of preoperative hemostatic gauze (g))/1.05 (specific gravity of blood).

The main safety outcomes were major access site complications, minor access site complications within 30 days postoperatively, rate of device defects, and rates of all adverse events and serious adverse events during the trial period.8 16 Major access site complications included (1) vascular injury at the access site requiring repair by surgical or interventional procedures; (2) bleeding at the access site requiring blood transfusion; (3) infection associated with the access site requiring antibiotic treatment or extended hospitalization; (4) new ischemia of the ipsilateral lower extremity caused by vascular closure at the access site requiring surgical or additional. intervention; (5) access site-related permanent (lasting >30 days) or neurologic injury requiring surgical treatment. Minor access site complications within 30 days postoperatively included (1) recurrent localized bleeding requiring hemostatic intervention; (2) hematoma ≥6 cm; (3) pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula; and (4) deep vein thrombosis.8 16

Statistical analysis

Assuming a device success rate of 94% for the experimental VCD and a non-inferiority margin of −10%, a one-sided alpha level of 0.025 and a power of 80% indicated that 89 patients were required per group. Considering a maximum anticipated dropout rate of 15% due to the short follow-up period, the sample size was adjusted to 105 patients per group. Thus, a total of 210 patients were required for the study.

The statistical analysis of data from the main cohort (randomized controlled component) was conducted on the following pre-determined analysis populations: full analysis set (FAS), intention-to-treat (ITT) set (all randomized patients who used the VCD), and per-protocol set (PPS) (all patients who completed the trial without major protocol deviations). The efficacy analyses were performed on the FAS and the PPS, and all analyses of baseline demographics were performed on the FAS. The safety analysis set (SAF) included all patients who used the VCD after randomization and who underwent at least one safety evaluation. The safety evaluation was performed in the SAF. Hemostasis time and blood loss were analyzed using t-tests for normally distributed variables or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test when normality assumptions were not met. In addition, a separate descriptive analysis was performed for patients in the single-group observational component. The software used for the statistical analysis was SAS 9.4 or above. All statistical tests were two-sided and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. More details on the statistical analyses are provided in online supplemental file 3.

Results

Patients and procedures

From January 2023 to August 2023, 202 patients were enrolled, including 102 patients in the experimental group (hydrogel VCD) and 100 patients in the control group (ExoSeal VCD). One patient in the control group was excluded from the PPS because they met the exclusion criterion and seriously deviated from the protocol. Therefore, in the randomized controlled component there were 202 patients in the FAS, 201 in the PPS, and 202 in the SAF. Two centers participated in the enrollment of the single-group observational cohort, enrolling a total of 10 patients. All 10 patients in the single-group observational cohort were enrolled and used the experimental device, and all were included in the FAS, PPS, and SAF. In the FAS, the VCD success rate in the experimental and control groups was 99.02% and 94.00%, respectively. The lower limit of the 95% CI for the difference between the two groups was −0.23%, greater than the non-inferiority cut-off value of −10%. Therefore, the conclusion of non-inferiority was established (see table 2 and online supplemental table 1). Subgroup analyses further supported the efficacy of the hydrogel VCD across different patient strata, as shown in online supplemental tables 2 and 3. Security indicators

In both the randomized controlled cohort and the single-group observational cohort, neither the experimental group nor the control group experienced any major access site complications within 30 days after surgery. Regarding the incidence of secondary complications, no patients in the experimental group experienced any secondary access site complications within 30 days after surgery. One case of deep vein thrombosis occurred in the control group, with an incidence rate of 1.09%. The difference in the incidence rate between the two groups was not statistically significant (P=0.497) (table 3). In the single-group observational cohort, one secondary access site complication occurred within 30 days of surgery. The complication was a recurrent localized hemorrhage requiring hemostatic intervention, with an incidence of 10%.

Discussion

In this multicenter randomized clinical trial of patients undergoing a cerebrovascular intervention via transfemoral access, we investigated the efficacy and safety of a hydrogel VCD for hemostasis and compared it with the ExoSeal VCD. We found that, in patients undergoing a cerebrovascular intervention via transfemoral access, the hydrogel VCD was non-inferior to the traditional VCD in terms of the device success rate, time to hemostasis, and blood loss volume during vessel closure.

VCDs are widely used for a variety of procedures requiring transfemoral puncture, including percutaneous coronary endoluminal surgery, neurointerventional procedures, lower extremity arterial intervention, transcatheter aortic valve replacement, and aortic endovascular interventions, among others. Numerous randomized controlled clinical studies have shown that VCDs reduce the time to hemostasis compared with manual compression, and that they do not increase the risk of puncture site complications.5817 18

A 34-trial meta-analysis by Cox et al confirmed that VCDs reduce median hemostasis time by 74% compared with manual compression (5.95 vs 22.9 min, P<0.001), with equivalent complications (13% vs 12%, P=0.38). VCDs also accelerate postoperative recovery (ambulation 3.2 hours vs 6.8 hours; discharge 18.4 hours vs 24.1 hours).19 Safety analyses showed device-specific risk profiles. A 19-study review by Biancari et al20 (n=15 782) associated VCDs with higher rates of infection (0.6% vs 0.2%, RR 2.83 (95% CI 1.15 to 6.98)), limb ischemia (0.3% vs 0%), and surgical repair (0.7% vs 0.4%, P=0.01), whereas a propensity analysis by Arora et al21 (n=7856) showed 58% fewer hematomas in diagnostics (P=0.003) and 42% lower percutaneous coronary intervention complications (P=0.02), despite non-significant increases in retroperitoneal bleeding (0.4% vs 0.2%, P=0.15). These divergent findings emphasize the necessity for procedure-specific risk stratification, particularly considering intervention type (diagnostic vs therapeutic), anticoagulation intensity, and anatomical complexity.

Most VCDs, including ExoSeal, use traditional absorbable materials for the sealing plug. The absorption time is relatively long and the operation requires the outflow of a large volume of blood.810 Therefore, it is important to develop a hemostatic material that meets the needs of irregular bleeding from wounds, but that also has good biocompatibility and antimicrobial properties. Hydrogels, as a class of extremely hydrophilic gel with a multidimensional network structure, have great advantages as hemostatic materials. Hydrogel hemostatic materials have unique advantages over other types of hemostatic material. Their injectability makes them easy to apply to a variety of irregular wounds and, moreover, their strong adhesion allows for rapid wound closure. They have the unique ability to maintain a wet wound environment, avoiding wound infection, and appropriate water absorption and swelling can be achieved by physical means to achieve rapid wound hemostasis.1215 This randomized controlled trial showed that hydrogel VCDs achieved superior hemostatic speed (1.99 vs 3.14 min, P<0.001) and 90% less procedural blood loss (0.83 mL vs 8.93 mL, P<0.001), with equivalent safety profiles. Accelerated hemostasis confers critical clinical benefits by mitigating risks of access site complications including hematoma and pseudoaneurysm formation through reduced bleeding duration.5 This advantage proves particularly critical in anticoagulated patients or those on antiplatelet regimens, where prolonged bleeding propensity increases the risk of complications. Furthermore, rapid vascular sealing preserves endothelial integrity, potentially reducing iatrogenic vascular trauma and thromboembolic sequelae.9 22 The present study suggests that hydrogels may have application potential for blockage and hemostasis after femoral artery puncture, which deserves further in-depth exploration.

Limitations

This is the first multicenter randomized controlled clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a hydrogel VCD for hemostasis after transfemoral artery puncture.7 10 23 However, this study has some limitations that should be considered. First, owing to the different operating procedures and methods of use, blinding of the surgeons to the treatment allocation was not feasible. However, we attempted to minimize the bias introduced by the open-label design by implementing precise criteria for endpoint assessment, including the use of video recording of the whole process of hemostasis and blockage. Despite these efforts, we cannot fully exclude the possibility of bias inherent to the open-label study design. Second, patients with visual calcification at the access site or the presence of clinically significant peripheral vascular disease were excluded. Third, one patient in the control group was excluded from the PPS because of major protocol deviation. Finally, although catheterization via the radial artery route has been shown to reduce access site complications, a comparison with this strategy was beyond the scope of the current trial.2427

Conclusions

In patients undergoing cerebrovascular intervention via transfemoral access, the hydrogel VCD demonstrated non-inferiority to the ExoSeal VCD in terms of the device success rate, time to hemostasis, and blood loss volume during vessel closure. This study suggests that hydrogel VCDs may have application potential for blockage and hemostasis, which deserves further in-depth exploration.
