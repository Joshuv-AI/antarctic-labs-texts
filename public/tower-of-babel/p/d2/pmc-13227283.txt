Comment on“Left ventricular systolic recovery after TAVI in severe aortic stenosis: A systematic review and meta-analysis”
Authors: Bin Cao, Ming Ouyang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest Hasabo et al.'s meta-analysis on LVEF recovery after TAVI [1], reporting that nearly half of patients with baseline systolic dysfunction achieve significant recovery, with baseline LVEF as the primary predictor. While this consolidates evidence on reverse remodeling, several methodological considerations warrant discussion.

First, the study overlooks cardiac amyloidosis (ATTR-CM),a critical confounder present in 8%–16% of elderly TAVI candidates. ATTR-CM involves infiltrative pathophysiology distinct from afterload mismatch. Dobner et al. showed ATTR-CM patients have higher 2-year mortality post-TAVI (42.1% vs. 18.3%; HR = 2.47) [2], with LVEF non-recovery as the strongest mortality predictor. Since most original studies did not exclude ATTR-CM, the reported 49% recovery rate likely overestimates true TAVI benefit, warranting future stratification.

Second, the inverse association between baseline LVEF and recovery may reflect mathematical coupling—patients with lower baseline values have greater capacity for a ≥10% absolute increase. A recent meta-analysis confirmed LVEF's prognostic value varies across baseline strata [3]. Alternative metrics like relative change or absolute thresholds (e.g., LVEF >50%) may better identify true reverse remodeling. Furthermore, the meta-regression omitted key confounders (e.g., mean aortic gradient) [4], highlighting the need for individual patient-data analysis.

Third, defining recovery solely by absolute LVEF increase conflates distinct phenomena. LVEF is load-dependent; afterload reduction alone can increase LVEF without biological recovery. Reskovic Luksic et al. found that while LVEF remained unchanged early post-TAVI, radial strain improved, whereas left ventricular mass index decreased only at late follow-up [5]. Future studies should distinguish early hemodynamic improvement from late biological reverse remodeling—the latter alone correlating with long-term outcomes.

In conclusion, we commend the authors' work and suggest refining future analyses by stratifying for ATTR-CM, employing alternative recovery metrics, and differentiating hemodynamic from biological recovery to better identify true reverse remodeling.
