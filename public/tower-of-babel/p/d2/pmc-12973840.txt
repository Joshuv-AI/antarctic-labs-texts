Understanding the benefits of art therapy for cancer-related pain: a call for action
Authors: Calliandra Perry, Stella Chang, Megan Winkel, Steven S Chang, Eric Adjei Boakye
Published: 2026

Introduction

Over 1.8 million cancer diagnoses were reported in 2020 in the United States with more than 600,000 deaths from cancer in that same year [1]. In 2025, it is predicted that an estimated two million new diagnoses will be reported with an incidence rate of 445.8 per 100,000 adult men and women per year [2]. The 2025 estimation for cancer-related deaths will be over 618,000, a mortality rate of 145.4 per 100,000 adult men and women [2]. Receiving a cancer diagnosis and treatment often bring a range of complex physical, emotional, and psychological challenges to patients [3,4]. One of the most prevalent and debilitating symptoms experienced by individuals undergoing cancer treatment is pain [4]. The complicated nature of cancer-related pain and its impact on quality of life has led to a growing interest in exploring complementary therapies to support traditional medical interventions [3,4]. Within this track of expanding treatment options, art therapy has gained recognition for its potential to alleviate not only the emotional and psychological distress associated with cancer but also its role in managing pain [5].

Art therapy is a creative and expressive intervention that enables individuals to communicate emotions and experiences which may be difficult to express through words alone [5,6]. This therapeutic approach encourages patients to engage with their inner world and externalize their struggles, fostering self-reflection and emotional processing [5,6]. The relationship between art therapy, pain management, and quality of life in adult cancer patients is an emerging area of research that warrants further attention [4,5]. Studies suggest that engaging in creative activities can activate neurobiological processes associated with pain relief, while simultaneously improving mood, reducing anxiety, and increasing feelings of empowerment [7]. By integrating a holistic approach to pain management such as art therapy, patients are offered a promising avenue for enhancing their lives, providing a means of improving personal healing and self-expression during a challenging journey [8,9]. There is a specific need in examining the potential benefits of art therapy in managing cancer-related pain and the implications for clinical practice [6–8]. Thus, the purpose of this commentary is to describe benefits of art therapy in reducing pain, the Henry Ford Cancer art therapy program, and implications for clinical practice.

Cancer pain

Cancer-related pain can be difficult to discern and treat as it may arise from a patient’s tumor or tumor site, treatments such as chemotherapy or surgeries, or additionally, emotional or psychological suffering. The prevalence of pain in cancer patients is doubled that of the percentage of the general US population experiencing chronic pain [10]. Approximately 45% of cancer patients have reported experiencing cancer-related pain, and one-third of patients report that pain as being moderate to severe [11]. There is a significant association between the severity of a cancer patient’s pain and their perceived quality of life, including an impact on their physical function, emotional function, and their ability to continue living in the role they held prior to diagnosis [11]. This reality for many cancer patients demonstrates that pain is not only a physical sensation, but instead, can be shaped by individual perception, prior experiences, mental health status, and overall psychosocial context [12]. There is little research on fear of being in pain as it relates to cancer patients and their treatment choices, or on how art therapy can have an impact on reducing this fear. However, one study reported that cancer patients face a fear of having limitations in function in response to knowing there may be pain associated with their treatment, which could be mitigated by participation in art therapy groups and conversations with others who may have faced the same concerns, as well processing these fears with art [13].

Conventional pain management typically relies on pharmacologic treatments from pain and palliative medicine teams including the prescription of opioids or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and sometimes with adjunct non-pharmacological interventions from a psychologist or psychiatrist. Some patients share a fear of addiction when presented with opioid treatment and may even refuse the prescription [14]. In this way, medication-based approaches can fall short of fully addressing the emotional components of pain, and they may bring additional challenges such as sedation, constipation, or fear of addiction [13,14]. For instance, in a study among a community sample of patients using opioids for chronic pain, three-quarters (73.9%) of participants expressed some concern regarding opioid use, with about 14% specifically highlighting concerns about side effects [15]. Given these potential side effects, patients, nurses, occupational therapists, recreational therapists and physicians alike are becoming more open to integrative options that address both body and mind with far fewer side effects. Art therapy can be offered alongside safe prescription of pain relievers such as opioids, which the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention suggests includes, at a minimum, regular follow ups with the prescribing provider and lab work to maintain safe dosage [16]. While other non-pharmacologic treatment options for pain include modalities such as psychotherapy with a psych-oncology team or talk-based support groups via social work teams, art therapy offers a non-verbal channel for patients to process and express difficult emotions associated with illness, potentially reducing psychological contributors to pain perception [17,18].

Art therapy: a creative outlet for healing

There is a growing body of research about the use of various art-forms in cancer care. The most reported arts interventions are music therapy and art therapy. Art therapy involves guided artistic creation within a therapeutic setting, often facilitated by fully registered art therapists. It allows individuals to explore and externalize feelings that may be difficult to articulate verbally [15]. For cancer patients, this can include anxiety about prognosis, anger over physical changes, grief, or uncertainty about the future [19]. Beyond mere distraction, art therapy provides an opportunity for emotional processing, self-reflection, and resilience-building. This process may modulate the experience of pain by reducing stress-related physiological responses, fostering a sense of agency, and promoting relaxation [20].

Pain is one of the most widely prevalent symptoms among cancer patients [9,10]. Numerous anecdotal reports and other small-scale studies suggest that creating art can help cancer patients feel more in control, connected to others, and emotionally supported [9]. Organizations such as the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) suggest an integrative approach for cancer patients to assist in managing the anxiety, depression, fatigue, and pain that they may be experiencing, including mindfulness-based modalities such as art therapy [21,22] Current application and recommendation of art therapy within oncology covers management of mood and anxiety rather than direct impact on perceived pain, a conversation that with the proper avenues of data recruitment can expand the conversation and extend the reach of art therapy interventions to pain management. Despite its potential, additional information on the impact of quantifiable art therapy outcomes such as pain remains limited. Art therapy should be targeted not only to patients with pain, but it should be offered universally as a supportive care service.

Henry Ford cancer art therapy programme

Recognizing the benefits of art therapy in pain management, Henry Ford Cancer has created an Arts in Health programme run by a fully registered Art Therapist. The Arts in Health programme offers art therapy opportunities for adult cancer patients who participate after provider referrals or as a personal decision. The open-format, ongoing group sessions are free of charge and available to patients at any stage of their cancer treatment or survivorship journey with no limit as to how many sessions they can attend. Each 90-minute group session, held in a hybrid, synchronous in-person and virtual format, begins and ends with a well-being check-in. Followed by the initial well-being check-in, most of the remaining time is spent actively working on a guided project that incorporates an art-based coping skill and concludes with group reflection of their completed artwork and a final post-art-making well-being check-in. While there is not a standard set of therapeutic goals for these groups, patients have given self-reported testimonials related to feeling more relaxed, less stressed out or anxious, and being more creative, suggesting that each person may approach the group session differently but most experience similar improvements in mood.

Future research are needed to access the benefits of art therapy and pain and should focus on a broader assessment of both subjective and objective pain outcomes, ideally with larger and more diverse samples, randomized controls, and longitudinal follow-up. They should also look at other outcomes such as psychological distress, fatigue and other cancer diagnosis and treatment symptoms. Moreover, exploring biological markers of stress (such as cortisol levels or heart rate variability) in conjunction with self-report measures could provide more robust evidence of art therapy’s physiological impact [23,24]. It should be noted that biological markers of stress should complement, not replace, patient reported pain outcomes and that feasibility and burden on patients will need careful consideration in oncology settings. Finally, different art therapy formats (individual vs. group, visual arts vs. music or movement) may have varied effects on patient outcomes. Tailoring the modality to individual preferences and needs could further enhance therapeutic benefits.

Implications for clinical practice

By offering patients an expressive outlet, art therapy may help them better cope with the complexity of pain, especially when conventional therapies alone are not enough. The group-based structure of the sessions creates a sense of community and shared experience from a patient’s first attendance which can be therapeutic in and of itself. Cancer patients often feel isolated and disconnected, so the opportunity to create alongside others can restore a sense of belonging and reduce feelings of alienation [17]. Clinicians and cancer centers should consider integrating art therapy more fully into supportive oncology services. Doing so aligns with the growing emphasis on patient-centered, holistic care that goes beyond tumor control to address overall quality of life. Using fully registered Art Therapists to conduct group sessions for patients and providing further education related clinicians’ understanding of art therapy and its benefits for their patients could lead to be better integration of art therapy referrals into routine oncology workflows.

Conclusion

This commentary offers timely and valuable insight into the use of art therapy as a complementary tool for managing pain and enhancing emotional well-being in cancer patients. Art therapy is not a substitute for conventional treatment, but if incorporated as part of a more patient-centered strategy, it can provide patients an opportunity to restore a sense of independence from their cancer diagnosis, improve emotional resilience, and increase quality of life while facing the challenges that cancer brings. As more is learned about the benefits and positive impact of art therapy, oncology clinicians should take note to ensure that healing includes not just the body, but the mind as well. Art therapy should be integrated as a complement to, not a replacement for, evidence based pharmacologic and psychological pain management.
