“People are going to do drugs:” harm reduction as a strategy for survivors of sex trafficking who use substances
Authors: Lindsay B Gezinski, Ariel M S Richer, Aundrea Dilanchian, Theresa Anasti, Nathaniel A Dell, Cassandra Price
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurvivors of sex trafficking often experience co-occurring substance use and face significant barriers to accessing care. Harm reduction offers a nonjudgmental, survivor-centered approach to mitigating health risks associated with substance use and commercial sex. However, limited research has explored how harm reduction is understood and implemented by service providers working with this population. This study aimed to examine service providers’ perspectives on the role, impact, and challenges of harm reduction in supporting survivors of sex trafficking who use substances.MethodsThis qualitative study employed purposive and snowball sampling to recruit 21 service providers from human trafficking, domestic violence, and other agencies across the southwestern US who serve survivors of sex trafficking. Semi-structured interviews were conducted to explore participants’ experiences with harm reduction strategies, perceived barriers, and relational dynamics in service provision. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-step process. A constant comparative method was used to refine codes and identify emergent themes.ResultsFive major themes were identified: (1) comprehensive harm reduction as a lifesaving strategy, (2) education and health literacy as critical intervention tools, (3) navigating barriers to harm reduction, (4) relational foundations of harm reduction, and (5) ambivalence toward harm reduction as a standalone strategy. Participants described harm reduction as essential to preventing overdose, reducing disease transmission, and fostering engagement through nonjudgmental care. They emphasized the importance of peer-led outreach, trauma-informed education, and holistic support that includes meeting basic needs. However, providers also reported legal and institutional constraints, funding challenges, and public misconceptions that hindered implementation. A minority of participants expressed ambivalence about harm reduction, reflecting ongoing tensions between abstinence-based and non-abstinence approaches.ConclusionsHarm reduction is viewed by providers as a vital, relational, and flexible approach to supporting survivors of sex trafficking who use substances. Findings highlight the need for policy reform, increased investment in peer-led and trauma-informed models, and broader cultural shifts to reduce stigma. Future research should include survivor perspectives and explore harm reduction strategies across diverse geographic and demographic contexts.Keywords: Human trafficking, Substance use, Survivor-centered care, Trauma-informed practice, Service providers, Peer outreach, Stigma and structural barriers, Health literacy

Introduction

Human trafficking is defined as the recruitment, transportation, or retention of individuals for labor, sexual exploitation, or other forms of involuntary servitude through force, fraud, or coercion [1]. Sex trafficking is a form of human trafficking in which individuals are compelled to engage in commercial sex acts against their will. While the exact prevalence of human trafficking is unknown, it is estimated that 27.6 million people have been forced into labor and 6.3 million into the commercial sex industry worldwide [2, 3]. Survivors of sex trafficking experience severe physical and psychological trauma, which can have long-lasting effects [4]. Common issues include substance use disorder (SUD), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, depression, violence-related injuries, untreated sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and pregnancy complications [5–8]. Survivors often face significant barriers to healthcare, financial strain, victim-blaming, stigmatization, and fear of legal consequences [4, 9].

Traffickers frequently target vulnerable populations, including individuals experiencing homelessness and those with SUD [10–12]. Although the data on the prevalence of SUD among sex trafficking survivors remains limited, recent findings indicate that approximately 43% of US emergency department visits involving sex trafficking involved individuals with SUD [10]. This is significantly higher than rates observed for survivors of labor trafficking and non-trafficked individuals. Exposure to traumatic events is a vulnerability associated with increased risk of both PTSD and SUD, with these conditions sharing overlapping symptoms, such as alterations to arousal and reactivity, cognition, and mood, as well as sharing similar biological and behavioral mechanisms [13]. The high rates of co-occurring substance use and trafficking suggest the importance of addressing both sex trafficking and SUD, prioritizing a harm reduction approach that is survivor-centered and non-judgmental. The purpose of this study was to explore how providers understand, implement, and navigate harm reduction practices when serving survivors of human trafficking.

Harm reduction and sex trafficking

Harm reduction, which focuses on minimizing the negative consequences of behaviors, has shown efficacy in addressing a variety of issues, including injection drug use, tobacco smoking, domestic violence, and eating disorders [14–16], which is promising for marginalized and vulnerable communities such as survivors of sex trafficking who use drugs. Harm reduction services encompass a range of practices, including providing information on safer drug use, syringe service programs (SSPs), drug checking, drug consumption rooms, overdose prevention and reversal, and legal/paralegal services [14]. Studies have shown harm reduction practices such as SSPs and fentanyl test strip distribution programs to be effective at limiting the risks of substance use, including the spread of infectious diseases and accidental ingestion of and poisoning by fentanyl [17–19]. Hagan and authors [18] reported that individuals who utilized SSPs before entering substance use treatment had greater retention and higher chances of reducing or abstaining from substance use after treatment. Indeed, a scoping review by Jakubowski and authors [20] noted that participation in SSPs showed an association with entering substance use treatment. The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) [21] has previously recognized the importance of harm reduction programs in creating meaningful connections with participants, including the use of peer support specialists or individuals with lived experience.

To illustrate the positive impact of harm reduction programs, evidence suggests SSPs limit the spread of infectious diseases without increasing a person’s likelihood of substance use [17, 22]. Naloxone distribution is important in limiting deaths due to overdose in survivors of trafficking who may be using opioids. Lastly, distribution of safe sex kits, which may include condoms, dental dams, lubricant, information on HIV/STIs, and other supplies, can be especially beneficial to prevent STIs and unwanted pregnancies among people engaged in commercial sex [23–26]. Preble and authors [22] encourage those utilizing the harm reduction model to maintain a nonjudgmental approach, recognize the resilience of survivors, build on clients’ strengths, and establish safe, trauma-informed support systems. Harm reduction seeks to treat each person individually and in the manner that best suits them and not penalize those who do not abstain completely from behaviors that may have negative health consequences, such as drug use or commercial sex [27, 28].

Barriers to service access

Survivors of sex trafficking face substantial barriers to accessing health, social, and SUD services, many of which are rooted in stigma, criminalization, and systemic mistrust of service institutions [29, 30]. Research consistently demonstrates that anti-trafficking services are largely grounded in abstinence-oriented, compliance-based, or rescue frameworks that inadequately meet the needs of survivors with ongoing substance use, often resulting in exclusion from services, premature discharge, or coercive treatment practices [31, 32]. Survivors of sex trafficking experience distinct and intersecting barriers to care, including criminalization related to substance use and commercial sex, distrust of healthcare and social service systems, and fear of legal consequences, which collectively undermine engagement with conventional substance use and health services [33]. These barriers overlap significantly with those experienced by sex workers more broadly, including moral regulation within healthcare and social services, heightened surveillance, and stigma [23, 24, 34].

Importantly, scholarship has documented how the persistent conflation of sex work and sex trafficking amplifies these barriers, often legitimizing rescue-oriented, abstinence-based, or compliance-driven interventions that fail to account for autonomy, self-determination, and economic survival [35, 36]). Within SUD and anti-trafficking service systems, stigma related to drug use and commercial sex operates not only interpersonally but structurally, shaping eligibility criteria, funding priorities, and institutional responses to survivor engagement [37]. Understanding barriers to care requires situating trafficking survivors’ experiences within broader sex work and harm reduction research while attending to this distinct form of exploitation. This study examines how service providers navigate these overlapping barriers in practice and how harm reduction is interpreted and constrained within social service contexts.

Purpose of study

While harm reduction is a well-established, evidence-based public health approach among people who use substances, most harm reduction research has focused on general populations and has rarely examined its application within trafficking-specific service contexts [15, 16, 38]. Little research exists on understanding and implementing harm reduction strategies for survivors of sex trafficking who use substances; however, harm reduction techniques such as SSPs, naloxone provision, safe sex kit distribution, and decriminalization of sex work may be beneficial to survivors of sex trafficking [26, 34]. Existing trafficking research suggests that survivors’ experiences of coercion, trauma bonding, and structural violence introduce implementation challenges that may complicate the direct transfer of standard harm reduction models into anti-trafficking settings [31, 39]. Despite increasing calls for survivor-centered and low-barrier approaches [35], there remains a dearth of empirical research examining how harm reduction principles are interpreted, adapted, or constrained within organizations serving trafficking survivors who use substances.

Provider perspectives represent a critical and understudied lens for understanding how harm reduction is operationalized within the ethical, legal, and organizational constraints of trafficking response systems. Existing studies of service providers working in substance use treatment, harm reduction, and gender‑based violence service systems indicate that frontline workers often navigate ethical tensions between institutional mandates, funding structures, and survivor‑centered practice [40–42]). Studies that include service providers in anti‑trafficking settings have largely focused on identification, risk indicators, or service coordination, with comparatively less attention to how harm reduction principles are interpreted, adapted, or constrained when serving survivors with ongoing substance use [11]. There is limited empirical understanding of how providers negotiate the overlapping demands of trafficking response, SUD services, and stigma‑laden policy environments in practice. Examining providers’ perspectives, therefore, addresses a key conceptual and empirical gap by illuminating real-world implementation challenges at the intersection of trafficking response, substance use, and harm reduction, an area essential to advancing equitable, trauma-responsive models of care. By centering providers’ experiences, this analysis seeks to illuminate the opportunities and constraints of harm reduction in anti-trafficking work and to situate harm reduction as both a practical service delivery approach and a relational, rights-informed orientation.

Methods

Sample and recruitment

Guided by a constructivist/interpretivist paradigm, this study employed phenomenology to center participants’ subjective meanings and lived experiences of providing harm reduction-oriented care to survivors of sex trafficking [43]. Using purposive and snowball sampling techniques, participants were recruited from agencies in the southwestern US that provide services to survivors of sex trafficking. Given that survivors often engage with multiple systems of care [40, 44], recruitment efforts targeted a diverse range of agency types, including those specializing in human trafficking, domestic violence, and SUD services. Inclusion criteria were: (1) being a service provider working in sex trafficking, sex work, HIV, sexual health, SUD, domestic violence, sexual violence, or homelessness; (2) having encountered survivors of sex trafficking in a professional role that involved substance use-related issues; (3) residing in the southwestern US (Arizona, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, or Utah); and (4) being at least 18 years of age. Individuals were excluded if they did not have direct professional experience with survivors of sex trafficking in the context of substance use, resided outside the southwestern US, or were under 18 years of age.

Over 50 agencies were contacted via email and invited to share the study information with their staff and professional networks. Recruitment continued until data saturation was reached, as indicated by thematic redundancy across interviews [45]. Semi-structured interviews averaged approximately one hour in duration and were conducted in private settings via Zoom from May to August 2024. Qualitative studies typically involve relatively small samples, often ranging from five to 25 participants, as analytic depth and richness are prioritized over statistical representation [43].

A total of 21 service providers participated in the study. The majority identified as white (81%) and cisgender women (76.2%). Nearly half (48%) held a master’s degree, and approximately 50% reported over a decade of experience working with survivors. Nineteen percent reported that their organization had served 100 or more survivors in the past year. This study was approved by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board (IRB #00177576) (Table 1).

Data collection

Three research team members conducted interviews, including the PI and two graduate-level research assistants. Immediately before each interview, the informed consent procedures were reviewed, emphasizing the voluntary nature of participation and the participant's right to withdraw at any time. During the interviews, we asked participants to discuss various aspects of harm reduction interventions. We invited them to share their experiences working with clients as well as their perceptions of harm reduction within their respective communities. Semi-structured interviews were guided by questions focused on participants’ experiences with harm reduction service provision for survivors of sex trafficking. The interview guide was informed by relevant literature, study objectives, and consultation with subject‑matter experts. Example interview guide questions included: (1) Can you suggest any strategies or interventions that could address the barriers faced by survivors in accessing and engaging with services? (2) What are your thoughts on the concepts of harm reduction for survivors of sex trafficking? (3) Do you foresee any challenges in implementing harm reduction with survivors of sex trafficking? Interviews were conducted iteratively, allowing emerging insights to inform minor refinements to probing questions while retaining a consistent core interview structure.

Immediately following the interview, participants were asked open-ended demographic questions related to age, gender, racial/ethnic identity, level of education, agency type, professional role, years of experience, time working specifically with survivors of sex trafficking, and the estimated number of survivors served in the past year. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and the audio-recordings were destroyed after the transcripts were verified for accuracy. To enhance accuracy and trustworthiness, transcripts were shared with participants via email for member checking, allowing for revisions or additions. Participants received a $50 electronic gift card upon completion of the interview.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using a constant comparative method [46], within a reflexive thematic analysis framework [47, 48], beginning with the first interview and continuing iteratively through the end of data collection. Two members of the research team independently coded the transcripts following Braun and Clarke’s [47] six-step process for thematic analysis. The analysis began with immersive, repeated readings of the transcripts to ensure familiarity with the data, followed by initial, inductive code development in Dedoose based on emergent patterns. Reflexive discussions were used to interrogate analytic assumptions, refine interpretations, and enhance conceptual depth. Codes were compared, refined, and organized into broader themes. The team collaboratively assessed the coherence and conceptual alignment of codes within themes and refined them through iterative discussions to ensure alignment with the study’s research questions. The research team maintained a detailed audit trail documenting analytic decisions, code development, theme refinement, and methodological adjustments across the study. Regular peer debriefing meetings were held to critically examine emerging themes, challenge interpretations, and explore alternative explanations.

Researcher reflexivity

The authors bring diverse positionalities related to race, gender, and sexual identity and are united by a harm reduction orientation that centers survivor autonomy [27, 49, 50]. Our research team includes individuals with varying levels of experience, ranging from emerging scholars to researchers with over 15 years’ experience conducting survivor-centered research in domestic and international contexts. These social locations and professional backgrounds shaped the research design, data collection, and analysis processes. Specifically, the harm reduction framework informed the development of the interview guide, allowing participants to define safety and risk on their own terms. During data analysis, researchers attended to how their positionalities and commitments to survivor autonomy influenced coding decisions and thematic development. Analytic discussions explicitly prioritized participants’ meaning‑making and situated individual experiences within broader structural and contextual conditions.

Results

Service providers had diverse interpretations of harm reduction interventions. While some emphasized the importance of SSPs, others highlighted a more comprehensive harm reduction strategy aimed at enhancing safety and protection in terms of sexual health education. Findings reveal five major themes: (1) comprehensive harm reduction as a lifesaving strategy, (2) education and health literacy as critical intervention tools, (3) navigating barriers to harm reduction, (4) relational foundations of harm reduction, and (5) ambivalence toward harm reduction as a standalone strategy.

Comprehensive harm reduction as a lifesaving strategy

Participants overwhelmingly described harm reduction as a vital and often life-saving approach for survivors of sex trafficking who use substances. They emphasized its role in preventing overdoses and fatalities, reducing the transmission of infectious diseases, and fostering trust through non-judgmental care. Free and accessible tools, such as clean syringes, naloxone, fentanyl test strips, condoms, Plan B, and free STI and Hepatitis C testing, were seen as essential, particularly for individuals engaged in street-based commercial sex. These resources were not only practical but also symbolic of a compassionate, person-centered approach. Harm reduction was described as a way to meet survivors where they are, addressing their specific needs and desires without requiring abstinence or behavioral change as a condition for support.

Participants framed harm reduction as healthcare, as Participant 2 (human trafficking services) noted, “People are going to do drugs, no matter what we do, we can at least provide a safe way for people to do their substances so they're not able to harm themselves more. It's all about healthcare.” Service providers also emphasized the importance of meeting the needs of the whole person. For example, Participant 11 (human trafficking services) shared, “I've got Plan B, sharps containers, women's hygiene like feminine hygiene, I've got socks in here.” Participant 3 (human trafficking services) recalled, “We had someone come in for a test and leave with condoms, a sandwich, a clean [drug] kit, and a referral for housing. That’s harm reduction, too.” Participants also discussed how street outreach was a first point of contact with clients, opening doors to further services. These examples illustrate how harm reduction extends beyond immediate health concerns to address basic needs and promote dignity. By creating low-barrier access points for care, organizations foster trust and long-term engagement with vulnerable populations.

As a corollary to healthcare, participants also emphasized the relational and humanizing aspects of harm reduction. Participant 5 (medical/healthcare setting) stated: “I don’t look at harm reduction as enabling. I look at it as loving people where they are. You don’t have to wait until someone’s ready to quit. You just meet them where they’re at.” Overall, participants framed harm reduction as a strategy rooted in respect for autonomy and dignity. Rather than requiring individuals to stop using substances or exit commercial sex to receive help, harm reduction was described as empowering survivors to make informed choices and access care on their own terms.

Importantly, participants’ framing of harm reduction as “lifesaving” reflects trafficking‑specific contexts in which traditional SUD treatment options may be inaccessible or incompatible with survivors’ lived realities. Providers described working with individuals whose substance use was shaped by trafficker control, survival needs, and criminalization, circumstances that frequently precluded abstinence‑based or highly regulated treatment programs. In this context, harm reduction served not only to prevent overdose or infection but also to create rare points of voluntary, survivor‑directed engagement with care.

Education and health literacy as critical intervention tools

Participants emphasized that harm reduction interventions include prevention through education on topics ranging from STIs to insurance coverage. Education was not viewed as one-directional. Instead, it was collaborative, grounded in real-life concerns, and aimed at building long-term empowerment. Participants emphasized the importance of educating adults about the transmission of diseases related to sexual activity and substance use through their harm reduction programs. Participant 2 (human trafficking services) noted that teaching individuals safer substance use practices could empower them to share this knowledge with others, promoting a culture of safety and awareness:

…we do offer naloxone at our drop-in center every week, and we teach people how to use it every week. We have to be educated on how to do it ourselves, and then we teach individuals, because we want people to be safe on the streets. And if they're not using it for themselves, they can use it for someone else if they know how to use it properly. Even if they're not using the needles themselves, maybe they can bring their friend's needles, because they don't feel safe coming here, and they can exchange them, no questions asked. It's all about spreading knowledge, because knowledge is power. The more people that can understand and have the education of the importance of being safe, the better.

By noting that “knowledge is power,” this participant emphasizes the importance of education in overdose prevention and how this can result in client empowerment through the provision of skills and knowledge rather than attempts to mandate behavior change institutionally.

Participant 7 (SUD services) discussed the various ways they educate the community on safer substance use:

So, we've got a lot of different little flyers that we give out about safer injection use or ways to inject drugs more safely. We have flyers about different drugs like fentanyl and xylazine, and then we've got Hep C flyers and stuff.

Testing for infectious diseases such as Hepatitis C often served as an entry point for deeper conversations about transmission, prevention, and treatment. These interactions created opportunities to build trust and offer tailored education in a non-judgmental way. Participant 14 (criminal/legal setting) emphasized the importance of dialogue over instruction, “When I talk about STIs or safe injection, I don’t preach. I ask them what they know already, and we go from there. It’s about dialogue.” Participant 15 (medical/healthcare setting) added that education also plays a critical role in correcting misinformation, “We have youth come in who don’t know how pregnancy works, who think you can’t get HIV from oral sex. That’s not judgment, it’s just where they’re at. We step in there.” Together, these insights reflect how harm reduction education is not just about information delivery but also empowerment, trust-building, and meeting people where they are.

Providers’ emphasis on education must also be understood within trafficking‑specific conditions of misinformation, restricted autonomy, and disrupted access to healthcare. Participants described working with survivors whose knowledge about substance use risks, sexual health, and bodily safety had been shaped by coercion, isolation, and limited institutional contact. Education operated as a form of engagement with SUD services that prioritized informed choice rather than readiness for treatment.

Relational foundations of harm reduction

Across interviews, participants spoke about harm reduction as a deeply relational practice, emphasizing that their work extended beyond the distribution of supplies or services. Central to their approach was the cultivation of trust, consistency, and human connection. Participants spoke of the importance of remembering clients’ names, listening without judgment, and showing up consistently, acts that conveyed care and respect. According to participants, these relational gestures often had a greater impact than any single intervention. Participant 11 (human trafficking services) reflected on their street outreach,

A lot of our participants will be with somebody else, and I’ve been doing this so long now that they know to look for me or having a red sharps container outside of my trunk… The harm reduction piece always helps in building that community. There’s people that come up to me and they’re like, “Hey, are you [name]?” I’m like, “Yeah.” That’s how I know I’m doing my job. They remember me.

Thus, recognition and familiarity became markers of effective engagement. Participants emphasized that clients responded most to “being seen” as whole people. This relational dynamic was evident in everyday interactions, such as those described by Participant 2 (human trafficking services), “It’s not just a transaction. It’s like, ‘Hey, how’s your mom?’ ‘Did you get your SSI [Supplemental Security Income] paperwork done?’ We know each other.” Trust-building was especially critical given the histories of systemic harm many clients had experienced. Participant 8 (SUD services) described the importance of persistence, “A lot of folks have been burned by the system. They don’t trust easily. So, we show up. Again and again. And we don’t give up” as a way to rebuild trust and reduce stigma.

Peer-based outreach emerged as a powerful tool in this relational framework. Participants highlighted how shared lived experience and peer distribution of supplies helped dismantle traditional service hierarchies and fostered mutual respect. Clients were not only recipients of care but also active contributors in roles like teachers, allies, and advocates within their communities. Participant 10 (human trafficking services) noted, “Peer distribution, that reduction, that harm reduction by peers and distributing the supplies, distributing the harm reduction, that peer distribution is really decreasing the stigma, the connection.” Finally, participants indicated that harm reduction often served as a gateway to broader goals. Rather than assuming abstinence as the primary objective, workers prioritized clients’ self-identified needs as foundational steps toward recovery. Participant 21 (domestic violence services) noted the importance of asking clients,

“What's your number one priority?” Most of the time, it wasn't, “I need to quit heroin.” Most of the time, it was like, “I need to get off the street.” Once they're off the street, that makes it a lot easier to quit heroin. So, just trying to figure out what people need.

This client-centered approach allowed for more meaningful and sustainable engagement, reinforcing the idea that harm reduction is not just a method, but a philosophy rooted in dignity, autonomy, and connection The centrality of trust and consistency described by participants reflects the relational ruptures common in trafficking experiences, including betrayal, surveillance, and punishment. Within this context, harm reduction relationships functioned as counter‑experiences to coercion, with participants emphasizing predictability and non‑punitive responses. These findings suggest that relationality does not merely support SUD engagement but actively repairs conditions that otherwise render engagement unsafe or impossible for trafficked individuals.

Navigating barriers to harm reduction

Despite their deep commitment to harm reduction, participants described operating within a web of barriers that constrained their work. These barriers clustered across legal, organizational, and cultural domains, shaping how harm reduction services were delivered and how survivors engaged with care. Participants described restrictive laws and policy environments that limited their ability to provide harm reduction services, often requiring informal workarounds. Participant 16 (medical/healthcare setting) illustrated how policy constraints force providers to adapt their practices, “We’re not technically allowed to give out needles, but we can have them on premise… But if people take them, they’re allowed to take them. That’s how we do it. It’s a way to get around it.” Even when participants followed the law, they described how clients were still criminalized for their drug use. Participant 7 (SUD services) detailed how carrying harm reduction supplies could lead to legal trouble, “Here’s this person doing something to try and limit the harms associated with doing drugs and not have syringes in the street and stuff getting punished [by law enforcement] for trying to do good.”

Participants also described institutional resistance and funding constraints that shaped the scope of their work. For some, even the language of “harm reduction” was controversial, as Participant 7 (SUD services) said, “I’ve had funders say they can’t support anything with ‘harm reduction’ in the name. We have to rename things just to get the money to save lives.” Some leaders and institutions continued to endorse abstinence-only models. As Participant 6 (SUD services) stated, “Some people in leadership still think abstinence is the only way. They don’t understand that for a lot of folks, harm reduction is the first and only option that feels possible.” This workaround mentality was common with providers who had to bend rules or reframe their services to continue reaching people in need.

Beyond legal and institutional constraints, participants described broader societal stigma surrounding drug use, commercial sex, and harm reduction itself. The stigma extended beyond funding; participants described being misunderstood or even targeted by their communities. Participant 12 recounted, “Our building got vandalized because people think we’re encouraging drugs. That’s what they see. They don’t see the people we’re saving.” This disconnect between public perception and the reality of harm reduction work was a recurring theme across participant accounts.

Notably, the barriers described by participants illustrate how harm reduction for trafficking survivors is constrained by overlapping legal, organizational, and cultural systems that prioritize surveillance and criminalization over autonomy and safety. Providers emphasized that survivors’ possession of harm reduction supplies could expose them to law enforcement scrutiny, and low‑visibility harm reduction strategies were often prioritized. Rather than reflecting individual resistance to treatment, limited engagement with SUD services emerged as a rational response to structurally produced risk. Despite these challenges, participants remained deeply committed to their work, drawing strength from their own lived experiences or the trust they had built with clients to continue to show up, advocate, and adapt in the face of systems that often worked against them.

Ambivalence toward harm reduction as a standalone strategy

While most participants viewed harm reduction strategies positively, a few participants expressed more ambivalent or critical perspectives, highlighting the complexity of implementing these approaches in practice. Participant 6 (SUD services) acknowledged the life-saving potential of SSPs but also voiced concern that they might inadvertently “promote access to the use [of drugs] versus getting help or getting treatment.” This view extended to medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD; formerly referred to as medication-assisted treatment), such as Suboxone and methadone, which the same participant described as “highly abusable” and sometimes used to “avoid treatment.” As Participant 8 (SUD services) stated, “We don't want your medication-assisted treatment, your harm reduction to replace your addiction to be something that you're seeking out or you're seeking more of instead of using coping skills.” Participant 13 (domestic violence services) echoed this tension, describing harm reduction tools like syringe exchange and naloxone training as “fantastic things,” but ultimately likened them to “a band-aid over a bullet wound,” stressing that “actual prevention and education is the key aspect.” These perspectives highlight the importance of balancing safety and long-term recovery with client self-determination in harm reduction practices.

Participants’ ambivalence toward harm reduction as a standalone strategy reflects broader tensions shaped by trafficking‑specific service contexts rather than simple opposition to harm reduction principles. In trafficking contexts, where substance use may be intertwined with coercion, coping, or survival strategies, some providers expressed concern that harm reduction alone could not adequately address underlying harms. Providers’ concerns point to the necessity of integrating harm reduction within broader, trafficking‑informed continuums of care rather than positioning it as an endpoint.

Discussion

This study contributes to a growing body of literature that positions harm reduction not only as a public health strategy but also as a relational and rights-based approach to care for survivors of sex trafficking. Consistent with Hawk et al. [15] participants in this study framed harm reduction as a compassionate, nonjudgmental philosophy that centers individual autonomy and dignity. Service providers emphasized that harm reduction is not merely about distributing supplies, such as naloxone or clean syringes, it is about building trust, fostering safety, and meeting survivors where they are. This aligns with Preble et al. [22], who advocate for harm reduction models that recognize survivor resilience and prioritize trauma-informed, client-centered care. This emphasis on trust-building, survivor leadership, and integrative care beyond provision of supplies is also documented in collaborative victim services models [45]. By centering service provider perspectives, this study theorizes how harm reduction operates amid criminalization, institutional surveillance, and structural vulnerability. Rather than functioning as a freely implemented public health model, harm reduction emerged as an adaptive response to systems that constrain survivor autonomy and service accessibility.

The findings also reinforce the importance of peer-led and community-based interventions, echoing Jenkins et al. [46] and Hraber et al. [27], who highlight the effectiveness of harm reduction strategies that are embedded in local contexts and led by individuals with lived experience. Peer outreach was described by participants as a powerful tool for reducing stigma and enhancing service accessibility, particularly in communities where institutional trust is low. However, the study also reveals persistent structural and institutional barriers that complicate the implementation of harm reduction. Participants described navigating restrictive laws, institutional resistance, and public stigma, challenges that mirror those identified by Clarke et al. [17] and Henwood et al. [38]. These barriers often forced providers to adopt workaround strategies or rebrand their services to secure funding, underscoring the need for policy reform that explicitly supports harm reduction as a legitimate and essential component of public health and social services.

Service providers’ descriptions can be understood as evidence of what may be conceptualized as “workaround labor:” the routine, often invisible work required to bend, reframe, or creatively interpret policy and institutional rules to deliver ethically responsive care [48]. This labor shifts responsibility for managing risk from systems to frontline providers and underscores policy misalignment with evidence-informed practice. Findings suggest that survivor disengagement may reflect a rational response to structurally produced risk, particularly criminalization, surveillance, and coercive service environments. For trafficking survivors, harm reduction relationships functioned as counter-institutional spaces grounded in consent, predictability, and non-punitive care.

Education emerged as a critical intervention tool, not only for clients but also for communities and institutions. Participants described harm reduction education as dialogic and empowering, consistent with the findings of Hawk et al. [15] and Park et al. [19], who emphasize the role of health literacy in reducing risk and promoting informed decision-making. This study extends that literature by illustrating how education can also serve as a relational practice that builds trust and fosters long-term engagement. This trust, consistency, and non‑punitive presence were foundational to service providers’ work, in that survivors’ prior experiences with authority figures were often characterized by surveillance, punishment, or forced compliance. Rather than positioning themselves as experts imparting instruction, providers described educational encounters as collaborative exchanges that acknowledged survivors’ existing knowledge, lived experience, and immediate safety concerns. In this way, education became a mechanism for trust-building and sustained engagement rather than behavior correction. ​​Education also functioned relationally through co-learning and peer-mediated dissemination of knowledge. Participants described teaching harm reduction strategies in ways that allowed survivors to share information within their own social networks. Within trafficking-specific contexts marked by misinformation, disrupted healthcare access, and constrained autonomy, these relational educational practices functioned as low-risk entry points into care, fostering trust and longer-term engagement.

Against this backdrop, relational harm reduction practices functioned as forms of counter‑coercion, intentionally disrupting dynamics of control that mirrored trafficking itself. This insight extends trauma‑informed care frameworks [52] by emphasizing how relational practices are not merely supportive but structurally reparative. Findings call attention to the importance of relationality and peer-based models, particularly for populations whose contact with institutions has historically resulted in harm. These findings have direct policy relevance. Expanding harm reduction services without addressing criminalized policy environments will continue to rely on workaround labor while sustaining survivor risk. Decriminalization of harm reduction tools, low-barrier funding structures, and decoupling service access from surveillance are therefore foundational to effective implementation.

Finally, the study highlights tensions within the field regarding the goals of harm reduction. While most participants embraced harm reduction as a life-saving and empowering approach, a few expressed concerns about its potential to delay recovery or inadvertently normalize substance use. These ambivalent perspectives reflect ongoing debates in the literature [50] about how to balance immediate safety with long-term recovery goals. The findings suggest that harm reduction should not be viewed as antithetical to recovery but rather as a flexible, client-driven pathway that can support diverse trajectories of healing and change. Participants’ ambivalence toward harm reduction as a standalone strategy offers a critical analytic contribution. Providers expressed concern that harm reduction was often deployed in the absence of adequate housing, mental health care, or legal protection. In such fragmented systems, harm reduction was sometimes experienced as compensatory. That is, addressing immediate risks without altering the structural conditions that sustain both substance use and exploitation.

In sum, this study amplifies the need for harm reduction strategies that are holistic, relational, and responsive to the complex realities of survivors of sex trafficking. Practically, findings suggest that SUD systems that fail to integrate harm reduction may systematically exclude survivors whose circumstances make traditional engagement unsafe or infeasible. It calls for policy environments that support, rather than constrain, harm reduction efforts and for continued investment in peer-led, trauma-informed, and community-based models of care. Findings extend harm reduction theory by situating it within a framework of structural vulnerability, where health behaviors and service engagement are shaped less by individual choice than by legal, economic, and social constraints. Participants’ accounts suggest that harm reduction is uniquely positioned to operate within these constraints because it does not require disclosure, compliance, or readiness to change as preconditions for care. As such, harm reduction may be best understood as a counter‑institutional practice that interrupts cycles of exclusion produced by criminalization and moral regulation.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this research study, several limitations must be noted. Namely, the sampling strategy, small sample size, and southwestern geographical focus limit the generalizability of the study findings. Additionally, this study relied exclusively on service provider perspectives. While service providers offer critical insights into harm reduction, the absence of survivor-centered perspectives limits our understanding of how these approaches are experienced by survivors themselves. Moreover, study participants tended to work in agencies focused on lower-income, often unhoused populations, limiting our understanding of other types of survivors. Findings may also be influenced by social desirability bias, particularly given participants’ professional roles and commitments to harm reduction principles. Finally, our study examined substance use and harm reduction for survivors of sex trafficking specifically, without attention paid to survivors of labor trafficking. Even though substance use is less commonly documented among survivors of labor trafficking, SUD rates among labor trafficking survivors remain high [10], and future research is needed to examine how harm reduction strategies and outreach can be effectively tailored to address substance use among labor trafficking survivors. In future qualitative studies, including survivor-centered perspectives and expanding the geographic focus would deepen understanding of harm reduction attitudes, implementation, and impact across diverse settings and populations.

Conclusions

This study offers critical insights into the role of harm reduction in supporting survivors of sex trafficking who use substances, highlighting both the promise and the complexity of implementing these strategies in practice. Drawing on the perspectives of service providers, the findings support harm reduction as a lifesaving, dignity-affirming approach that prioritizes survivor autonomy, relational engagement, and holistic care. Ultimately, this research affirms that harm reduction is a vital approach that honors the lived realities of survivors and supports their right to access care on their own terms. Future research should build on these findings by exploring survivor perspectives, expanding to diverse geographic and demographic contexts, and examining the long-term outcomes of harm reduction engagement for survivors of sex trafficking.
