Assessment of Pain, Diet and Analgesic Use in Orthodontic Patients
Authors: Krithika P. Arunagiri, Abraham Thomas, Subhash C. Nayak, Sunil Kumar, Sonvita Debnath, Sonali Bhatt, Vikram Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Orthodontic treatment often causes varying levels of pain, particularly following appliance placement and adjustments.Objectives:To assess the intensity and progression of orthodontic pain, examine dietary modifications in response to discomfort, and evaluate the patterns and sources of analgesic use among orthodontic patients.Methods:A cross-sectional observational study was conducted with 92 patients undergoing fixed orthodontic treatment. Pain levels were measured using a visual analog scale (VAS) at 24 hours, three days, and seven days post-adjustment. A structured questionnaire assessed dietary changes and analgesic usage.Results:Pain was highest at 24 hours (mean VAS: 6.8 ± 1.4), decreased at three days (4.3 ± 1.2), and was minimal by day 7 (2.1 ± 0.9). Dietary modifications were reported by 88% of participants, with common food avoidances, including hard fruits, meats, and sticky snacks. The mean duration of dietary change was 4.7 ± 1.9 days. Analgesic use was reported by 73.9% of patients, mainly involving paracetamol and ibuprofen.Conclusion:It is concluded that pain following orthodontic treatment significantly influences patient behavior, particularly dietary choices and medication use. Proactive pain management and patient education are essential to ensure comfort, compliance, and nutritional well-being throughout treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Orthodontic treatment is an integral part of modern dentistry, aimed at correcting malocclusions, aligning teeth, and enhancing oral function and aesthetics. The advantages of orthodontic treatment last long, but patients must deal with substantial physical and psychological challenges throughout their treatment.[1] The most frequently experienced and uncomfortable side effect results from the application of orthodontic forces which impact the teeth along with periodontal tissues. The acute pain after appliance installation or readjustment affects each person differently because of their sensitivity level and treatment type and psychological health aspects.[2] Patients experience pain as both an objective medical symptom and a highly individual matter which depends on various variables, such as their age group and gender status and their anxiety levels and cultural attitudes.[3] The large proportion of orthodontic patients within the adolescent and young adult age group faces substantial difficulties managing this pain because it negatively impacts their treatment results and care compliance.[4] Orthodontic pain ranks as the number one reason for active treatment interruption and people not following recommended oral hygiene routines and appliance usage. Orthodontic pain management employs analgesics that include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) together with acetaminophen as pain relievers.[5] The medication therapies used to decrease discomfort show different patterns of usage by patients. People heavily consume both counter and over-the-counter pain relievers although this practice exists outside medical supervision which presents risks based on potential misuse or excessive consumption. People often choose not to take sufficient pain medicine either from concern about side effects or personal thinking or because they lack medicines.[6]

OBJECTIVE

To assess the intensity and progression of orthodontic pain, examine dietary modifications in response to discomfort, and evaluate the patterns and sources of analgesic use among orthodontic patients.

METHODOLOGY

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted on 92 patients receiving fixed orthodontic treatment. All participants provided informed consent before enrollment. Patients aged 12 years or older, undergoing fixed orthodontic treatment, in good general health, and capable of independently completing the questionnaire were included in the study. The study excluded patients who had chronic pain or neurological problems or took medicine that affected pain levels or were pregnant or lactating or had undergone recent orthodontic treatments less than 48 hours ago. The study applied a self-administered questionnaire with established formats to gather information from participants. The data collection instrument explores four principal sections which include demographic information along with pain intensity evaluation and dietary adjustments as well as the analysis of medication usage. The visual analog scale (VAS) permitted participants to rate their pain intensity on a scale between zero (no pain) and ten (worst possible pain) at three specified times when measuring pain following appliance placement or adjustment at 24 hours, three days, and seven days, respectively. Patients noted both what specific foods they chose and which foods they excluded, and how long these choices lasted among their dietary adjustments. The questionnaire surveyed analgesic utilization by recording information about prescription or over-the-counter medication type and dosage together with timing of administration and the source of pain management information. Data were analyzed using SPSS v26. The analysis used descriptive statistics to present patient information through mean values along with standard deviations and frequency charts. The research used Chi-square tests to investigate how age and gender relate with patient pain perception.

RESULTS

A total of 92 participants were included in the study, with a mean age of 17.8 years (±3.2). The sample consisted of 58 females (63%) and 34 males (37%), with 67.4% of participants in the early phase of orthodontic treatment. Pain intensity, assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS), was highest at 24 hours post-adjustment (mean score: 6.8 ± 1.4), decreased by the third day (4.3 ± 1.2), and further subsided by the seventh day (2.1 ± 0.9), indicating a clear trend of pain reduction over time. Dietary modifications were reported by 81 participants (88%) in response to orthodontic pain. The most commonly avoided foods included hard fruits and vegetables (74%), meats (56%), and sticky snacks (47%). A moderate positive correlation was observed between pain intensity and dietary restriction (r = 0.51, P < 0.001), indicating that greater pain was associated with more pronounced dietary adjustments [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study provide valuable insight into the multifaceted experiences of orthodontic patients, particularly about pain perception, dietary adaptation, and the use of analgesics. Patients reported maximum pain levels during the initial 24 hours after getting their braces activated because activation pain became their main worry. Ambiguous studies have shown the beginning adaptation phase creates the most agony because of acute periodontal tissue strain. The measured pain scores showed major improvement from the third day onward in all participants after the first seven days of observation.[7] Pain scores recorded from females surpassed those from males at every evaluation period although these variations never reached statistical significance. The research data demonstrate that gender differences in pain perception shown in real-life scenarios do not show enough significance to affect orthodontic clinical treatment outcomes.[8] Nearly all participants adjusted their diets through eating alterations because of pain according to survey results (88%). The majority of patients were selected to eat foods which required gentle chewing instead of raw vegetables and meats together with sticky snacks. Patients treated with analgesics reached 73.9% among the overall sample base who consumed paracetamol and ibuprofen. Patients mostly followed a pain-onset approach when using analgesics because they typically did not premedicate for potential pain. Research findings demonstrate that temporary pain intensifies patient consumption of medications and affects their dietary choices during orthodontic treatment. Both early pain recognition and anticipatory guidance delivery need implementation by orthodontists as part of their patient support against treatment-related pain.

CONCLUSION

It is concluded that pain experienced during orthodontic treatment is most intense within the first 24 hours following appliance activation and gradually subsides over the course of a week. This discomfort significantly influences patients’ dietary habits, leading to temporary shifts toward softer foods and avoidance of harder, chewier options. A majority of patients resort to analgesics, primarily paracetamol and ibuprofen, often without formal guidance.
