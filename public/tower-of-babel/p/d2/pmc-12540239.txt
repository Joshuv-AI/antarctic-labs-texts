Identification and validation of prognostic genes for lung adenocarcinoma prognosis based on PANoptosis-related genes
Authors: Guo-qiang Song, Yu-ying Wu, Tian-li He, Ke-jie Ji, Yi-meng Duan, Jia-wen Zhang, Sheng-qi Fei, Guo-qiang Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPANoptosis, a newly identified form of cell death, has been linked to both the destruction of cancer cells and the regulation of antitumor immunity. This study aimed to establish a PANoptosis-related signature associated with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), highlighting its role in LUAD pathogenesis.MethodsTranscriptome data from the TCGA-LUAD dataset were analyzed to identify PANoptosis-related differentially expressed genes (PANR-DEGs). A LUAD gene signature was developed using Cox regression and LASSO analyses. The study also evaluated somatic mutations, the immune microenvironment, and immunotherapy potential across two risk groups. Additionally, prognostic gene expression was validated clinically by reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR).ResultsThe TCGA-LUAD dataset revealed 520 PANR-DEGs. A prognostic signature based on six key genes—CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, KRT6A, MFI2, and P2RY13—was constructed. Prognostic analysis indicated that pathological T and N statuses, along with the risk score, could serve as independent prognostic markers. Significant differences in the immune microenvironment were observed between the two risk groups, with TP53 showing the highest mutation frequency. Drug sensitivity assays suggested that the PANoptosis-related gene signature could serve as a predictive tool for therapy. Elevated expression levels of CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, KRT6A, and MFI2 were observed in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues. RT-qPCR results confirmed the findings from the bioinformatic analysis.ConclusionA prognostic signature composed of CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, KRT6A, MFI2, and P2RY13, based on PANR-DEGs, provides a theoretical framework and reference for further LUAD research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03690-x.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, PANoptosis, Prognostic genes, Risk score, Immune microenvironment

Introduction

Lung cancer ranks as the second most prevalent cancer globally [1, 2], accounting for a staggering 25% of all cancer-related deaths [3]. Among its various histological subtypes, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common and widely encountered form [4–6]. In the United States, approximately 85% of lung cancers are classified as non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLC), with adenocarcinomas comprising the majority [7]. Treatment options for LUAD primarily include surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiation, and molecular targeted therapies [8]. Despite advancements in diagnosis and treatment, recurrence and metastasis remain significant challenges, with a 5-year survival rate of only 10% to 20% following diagnosis [1]. In recent years, the emergence of multi-omics approaches—integrating genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, proteomics, and metabolomics—has provided comprehensive insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying LUAD [9, 10]. These integrative analyses enable the identification of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets by capturing the complex biological landscape of tumor development and progression. Consequently, there is an urgent need for the identification of novel prognostic genes to enhance the prognosis, diagnosis, and management of LUAD.

Cell death is a fundamental process essential for organismal homeostasis. PANoptosis is an inflammatory programmed cell death pathway that integrates pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, and is regulated by the PANoptosome complex. However, a comprehensive understanding of PANoptosis cannot be achieved by examining these individual pathways in isolation [11]. PANoptosis has been implicated in infectious diseases, sterile inflammation, and cancer [12–14]. This complex regulation is mediated by the PANoptosome [15], a multi-protein complex that acts as a molecular scaffold, containing critical proteins that trigger cell scorching, apoptosis, and programmed necrosis [16]. The PANoptosome induces the release of proteins that promote pyroptosis (NLRP3), apoptosis (CASP8), and necroptosis (RIPK1/RIPK3), leading to cell death [17]. PANoptosis represents a promising mechanism to eliminate cancer cells and reduce resistance, even when other cell death pathways are inhibited [18]. In recent years, the dual role of PANoptosis in tumor research has gradually gained attention. Activating PANoptosis can inhibit tumor growth by inducing cell death in cancer cells, and thus is considered a potential strategy for cancer treatment [19]. However, excessive activation of PANoptosis may lead to chronic inflammatory responses, thereby promoting tumor metastasis or immune escape. The role of this dual effect in cancer progression still needs further exploration [20]. However, the impact of PANoptosis-related differentially expressed genes (PANR-DEGs) on LUAD prognosis and the immune microenvironment remains unclear.

While research on the expression patterns, predictive significance, and molecular role of PANoptosis-related genes (PANRGs) in LUAD has been limited, this study analyzed TCGA-LUAD expression data to validate prognostic PANR-DEGs and clinical samples. It developed a predictive model to explore the potential importance of PANR-DEGs in LUAD. Additionally, the study examined the immunological landscape, tumor microenvironment, somatic mutations, and drug sensitivity across risk groups, aiming to offer novel therapeutic strategies for patients with LUAD.

Materials and methods

Data sources

LUAD RNA sequencing data, including survival information, were sourced from TCGA, encompassing 58 normal and 510 tumor samples. (Table S1). GSE31210 from GEO, used for validation, included 226 tumors and 20 normal samples. Nineteen PANRGs were identified from existing literature [21]. Additionally, datasets GSE43458 (platform: GPL6244), GSE10072 (platform: GPL96), and GSE116959 (platform: GPL17077) were utilized for validating the expression levels of predictive genes. GSE43458 comprised 80 LUAD tissue samples and 30 adjacent normal tissue samples; GSE10072 included 58 LUAD tissue samples and 49 adjacent normal tissue samples; while GSE116959 contained 57 LUAD tissue samples and 11 adjacent normal tissue samples.

Differential analysis

To compute the PANoptosis score for LUAD samples in TCGA, the gene set variation analysis software was combined with the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm [22]. Based on median scores, LUAD samples were categorized into low-score and high-score PANoptosis groups. DEGs1 between these groups were identified using edgeR software. A modified p-value threshold of < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change of >0.5 were applied to determine liminal values. This approach also identified DEGs2 in the TCGA-LUAD dataset that distinguished tumor samples from normal ones. The DEGs were visualized using a volcano plot generated by ggplot2 (v 3.4.0) and a heatmap constructed with pheatmap (v 1.0.12) [23]. PANoptosis-related DEGs (PANR-DEGs) were derived by intersecting DEGs1 and DEGs2, visualized using a Venn diagram. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses of PANR-DEGs were performed using DAVID (v 6.8) and visualized via ggplot2, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05 and a count >2.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

A protein interaction network for PANR-DEGs was established and visualized using STRING (https://string-db.org/) and Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org) [24].

Risk score (RS)-based subgroup analysis of LUAD patients

The TCGA-LUAD dataset was used for training, and prognostic PANR-DEGs were identified through univariate Cox, LASSO regression, and multivariate Cox analyses. Univariate Cox regression analysis was first performed to identify genes associated with patient prognosis, allowing for the exclusion of irrelevant variables. Next, LASSO regression was applied to further narrow down the candidate genes by reducing overfitting and addressing multicollinearity through L1 regularization. Finally, multivariate Cox regression analysis was used to confirm the independent prognostic value of the selected genes and to construct a robust risk model. The risk score for each patient was calculated based on the expression levels of selected genes and their corresponding regression coefficients from the multivariate Cox analysis. To assess multicollinearity among variables in the multivariate Cox regression model, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for each predictor. A VIF value less than 5 was considered to indicate no significant multicollinearity. Specifically, the risk score was the sum of each gene’s expression multiplied by its coefficient. Kaplan-Meier and ROC curves (1, 3, 5 years) were utilized to assess the prognostic accuracy of the model, with GSE31210 serving as the external validation set. To explore the relationships between RS and clinical features, variations in RS were analyzed with respect to several clinicopathological parameters, including age, sex, and pathological N/T/M status.

Clinical nomogram model

Age, sex, pathological N/T/M status, and RS were incorporated into univariate (p < 0.05) and multivariate Cox analyses to identify independent prognostic factors. The cox.zph function from the survival package was used to test the proportional hazards assumption for significant factors in the multivariate Cox model. A nomogram based on independent factors was developed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates in patients with LUAD, with its predictive accuracy assessed using a calibration curve.

Immune feature assessment and chemotherapy analysis

The expression of 28 immune cell types in both LUAD risk categories was evaluated using the ssGSEA method. The relationships among prognostic genes, RSs, and differential immune cell types were assessed through Spearman correlation analysis. Furthermore, the infiltration abundance of 22 immune cell types in high- and low-risk groups within the TCGA-LUAD cohort was assessed using the CIBERSORT algorithm, excluding samples with P > 0.05. The Wilcoxon test identified significantly different immune cell types between groups (P < 0.05). Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate associations between prognostic gene expression and the infiltration levels of these differentially abundant immune cells. Tumor purity was estimated using the ESTIMATE algorithm implemented via the estimate software (v1.0.13). This method calculated stromal scores, immune scores, and combined estimate scores to indirectly reflect tumor purity. Generally, lower stromal and immune scores indicated higher tumor purity, while the estimate score integrated both to assess the proportion of non-tumor components in the tumor microenvironment. The Wilcoxon test were used to compare stromal, immune, and estimate scores across groups, providing an indirect assessment of the impact of tumor purity on immune infiltration results. The immune landscape of LUAD was examined by comparing the tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) score and the immunophenotype score (IPS) between the high-risk group (HRG) and low-risk group (LRG), as well as analyzing the expression of 44 immune checkpoint genes.

Somatic mutation and tumor mutational burden (TMB) analysis and drug sensitivity analysis

Somatic mutation landscapes were investigated using the maftools package in R (version 2.4.10) [25]. Somatic mutation frequencies were compared between the two risk groups. TMB was calculated and compared between HRG and LRG, with an analysis of its correlation with RS. Based on median TMB values, LUAD samples were classified into high- and low-TMB groups, creating four categories: high risk–high/low TMB and low risk–high/low TMB. The association between these groups and patient survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. Drug sensitivity analysis was performed using gene expression data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer database (GDSC) (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) and LUAD samples from the TCGA dataset. A total of 138 pharmaceuticals included in the analysis were those with available expression profiles in the GDSC database. The pRRophetic package (v 0.5) [26] was used to build ridge regression models to predict the IC50 values of each drug for TCGA samples. Lower IC50 values indicated higher sensitivity. The Wilcoxon test was then applied to compare the predicted IC50 values between high- and low-risk groups, and drugs with significant differences were identified based on p < 0.05.

Validation by reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Five patients with pathologically confirmed primary LUAD from Changxing County Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, diagnosed between December 2022 and March 2023, were included in the study, which was approved by the hospital’s ethical committee and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (Table S2). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. None of the patients had received radiation or chemotherapy, nor did they have a history of other cancers. Total RNA was extracted from lung tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and reverse transcription was performed using the SureScript™ First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ServiceBio, Wuhan, China) on an S1000™ Thermal Cycler PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories) according to standard protocols. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3. The real-time quantitative PCR program involved an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 60 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 55 °C for 20 s, and amplification at 72 °C for 30 s. mRNA expression of six genes was calculated using the 2−△△Ct method, with beta-actin as the reference gene.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1), GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA), and ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses using the Wilcoxon test were performed as two-tailed tests. The two-tailed approach was chosen because the primary aim was to assess whether there was a significant difference between groups in specific measures, without assuming a specific direction of change. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between prognostic genes and differentially immune cells. The t-test was deployed for normal distribution data, while employing the Mann-Whitney U test for not normally distributed data.

Results

PANR-DEG identification in patients with LUAD

Nineteen PANRGs (CASP1/6/7/8, FADD, NLRP3, Table 2/3, PSTPIP2, TNFAIP3, PARP1, GSDMD, MLKL, IRF1, AIM2, ZBP1, RIPK1/3, and TRADD) were obtained from previous research [21]. In the TCGA-LUAD dataset, 891 DEGs1 were identified, including 757 significantly upregulated and 134 significantly downregulated genes (Fig. 1). Similarly, 5265 DEGs2 were identified, of which 2996 were significantly upregulated and 2269 were significantly downregulated (Figs. 1B, S1B). Genes that were simultaneously upregulated and downregulated in DEGs1 and DEGs2 are listed in Table S4. A total of 520 PANR-DEGs were identified by overlapping DEGs1 and DEGs2 (Fig. 1C). Functional enrichment analysis of these PANR-DEGs revealed their primary involvement in T-cell activation and inflammatory responses (Fig. 1D). Moreover, KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these genes were notably enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion molecules and the hematopoietic cell lineage (Fig. 1E). A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for the 520 PANR-DEGs, consisting of 259 nodes and 1,268 edges, with a highest confidence score of 0.9 (Fig. 1F). Within this network, ARHGEF6 emerged as the central hub, displaying the highest degree of connectivity among all nodes.

Prognostic signature based on survival-associated PANR-DEGs

Eighteen significant genes were identified through univariate regression analysis (hazard ratio ≠ 1, p < 0.001) in TCGA-LUAD (Fig. 2A; Table 1). LASSO regression analysis of these 18 genes revealed 12 key genes: ARNTL2, CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, GAB3, GJB2, HLA-DMA, KRT6A, MFI2, MS4A1, P2RY13, and UNC13B (Figs. 2B, S1C). Furthermore, six genes (CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, KRT6A, MFI2, and P2RY13) were identified as predictive markers for constructing an RNA-based prognostic signature for LUAD via multivariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 2C). Both CLIC6 and KRT6A showed significant associations with prognosis in the initial univariate Cox analysis, suggesting their potential relevance to patient survival. They were retained in the LASSO regression, indicating their value in reducing overfitting and improving model performance. Although their p-values in the multivariate analysis were not statistically significant, their inclusion may reflect synergistic effects with other genes and contribute to the overall predictive accuracy of the risk model. All six genes had VIF values below 5, indicating the absence of multicollinearity (Table S5). Patients with LUAD were then stratified into two distinct risk groups (Fig. 2D). Higher expression levels of CDCP1, FURIN, KRT6A, and MFI2 were predominantly associated with HRG, whereas CLIC6 and P2RY13 were overexpressed in LRG (Figure S1D). Survival analysis revealed significant differences between the two risk groups, with high-risk patients exhibiting poorer survival outcomes (Fig. 2E). The model’s reliability was further assessed by the area under the curve (AUC) for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates in the TCGA dataset, yielding AUC values of 0.782, 0.630, and 0.693, respectively. These results indicate that the model effectively predicts the survival outcomes of patients with LUAD (Fig. 2F). Validation of the risk model was performed using the GSE31210 dataset. In alignment with the TCGA findings (Figs. 3A and S1E), patients in the HRG displayed significantly lower overall survival (OS) (Fig. 3B). The AUC for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival predictions exceeded 0.60 (Fig. 3C). Clinical analysis revealed that the RS varied significantly based on sex and pathological M/N/T classifications (Fig. 3D–H).

Immune microenvironment analysis

The immunological microenvironment of LUAD was assessed by comparing 28 immune cell infiltration levels across two TCGA sample groups. Statistically significant differences were observed in the abundance of 20 immune cell types, particularly activated B cells, mast cells (MCs), and eosinophils (Fig. 5A). Correlation analysis revealed a negative association between the RS and the presence of MCs and eosinophils (Fig. 5B). P2RY13 was positively correlated with several immune cells, including T follicular helper cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and regulatory T cells (Fig. 5C). Additionally, CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to conduct immune infiltration analysis on the TCGA-LUAD samples (Fig. S2A). The results revealed that significant differences (p < 0.05) in infiltration levels were observed for 6 immune cell types between the high-risk and low-risk groups, such as monocytes (Fig. S2B). Of these, P2RY13 exhibited the strongest positive correlation with resting dendritic cells (Fig. S2C). Thirty differential immune checkpoints were identified between risk subgroups, including ADORA2A, BTLA, BTNL2, and CD200R1/244 (Fig. 5D). Significant differences in ESTIMATE, immune, and stromal scores, as well as in TIDE and IPS scores, were observed between HRG and LRG (Fig. 5E-G).

Somatic mutations and TMB analysis in patients with LUAD

Here, to investigate the genomic differences between LUAD risk groups, we analyzed somatic mutation profiles from the TCGA cohort. As shown in Figs. 6A–B, TP53 exhibited the highest mutation frequency in both groups, and missense mutations were the most common mutation type. The TMB was significantly higher in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (P = 7.3e–3; Fig. 6C). In addition, the risk score showed a weak positive correlation with TMB (r = 0.20, P = 1.4e–3; Fig. 6D), suggesting that higher-risk patients tend to have greater mutational loads. Survival analysis integrating both risk level and TMB revealed that patients in the low-risk/high-TMB group had the best outcomes, whereas the high-risk/low-TMB group had the worst prognosis (P < 0.0001; Fig. 6E), emphasizing the added prognostic value of TMB when combined with the gene-based signature.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To explore potential pharmacological agents targeting prognostic genes, IC50 values of 138 drugs were analyzed across risk subgroups. There were 75 differential drugs in both risk subgroups, such as axitinib and bosutinib (Table S6). In the low-risk LUAD subgroup, the IC50 values for docetaxel (log2FC = 1.04539) and paclitaxel (log2FC = 1.100804) — two widely used chemotherapeutic agents — were significantly elevated compared with those in the high-risk subgroup (Fig. 6F-G).

Expression validation of prognostic genes

To further validate the expression patterns of the six prognostic genes identified in our study, we analyzed their expression levels in both the training set and the GSE31210 dataset. The results revealed that CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, KRT6A, and MFI2 were consistently upregulated in tumor tissues, whereas P2RY13 was downregulated (Fig. 7A–B). We then extended the validation to three additional independent GEO datasets (GSE10072, GSE43458, and GSE116959) to assess the reproducibility of the findings. In these datasets, CDCP1 and KRT6A exhibited significant upregulation, while P2RY13 showed significant downregulation (p < 0.05, Figs. 7C–E). These consistent results across multiple datasets strengthen the reliability of our bioinformatic predictions and provide a robust foundation for further experimental validation.

Verification in LUAD tissue samples

To experimentally confirm the bioinformatic results, we measured the mRNA expression levels of the six prognostic genes in paired tumor and adjacent noncancerous LUAD tissues from five patients using RT-qPCR (Fig. 8). The analysis demonstrated that tumor tissues exhibited higher expression levels of CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, KRT6A, and MFI2 compared with adjacent tissues. Among them, CDCP1, KRT6A, and MFI2 showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), which were consistent with the bioinformatic findings described in Sect. 3.7. These results collectively confirm the robustness of the identified prognostic gene signature and support its potential clinical relevance in LUAD.

Discussion

With the advancement of targeted therapies and immunotherapy, interest in the management of LUAD has grown. However, many patients fail to benefit from these treatments, facing challenges related to drug resistance and recurrence. Consequently, further exploration of LUAD’s development mechanisms and the identification of novel therapeutic targets remain essential.

PANoptosis, a recently discovered form of cell death, plays a pivotal role in various diseases, including infectious conditions, sterile inflammation, and cancer [27–29]. In oncology, PANoptosis is recognized as a potential anti-tumor mechanism due to its capacity to eliminate cancer cells through immune response activation [30]. Recent research has highlighted increasingly complex regulatory mechanisms of PANoptosis within the tumor microenvironment. In some solid tumors, PANoptosis not only directly induces cancer cell death but also indirectly modulates immune evasion by altering the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [31]. Under PANoptosis-driven signaling, macrophages may adopt an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, promoting tumor growth and metastasis [32]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that specific small-molecule compounds can precisely regulate the assembly of the PANoptosome—an essential molecular complex driving PANoptosis—offering new possibilities for the development of anti-cancer drugs [19]. These findings deepen our understanding of PANoptosis in tumorigenesis and progression, providing both a strong theoretical foundation and potential therapeutic targets for novel cancer treatment strategies. However, the role of PANR-DEGs in LUAD prognosis and the immune microenvironment remains poorly understood.

This study explored the potential regulatory roles of PANRGs in LUAD. Through comprehensive analysis, PANR-DEGs were identified in LUAD, and a prognostic signature based on survival-associated PAN-DEGs was constructed. Independent prognostic factors for LUAD, such as the RS and pathological T/N/M status, were evaluated, alongside an analysis of 28 immune cell infiltration levels and somatic mutation profiles. Additionally, the IC50 values of common cancer drugs, such as docetaxel and paclitaxel, were compared, with significant results observed in the low-risk group. Finally, the prognostic genes were validated in both the training set and the GSE31210 dataset, with further confirmation obtained through PCR. Furthermore, through PPI network analysis, we discovered that ARHGEF6 may interact with multiple members of the PANoptosis pathway. Although these predictions provide preliminary clues for the interaction between ARHGEF6 and the members of the PANoptosis pathway, there is currently a lack of direct experimental verification to further confirm the existence of these interactions. Therefore, future research should validate these potential interactions through a series of functional experiments. For instance, using co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments, one can directly detect whether ARHGEF6 forms a physical binding with other PANoptosis-related proteins.

Six prognostic genes (CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, KRT6A, MFI2, and P2RY13) were identified in the TCGA-LUAD dataset, and prognostic modeling based on RSs was performed. CDCP1, a transmembrane protein with intracellular tyrosine residues, is implicated in the proliferative activity and anti-apoptotic effects of cancer cells [26]. Gao et al. [33]. Gao et al. [34] reported that CDCP1 facilitates colorectal cancer cell adhesion to pulmonary endothelium, thereby promoting metastasis. Ikeda et al. [35] found CDCP1 expression to be an independent prognostic factor for both OS and disease-free survival in patients with LUAD. Additionally, CDCP1 has been shown to contribute to resistance against EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors by regulating EGFR signaling pathways [36]. Dysregulation of CDCP1 expression occurs years before LUAD diagnosis [37]. CLIC6 has been associated with abnormal traits in 21q microdeletion acute myeloid leukemia and may contribute to developmental delays [38, 39]. Huang et al. demonstrated that CLIC6 promoter DNA methylation levels were significantly lower in hepatocellular carcinoma tissue compared to normal tissue [40]. In prostate cancer, CLIC6, in conjunction with miR-200c-3p, was found to have favorable prognostic potential [41]. Our findings corroborate this.

FURIN inhibition has been shown to prevent LUAD proliferation and migration by downregulating proteins associated with migration and apoptosis [42]. In patients with LUAD, FURIN expression is linked to several key immune cell and macrophage markers [43]. Single-cell analysis revealed that FURIN expression is heterogeneous across individuals, primarily expressed in cancer and alveolar cells, with higher expression rates in LUAD samples compared to lung squamous cell carcinoma. Interestingly, FURIN overexpression was not observed in any cell type of smokers, unlike in nonsmokers [44]. KRT6A promotes LUAD growth and metastasis by inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition and tumor stem cell transformation [45]. KRT6A plays a crucial role in tumor metastasis. Its mechanism of promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) mainly involves activating the Wnt/β catenin signaling pathway, which leads to the downregulation of E-cadherin and the upregulation of EMT markers such as N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail, thereby enhancing the migration and invasion abilities of tumor cells [46]. KRT6A is overexpressed in tumor tissues in contrast to normal ones, which is linked to poor patient prognosis [47, 48]. Furthermore, the high expression of KRT6A is closely related to the pathological progression of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). KRT6A can activate the MYC pathway and increase the expression of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), thereby enhancing the pentose phosphate pathway and supporting cell invasion and metabolic reprogramming. This may provide energy requirements and phenotypic basis for EMT [48]. From a clinical perspective, EMT is closely related to tumor metastasis, drug resistance, and immune escape [49]. Therefore, the promoting behavior of KRT6A during EMT process may give it the potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. MFI2 expression is notably higher in young, never-smoking patients with LUAD [50]. Furthermore, the overexpression of MFI2 long noncoding RNA enhances osteosarcoma cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis [51]. P2RY13, which is relatively suppressed in LUAD tissue, is associated with better prognosis [52]. It has also been identified as a prognostic biomarker for LUAD, linked to immune-related pathways, with higher expression levels correlating with more enriched immune pathways [53]. PCR results further validated the significance of these prognostic genes in LUAD. In conclusion, CDCP1, CLIC6, FURIN, KRT6A, MFI2, and P2RY13 play pivotal roles in LUAD through distinct molecular mechanisms. Collectively, they influence key processes such as tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, drug resistance, immune modulation, and disease comorbidities, ultimately driving the progression of LUAD and shaping patient prognosis.

In this study, we observed that the risk score of female patients with LUAD was significantly higher than that of male patients, suggesting that gender may have a potential impact in the risk model constructed based on genetic characteristics. This difference may stem from various biological bases. On one hand, females differ significantly from males in immune response, hormone levels (such as estrogen), and epigenetic regulation, which may affect the expression patterns of the tumor microenvironment and related genes, thereby leading to the gender distribution difference in the risk score [54]. Existing studies have pointed out that estrogen, through ERβ, upregulates osteopontin, activates the MEK-ERK pathway and interacts with the EGFR signal, significantly promoting the migration of lung adenocarcinoma cells; clinical data also confirm that premenopausal non-smoking female patients with lung adenocarcinoma have more severe conditions and poorer prognosis, suggesting that estrogen is not only a key driver of lung adenocarcinoma progression but also a potential target for prognosis markers and combined treatment [55]. Additionally, the study also found that estrogen can directly promote the occurrence and deterioration of lung adenocarcinoma by accelerating the proliferation of activated cells [56]. This finding suggests that considering gender factors when making individualized treatment decisions based on genetic characteristics is helpful for further optimizing the applicability and interpretability of the model, and also provides clues for in-depth research on the molecular mechanisms of tumor gender differences.

Significant differences were observed in the abundance levels of 20 immune cell types. In interstitial lymphocytes, no notable correlation was found between PD-L1 expression and T-bet+ (a marker for Th1 pathway activation) [57]. In the TCGA dataset, patients with PD-L1 overexpression showed significantly elevated proportions of IFN-γ, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, NK CD56 dim cells, and Th1/2 cells [58]. Recent studies have revealed a shared Treg cell-dependent genetic program. Specifically, genes related to the VEGF and CCR2 signaling pathways were significantly upregulated in Treg cell-deficient, Treg cell-deprived, and Treg cell-rich LUAD samples [59]. Further cell signaling analysis indicated that the MIF-(CD74+CXCR4) interaction is crucial for Treg cell engagement with various cell subpopulations, and Treg cell-associated labeling (TRAS) effectively stratifies LUAD cohorts into high- and low-risk categories [60]. The acetic acids/FFAR2 axis contributes to cancer progression by promoting MDSC-mediated immunosuppression through the Gαq/calcium/PPAR-γ/Arg1 signaling pathway [61]. Additionally, A. sydowii facilitates lung cancer progression by inducing IL-1β-mediated expansion and MDSC stimulation, which suppresses cytotoxic T lymphocyte function and PD-1+ CD8+ T cell accumulation [62]. High infiltration of immune cells, such as Type 1 T helper cells, regulatory T cells, and MDSCs in the low-risk group, may lead to immunosuppression, fostering LUAD onset and progression while hindering effective treatment. These genes and related factors significantly influence the immune microenvironment of LUAD through various complex mechanisms. Dysregulation or abnormal activation of these genes and signaling pathways may not only drive LUAD progression but also impact therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, targeting these key genes and pathways holds promise as a novel direction for LUAD immunotherapy.

Thirty differential immune checkpoints were identified between the two risk subgroups, including ADORA2A, BTLA, BTNL2, and CD200R1/244, with significant differences observed in TIDE scores and IPS between the groups. While immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown survival benefits in patients with advanced NSCLC [63], many aspects of ICI therapy in NSCLC remain unclear. Zhang et al. [64] identified CCL7 as a potential biomarker and adjuvant for checkpoint immunotherapy in patients with NSCLC. FHX3 mutations were strongly correlated with prolonged OS in patients with NSCLC undergoing ICI treatment, suggesting that FHX3 could serve as a novel predictor to guide ICI therapy [65]. In the present study, significant differences in immunity, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores were observed between HRG and LRG. A 21-gene prognostic model revealed that HRG patients exhibited increased tumor purity and decreased stromal, ESTIMATE, and immune scores, indicating that this genetic signature is associated with poor prognosis in LUAD and influences tumor progression, proliferation, and metastasis [66]. Both ESTIMATE and stromal scores correlated with RSs derived from a gene signature associated with cancer-associated fibroblasts in LUAD [67]. Tumors in high-risk populations are likely to exhibit an immunosuppressive microenvironment that hampers antitumor immunity and promotes tumor growth.

TP53 exhibits the highest mutation frequency across different risk groups of patients with LUAD. Moreover, missense mutations constitute the largest proportion of all mutation types. TP53 mutations compromise the tumor-suppressive function of p53 and are present in nearly all cancers, including ALK-positive NSCLC, where they represent the most frequent genetic alteration. These mutations are associated with poor progression-free survival in patients [68]. Kim et al. [69] found that TP53 mutations predict a poor response to immunotherapy in patients with metastatic solid tumors. Additionally, TP53 mutations are linked to a diminished immune response in patients with LUAD [70]. However, some studies suggest the opposite, showing that TP53 mutations are associated with remission and longer survival after ICI therapy in patients with advanced NSCLC [71]. Further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms. Studies in hepatocytes and neuroblastoma cells have revealed the complex interplay between TP53 and other cellular processes. Inhibiting phosphatase 1, induced by wild-type p53, triggers mTORC1 signaling, promoting liver cell proliferation following hepatectomy [72]. In neuroblastoma cells, knocking out WIP1 or using the WIP1 inhibitor CCT007093 led to the phosphorylation of known WIP1 targets, suppressed HDM2, and reduced growth [73].

These results clearly demonstrate that varying immune cell infiltration levels alter the tumor microenvironment, that somatic mutations exacerbate LUAD, potentially leading to immune escape, and that hypermutation correlates with decreased OS in LUAD.

For the first time, this study explored the prognostic value of PANRGs in patients with LUAD and identified prognostic genes.

However, this study has several limitations. RT-qPCR verification currently only assesses mRNA levels, and while prognostic-related genes have been identified, the specific molecular mechanisms through which they influence LUAD prognosis and the immune microenvironment remain underexplored. Additionally, the small sample size in the RT-qPCR validation experiment impacts the statistical reliability and generalizability of the results. Future studies will address these issues by including more independent GEO datasets for supplementary validation to confirm the robustness and clinical utility of the identified prognostic genes. In terms of validation, future research will detect protein expression of these genes in LUAD and normal tissue through immunohistochemistry to further enhance the reliability of the findings. Mechanistic investigations will involve gene knockout and overexpression experiments, as well as the construction of relevant cell and animal models to explore the detailed pathways by which these genes regulate LUAD development and clarify their roles in prognosis and the immune microenvironment. To overcome the limitation of sample size, future work will involve multiple centers to expand the sample collection and increase the number of samples, ensuring that the follow-up results are more statistically significant and widely applicable, thus providing a stronger theoretical foundation and practical guidance for LUAD prognosis evaluation and treatment.

Conclusion

This study identified molecular classifications in LUAD based on PANR-DEGs and developed a prognostic signature. Immune infiltration, gene mutation status, and pharmacological predictions across different risk groups were also analyzed. Additionally, clinical samples were validated through RT-qPCR. Overall, this signature holds potential for prognostic evaluation and guiding drug therapy.
