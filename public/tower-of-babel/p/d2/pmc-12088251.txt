The Orthopaedic Burden of Gun-shot Injury in Children: A Single-Institution Analysis
Authors: Devin A. Maez, Samuel L. Flesner, Angela M. Martz, Patrick P. Bosch, Richard J. Miskimins
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rates of firearm injuries among pediatric patients have risen significantly in recent years. Extremity injuries resulting from gunshot wounds (GSWs) often require orthopaedic management. In addition to rare nationwide database studies, there are 2 regional studies examining this trend in the South only. Our state has notably high gun ownership and lenient gun protection laws. To examine the orthopaedic burden of gunshot injuries in our region, we reviewed the pediatric patients presenting with GSWs at a tertiary level-1 trauma center over a 7-year period.MethodsA retrospective chart review was conducted of pediatric patients <18 years of age presenting with GSW-related injuries at our state's only level-1 trauma center from 2016 through 2022. Data collected included demographics, injury characteristics, fracture locations, surgical management, antibiotic treatment, and long-term complications. Statistical analysis was performed to identify significant trends and disparities.ResultsGSW-related presentations increased 109% from 2016 to 2022 (P ​= ​.005), while orthopaedic consults increased 83% (P ​= ​.032), and GSW-related fractures increased 280% (P ​= ​.009). A total of 46.4% of all patients required orthopaedic consultation.Most patients were male (80.2%), with a mean age of 14.6 years (range: 16 months-17 years). Hispanic patients were over-represented compared to the state population (64.1% versus 47.7%, P ​< ​.001), as were African Americans (6.1% versus 1.8%, P ​< ​.001).A total of 4.8% of patients were uninsured. Privately insured individuals were under-represented (16.9% versus 35.9%, P ​< ​.001), whereas publicly insured patients were over-represented (78.2% versus 56.5%, P ​< ​.001). As per chart documentation, accidental discharge was the most common cause of injury (56.5%), followed by assault (33.9%).The overall fracture rate was 43.1%. Long-bone fractures accounted for 50.5% of all fractures. A total of 57.4% of orthopaedic consults required operative management. Documented antibiotic administration was noted in 84.4% of patients, predominantly cefazolin. Complications were observed in 36.5% of orthopaedic consults, with nerve injury being the most frequent (17.4%). Only 5.2% experienced infections.ConclusionsPediatric GSW-related injuries in our state present a significant orthopaedic burden, with increasing trends in presentations and ballistic fractures over the study period. Orthopaedic surgeons have a significant role in managing these patients, improving treatment protocols and providing guidance for evidence-based approaches to improve laws to protect children.Key Concepts(1)The study analyzes a 7-year trend of pediatric gunshot wound (GSW) presentations at our state's only level-1 trauma center, focusing on those requiring orthopaedic consultation.(2)Findings indicate a statistically significant increase in pediatric GSW-related presentations and orthopaedic consultations, highlighting an escalating clinical burden on orthopaedic services in this community.(3)Demographic analysis reveals overrepresentation of Hispanic and African American patients and a high prevalence of publicly insured individuals among pediatric GSW cases at this institution.(4)This research underscores the high rate of fractures and complications associated with pediatric GSWs, emphasizing the critical role of orthopaedic management.(5)The study provides valuable data on the epidemiology and significant orthopaedic burden of managing pediatric GSWs, with broader implications for improving treatment strategies and public health policies.Level of EvidenceIII (Retrospective Cohort Study)

Introduction

Gun violence is a major public health concern in the United States [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Among the pediatric population specifically, the rising prevalence of firearm injuries contributes substantially to child morbidity and mortality [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. In 2017, firearms overtook motor vehicle collisions as a leading cause of death among children [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. In nonfatal cases, a significant number of gunshot-wound (GSW)-related injuries result in extremity injuries and ballistic fractures requiring orthopaedic management. Nationwide database research demonstrates a significant musculoskeletal burden from the increase in pediatric firearm injuries [[14], [15], [16]]. A more granular assessment of the pediatric orthopaedic involvement in gun violence is provided by two studies reporting regional experience, both in the American South, up to 2015 [17,18].

Our experience as the only level-1 pediatric trauma center for our state has been concerning. Similar to the American South [13], our state has high gun-per-capita rates, with 55.34 registered firearms per 1000 residents, and a gun ownership rate of 46.2% was recorded in early 2024 [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Related to this, the state lacks child access prevention (CAP) laws and ranks poorly on gun-law scores, such as the Brady scorecards or the Gifford Law Center Annual Gun Law Scorecard [2,[23], [24], [25]].

In order to analyze the pediatric orthopaedic burden of gun violence at our own institution, we reviewed our institutional volume of GSW-related presentations in children and adolescents for a 7-year span to the end of 2022. We hypothesized that there would be a continuous increase over this time period and that further analysis of these patients could add to the literature of the gun injury epidemic in children, guide proper resource allocation, and give directions for advocacy.

Materials and methods

Institutional research board approval for data collection and analysis (protocol no. 23-494) was approved for this study, and all electronic medical records (EMRs) for patients diagnosed with gunshot injuries presenting at a tertiary level-1 trauma center in our state's largest city from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022, were queried utilizing the International Classification of Diseases 10th integration (ICD-10) codes. This study period was selected to minimize limitations when utilizing data spanning the ICD-9 and ICD-10 switch on October 1st, 2015.

The ICD-10 code list included X93 “Assault by handgun discharge,” X94 “Assault by rifle, shotgun and larger firearm discharge,” X95 “Assault by other and unspecified firearm and gun discharge,” X72 “Intentional self-harm by handgun discharge,” X73 “Intentional self-harm by rifle, shotgun and larger firearm discharge,” X74 “Intentional self-harm by other and unspecified firearm and gun discharge,” W32 “Accidental handgun discharge and malfunction,” W33 “Accidental rifle, shotgun and larger firearm discharge and malfunction,” and W34 “Accidental discharge and malfunction from other and unspecified firearms and guns.”

This coding system generated a list of pediatric patients aged 0 through 17 years who presented to a level-1 trauma center in our state's largest city. Of note, this is the only such institution of this acuity in the state. Therefore, the main area of interest was the greater urban area in which this hospital is located and, to a lesser extent, the entirety of the state as many patients from across the state are commonly transferred to this hospital for escalating levels of care. All children and adolescents through 17 years of age, presenting to the hospital, fatally or nonfatally injured with a firearm under any circumstance were included in this study. Patients sustaining firearm injuries who were 18 or older at the time of injury, sustained an injury outside of the given date range, or were injured by a non–firearm type gun (eg, BB, airsoft) were subsequently excluded.

After the data set was generated, the EMRs of each patient were reviewed retrospectively in depth. Basic information from each chart was recorded including date of injury, age at time of injury, sex, race/ethnicity, anatomic location(s) of projectile injury, and projectile type. Trips to the emergency department (ED) as opposed to outpatient presentations, orthopaedic consults, and operating room (OR) visits, if applicable, were also recorded, including the specialty of the attending surgeon (eg, general surgeon, orthopaedics, neurosurgery, etc.). For those who required orthopaedic consultation, bones fractured (if applicable), and treatment with an antibiotic course (including the medication name, dose, and length of treatment in days) and tetanus vaccine administration were noted, as well as outpatient follow-up duration. Any patient who required orthopaedic intervention and had complications related to their presenting injury or subsequent care was further reviewed in depth by a board certified pediatric orthopaedic surgeon.

Statistical analysis was then performed comparing demographic data of patients included in our study to the State's demographic data from the 2020 United States census reported on census.gov using a Chi-Squared test in the XLSTAT software (RRID: SCR_016299) [29]. A P-value was computed to determine a link (or lack thereof) between our data and the demographic data of the state. All trends were analyzed using a Mann–Kendall trend test in XLSTAT. Kendall's Tau was obtained to determine strength and direction of trends, and a P-value was obtained to determine statistical significance. For all statistical analyses, a P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General trends

A total of 353 pediatric patients presenting with GSW-related injuries were identified from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022. There were 9 reported fatalities that occurred either en route to the hospital or at the hospital and were subsequently excluded from evaluation. Ninety-six remaining patients sustained BB/airsoft injuries and were also excluded, leaving 248 patients who met inclusion criteria. Out of the 248 patients, 115 (46.4%) required orthopaedic consultation and were reviewed in further detail.

During the study period, GSW-related presentations rose from 21 in 2016 to 44 in 2022, a 109% increase. When analyzed on a quarterly basis, a significant upward trend was identified (τ ​= ​0.388, P ​= ​.005). The number of orthopaedic consults for these injuries also increased significantly over the study period (τ ​= ​0.310, P ​= ​.032), from 12 in 2016 to 22 in 2022, an 83% increase. It is important to note that while we saw significant increases for both total GSWs and orthopaedic consults, the proportion of orthopaedic consults relative to total GSW presentations remained relatively unchanged over the study period (12/21 [57.14%] of GSW presentations in 2016 versus 22/44 [50%] in 2022). Additionally, GSW-related fractures significantly increased 280% from 5 in 2016 to 19 in 2022 (τ ​= ​0.371, P ​= ​.009) [Fig. 1]. Demographics

The majority of patients sustaining GSW were male (n ​= ​199, 80.2%), with a mean age of 14.6 years (range: 16 months–17 years). Hispanic ethnicity was predominant, comprising 64.1% of cases (n ​= ​159), significantly higher than the state's 47.7% Hispanic population (P ​< ​.001). Additionally, African-American patients were over-represented in our data (6.1% of patients versus 1.8% Statewide, P ​< ​.001). Native American and Asian victims of GSW were proportionate to the state population, with no significant difference observed (P ​= ​.383 and P ​= ​.120, respectively). Finally, non-Hispanic White individuals comprised 14.1% of our dataset compared to 36.5% statewide (P ​< ​.001) [Fig. 2].Figure 2Demographic breakdown of race/ethnicity among GSW victims and orthopaedic consults at our state's only level-1 trauma center from 2016 to 2022 versus population of the state as reported in the 2020 United States census [29]. With regards to insurance coverage, 4.8% (n ​= ​12) of pediatric patients sustaining GSWs were uninsured, not differing significantly from a mean of 4.2% of children in the general state population (P ​= ​.597) [26]. Of insured patients, 42 were insured through private companies (16.9%), compared to 35.9% Statewide (P ​< ​.001). Additionally, 194 (78.2%) of the patients were covered under government-sponsored insurance plans (eg, Medicaid) compared to 56.5% of all children statewide (P ​< ​.001).

Accidental discharge was the most commonly reported cause of injury, accounting for 56.5% (n ​= ​65) of cases, followed by assault (33.9%, n ​= ​39). Only one case of deliberate self-inflicted injury was reported (0.9%). For children under the age of 13, accidental discharge constituted 77.0% (n ​= ​23) of all GSW types. The majority of patients presented to the ER setting (89.6%, n ​= ​103). For all GSW patients, the average length of inpatient hospital stay (LOS) was 7.6 days, with a median LOS of 2.0 days.

Management

A total of 115 of the 248 patients (46.4%) presenting with a GSW required surgical intervention to manage their injuries, including 66 of the 115 orthopaedic consults (57.4%). Among the 66 operative orthopaedic consults, 51 were surgically managed by the orthopaedics team directly (77.3%) for their musculoskeletal injuries. In patients sustaining long-bone fractures among this group, 77.8% (n ​= ​42) underwent surgical debridement of their injury site, 50.0% (n ​= ​27) received operative fracture fixation, and 37.04% (n ​= ​20) had the bullet fragments removed acutely. Many of the orthopaedic consults also received surgical care from other services including general surgery (30.3%, n ​= ​20), vascular surgery (9.1%, n ​= ​6), otolaryngology (6.1%, n ​= ​4), and neurosurgery (3.03%, n ​= ​2). Multidisciplinary care involving more than one surgical team per patient was seen in 25.8% (n ​= ​17) of patients.

Although there is no specific protocol for antibiotic management of GSW injuries within the orthopaedic department at our institution, a single dose of broad-spectrum Gram-positive coverage has been considered standard of care for GSW-associated injuries. Documented antibiotic administration was noted in 84.4% (n ​= ​97) of patients over the course of their care. Cefazolin was the predominant antibiotic used (82.5%). Eighty-five of these patients received intravenous (IV) antibiotics (87.6%); the majority of which (74.1%, n ​= ​63) only received a single dose of IV antibiotics on presentation, whereas 12.9% (n ​= ​12) received a longer course of IV treatment. Twenty-three patients received a course of oral antibiotics (24.7%). The average treatment duration was 3.1 days. Only 28.7% (n ​= ​33) of patients were documented as receiving a tetanus vaccination.

Discussion

Gun violence among children and adolescents remains a national concern [10]. Increasing numbers of pediatric and adolescent patients are presenting with GSW injuries [5,15,18]. The experience we present from our institution over a 7-year stretch from 2016 to 2022 indicates a continuation of that trend, with statistically significant quarter-on-quarter increases over the study period (τ ​= ​0.388, P ​= ​.005). Specifically, the orthopaedic burden (n ​= ​115 patients) has also matched that increase (τ ​= ​0.310, P ​= ​.032); the orthopaedic service was consulted for nearly half (46.4%) of all pediatric patients sustaining firearm-related injuries at our institution.

Similar regional reports currently exist in the literature. Naranje et al. reported on 49 patients (<18 years of age) sustaining orthopaedic injuries due to GSW at two level-1 pediatric trauma centers in Memphis, Tennessee and Birmingham, Alabama for 2005 to 2013 [17]. Similar to our data, they report a predominance of long-bone involvement, high rate of complications, but low rate of direct infection [17]. Another assessment of the orthopaedic burden of musculoskeletal firearm injuries came from Nashville, Tennessee. Evans et al. reported on 140 patients (<18 years of age) that presented from 2002 to 2015. They emphasized a high financial burden to the medical system due to the often complicated patients who are predominantly publicly insured (61%) [18]. Our paper updates these trends into the pandemic years, supports the claimed burdens to the medical system, and adds the southwest region to the national epidemic of pediatric GSW orthopaedic injuries.

In the pediatric population, fracture rates associated with firearm injuries vary considerably in the literature. Naranje et al. reported that approximately half of children and adolescents presenting with projectile injuries have a fracture [17]. In contrast, Loder et al. reported a fracture rate of just 4.4% in a 19-year United States–wide epidemiologic study [14]. Our dataset demonstrated 33.9% of all 248 patients (n ​= ​84, 73.0% of orthopaedic consults) sustained a fracture in one or more bones. We report a total of 36 fractures involving the lower extremity (excluding foot) and 22 in the upper extremity (excluding hand). The most commonly fractured bones were the tibia (16.9%), femur (15.5%), and humerus (10.7%), suggesting a predominance of long-bone involvement. These findings were consistent with those reported by Naranje et al., who also found the most common sites of fracture to be the femur and tibia in a retrospective case series examining GSW-associated fractures among pediatric patients [17]. Fractures involving bones of the foot (12.1%), hand (12.1%), spine (5.6%), and pelvis (5.6%) were also highly variable in our study group.

We note a higher percentage of accidental GSW and lower percentage of assault than previously reported in the literature [2,4,5,15,18,23]. However, we should point out that the type of GSW (accidental versus assault versus self-inflicted) is often misclassified within medical documentation, and it is possible that many of the sustained GSWs in our study were misclassified as accidental discharge. With this in mind, we still did find a very high rate of accidental injury (56.5%), especially among the younger patients in our study (77.0% for patients 0–12 years old), highlighting a significant opportunity for prevention. Our state has a high rate of gun ownership and lacks safety laws as evidenced by low scores in both the Brady and Gifford Law Center Annual Gun Law Scores [2,23,24]. Access prevention laws requiring safe firearm storage have proven efficacious in decreasing firearm-related deaths among children and could be a particularly effective evidence-based approach in the case of our state [10,24].

Firearm injuries have disproportionately affected the poor and certain ethnic groups, specifically among children [6]. Our dataset aligned consistently with many of the established trends reported in the GSW literature reflected by male predominance (81.7%), with a majority of patients identifying as Hispanic/Latino (63.5%) and a mean age of 14.9 years (range of 16 months–17 years). Non-Hispanic Black or African American are the most common ethnicity in most evaluations of GSW presentation in children, even more pronounced when assault data had been separated [6,13,14,18]. Our regional ethnic proportions are such that African Americans were a much lower number of the total GSW patients (7.0%); however, they were still proportionately and significantly more likely to be affected by gun violence (P ​< ​.001). The significantly higher distribution of Hispanic children in our cohort exceeded their majority status of the state population (63.5% compared to 47.7% state-wide, P ​< ​.001).

Native Americans, who comprise a large portion of the state population (8.9%), were also well represented in our dataset (10.5%) and did not differ significantly from the state population (P ​= ​.383). Based on our findings, it appears that firearm-related injury is not a healthcare disparity that disproportionately affects the pediatric Native American population in our state, although prior studies have found higher rates of firearm-related suicide among this demographic [1]. Given that our data included only patients who survived to reach the hospital, we suspect that suicide by firearm among this demographic may still be under-represented and therefore not entirely reflected in our findings. Due to the scarcity of research on firearm-related injury in the Native American pediatric population, our data may still help elucidate useful patterns within this demographic for future research.

Lack of health insurance is another independent risk factor for sustaining blunt or penetrating trauma and has been associated with worse health outcomes in both adults and children [27,28]. Roughly 4.2% of all children statewide are uninsured [26]. We found no statistically significant difference in uninsured rates among our cohort (P ​= ​.597) as 4.8% of patients sustaining GSWs lacked health insurance. Interestingly, in our dataset, privately insured individuals were significantly under-represented (16.9% versus a state-wide rate of 35.9%, P ​< ​.001), whereas publicly/government insured individuals were heavily over-represented (78.2% versus a statewide rate of 56.5%, P ​< ​.001). Our findings are consistent with those reported by Evans et al. who emphasized a high financial burden to the medical system due to the predominance of public insurance rates among a similar population [18]. Thus, our findings likely reflect the socioeconomic status of the individuals included in our study and similarly demonstrate the high financial burden of gun violence within our state.

Throughout the study period, two separate gun laws were passed in our state: NMSA 30-7-7.1, which became effective on July 1st, 2019, and required background checks on all private firearm sales, and NMSA 40-17-1 to 40-17-13, a red-flag law, which became effective on May 20th, 2020 [30,31]. When our data were analyzed for trends based on quarterly numbers beginning July 1st of 2019, we do not see a significant trend (P ​= ​.460) between Q3 of 2019 and Q4 of 2022. In fact, we even observed a slight downtrend (τ ​= ​−0.157), although not significant. This may be evidence of the efficacy of these laws at stagnating pediatric GSW-related hospitalizations but should be interpreted with caution.

Limitations and future directions

There are limitations to our study that should be highlighted. We believe our single-cohort study from the only level-1 pediatric trauma center in the state is important to establish national trends within our region. However, there are natural limitations when extrapolating data to other regions. A retrospective review also has inherent concerns with documentation and reporting within the medical record. This could be more of a concern in the case of gun injuries when claims of assault bear a significant legal consequence, along with possible retaliation. For this reason, it is a significant limitation to draw any definitive conclusions based on the reported type of GSW injury (eg, assault, accidental discharge, or self-inflicted) in the medical documentation. Additionally, the transition from the ICD-9 to ICD-10 coding systems introduced the potential for inaccuracies or inconsistencies in data interpretation due to coding crossover during a longer study period. Initially, we attempted to include data collected via ICD-9 coding starting in 2012 but encountered several inaccuracies with insufficient data. These inaccuracies improved after limiting to ICD-10 reporting after 2015, so we elected to decrease our review period to avoid this issue. Finally, due to limitations of the United States census data, which is only reported every 10 years, we could not directly compare demographic data of our state for 2016–2022 to the demographic data of the cohort included in our study for the entire study period. We chose to use the demographic data reported in the 2020 United States census as it occurred during the study period but may not be completely representative of the true demographic breakdown of our state's population during the 7-year study period. Additionally, the demographics of our hospital's patient population may not be truly reflective of state-wide demographics.

As we confront the pediatric GSW injury epidemic, important future directions must involve continuous gathering of objective data. Multicenter and prospective studies will improve our ability to present evidence-based solutions to this problem and make better recommendations for treatment. Advocating for our patients’ well-being should be possible while avoiding the political and emotional considerations that seem to have paralyzed efforts to date. This study reinforces the role the pediatric orthopaedic community should take.

Conclusion

Pediatric GSWs continue to represent a significant concern with broad implications for medical care and public health. We present our single center 7-year cohort (2016–2022) of pediatric (<18 years old) GSW injuries: 248 total patients included, 115 (46%) with musculoskeletal injuries meriting an orthopaedic consult, and 107 (42%) total ballistic fractures. The rates of these injuries continued to rise throughout the full study period, indicating that trends persist beyond what is reported in the literature. Orthopaedic surgeons will have a role in recognizing this epidemic, improving treatment protocols and providing guidance for practical improvements in laws to protect children.
