Phenotype-specific muscle proteomic profiling in titinopathies

ABSTRACT
Titinopathies are complex neuromuscular disorders with multiple phenotypes. The gene's size, comprising 364 exons, as well as the protein's size of 3.8 MDa and its extensive network of protein interactors, are key factors underlying this complexity. Various phenotypes characterize titinopathies, and this study focuses on two of them: arthrogryposis and myofibrillar myopathies. The protein deregulations associated with these two phenotypes remain unknown or have been minimally explored; however, understanding these consequences is essential for better characterizing the pathophysiological aspects of these titinopathies.The objective was to analyze protein deregulations in two cohorts of French patients with titinopathies exhibiting the arthrogryposis and myofibrillar myopathy phenotypes, and to compare them with control individuals. Protein extracts were obtained from muscle biopsies of patients, and changes in protein levels within these two groups were analyzed by mass spectrometry. The results indicate specific deregulations in each group. The networks analyzed revealed deregulation of proteins involved in fibrosis mechanisms or in the actomyosin complex for the arthrogryposis phenotype. Regulation of the muscle contraction system through deregulation of proteins involved in the cytoskeleton is impacted in patients with myofibrillar myopathy. The proteins that are quantitatively abnormal in these two groups also provide insights into the major signaling networks disrupted in titinopathies. These findings will contribute to a more precise characterization of titinopathies, enabling the identification of phenotype-specific biomarkers and potentially guiding the search for targeted therapies for these neuromuscular disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02363-2.

Introduction

Titinopathies are myopathies caused by pathogenic variants in titin gene (TTN). Titin is a muscle protein encoded by the TTN gene with 364 exons. This protein is known to be the largest protein of the human body. Its function is to ensure muscle structure and elasticity thanks to its localization in the sarcomere [1]. The protein is divided into four major regions: [2]. The Z-disk is the outer part of the sarcomere that delimits the border between two units. The I-band is the portion allowing the elasticity of the titin [3], the A-band is the titin filament in contact with the myosin, and the M-line is a line in the center of the muscular unit.

Many transcripts and protein isoforms are encoded by the TTN gene due to alternative splicing depending on the muscle tissue [2, 4]. This number of isoforms adds a certain complexity to the study of this major protein.

Few quantitative proteomics data are available in the literature on myopathies, and one recent study has shown global protein deregulation in patients with titinopathies [5]. This study investigated the differences between desminopathy and titinopathy in myofibrillar myopathy, identifying 113 differentially abundant proteins. Key distinctions include differential regulation of glycolysis and extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, which were mostly downregulated.

The particularity of the arthrogryposis due to TTN variants is that at least one variant is in an exon of the metatranscript (i.e. an exon that is not included in one of the canonical post-natal isoforms of the TTN gene [6]). Myofibrillar myopathies, a group of genetic disorders that generally start in adulthood and are characterized by disorganization of myofibrils and an accumulation of proteins in muscle cells and are due to several genetic diseases. Myofibrillar myopathies are often associated with cardiac disorders [7]. Mutations in the TTN gene are likely to give different types of phenotypes, so understanding how the same gene can give such different phenotypes is essential.

The aim of this study was to search for specific protein deregulations on these two distinct titinopathy phenotypes (congenital arthrogryposis and myofibrillar myopathy), to identify new biomarkers that would help diagnosing titinopathies and advance our understanding of their pathophysiological mechanisms.

Methods

Patients

Six patients with arthrogryposis and seven with myofibrillar myopathy due to TTN variants were included in the cohort. Patients were selected based on clinical presentation consistent with arthrogryposis or myofibrillar myopathy (MFM), pathological and radiological findings indicative of a titinopathy, genetic findings implicating TTN variants, and the availability of muscle biopsy for transcript and proteomic analyses. Clinical, pathological and muscle imaging features of patients were reported by physicians of the national titin consortium from Filnemus (The French Health Care Network for rare neuromuscular diseases). Clinicobiological details are presented in supplemental Table 1. Our last publication reported 20 patients with autosomal recessive arthrogryposis, a condition characterized by multiple joint contractures (more than two affected joint levels) [6]. Five patients from the six patients with arthrogryposis reported in the present study were included in the previous one (Table 1). Patients biopsies were obtained from muscles with localization specified in Table 1. Five biopsies from adult patients without neuromuscular pathologies were used as controls. They were collected from quadriceps of two men aged 73 and 69 and a woman aged 77, and from tensor fascia lata of two men aged 52. Patients or parents gave informed consent for the genetic analysis according to French legislation (Comité de Protection des Personnes OUEST 6-CPP1128HPS3 IDRCB-2018-AO2287-48). Mass spectrometry protein analysis

Protein solubilization from muscle biopsies was prepared as previously described [8]. Sample preparations were processed by tryptic digestion of the samples using S-Trap Mini Spin columns (Protifi). Samples were loaded onto a 25 cm reversed-phase column (75 mm inner diameter; Acclaim PepMap 100 C18; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated with an UltiMate 3000 RSLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a QExactive HFX system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Tandem mass spectrometry analyses were performed in a data dependent mode. Full scans (375–1500 m/z) were acquired in the Orbitrap mass analyzer with a resolution of 60,000 at 200 m/z. For the full scans, 3 × 106 ions were accumulated within a maximum injection time of 60 ms. The 12 most intense ions with charge states ≥ 2 were sequentially isolated (1 × 105) with a maximum injection time of 45 ms and fragmented by higher-energy collisional dissociation in the collision cell (normalized collision energy of 28%) and detected in the Orbitrap analyzer at a resolution of 30,000.

Proteomics data treatment and statistical analysis

Raw spectra were processed with MaxQuant (v2030 [9]; using standard parameters with label-free quantification (LFQ), iBAQ and match between runs options using Andromeda [10]. A representative protein ID in each protein group was automatically selected using an in-house developed bioinformatics tool (Leading tool v 3.5) as the best annotated protein in UniProtKB (reviewed entries rather than automatic ones) (release 2024_04; http://www.uniprot.org) of Homo sapiens (proteome ID UP000005640) and 250 frequently observed contaminants, as well as reversed sequences of all entries. A representative protein ID in each protein group was automatically selected using an in-house developed bioinformatics tool (Leading tool v 3.5) as the best annotated protein in UniProtKB (reviewed entries rather than automatic ones) [11].

Perseus software (v1.6.15.0) was utilized to process the reported protein group intensities and find differentially expressed proteins between each phenotype group and the negative control group. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [12] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD057482 and https://doi.org/10.6019/PXD057482.

Volcano plots were extract from Perseus software (v1.6.15.0). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using STRING (https://string-db.org/), the network type is full STRING network, the edges indicate both functional and physical protein associations. Disorders over-representation analysis using quantitatively abnormal proteins identified by proteomics data was performed with Metascape (Version 3.5.20240101).

Immunoblotting

After SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, muscle extracted proteins were transferred on nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham), membranes were blocked with Intercept PBS blocking buffer (LI-COR Biosciences) for 1 h at room temperature. Then, membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibody Rabbit anti-XIRP2 (Proteintech, ref 11,896–1-AP) or Rabbit anti-Nestin (Invitrogen, ref MA5-32,272), rabbit anti-GAPDH (abcam, ab9485), rabbit anti-MYH2 (ABCD antibodies company, ABCD-AW216 in blocking buffer. After washes in PBS/Tween 0,1% under gentle agitation, membranes were incubated for 45 min in the dark with the fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies (700 or 800 nm) and finally quantified using Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences).

Immunohistochemistry

Frozen 20 µm-transverse sections of vastus lateralis biopsies were stained using a Mouse anti-myosin heavy chain 1 MyHC-I, anti-MYH7 antibody (Sigma‐Aldrich, ref M8421, clone NOQ7.5.4D), Rabbit anti-Titin-MIR (Myomedix, ref 5879), Rabbit anti-MYBPC2 (Sigma‐Aldrich, SAB108180), Rabbit anti-XIRP2 (proteintech, ref 11,896–1-AP). Images were captured on a confocal Zeiss LSM800 microscope (Carl Zeiss).

To determine the proportion of each fibre type (myosin heavy chain 1 (MHC1) and non-MHC1), the images were analyzed in a blinded fashion using Fiji software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) [13], [14].

Results

Phenotypes and Genetic characteristics of the cohorts

Clinical, paraclinical, familial and genetic data were collected for patients in the two phenotypic subgroups, six patients with arthrogryposis (T6, T48, T27, T36, T21, T11) five of them were reported in Perrin et al. [6] and seven previously unreported patients with myofibrillar myopathy (T5, T7, T13, T17, T19, T23, T31) (Table 1). T19 suffers from cardiomyopathy, so the hypothesis of autosomal dominant transmission with skeletal muscle involvement cannot be ruled out, in line with recent literature [15]. Genetic details are presented in Table 1.

Immunohistochemical analysis of patient T11 and T36 biopsy sections revealed a significant reduction in the number of type II fibers in arthrogryposis sub-cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Validation of quantitative protein differences

We validated highly quantitative protein differences with immunostaining experiments on muscle biopsies. The decreases in the abundance of MYH2 (MHCII) or MYBPC2 proteins in patients with arthrogryposis was confirmed in patients T36, T21 and T27 (Fig. 2). The validation of protein NES and XIRP2 over-abundance in myofibrillar myopathy was performed using western blot in patient biopsies (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 3). XIRP2 labelling shows characteristic images of the increase in abundance of this protein with the formation of typical XIRP2-positive aggregates (Fig. 3B).

Proteins networks

We then used UniProt data to determine the functions and biological processes associated with quantitatively abnormal proteins. Among the proteins over-represented in the arthrogryposis group, several are involved in extracellular matrix organization (e.g., SERPINH1, FBLN5, and COL14A1), while GNB1 is implicated in GTPase activity (Supplementary Table 4). In contrast, under-represented proteins in arthrogryposis are involved in muscle contraction (MYBPC2) and in metabolic processes (GPD2, ALDH1L1, and CMBL) (Supplementary Table 4).

Gene ontology and STRING analyses confirmed that over-represented proteins in arthrogryposis are primarily associated with extracellular matrix organization, whereas under-represented proteins are linked to metabolism, particularly glycogen and carbohydrate metabolic pathways. MYBPC2 is a notable exception, as it is directly related to muscle contraction and structural integrity (Supplementary Table 4). Analysis carried out using metascape confirms these observations: the known pathologies associated with deregulation of these proteins are metabolic pathologies (“glycogen storage disease”) and neuromuscular pathologies (“myopathy, contracture and muscle weakness”). These pathology/ quantitatively abnormal protein associations provide clues to understanding genotype/phenotype mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In patients with myofibrillar myopathy (MFM), the proteomic profile differed: all quantitatively abnormal proteins were overrepresented (Fig. 1). These proteins were predominantly linked to structural functions, especially cytoskeletal organization (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table 5). Quantitatively abnormal proteins highlight distinct biological processes: metabolic pathways are more prominent in arthrogryposis (Fig. 4A), whereas structural pathways dominate in MFM (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table 4). Structural network alterations in arthrogryposis were driven by over-abundance of various collagen family members (COL6A1, COL6A2, COL6A3, COL14A1, COL1A1, COL1A2) and under-abundance of MYBPC2, MYH1, and MYH2 (Supplementary Table 2).

In MFM, over-represented proteins disrupted cytoskeletal structural networks. Gene ontology analysis revealed enrichment in pathways such as muscle contraction, cytoskeletal organization, actin filament-based processes, and cytoskeletal protein binding (Supplementary Table 3). STRING analysis with integration of titn in network processing also shows for the MFM phenotype that titin creates a node at the center of independent quantitatively abnormal protein networks (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

In our study, we used a proteomic approach to explain how dysfunction of the same gene, TTN, can lead to very different muscular phenotypes, arthrogryposis and MFM. The correlation between the location of variants in the gene and a phenotype of titinopathies is still an important issue. In past studies, the arthrogryposis phenotype correlated with the location of truncating variants in metatranscript exons localized in PEVK domain. Patients T6, T11, T21, T27, T36 were previously published, and phenotype description and variant characterization were detailed [6]. For the second phenotype of myofibrillar myopathy, there was no established correlation in our sub-cohort between the location of the variants and the deregulations observed. Regulation by nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), which enables degradation of truncating variants, is not well understood in the field of titinopathies. It is possible that NMD is not total in titinopathies [16], which makes it even more difficult to correlate variant localization with observed phenotypes.

Strikingly, we observed that the sets of quantitatively abnormal proteins had very little overlap between the two phenotype groups. Thus, proteomic changes caused by pathogenic variant in the TTN gene are specific to each phenotype and not universal to all titinopathies Interestingly, protein network analyses shows that titin is at the center of these interactions. These results represent a preliminary work in the search for specific biomarkers of arthrogryposis-type phenotype or myofibrillar titinopathies. But it is essential to identify the affected networks in these patients to enable a more detailed investigation of these neuromuscular disorders.

Quantitatively abnormal proteins form a complex network regulating muscle structure and contraction, as well as the metabolic processes and cellular signals. Several of these proteins are involved in muscle structure and function: Collagens (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL6A1, COL6A2, COL6A3, COL14A1), associated with regulators such as DCN [17], OGN [18], and FBLN5 [19], create a solid base for the extracellular matrix that surrounds and stabilizes muscle cells. Contractile proteins such as MYH1, MYH2, TPM4 and CASQ1 are directly involved in muscle movement and deregulation due to pathogenic variants in the corresponding genes are known to cause myopathies [20].

Other proteins regulate energy metabolism: glycolytic enzymes (GAPDH, PGK1, PFKM, LDHA) and glycogen metabolism enzymes (PYGM, AGL, PHKA1, PHKB) are crucial for supplying the energy required for muscle contraction [21]. FABP5 is involved in the use of fatty acids as an energy source and in cell signaling and regulation [22].

The collagen family is well known to be responsible for myopathies with joint contractures due to mutations in the COL6A1, COL6A2 and COL6A3 genes (type VI collagen) associated with congenital myopathies [23]. These diseases affect the extracellular matrix of skeletal muscle, leading to a loss of muscle function. The large family of myosin with MYH1, MYH2, MYBPC2 quantitatively abnormal in patients with arthrogryposis is known to be associated, when mutated, with myopathies and particularly with arthrogryposis for MYBPC2 [24, 25]. Pathogenic variants in the MYH2 genes and others myosin genes can cause various forms of myopathies, such as distal myopathy [26] characterized by progressive muscle weakness of the distal muscles. Deregulation of the FBLN5 (Fibulin 5) protein is of interest given its function and involvement in skin elasticity pathologies [27], and in Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease with joint contracture [19] mainly affecting connective tissues, but which may also have implications for muscle function. Analyses of the Arthrogryposis group highlighted the under expression of the MYBPC2 protein. It is interesting to note that mutations in MYBPC2 cause an arthrogryposis phenotype. This protein is a “myosin binding protein C. A study has demonstrated the impact of a knock-out of the MYBPC2 gene in mice, by identifying the protein's role in strengthening the interaction between actin and myosin [25]. Moreover, Song et al. [25], confirmed that MYBPC2 facilitates the interaction between myosin and actin, influencing muscle contraction strength and speed.

The quantitatively abnormal protein network in myofibrillar myopathies muscles interestingly shows only protein over-representation. These over-abundance proteins have biological functions and interactions linked to different major signaling pathways. Proteins such as XIRP2, NES, MAP4, DCTN2 and SYNC are all involved in the dynamics of the cytoskeleton (actin, microtubules, intermediate filaments) [28–31]. CAMK2D and PTPN11 are both involved in the regulation of intracellular signaling pathways. CAMK2D regulates calcium-dependent processes, and this protein is known to be overexpressed in a mouse model of titinopathy, in which the M band was deleted or conditional KO mouse [32]. XIRP2 and NRAP are found in myocytes intracytoplasmic aggregates reported in desminopathies [33]. Also, in this study from Maerkens et al. [33], HSPB1 was found in aggregates and HSPB2, which is upregulated in MFM patients in our study. Another study by Maerkens et al. [34] using a quantitative proteomics approach based on laser microdissection of aggregates in myotilinopathy, showed the over-expression of 72 proteins. As in our study, it showed overexpression of the proteins XIRP2, NES, NRAP, MAP4, and proteins in the same families as HSPB2, HSPB1 and B8. In total, when comparing the proteins present in the aggregates in the study by Merkens et al. [34] and those identified in our study, seven proteins were found in both studies: XIRP2, NRAP, MAP4, SYNC, MYH14, LNPEP and NES. It would be interesting to perform, as in this study by Merkens, a protein extraction from the XIRP2-positive aggregates of our patients and to investigate the specificities of these aggregates in patients with titinopathy with a myofibrillar myopathy phenotype.

Three proteins were found in greater abundance in both phenotypic subgroups (XIRP2, HUWE1, ATP1B4). XIRP2 is a protein belonging to the Xin protein family specific to striated muscles, found at the myotendinous junction in skeletal muscles. In a study, Xin was analyzed by immunofluorescent staining in skeletal muscle samples from 47 subjects with various forms of myopathy, including muscular dystrophies, inflammatory myopathies, mitochondrial/metabolic myopathies, and endocrine myopathies. The Xin protein is found only in myopathic or damaged muscles, while XIRP2 is present in control muscles but its distribution is remodeled in the form of accumulation in areas of damage muscles [29]. HUWE1 encodes the Huwe1 protein that is an E3 ligase targeting the TBP protein for K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasome-mediated degradation. This protein in skeletal muscle is involved in the processes of differentiation and repair of damaged or overexpressed in muscles in response to muscle stress. Huwe1 is required for muscle differentiation and maintenance of normal muscle morphologies [35]. XIRP2 and HUWE1 appear to be involved in muscle stress response processes, which could explain their overexpression in the myopathic processes described in this study. The ATP1B4 gene encodes the BetaM protein, a unique member of the Na,K-ATPase β-subunit family. BetaM is mainly expressed in skeletal and cardiac muscle tissue [36]. The literature does not allow us to understand the link between overexpression of this protein and the phenotypes described in this study. However, the functional importance of Na,K-ATPase subunits in cardiac muscle could contribute to the cardiac phenotype observed in patients with myofibrillar myopathy [37].

All these deregulations appear to be phenotype-specific, so despite small patient samples, the deregulations appear to be consistent and significant. A limitation of this study is the absence of age-matched healthy control muscle samples. Because skeletal muscle biopsy is an invasive procedure, the collection of samples from healthy individuals—particularly children—is associated with important ethical and regulatory constraints. Consequently, obtaining age-matched control muscle samples was not feasible during the initial phase of this work. Although the available control samples were not age-matched, they nevertheless provided a preliminary reference framework for proteomic comparison. Importantly, the main conclusions of this study rely primarily on comparisons between patient groups with distinct clinical phenotypes rather than solely on comparisons with control samples. In this context, the proteomic differences observed between patients presenting with arthrogryposis and those with myofibrillar myopathy are unlikely to be explained by age differences alone. Despite these limitations, our findings provide novel insights into the molecular heterogeneity associated with these phenotypic presentations and, to our knowledge, represent the first report of such proteomic differences in titinopathies. Further studies including larger cohorts and improved age matching will be required to confirm and extend these observations.

It is difficult to determine the mechanisms responsible for the phenotypes observed, as certain of these protein deregulations may be compensatory mechanisms while others are direct causes of the pathology. More homogeneous models such as cell models derived from patients and animal models will be interesting to analyse these protein deregulations and understand the origin of the pathology. The analysis of these networks and the knowledge acquired about the set of quantitatively abnormal proteins could enable the development of targeted therapeutics by targeting abnormal signaling pathways responsible for the observed phenotype. However, this will require a clear understanding and specific, in-depth studies to understand the pathophysiological mechanisms of these titinopathies.

Conclusions

Our study shows for the first time specific protein deregulations in two types of titinopathy. More studies using similar proteomic approaches are needed to explore other known titinopathy phenotypes such as distal myopathies, which are well characterized from a genetic aspect [38].
