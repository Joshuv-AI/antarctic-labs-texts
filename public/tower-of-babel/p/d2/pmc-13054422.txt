Suture tension band fixation of olecranon fractures: description and early outcomes of a novel technique
Authors: Joseph G Monir, Frank L Vazquez, Musab Gulzar, Kevin Cuneo, Thomas McQuillan, Michael B Gottschalk, Eric R Wagner
Published: 2026

Olecranon fractures account for 10% of upper extremity fractures, with an incidence of 30 per 100,000 people.13,27 These injuries most commonly occur through the articular surface and are associated with unopposed pull from the triceps on the proximal fragment. Surgical fixation is frequently required for displaced fractures to restore ulnohumeral congruity, triceps function, and elbow stability.7,22

Numerous techniques for open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF) of olecranon fractures have been described, including tension banding, plating, and intramedullary nail fixation.7,21 Historically, tension band constructs with surgical steel wire have demonstrated good results and low cost for simple fracture patterns (Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopedic Trauma Association type 2U1B1, Mayo type IIa).4,20 However, these wire tension band (WTB) constructs have a high rate of removal due to hardware irritation, with studies reporting hardware removal rates as high as 30%-50%.8,17,23 Precontoured olecranon locking plates (LPs) were subsequently developed to provide more rigid fixation.8 These plates have similarly good clinical outcomes and can be used in more versatile settings, but they are also plagued by high cost and high rates of hardware removal, reported to be around 38%.15

All-suture olecranon tension band constructs have shown promising biomechanical results, though there is a paucity of clinical outcomes available.9 In addition, Kirschner wires with eyelets have been used with WTB,14 but to our knowledge, no technique has been described utilizing a suture tension band (STB) with eyelet wires. The authors describe a technique and early outcomes of fixation of simple olecranon fractures using a STB with eyelet wires. The authors hypothesize that this technique will have satisfactory outcomes in patients when assessing pain, functional outcomes, and radiographic union.

Methods

A search was conducted, following approval of the institutional review board (IRB ID: STUDY00007026), for patients who underwent an olecranon ORIF with the STB technique at our institution from July 2017 to March 2024. Only patients with isolated olecranon fractures were included in our analysis. In addition, patients with prior surgical history in the injured elbow were excluded. Patients with less than 6 months of follow-up were excluded.

Data collection and statistics

Chart review was performed retrospectively for patients who met inclusion criteria. Information pertaining to patient demographics, intraoperative, and post-operative data were collected. Patient age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, and diabetes diagnosis were recorded for demographic data. Intraoperative data included surgical time and any reoperations. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), such as Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand, Mayo Elbow Performance Score, elbow subjective value (ESV), and visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores were recorded for pre-operative and post-operative outcomes analysis. Range of motion (ROM) measures of the affected and unaffected sides were recorded in the electronic health record by the patient's surgeon or physician assistant during post-operative visits. Measurements included elbow flexion, extension, pronation, and supination. Radiographic images obtained at a minimum of 8 weeks post-operatively were reviewed by the senior author, a fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon. Fracture union was determined based on radiographic evidence of cortical continuity across the fracture site. Patients who did not meet the minimum follow-up criteria were contacted directly to obtain PROM data. Pre-operative and post-operative PROMs, as well as affected and unaffected ROM, were compared using a related samples Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A P value ≤.05 was deemed to be statistically significant. All statistical calculations were conducted using SPSS (version 29.0.0.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Surgical technique

This technique is indicated for Mayo type IIa olecranon fractures (Fig. 1). The patient is positioned in the lateral decubitus position with the operative side up. The operative arm is draped over a radiolucent paint roller. A sterile tourniquet is applied.

Radiograph of a 71-year-old female with a displaced Mayo type IIa olecranon fracture.

An incision approximately 15 cm in length is made in a standard posterior approach, starting about 5 cm distal to the tip of the olecranon and curving radially around the tip of the olecranon. Medial and lateral subcutaneous flaps are elevated to facilitate layered closure. A standard subcutaneous approach to the ulna is made, elevating the extensor carpi ulnaris and flexor carpi ulnaris off the shaft of the ulna. The fracture site is exposed and débrided of interposed hematoma and soft tissue. Irrigation and metal-tip suction are particularly useful for clearing debris. The triceps tendon is then fully exposed and separated from the overlying fascia. An exposure of approximately 8-10 cm of distal triceps tendon is ideal.

A #2 FiberWire suture is used in a locking Krakow configuration in the triceps tendon, leaving 2 free tails distally at the triceps insertion on the olecranon (Fig. 2). Next a 2.0 mm drill is used to drill 2 holes from ulnar to radial in the ulnar shaft. The first is approximately 3 cm distal to the fracture and the second is about 5 cm distal, though the exact positioning of these holes is not of critical importance. Next a large, pointed reduction clamp is used, with one tine placed in one of these holes and the other placed on the tip of the olecranon. This is used to manually reduce the fracture. The reduction is then checked on multiplanar fluoroscopy, taking care to obtain a perfect lateral radiograph with overlapping distal humeral condyles.

Right arm demonstrating the (a) posterior approach to the olecranon, exposing approximately 8-10 cm of triceps tendon and proximal ulna. (b) A #2 FiberWire is used to Krakow the triceps tendon.

Next the eyelet wire is passed antegrade from the tip of the olecranon on either the radial or ulnar side, aimed down the canal of the ulna. The second eyelet wire is passed on the opposite side in the same fashion (Fig. 3). The placement of these wires and fracture reduction is then checked on multiplanar fluoroscopy.

The fracture is reduced with a large pointed reduction clamp, and both eyelet wires are advanced across the fracture site under the guidance of multiplanar fluoroscopy.

The ulnar limb of the #2 FiberWire that was Krakowed in the triceps tendon is then passed from ulnar to radial through the proximal drill hole (Fig. 4). Next, one limb of a #5 FiberWire suture without a needle is passed from ulnar to radial through both of the eyelet pins using a suture passer. The second limb of the same #5 FiberWire suture is passed again in the same direction through both eyelet pins. This leaves a loop on the ulnar side and 2 free tails on the radial side. The 2 tails must be passed individually because the size of the eyelets precludes passing both limbs simultaneously. Next, the looped end is passed from ulnar to radial through the more distal drill hole that was previously drilled. This orientation is important because it results in all knots being on the radial side of the ulna, which we believe minimizes the risk of ulnar nerve irritation (Fig. 5).

The ulnar tail of the #2 FiberWire suture is passed from ulnar to radial through the more proximal drill hole in the ulna. The suture passer is passed from radial to ulnar through the eyelets. This will be used to pass the #5 FiberWire, leaving the tails on the radial side of the ulna.

Right arm demonstrating the final position of all suture limbs before tying. All suture ends are on the radial side, to avoid ulnar-sided knots, which may lead to ulnar nerve irritation.

Tension is pulled on the #5 FiberWire suture, and the portion of the triceps tendon between the 2 eyelet pins is incised to allow the sutures to sit flush to bone. The eyelet pins are then tamped down and the ends broken off so that they are no longer prominent above the triceps tendon. The #5 FiberWire sutures are pulled taut by an assistant to reduce the fracture while the surgeon ties the #2 FiberWire suture securely. The #5 FiberWire suture is then tied using a modified Nice knot, which is the authors' preference (Fig. 6). The eyelet pins should be flush or below the level of the triceps to minimize potential for hardware irritation (Fig. 7).

(a) Final construct after all sutures have been tied. (b) The elbow is taken through range of motion to ensure no gapping is present at the fracture site.

Final construct showing no prominence of the pin heads.

The elbow joint is then taken through full ROM to ensure no gapping of the fracture site, and final multiplanar fluoroscopic images are taken (Fig. 8). The fascia overlying the extensor carpi ulnaris and flexor carpi ulnaris is closed to bury the knots so that the knots are not prominent on the ulnar border. The small transverse incision in the triceps tendon between the eyelet pins is closed with absorbable suture. This acts to contain the suture bridge between the eyelet wires, preventing the wires from backing out. The wound is then closed per the surgeon's preference. The authors' prefer absorbable suture and skin glue (Video 1).

Post-operative radiographs of the final construct demonstrating appropriate reduction. Final radiographs, including AP (a) and lateral (b), approximately 14 weeks after surgery showing healed fracture with the eyelet wire STC technique.

The patient is placed in a splint in elbow flexion. They remain in the splint for 2 weeks to allow for healing of the posterior elbow wound. At 2 weeks, they are allowed to begin ROM without weightbearing. At 8 weeks post-operatively, the patient begins gentle weightbearing. Patients may return to unrestricted activity at 3 months, once radiographs demonstrate healed fracture (Fig. 9).

Final radiographs approximately 14 weeks after surgery showing healed fracture with the eyelet wire STB technique. Postoperative radiographs including AP (a) and lateral (b) of the final construct demonstrating appropriate reduction. STB, suture tension band.

Pearls and pitfalls

While this technique is not overly technically demanding, certain technical factors should be considered to improve the efficiency and outcome of surgery. When preparing the bed, it is the authors' preference to always place the paint roller at or slightly below the level of the shoulder, as this facilitates obtaining radiographs. If the paint roller is above the level of the shoulder, adducting the arm toward midline, if needed to get a lateral radiograph, becomes more difficult.

Care should be taken when manipulating the fracture, as the dorsal cortex of the olecranon can be fragile. Iatrogenic fracture of the dorsal cortex may preclude use of this technique and require conversion to LP fixation. All tension band constructs, including this STB technique, require intact cortices for force transmission. If unexpected comminution is encountered, LP fixation should be utilized instead. In addition to fracture morphology, intraoperative assessment of anteroposterior ulnohumeral stability should be performed following provisional fixation; the presence of instability is an indication for conversion to LP fixation. Surgeons should always discuss the possibility of LP fixation with patients, and these plates should be immediately available.

Results

Demographics and surgical factors

A total of 17 patients were initially identified. Seven patients with less than 6 months of follow-up were contacted to obtain additional outcome data; 1 did not respond, resulting in 16 patients available for final analysis. There were no complications or reoperations. Follow-up for both PROMs and ROM ranged from 6.0 months to 35.4 months. Demographic data on the patient population are available in Table I.

Outcomes and complications

At mean follow up of 15.5 ± 9.4 months, the median post-operative Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand score was 6.8, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 0-25. Median post-operative Mayo Elbow Score was 85 (IQR: 85-100). Median VAS improved from 7 (IQR: 5-9) pre-operatively to 0 (IQR: 0-2) post-operatively (P < .001). The median post-operative ESV improved from 0 (IQR: 0-0) pre-operatively to 90 (IQR: 70-99) post-operatively (P < .001).

Range of motion

The median post-operative total arc of ROM of the affected side for flexion was 140° (136.25˚-147.5°), while the unaffected side was 150° (IQR: 150˚-150°) (P = .006). The median post-operative extension of the affected side was 0° (IQR: 0˚- 13.7°), while the unaffected side was 0° (IQR: 0˚-0°) (P = .042). The median post-operative pronation of the affected side was 87.5° (IQR: 80˚-90°, while the unaffected side was 90° (IQR: 90˚-90°) (P = .027). The median post-operative supination of the affected side was 82.5° (IQR: 70˚-90°), while the unaffected side was 90° (IQR: 90˚-90°) (P = .020). Average follow up time for patients with reported ROM measurements was 15.5 ± 9.3 months.

Radiographic outcomes

Sixteen of 16 patients (100%) achieved fracture union, with maintenance of reduction and no nonunions or malunions. There were no periprosthetic fractures or signs of hardware migration on the most recent follow up radiograph.

Discussion

Mayo type IIa olecranon fractures can be managed using several techniques, including WTB, LP, and STB fixation. WTB constructs were widely used but often lead to hardware irritation and high reoperation rates.23 LP fixation provides rigid stability but at higher cost and with similarly significant hardware-related complications and reoperations.1,20,22 More recently, suture-based constructs have gained interest for providing rigid fixation while reducing these implant-related complications.18 Our study adds to this growing body of work by describing a novel STB technique utilizing eyelet Kirschner wires. Our study demonstrates that the described eyelet STB technique yields satisfactory early clinical outcomes, with improvements in PROMs and no reported complications or reoperations.

This STB technique leads to satisfactory early clinical outcomes in this series, with all patients with reported pre-operative and post-operative VAS and ESV demonstrating improvement in their PROMs. While improvement is expected following acute injury, our results are consistent with outcomes reported in a randomized controlled trial comparing tension band wiring and plate fixation, which demonstrated comparable functional scores and ROM between constructs despite differing complication profiles.8 In the randomized study by Duckworth et al,8 mean Mayo Elbow Scores at 52 weeks in the WTB group was 90, and mean elbow flexion arcs were 137°. In comparison, our cohort at a mean of 62 weeks demonstrated a median post-operative Mayo Elbow Score of 85 (IQR: 85-100), with a median flexion arc of 140° and full extension in most patients. While direct comparison is limited by study design, these findings suggest that functional recovery achieved with our hybrid eyelet K-wire STB construct is consistent with outcomes reported in prior randomized trials. Vasen et al25 reported that a flexion–extension arc of 120°-75° is sufficient for activities of daily living, well within the median flexion–extension arc observed in our patients (140°-0°). Similarly, Gates et al10 identified peak pronation and supination requirements for activities of daily living at 13° and 53°, respectively, both of which are lower than our reported medians of 87.5° and 82.5°, respectively.

LP fixation is associated with notable rates of hardware irritation and reoperation, reported between 22% and 42% in the literature.3,15,24 In contrast, no patients in our cohort required hardware removal, as determined through routine postoperative follow-up visits with the treating surgeon and confirmed by electronic medical record review for any reoperations related to symptomatic implants. In addition to a favorable complication profile, this novel eyelet STB technique may also reduce operative time. Kang et al12 found average operating time for WTBs to be 94 minutes, compared to mean 68 minutes using this novel STB technique. Although WTB constructs are more commonly associated with minor complications such as hardware irritation, LP fixation carries a higher risk of more severe complications, including deep infection.2,7 Notably, no patients in our cohort experienced postoperative complications.

WTB constructs carry a significant burden of reoperations, with Romero et al23 reporting a 71.7% reoperation rate, predominantly due to hardware irritation. Although other series have reported lower rates, the incidence remains substantial, with a systematic review and meta-analysis showing the rate of complication to be at 44.5%.11 Compared to WTB, our eyelet STB construct avoids metal hardware prominence while providing stable bony fixation. As a result, our cohort experienced no reoperations.

Other suture-based tension band constructs have been reported, including the technique described by Phadnis et al,18 which involves an all-suture construct passed through ulnar bone tunnels. They showed in a separate analysis that their technique has similarly good clinical outcomes compared to LP and WTB fixation, but with lower rates of hardware removal.19 Our technique builds on this approach by incorporating eyelet pins for both bony and soft-tissue fixation, along with a locking Krakow suture to enhance triceps tendon fixation. Dogramatzis et al6 performed a systematic review of the literature comparing clinical outcomes between all-suture or suture anchor STB and WTB. They reported that STB techniques had lower rates of fracture or implant displacement, implant irritation, reoperation, and overall complications. Patients had similar final flexion and extension arcs of motion.

Although eyelet K-wires and suture-based tension band techniques have each been described independently, their combination has not previously been reported.14 Unlike purely all-suture constructs, the addition of eyelet Kirschner wires in our technique preserves the biomechanical advantages of traditional bony fixation while eliminating the symptomatic stainless-steel wire used in conventional tension band constructs.19,26 The eyelet pins provide controlled intramedullary anchorage and facilitate creation of a true tension band across the fracture site, converting triceps tensile forces into articular compression.14 We believe this hybrid design improves reproducibility by standardizing implant positioning and simplifying suture passage, resulting in a technically easier operation with more consistent construct assembly. In our experience, the eyelet wires allow surgeons to achieve reliable reduction and fixation while avoiding the soft tissue irritation commonly associated with metal wire prominence.

The STB construct is grounded in the same tension band principles as traditional WTB techniques, converting tensile triceps forces into compression at the articular surface.26 However, unlike stainless steel wire, high-strength suture tape provides broader force distribution, reduced elongation, and greater resistance to cyclic loading.9 A recent biomechanical study demonstrated that STB fixation exhibits significantly less displacement and higher load to failure compared to conventional WTBs, indicating superior construct stability.9 By eliminating prominent metal hardware, STB also minimizes risks of soft tissue irritation, hardware prominence, wire migration, and reoperation, offering a mechanically robust and biologically favorable alternative for olecranon fractures.

Achieving good outcomes with this technique relies on appropriate patient selection. The force coupling inherent to all tension band constructs requires intact cortices, so this technique would not be appropriate for comminuted fractures (Mayo type IIb). In addition, ulnohumeral instability (Mayo type III olecranon fractures) is a contraindication to this technique, and the authors use LP fixation for those cases.

Potential complications of longitudinal K-wire constructs include wire migration, cortical breach, and neurovascular injury.19 In our technique, the eyelet design provides a mechanical stop that mitigates proximal migration.16 Although theoretical failure of the eyelet remains possible, Lee et al16 identified wire loosening or breakage as the most common modes of construct failure in traditional wire tension band fixation. In addition, Ernstbrunner et al9 demonstrated comparable strength between suture tape and stainless-steel wire, suggesting that similar failure mechanisms would be expected in our construct. Unlike techniques that advance K-wires through the anterior cortex, our method avoids anterior cortical penetration, thereby minimizing the risk of injury to adjacent neurovascular structures and reducing the potential for radioulnar interference or loss of forearm pronosupination.5 As with all tension band constructs, careful intraoperative wire placement and confirmation of stability through elbow ROM are essential to optimize outcomes and reduce complications.

This study has several limitations. While early outcomes of this technique are promising, long-term studies are needed to fully understand outcomes and complication rates of this technique. This technique appears to produce satisfactory clinical results, but future biomechanical studies are warranted to compare fixation strength to established techniques. This study was retrospective at a single institution, and future prospective randomized trials could better compare these techniques of olecranon fixation. Formal cost analysis, including financial implications of reduced surgery time and reduced reoperation, can be performed to more accurately understand the overall cost-effectiveness of this technique.

Conclusion

This novel technique of STB fixation using eyelet Kirschner wires is a reliable option for ORIF of Mayo type IIa olecranon fractures, leading to satisfactory clinical and radiographic outcomes. Compared to traditional methods such as WTB or LP fixation, this STB technique is expected to have lower rates of hardware irritation or removal. Future comparison trials are necessary to help elucidate the optimal algorithmic approach for olecranon fractures.
