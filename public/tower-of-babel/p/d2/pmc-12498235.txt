Human body modifications and transformations in remaining human and the future of nursing practice
Authors: Joseph Andrew Pepito, Freslyn Lim-Saco, Rainier Moreno-Lacalle, Phanida Juntasopeepun, Rose Constantino, Reynaldo Rivera, Rozzano C. Locsin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
People strive to remain ‘human,’ even when facing biological traits they see as ‘non-characteristic.’ Oftentimes, this fundamental drive leads individuals to use technology for self-change and improvement, aiming to realize their authentic sense of self. In this paper, we undertake a discursive, concept-driven inquiry to explore how the pursuit of completeness through body modification influences what it means to ‘remain human’ and how this, in turn, shapes the future of nursing. Drawing from a posthumanist framework that integrates the philosophies of Braidotti, Deleuze, and Heidegger with the nursing theories of Parse, Locsin, and Boykin & Schoenhofer, we argue that this technological transformation is a profound process of ‘becoming.’ Our analysis, informed by a purposive review of relevant literature, suggests that in a technology-rich world, nursing faces new opportunities that demand refined practices. To remain relevant, the nursing profession must adapt to these ongoing human transformations by expanding its understanding of personhood and human care.

Introduction

“Transhumanism has always shaped human society and culture, but today the convergence of nanotechnology, biotechnology, robotics, informatics and communication technology, and applied cognitive science poses a new situation: humans are not only shaping the external environment to improve the conditions of human life, but they seek to change human biological existence itself” (Tirosh Samuelson, 2012, p. 711)

Not everyone has the same level of freedom to change, even though change is inevitable. It is not always something humans can control. Some human beings may have more freedom than others due to their place of birth, social structure, laws, or even personal circumstances (Merzlyakov, 2022). The concept of freedom takes center stage when examining human body modifications and transformations, especially when viewed through the lens of transhumanism and posthumanism. Transhumanism uses technology to enhance human capabilities and overcome limitations when plagued with disease conditions, and oftentimes when growing older. Posthumanism, on the other hand, challenges the traditional understanding of what it means to be human and explores how technology can reshape our identities and experiences (Jepsen, 2017). Still, the self-determination to modify and transform oneself through technology is not equally accessible to all, raising ethical concerns about equity and potential societal implications. Freedom can be in the relationship between nature and culture, and humans and machines.

Deleuze (1994) explains this freedom as foundational to the primary “form of existence” that makes up the revolutionary transformed human, becoming committed to being human. Nowadays, human beings live in a world where advanced technologies and artificial intelligence (AI) enhance life activities, such as increasing mobility in persons with arthritic joints through pain medications or other technological interventions, by easing the seeming embarrassment of an imperfect “look” through cosmetic surgery, and by using prosthetic devices to replace missing human anatomical parts to remain “wholly” human.

While there have been successes in using sophisticated technological devices to modify perceived “uncharacteristic” human physiognomies, leading to improved self-esteem and social acceptance, the concept of “remaining human” in the face of such modifications requires further exploration (Körner et al., 2021). Posthumanism and transhumanism offer the potential to improve the persons’ quality of life by enhancing human capabilities and overcoming human limitations, but it is essential to critically examine the ethical implications while ensuring equitable access to these technologies. The intersection of nature and culture raises additional questions about how these modifications affect our understanding of human identity and the boundaries between the natural and the artificial.

In her seminal work, Haraway (2013) challenges the foundational division between nature and culture. She uses the figure of the ‘cyborg’—a hybrid of organism and machine—to dismantle traditional dualisms such as human/machine and human/animal. For Haraway, the cyborg is not a fixed entity but a fluid, ever-changing identity that embraces multiplicity and hybridity. This blurring of boundaries is presented as a path toward liberation and empowerment, allowing for a more inclusive and diverse understanding of humanity. With these enhanced capabilities, dismantling oppressive power dynamics leads to a more actualized human body and experience. The actualization of the appreciated “real” human being potential in the process of becoming is critically essential as Parr (2010) explained, however, the underlying question remains: what does it mean to be human, in the context of body modifications or transformations? Tirosh Samuelson (2012, p. 711) expressed it well in that “these rapid technological developments inspire the hope that human beings will live longer, healthier, and happier lives because technologies will remove biological imperfections and the social ills they cause.”

The explicit question of our paper is, how does the philosophical drive for completeness through body modification impact the concept of ‘remaining human’ and shape the future of nursing?

Methods

In this paper, we employed a discursive, concept-driven approach to explore the idea of being human amidst technological transformation. Our inquiry was anchored in a posthumanist philosophical framework, where we drew principally from the works of Braidotti (2013) on the process of “becoming,” Deleuze (1994) on “difference and repetition,” and Heidegger (1977) on technology as a mode of “revealing.” We integrated this philosophical lens with key nursing theories that address the human experience in health and technology: Rizzo Parse’s Theory of Humanbecoming (Rizzo Parse, 2007, 2014), Rozzano Locsin’s theory of Technological Competency as Caring in Nursing (Locsin et al., 2021; Locsin & Purnell, 2015), and Anne Boykin and Savina Schoenhofer’s theory of Nursing As Caring (Schoenhofer et al., 2019).

To inform our analysis, we conducted a targeted literature search of databases including PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. This was not a systematic review; instead, we undertook a purposive exploration to identify sources that would enrich our philosophical and theoretical dialogue. Thirty-eight articles were selected as instrumental to our analysis. We were guided by the following criteria: (1) articles focusing on human body modifications and the use of advanced technologies for human enhancement; (2) works exploring philosophical perspectives on being, becoming, and the role of technology in shaping human identity; and (3) literature examining the implications of these concepts for nursing theory and practice, with particular attention to the works of Parse and Locsin. While our research prioritized contemporary scholarship from 2020 to 2024, the analysis also incorporates foundational philosophical and theoretical texts published prior to this period, which were essential for establishing the paper’s conceptual framing.

Our analytical strategy, guided by the hermeneutic approach of Verbeek (2006), involved a three-stage interpretive process: (1) we identified and extracted the core concepts from the selected philosophical and nursing frameworks; (2) we examined the convergences and divergences between these perspectives to synthesize a nuanced understanding of human body modification and its relationship to nursing; and (3) we articulated the implications of this synthesis for nursing practice, education, and policy. The result of our work was an interpretive-philosophical reflection that integrated existing thought to generate new insights on future Nursing. Discoveries and Discussion

“What makes one aspire to perfection – is it a personal or a social expectation? Whose aspirations am I living up to when I wish to change my ugly visage – my own, so that I am pleasing to myself, or society’s, so that I am not repugnant to others? I suppose the answer to this dilemma lies in “both” – social expectations create or at least tailor personal expectations” (Savina Schoenhofer, personal communication, May 12, 2021).

The discussion is approached from three philosophical positions of Deleuze, Braidotti, and Heidegger, and theoretical arguments in Nursing by Parse, Locsin, and Boykin, and Schoenhofer. The concepts of being human, human becoming, and the consequent influences of technologies and AI reveal the nature of nursing and its practice. Sophisticated technologies are essential accouterments for human body transformations and AI, allowing humans to have the perfection and realization of social and personal expectations to “remain” human beings.

Becoming and Revealing

Andrés (2019) explained that, to Braidotti, to be human is to deal with “what (we) want to become,” or “what (we) are capable of becoming,” suggesting that a human is understood as a dynamic and interconnected entity, constantly shaped by its interactions with the social, cultural, and technological forces surrounding it. Observing that human values, representations, and ways of understanding are still linked to the antediluvian concept of the human being, Braidotti (2013) claimed that the political chain of historical enslavement from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights during the Enlightenment Period, which is the capacity to become, needs to be revisited. While this Declaration aims for universality, its framing may inadvertently perpetuate existing inequalities by reflecting on the priorities of dominant groups and marginalizing diverse voices and experiences (Ben Achour, 2024). To counter this perpetuation, courageous, democratic, and critical dialogues are needed to collectively determine “what we want to become and what we are capable of becoming.” As Braidotti suggests, achieving this requires a fearless, open, and analytical approach to collective discussion, ensuring that diverse perspectives are included and valued in shaping one’s future.

According to Williams (2013), Deleuze offered a groundbreaking perspective on the human journey of becoming. Central to this was his concept of “individuals,” which, much like Heidegger (1927) exploration of “being” in Being and Time, empowers people to use thoughtful reasoning in pursuing truth, to remain open to new understandings, and to resist confinement of past representations. However, approaches to realizing human potential diverge. Heidegger connects the meaning of Being fundamentally with Time, proposing that our true potential is actualized through committed, genuine choices and a projection of ourselves into that future. Deleuze, conversely, views becoming as a series of dynamic processes that interlink tangible things, thoughts, and sensations with the pure passions and perspectives they imply. This contrast highlights a key philosophical distinction: Heidegger emphasizes the individual’s relationship with time as crucial for self-realization, whereas Deleuze focuses on the continuous processes of transformation. It is this emphasis on perpetual flux that underpins Deleuze’s principle of “connecting and discarding”—a call to engage with all things yet avoid fixed identification with actual objects or static ideas, as these are precisely what must be continually left behind in the process of becoming.

In nursing, Parse’s theory of human-becoming addressed the connecting view in the Humanbecoming paradigm specified as the fundamental beliefs about the humanuniverse, the ethos of Humanbecoming, and living quality. Like Deleuze’s principle of connecting and discarding, encompassed in a theoretical, Parse-abiding theme is the paradox of connecting-separating, “an intricate rhythm expressed as a pattern preference” (Rizzo Parse, 2014, p. 36), not “opposites to be reconciled or dilemmas to be overcome but, rather, liv[ing] rhythms” (Rizzo Parse, 2007, p. 309).

Hostage to Being an Organic Human

It is important that persons as human beings should not be limited by the rigid, past definitions of what it means to be human. While acknowledging our history is important, it must not hinder our potential for growth and self-realization. Resistance to change, often seen in attempts to uphold a static notion of “pure humanity”—such as prohibitions against blood transfusions or avoidance of necessary prosthetics in some nursing contexts—can be detrimental. Deleuze (1994) offers a way to understand this dynamism through his concept of “becoming.” He described this as a process fueled by “virtual intensities,” the underlying, often unseen, forces and potentials that drive change and shape the transformation of ideas and experiences. In our appreciation of influences on nursing, these “virtual intensities” can be understood as deep-seated motivations, beliefs, and desires that significantly impact a person’s health-wise journey. Recognizing these underlying influencers allows nurses to grasp the deeper meaning of a person’s actions and choices, paving the way for more effective and personalized care.

This Deleuzian emphasis on underlying potentials and continuous becoming finds a strong resonance with Rizzo Parse (2014) nursing theory, particularly her concept of the “humanuniverse-health process.” For Parse, this process is the core concern of nursing; it describes how individuals continuously co-create their unique health experiences, expressions, and meanings through their dynamic interactions with their world. From this perspective, the central aim in nursing is to enhance a person’s, family’s, or community’s quality of life as they define it. Being truly present, understanding, and moving with the individual’s co-created health journey profoundly shapes those very “virtual intensities” that Deleuze has identified.

Technologies and Becoming Human

In healthcare, technology plays a vital role in facilitating the modification and transformation of human bodies. By utilizing sophisticated devices and procedures, individuals can modify their physical appearance to align with their desired sense of self. This can encompass a range of adjustments and alterations, such as from aesthetic enhancements to gender-affirming procedures. The concept of “pure becoming” highlights individuals’ transformative journey to achieve their desired physical and personal identity (Williams, 2013). Although Anowai and Chukwujekwu (2019) asserted that human beings can live satisfying human lives by revealing their desired realness, however this is often hampered by beliefs in inauthentic but natural human physiognomies. For Heidegger and Deleuze, these conditions can allude to concerns about the real existence of the authentic self, brought about by beliefs of unnatural human physiognomies. Heidegger (1977) on technology is that it is a mode of revealing, that is, a way in which the world and our own possibilities are disclosed or shown to us. However, he cautioned that modern technology tends to ‘enframe’ this revealing, constraining how we understand and interact with the world by framing everything, including ourselves, as a resource to be optimized. In his essay “The Question Concerning Technology,” Heidegger somehow aligns with the posthumanist standpoint that seeks to decenter humans, emphasizing the interconnectedness of beings and the environment. This aligns with the posthumanist transformative effects of technology on human subjectivity and the blurring of the distinction between human and nonhuman. Heidegger claims that the real self is recognized in projecting possibilities of becoming, as handsome or beautiful beyond the “horizon of time,” and returning with the chosen possibility, then living with it, thus, living a real life of becoming, giving meaning to one’s existence (Reinhardt, 1952).

The notion of an authentic self, often perceived as inherently natural and organic, is increasingly understood as dynamic and fluid, reflecting humanity’s constant state of becoming. This resonates with the ever-changing nature of the world. Philosophically, both Heidegger’s emphasis on authentic Being revealed through existence and Deleuze’s concept of immanent becoming contribute to understanding a “real self” that is not static but continually unfolding. This perspective aligns closely with Parse’s nursing theory, where the central concern for a person’s quality of life is met by the Humanbecoming nurse being truly present. Such presence supports the individual in their unique journey of health and self-discovery, embracing their evolving nature rather than a fixed “natural self.”

This evolving “real self,” or “being human,” is capable of boundless development across biological, physiological, socio-cultural, and political spheres, moving beyond essentialist ideas of a fixed core. Braidotti (2013) powerfully articulates this through her concept of “being” as a continuous process of self-creation—ever-growing, evolving, and relational. This perspective, which embraces human multiplicity and constant change, directly supports Parse’s Humanbecoming theory by framing the human as an ever-evolving entity. Such a dynamic understanding of the human also engages with broader posthumanist thought, which challenges traditional anthropocentrism, the human-centric view of the universe (Oriel, 2014). As Dillard Wright et al. (2024) propose, positing nursing within a posthumanist framework involves questioning established categories, such as human/nonhuman and nature/culture, thereby expanding our understanding of care in an interconnected world. In a complementary way, the nursing practice process of “knowing persons as caring” highlights this dynamism (Locsin & Purnell, 2015); it compels nurses to continuously engage with individuals precisely because they are ever-changing, caring beings, whose humanness is constantly in process.

This ongoing process of becoming stands in stark contrast to historical ideals of a fixed and perfectible human form, famously captured in Leonardo da Vinci’s ‘Vitruvian Man.’ That image represented a universal, mathematically perfected ‘man’—a static ideal to which one could aspire. Posthumanist thought, particularly from a Deleuzean viewpoint (Deleuze, 1994), dismantles this very notion. The goal is not to achieve a pre-defined perfection, but to engage in the continuous ‘desiring-production’ of the real self. This points away from a stable category of ‘human’ and toward a celebration of movement, difference, and the infinite ways a human being may transform. Moreover, Heidegger’s concept of ‘standing reserve’ provides a distinct perspective on this desire for self-revelation (Heidegger, 1977). He argued that modern technology positions everything—from a river to human beings—as a resource on standby, ready to be used and optimized. From this viewpoint, body modifications can be seen as a person treating their own body as a ‘standing reserve’ to be improved and reconfigured in pursuit of an authentic self. From this viewpoint, advanced technology and its sophistication can readily facilitate the transformations individuals undertake to realize their authentic selves.

This dynamic is clearly visible today. Sophisticated technologies, such as implants that ease self-improvement, enhance self-protection, or increase tolerance, are increasingly commonplace. The accessibility of implantable, wearable, or remote technological devices hastens the achievement of what Pelegrín-Borondo et al. (2020) describe as realizing human “completeness.” In this way, advanced technology acts not merely as a passive tool, but as an active force in the ongoing process of individuals fulfilling and transforming their sense of an authentic, real self.

Transformations in Being Human

Why do humans choose to transform their bodies? (Deleuze, 1994), much in the same sense as Parse’s notion of cocreation in the human universe-health process, explained that human beings have the creative flow of thought and are capable of freely acting to change; they have the freedom to change. Constraints and limitations influence the “act” of human transformations of the organic and biological body, but this “act,” according to Deleuze, can be replayed, freeing past constraints and limitations on the event of human body transformation toward a new “drama.” Formerly, aging and its consequences prompted humans to seek human body modifications. Cosmetic alterations in human physiognomies became popular, and youthfulness and aging gracefully were prized.

Today, moved by desires to live better, look better, or achieve better, aging is no longer the main animator for figuring out physical, physiological, psychological, mental, or transcultural inspirations for human body transformations in revealing the human being. However, it should be noted that human body transformations were once, in the sense of Deleuze (1994), simulated towards living better. These were once the pure intensities and ideas implied by actual things (i.e., body changes through cosmetic alterations) of the event in the real world. Much like body tattoos, initially, choosing one as a sailor made them symbols of identity for the underclass or outlaws, so to speak; however, now they are desirable because everyone who is anyone (or considered cool) has some kind of tattoo. It is a socially-constructed desire!

Human enhancement technologies, when applied to disability, raise philosophical questions about normalization and accommodation (Hall, 2016). The aesthetic transformation of disability involves challenging normative notions of the body and redefining what is considered aesthetically pleasing. The Crip theory examines how the intersection of normalcy and human transformation challenges ableist assumptions to foster a more inclusive understanding of disabled lives (McRuer, 2011). Will human transformations through body modifications allow human beings to be human while enjoying the freedom of living with an unlimited healthspan (National Institute on Aging, 2020), or is that part of life spent in good health? Will society allow the freedom to evolve and accept humans as human beings who have transformations through body modifications?

The all-important essential element of maintaining health and healthspan fosters human eagerness to embark on bodily transformations. Machines and humans share a complementary symbiotic relationship where technical objects intervene as mediators between organic humans and their naturalness (Hoel, 2018). Human-technology connections are derived from embodiment relations, alterity relations (or otherness), hermeneutic relations, and background relations (Locsin et al., 2021). Nevertheless, in support of this connection, Ihde (1990) declared that no human who is separated from technology could resist the effects of age and organic deterioration.

In synthesis, the desire to maintain health and extend healthspan, (Constantino & Zalon, 2019) is a key concern in nursing, often motivating individuals to embrace bodily transformations through technology. This pursuit highlights the increasingly intertwined relationship between humans and machines, where technology acts as a mediator between individuals and their organic limitations. As Braidotti’s posthumanist perspective suggests, human identity is constantly evolving and shaped by interactions with technology. Deleuze’s concept of “becoming” further emphasizes this dynamic interplay, implying that human beings are always in a state of flux, adapting and changing through their relationship with technology. Heidegger’s notion of technology as a “mode of revealing” adds another layer to this understanding, suggesting that technology can both enhance and constrain our understanding of ourselves and the world. Parse’s Humanbecoming theory complements these perspectives, emphasizing the co-creation of human experiences within a dynamic universe that includes technology. This convergence of philosophical ideas highlights the complex relationship between technology, human identity, and the pursuit of health in the context of nursing.

Human Body Modifications and Artificial Intelligence

Having established how technology can be a mode of ‘revealing’ an authentic self, we now turn to the most powerful contemporary catalyst for this transformation: artificial intelligence. As humans, we are constantly changing and often feel limited by our physical bodies. These limitations, however, create opportunities for growth and transformation—becoming stronger, healthier, or more connected to others—in the pursuit of a more meaningful and fulfilling life. The desire to express our full potential often inspires us to explore ways to enhance our physical selves, sometimes through modifications made possible by advanced technologies and AI. We have a wide range of options available, from self-improvement to self-expression, all aimed at adapting to and thriving in an increasingly technological world. This perspective aligns with the core values of nursing, recognizing the importance of individual choices and empowering people to live authentically. This viewpoint influences a disciplinary practice of nursing, appreciating that persons can freely choose that which is meaningful in their lives, a perspective that Boykin and Schoenhofer (2001) embrace, and which is grounded in their theory of Nursing As Caring.

For instance, someone who has facial peculiarities, viewed as anomalies by socio-cultural norms and emotional reaction to those norms, may be motivated to undergo body modification. However, Workman et al. (2021) found that human responses to anomalous faces varied from sensitivity to antipathy. The lack of attractiveness, less prosociality, exposure to obvious partialities, decreased dispositional empathic concerns, and a stronger “just world” determine that these humans deserve what they get. Nevertheless, in exploring the consequences of human body transformations using technology and AI, understanding the potential impact and significance that human body modifications exert on how one ‘looks’ can potentially be off-putting and restraining.

In the present day, with AI integrated into most facets of human existence, the rapid advancement of technology continues to accelerate the pace of bodily modifications, impacting the open acceptance of human body transformations. AI algorithms are currently employed to design advanced prosthetic limbs that provide enhanced functionality and possess the ability to learn from user movements. To illustrate, consider a clinical scenario: a nurse is caring for ‘John,’ a patient who has recently been fitted with an advanced, AI-driven prosthetic arm that learns from his movements. Applying Locsin’s theory in this context requires more than just managing the technical aspects of the device. The nurse must practice ‘knowing John as caring’ by engaging with his personal journey of integrating this artificial limb into his sense of self and his daily life. This is a literal process of ‘becoming’ a new version of his ‘human,’ co-created with technology. The nurse’s role is to be present for this transformation, supporting John as he navigates the physical challenges and the profound changes to his own identity and embodiment. Moreover, AI is facilitating personalized medicine, enabling the customization of treatment plans and rehabilitation programs to meet the specific needs of individuals who have undergone body modifications. In the surgical domain, AI assists with intricate procedures, enhancing precision and accuracy in minimally invasive surgeries and robotic systems. This results in quicker recovery times and improved patient outcomes. AI’s influence extends to gene editing technologies, raising profound ethical questions regarding human enhancement and the future trajectory of our species.

Perspectives on the Sophistication of Technology and Artificial Intelligence

Foundational directives support the discussion on the realities of being human. Aging is an a priori reason for transformations of human frailty (Locsin et al., 2021), often realized because of sophisticated technologies and artificial intelligence to live meaningful and quality lives. Furthermore, the desire (Gao, 2013) to be beautiful as if being natural argues for perfection through the complex transformation of becoming a human being (Deleuze, 1994). Driven by possibly being able to free oneself from the felt inauthentic self, Deleuze’s delineation of stable thought and substance, movement and moving of beings in flux, complements the understanding of selfhood, from “not the real me,” or the capitulated being, to valuing oneself to be realized as being human.

Deleuze (1994) notion of spaces between things as ways of revealing new things or their newness defined the posthumanist views, supporting conditions of the ‘being human” of human beings. In nursing, posthumanism might involve recognizing the agency and interconnectedness of relational understanding of care. An explicit example in the text is BLACK and white to decenter the dominant social group (Smith et al., 2022). It is hoped that the start of leveling the plain field in healthcare is using correct language and pronouns. The desire to uncover the real self and connectivity within differences and repetitions transforms desirable meanings as a product. Connectivity within differences refers to the idea that, despite our individual and unique experiences, an underlying interconnectedness unites us as human beings. This concept has practical relevance in nursing as it promotes a holistic and inclusive approach to patient care. By recognizing and appreciating the diverse experiences and perspectives of each patient, nurses can provide more effective and compassionate care.

One must be attuned to the thought that the more living matter becomes complex, the more it transforms (i.e., predictability), while a nonrealistic human feature illuminates possibilities promised by the outcomes of transformations driven by high-fidelity technologies and AI. The transformation of a person’s biological physiognomy and physiological abilities can reveal the true human self in certain, preferred forms, and with desired functionalities.

Anowai and Chukwujekwu (2019) declared that Plato’s viewpoint on existentialism or realism supports the claim that being natural highlights reality, genuineness, and truth, while inauthenticity is being fake, false, fraudulent, and pretentious. The socio-ethical aspects of technology, on which the humanities’ philosophy of technology is founded, support the appropriateness of human body transformations through technologies and AI. The developmental process of transformation through sophisticated technology fosters a societal appreciation of the human being (Verbeek, 2006).

As a posthumanist ambition in a highly demanding technological world, freedom from the reactionary and critical posthumanist train of thought advances the analytic design of the self. Exercising the freedom to express “being human” with empathy and resilience (Reyes et al., 2020), the supposition that all persons have the desire to realize their hoped-for self is a critical assumption that the desire and the act of desiring are inherently real, natural, and evolutionary. Smith et al. (2022) use the metaphor of composting as a form of transcendental point to challenge the transition inception of humanism. The sophistication of advanced technologies allows for refined revelations of self, though not only a “temporary make-up,” as in cosmetic designs and implementations, but also the reconfiguring of self to be the authentic, beautiful, and real human being. In 2017, the average cost of cosmetic surgery, such as a facelift, was $7,448, according to the American Society of Plastic Surgeons. That excludes hospital or surgical center costs, anesthesia, or related expenses, the individual’s desired results, the ability of the surgeon, and geographical location (Herndon, 2023).

Nevertheless, a new locus of priority is the perspective of human space-time. To wit, Lim-Saco et al. (2018) advanced the worldview of not only space and time elements but also the humanness context in illuminating the authentic self and one’s existence. Being human is more than simply existing or surviving as an organic being; technological advancements and the healthspan are made possible with human transformations co-existing in expressions of humanness and technological dependence. Fundamentally, technological advancements contribute to the enrichment of the purpose and meaning of one’s life,

With a grounded sense of purpose and meaning, human beings seem able to understand the contributions, associations, and impact of advanced technologies and AI, achieving human transformations in revealing their true selves. It appears that a conscious, meaningful existence allows human beings to liberate themselves from inauthenticity, potentially opening self-consciousness to understand and appreciate the true significance of human living. Human transformations relieve constraints and limitations of unnaturalness (Anowai & Chukwujekwu, 2019), giving them the freedom to thrive and realize the fullness of their real human potential.

Nevertheless, in the past, intelligent machines, even those with humanlike features, were often treated as entertainment, like toys, in part because of their limited capabilities. However, robots with advanced AI and more sophisticated capabilities are now entering the healthcare field (Locsin et al., 2021). Humanoid robots like Pepper©, although endowed with limited conversational and interactive capabilities and often relegated to entertainment roles, have been used in ways to instigate healthful human performances, particularly among older persons (Tanioka et al., 2021). In the future, remarkably intelligent machines with accouterments for persons with diminished capabilities will play critical roles in aiding them to live meaningful lives. Furthermore, intelligent machines equipped with advanced technological sensors and natural language processing or NLP (Betriana et al., 2021) can promote human health and well-being. In addressing human frailty among older persons, a transhumanist perspective on human-technology enhancements and replacements delivers solutions toward transcending this frailty (Locsin et al., 2021).

The body is often viewed as a platform for enhancement, with technology serving to overcome inherent limitations like aging. However, critical posthumanism challenges this perspective, suggesting that frailty among older individuals can be seen as a socio-cultural construct. When analyzing transhumanism and critical posthumanism perspectives on aging and frailty, the focus should be on how they contribute to shaping experiences in a technologically advanced world. Critical posthumanism challenges the notion of an idealized or perfect body, arguing that such ideals are founded on narrow and exclusionary standards that perpetuate social inequalities. It recognizes that the body is always situated, subjective, and inherently limited, and that accepting these limitations is a fundamental aspect of the human experience. Critical posthumanism acknowledges that aging is a natural and unavoidable part of life, celebrating the diversity of aging experiences across individuals and cultures. Technological innovations have the potential to shape these aging experiences.

It is essential, however, to acknowledge a potential tension within this philosophical framework. While transhumanism often optimistically embraces technology as a tool for overcoming human limitations, Heidegger’s perspective is more cautious. He warned that technology’s tendency to ‘enframe’ the world as a mere resource could lead to a profound sense of alienation, reducing human existence to a technical problem to be solved. For nursing, this tension is critical. It raises the question of how to support a patient’s journey of technological enhancement while simultaneously preserving a sense of their intrinsic worth, beyond their function or ‘optimization’.

Being Human in a High-Tech World

Technology can be thoughtfully used to help people escape the limitations of feeling unreal or not true to themselves. This can free them from the restrictions and boundaries that make them feel inauthentic. Regardless of innovations with technologies that instigate possible disruptive engagements, Constantino and Zalon (2019) declared that innovations promote incremental changes in processes or services and transform or interrupt the advancement toward frugal innovation. The clarity of the issue and proposition that healthcare practice involves persons as entities that remain whole, regardless of missing parts, dictates the conditions needed for valuable practice.

As technology, especially AI, becomes more influential, it can help people live their lives to the fullest. This is especially true in transhumanism and posthumanism, where the modification of our bodies through technology is seen as a means to improve ourselves and connect with others in meaningful ways (Merzlyakov, 2022; Tirosh Samuelson, 2012). As people rely more on technology for everyday tasks, it frees them from physical limitations and allows them to focus on the qualities that make individuals truly human: compassion, kindness, and empathy. While these technological advancements can potentially reduce inequalities, it is essential to be mindful that they could also worsen them. Human experience comes in many forms, and it is necessary to value that diversity even as individuals explore the possibilities of technology.

Impact on Nursing as a Discipline, Science, and Practice

The profound technological and philosophical shifts discussed—particularly regarding human transformation and the evolving nature of ‘being human’—directly challenge and offer new horizons for nursing. As individuals increasingly utilize technology to modify their physical forms, sometimes blurring the lines between the organic and the artificial, the very essence of nursing care and its relevance in a technologically integrated future come into question.

In this evolving landscape, foundational nursing theories offer crucial guidance. Locsin’s emphasis on “knowing persons as caring” becomes paramount. Understanding the individual’s motivations, values, and unique experiences behind their desire for body modification is essential for providing compassionate and effective nursing care. This approach requires a deep engagement with the person, especially as they navigate transformations that redefine their sense of embodiment. Similarly, Parse’s Humanbecoming theory, with its focus on lived experience and the co-creation of health, provides a robust framework. This theory can be naturally extended to encompass the technologically enhanced human; the concept of “becoming” takes on new dimensions as individuals integrate technology into their very being, a process involving personal growth intertwined with technological enhancement. For nurses, this means expanding their understanding of “knowing persons as caring” to authentically connect with and support individuals who have undergone body modifications, recognizing their specific needs and the deeply personal meanings attached to their transformations. The interplay of life satisfaction, emotional support, and perceived health with an individual’s healthspan also grows more complex and requires nuanced nursing attention, considering these technological human experiences. By engaging with these evolving concepts, nursing can remain relevant in a high-tech world while continuing to provide compassionate and person-centered care.

Consequently, the growing prevalence of technologies that transform human bodies necessitates a significant evolution in nursing practice. Nurses must acquire the knowledge and skills to adeptly care for patients who have undergone diverse body modifications, recognizing the unique physical, emotional, and social challenges and opportunities these entail. This includes fostering an empathetic, comprehensive approach centered on understanding the individual’s journey and motivations.

To maintain its relevance and effectively serve in a future where technological body modifications are increasingly common, the nursing profession must proactively adapt. This involves developing updated practice guidelines that address the specific care needs of this population. Furthermore, nursing curricula should be revised to incorporate comprehensive content on human body modifications, covering the ethical, legal, social, and psychological dimensions. Such educational enhancements will equip future nurses with the competence and compassion required to care for a diverse and evolving patient population, ensuring nursing remains a vital and responsive discipline.

Limitations and Future Directions

The scope and methodology of this paper present several limitations that also point toward avenues for future inquiry. First, as a discursive and conceptual paper, our reflections are not based on empirical data; they are primarily theoretical. As such, a key direction for future research is to test and operationalize these philosophical concepts in practice. The lived experiences of these populations are essential for a complete understanding of how technology shapes the sense of ‘remaining human.’ Future research should prioritize qualitative studies, such as phenomenology or ethnography, to explore these first-hand perspectives in depth. Such work would ground the abstract concepts of ‘becoming’ and ‘revealing’ in the tangible realities of patient and provider experiences.

Second, the philosophical framework, while robust, is rooted in specific Western posthumanist and post-structuralist traditions. This focus necessarily excludes a rich diversity of other cultural, spiritual, and philosophical viewpoints on the body, technology, and identity. Future cross-cultural research could compare and contrast these perspectives, enriching the global dialogue on transhumanism and providing a more inclusive foundation for nursing practice worldwide. Third, this paper raises significant ethical and equity concerns regarding freedom, access, and justice, but they are not fully resolved within our conceptual scope. A critical area that remains unresolved is how to translate these ethical principles into equitable healthcare policy and practice in an age of advanced technological enhancement. Our analysis does not deeply investigate the socio-economic and political barriers that create a ‘technological divide,’ an issue central to justice.

Finally, while this paper proposes broad implications for nursing education and policy, these must be translated into specific, actionable next steps. Future work should move beyond theoretical discussion to focus on concrete curriculum redesign, including the development and evaluation of educational modules that equip nurses with the philosophical and ethical tools to navigate posthumanist care. There is also an urgent need to formulate clear ethical guidelines for practice, addressing the unique challenges posed by technologically enhanced patients. Furthermore, research should pioneer and assess pilot models of posthumanist nursing care to determine how to best support individuals in their process of ‘becoming’ within a clinical setting. This will translate the abstract concepts of this paper into testable interventions and validated clinical practice guidelines for this emergent and growing patient population.

Impact on Nursing as a Discipline, Science, and Practice

As individuals increasingly use technology to modify their bodies, blurring the lines between the organic and the artificial, the very meaning of person-centered care is called into question. Foundational nursing theories, such as Locsin’s “knowing persons as caring” and Parse’s Humanbecoming, provide essential guidance. These frameworks compel nurses to look beyond the physical changes and engage with the person’s unique motivations, values, and co-created health journey. In this context, ‘becoming’ is a process intertwined with technological enhancement, and the nurse’s role is to authentically support the individual’s lived experience of this transformation.

To remain relevant, the nursing profession must translate this theoretical understanding into tangible changes in practice, education, and policy. The growing prevalence of technologically modified bodies necessitates that nurses acquire new competencies to address the unique physical, emotional, and social needs of this population. This requires a proactive evolution of the discipline, including the development of new practice guidelines and the redesign of nursing curricula to incorporate the complex ethical, legal, and social dimensions of posthumanist care. While this paper offers a philosophical integration of posthumanist thought and nursing theory, it does not provide empirical evidence to support its claims. Future inquiry should investigate how these ideas are translated into clinical practice, nursing education, and policy, particularly in addressing the equity of access, the ethical regulation of body modifications, and the role of nurses in shaping socially just technological futures.

Conclusions

This article explored the reality of “being human” through bodily modifications and transformations using sophisticated and advanced technologies. When people change their bodies with technology to improve their abilities, they also become more reliant on that technology. This process of redesigning themselves involves changing, adding to, or removing parts of their bodies that they feel are holding them back. This allows them to become their true selves. Looking at these new types of humans, shaped by transhumanism and posthumanism, shows how technology can turn weaknesses into strengths. This helps us understand what it means to be human in a world increasingly shaped by technology.

The collective insights from Braidotti, Heidegger, and Deleuze offer a nuanced understanding of human existence as inherently diverse, interconnected, and in a constant state of transformation. Rather than viewing individuals as fixed entities, this synthesized philosophical perspective emphasizes the dynamic interplay of experiences, perceptions, and the continuous process of becoming that shapes each person’s unique journey and their understanding of the world. This view highlights the crucial role of nursing in supporting individuals in their pursuit of a fulfilling life, particularly as technology increasingly mediates these pursuits. Braidotti’s call for a collaborative approach, where human connection and technology work in tandem, becomes especially pertinent, highlighting resilience in navigating an evolving, technologically infused world.

This integrated philosophical framework fundamentally challenges nursing to adopt a more expansive view of “being human.” It calls for a critical engagement with the ongoing quest for meaning and authenticity in a technologically shaped world, compelling a thorough examination of the ethical implications of technological advancements and their profound impact on the future of healthcare and the very definition of humanity.

In summary, as the boundaries between humans and technology continue to blur, the drive to ‘remain human’ through bodily modification and enhancement raises profound questions about identity. The accelerating integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into healthcare further intensifies this dynamic, pushing the limits of what it means to be human. It is the synergistic application of philosophical perspectives from Deleuze, Braidotti, and Heidegger, interwoven with the nursing theories of Parse and Locsin, that provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the complex implications of human body modifications and their impact on the future of nursing. This collective lens allows for a deeper appreciation of the motivations behind such transformations and the evolving human-technology relationship. The critical considerations emerging from this synthesis lead directly to Schoenhofer’s apt question: “is it not so that today’s view of the focus of nursing and healthcare is subject to change as the zeitgeist leads us all toward transhumanist thought and whatever is coming after that?” (Personal communication, July 8, 2021). This provocative inquiry highlights the imperative for nursing to adapt and evolve in response to the transformative power of technology, thereby ensuring its continued relevance and capacity to support human potential in an increasingly technologically intertwined future.
