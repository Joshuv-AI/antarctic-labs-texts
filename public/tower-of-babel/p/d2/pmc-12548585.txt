Assessment of adherence to guidelines for biochemical monitoring and ursodeoxycholic acid treatment response in a retrospective cohort of US patients with primary biliary cholangitis
Authors: Stuart C Gordon, Amandeep Sahota, Mark A Schmidt, Yihe G Daida, Loralee B Rupp, Sheri Trudeau, Christina Melkonian, Christopher L Bowlus, Mei Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAmerican Association for the Study of Liver Disease guidelines recommend regular testing of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) among patients with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) to monitor disease progression and response to treatment with ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), but previous studies have shown that adherence to recommended testing intervals is low. We used data from the Fibrotic Liver Disease (FOLD) Consortium to evaluate rates of adherence among US routine care patients.MethodsPBC cases were confirmed with chart abstraction. Patients from three FOLD sites (Henry Ford Health (Detroit, MI), Kaiser Permanente-Southern California (Los Angeles, California) and Kaiser Permanente-Northwest (Portland, Oregon)) were observed from 1 January 2018 through 31 December 2021. We divided our evaluation of adherence to monitoring guidelines into two segments: (1) the first 12 months post-UDCA initiation; and (2) >12 months post-UDCA initiation.ResultsA total of 1756 patients were identified for the 2018–2021 period; 67 patients did not receive UDCA and were excluded from the sample. A total of 1689 patients were included in one or both segments (segment 1: 740, segment 2: 1689). Only 52% of patients received appropriate ALP testing to ascertain response to UDCA after roughly 1 year of treatment; rates were significantly higher among patients with specialist care compared with those without (54% vs 45%, p=0.001). For the period following the first year of UDCA treatment, the observed monitoring rate was 67%, where hepatology or gastroenterology specialist care was associated with significantly higher rates of monitoring (65%–78%) compared with those without care from a specialist (30%–57%, p<0.0001) with the same level of comorbidity.ConclusionIn a large US PBC cohort, there were concerning levels of non-adherence to recommended biochemical monitoring. Receipt of care from a specialist was associated with higher rates of monitoring. Strategies to increase rates of biochemical testing are needed.Keywords: PRIMARY BILIARY CIRRHOSIS, AUTOIMMUNE HEPATITIS, EPIDEMIOLOGY

Introduction

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is an autoimmune cholangiopathy that may lead to cirrhosis and its complications. Guidelines from major medical organisations, including the American Association for the Study of Liver Disease (AASLD),1 2 recommend that all PBC patients undergo routine monitoring of liver biochemistries, particularly alkaline phosphatase (ALP). All PBC patients with abnormal liver enzymes should also be treated with ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) and have ALP testing roughly 1 year after treatment initiation in order to determine response to treatment. European and Asian professional bodies offer similar guidelines.3 4 Patients with intolerance to UDCA or inadequate treatment response (ITR) may be offered second-line treatments, including the recently approved drugs seladelpar and elafibranor, or a number of off-label treatments, including fibrates.2 5 Thereafter, it is recommended that patients have liver biochemistry levels measured every 6 to 9 months to monitor ongoing response to treatment and disease progression.

Despite these recommendations, it is not clear that all patients are receiving appropriate follow-up, especially in real-world settings. A recent study of National Health Service patients in the UK assessed adherence to British Society of Gastroenterology standards for PBC treatment and monitoring.6 The authors found several critical shortfalls, including lack of UDCA treatment among a large proportion of patients, underdosing among those who had received treatment and low rates of second-line treatments among the roughly 50% of patients who did not respond to UDCA.

We sought to assess adherence to AASLD guidelines for monitoring of US PBC patients receiving routine care. Using data from the Fibrotic Liver Disease (FOLD) Consortium collected through 2021, we evaluated the proportion of patients receiving recommended ALP monitoring, first-line treatment with UDCA and second-line treatments in the event of ITR to UDCA. We also evaluated factors associated with receipt of recommended biochemical monitoring.

Methods

The FOLD Consortium has been previously described.7 8 Briefly, FOLD comprises 11 geographically diverse health systems, representing four US Census Bureau-defined regions (Northeast, Midwest, Northwest and South). FOLD follows the guidelines of the US Department of Health and Human Services for the protection of human subjects. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of each participating site (Henry Ford Health, Kaiser Permanente Southern California, Kaiser Permanente Northwest, Kaiser Permanente Hawai'i; coordinating site IRB number 9762–01). The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the observational and deidentified nature of the data. All authors had access to the study results and reviewed and approved the final manuscript. This analysis reflects funding available for identifying new PBC patients and additional cycle of data collected through 2021 from three FOLD sites: Henry Ford Health (HFH, Detroit, Michigan), Kaiser Permanente-Southern California (KPSC, Los Angeles, California) and Kaiser Permanente-Northwest (KPNW, Portland, Oregon). The manuscript conforms to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist reporting guidelines for observational studies (see online supplemental file for STROBE checklist).

Patient cohort and data collection

FOLD PBC patient identification methods have been previously described.8 We have previously developed and validated an automated Classification and Regression Tree method9 for accurately identifying PBC patients using electronic health record (EHR) data.8 Classifier variables included: (1) an antimitochondrial antibody (AMA)-positive test with at least one PBC diagnosis code; (2) an AMA-positive test with a recorded ALP>150 IU/L (in the absence of a PBC diagnosis code); or (3) presence of two PBC diagnosis codes (in the absence of an AMA-positive result). This classification model was validated using eight independent FOLD data sets and had an area under the receiver operator characteristic curve value of over 93% (considered ‘excellent’ predictive accuracy). All cases were confirmed with chart abstraction performed by trained medical abstractors; FOLD hepatologists provided adjudication of indeterminate cases. This analysis included patients with confirmed PBC from three FOLD sites (HFH, KPSC and KPNW) who had at least 4 months of post-PBC diagnosis follow-up during the period of 1 January 2018–31 December 2021. Patients who had evidence of referral to the participating site strictly for specialty consultation (ie, not for patient management) or transplant evaluation or those who were enrolled in a clinical trial were excluded.

Using AASLD guidelines, we created two discrete time segments (defined in table 1): (1) from UDCA initiation through 12 months; (2) >12 months post-UDCA initiation. The two segments were not mutually exclusive; patients in segment 2 also included those who had UDCA treatment initiated prior to 1 January 2018 with >12 months observation after treatment initiation in the period of 1 January 2018–31 December 2021.

Laboratory data (ALP, total bilirubin (TB), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase were electronically extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR). Data regarding UDCA and obeticholic acid (OCA) treatment were collected via chart abstraction, including: medication start/stop dates; dosages; dose changes (increasing, decreasing and discontinued); reasons for stops and dose changes and adverse reactions (gastrointestinal problems, pruritus, toxicity) during treatment. Data regarding off-label treatment with other medications (eg, fibrates) were collected from electronically extracted outpatient prescription drug orders and pharmacy fill data. We also collected data to determine cirrhosis status using a previously validated hierarchical algorithm for identifying cirrhosis in real-world samples.10 11

Adherence to AASLD biochemical monitoring guidelines

As shown in table 1, adherence was defined in relation to published AASLD guidelines for patient monitoring based on liver biochemistry testing. For segment 1, adherence was defined by whether patients received recommended ALP testing at 12 months (±3 months) post-UDCA initiation. We also calculated the percentage of patients who had ITR to UDCA, using Toronto criteria (ALP >1.67 times the upper limit of normal (xULN)).2 For segment 2, adherence to guidelines was defined as the percentage of the observation time that was covered by recommended ALP testing (every 6–9 months ±1 month interval) in the subgroup of patients with sufficient follow-up. We also calculated the proportion of patients who had ITR to UDCA. For both segments, we assessed whether patients were offered second-line treatments and also noted UDCA dose changes and adverse reactions. Patients with insufficient follow-up in any given time segment (<3 months in segment 1 or <9 months in segment 2) were excluded from adherence analysis for that time segment. Given a lack of consensus regarding the most appropriate measures to assess disease progression and response to UDCA treatment, we performed secondary analyses of the proportion of patients that had ALP >2.0 xULN as well as biochemistry tests used in the Paris II criteria12 (ALP ≥1.5 xULN, AST ≥1.5 xULN or TB ≥1.0 mg/dL; online supplemental table S1).

For both segments, we performed descriptive analyses for the full patient sample and for the subsample of patients with sufficient follow-up to assess adherence to monitoring guidelines. Stratified analyses were performed to assess the impact of receipt of gastroenterology/hepatology (GI/HP) specialist care on adherence to monitoring, using generalised regression, after controlling for patient demographics (age, gender and race/ethnicity) and baseline clinical condition (abnormal ALP, cirrhosis status and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI),13 calculated after excluding liver comorbidities). We also considered possible interactions between variables and receipt of specialist care, with a significant interaction indicating that the impact of a particular variable on adherence depends on receipt of specialty care or vice versa.

Results

A total of 1756 patients were identified for the 2018–2021 period of interest. Among them, 67 (20% of newly diagnosed patients) did not receive UDCA treatment and were excluded from the sample. A total of 1689 patients with confirmed PBC were available for analysis (online supplemental figure S1); 740 were included in segment 1 and 1689 in segment 2 (patients could be included in both segments). As shown in table 2, 50%–60% of patients were 60 years or older, and almost all were women (91%). The majority of patients was white (64%–69%) but Black and Asian American/Pacific Islander patients represented 15%–16% of the sample; over one-third of patients (31%–39%) were Hispanic of any race. In general, patients with sufficient follow-up for adherence analysis were similar to those of the full sample (table 2).

Segment 1

As shown in table 2, 740 patients were included in the full segment 1 sample. Based on Toronto criteria (ALP >1.67 xULN), 90 patients in the full sample (12%) had inadequate response to UDCA treatment but only 15 of these patients (17%) were offered a second-line treatment (OCA: 11; fibrates: 4).

A total of 716 patients (97%) had sufficient observation for inclusion in the monitoring adherence analysis (table 2); characteristics of the sample closely matched those of the full sample. Rates of appropriate monitoring were low; only 52% of patients received appropriate ALP testing to ascertain response to UDCA after roughly 1 year of treatment. As shown in table 3, ALP testing rates were significantly higher among patients with specialist care compared with those without (54% vs 45%, p=0.001), as were rates of UDCA dose adjustments (21% vs 11%, p<0.01). Although less than 1% of patients reported adverse events or pruritus related to treatment, the overall rate of UDCA discontinuation was 12% and did not differ significantly between patients with and without specialist care.

For the multivariable model of factors associated with testing for UDCA response, two variables (cirrhosis at treatment initiation and receipt of specialty care) were retained in the final analysis (figure 1). Segment 2

As shown in table 2, 1689 patients were included in the full segment 2 sample, with a mean observation time of 37.8 (±20.3) months. Compared with segment 1, patients in segment 2 were older, with a larger proportion of white and non-Hispanic patients. Slightly fewer were seen by a GI/HP specialist compared with segment 1 (63% vs 69%). A total of 326 patients (19%) had ALP >1.67 xULN at the segment 2 baseline, indicating inadequate response to UDCA therapy; although only 69 received second-line therapy (OCA: 49; fibrates: 20), this was a roughly fourfold increase compared with Segment 1.

A total of 1468 patients (87%) had sufficient follow-up for inclusion in the adherence analysis (table 2). Patients in the adherence sample were slightly more likely to be seen by a specialist compared with the full sample (68% vs 63%), but patient characteristics were otherwise consistent between the two samples. As shown in table 3, patients seen by a specialist were more likely to undergo transient elastography (p<0.01). Rates of appropriate monitoring were higher in segment 2 than in segment 1 (67% vs 57%, respectively) and were significantly higher among patients receiving care from a GI/HP specialist (74% vs 51%, p<0.01). Similar to segment 1, 18% of patients had UDCA dose adjustments, with higher rates among patients with specialist care than without (21% vs 16%, p=0.02). Although only 1% of patients reported adverse events or pruritus related to treatment, the UDCA discontinuation rate was 22%.

For the multivariable model of factors associated with adherence to monitoring guidelines, two variables (CCI at index date and receipt of care by a specialist) and a variable-by-variable interaction were retained in final analysis. As shown in figure 2 and online supplemental table S1, patients seen by a GI/HP specialist were consistently more likely to receive recommended ALP testing. For patients with a CCI ≥1, specialist care was associated with consistently higher rates of monitoring (73%–78%) compared with patients with any number of comorbidities but without care from a specialist (30%–57%, p<0.001). For patients with CCI=0 (no comorbidities), specialist care was associated with significantly higher rates of monitoring compared with patients with CCIs of 0 or 1 without specialist care (65% vs 30% or 55%, p<0.0001 and 0.02, respectively). Among patients without specialist care, those with CCIs ≥1 had higher rates of monitoring compared with those with CCIs of 0.

Discussion

Using recent data drawn from 1689 US routine-care patients, we observed low rates of adherence to AASLD guidelines for (a) ALP testing to confirm response to UDCA at roughly 1 year after treatment initiation and (b) routine monitoring every 6–9 months thereafter. We observed that roughly 25%–50% of patients did not receive timely routine testing in either time segment. In segment 1, roughly one-third of patients did not have timely evaluation of their response to UDCA. Moreover, in segment 2, less than one-quarter of patients with inadequate response to treatment were offered second-line therapy.

Until recently, there were no approved treatment options for patients who failed to respond to UDCA; as a result, there was perhaps little clinical impetus to ensure that patients underwent ALP testing within a specific window after treatment initiation. However, since 2018, a number of second-line therapies have been approved, including obeticholic acid, elafibranor and seladelpar, with ongoing research on bezafibrate, fenofibrate and other treatment modalities.5 There has also been growing recognition that ‘deep’ response to treatment is associated with improved long-term outcomes14—achievement of this goal may require use of UDCA in combination of one or more second-line therapies.5 15 All of these shifts underscore the urgency of measuring ALP within the recommended 12-month window after initiation of UDCA in order to optimise patient care.

A consistent observation across both segments was the positive effect of receiving care from a GI/HP specialist, even when other factors—such as cirrhosis, age or comorbidities—impacted testing rates. Patients seen by a specialist were also more likely to undergo transient elastography assessment, and to have their UDCA dose adjusted, suggesting a relationship between increased monitoring and decisions about clinical management. However, given that roughly 30%–45% of patients in our sample were not seen by a gastroenterologist or hepatologist, opportunities clearly remain to increase education among general practitioners regarding the importance of routine ALP monitoring and/or specialist referrals for PBC, particularly now that telehealth options are more broadly available.

Our analysis has some limitations. Missing data are unavoidable in ‘real-world’ samples of patients under routine care. We have attempted to address this by using a full sample (with ‘unknowns’ included) in the multivariable analysis as well as a subsample of patients with sufficient follow-up data for the ALP monitoring analysis. Strengths of this study include our racially diverse sample of patients—including large proportions of Black, Asian and Hispanic patients—from disparate geographic areas of the US (Michigan, Southern California and Oregon). We also addressed the realities of patient care in the USA, where a strict 9 or 12-month window for appointments may not be realistic for patients or covered by insurance, by using a slightly more flexible definition of the monitoring period as well as taking total observation time into account for segment 2.

We note that 20% of patients newly diagnosed in the 2018–2021 period did not receive UDCA treatment. This is consistent with the 22% non-treatment rate we previously reported using earlier data (2006–2016) from 11 FOLD sites8 as well as from a recent study from the UK National Health Service.16 There are remarkably few studies published regarding why patients do not receive UDCA despite it being the standard first-line treatment for PBC and recommended to be initiated on diagnosis. In a recent study of patients from one FOLD site using data from 2006 to 2016, the most common reasons found for lack of UDCA treatment included financial barriers, loss to follow-up, severe decompensated disease at diagnosis and lack of referral to specialists for further evaluation.17 Future interventions are needed to target modifiable provider and patient barriers that may improve rates and timeliness of PBC diagnosis and treatment.

As shown in this large sample of US PBC patients under routine clinical care, at least one-third of patients were not undergoing biochemical monitoring within AASLD timelines. Although rates of monitoring were higher among patients seen by GI/HP specialists, there remained large proportions of patients, particularly those who were young and without serious comorbid conditions, who did not receive testing within recommended intervals. Novel solutions—possibly incorporating automated alerts to both providers and patients using the EMR—are urgently needed to improve monitoring rates across all stages of PBC care.
