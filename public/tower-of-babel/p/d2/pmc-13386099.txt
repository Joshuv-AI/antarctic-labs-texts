ABSTRACT
Key Points•CD49d regulates BCR functionality in CLL, linking integrins to cytoskeletal dynamics and antigen responsiveness.•CD49d shapes human BM infiltration patterns and patient outcomes in CLL.

Introduction

B-cell receptor (BCR) characteristics and downstream signaling pathways are essential for normal and malignant B-cell development, activation, and survival. In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), BCR characteristics and the interaction of malignant B cells with the lymphoid tumor microenvironment are key pathophysiological determinants. BCR stimulation in CLL can be triggered by exogenous antigens, autoantigens, or antigen-independent mechanisms (ie, BCR autonomous signaling).1

CD49d, the α4 subunit of the integrin very late antigen 4 (VLA-4; α4β1; CD49d/CD29), is a strong negative prognostic marker in CLL.2 VLA-4 functions as a homing factor, enabling peripheral blood (PB) CLL cells to recirculate into protective lymphoid organs,3,4 and as a retention factor, mediating their adhesion to lymphoid stromal cells. Patients with CD49d-high CLL experience reduced and/or shortened lymphocytosis when treated with Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors, leading to decreased progression-free survival.5,6 This phenomenon has been attributed to BCR stimulation within lymph nodes, which activates VLA-4 and enhances CLL cell adhesion to the supportive microenvironment despite Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibition.5,6

However, the interaction between BCR and integrins may not be unidirectional. In normal B cells, antigen recognition through formation of immune synapses (ISs) involves spreading over antigen-containing surfaces followed by contraction to gather bound antigen into a central aggregate. In situations where membrane-bound antigens bind the BCR only weakly or are presented in low density, the integrin lymphocyte function–associated antigen 1 (LFA-1) can serve as a costimulatory molecule that enhances antigen-BCR binding.7, 8, 9 Less is known about the role of VLA-4 in this context. CLL cells differ from normal B cells in their integrin use, as most CLL subgroups have dysfunctional LFA-1,10 whereas CD49d is expressed on leukemic cells in ∼40% of the patients with CLL.2

In this study, to deepen our understanding of the role of CD49d in CLL, we used in vivo models based on B-cell–specific deletion of CD49d in TCL1-transgenic (TCL1-tg) mice and primary human CLL cohorts displaying high or low CD49d expression. We found that CD49d affects bone marrow (BM) infiltration in murine models and human CLL and is essential for tissue localization in B-cell follicles. CD49d colocalized with BCR components at the CLL cell membrane, supported IS formation on antigen-presenting membranes, and potentiated BCR-induced calcium influx, converging on a focal adhesion kinase (FAK)–actin axis. Consistently, CD49d modulated BCR kinase activity and downstream BCR signaling in both murine and human CLL. Our data propose a new view on the functional role of CD49d as a negative prognostic marker in CLL, contributing to BCR kinase synchronization through organization of the actin cytoskeleton.

Methods

Patient samples

The study, conducted under institutional review board (IRB) approval numbers IRB-05-2010 and IRB-05-2015 (Centro di Riferimento Oncologico of Aviano) and number CE1933/2011 (University of Verona), included paired PB samples and BM biopsies (BMBs) from 171 patients with CLL. Diagnosis was made according to the International Workshop on Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia-National Cancer Institute criteria,11,12 and all samples were collected following informed consent as part of routine diagnostic and follow-up procedures. CD49d expression was assessed on PB samples in all cases, as reported.2,13 Time to first treatment was calculated from the time of diagnosis to the initiation of first treatment or was censored at the date of last follow-up. Time to first treatment curves were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier plots, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Morphological evaluation of the BMBs was conducted on hematoxylin and eosin–stained, formic acid–decalcified sections. The BM infiltration pattern was classified as nodular, interstitial, or diffuse, as previously described.14 Additional details are provided in the supplemental Methods, available on the Blood website. BM infiltration percentages of CD20-expressing CLL cells were quantified within BMBs’ regions of interest.14

Mouse models

All protocols were carried out in accordance with German ethical guidelines for laboratory animals and approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of the University of Freiburg and the Regierungspräsidium Freiburg under approval number G20/033 and G25/008. CD49dΔB TCL1-tg (Itga4tm1Tpa Cd19tm1[cre]Cgn Tg[Igh-V186.2-TCL1A]3Cro) mice were generated by crossing ITGA4 (CD49d gene) floxed mice (kindly provided by Thalia Papayannapoulou, Washington, D.C.) with CD19-Cre (Cd19tm1[cre]Cgn) and Eμ-TCL1-transgenic (TCL1-tg) mice. Genotyping was performed by polymerase chain reaction, and CD49d deficiency was confirmed by flow cytometry. For adoptive transfer experiments, wild-type CD45.1 C57/BL6J mice (B6.SJL-PtprcaPepcb/BoyCrl, strain 494; RRID: IMSR_CRL:494) were used as recipients. For specific experiments, triple knockout (TKO) TCL-1 cells15 were used, in which CD49d was knocked out by CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Leukemia progression was monitored by quantifying the number of CD5+CD19+ leukemic cells in PB using flow cytometry.

A murine custom-defined BCR gene signature was generated using antigen-stimulated CD49d-expressing TCL1 cells, as previously reported15 (details are reported in supplemental Methods and materials).

Real-time microscopy on planar lipid bilayers

Artificial planar lipid bilayers were assembled in fetal calf serum 2 chambers (Bioptechs) as previously described.16 TCL1-tg and CD49dΔB TCL1-tg leukemic cells (4 × 106) were purified from total splenocytes by negative selection with EasySep Mouse Pan-B cells Isolation kit (STEMCELL Technologies) and coinjected (4 × 106) into the warmed chamber (37°C) for imaging. To distinguish between genotypes within the same chamber, 1 cell type was violet-tracer–labeled (0.1μM, 10 minutes, 37°C; molecular probes). For human cells, frozen PB mononuclear cells from patients with CD49d-high and CD49d-low CLL were thawed, and CLL cells were purified by negative selection using the EasySep Human B Cell Enrichment Kit without CD43 depletion. Cells were allowed to recover for 1 hour before use, and CD49d-high and CD49d-low CLL cells were assessed separately. Immune synapse experiments with human CLL cells were approved by the Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Científicas ethics committee (code 258/2024). Cells were left in contact with lipid bilayers for 5 to 10 minutes to form ISs and were then imaged for up to 30 minutes. Confocal fluorescence (1 μm optical section), differential interference contrast, and interference reflection microscopy images were acquired in several membrane fields. Assays were performed in chamber buffer (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS], 0.5% fetal calf serum, 0.5 g/l D-glucose, 2mM magnesium chloride, and 0.5mM calcium chloride). Images were acquired on an Axiovert LSM 510 META microscope (Zeiss) at 40×. For F-actin analysis, cells were left in contact with bilayers containing intracellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1)/surrogate antigen (su-Ag) for 30 minutes, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) at 37°C for 10 minutes, permeabilized (PBS, 0.1% Triton-X100) for 5 minutes at room temperature and blocked (PBS, 1% bovine serum albumin, 0.02% Tween 20, and 50mM sodium cholride) overnight at 4°C, then stained with tetramethylrhodamine B isothiocyanate-conjugated phalloidin (1:1000, molecular probes) in blocking buffer at room temperature for 30 minutes. Images were acquired directly after washing (1 μm optical section at the synapse plane) on a STELLARIS 5 multispectral confocal system with a 63× oil immersion objective (Leica).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 9. Normal distribution was tested for all data sets. Normally distributed groups were compared using unpaired or paired t test and nonnormally distributed data sets using Mann-Whitney matched-pairs signed-rank test, where appropriate. Significance threshold was P < .05. For IS experiments, graphs were generated and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.2.3) software. Two-tailed unpaired Student t tests were applied. TKO engraftment experiments were compared using a nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test. TKO actin quantification was compared using a 2-way analysis of variance. Survival analysis was compared using the log-rank Mantel-Cox test.

Additional details regarding murine and human transcriptome (RNA sequencing) analysis, real-time microscopy, image data analysis, adoptive transfers, kinome profiling, InFlow microscopy, calcium influx, colocalization, and western blotting are provided in supplemental Methods.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

CD49d defines BM infiltration in the TCL1-tg model and human CLL

To understand the biological role of CD49d in CLL pathophysiology, we established a murine model harboring a B-cell–specific deletion of CD49d on the TCL1-tg background, further referred to as CD49dΔB TCL1-tg mice. These mice showed consistent ablation of CD49d in B cells across all investigated organs (Figure 1A; supplemental Figure 1). Leukemia development was monitored monthly by analyzing CD5+CD19+ cells in the PB of CD49dΔB TCL1-tg mice and TCL1-tg littermates, with a nonsignificant trend of higher leukocytosis at months 5, 6, 9, and 10 in the CD49dΔB TCL1-tg model (Figure 1B). In human CLL, the pattern of BM infiltration, that is, diffuse, interstitial or nodular, reflects different levels of CLL cell infiltration and is associated with disease aggressiveness and patient outcome.12,14 Here, we investigated a BM series of 171 patients and found that the percentage of CLL cell infiltration was significantly higher in diffuse vs interstitial or nodular infiltration patterns. Comparison of BM infiltration patterns in patients with CD49d-high expression (n = 73) to those with CD49d-low expression (n = 98) revealed that high CD49d expression was significantly associated with a diffuse infiltration pattern (Figure 1D), and survival probability was significantly reduced in patients with this pattern (Figure 1E). Consistently, survival probability was also reduced when comparing CD49d-high with CD49d-low patients (Figure 1F). In summary, both the murine model as well as human CLL rely on CD49d regarding BM infiltration.

CD49d defines the localization of leukemic cells within follicles in secondary lymphoid organs

We further assessed the engraftment capacity of CD49d-deficient leukemic cells in various organs. For this, CD45.2+ leukemic splenocytes derived from TCL1-tg or CD49dΔB TCL1-tg mice were transplanted into CD45.1 wild-type recipients, allowing identification of transplanted tumor cells using an anti-CD45.2 antibody (Figure 2A). Compared with CD49d-expressing TCL1-tg leukemic cells, recovery rates of CD49d-deficient leukemic cells from the peritoneal cavity and BM were significantly lower (Figure 2B), suggesting a role of CD49d in leukemia cell homing and/or engraftment in these organs. Homing is defined as the first and fairly rapid process of actively crossing the endothelium and transiently lodging in organs before proliferation, whereas engraftment includes migration and proliferation processes.17 To differentiate between these processes, we performed a 3-hour short-term homing experiment (Figure 2B, right). Migration to the BM was significantly lower when CD49d was lacking, suggesting that entry into this compartment depends on the integrin. Within the spleen, CD49d promoted leukemic cell localization in the white pulp and marginal zone of B-cell follicles and, to a lesser extent, the red pulp of the spleen (Figure 2C). Loss of CD49d impairs IS formation in mice and human leukemic cells, accompanied by actin cytoskeleton perturbation

Within B-cell follicles, B cells form ISs with stromal elements such as follicular dendritic cells that present antigens.18 To explore how CD49d affects this process, which initiates BCR signaling and subsequent B-cell activation, we used a biomimetic model combined with real-time confocal microscopy, as previously described.9 This model recreates the surface of an antigen-presenting follicular dendritic cell using planar artificial lipid bilayers containing glycosylphosphatidylinositol-linked ICAM-1 or biotin-linked vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)-1-Fc, as well as tethered su-Ag at different densities.19 Leukemic splenocytes derived from TCL1-tg mice were able to attach to both ICAM-1– and VCAM-1–bearing membranes and formed ISs at each su-Ag density (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 3A, B). On the contrary, CD49d-deficient cells had a significantly reduced IS frequency and area of contact on membranes containing either integrin ligand, ICAM-1 (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 3A) or VCAM-1 (supplemental Figure 3B). We fully recapitulated these findings in the human setting (Figure 3B; supplemental Figure 3C, D). To this end, we purified human CLL cells from PB mononuclear cells of CD49d-high or CD49d-low CLL samples by negative selection, ensuring that the BCR remains unstimulated. CD49d-high and CD49d-low samples were purposely chosen to harbor comparable immunoglobulin M (IgM) surface levels to reflect potentially similar BCR responsiveness despite differences in CD49d expression (data not shown). CD49d-high samples formed ISs on membranes bearing high antigen densities (300 molecules/μm2) on ICAM-1 or VCAM-1 (supplemental Figure 3D), but this was impaired in situations of low CD49d expression. Similar to the murine situation, the quantity of antigen accumulation was dependent on CD49d in human CLL samples (Figure 3B).

These data suggest a dominance of the VLA-4 (CD49d/CD29) integrin for the formation of ISs that cannot be compensated by the LFA-1 integrin, although the integrin could functionally bind its ligand in both genotypes (supplemental Figure 3E). This points to CD49d-mediated functions beyond mere adhesion. In fact, F-actin rearrangement during IS formation was clearly intact in TCL1-tg mice but strongly affected when CD49d was lacking in leukemic mouse cells (Figure 3C). This was recapitulated in representative human samples (Figure 3D). In addition, to assess the functional effect of CD49d on the actin cytoskeleton and organ infiltration, we used a highly aggressive transformed CLL model.20 The model is based on CRISPR/Cas9-edited TCL1-derived murine cells harboring biallelic disruptions of TP53, CDKN2A, and CDKN2B (TKO). In these cells, we introduced an additional CD49d KO (supplemental Figure 4A) and investigated the F-actin (phalloidin) distribution. CD49d-expressing cells contracted actin into denser, patch-like structures, whereas CD49d-deficient cells showed a diffused and disorganized F-actin pattern, with increased actin volume (supplemental Figure 4B). To test whether this phenotype is accompanied by engraftment defects, we competitively transplanted TKO and CD49d-KO TKO cells into wild-type C57BL/6J (supplemental Figure 4C). Recipients were euthanized 4 weeks after transplantation, and we observed a reduction of CD49d-deficient cells in spleen and BM, indicating a general disadvantage of CD49d-deficient TKO cells in their engraftment capacity. In summary, the presence of CD49d promotes IS formation to antigen-bearing membranes and organ infiltration.

CD49d regulates transcriptomic programs of actin regulation and BCR-associated gene signatures in murine and human CLL

To understand the potential role of CD49d in modulating the expression of BCR pathway components and actin effectors, we performed RNA sequencing analysis of CD49d-expressing leukemic splenocytes vs CD49d-KO TCL-tg leukemic splenocytes as well as CD49d-high vs CD49d-low primary CLL cells (both upon sorting the leukemic fractions). First, principal component analysis of murine transcriptomes segregated the samples into clusters depending on their genotype (supplemental Figure 5A). Gene ontology analysis revealed that cellular components linked to the actin cytoskeleton were among the top 20 upregulated gene sets in murine CD49d-expressing leukemic splenocytes compared with CD49d-deficient leukemic splenocytes (supplemental Figure 5B). Gene set enrichment analysis showed an upregulation in gene signatures associated with the actin cytoskeleton (Figure 4A), membrane embedding, and adhesion (supplemental Figure 5C). Thus, gene set enrichment of TCL1-tg leukemic splenocytes mirrors the effects observed in CD49d-high human CLL cells.

To gain deeper insights into the cross talk between CD49d and the BCR, we used several gene-set enrichment analyses using custom-defined and publicly available BCR gene signatures in both murine and human settings. First, gene set enrichment analysis on a custom-defined murine BCR gene signature15 (supplemental methods) revealed a positive upregulation in CD49d-expressing leukemic splenocytes compared with CD49d-deficient leukemic splenocytes (Figure 4C). In parallel, analysis of publicly available human BCR gene sets associated with BCR downstream signaling or antigen responsiveness21, 22, 23, 24 (indicated as BCR gene signature 1-4, Figure 4D) revealed a consistent upregulation in patients with CD49d-high CLL vs CD49d-low CLL. Although these signatures share only limited gene overlap, as illustrated by the leading-edge plot, which shows the main contributing genes of each signature (supplemental Figure 6C), they consistently point to enhanced BCR responsiveness. The heat map comparing CD49d-high CLL samples with CD49d-low CLL samples ranked the top 37 differential gene expressions within these specific human BCR gene signatures (Figure 4E), which were exemplarily validated by real-time polymerase chain reaction (supplemental Figure 6D). These data suggest a link between CD49d expression and actin and BCR gene signatures in murine and human CLL.

Human CLL cells require CD49d for functional actin polymerization and BCR signaling

The differential expression signatures suggested dysregulation of BCR-actin cooperation in the CD49d-deficient mouse model and in human CLL with low CD49d expression, which we subsequently addressed functionally. BCR responses in human CLL are highly heterogeneous, with known differences between patient subgroups harboring mutated immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region genes (IGHV) and unmutated IGHV, the latter being indicative of a negative disease prognosis.25 Here, we focused on IGHV-unmutated CLL, corresponding to the IGHV-unmutated BCR present in the TCL1-tg mouse model, and selected human CLL cells harboring similar IgM expression levels to rule out confounding factors for BCR signaling. CLL cells with or without prior BCR stimulation using anti-IgM were subjected to InFlow microscopy as previously described.26 F-actin polarization was quantified using circularity values of the fluorescence signal, with lower values indicating a more homogeneous and higher values indicating a more polarized actin distribution. Representative images of polarized and unpolarized CLL cells from patients with CD49d-high and CD49d-low expression are shown in Figure 5A. In particular, CD49d-high CLL cells had 18% mean polarized actin at baseline, which more than doubled (42%) upon BCR stimulation, whereas CD49d-low CLL cells displayed 35% prepolarized actin at baseline and a less significant increase up to 54% polarized actin upon stimulation (Figure 5A, left). Notably, baseline circularity variance differed significantly between CD49d-high and CD49d-low samples, consistent with a disorganized actin cytoskeleton in the absence of CD49d. (Figure 5A, right). CLL cells from patients with CD49d-low expression exhibited increased actin volume compared to patients with CD49d-high expression, under both basal and BCR-stimulated conditions, without dynamic remodeling upon stimulation (Figure 5B). CD49d-deficient leukemic splenocytes are antigen-unresponsive because of BCR-associated kinase alterations

Early events of BCR signaling include a tight actin reorganization, which allows BCR molecules to cluster on the membrane, thereby regulating the sequential recruitment of the kinases.27 Therefore, we determined the expression and signaling of BCR components and other related molecules dependent on CD49d expression. No significant alterations were found in IgM and IgD as well as CXCR4, CXCR5, and CCR7 expression (supplemental Figure 7) in TCL1-tg compared to CD49dΔB TCL1-tg leukemic splenocytes. Moreover, BCR sequencing revealed similar heavy- and light-chain variable diversity joining rearrangements between CD49d-deficient and CD49d-expressing leukemic cells (data not shown), also displaying an unmutated-IGHV mutational burden, as previously described.28

To get functional insights into the role of CD49d expression on BCR signaling, we performed PamGene kinome analysis of leukemic splenocytes derived from TCL1-tg or CD49dΔB TCL1-tg mice with or without prior anti-IgM stimulation. BCR stimulation led to significantly altered phosphorylation in CD49d-expressing TCL1-tg cells, with 27 upregulated phospho-sites compared with unstimulated cells (Figure 6A; supplemental Figure 8A-C). In CD49d-deficient leukemic cells, anti-IgM stimulation did not induce significant phosphorylation, with only 2 upregulated and 3 downregulated phospho-sites compared with the unstimulated condition, indicating that the lack of CD49d significantly impaired kinase activity upon BCR stimulation. Bioinformatic analysis revealed that BCR-stimulated upstream kinases in CD49d-expressing cells belong to the Src (such as Lyn and BLK) and Syk (such as Syk and ZAP70) family tyrosine kinases (Figure 6B). The relative unstimulated activity of Syk kinase was higher in CD49d-deficient cases than in CD49d-expressing cases (Figure 6C). Validation experiments revealed an elevated phospho-Syk to total Syk ratio and unresponsiveness to anti-IgM stimulation (Figure 6D; supplemental Figure 8D). To determine the impact of integrin activity on the BCR, we used a minimized CD49d ligand based on the LDV integrin-recognition motif, routinely used to engage CD49d in functional assays.31,32 CD49d engagement with this ligand enhanced anti-IgM–induced calcium responses, indicating an integrin contribution to BCR signaling (Figure 7B). To define the cytoskeletal linkage, we focused on FAK, a key mediator of integrin-actin coupling. In CLL, FAK is present as full-length (fl) and various cleaved forms with distinct functions.33 We focused on fl FAK (Y397) as the canonical integrin-binding form, binding the β-cytoplasmic tail of integrin heterodimers, interacting with integrin adaptors such as talin that connect integrins to the actin cytoskeleton. Higher basal FAK levels were found in CD49d-expressing cells compared with CD49d-deficient cells, and anti-IgM stimulation triggered fl FAK phosphorylation (Figure 7C; supplemental Figure 8G). Higher basal activation was recapitulated in human CLL expressing high CD49d (Figure 7D, supplemental Figure 8H). Short-term inhibition of FAK with defactinib, without affecting CLL viability, significantly reduced integrin-dependent, anti-IgM–induced calcium responses (Figure 7E), supporting a role for FAK as a functional linker in the CD49d-actin-BCR axis.

In summary, our data suggest that in both the TCL1-tg mouse model and human primary CLL, the CD49d integrin supports BCR signaling through a close spatial association with the BCR, involving a converging FAK-actin signaling axis downstream of the CD49d-BCR interplay.

Discussion

Integrin-dependent anchoring of B cells in tissue sites during differentiation is well established;34 however, there is a lack of understanding in how integrins contribute to antigen recognition in malignancy. Here, by taking advantage of a CD49dΔB TCL1-tg mouse model and primary human CLL variably expressing CD49d, we provide evidence that CD49d is required for BM infiltration patterns as well as for antigen-dependent BCR signaling by shaping the organization of the actin cytoskeleton.

In CLL, BM is invariably infiltrated; yet the pattern of infiltration provides information on disease aggressiveness. A diffuse infiltration pattern is associated with advanced clinical stages and predictive of poor outcomes.35 In the TCL1-tg mouse model, we identified a critical role for CD49d in determining the extent of BM infiltration and short-term homing, reflecting the function of the CD49d/CD29 heterodimer (VLA-4) as a homing and retention molecule in CLL, likely being inside out activated by CXCR4.36, 37, 38, 39 This key role was also reflected in our retrospective analysis of human BMBs. Low CD49d expression in human leukemia may preferentially direct cells to niches with particularly high VCAM-1 expression, producing a nodular or interstitial infiltration pattern, whereas high CD49d levels may support broader, more diffuse appearing tissue involvement.

Our data further show that CD49d-deficient leukemic cells exhibited a reduced capacity to enter B-cell follicles within the white pulp, a process that usually requires traversing the marginal zone in an integrin-dependent manner.40 Normal B cells are dependent on both LFA-1 and VLA-4 during this process, as this environment is exposed to a certain amount of shear stress.40 In our leukemic model, LFA-1 could not compensate for the lack of CD49d. Classical integrin profiling in CLL has identified a restricted CD29-integrin repertoire with consistent CD29 expression but variable expression of the CD49c, CD49d, CD49e subunits.41 Among these CD49d/CD29 (VLA-4) together with CD11a/CD18 (LFA-1) emerged as the functionally dominant adhesion receptors.3,42 Here, we observed that IS formation in murine and human leukemic cells, unlike in normal B cells,8 is critically dependent on CD49d rather than LFA-1, proposing that CD49d functions in CLL as a dominant and qualitatively unique.

Notably, we found a previously unrecognized role of CD49d beyond adhesion in regulating BCR responsiveness and synchronization. Central antigen aggregation during IS formation requires not only antigen recognition and adhesive interactions but also substantial actin remodeling.43 Actin structures regulate the residence time of stimulatory kinases within the BCR complex, thereby shaping the strength and duration of BCR signaling.44 Accordingly, SYK and PLCγ2 activation correlated in CD49d-high CLL samples but not in CD49d-low CLL samples, supporting a model in which loss of CD49d leads to actin dysregulation and altered kinase or phosphatase residence times, causing BCR signal desynchronization. Moreover, we found that several BCR-related downstream molecules are phosphorylated upon BCR triggering in TCL1-Tg mice but not in the CD49dΔB counterpart. Consistently, both actin-related and BCR-related RNA signatures turned out to be upregulated in CD49d-expressing mice and human CLL. Although CD19 expression was lower in the murine CD49dΔB TCL1-tg model, similar signaling defects were observed in human CLL samples in which CD19 levels were unaltered, indicating that these effects are not driven by CD19.

In this context, the colocalization of IgM and CD49d following BCR stimulation demonstrated here provides a mechanistic basis for functional interplay between the BCR and the VLA-4 integrin. Engagement of CD49d by its specific ligands potentiated BCR-induced calcium flux, at least in part through the involvement of FAK, a key mediator of integrin-actin cross talk that also contributes to BCR signaling.33 Consistent with this model, pharmacological inhibition of FAK with defactinib resulted in a marked reduction of BCR-triggered calcium influx.

Little is known about the specific role of integrins in the context of CLL transformation. One study identified transcriptomic upregulation of CD49d in a TCL1-tg mouse model with genetic overactivation of AKT.45 Consistently, we observed that CD49d KO in the highly proliferative transformed TKO cell line was sufficient to significantly reduce in vivo engraftment. Furthermore, CD49d-deficient TKO cells had significantly more actin volume in both unstimulated and stimulated conditions than CD49d-expressing cells. We attribute this effect to comparable disruptions of actin organization in the CD49d-deficient mouse model and CD49d-low human primary CLL cells.

To conclude, we propose a novel role for CD49d as a central part of IS formation between follicular dendritic cells and CLL cells. Together with our previous data,6 these findings suggest considering therapeutic exploitation either directly by targeting CD49d or by including targeting of FAK. Most importantly, we propose a revised perspective on CD49d as a negative prognostic marker in CLL, not only contributing to leukemic cell localization in lymphoid organs but also participating in BCR signaling as an actin-coordinating component during BCR-kinase recruitment.
