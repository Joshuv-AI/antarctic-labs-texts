Mediating effects of artificial intelligence readiness and self-efficacy on innovative behavior in Chinese standardized training nurses: a structural equation model study
Authors: Xiting Huang, Qingxuan Niu, Zhisheng Feng, Fang Shen, Hong Yu, Xiuhong Yuan, Xiaoxue Chen, Fang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInnovative behavior is crucial for enhancing clinical efficiency, promoting patient recovery, and advancing the nursing discipline. As an emerging group within the nursing workforce, standardized training nurses represent a key cohort for fostering innovation. Nevertheless, the psychological mechanisms underlying their innovative behavior remain insufficiently explored. In particular, the chain mediating roles of AI readiness and self-efficacy in the relationship between self-directed learning, perceived organizational support, and innovative behavior have yet to be systematically examined. This study aims to address this research gap by investigating the mediating effect of AI readiness and self-efficacy between self-directed learning ability, perceived organizational support and innovative behavior among standardized training nurses.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between January and July 2025 using convenience sampling. Participants were recruited from two tertiary hospitals in Guangdong Province and one tertiary hospital in Gansu Province. Data were collected using the following instruments: the General Information Questionnaire, Innovative Behavior Inventory, Medical AI Readiness Scale for Medical Students, General Self-Efficacy Scale, Self-Directed Learning Ability Instrument, and Perceived Organizational Support Scale. Structural equation modeling was performed using AMOS software for analyze the data.ResultsThe structural equation model exhibited a good fit. Path analysis showed that both self-directed learning and perceived organizational support were directly associated with the innovative behavior. For the self-directed learning pathway, only the sequential indirect effect through AI readiness and self-efficacy was statistically significant (small indirect effect, β = 0.018, 95%CI: 0.004, 0.048); For the perceived organizational support pathway, both the indirect effect through the sequential indirect effect through AI readiness and self-efficacy was statistically significant (small indirect effect, β = 0.014, 95%CI: 0.004, 0.045).ConclusionsThe relationships between self-directed learning, perceived organizational support, and innovative behavior are mediated by AI readiness and self-efficacy. These findings highlight that fostering AI readiness and self-efficacy could be of practical significance in improving innovative behavior of standardized training nurses.

Background

The International Council of Nurses (ICN) 2025 Congress, held in Helsinki, adopted the theme “Nursing Power to Change the World” [1]. Innovative behavior among nurses is of great significance for enhancing work efficiency, accelerating patient recovery, and advancing the nursing discipline [2]. At the congress, the ICN Student Assembly highlighted the vital role of early-career nurses in co-creating the future of health care, encouraging them to become proactive changemakers in advancing the global health goal of Universal Health Coverage [3]. In China, newly recruited nurses must complete a 2- to 3-year standardized training program to transition from theoretical knowledge to clinical practice [4]. However, this transition period is characterized by significant barriers to professional growth, including heavy workloads and cognitive rigidity, which are often associated with less favorable attitudes toward innovative behaviors [5, 6] and with higher turnover intention [7]. To address these challenges, it is critical to identify factors that can foster innovative behavior among newly recruited nurses undergoing standardized training.

Innovative behavior is influenced by a combination of individual and organizational factors [8, 9]. On the individual dimension, self-directed learning (SDL)—characterized by the individual’s initiative to identify learning needs and implement strategies [10]—has been recognized as a core skill for sustaining lifelong learning in medicine [11, 12] and recent research suggests that it is significantly correlated with innovative behavior among nursing professionals [13]. On the organizational dimension, perceived organizational support (POS) reflects employees’ belief that their organization values their contributions and well-being [14, 15]; a supportive workplace is critical for fostering innovation by providing resources and psychological safety [16, 17]. However, newly recruited nurses undergoing standardized training often encounter insufficient organizational environments, such as lack of mentorship and peer interaction, which can undermine their sense of belonging [18]. No study has yet examined how SDL and POS jointly—through mediating mechanisms—influence innovative behavior specifically in this transitional population. This study focuses on how SDL and POS may help overcome these barriers.

Drawing on self-determination theory (SDT), which posits that autonomy, competency, and relatedness drive personal growth and well-being [19], we propose two mediators representing the competence dimension. First, artificial intelligence (AI) readiness refers to preparedness of healthcare providers in terms of knowledge, skills, and attitudes to integrate AI into clinical practice [20]; a lack of AI readiness may hinder the integration of clinical information and evidence-based practice, ultimately affecting care quality [21], and prior work suggests SDL can improve AI readiness [22]. Second, self-efficacy is defined as an individual’s belief in their ability to successfully perform specific behaviors [23]; previous studies have confirmed a positive correlation between creative self-efficacy and innovative behavior among nurses [24]. Despite growing interest in nursing innovation, a significant research gap remains. Most existing studies focus on either senior clinical nurses [25] or undergraduate nursing students [26], leaving the unique transition-to-practice phase of standardized training nurses under-examined. Moreover, the underlying psychological pathway by which AI readiness and self-efficacy work together to link SDL and POS with innovative behavior among Chinese standardized training nurses has not yet been adequately examined.

Taken together, these considerations suggest that innovative behavior among standardized training nurses may be shaped by both individual and organizational factors. SDL may provide the internal drive for continuous learning and problem solving, whereas POS may offer the external resources and psychological safety needed for innovation. However, these antecedents may not necessarily translate into innovative behavior directly. In increasingly digitalized healthcare settings, AI readiness may reflect nurses’ preparedness to understand and use emerging technologies, whereas self-efficacy may reflect their confidence in applying knowledge and skills to solve clinical problems. Accordingly, a more coherent framework may be one in which SDL and POS are associated with innovative behavior through the sequential roles of AI readiness and self-efficacy.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) is a method for exploring psychological and behavioral mechanisms [27]. The application of chain mediation models in nursing innovation research has gained increasing recognition. For instance, Qian et al. successfully demonstrated a chain mediation model involving creative self-efficacy in the relationship between SDL and creativity among healthcare students [13]. Similarly, Agaoglu et al. identified the serial mediating roles of leadership and AI perception in influencing nurses’ innovative work behavior [28]. Building on these studies, we propose that the path to innovative behavior among standardized training nurses is likely sequential, requiring both technical preparedness and subsequent psychological empowerment.

Methods

Design

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted between January and July 2025. To ensure geographical diversity, participants were recruited from three tertiary hospitals in China, including two hospitals in Guangdong Province (South China) and one hospital in Gansu Province (Northwest China). A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit standardized training nurses who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. This study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional studies [29] and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinical nurses holding professional qualification certificates and continuous registration; (2) at least half a year of nursing experience to ensure possession of basic nursing skills and can perform clinical work independently; and (3) willing to participate in this study. Exclusion criteria included standardized training nurses who were: (1) undergoing refresher training; (2) absent from duty due to illness or other reasons during the survey period; (3) unable to use the electronic questionnaire platform. This criterion was justified by the fact that all participants were newly recruited nurses in tertiary hospitals, where proficiency in digital clinical systems is a mandatory job requirement; thus, the impact on selection bias for this tech-active cohort was deemed minimal; (4) self-reported concurrent participation in other studies. This was verified through a dedicated screening question at the start of the online survey to minimize respondent fatigue.

Power analysis

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 [30] to determine the minimum sample size required to test the hypothesized chain mediation models. In these models, SDL and POS were hypothesized to relate to innovative behavior through the sequential mediators of AI readiness and self-efficacy. To maintain a familywise alpha of 0.05 across the two independent hypothesis tests, a Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in a per-test alpha level of 0.025. This correction was achieved by dividing the initial alpha (0.05) by the number of independent tests (2) to control the familywise error rate and reduce the risk of Type Ⅰ errors (false positives) across the multiple mediation models. The analysis was configured for an F-test of a linear multiple regression model (R² deviation from zero). Specifically, we clarified that 3 predictors were assumed for the primary paths influencing innovative behavior (e.g. SDL/POS, AI readiness, and self-efficacy). We have explicitly stated the resulting required sample size (N = 478). Accounting for a 10% attrition buffer, the final target sample size was 526 participants. Furthermore, according to the power calculation guidelines for mediation models provided by Fritz and MacKinnon [31], a sample size of approximately 400 to 500 is typically sufficient to achieve a statistical power of 0.80 for detecting small-to-medium indirect effects. To ensure that the final valid sample size would remain sufficient for robust analysis after excluding potential invalid responses from the online platform, a total of 600 participants were initially recruited. Following data cleaning, 564 valid responses were retained for analysis.

Instruments

In the current study, total scores were calculated for each instrument and utilized for all statistical analyses, including correlation and structural equation modeling.

The general information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire, including information on socio-demographic and occupation-related data, was developed based on a comprehensive literature review and expert consultations, including gender, marital status, monthly personal income (including bonus) (Yuan), health-related condition, the highest education level, employment form, daily working hours, and average weekly night shifts.

The innovative behavior inventory (IBI)

The IBI [32, 33] consists of 20 items across 5 dimensions, including idea generation and search (6 items), plan communication and implementation (5 items), human resources access (3 items), obstacles overcoming (3 items), and clinical application (3 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 20 to 100. Higher scores reflect stronger innovative behavior. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.914.

The self-directed learning ability instrument (SDLI)

The SDLI [34] consists of 20 items across 4 dimensions, including learning motivation (6 items), planning and implementing (6 items), self-monitoring (4 items), and interpersonal communication (4 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 20 to 100. Higher scores reflect stronger ability of self-learning. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.908.

Perceived organizational support scale (POSS)

The POSS [35, 36] consists of 13 items across 2 dimensions, including affective support (10 items) and instrumental support (3 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with total score ranging from 13 to 65. Higher scores reflect stronger sense of perceived organizational support. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.940.

The medical artificial intelligence readiness scale for medical students (MAIRS-MS)

The MAIRS-MS [20, 37] consists of 22 items across 4 dimensions, including cognition (8 items), ability (8 items), vision (3 items), and ethics (3 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with total score ranging from 22 to 110. Higher scores reflect higher level of AI readiness. Although originally validated for medical students, the MAIRS-MS assesses broad domains of AI readiness, including cognition, ability, vision, and ethics, that are also relevant to standardized training nurses working in digitally evolving clinical environments. In the present study, this scale showed good internal consistency in this sample [37]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.895, further confirming its appropriateness and reliability for the participant cohort.

The general self-efficacy scale (GSES)

The GSES [38, 39] consists of 10 items. Several studies have shown that the GSES could be utilized to evaluate nursing professions’ degree of confidence in solving problems [40, 41]. The response to each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true to 4 = exactly true). The score of GSES ranges from 10 to 40 points, and the higher scores indicated greater self-efficacy. In our study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.895.

Data collection and quality control

Before initiating the formal survey, two investigators underwent standardized training to ensure consistency in data collection, implementation procedures, platform operation, and communication techniques. They then contacted the nursing managers of each department via WeChat platform. These nursing managers were provided with unified instructions and training, after which they distributed the online questionnaire to eligible nursing staff.

The questionnaire, which included detailed standardized instructions, was administered using the Questionnaire Star platform (Questionnaire Star, Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd.). Respondents were required to provide electronic informed consent before proceeding. The platform was configured to reject incomplete submissions and to ensure participants were aware that their responses would be used for publication as anonymous aggregate data. All data were checked by two investigators and then entered into Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corp).

Statistical analysis

In this study, IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (SPSS for Windows, version 26, SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA) and Amos version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) were used for statistical analyses. Categorical variables were described as numbers (n) and frequencies (%), whereas continuous variables were presented as mean (M) and standard deviation (SD). Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among the IBI, SDLI, POSS, MAIRS-MS, and GSES. Structural equation modelling was then performed to test the hypothesized relationships among the study variables.

Based on the theoretical framework, direct paths from SDL and POS to innovative behavior were specified a priori, while indirect effects through AI readiness and self-efficacy were tested as hypothesized mediation pathways. Before model estimation, statistical assumptions were assessed. Normality was verified using skewness (absolute values < 2) and kurtosis (absolute values < 7). Multicollinearity was checked using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF < 5), and multivariate outliers were screened using Mahalanobis distance. Model fit was evaluated using conventional SEM fit indices, including CMIN/DF, GFI, AGFI, PGFI, IFI, TLI, CFI, and RMSEA. Preliminary analyses showed that demographic variables (e.g., gender, education level) had no significant correlations with the main study variables; therefore, they were not included as covariates in the final SEM to maintain model parsimony. Mediation effects were tested using bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CI) based on 5,000 resamples [42], and the indirect effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% CI did not include zero. Where model refinement was considered, modification indices were consulted only for theory-consistent adjustments.

Results

A total of 600 standardized training nurses from three tertiary hospitals across Guangdong and Gansu provinces were recruited, yielding 564 valid responses after quality control (the effective response rate was 94.0%), meeting the required sample size for the study. 36 standardized training nurses were excluded due to issues such as excessively short response time (n = 18), identical or patterned answers (n = 16), and internal contradictions (n = 2). Common method bias

All data were collected from self-reported responses in the questionnaire, which introduces the potential for self-report bias. To avoid potential common method variance, an unrotated exploratory factor analysis of all measures was carried out using Harman’s single-factor test [43]. This revealed 16 common factors in total, each with an eigenvalue exceeding 1. The first factor accounted for 22.39% of the total variance, falling below the recommended threshold of 40% proposed by Podsakoff et al. [44]. While this suggests that no single factor accounted for the majority of the variance, we acknowledge the limitations of Harman’s single-factor test in terms of sensitivity. Therefore, the possibility of common method bias cannot be completely dismissed given the self-reported nature of the survey.

This study recruited 554 women (98.2%) and 10 men (1.8%), with the majority being married (82.6%). The average monthly personal income was predominantly between 3,000 and 9,000 yuan (89.3%). More than half of the participants held an associate degree (junior college) as their highest education level (54.8%). 442 nurses were employed under contract (78.4%). Structural equation modeling of factors influencing innovative behavior of standardized training nurses

The hypothetical model demonstrated a good fit to the data (CMIN/DF = 2.678, GFI = 0.946, AGFI = 0.924, PGFI = 0.668, IFI = 0.922, TLI = 0.901, CFI = 0.921, RMSEA = 0.055). The results indicated that the critical ratios (C.R.) for each effect ranged from 0.819 to 6.870, all exceeding the recommended C.R. threshold of 0.60 [46], indicating satisfactory internal consistency. In the structural model (Table 3), the direct path from MAIRS-MS to IBI was not statistically significant (β = 0.056, P = 0.413), whereas GSES was positively associated with IBI (β = 0.206, P < 0.001).

As shown in Table 4, the total effect of SDLI on IBI was statistically significant (β = 0.563, P = 0.003), whereas the direct effect of SDLI on IBI became non-significant after the mediators were included in the model (β = 0.059, P = 0.318). In addition, the indirect effects through MAIRS-MS alone (β = 0.028, P = 0.511) and through GSES alone (β = 0.013, P = 0.414) were not statistically significant. By contrast, the sequential effect of MAIRS-MS and GSES was statistically significant (β = 0.018, 95%CI: 0.004 to 0.048, P = 0.005), accounting for 31.97% of the total effect. According to standard benchmarks, this represented a small indirect effect. Therefore, this pathway should be interpreted cautiously as a modest but statistically supported associative pathway rather than a large practical effect. Taken together, these findings suggest that the association between SDL and innovative behavior may operate primarily through the sequential pathway involving AI readiness and self-efficacy.

For the POSS pathway, the direct effect on IBI remained statistically significant (β = 0.828, 95%CI: 0.016 to 0.0168, P = 0.023). The indirect effect through MAIRS-MS alone was not statistically significant (β = 0.021, P = 0.408), whereas both the simple indirect effect through GSES alone (β = 0.047, 95%CI: 0.017 to 0.097, P = 0.004) and the sequential indirect effect through MAIRS-MS and GSES (β = 0.014, 95%CI: 0.004 to 0.045, P = 0.002) were statistically significant. The total effect of POSS on IBI was also statistically significant (β = 0.910, 95%CI: 0.684 to 1.226, P = 0.002). Although these indirect effects were also small in magnitude, they should not be overinterpreted as indicating large practical impact, as they likely reflect only part of a broader set of factors associated with innovative behavior among standardized training nurses. Overall, these results indicate that POS was associated with innovative behavior both directly and indirectly through the sequential pathway involving AI readiness and self-efficacy. Discussion

Main findings

This study investigated standardized training nurses from three tertiary hospitals in Guangdong and Gansu provinces. Four main findings emerged from the analysis. First, POS remained directly associated with innovative behavior in the mediation model. Second, for the SDL pathway, only the sequential indirect effect through AI readiness and self-efficacy was statistically significant after the mediators were included. Third, for the POS pathway, the simple indirect effect through self-efficacy alone was statistically significant. Fourth, the sequential indirect effect through AI readiness and self-efficacy was also statistically significant for the POS pathway. Taken together, these findings suggest that POS may be associated with innovative behavior both directly and indirectly, whereas the association between SDL and innovative behavior may operate primarily through the sequential pathway involving AI readiness and self-efficacy.

Relationship between self-directed learning and innovative behavior

Our findings confirmed a direct, positive association with SDL on the innovative behavior of standardized training nurses, supporting hypothesis H1a. This result is consistent with the existing studies [13, 28, 47]. A potential explanation is that SDL enhances critical thinking and problem-solving abilities [48], fostering a growth mindset, whereby individuals believe creativity can be cultivated through education and practice. Research by Chinese scholar Guo indicated that nurses with strong SDL ability exhibit improved critical thinking, more comprehensive problem analysis, and greater creativity in addressing clinical challenges, thereby facilitating innovative behaviors [49]. However, our study found that neither technical readiness (AI readiness) nor psychological confidence (self-efficacy) alone could drive an innovative behavior. (H1b and H1c were rejected). As Agaoglu et al. [28] noted, academic support serves as a moderating factor in the relationship between the application of AI and digital literacy. This indicates that beyond individual autonomous learning of AI technology, effective integration of educational resources of AI technology in the learning environment can help individuals overcome the difficulties they encounter in the learning process and provide opportunities to enhance their innovative thinking ability. Our results suggest that self-efficacy serves as a critical psychological bridge in this process. Without the confidence to apply AI-related knowledge to solve clinical problems, a nurse’s readiness may remain purely theoretical. This aligns with the principle that knowledge without confidence yields no behavioral change. Therefore, the development of innovative behavior requires a two-stage process: first, building the technical foundation (AI readiness), and second, fostering the psychological conviction (self-efficacy) to utilize those tools in clinical innovation.

Relationship between perceived organizational support and innovative behavior

In addition, POS was positively associated with innovative behavior among standardized training nurses, supporting the research hypothesis H2a. This result is consistent with Amabile et al. [8]. Beyond this direct association, the present study identified two statistically significant indirect pathways. Specifically, self-efficacy alone mediated the association between POS and innovative behavior, and the sequential pathway through AI readiness and self-efficacy was also statistically significant (H2c and H2d confirmed). By contrast, the indirect pathway through AI readiness alone was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that organizational support may be associated with innovative behavior not only by providing resources and psychological safety directly, but also by strengthening nurses’ confidence in their ability to address clinical challenges and by supporting the development of technology-related preparedness that can be translated into practice through self-efficacy. POS can foster nurses’ psychological safety within a supportive organizational culture, thereby directly promoting nurses’ innovation [50]. Similar to the situation of nursing students in the Philippines, social support and self-efficacy can help nurses better cope with the identity transformation from school learning to clinical work in hospitals. Those with strong self-efficacy have higher psychological resilience, can self-regulate negative emotions such as stress, and are capable to complete their training courses [40]. However, our study also found that individuals with a sense of collective belonging (perceived organizational support) and technical support (AI readiness) also failed to promote innovative behavior (H2b were rejected). As Alruwaili et al. [51] noted, the integration of new technologies requires both technical preparedness and the psychological confidence to navigate professional challenges. This suggests that in clinical nursing, technical preparedness (AI readiness) is a necessary but insufficient driver of innovation in isolation. Therefore, standardized training nurses should cultivate a growth mindset alongside clinical skills, so as to effectively navigate professional challenges with AI tools [52]. This approach encourages nurses to view AI integration as a learning opportunity rather than a barrier, thereby enhancing self-efficacy and preparing them for practice in a technology-driven healthcare environment.

The chain-mediating role of AI readiness and self-efficacy

The central finding of this study was the confirmation of the chained mediating role of AI readiness and self-efficacy. Specifically, only the full sequential chain mediation (SDL→AI Readiness→Self-Efficacy→Innovative Behavior) was significant, supporting hypothesis H1d and confirming a complete sequential chain mediation model. The non-significant direct path from MAIRS-MS to IBI (β = 0.056, P = 0.413) was also theoretically important. In other words, simply possessing AI-related knowledge (AI readiness) is insufficient for fostering innovation without the subsequent development of self-efficacy suggesting a “knowledge-to-action” gap in the nursing context. While AI readiness provides the technical and cognitive “raw materials” for innovation, in the complex and high-stakes clinical environment, knowledge alone does not automatically translate into behavioral change. As suggested by our results, self-efficacy serves as the critical psychological bridge in this process. This suggests that, in the AI era, the association between SDL and innovative behavior may operate through a sequential pathway involving AI readiness and then self-efficacy, a pattern that is consistent with SDT. A nurse’s proactive learning (SDL) must first improve preparedness for new technology (AI readiness), which in turn strengthens confidence in one’s capabilities (self-efficacy), ultimately facilitating innovative behavior.

In the pathway from POS, this study confirmed both the simple mediating effect of self-efficacy alone (POS→Self-Efficacy→Innovative Behavior) and the chain mediating effect through AI readiness and self-efficacy (POS→AI Readiness→Self-Efficacy→Innovative Behavior), whereas the indirect pathway through AI readiness alone was not statistically significant. These findings support hypotheses H2d and H2c, respectively, while H2b was not supported. Compared with the Nurse Residency Programs in the United States [53], it is more mandatory for newly recruited nurses in China to participate in standardized training program, which may strengthen the internal validity of our findings in the Chinese context. According to the SDT, POS can strengthen the relatedness between standardized training nurses and hospital. High-quality leader-member exchange encourages mutual concern for work-related and personal interests [54]. Support from the nursing managers and peers may be associate with greater confidence among standardized training nurses in addressing complex clinical problems and with higher engagement in nursing innovation, which is consistent with the findings reported by Yan [55]. POS may also be associated with innovative behavior through the full pathway involving AI readiness and self-efficacy, as proficiency with AI tools enables standardized training nurses to analyze complex data, promotes interdisciplinary collaboration, and supports clinical innovation. These experiences enhance nurses’ confidence in their talents and career potential, aligning with the findings of Labrague et al. [56]. Based on the finding, we suggest that hospital managers can integrate resources to support innovation and provide positive feedback on the innovative behaviors of standardized training nurses. Such measures can help foster an innovation-friendly atmosphere and encourage these nurses to actively participate in optimizing work processes, ultimately contributing to improved nursing quality. Nevertheless, the statistically significant indirect effects identified in this study were small in magnitude and should therefore be interpreted as modest associative pathways rather than strong practical effects in isolation.

Implications for nursing practice

This study suggests that SDL and POS were associated with innovative behavior through pathways involving AI readiness and self-efficacy. However, the statistically significant indirect effects identified in this study were small in magnitude and should not be interpreted as indicating large practical impact at the individual level. Rather, these findings suggest that AI readiness and self-efficacy may constitute only part of a broader set of individual and organizational factors associated with innovative behavior among standardized training nurses.

Based on our findings, we recommend a multi-faceted strategy to foster innovation among standardized training nurses. For clinical practice, mentors may focus on strengthening self-efficacy by providing positive reinforcement, constructive feedback, and opportunities for mastery experiences. For educational programming, training curricula may incorporate problem-based learning and AI-related content to support the development of both SDL and AI readiness [57]. For organizational and policy-level planning, a supportive climate, protected time, appropriate resources, and recognition for innovative behavior are more likely to emerge [58]. Although the indirect effects were small, they may still be meaningful in nursing education and workforce development when considered cumulatively at the organizational level and in combination with broader interventions.

Limitations

Several limitations to this study must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents any causal relationships of the observed relationships. Although mediation pathways were examined statistically, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, and no temporal sequence can be established among the variables. Longitudinal or prospective studies are needed to further examine whether the proposed pathways are supported over time. Second, this study used convenience sampling from only three tertiary hospitals in two provinces (Guangdong and Gansu), which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution and cannot be overgeneralized to all standardized training nurses in China. Future studies should employ random sampling across a broader range of regions to validate these findings. Third, the sample was highly homogenous in terms of gender (98.2% women). Although this distribution reflects the demographic profile of the nursing workforce in the study context, it may limit the applicability of the findings to a more diverse nursing population. In addition, the use of self-reported questionnaire collection may introduce a common method bias. Although Harman’s single-factor test suggested no serious common method bias, its sensitivity is limited. Future studies should consider more rigorous methodological approaches to further assess and reduce this potential bias.

Conclusion

This study shows that SDL and POS were positively associated with innovative behavior among standardized training nurses. It may represent a potential associative pathway underlying these relationships, especially in the sequential pathway involving AI readiness and self-efficacy. From a practical perspective, nurse educators may strengthen AI-related and problem-based learning in training programs, hospital leaders may foster supportive environments that build self-efficacy and encourage innovation, and policymakers may provide institutional support and resources that enable early-career nurses to engage in innovation more effectively.
