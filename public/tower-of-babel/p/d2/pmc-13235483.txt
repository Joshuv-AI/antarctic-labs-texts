MSLN expression predicts a high risk of EMD in AML by promoting cell adhesion and metastasis via interaction with MUC16
Authors: Lan Wang, Qian Zhan, Jing Luo, Ju Li, Chaocan He, Yan Zhu, Caixia Pei, Yuting Chen, Na-Na Tang, Jiaxiu Yin, Li Su, Jiaojiao Chen, Mingling Xie, Lin Liu, Xiao-Hua Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•MSLN expression is an independent risk factor for EMD in patients with AML.•MSLN activates the PI3K signaling pathway and upregulates CD56 and NCAM2 via interaction with MUC16 in AML cells.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous group of clonal hematopoietic progenitor cell disorders in the bone marrow. Despite improvement in standard chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), and the development of novel targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the prognosis of patients with AML remains unsatisfactory, with 5-year survival of ∼30%.1,2 Extramedullary disease (EMD) is a common entity in AML, with an incidence ranging from ∼2% to 30.5%.3,4 Patients with AML and EMD usually have a low complete remission rate and a short overall survival (OS).5, 6, 7, 8 However, the risk factors for EMD in patients with AML and the molecular mechanisms are poorly understood.

Emerging data revealed that mesothelin (MSLN) was aberrantly expressed in 36% of pediatric and 14% of adult patients with AML determined by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and particularly in those with EMD.9 MSLN is a 40 kDa glycophosphatidylinositol–linked glycoprotein present on a restricted set of mesothelial cells but is particularly highly expressed in malignant mesothelioma, ovarian cancer, and pancreatic cancer.10, 11, 12 MSLN and its ligand, mucin 16 (MUC16; cancer antigen 125), are frequently coexpressed in these cancer types, and their binding possibly contributes to tumor invasion and metastasis.11,13

The process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) has been linked to solid tumor functions such as migration, blood intravasation, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy.14,15 However, unlike solid tumors, the development of EMD is related to cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) in acute leukemia, such as CD56, CD11b, MAC-1, and integrins.16, 17, 18 Nevertheless, the identification of an effective and reliable predictor for EMD remains a critical unmet need in clinical practice. It is imperative to investigate the role of MSLN in AML, and potential utility as a biomarker for EMD risk.

To address this question, we assessed MSLN expression in patients with AML and determined the clinical relevance of EMD. The functional implications and specific molecular mechanisms underlying the binding and interaction between MSLN and MUC16 in the development of EMD were also explored.

Material and methods

Patients

This study enrolled adult patients aged ≥18 years who were newly diagnosed with AML (n = 118) and controls (healthy donors, n = 5; those with iron deficiency anemia, n = 20) at The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between January 2022 and February 2024. Follow-up data were collected until death, loss to follow-up, or 31 December 2024. AML diagnosis was based on the current 2022 World Health Organization criteria and the 1976 French-American-British classification (FAB). AML risk stratification was performed in accordance with the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2022 guidelines. Patients with acute promyelocytic leukemia and isolated extramedullary involvement without bone marrow involvement were excluded.

EMD definition

EMD is the presence of leukemic aggregates in nonhematopoietic tissues, leading to the distortion of normal tissue architecture.3 It can be diagnosed with clinical, pathological, or radiological evidence at any time after an initial AML diagnosis. The primary diagnostic method of EMD is based on pathology. If no histopathological findings can be used to definitively diagnose EMD, then the diagnosis is confirmed through radiology and clinical features. Patients whose extramedullary manifestations are restricted to hematopoietic organs (the spleen, liver, and lymph nodes) and those with isolated EMD without bone marrow infiltration were excluded. EMD can be classified into 3 types4,6: central nervous system (CNS) involvement, serosal involvement, and tissue involvement.

Flow cytometry for cell surface MSLN

The surface expression of MSLN on cells was assessed by multidimensional flow cytometry using bone marrow samples from patients newly diagnosed with AML. The cell suspension in phosphate-buffered saline was incubated with 1 μL of monoclonal anti-MSLN allophycocyanin-conjugated antibody (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Other monoclonal antibodies against myeloid-associated antigens, such as CD13 and CD33, lymphoid-associated antigens, such as CD7 and CD19, and lineage-nonspecific antigens, such as CD34, CD38, and CD56 were used to characterize the immunophenotype of leukemia cells. The samples were incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes in the dark and then measured by multidimensional flow cytometry. MSLN positivity was defined as blast cells with MSLN expression of ≥5% of all leukemic blasts.

Cell lines and cell culture

A total of 4 mixed lineage leukemia (MLL/KMT2A)-rearranged (MLL-r) AML cell lines, namely, NOMO-1, MOLM-13, MV-4-11, and THP-1, were purchased from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. NOMO-1, MOLM-13, and THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum, and MV-4-11 cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The ovarian cancer cell line OVCAR-3 was cultured in RPMI-1640 (NaHCO3 1.5 g/L; iCell, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10 μg/mL insulin. All the cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Animal model

Non-obese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficiency (NOD/SCID) mice (female, 6-7 weeks old, 20-21 g) were purchased from Cradl (Beijing, China). Eight mice were randomized into 2 groups (n = 4 in each group), the MSLN-overexpressing (MSLN-OE) group and the control group, using stably transfected MOLM-13 cells. The cells were suspended in phosphate-buffered saline and injected via the tail vein at 3 × 106 cells per mouse. The mice were observed for at least 7 to 8 weeks until they presented extramedullary manifestations or life-threatening complications. The appetite and activity of the mice were recorded and their body weights were measured once a week. On the day of euthanasia, the proportion of green fluorescent protein–labeled leukemia cells in peripheral blood was quantified using flow cytometry. Furthermore, the brain, femurs, spleens, livers, kidneys, and tumors were collected for hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunohistochemistry staining.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8 (version 8.0.1, GraphPad Software) and SPSS (version 20.0, IBM Corp). Patients were stratified into MSLN-positive and MSLN-negative groups using a predetermined cutoff value of 5.33. This threshold, representing a 10-fold increase over the mean MSLN mRNA expression level observed in healthy individuals, was established to define MSLN positivity in patients with AML. The correlation between MSLN expression and clinicopathologic factors in patients with newly diagnosed AML was assessed using the χ2 test or Mann-Whitney U test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. The prognostic significance of EMD risk and clinicopathologic features was determined using a Cox proportional hazard regression model. Variables with P value <.05 in univariate analyses were entered into the multivariate analyses. The experimental data are presented as the means ± SEM and individual values unless otherwise indicated. Comparisons between 2 groups were conducted using an unpaired t test. Comparisons of multiple groups were performed using 1-way analysis of variance with the Dunnett multiple comparisons test. The results with P value <.05 were considered statistically significant (∗); ∗∗P < .01; ∗∗∗P < .001; ∗∗∗∗P < .0001; nonsignificant P values: P > .05.

Additional methods are provided in the Supplemental Materials, including Supplemental Figures 1-4 and Supplemental Methods.

This study was reviewed and approved by the ethics board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, China (number 2023-383-02). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the National Institutes of Health, and all animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the animal care and welfare committee of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC-CQMU-2024-0739).

Results

MSLN was highly expressed in AML patients with EMD

MSLN transcript levels were elevated in bone marrow samples from patients with de novo AML (n = 118) and controls (n = 25). The MSLN transcript levels were significantly higher in the patients with AML than controls (P < .05; Figure 1A). We subsequently conducted linear correlation analysis, which revealed a significant positive correlation between MSLN and MUC16 transcript levels in patients with AML (P < .0001; supplemental Figure 1). Furthermore, MSLN was highly expressed in a subset of patients with AML, particularly those with FAB M4/M5 subtypes and MLL-r (Figure 1B), which are consistent with reported data of MSLN expression distribution in AML subtypes determined by RNA-seq.9 The characteristics of the patients with AML are summarized in Table 1. In our cohort, we confirmed that MSLN transcript levels were elevated in patients with EMD compared with those without EMD (P < .01; Figure 1C).

The MSLN transcriptional and protein levels were relatively high in NOMO-1 (MLL-AF9), which is considered the only MSLN-expressing AML cell line. Nevertheless, there was no MSLN expression in the other 3 MLL-r AML cell lines, namely, MOLM-13, MV-4-11, and THP-1 (Figure 1D-E).

MSLN expression is an independent risk factor for EMD

Among 118 patients with newly diagnosed AML, 40 patients (33.90%) were MSLN positive at transcript levels, and 27 (22.88%) presented with EMD after initial diagnosis. Among these 27 patients with EMD, 55.6% were female, and the median age was 50.0 years (range, 18.0-81.0). Of 27 patients, 17 (62.96%) were diagnosed pathologically. As shown in Figure 2A, most of these 27 patients had tissue involvement (16/118 [13.56%]), followed by CNS involvement (10/118 [8.47%]) and serosal involvement (6/118 [5.08%]). The MSLN expression levels were not significantly different among these 3 different involvement types (Figure 2B).

Univariate analyses using a Cox regression model (Table 2) revealed that MSLN positivity at transcript levels (hazard ratio [HR], 4.55; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.04-10.17; P < .001), MSLN positivity of ≥5% on flow cytometry (HR, 6.65; 95% CI, 2.98-14.85; P < .001), age ≤60 years (HR, 3.38; 95% CI, 1.28-8.93; P = .014), FAB M4/M5 subtypes (HR, 2.43; 95% CI, 1.09-5.41; P = .030), hepatosplenomegaly (HR, 2.84; 95% CI, 1.31-6.16; P = .008), and MLL-r (HR, 4.59; 95% CI, 1.99-10.57; P < .001) were significant predictors of EMD. Multivariate analyses confirmed that MSLN positivity at transcript levels (HR, 3.43; 95% CI, 1.44-8.20; P = .006) and MSLN positivity on flow cytometry (HR, 3.28; 95% CI, 1.37-7.86; P = .008) at diagnosis were independent risk factors for EMD. However, Kaplan-Meier analyses revealed no significant differences in OS between the groups with positive and negative MSLN transcript expression (Figure 2C) or between the patients with and without EMD (Figure 2D).

MSLN promotes cell proliferation and regulates the cell cycle in AML cells

We used a lentivirus to knock down MSLN in the MSLN-expressing AML cell line NOMO-1, and used a lentivirus to overexpress MSLN in the AML cell lines MOLM-13 and MV-4-11. Transfection efficiencies were assessed by fluorescence microscopy, with >90% fluorescence efficiency observed in the abovementioned cells. The knockdown and overexpression efficiencies were then verified via quantitative polymerase chain reaction and western blot analyses (Figure 3A).

To investigate the effect of MSLN on AML cell growth, we conducted apoptosis, cell cycle, proliferation, and colony formation assays. CCK-8 assays (Figure 3B) showed that MSLN knockdown in NOMO-1 cells resulted in a significant decrease in the cell growth rate (P < .01), and MSLN overexpression markedly increased the cell growth rates of MOLM-13 (P < .0001) and MV-4-11 cells (P < .001). Consistently, 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays (Figure 3C-D) verified that MSLN knockdown impaired the cell proliferation capacity of NOMO-1 cells (short hairpin control [shCtrl], 62.4% ± 1.6% vs shMSLN-1, 38.1% ± 1.7% [P < .0001]; shMSLN-2, 40.3% ± 1.7% [P < .0001]), and MSLN overexpression facilitated the proliferation of MOLM-13 (44.0% ± 3.7% vs 67.4% ± 5.1%; P < .01) and MV-4-11 cells (38.0% ± 2.5% vs 64.7% ± 2.9%; P < .001). Methylcellulose-based colony formation assays (Figure 3E-F) demonstrated that MSLN knockdown significantly reduced the colony formation of NOMO-1 cells (shCtrl, 112.3 ± 15.5 vs shMSLN-1, 51.33 ± 6.5 [P < .001]; shMSLN-2, 71.7 ± 7.6 [P < .01]), and MSLN overexpression led to a large increase in the colony formation of MOLM-13 (15.5 ± 3.5 vs 58.3 ± 9.7; P < .01) and MV-4-11 cells (50.0 ± 3.0 vs 215.0 ± 16.7; P < .0001).

However, neither forced expression nor knockdown of MSLN significantly affected the percentage of apoptotic cells (supplemental Figure 2A). The forced expression of MSLN in MOLM-13 and MV-4-11 cells promoted G1 to S cell cycle progression, and MSLN knockdown in NOMO-1 cells caused significant accumulation in the G1 phase and a reduction in the S phase (Figure 3G). These findings suggest that MSLN stimulates the G1/S transition and facilitates the proliferation of AML cells.

MSLN promotes metastasis and invasion in AML

We performed Matrigel-based invasion assays to determine the metastatic ability of AML cells. After shRNA was used to knock down MSLN (shMSLN), the number of metastatic AML cells markedly decreased in MSLN-knockdown NOMO-1 cells compared with control cells (shCtrl, 378.3 ± 58.2 vs shMSLN-1, 109.7 ± 39.1 [P < .01]; shMSLN-2, 129.7 ± 23.5 [P < .01]) and strongly increased in MSLN-OE MOLM-13 (110.0 ± 27.2 vs 840.3 ± 115.5; P < .001) and MV-4-11 cells (70.3 ± 13.8 vs 587.0 ± 35.0; P < .0001; Figure 4A-B). Transwell assays showed that MSLN knockdown significantly reduced the migratory rate of NOMO-1 cells (shCtrl, 100% vs shMSLN-1, 67.5% ± 9.1% [P < .01]; shMSLN-2, 65.8% ± 8.3%; P < .01), and MSLN overexpression substantially increased the migratory rates of MOLM-13 (100% vs 126.6% ± 7.0%; P < .01) and MV-4-11 cells (100% vs 141.0% ± 16.6%; P < .05; Figure 4C). These results indicate that MSLN promotes the metastasis and invasion of AML cells in vitro.

Animal experiments were conducted using non-obese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficiency (NOD/SCID) mice to further investigate whether MSLN could influence AML dissemination in vivo. Approximately 40 days after the intravenous engraftment of MOLM-13 cells (MSLN-OE and control), 2 of 4 mice (50%) in the MSLN-OE group presented extramedullary manifestations. One mouse developed a facial tumor on day 37, and another mouse developed hindlimb paralysis on day 40. One mouse from each group died of infection on days 38 and 40, and the remaining 6 mice were euthanized on day 42. The mice in the MSLN-OE and control groups displayed signs of lethargy, poor appetite, decreased activity, and weight loss after 3 weeks. However, the mice in the MSLN-OE group experienced more severe weight loss than those in the control group (Figure 4D). The spleen and liver weights (Figure 4E) and the white blood cell count (Figure 4F) were markedly elevated in the MSLN-OE group compared with those in the control group.

As shown in Figure 4G, hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed varying degrees of blast cell infiltration in the tissues. We confirmed that the extent of AML dissemination was greater in the brain, bone marrow, spleen, liver, and kidney of MSLN-OE mice than in those of control mice. Moreover, immunohistochemical staining of these tissue sections confirmed that MSLN and MUC16 expression levels were dramatically increased in MSLN-OE mice (Figure 4H-I). Immunohistochemical analysis of a facial tumor biopsy from an MSLN-OE mouse demonstrated infiltration by leukemia cells, with positive CD45 staining (Figure 4J). Facial tumors biopsies verified that the expression levels of MSLN and MUC16 were greater in the central leukemia-infiltrated region than in the surrounding lymphocytic region (Figure 4K-L).

MSLN promotes AML cell adhesion by binding with MUC16

We used a lentivirus to overexpress MSLN, which led to the upregulation of MUC16 expression at the transcriptional and protein levels (Figure 5A-B). We then used a lentivirus to knock down MSLN, which also led to the downregulation of MUC16 expression at the transcriptional and protein levels (supplemental Figure 3). In contrast, we used small interfering RNA to knock down MUC16, which led to the downregulation of MSLN expression at the transcriptional and protein levels. These results indicate a positive correlation between MSLN and MUC16 expression levels in AML cells (Figure 5A-B). The binding between MSLN and MUC16 was examined by coimmunoprecipitation. Endogenous MSLN was coimmunoprecipitated with ectopically expressed MUC16 in NOMO-1 cells. Moreover, endogenous MUC16 was coimmunoprecipitated with ectopically expressed MSLN (Figure 5C).

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of our RNA-seq data showed that MSLN overexpression modulates focal adhesion (Figure 5D). To confirm the RNA-seq analysis, we performed immunofluorescence and observed that MSLN and MUC16 were primarily colocalized on the membranes of AML cells (Figure 5E). The MSLN-MUC16 interaction promoted the formation of cell aggregates, with their coexpression being upregulated at the AML cell-cell binding sites in the NOMO-1 cells (Figure 5F).

MSLN activates the PI3K pathway to modulate CD56 and NCAM2 expression through its interaction with MUC16

Analysis of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and differentially expressed genes from the RNA-seq data revealed that MSLN influences the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway and modulate CAMs (supplemental Figure 2B). Next, we investigated whether MSLN modulates PI3K pathway activity and the expression of the neural CAMs CD56 and NCAM2 in a MUC16-dependent manner in AML cells.

Western blot analyses demonstrated that the forced expression of MSLN increased MUC16 expression levels. Moreover, MSLN overexpression strongly increased the phosphorylation level of PI3K compared with those in the control (Figure 6A), but did not affect the phosphorylation level of protein kinase B (AKT; supplemental Figure 4A). These results indicate that MSLN upregulation results in an increased levels of phosphorylated PI3K (p-PI3K) and MUC16 expression in AML cells.

To investigate the interaction between MSLN and MUC16, we used small interfering RNA targeting MUC16 (siMUC16) to knock down MUC16 in MOLM-13 and MV-4-11 cells with stable MSLN overexpression. Western blot analyses revealed that MUC16 silencing decreased the expression levels of MSLN, p-PI3K, CD56, and NCAM2 (Figure 6B), but had no effect on p-AKT (supplemental Figure 4B). These data suggest that MSLN activates the PI3K signaling pathway and upregulates CAMs by interacting with MUC16 in an AKT-independent mechanism.

To further explore whether a PI3K pathway inhibitor could influence MSLN and CAMs expression levels, we examined the viability of MOLM-13 and MV-4-11 cells cultured with the PI3K pathway inhibitor LY294002, and the IC50 concentration was calculated (Figure 6C). When the IC50 concentration of LY294002 was used, impeding the PI3K pathway abolished p-PI3K and p-AKT, and reduced the expression levels of MSLN, CD56, and NCAM2. However, the reduction in p-AKT levels remained unaffected by MSLN expression levels, further supporting the role of MSLN in activating the PI3K pathway, which is not related to the canonical substrate AKT (Figure 6D).

MSLN interacts with MUC16 to promote metastasis and adhesion in AML cells

On the basis of the aforementioned results, we hypothesized that the interaction of MSLN and MUC16 in AML might affect cell proliferation, adhesion, and metastasis. To validate this speculation, we used siMUC16 to suppress MUC16 expression in stable MSLN-OE MOLM-13 and MV-4-11 cell lines. The CCK8 assay results showed no significant difference between the MSLN-OE and siMUC16-1 groups or between the MSLN-OE and siMUC16-2 groups (supplemental Figure 2C).

Despite the remarkable increase in the number of metastatic leukemia cells in the MSLN-OE group, the number of metastatic MOLM-13 and MV-4-11 cells substantially decreased after MUC16 interference (Figure 7A). As shown in Figure 7B, MSLN upregulation enhanced cell cluster formation, and MUC16 interference had the opposite effect. After MUC16 interference in the MSLN-OE group, confocal immunofluorescence showed that the fluorescence associated with AML cell-cell adhesion was significantly weakened and that many cell clusters were dispersed into suspension (Figure 7C).

Discussion

The definition of EMD in AML has historically been ambiguous, with terms such as EMD, extramedullary involvement, extramedullary infiltrates, extramedullary leukemia, and extramedullary AML tumor used interchangeably to describe this complication.3,5,6,19 Common sites of EMD include the skin, CNS, bone, and gastrointestinal system.4,19,20 In fact, EMD has been observed at nearly all anatomic sites except the gallbladder and appendix.21 In this study, we found that the incidence of EMD was 22.88% among adult patients with newly diagnosed AML, and tissue involvement was the most common. Moreover, more than half (55.56%) of patients’ biopsies were obtained from cerebrospinal fluid, pleural fluid, and ascitic fluid, suggesting that EMD probably develops in MSLN-expressing tissues, such as the mesothelium of the pleura, pericardium, and peritoneum, usually leading to effusion.

Recent studies have shown that MSLN, a cell surface glycoprotein, may be correlated with EMD in AML.9,22 As previously reported, by binding with MUC16 in solid tumors such as ovarian carcinoma and pancreatic cancer, MSLN activates multiple downstream signaling pathways, including the SGK3/FOXO3, p38/MAPK, and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) pathways, and then promotes cell proliferation, metastasis, and invasion.13,23,24 In our study, both flow cytometric and transcriptional MSLN expression at diagnosis demonstrated clinical utility in screening patients with high-risk EMD. Mechanistically, MSLN expression facilitates EMD in AML by promoting cellular adhesion and cell metastasis, leading to more aggressive behaviors in leukemia cells. Collectively, these findings potentially support MSLN positivity at transcript and protein levels, particularly MSLN positivity on leukemic blasts detected by flow cytometry, which can serve as feasible and effective predictors for EMD in patients with AML.

We also observed a high rate of early mortality among patients with MSLN positivity, with approximately half succumbing within the first year. However, the probability of OS did not differ significantly between patients with MSLN positivity and those with MSLN negativity or between those with and without EMD. One possible explanation is that patients with AML with EMD are typically younger and more likely to receive intensive treatments, such as HSCT, which might mitigate the adverse impact of EMD on clinical outcomes. However, patients with AML with CNS involvement at diagnosis generally exhibit inferior outcomes,8,25,26 and the prognosis subsequently worsens after CNS relapse.26,27

EMD development has been associated with adhesion molecules that interact with the CNS microenvironment and bone marrow extracellular matrix.13,16,17 As suspended cells, leukemic blasts tend to exhibit enhanced invasive and migratory behaviors when they aggregate into clusters compared to those in single-cell state.28 Analogous to EMT observed in solid tumors, CAMs collaborate to detach leukemia cells from their bone marrow niche, enabling their dissemination to the peripheral blood, and subsequent colonization of extramedullary sites such as the liver and spleen.29 In addition, previous studies have shown that leukemia cells overexpress CAMs, which facilitate distant migration, CNS infiltration, and enhance chemotherapy resistance.29,30 Both AML and acute lymphoblastic leukemia cells highly express a wide variety of CAMs that aid in their adhesion and migration, such as CD56, MAC-1, ICAM-1.16, 17, 18,31 CD56 (NCAM1) is associated with EMD and adverse prognosis in patients with AML,6,32,33 whereas NCAM2, primarily expressed in neural tissue, also facilitates cell adhesion.34 Our data suggest that MSLN modulates CD56 and NCAM2 expression, as their levels paralleled MSLN expression abundance, potentially influencing the adhesion function of leukemia cells, thereby promoting extramedullary dissemination.

Besides cellular adhesion, another biological role of MSLN is generally involved in cell signaling.10,35 In multiple malignancies, MSLN silencing significantly decreases PI3K/AKT activity, resulting in a reduction in cell adhesion and invasiveness.36 As a central signaling cascade, PI3K/AKT transduces extracellular signals that regulate cell growth and survival, is essential for the survival of AML blasts.37,38 However, our data demonstrated that MSLN activates the PI3K pathway in a noncanonical AKT substrate manner, and upregulates CD56 and NCAM2 expression levels through its interaction with MUC16. We proposed that MSLN-mediated activation of the PI3K pathway may involve alternative substrates such as PAK1, Rac, and SGK3 in AML. These results indicate that MSLN may promote EMD in AML by regulating CAMs via a noncanonical PI3K pathway mechanism.

The distant invasion and metastasis of solid tumors are related to EMT. EMT is a reversible process in which epithelial cells lose their properties and become mesenchymal cells, with altered expression of CAMs, and gain more aggressive invasion and metastasis capacities.14,15 MSLN expression in healthy human tissues is limited to mesothelial cells lining the pleura, peritoneum, and pericardium but is also highly expressed in a variety of solid tumors.12,39,40 Of note, MSLN-MUC16 binding has been postulated to play an important role during tumorigenesis and metastasis in solid tumors. In ovarian cancer, studies demonstrated the MSLN-MUC16 interaction facilitates cell-cell adhesion, contributing to extensive implantation and peritoneal spread.11 Moreover, MSLN and MUC16 are coexpressed in advanced ovarian cancer and the invasion front in pancreatic cancer.41,42 The blockage of the interaction between MUC16 and MSLN suppressed invasion and migration of pancreatic cancer cells, and the use of anti-MSLN in xenograft mouse models significantly reduced tumor growth and metastasis accelerated by MUC16 in ovarian cancer.23,42 Given its established role in distant invasion and metastasis in solid tumors, MSLN may similarly promote extramedullary infiltration in AML by interaction with MUC16. Crucially, we confirmed coexpression and coimmunoprecipitation of MSLN and MUC16 in AML cells. Moreover, their expression levels showed a strong positive correlation in both cultured AML cells and primary patient samples. Based on these findings, we propose that MSLN-MUC16 binding is the key process to enhance intercellular adhesion, thereby facilitating cell cluster formation and metastasis capacities in AML. Additionally, MSLN upregulates CAMs expression, and this CAM-mediated increase in intercellular adhesive capacity may also promote distant leukemia dissemination, ultimately contributing to EMD development.

In conclusion, MSLN positivity at both the transcript and protein levels can serve as an independent risk factor for EMD in patients with AML, with particularly heightened risk observed in cases demonstrating MSLN positivity by flow cytometry at diagnosis. Mechanistically, the MSLN-MUC16 interaction activates the PI3K pathway and upregulates CAMs, such as CD56 and NCAM2. Our findings elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying MSLN-driven extramedullary progression and provide a strong rationale for exploring MSLN-targeted strategies to mitigate EMD in MSLN-OE AML.
