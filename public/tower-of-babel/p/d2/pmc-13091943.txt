The role of stereotactic body radiotherapy in oligoprogressive breast cancer: A site-specific analysis of the prospective, phase-II RADIANT trial
Authors: Kara M Ruicci, Joelle Helou, Aisling Barry, Xiang Y Ye, CAnne Koch, Jennifer Croke, Kathy Han, Danielle Rodin, Ezra Hahn, Jennifer YY Kwan, Michael Yan, Fei-Fei Liu, Patricia Lindsay, Joanna Javor, Philippe Bedard, David Cescon, Vikaash Kumar, Eitan Amir, Michelle B Nadler, Ronita Lee, Rachel M Glicksman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Oligoprogressive metastatic breast cancer is a common clinical presentation.•SBRT to oligoprogressive sites may be used to delay next-line systemic therapy.•SBRT is associated with low rates of toxicity.•Health-related quality of life is preserved following delivery of SBRT.Keywords: Clinical trial, Breast cancer, Metastasis, Radiation, SBRT, Stereotactic body radiation therapy, Systemic therapy, Immunotherapy, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Standard-of-care management for patients with metastatic breast cancer is systemic therapy [1]. An enhanced understanding of tumour biology has led to significant advancement in systemic therapy options, however many patients ultimately experience disease progression [2], [3]. In the setting of clinically impactful progressive metastatic disease, standard-of-care management is changing systemic therapy. A subset of metastatic patients may experience only limited disease progression, i.e. oligoprogression, of a few metastases (typically 5 or fewer), while the remaining metastatic sites are stable on the current systemic therapy [4]. There is growing data across various primary disease sites to support stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) as a safe and effective treatment for patients with such oligoprogressive disease (OPD) [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. SBRT to progressive sites is therefore a potential alternative to changing systemic therapies—enabling preservation of often limited subsequent systemic options and helping mitigate additional and often cumulative toxicities for patients.

Despite its promise, at present there are few prospective studies examining the utility and safety of using SBRT for patients with oligoprogressive breast cancer. The phase-II CURB (NCT03808662) and STOP (NCT02756793) trials were randomized basket trials that each included a subset of oligoprogressive breast cancer patients. While CURB found no detectable survival benefit for SBRT in the breast cancer patient cohort, STOP noted a potential progression-free survival (PFS) benefit amongst the breast cancer patients, though patient numbers were small [7], [12]. Additionally, the single-arm phase-II TROG 20.03 AVATAR (ACTRN 12620001212943) trial—which focused specifically on estrogen receptor (ER)-positive patients on first-line combination cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 inhibitor (CDK4/6i) and aromatase inhibitor (AI)—found that following SBRT to oligoprogressive sites, 47% of patients remained event-free for at least 6 months [8]. It would therefore appear that there may be a role for SBRT for select patients with oligoprogressive breast cancer; however, selection of candidate patients and disease context requires further study. Herein, we present the clinical outcomes, adverse events and quality of life (QOL) findings for patients with oligoprogressive breast cancer enrolled on a prospective phase-II clinical trial to contribute to this currently limited body of literature.

Materials and methods

Trial design

The Role of SBRT in Oligoprogressive Malignant Disease (RADIANT) trial was an investigator-initiated, single-institution, single-arm, open-label, phase-II basket trial (NCT04122469). This study was approved by the University Health Network Research Ethics Board (REB; UHN18-6047). All patients provided informed written consent. The details of the study design have been described previously in detail[5]. In brief, patients with a primary diagnosis of metastatic breast, genitourinary or gastrointestinal cancer, who were on systemic therapy for ≥ 3 months and had radiographic oligoprogression (according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (RECIST) v1.1) in ≤ 5 lesions (on either conventional or functional imaging), were enrolled [5], [13]. After completing initial study accrual, with sample size of 70 across all cancer subtypes, a decision was made to continue accrual only for the genitourinary and breast cohorts, given sustained physician and patient interest in OPD for these cancers. The present study analyzed all enrolled patients with a primary pathological diagnosis of breast cancer. SBRT was delivered to all sites of OPD. Radiation treatments were based on established institutional policies for the applicable body sites (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Patients were assessed during SBRT, at 6-weeks, 3- and 6-months post-SBRT, and then routinely every 6 months for clinical outcomes, toxicity and QOL data.

The primary study endpoint was the time from SBRT to the change in systemic therapy (which could also include best supportive care); this decision was at the discretion of the treating oncologist(s)[5]. Secondary endpoints included the time to the events, such as the radiographic local failure (LF; based on physician discretion), radiographic distant failure (DF), radiographic PFS (defined as the composite of local and/or distant failure), overall survival (OS), toxicity related to radiation (using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0), and health-related QOL (HRQOL; using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Core QOL (QLQ-C30) questionnaire)[5]. An alpha/beta ratio of 10 Gy was used for metastatic oligoprogressive breast cancer when calculating biologically effective dose (BED).

Statistical analysis

Analysis by disease site was planned a priori and an analysis of the prostate cancer patients enrolled on RADIANT has been previously published[6]. Demographic and tumour characteristics were summarized using medians and ranges for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. Time-to-event data were calculated as the time from SBRT start date to first event, death or last follow-up, whichever was earliest. The cumulative incidence of change in systemic therapy, LF and DF were each estimated using the Aalen-Johansen method, where death without the event of interest was treated as a competing risk event, while alive without the event of interest was censored. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Univariate Fine & Gray competing risk models were applied to examine the association between time to change in systemic therapy, LF, DF and patient characteristics. Death(s) without the event(s) of interest were treated as competing events. Univariate Cox proportional hazard models were used to examine the association of PFS and OS with patient characteristics. Variables assessed in the model included age, de novo versus recurrent disease status, number of metastases at trial enrollment (1 versus ≥ 1), line of systemic therapy at the time of SBRT (1 versus ≥ 1), number of lesions treated with SBRT (1 versus ≥ 1), sites treated with SBRT (categorized as visceral, bone or lymph node—if patients had more than one, they were categorized as visceral if they had any visceral disease), ER status, HER2 status and BED. Cumulative incidence of acute adverse events (defined as occurring ≤ 3 months from the time of SBRT) and late adverse events (defined as occurring > 3 months from SBRT) were each estimated using the Aalen-Johansen method. HRQOL/QLQ-C30 summary scores over time were summarized descriptively and compared using the F-test or Kruskal Wallis test as appropriate. The change in global HRQOL at 6-months from baseline were further analyzed by paired t-tests or quantile regression as appropriate. The minimal clinically important difference, defined as a change of > 10 points compared with baseline on a 100-point scale, was also examined[5], [14]. A higher score represents better HRQOL. The data management and all statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS institute Inc., Cary, NC). A 2-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

From October 2019 to November 2023, 30 eligible patients with a diagnosis of metastatic breast cancer were enrolled and analyzed (Fig. 1). Baseline patient demographics are outlined in Table 1. At enrollment, the median patient age was 60.0 years (range 32–86) and patients were mostly Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG) 0 or 1 (93.3%, n = 28). Most patients had invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) (80.0%, n = 24) and a minority had invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) (10.0%, n = 3). Four patients (13.3%) had BRCA1/2 gene mutations. Most patients (90.0%, n = 27) were ER-positive, 13.3% (n = 4) were HER2-positive, and 6.7% (n = 2) were triple-negative. Most patients in this cohort presented with recurrent metastatic disease (63.3%, n = 19), while 36.7% (n = 11) presented with de novo metastatic disease. The majority of patients (86.7%, n = 26) had prior surgery for their local disease and/or had prior local or locoregional radiotherapy (66.7%, n = 20). All but one patient with initially localized disease had systemic therapy as part of their management. Types of systemic therapy received for localized disease are listed in Table 1. In terms of metastatic disease burden, at trial enrollment, a minority of patients had lymph node only metastases (13.3%, n = 4), while 33.3% (n = 10) of patients had bone metastases and 53.3% (n = 16) had visceral metastases—not mutually exclusive. The median number of oligoprogressive metastases at enrollment was 1 (range 1–4). Most OPD was diagnosed on conventional imaging, as compared to positron emission tomography (PET), which was used in 13.3% (n = 4) of patients. At the time of trial enrollment, most patients were on first-line (66.7%) systemic therapy.

SBRT details

Details regarding SBRT are outlined in Table 2. Dose fractionation schema and dose constraint details are provided in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2. Most patients had a single lesion treated (86.7%, n = 26), while 10.0% (n = 3) had two lesions treated and one patient had 4 lesions treated. Liver metastases were the most common target (40.0%, n = 12), followed by bone metastases (23.3%, n = 7). The median gross tumour volume (GTV) (considering all targets, if multiple targets were treated) was 8.5 cc. The most common dose-fractionation was 35 Gy in 5 fractions. Median BED was 59.5 Gy. The median follow-up time for this study was 33.7 months (range 2.5–57.2 months) (Table 2).

Outcomes – change in systemic therapy

The cumulative incidence of change in systemic therapy at 1-year was 30.0% (95% confidence interval (CI), 17.2–52.4%) and at 2-years was 50.4% (95% CI, 34.9–72.8%) (Fig. 2a). The median time from SBRT delivery to change in systemic therapy was 19.1 months (95% CI, 7.7 − not estimable (NE)) (Fig. 2a). Three patients died without a change in systemic therapy, all due to breast cancer. All but one patient who changed systemic therapy did so due to disease progression (one patient subsequently had a concurrent diagnosis of pancreatic cancer and changed therapies as guided by this new diagnosis). On univariate analysis, there were no significant associations found between clinicopathologic variables and change in systemic therapy, including for patients who presented with de novo metastatic versus recurrent metastatic disease (Supplemental Table 3). Patients who changed systemic therapy after SBRT most often commenced chemotherapy (61.1%, n = 11), followed by targeted therapy (16.7%, n = 3), anti-HER2 therapy (11.1%, n = 2) and endocrine therapy or best supportive care (5.6%, n = 1 each).

Cumulative incidence of (a) change in systemic therapy, (b) local failure, and (c) distant failure. Death without event of interest was treated as a competing risk event.

Outcomes – Local and distant failure

The cumulative incidence of local failure (95% CI) at 1-year was 10.0% (3.4–29.8%) and at 2-years was 16.8% (7.4–38.0%) (Fig. 2b). The cumulative incidence of distant failure (95% CI) at 1-year was 43.3% (28.5–65.9%) and at 2-years was 57.4% (41.6–79.3%) (Fig. 2c). The median time to distant failure was 24.0 months (95% CI, 9.0 − NE) (Fig. 2c). Amongst the 63.3% (n = 19) patients who had distant disease progression, 68.4% (n = 13) developed new metastatic lesions, while 31.6% (n = 6) experienced progression of pre-existing, non-irradiated lesions. No clinicopathologic variables were found to be associated with LF or DF on univariate analysis.

Outcomes – Progression-free and overall survival

The median PFS was 20.3 months (95% CI, 9.2 − NE) (Fig. 3a). At 1-year, PFS was 56.7% (95% CI, 41.4–77.5%) and at 2-years PFS was 42.6% (95% CI, 27.9–64.9%), respectively (Fig. 3a). OS at 1-year was 96.7% (95% CI, 90.5–100.0%) and at 2-years was 79.6% (95% CI, 66.3–95.6%) (Fig. 3b). Eleven patients died during study data collection; 10 deaths were due to breast cancer progression; 1 death was due to COVID-19. On univariate analysis, there were no significant findings associated with PFS or OS (Supplemental Table 3).

Kaplan-Meier estimates of (a) progression-free survival, and (b) overall survival.

Treatment-related adverse events

Nineteen patients (63.3%) experienced an acute adverse event of any grade that was attributed to SBRT and 4 patients (13.3%) experienced a grade 2 acute adverse event that was attributed to SBRT (Supplemental Figs. 1a and 1b). There were no grade ≥ 3 acute or late adverse events. The most common acute adverse events and overall adverse events of any grade were fatigue and diarrhea. Eight patients (26.7%) experienced a late adverse event of any grade that was attributed to SBRT and 2 patients (6.7%) experienced a late grade 2 attributed to SBRT (fatigue, humerus fracture) (Supplemental Figs. 1c and 1d). Given the small number of adverse events, regression analyses were not performed.

Quality of life

The mean (SD) global HRQOL was 69.19 (19.71) at baseline, compared to 69.83 (18.29), 65.83 (21.37) and 68.89 (22.20) at 6-weeks, 6-months and 1-year following SBRT, respectively. Throughout the follow-up period, there were no statistically or clinically significant changes in global HRQOL status scores. The mean (SD) QLQ-C30 summary score was 83.54 (10.24) at baseline, 82.75 (11.82) at 6-weeks, 81.52 (14.58) at 6-months and 78.14 (15.61) at 1-year after SBRT. Overall, there was no difference in the change in QLQ-C30 summary score from baseline with delivery of SBRT. Adherence to QOL measures was high, with all enrolled patients completing QOL scores at each timepoint, with the exception of one missing patient at 6 weeks following SBRT.

Discussion

Growing evidence supports metastasis-directed local therapy using SBRT for patients with oligoprogressive metastatic cancers to maintain patients on their current systemic therapy and preserve future treatment approaches [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. However, data in support of this strategy for breast cancer patients have been inconsistent, despite the prevalence of metastatic breast cancer and enthusiasm for clinical trials among this patient population [15]. Our results, analyzing 30 patients with oligoprogressive breast cancer enrolled on the phase-II RADIANT clinical trial, provide data supporting SBRT as a tool to delay next-line systemic therapy. At 1-year, 70% of patients in our cohort remained on their current systemic therapy, local control was high (90%) and there were few high-grade adverse events. Overall, these findings provide support for further studies to evaluate the role for SBRT in breast cancer patients with OPD.

To date, there has been two randomized trials investigating the role of SBRT in oligoprogressive cancer which have included breast cancer patients. The CURB trial included breast cancer patients alongside non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients and demonstrated a differential benefit for these disease sites [7]. While patients with NSCLC experienced a significant improvement in PFS with SBRT, SBRT did not significantly improve PFS for patients with oligoprogressive breast cancer (median PFS of 4.2 months with SBRT, as compared to 4.4 months in the standard-of-care group) [7]. The authors speculated on the differences observed in metastatic patterns between the breast cancer and NSCLC patients—noting that patients with breast cancer more commonly developed new metastases (as opposed to experiencing progression of pre-existing but non-irradiated sites). We and others have hypothesized that SBRT for OPD may be of most benefit to patients in the latter category [6], [7]. Our results are congruent with CURB: in RADIANT, 68% of patients who progressed developed new metastases, compared to 62% in CURB. CURB denoted their breast cohort as either triple-negative (n = 16 patients overall, constituting 29% of breast patients who received SBRT) or non-triple-negative breast cancer. In contrast, in RADIANT only 6.7% of patients (n = 2) had triple-negative breast cancer, despite a similar number of patients overall (30 patients treated with SBRT in RADIANT, versus 24 patients with breast cancer on the SBRT arm of CURB) [7]. In both CURB and RADIANT, there were no grade 3–5 adverse events following SBRT delivery [7].

In the STOP trial—the only other randomized study for oligoprogressive cancers published to date—there were no improved outcomes for any primary or secondary outcomes for SBRT compared with the standard arm across the included histologies. An exploratory analysis of the breast cancer patients noted an improvement in PFS in this group, although the patient numbers were small (n = 12), molecular subtypes were not reported and heterogeneity amongst included patients may also have affected findings in the basket cohort [12]. From these studies collectively, which enrolled 59 breast cancer patients in total (47 in CURB—24 of whom received SBRT and 12 in STOP), it remains uncertain whether there is promise in SBRT for breast cancer patients with OPD. However, if SBRT is beneficial for OPD, it is likely not for all patients and there remains a significant challenge in terms of selecting ideal patients and/or disease contexts.

There is just one breast cancer-specific published clinical trial examining SBRT for OPD. The phase-II TROG 20.03 AVATAR trial similarly examined SBRT in lieu of changing systemic therapy and reported that 47% of enrolled patients remained on the same agent for ≥ 6 months [8]. Of note, this study focused on a specific disease context: patients with ER-positive, HER2-negative disease on first-line combination CDK4/6i and AI therapy. The focus on a specific homogeneous population for enrollment provides important data for this group of patients, however it is challenging to compare to our more heterogeneous cohort, of which 30% were on second or greater-line systemic therapy, with varied hormone receptor status and differing prior management, as well as a mix of patients with de novo metastatic disease versus recurrent disease. Despite this heterogeneity, the primary endpoint of delaying change in systemic therapy was observed in the majority of our patients at 1-year, and in half of patients at 2-years after SBRT delivery.

Beyond data on SBRT for breast cancer patients with OPD, there have also been trials examining SBRT for oligometastatic breast cancer [16], [17]. In contrast to oligoprogression, the oligometastatic state represents a clinical classification whereby there are few (typically ≤ 5) sites of clinically-apparent metastatic disease, such that long-term control remains potentially achievable by treating all sites of radiographic disease [18]. Findings from studies using local therapy such as SBRT for oligometastatic disease (OMD) have also been mixed for breast cancer patients. SABR-COMET (NCT01446744) observed breast (with n = 13 patients in the SABR group) to be among the most favourable histologies in terms of long-term survival [19], [20]. In contrast, in the NRG-BR002 (NCT02364557) phase-IIR/III randomized trial, which examined PFS in patients assigned to standard-of-care systemic therapy with or without SBRT to oligometastases, investigators reported no benefit from SBRT for PFS or OS and the trial did not advance to phase-III [17]. In BR002, similar to RADIANT, most patients (79.0%) were hormone receptor positive, 13.0% were HER2-positive (13.3% in RADIANT) and 8.0% were triple-negative (6.7% in RADIANT). Likewise, in EXTEND (NCT03599765), a phase-II randomized basket trial examining the impact of local therapy on PFS for patients with oligometastatic breast cancer, the addition of local therapy did not improve PFS, OS or time to next line systemic therapy [16]. In EXTEND, four patients (18.2%) who received local therapy were triple-negative, 22.7% (n = 5) of patients were HER2-positive and 72.7% (n = 16) were ER-positive. Together, as of now, there is no high-level data supporting benefit of SBRT for unselected patients with oligometastatic breast cancer. Randomized trials such as TAORMINA (NCT05377047), OLIGOMA (NCT04495309), STEREO-SEIN (NCT02089100), are currently underway to identify patients who may benefit from SBRT, and their results are eagerly awaited.

It is evident that future work is needed to better clarify the optimal clinical context and/or patient(s) most likely to benefit from SBRT for OPD. Over one-third our cohort, for example, presented with de novo metastatic disease and there are hypotheses as to the differing biology of such patients as compared to those with recurrent metastatic disease [21], [22], [23], [24]. In CURB, 21% of the SBRT arm of breast cancer patients were patients with de novo metastatic disease [7]. Additionally, as mentioned, molecular subtype likely affects the impact of SBRT. TROG 20.03 AVATAR focused on strictly ER-positive, HER2-negative patients, while CURB included a mix of subtypes, with perhaps an over-representation of triple-negative patients (29% of breast patients in the SBRT arm) [25]. There is also growing literature interrogating circulating tumour (ct) DNA metrics in relation to radiotherapy, with the hypothesis that ctDNA may be a promising biomarker to determine whether patients have “true” OPD; hence, may benefit from local therapy [26]. In CURB, analyses of paired blood samples demonstrated that SBRT led to changes in ctDNA metrics in patients with NSCLC, but not in patients with breast cancer, mirroring the clinical findings of that trial [7]. As part of the RADIANT protocol, serum/plasma samples were collected where possible for patients before, during and after SBRT with the goal of analyzing the ctDNA kinetics in response to treatment [5]. An analysis of these findings is ongoing, and will be reported shortly.

Finally, our study has limitations, within which it should be considered. First, RADIANT was a single-institution, open-label, non-randomized study, which introduces biases and limits the interpretation of findings—direct comparison to a standard-of-care arm is required to draw definitive conclusions. Our sample size is also relatively small; however, it is comparable to that of TROG 20.03 AVATAR and the experimental arm of the CURB breast cohort [7], [8]. Further, while our analysis is restricted to breast cancer patients, there is heterogeneity among those enrolled. As a result of a small sample size and cohort heterogeneity, we were limited in our ability to undertake subgroup and regression analyses. Finally, our primary endpoint of change in systemic therapy can be considered a limitation. The decision to change systemic therapy were not mandated by our study protocol and was at the discretion of treating physicians (due to the initial basket design of RADIANT) [5]. However, this endpoint is pragmatic for a phase-II design and reflects a clinically meaningful event.

Conclusion

In summary, the present analysis of all patients with oligoprogressive breast cancer enrolled on the phase-II RADIANT clinical trial reveals SBRT to be a safe and promising treatment option. A heterogeneous cohort of breast cancer patients with OPD were included, including a mix of patients with de novo metastatic versus recurrent metastatic disease and varying lines of systemic therapy, with most patients harboring hormone receptor positive, HER2 negative disease. Despite this, the primary endpoint of delaying change to next-line systemic therapy was reached in most patients at 1-year and in half of patients at 2-years following SBRT. Future research is needed to best select candidate patients for this therapeutic strategy however, as there remains significant controversy in the current literature.
