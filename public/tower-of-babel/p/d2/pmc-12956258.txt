A representation of abstract linguistic categories in the visual system underlies successful lipreading
Authors: Aaron R Nidiffer, Cody Zhewei Cao, Aisling O’Sullivan, Edmund C Lalor
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is considerable debate over how visual speech is processed in the absence of sound and whether neural activity supporting lipreading occurs in visual brain areas. Much of the ambiguity stems from a lack of behavioral grounding and neurophysiological analyses that cannot disentangle high-level linguistic and phonetic/energetic contributions from visual speech. To address this, we recorded EEG from human observers as they watched silent videos, half of which were novel and half of which were previously rehearsed with the accompanying audio. We modeled how the EEG responses to novel and rehearsed silent speech reflected the processing of low-level visual features (motion, lip movements) and a higher-level categorical representation of linguistic units, known as visemes. The ability of these visemes to account for the EEG – beyond the motion and lip movements – was significantly enhanced for rehearsed videos in a way that correlated with participants’ trial-by-trial ability to lipread that speech. Source localization of viseme processing showed clear contributions from visual cortex, with no strong evidence for the involvement of auditory areas. We interpret this as support for the idea that the visual system produces its own specialized representation of speech that is (1) well-described by categorical linguistic features, (2) dissociable from lip movements, and (3) predictive of lipreading ability. We also suggest a reinterpretation of previous findings of auditory cortical activation during silent speech that is consistent with hierarchical accounts of visual and audiovisual speech perception.Keywords: Visual speech, EEG, Lipreading, Linear modeling

Introduction

Although spoken language is primarily transmitted via acoustic signals, visible cues generated during speech production play a prominent role in shaping our speech and language perception. Access to those visible cues improves both comprehension (Erber, 1969; Sumby and Pollack, 1954) and the ability to focus attention toward the speaker (O’Sullivan et al., 2019; Zion Golumbic et al., 2013), especially in challenging listening environments. These benefits are likely mediated in part by the correlated dynamics of articulatory movements and acoustic features (Chandrasekaran et al., 2009). In contrast, visible speech can alter the phonemic perception of audible speech (McGurk and MacDonald, 1976) whereby incongruent audiovisual inputs combine to produce a single percept that is absent in either speech signal, providing evidence for separate and complimentary linguistic representations from acoustic and visible speech. Moreover, in the absence of audible speech, visible cues can form the entire basis of language comprehension (Andersson et al., 2001).

Exactly how visual speech contributes to the reception of spoken language is a matter of debate. Based on the finding that auditory cortex is active during silent speech (Besle et al., 2008; Calvert et al., 1997; Ludman et al., 2000; Pekkola et al., 2005; Sams et al., 1991), much of that debate has focused on how visual timing cues influence auditory temporal processing (Biau et al., 2021; Bourguignon et al., 2020; Crosse et al., 2015; Mégevand et al., 2020; Zion Golumbic et al., 2013; c.f., Plass et al., 2020). Importantly though, auditory cortex is known to similarly track non-speech and other non-auditory signals (Cohen et al., 2011; Kayser et al., 2008; Lemus et al., 2010) and the timing information that is tracked by auditory cortex (i.e., broadband envelopes of the unheard acoustic waveform and lip movements) is insufficient to support speech comprehension (Shannon et al., 1995; Smith et al., 2002). Moreover, there is evidence that visual input to auditory cortex does not evoke activity directly, but modulates the local field potential (Ahveninen et al., 2022). Therefore, it is unclear if this pathway specifically supports visual speech processing or if it simply reflects a more general multisensory process driven by correlated temporal dynamics (Maddox et al., 2015; Nidiffer et al., 2018; Parise and Ernst, 2016) that increases the sensitivity to incoming sounds. A different perspective suggests that the visual system does its own heavy lifting during speech perception (Bernstein and Liebenthal, 2014). According to this view, each level of the speech hierarchy that is conveyed through acoustics is also available to the visual system and suggests that visual cortex contains the necessary machinery to extract and interpret the rich visible cues of speech in the formation of a visual representation of language (Bernstein et al., 2011; Files et al., 2013). This view is further supported by two independent proposals. One Campbell (2008) proposes that visual speech provides two distinct forms of information in relation to acoustic speech, viz. redundant (i.e., correlated) and complementary (i.e., unique) information, and another Peelle and Sommers (2015) suggests that audiovisual speech processing occurs in a multistage process that includes early and late components.

Recently, numerous studies have explored how the dynamics of silent visual speech are reflected in neural data (Bourguignon et al., 2020; Bröhl et al., 2022; Files et al., 2013; Hauswald et al., 2018; Mégevand et al., 2020; O’Sullivan et al., 2017; Ozker et al., 2018; Suess et al., 2022), making great strides toward our understanding of how the brain processes visual speech. However, their ability to speak to linguistic processing and their pertinence to lipreading comes with some important caveats. They have largely been limited by individuals’ naturally poor lipreading skill, limiting the extent of measurable linguistic activity; they have often lacked critical behavioral manipulation, limiting the ability to relate neural activity to language reception (c.f., Bröhl et al., 2022), which is highly variable across individuals (Bernstein and Liebenthal, 2014); and perhaps most importantly they often utilize an impoverished, proxy measure of visual speech (e.g., the acoustic speech envelope), which limits the potential to disentangle linguistic and energetic contributions of visual speech.

In the current study, we address these caveats to probe our hypothesis that linguistic processing of visual speech occurs in visual cortex and that this activity is central to meaningful lipreading. To this end, we asked human observers to rehearse a set of videos of audiovisual speech with the goal of later lipreading that speech. After rehearsal, we recorded EEG from the participants as we replayed silent versions of the rehearsed videos and a novel set of unrehearsed videos, providing a more powerful within-subjects comparison and avoiding the large variability across individuals. During EEG testing, we found improvements in subjective and objective measures of lipreading. We were able to relate the dynamics of visual phonetic features (lip movements) and abstract linguistic units (visemes) to unique components in on-going brain activity in visual cortex. Consistent with our hypothesis, only the higher-level categorical linguistic representation was enhanced in brain activity and that enhancement was reflected across participants’ lipreading scores.

Methods

Participants

Sixteen individuals (age = 25 ± 6.3, 9 females) participated in the current study. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing and were right-handed. The study was conducted in accordance with procedures approved by the University of Rochester Human Subjects Review Board. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants prior to any procedures. When applicable, participants were given monetary compensation for participation.

Apparatus and stimuli

The stimuli used in the current experiment consisted of videos of a well-known male speaker. The videos contained the speaker’s head, shoulders, and chest centered in the frame. The speech was conversational and directed at the camera, and the linguistic content focused on political policy. Fifteen 60-second videos were rendered into 1280 × 720-pixel movies in VideoPad Video Editor (NCH Software) at 30 frames-per-second. Audio was digitized at 48,000 kHz with 16-bit resolution. A very brief (10 ms) audible click was inserted at the beginning of the audio track for EEG synchronization (see next section). On visual-only trials, the speech sounds were removed, leaving only the click in the audio soundtrack.

Videos were presented via Presentation Software (Neurobehavioral Systems) on a desktop computer running Windows 10. Visual stimuli were presented on a 24″ widescreen (16:9) LCD monitor (BenQ ZOWIE XL2411P) at a refresh rate of 60 Hz. Audible stimuli were presented diotically through a set of open-back circumnaural headphone (Senn-heiser HD650) at a moderate listening level. During the experiment, participants were seated comfortably in a dark, sound-attenuated, and electrically shielded room (Industrial Acoustics Company) approximately 70 cm from the video screen.

Procedure

The experiment was divided into two parts: rehearsal and testing (c.f., Crosse et al., 2015b). In the rehearsal phase, five videos were selected for a participant to learn (randomized across participants). Each video was presented to the participant ten times in a random order with its accompanying audio soundtrack. Participants were instructed to watch and listen attentively and were aware that they would be tested on silent versions of the video after rehearsal. The rehearsal was self-paced with participants advancing to the next trial with a button press. After rehearsal, participants were offered a 10 min break before testing.

During the testing phase, we collected EEG from participants as they lipread the same five videos encountered during rehearsal plus five new videos (also randomized across participants). The speech audio was stripped from these videos for the silent conditions (the click was left intact). We repeated the “rehearsed” and “novel” silent videos four times each and their order was interleaved and randomized. Before the presentation of each silent rehearsed video, the audiovisual version was replayed to refresh participants’ rehearsal. This also served to provide us with EEG data reflecting audiovisual speech processing with which we could compare the EEG results obtained to visual only speech. In all, participants completed 60 trials: 20 silent rehearsed (5 videos x 4 repeats), 20 silent novel (5 videos x 4 repeats), and 20 audiovisual rehearsed (5 videos x 4 repeats). Prior to each trial, participants were given a target word to detect in the upcoming speech. The target word was randomly drawn from a set of 105 (7 unique words from each of 15 videos; see Table 1 for a random selection) and were different for every trial – no target word was used more than once. Target words appeared 1–3 times per video. Participants were instructed to press the space bar as soon as they perceived the speaker uttering the target. Presses within 2 s of target-word utterances were considered a hit (H) and all other presses were recorded as false alarms (FA). EEG acquisition and preprocessing

Continuous EEG data were acquired (Fig. 1a) using an ActiveTwo system (BioSemi) from 128 scalp electrodes + 2 reference electrodes adhered to the skin over the mastoids. The data were low pass filtered online below 134 Hz and digitized at a rate of 512 Hz. Triggers were sent by an Arduino Uno microcontroller which detected the audio click at the start of each soundtrack to indicate the start of each trial (again, the speech audio was removed from the soundtrack in visual-only trials). Subsequent pre-processing was conducted offline in MATLAB: the data were imported and re-referenced to the average reference, bandpass filtered between 0.3 Hz and 15 Hz, and noisy channels were interpolated. To identify channels with excessive noise, the time series were visually inspected in Cartool (Brunet et al., 2011), and the standard deviation of each channel was compared with that of the surrounding channels in MATLAB. Channels contaminated by noise (twice the average standard deviation of the surrounding electrodes) were replaced by spline-interpolating the remaining clean channels with weightings based on their relative scalp location in EEGLAB (Delorme and Makeig, 2004). Finally, the EEG was epoched and downsampled to 64 Hz. Data can be found at https://osf.io/nqpzu/.

Speech representations

For each stimulus we quantified a set of features (Fig. 1b) that capture the temporal dynamics of a basic component of visual phonetic cues (i.e., lip movements; L) and their abstract categories (i.e., visemes; V). We chose to focus on these features because they represent two distinct levels of the linguistic hierarchy (Bernstein and Liebenthal, 2014) and roughly correspond to acoustic-linguistic (e.g., envelope, spectrogram, phonemes, phonetic features) cues often used in similar auditory speech research (Brodbeck et al., 2018; Di Liberto et al., 2015). Lip movements: We used the Face Tracker’s Detailed Features mode in Adobe After Effects to track a series of landmarks on the speaker’s face. For each video frame, we computed the vertical (Euclidean) distance between the upper and lower lips and the horizontal distance between the left and right corners of the mouth. To account for occasional changes in camera zoom, we divided vertical lip distance obtained in each frame by the average nose-to-chin distance across all frames within the current camera shot. Horizontal movements were similarly normalized by the average cheek-to-cheek distance for that shot. Visemes: We first obtained the phonemic representation of the speech (after Di Liberto et al., 2015). Briefly, given a sound file of the speech and its transcript, Prosodylab-Aligner (Gorman et al., 2011) automatically partitions each word into phonemes, performs a forced alignment (Yuan and Liberman, 2008), and returns the onset and offset time of each phoneme. This information is organized into a multivariate (39-dimension) time-series of binary variables, each indicating the presence or absence of a specific phoneme across time. Then, each of the 39 phoneme dimensions were mapped onto one of 12 viseme dimensions via the correspondence defined by Auer and Bernstein (1997) and based on their perceptual similarity (Fisher, 1968; Woodward and Barber, 1960). For example, /b/, /p/, and /m/ have a high rate of visual confusion and form one viseme, {b,p,m}. We also quantified a control measure, motion flow (M), to capture general visual motion in the scene. Briefly (after O’Sullivan et al., 2017), for each frame, a matrix of motion vectors was calculated using an “Adaptive Rood Pattern Search” block matching algorithm (Barjatya, 2004). An example of these vectors is depicted in Fig. 1b, with primary contributions from a hand movement and head nod. Motion flow was the sum of all motion vector lengths in each frame (Bartels et al., 2008). This unweighted sum theoretically could contain both “global motion” (attributable to locomotion or gaze shift) and “local motion” (attributable to external movements), we assume the contribution to this feature from “global motion” was zero due to the camera remaining stationary. Each stimulus feature was resampled to 64 Hz to match the rate of the EEG data. Continuous features – motion flow and lip movements – were normalized by taking their z-score along the time dimension. Visemes were represented as discrete binary variables.

Temporal response function analysis

In order to relate continuous EEG to the speech representations introduced above, we used a ridge regression analysis to compute a time-resolved mapping from a stimulus feature of interest to the EEG response in each electrode. This mapping, known as a Temporal Response Function (TRF), describes neural responses related to a stimulus feature of interest and can be envisioned as analogous to the more conventional event-related potential (ERP). We computed TRFs using the mTRFtoolbox (https://github.com/mickcrosse/mTRF-Toolbox), a custom-built toolbox in MATLAB (see also Bialas et al. 2023, Crosse et al. 2021, 2016). Because the effect of an event in the environment is not evident in brain activity until after the event and lasts for several hundred milliseconds, we compute the TRF across a series of time lags. We use ridge regression to compute the TRF because it accounts for the collinearity introduced by using lagged versions of the same stimulus feature. To normalize TRF predictions across stimulus features, we selected a range of time lags (0–300 ms; Fig. 1c, shaded regions) that contained the major components of the TRF across features and fit TRF models using only these time lags. Time lags outside this range as shown in Fig. 1c are for illustration only.

To estimate how well each stimulus feature is represented in neural activity, we first optimized TRFs for each stimulus feature or combination of features by varying the level of regularization applied and selected the lambda that led to the best prediction accuracy across all channels by leave-one-out cross-validation. We next measured each model’s ability to predict new EEG (Fig. 1d). Prediction accuracy was measured channel-by-channel as the Pearson’s correlation between the observed and predicted EEG signals. Where applicable, we used Wilcoxon signed-rank test to test TRF prediction accuracies against a noise floor generated by iteratively fitting TRFs fifty times on shuffled stimulus/response pairings and then evaluating on matched data. Likewise, prediction accuracies were compared across conditions and models with 2-way repeated-measures ANOVAs and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. All statistical analyses were implemented in MATLAB.

Due to inherent correlations between stimulus features, it becomes necessary to isolate unique responses to each feature. To this end, before fitting and evaluating TRFs as described above, we sometimes partialed out the contributions of other features from the EEG using the following procedure. First, we fit TRFs to the to-be-partialed feature(s). We used these TRFs to generate EEG predictions which we then subtracted from the original EEG signal. We used this residual EEG in place of the original EEG in further TRF analyses (conceptually shown in Fig. 1c, gray arrows). To quantify how well the partialing procedure worked, we measured how well a feature (M, L, or V) can predict residual EEG after subtracting its own predictions (rres; data for V are shown in Fig. 3b, bottom row). We also estimated the noise floor for predicting residual EEG (rres,null) by shuffling the trial labels when fitting TRFs to the residual EEG (importantly trial labels are not shuffled during the partialing procedure). The partialing procedure removes nearly all (>99 %) the predictable signal from the EEG and reduces prediction accuracy to nearly the noise floor, although a very small yet reliable difference can be detected (rres > rres,null, all T(15) > 2.40; all p < 0.03). Neural-behavioral correlation and linear mixed-effects modeling

To relate behavior to neural responses, we performed two correlational analyses and fit a linear mixed effects model. We measured Pearson’s correlation coefficient between word detection scores (d’) and the neural tracking measure uniquely attributed to visemes (i.e., after Motion and Lip Movements were partialed from the EEG) in two ways. First, we computed the Pearson correlation between these variables across all participants and rehearsal conditions. Second, to account for differences in overall neural and behavioral performance, we took the difference between the rehearsed and novel conditions before computing their Pearson correlation. Participants’ single-trial behavioral measures (d’) were modeled from our measure of viseme tracking averaged across the set of occipital electrodes (viseme) and rehearsal condition (condition) as fixed effects and participant variability as a random effect. For each fixed effect, a T-statistic was computed for the coefficient by dividing the estimate of the coefficient by its standard error and its corresponding probability was estimated from the T-distribution. Coefficients and statistics for this model are reported in Supplemental Table 1. A random slope term, (viseme|participant), was initially considered. We reasoned prediction viseme tracking would influence behavioral output differently across participants, yet the term had nearly zero effect on the fixed effects or the results (see Supplementary Table 2). Further, although we randomly assign each stimulus to rehearsal condition in each participant, it is necessary to account for the possibility that the stimulus itself could be driving some relationship between EEG activity and behavior, so in a third model, we included a random intercept with stimulus as a grouping factor, (1∣stimulus). Although stimulus was a small source of variability (see Supplementary Table 3), accounting for it only marginally changed the fixed effects and did not change the results.

Source localization

We localized the source of the viseme TRF using the Brainstorm toolbox in MATLAB (Tadel et al., 2019). Rather than attempting to localize each individual viseme response, we wished to localize viseme processing in general. To do this, we first created a generic viseme onset feature by summing our original viseme feature along the category dimension. We fit a TRF to this viseme onset feature using EEG recorded during novel and rehearsed visual-only speech and averaged the model weights across participants. We then computed a forward model using the 3-shell sphere method (Salu et al., 1990) and no noise modeling. Finally, we estimated the sources of the viseme onset TRF at a single time point (which we selected as the maximum global field power (Lehmann and Skrandies, 1980)) using Brainstorm’s weighted minimum norm estimation implementation with default options. To demonstrate the specificity of the analysis, we repeated the same steps using an envelope TRF fit to EEG recorded during visual only and audiovisual speech, which is possible due to the inherent correlations between mouth movements and the acoustic envelope (Chandrasekaran et al., 2009).

Exploratory viseme analysis

To bolster an argument that lip dynamics do not underpin enhancements to viseme tracking, we undertook an exploratory analysis to unravel contributions of lip movements to individual viseme categories and how that mapping might contribute to the ability of those visemes to predict the EEG signal. We first evaluated how each viseme was encoded in brain activity (viseme→EEG). To account for any shared predictive power between viseme categories, we first fit a standard multivariate viseme TRF (as was done for all prior viseme models), then separated that model into individual univariate TRFs (after Nunez-Elizalde et al., 2019), and finally predicted left-out EEG data with each univariate viseme model. Next, we wanted to explore how strongly each viseme is characterized by lip movements, whether it affects the encoding of each viseme in neural activity, and whether rehearsal was reinforcing that relationship. We fit TRFs that map individual visemes from horizontal and vertical lip movements (lips→viseme) and evaluated their ability to predict left-out viseme data. We evaluated whether the lips→viseme and viseme→EEG mappings varied across visemes using repeated-measures ANOVA. The fourth viseme ({ɔɪ}) was absent in four of the video clips. This was not an issue for the viseme→EEG mapping as no participant watched more than one of these videos per condition, but this became an issue when evaluating the lip→viseme mapping. We performed two complementary analyses. In one we omitted the fourth viseme (keeping all videos) from the ANOVA and in the other we omitted the four videos (keeping all visemes) from the ANOVA. Both yielded similar results. Finally, to test the association between these mappings, in other words the relationship between a viseme’s distinctiveness in lip movements and its representation in neural activity, we compared their prediction accuracies using Pearson correlations for each rehearsal condition.

Results

Audiovisual rehearsal promotes lipreading

We recorded 128-channel EEG from sixteen participants as they watched silent videos of continuous natural speech of a male speaker discussing political and economic topics (Fig. 1a). Prior to the EEG session, we asked participants to rehearse five videos of audiovisual speech with the goal of being able to better track the speaker’s visible speech articulations to lipread while we recorded EEG. During the EEG session, participants viewed silent versions of these rehearsed videos along with previously-unseen (novel) videos. During each trial, participants detected target words that were given at the start of the trial and gave a subjective intelligibility rating of that trial at its conclusion. Both the objective performance (d’; z = 2.59, p = 0.0097, Cohen’s dz = 0.86) and the subjective intelligibility rating (z = 2.78, p = 0.005, Cohen’s dz = 0.82) were higher for rehearsed videos suggesting that participants were able to better lip-read those videos (Fig. 2).

Speech rehearsal improved lipreading and subjective intelligibility during silent speech. During the silent videos, participants were asked to report their detection of a target word with a button press. After each video, they also rated the subjective intelligibility of the speech. Rehearsal improved both measures across participants.

Rehearsal improves tracking of linguistic features but not lip movements in visual cortex

With this evidence that our rehearsal paradigm was behaviorally successful, we investigated the possible neural underpinnings of participants’ improved lip reading. To relate their EEG responses to the visual speech, we fit and optimized temporal response functions (TRFs) to several features derived from the videos (Fig. 1b) – motion flow (M), lip movements (L), and visemes (V) – and then evaluated those TRFs (Fig. 1c) to predict left-out data. Focusing on the average prediction accuracy across 20 electrodes over occipital scalp (Fig. 1d), we first analyzed EEG responses to the silent videos of speech that participants had never seen (i.e., novel videos). We replicated previous findings that visual cortical activity encodes visual linguistic categories (O’Sullivan et al., 2017; see supplemental information for more details). Next, we compared the prediction accuracy of a family of models across speech features and both rehearsal conditions. We performed a two-way, repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA; factors of speech feature [M, L, and V] and rehearsal condition [novel and rehearsed]) which found significant main effects for both feature (F(2,30) = 8.98, p = 0.00088, partial η2 = 0.37) and rehearsal condition (F(1,15) = 4.80, p = 0.045, partial η2 = 0.24). Importantly, there was also a significant interaction (F(2,30) = 4.82, p = 0.015, partial η2 = 0.20), suggesting that rehearsal might be preferentially improving the neural encoding of certain features. Follow-up pairwise comparisons showed that this was indeed the case (Fig. 3a): viseme and motion tracking improved with rehearsal (viseme: z = 2.64, p = 0.0084, Cohen’s dz = 0.74; motion: z = 2.22, p = 0.026, Cohen’s dz = 0.54), but we found no evidence that lip movement encoding was affected by rehearsal (z = 1.24, p = 0.21). These results suggest that rehearsal is improving the brain’s capacity to encode these higher-order linguistic features, but we found no such enhancement of the underlying dynamics of lip movements.

These results do not account for the shared timing – and thus any shared predictive power – across features, so attributing enhanced neural tracking to unique aspects of any one feature is ambiguous. To disambiguate the correlations between our features, we measured the unique neural tracking of each feature of interest by first regressing out the contributions of other features. As before, we found no evidence for rehearsal-based improvements to neural tracking of lip movements (z = 0.10, p = 0.92) after regressing out the contributions of visemes (Fig. 3b). Similarly, the improvements to motion tracking disappeared after accounting for visemes (z = 1.03, p = 0.30). The partialing of visemes did not simply obliterate neural activity related to motion or lip movements; both features were still robustly represented in brain activity during both rehearsed (M vs shuffled M: z = 3.51, p = 0.00044; L vs shuffled L: z = 3.26, p = 0.0011) and previously unseen videos (M vs shuffled M: z = 3.31, p = 0.00094; L vs shuffled L: z = 3.26, p = 0.0011). Conversely, the benefit of rehearsal on viseme tracking remained intact (Fig. 3c) after partialing out motion flow (z = 2.43, p = 0.015, Cohen’s dz = 0.79) and lip movements (z = 2.28, p = 0.023, Cohen’s dz = 0.81) separately and in combination (z = 2.07, p = 0.039, Cohen’s dz = 0.87).

Viseme tracking can be roughly localized to visual cortex

The prominence of our effects over occipital scalp does not guarantee that they come from visual cortex but could be influenced by current sources from across the brain. To support an argument for visual cortical involvement in visual linguistic processing, we localized the source of two viseme-based TRFs that were fit to each visual speech condition (Fig. 4a and b, rehearsed shown). Responses to viseme onsets during novel (Fig. 4c) and rehearsed (Fig. 4d) visual speech produced similar topography, with a major contribution from visual cortex. Because the source localization was not pre-planned and thus our data were not optimized for it, we sought to test the specificity to other sources of speech information (i.e., speech acoustics). We chose to localize TRFs fit to the acoustic envelope in both visual and audiovisual conditions because (1) the acoustic envelope is strongly correlated to visible speech movements (Chandrasekaran et al., 2009) and is therefore sensitive to both auditory and visual sources (Crosse et al., 2015a) and (2) it removes any bias related to localizing separate auditory and visual features. By localizing TRFs fit to the acoustic envelope during visual and audiovisual speech (Fig. 4e and f, respectively), we were able to demonstrate the sensitivity of the analysis to the different sources of acoustic and visual components of the speech, with visual speech sources primarily localized to visual cortex and audiovisual speech revealing more distributed sources including auditory and visual cortices. We observe some potential topographical differences between sources derived from the envelope and visemes that may relate to the hierarchical nature of those features, but we hesitate to further interpret our source analysis due to its suboptimality.

Visual cortex encodes visual linguistic categories better than acoustic linguistic categories

Several previous findings (e.g., Bourguignon et al. 2020, Hauswald et al. 2018, O’Sullivan et al., 2017, Suess et al. 2022) relate visual speech to ongoing brain activity that tracks the unheard speech envelope. To exclude the possibility that neural tracking benefits conferred by rehearsal might be attributable to unique fluctuations of acoustic features (e.g., acoustic features from difficult to see articulations such as /g/ or /j/), we extended our previous analyses to include the acoustic envelope and phoneme categories. After partialing the contribution from the unheard envelope, we found that the rehearsal-based improvements to viseme tracking remained intact (z = 3.15, p = 0.0016, Cohen’s dz = 1.02) and consistent with our previous analyses, there were no benefits to envelope tracking after accounting for viseme activity (z = 0.61, p = 0.61). Further, it is possible that visual cortical activity might reflect participants “filling in” phonemes from the rehearsed speech. We reasoned that if this was the case, a phoneme model would at least perform to the level of the viseme model, especially in the rehearsed condition. However, a phoneme model imparts significantly less predictive power than the viseme model for both rehearsed (z = 3.41 p = 0.00064) and novel conditions (z = 2.12, p = 0.034), despite the larger number of parameters (12 visemes vs. 39 phonemes). These findings illustrate that EEG activity recorded over visual brain areas and the rehearsal-based changes to that activity are better captured by a feature that accounts for our limited ability to visually discriminate phonemes than a feature that does not. Our findings here cannot rule out top-down auditory system involvement (nor would we). However, they do support the interpretation that occipital responses are constrained by (or constrain!) visual speech perception.

Single-trial lipreading performance is predicted by cortical viseme tracking

The previous analyses showing both behavioral and visuo-linguistic enhancements identify viseme tracking in visual cortex as a potential covariate of lipreading ability. To explore this possibility, we examined lipreading performance in the context of our measure of viseme tracking. We found that across all participants and rehearsal conditions, word detection performance was positively correlated with our viseme tracking measure (Fig. 3d top; r = 0.47, p = 0.007). To visualize the contributions from rehearsal, we quantified improvement in word detection and viseme tracking and tested their relationship. This comparison also revealed a robust positive correlation (Fig. 3d bottom; r = 0.60, p = 0.015). This relationship was not abolished after partialing motion flow (r = 0.57, p = 0.021), lip movements (r = 0.55, p = 0.028), or both (r = 0.52, p = 0.040) from the EEG.

To probe this neural-behavioral relationship more directly, we fit a linear mixed-effects model to predict single-trial word detection performance from the strength of viseme tracking and rehearsal condition (see Methods and Supplementary Table 1 for details). After controlling for individual differences and the effects of rehearsal condition in the model, we found that behavioral scores were indeed reliably predicted by viseme tracking from trial-to-trial (β = 3.21, T(631) = 2.06, p = 0.040) and, consistent with our behavioral results discussed above, were also predicted by the rehearsal condition (β = 0.47, T(631) = 3.38, p = 0.00078). Taken together, these findings support the notion that the representation of visual linguistic categories in visual cortex is crucial to supporting lipreading (c.f., Bröhl et al., 2022).

Specific individual visemes contribute to improved linguistic processing in visual responses

Having established a connection between lipreading and the neural tracking of visemes in ongoing speech, we sought to bolster an argument that lip dynamics do not underpin the enhancements to viseme tracking reported above. We undertook an exploratory analysis to unravel whether contributions of visemes to the EEG signal can be attributed to any predictable relationship between those visemes and their underlying lip movements. We predicted EEG activity from individual (i.e., univariate – see methods for details) viseme models and evaluated the strength of those predictions (Fig. 5a, novel shown; Fig. 5b, rehearsal advantage shown). We submitted the resultant prediction accuracies to a repeated-measures ANOVA (with factors viseme category and rehearsal condition). A significant main effect of viseme category indicated that individual visemes do not contribute equally to the EEG signal (F(11,165) = 11.00, p = 4.1 × 10−15) with some being more strongly encoded than others.

We wondered if this differential encoding of visemes may be indicative of a mapping between lip movements and visemes that participants could be reinforcing during the rehearsal. That is, we sought to reveal if improvements to viseme tracking were driven by strengthening a predictable relationship between lip movements and visemes (Fig. 5c). Two complementary approaches (see methods for details) to this analysis revealed similar main effects of viseme category (removing {ɔɪ} from the analysis, keeping all trials: F(10,140) = 90.01, p = 4.69 × 10−56 vs. removing trials not containing {ɔɪ}, keeping all visemes: F(11,110) = 68.52, p = 3.66 × 10−44). In fact, the degree to which EEG encodes each viseme individually is highly correlated to how well those visemes are described by lip movements (Fig. 5d, gray line; r = 0.87, p = 2.04 × 10−4) for the unrehearsed videos, suggesting that viseme processing in the absence of successful lip reading may be largely attributable to how well lip movements are encoded in brain activity. However, when observers can successfully lipread, this relationship is perturbed slightly (Fig. 5d, red line; r = 0.60, p = 0.019). Importantly, this interruption is driven by the enhancement of individual visemes in a manner that is unrelated to the lip-viseme mapping (Fig. 5b and e, red arrows; r = 0.07, p = 0.82), suggesting rehearsal was not reinforcing this mapping. Specifically, three viseme categories ({ɔ,aɪ,ə,ɑ,ʌ,j}, {f,v}, and {l,n,k,g,h,η}) drive this enhancement derived from rehearsal (z = 2.28, 2.02, and 2.33; p = 0.022, 0.044, and 0.020, respectively). Taken together, these exploratory analyses revealed that viseme category can be predicted by timing of lip movements and that neural responses to visemes follow this relationship for unrehearsed speech, but rehearsal-based improvement of viseme encoding do not depend on this relationship. Instead, we suggest that benefits may rely more on learning to categorize the shape of the articulators, independent of their temporal properties.

Discussion

In the current study, we recorded brain activity over occipital scalp during silent speech and related it to a set of visual features derived from the speech signal. We were able to isolate the neural activity related to visual linguistic categories (visemes) from activity related to lip movements and other motion present in the videos. In doing so, we show that this linguistic representation is robustly tracked in continuous brain activity, replicating and extending previous work from our group (O’Sullivan et al., 2017). We found that brief and directed rehearsal of speech promotes improved lipreading while preferentially improving the representation of visual linguistic features of that speech. Finally, we linked single-trial behavioral performance to the strength of linguistic tracking over occipital scalp and source localized that activity to visual cortex. We interpret these results as supporting a direct role for visual cortex in the linguistic analysis of silent speech during lipreading.

Linking neural indices of visual speech processing to behavior

A recent flurry of studies has made substantial contributions to our understanding of the neural processing of visual speech and its contributions to audiovisual speech processing (Biau et al., 2021; Bourguignon et al., 2020; Files et al., 2013; Hauswald et al., 2018; Kim and Davis, 2014; Mégevand et al., 2020; O’Sullivan et al., 2017; Ozker et al., 2018; Suess et al., 2022; Venezia et al., 2017). These studies have detailed the representation of speech and language in visual cortex (Files et al., 2013; O’Sullivan et al., 2017), the tracking of unheard speech dynamics auditory cortex (Biau et al., 2021; Bourguignon et al., 2020; Mégevand et al., 2020), and the influence of top-down control of visual cortex during the transformation of visual speech representations into a linguistically relevant signal (Hauswald et al., 2018; Ozker et al., 2018; Suess et al., 2022). But studies have stopped short at directly relating neural activity in auditory or visual cortices to lipreading (c.f., Bröhl et al., 2022) because of two main limitations. One, many of these studies measure neural tracking with a reduced measure of the speech signal (e.g., the acoustic envelope) that is seemingly related to comprehension (Ahissar et al., 2001) until linguistic features are accounted for (Verschueren et al., 2022). In most cases, envelope tracking is, at worst, indexing energetic fluctuations (Lalor et al., 2009; Prinsloo and Lalor, 2022) or at best ambiguously measuring linguistic processing mixed with other responses that correlate with the envelope or other acoustic or linguistic features (Gillis et al., 2021). And two, most lack the necessary behavioral measurement to interpret the reception of visual speech, an important measurement due to the variability of human lipreading (Bernstein et al., 2000; Ross et al., 2007).

Our approach overcomes these limitations by relating a crucial measure of behavior to an explicit model of linguistic processing on a trial-by-trial basis while controlling for visual phonetic feature dynamics and general visual motion. By having participants rehearse a set of videos they were able to better track the content of visual speech. Comparing responses during rehearsed and unrehearsed videos, we were able to leverage within-subjects analyses to account for the large amount of person-to-person variability in lipreading ability. We stop short of labeling this approach as “training” since training typically requires feedback during training to generalize to untrained material (Bernstein et al., 2022, 2021). Although it is possible that the rehearsal paradigm did improve participant performance in the novel condition, we think it unlikely that participants are overall better lipreaders after an hour of rehearsal.

By relating our neural findings to simultaneously acquired behavior, we find strong evidence to support a basic level of behaviorally-relevant linguistic processing in visual cortex. Previous investigators have suggested that a novel area in visual cortex, which was given the moniker temporal visual speech area (TVSA), exhibits activity patterns suggestive of specialized speech processing (Bernstein et al., 2011) although their claim stops short of crediting TVSA with visual speech categorization duties. Using contrasts across several stimulus conditions, they found no support for specialized speech processing in other brain areas engaged in processing visual motion (e.g., middle temporal visual area) or faces (e.g., fusiform face area) that have been reported to be active during visible speech (Bernstein and Liebenthal, 2014; Calvert and Campbell, 2003). While our source localization results are supportive of a role for visual cortex in visemic processing, the low spatial resolution of EEG and the ill-posed nature of the inverse problem mean that we cannot be too firm in concluding that the sources of our visemic TRFs reside specifically in the TVSA. However, we were able to dissociate the neural signature of viseme tracking from activity related to lip movements themselves (Fig. 4b,c), consistent with findings from Bernstein et al. (2011). It seems possible that TVSA is indeed involved in this process, but more direct investigation is needed to determine if viseme-related activity overlaps with this area.

What is the role of auditory cortex during lipreading?

Our findings of a dominant role for visual cortex in processing visual linguistic features and its connection to lipreading performance raises the question of how to interpret the numerous findings showing activation of auditory cortex during silent speech. Indeed, some studies have shown a general activation of auditory cortex (Besle et al., 2008; Calvert et al., 1997; Ludman et al., 2000; Pekkola et al., 2005; Sams et al., 1991), while others have shown evidence that on-going activity in auditory cortex tracks speech dynamics (Biau et al., 2021; Bourguignon et al., 2020; Bröhl et al., 2022; Crosse et al., 2015b; Mégevand et al., 2020) and suggested that this could represent auditory cortex synthesizing acoustic features of unheard speech (Bourguignon et al., 2020; Bröhl et al., 2022), supporting a direct role for auditory cortex during lipreading. However, several findings complicate this interpretation. “Synthesis” (i.e., neural tracking) of acoustic features from visual speech could be explained by the strong correlations between those features and specific aspects of lip movements (Chandrasekaran et al., 2009; Plass et al., 2020); for example, in our experiment, the acoustic envelope can predict occipital EEG better than chance during visual speech, yet these results are not sufficient to conclude that visual cortex is representing the acoustic signal or phonological information. Still, broadband timing cues such as the envelope (and by extension, neural activity that tracks them) are not sufficient to support comprehension of speech (Shannon et al., 1995; Smith et al., 2002). Furthermore, these findings largely derive from low frequency neural activity (i.e., at or below 1 Hz), which is too slow to convey a rich representation of speech, such as at the rate of phonemes (9–13 Hz) or syllables (4 Hz). And finally, these effects are non-specific; auditory cortex tracks the dynamics of visual non-speech signals (Simon and Wallace, 2017; Thorne et al., 2011) and visual speech signal can affect the processing of acoustic non-speech signals (Biau et al., 2021; Kim and Davis, 2014). Together, these findings suggest that visual timing cues that are transmitted to auditory cortex may not be related to deriving representations of speech content or even specific to speech per se, but related to a more generalized multisensory process that is driven by temporal correlation (Maddox et al., 2015; Nidiffer et al., 2018; Parise et al., 2012) and exploits the natural redundancies in audiovisual speech to support perceptual sensitivity, attentional selection, and temporal prediction of speech in auditory cortex (Arnal et al., 2009; Plass et al., 2020; Zion Golumbic et al., 2013). The nature and function of auditory cortical activity during silent speech is very much an open question. More work is needed to determine if and how higher-order linguistic activity emerges in auditory cortex during silent speech and if one is discovered, how it interacts with its visual complement to support lipreading.

Viseme tracking is consistent with proposals of audiovisual speech processing

The abovementioned reinterpretation coupled with our findings dovetails nicely with two previous proposals related to audiovisual speech processing. In one proposal, visual speech cues are described, in relation to the acoustic signal, as providing two types of information: redundant and complementary (Campbell, 2008). Redundant features refer to the correlated dynamics of visual and acoustic speech (Chandrasekaran et al., 2009) while complementary cues refer to speech signals that are underspecified in the acoustic signal and conveyed more effectively visually. For example, some phonemes (e.g., /b/ and /d/ or /m/ and /n/) can be acoustically ambiguous, especially in noisy conditions, but their visible articulatory patterns are distinct and robust to noise. This duality of visual speech cues coincides neatly with another proposal suggesting that the integration of auditory and visual speech information occurs at two distinct levels of hierarchy (Peelle and Sommers, 2015). At an early integration stage and consistent with Campbell’s redundant mode, visual speech dynamics can increase sensitivity to acoustic information via early connections with auditory cortex (Bizley et al., 2007; Cappe and Barone, 2005; Falchier et al., 2010). A later stage integrates visual articulatory information, consistent with complementary cues, to constrain lexical selection of acoustically ambiguous phonemes and words. Although there is yet to be direct evidence for the integration of viseme and phoneme cues, indirectly the enhancement of acoustic representations (i.e., spectrogram) is similar in noisy and noise-free conditions while the enhancement of phonetic information appears to be more pronounced in noisy conditions (O’Sullivan et al., 2021), consistent with this role of complementary visual cues. Finally, the dual roles of visual speech may explain the finding of a “sweet spot” in audiovisual speech enhancement (Ross et al., 2007). As speech becomes contaminated by moderate levels of acoustic noise, redundant visual cues can successfully amplify degraded acoustic features (Plass et al., 2020) in tandem with an increased weighting of the visual speech representation (Nath and Beauchamp, 2011), leading to a peak in visual enhancement of acoustic speech at a signal-to-noise ratio of about −12 dB. But as noise levels increase further, the acoustic stream may become too corrupted for visual cues to repair and observers are forced to rely more on visual speech cues, which leads to a less robust linguistic representation and thus poorer comprehension.

Caveats

Several caveats to our findings are worth noting. Given the brief, intensive rehearsal paradigm, we anticipated that participants would rely on any of several bottom-up (e.g., increasing perceptual sensitivity) or top-down strategies (e.g., predictions based on their familiarity with semantic, lexical, phonological features) to better track the speech. Additionally, it is possible that the familiarity allowed participants to focus attention to the linguistic features necessary to lipread. Lastly, although we find it unlikely, it is possible that some participants were able to memorize the speech. Our analysis is unable to disambiguate between top-down or bottom-up influences, but regardless of the source, the benefits of better speech tracking are consistently restricted to higher-level linguistic (visemic) representation in visual cortex. Importantly this representation is evident even when participants had no prior knowledge of the speech (Fig. S1) and partially based on the mapping between lip movements and visemes (Fig. 5). Thus, some of that representation is likely driven by bottom-up processing. The fact that participants are potentially using primarily top-down information to track visual speech raises a concern that our paradigm is not improving lip-reading per se. However, top-down influence is a natural process of speech perception (Davis and Johnsrude, 2007) and visual speech perception is also shaped by top-down influences (Hauswald et al., 2018; Suess et al., 2022) albeit in a modality-specific manner (Bernstein et al., 2023). Based on the correlated multisensory nature of speech, visual speech processing is very likely shaped by activity in auditory cortex and vice versa during normal audiovisual speech perception and so we contend that our rehearsal paradigm is more closely approximating audiovisual speech conditions without the audio signal being present. Future work will be needed to disentangle these factors and to understand the influence of audiovisual experience (or the lack thereof – i.e., in deaf individuals) on lipreading.

Our findings could also be explained by a lack of engagement during novel speech (Bisley, 2011). According to this explanation, participants would be more engaged with a speech video that they were familiar with. Because of the nature of our paradigm, it is impossible to monitor for this effect in behavior. We therefore performed control analyses to exclude the possibility that participants were disengaging during novel videos. If participants were more engaged with the rehearsed videos, we would expect improved tracking of each of our visual features in the rehearsed condition (Bizley et al., 2016; Desimone and Duncan, 1995; Shinn-Cunningham, 2008). Yet, after isolating activity specific to each feature, we found no evidence of improved representation of the general (motion flow) or task-relevant (lip movements) features. We therefore think participant engagement is unlikely to explain our findings.

We are potentially under-characterizing the visual phonetic features in our study. We chose to focus on both horizontal and vertical dimensions of lip movement separately (e.g., Bourguignon et al. 2020) rather than a combined one-dimensional measure of mouth area (e.g., Chandrasekaran et al. 2009, Mégevand et al. 2020, Park et al. 2016) due to the correlation between certain mouth dimensions and acoustic features (e.g., horizontal expansion and contraction co-varying with phonetically-relevant spectral cues (Plass et al., 2020)). A more complete characterization of articulator dynamics (e.g., visible tongue, proper measurement of mouth area) may add some predictive value beyond lip movement alone, since tongue movements are manifested in speech acoustics. However, correlations between the dynamics of articulatory movements (Jiang et al., 2007, 2002) will limit this predictive value to instances where the tongue and lips are decoupled (e.g., /mama/ vs /lada/). Moreover, while characterizing these dynamics is surely to be informative on the nature of redundant visual cues, visemes describe an orthogonal feature (viz complementary cues) that incorporates the form of visible articulators, including the tongue. So we think characterizing the timing of tongue movements, other speech dynamics, or other derivative measures (after Daube et al., 2019) is unlikely to explain the improvement derived from rehearsal or its relation to lipreading.

Finally, although we found no evidence of visual speech tracking in auditory cortex, we cannot exclude the possibility that the auditory system is involved in shaping neural responses to visual linguistic cues or lipreading behavior in some capacity. In fact, we highly doubt that there is no interaction between auditory and visual linguistic processes in normal-hearing participants (Arnal et al., 2009) given the multisensory nature of the human experience with speech. However, from the topography of our TRF predictions and our source analysis, it is evident that our signal of interest is coming from visual cortical areas. Further, if those visual responses were simply recapitulating auditory responses to phonemes, we would expect a phoneme model to perform at least as well as a viseme model. In fact, the phoneme model, despite its extra features, performed significantly worse than the viseme model. However, it must be noted that our viseme model was suggested by Auer and Bernstein (1997) to optimally (in terms of balancing number of visemes and their phonemic information captured) account for averaged perceptual lip-reading data and is not necessarily an accurate description of individual visual speech perception (Bernstein et al., 2022). It is likely that individuals have better or worse (or qualitatively different) visual sensitivity to phonemes, making our measure of viseme tracking a conservative estimate. Importantly, this caveat is supported by evidence that individuals who are more adept at lipreading can discriminate some within-viseme articulations (Files et al., 2015), suggesting that no set of visemes is “one size fits all.” Future work could be aimed to optimize phoneme equivalence classes for each individual; individual viseme mappings could be related to behavioral measures of lipreading. Nevertheless, we interpret the better fit of a viseme model over a phoneme model as evidence that visual cortex cannot differentiate many phonemic categories. Based on the fact that many phonemes produce visually indistinguishable articulations in most observers (Fisher, 1968; Woodward and Barber, 1960), it follows that these categorical visual responses reflect visual perception to some degree, likely with some top-down involvement from auditory cortex and other brain areas associated with language processing.
