Physician Disagreement With Guidelines as a Critical Barrier to Achieving LDL-C Targets in Very High-Risk Patients: An Implementation Science Study
Authors: Kyung Hoon Cho, Young Rak Cho, Sang-Rok Lee, Jang-Whan Bae, Chang-Wook Nam, Yongwhi Park, Jin-Yong Hwang, Kyeong Ho Yun, Weon Kim, Min Chul Kim, Doo Sun Sim, Young Joon Hong, Ju Han Kim, Youngkeun Ahn, Joo-Yong Hahn, Myung Ho Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to investigate the challenges Korean physicians face in assisting their high-risk atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) patients in achieving guideline-recommended low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels.MethodsWe performed a prospective, implementation science study including 704 patients at very high risk of ASCVD with baseline LDL-C ≥ 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L) while receiving maximally tolerated statins and/or ezetimibe for secondary prevention between 2020 and 2022. The primary evaluation criteria were the proportions of participants achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL at each visit. The secondary evaluation criteria included the proportions of participants in diverse situations associated with guideline adoption.ResultsThe proportions of participants achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL at visits 2 (week 6–16) and 3 (week 18–30) were 56.3% and 58.7%, respectively. Among 307 patients on maximally tolerated statins and 397 patients on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 171 patients (55.7%) and 299 patients (75.3%) at visit 1, respectively. In both groups, physician disagreement with the guidelines was the major reason for not adopting the recommended therapy (70.8% and 46.2%, respectively), followed by patient refusal for reasons other than the cost, medical limitations such as comorbidities, and drug costs.ConclusionThis implementation science study for secondary prevention involving very high-risk ASCVD patients revealed that the proportion of patients achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL via lipid-lowering therapy was less than 60%. The main reason for not adopting the recommended LDL-C-lowering therapy was physician disagreement with the guidelines.Trial RegistrationClinical Research Information Service Identifier: KCT0005488Keywords: Cholesterol, LDL; Cardiovascular Diseases; Implementation Science; Drug Cost

INTRODUCTION

There is compelling evidence showing that low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) reduction therapy is beneficial for the primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).1,2 Recent guidelines on dyslipidemia emphasize reducing LDL-C levels by using maximally tolerated statins and/or non-statin therapy including ezetimibe and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors for patients with ASCVD.3,4,5,6 However, LDL-C-lowering strategies based on the best available evidence are poorly adopted in real-world settings. A considerable number of patients at very high risk of ASCVD have failed to achieve guideline-recommended LDL-C targets.7,8,9,10,11,12 Barriers to guideline implementation can exist at the physician, patient, and healthcare system levels.3 However, real-world data on this issue are limited.13 Furthermore, in recent guidelines, recommendations are inadequate to address the gap between optimal guideline-based lipid-lowering therapy and actual care.3,4,5,6

Implementation science facilitates a better understanding of the behavior of healthcare providers, one of the key variables in the adoption of evidence-based therapy, allowing the integration of the best evidence and healthcare practice. Here, as an implementation science study, we aimed to investigate the challenges physicians face in helping patients at high risk of ASCVD achieve guideline-recommended LDL-C levels for secondary prevention.

METHODS

Study design and population

The present study was an observational prospective, implementation science study involving 704 patients at very high risk of ASCVD with LDL-C ≥ 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L) while receiving maximally tolerated statins and/or ezetimibe for at least 3 months. A total of 704 patients were enrolled between October 2020 and November 2022 from the outpatient clinics of 10 tertiary teaching centers in South Korea. Of 704 patients, 307 patients were on maximally tolerated statins at baseline, and 397 patients were on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline. Data were collected online by a clinical research coordinator using the electronic case report form. This study has been registered at the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform of the World Health Organization (https://trialsearch.who.int/; KCT0005488). Patients who were at very high risk of ASCVD and needed intensified lipid-lowering therapy based on the guidelines were enrolled (visit 1) and were followed up at 6–16 weeks (visit 2) and 18–30 weeks (visit 3) after enrollment. Lipid profiles, physician feedback on the decision to change lipid-lowering therapy with the relevant reasons, and the implemented lipid-lowering drug regimen were recorded at each visit. Overall, 92.8% (653/704) and 95.3% (671/704) of patients were successfully followed up at visit 2 and visit 3, respectively. Repatha® (evolocumab) was the only available PCSK9 inhibitor in South Korea when the study was designed. A total of 54 physicians from 10 centers, who were mainly between 40 and 59 years old (80%) and predominantly male (90%), participated in this study with feedback.

Evaluation criteria and definition

The primary evaluation criteria were the proportions of participants achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L) at each visit. The secondary evaluation criteria included the proportions of participants in the following situations: receiving either ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors as add-on lipid-lowering therapy; not receiving either ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors mainly due to cost consideration/reimbursement limitations; not receiving either ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors mainly due to medical constraints such as comorbidities; not receiving either ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors mainly due to physician disagreement with the guidelines; not receiving either ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors mainly due to patient refusal (other than cost/reimbursement limitations). A very high risk of ASCVD was defined as any two major ASCVD events or one major ASCVD event with at least two high-risk conditions based on the 2018 American dyslipidemia guidelines (Supplementary Table 1).3 Familial hypercholesterolemia was considered when patients met the criteria of definitive diagnosis based on the Simon Broome or Dutch Criteria.14 Lipid profiles were directly measured by each participating center.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as the number of cases and percentages, which were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are expressed as the median (interquartile range [IQR]), which were compared using Student’s t-test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Descriptive data are presented separately for patients on maximally tolerated statins alone at baseline and on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline. The following variables with missing values were excluded from the analysis: height (n = 48), weight (n = 49), systolic blood pressure (n = 2), heart rate (n = 2), chronic kidney disease (n = 3), previous percutaneous coronary intervention (n = 1), previous coronary artery bypass graft (n = 8), previous heart failure (n = 2), previous cerebrovascular accident (n = 3), family history of premature coronary artery disease (n = 4), hemoglobin (n = 124), creatinine (n = 42), total cholesterol (n = 8), triglyceride (n = 3), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (n = 2), HbA1c (n =153), aspartate transaminase (n = 56), alanine transaminase (n = 57), and statin intensity (n = 7). Analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of each participating center, including Chonnam National University Hospital (CNUH-2020-229). All of the participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

Baseline and follow-up characteristics

The median age of 704 patients was 66.0 (59−72) years, and 580 (82.4%) patients were males. The median body weight was 70.0 (61.0−80.0) kg. Medical history demonstrated that 8.2%, 48.3%, 77.1%, 3.3%, 86.9%, 89.6%, and 10.3% of patients had heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, a previous myocardial infarction, a previous percutaneous coronary intervention, and previous cerebrovascular disease, respectively. In addition, 23.7% of patients were current smokers. The median (IQR) baseline LDL-C and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were 82.0 (74.0−94.5) and 45.0 (39.0−53.0) mg/dL, respectively (Table 1). Lipid-lowering therapy at baseline included statins for 697 (99.0%) patients (7 statin-intolerant patients) and ezetimibe for 397 (56.4%) patients.

Patients on statins at baseline (N = 307)

The baseline clinical and laboratory findings of patients on maximally tolerated statins at baseline are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Almost all patients (304/307, 99%) were on statins, and 1% of patients were statin-intolerant at enrollment. The median (IQR) baseline LDL-C level was 85.0 (75.0−97.5) mg/dL.

Among 307 patients on statins at baseline, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 171 (55.7%) patients at visit 1 (Fig. 2). The major reason for not adopting the recommended therapy was physician disagreement with the guidelines (70.8%), followed by patient refusal for reasons other than the cost (8.2%), medical limitations such as comorbidities (7.0%), and drug costs (6.4%). At visit 2, the median (IQR) LDL-C level was 68.0 (56.5−83.5) mg/dL (Supplementary Table 3). The proportions of participants achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL and < 55 mg/dL were 53.7% and 20.9%, respectively.

Physician feedback at visit 1 for patients on maximally tolerated statins at baseline (n = 307). (A) Decision of the physician regarding the current lipid-lowering therapy at visit 1. (B) Reasons for not changing the lipid-lowering therapy.

Among 124 patients on maximally tolerated statins at baseline with LDL-C > 70 mg/dL at visit 2, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 71.0% of patients (Supplementary Fig. 1). The major reason for not adopting the recommended therapy was physician disagreement with the guidelines (87.5%), followed by drug costs (12.5%) and patient refusal for reasons other than the cost (7.5%).

Lipid profiles at visits 2 and 3 according to the use of ezetimibe among patients on maximally tolerated statins at baseline (n = 307) are presented in Supplementary Table 4. In comparison with its counterparts, the use of ezetimibe was significantly associated with a greater tendency to achieve LDL-C < 70 mg/dL (66.4% vs. 41.6% at visit 2; 69.2% vs. 41.7% at visit 3).

Patients on statins and ezetimibe at baseline (N = 397)

The baseline clinical and laboratory findings of patients on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline are presented in Supplementary Table 5. Almost all patients (393/397, 99%) were on statins, and 1% of patients were statin-intolerant at enrollment. The median (IQR) baseline LDL-C level was 81.0 (74.0−92.0) mg/dL.

Among 397 patients on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 299 (75.3%) patients (Fig. 3). The main reason for not adopting the recommended therapy was physician disagreement with the guidelines (46.2%), followed by patient refusal for reasons other than the cost (15.7%), medical limitations such as comorbidities (14.4%), and drug costs (12.7%). At visit 2, the median (IQR) LDL-C level was 67.0 (54.0−80.0) mg/dL (Supplementary Table 6). The proportions of participants achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL and < 55 mg/dL were 58.8% and 26.7%, respectively.

Physician feedback at visit 1 for patients on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline (n = 397). (A) Decision of the physician regarding the current lipid-lowering therapy at visit 1. (B) Reasons for not changing the lipid-lowering therapy.

Among 139 patients on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline with LDL-C > 70 mg/dL at visit 2, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 87.1% of patients (Supplementary Fig. 2). The major reason for not adopting the recommended therapy was physician disagreement with the guidelines (50.4%), followed by patient refusal for reasons other than the cost (19.0%) and drug costs (16.5%).

Lipid profiles at visits 2 and 3 according to the use of evolocumab among patients on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline (n = 397) are presented in Supplementary Table 7. In comparison with its counterparts, the use of evolocumab was significantly associated with a greater tendency to achieve LDL-C < 70 mg/dL (87.5% vs. 55.8% at visit 2; 82.4% vs. 58.0% at visit 3).

Physician feedback and lipid-lowering therapy according to statin intensity at visit 1

Data on physician feedback and the use of lipid-lowering therapy according to statin intensity are presented in Supplementary Tables 8 and 9. Among patients on statins at baseline, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 55.7% (108/194) of patients on moderate-intensity statins and 54.6% (59/108) of patients on high-intensity statins at visit 1 (P = 0.974). The major reason for not adopting the recommended therapy was physician disagreement with the guidelines in both groups. Among 180 patients on moderate-intensity statins at baseline, 9.4% (17/180) of patients started to receive high-intensity statins and 42.8% (77/180) of patients started to receive ezetimibe at visit 1. Among 101 patients on high-intensity statins at baseline, 6.9% (7/101) of patients started to receive moderate-intensity statins and 54.5% (55/101) of patients started to receive ezetimibe at visit 1. Among patients on statins and ezetimibe at baseline, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 69.9% (95/136) of patients on moderate-intensity statins with ezetimibe and 78.5% (201/256) of patients on high-intensity statins with ezetimibe at visit 1 (P = 0.140). The major reason for not adopting the recommended therapy was physician disagreement with the guidelines in both groups. Among 127 patients on moderate-intensity statins with ezetimibe at baseline, 26.0% (33/127) of patients started to receive high-intensity statins and 2.4% (3/127) of patients started to receive evolocumab at visit 1. Among 235 patients on high-intensity statins with ezetimibe at baseline, 4.7% (11/235) of patients started to receive moderate-intensity statins and 12.0% (28/235) of patients started to receive evolocumab at visit 1.

DISCUSSION

This implementation science study enrolled 704 patients who were at very high risk of ASCVD and needed intensified lipid-lowering therapy for secondary prevention based on the guidelines (LDL-C ≥ 70 mg/dL). Lipid-lowering therapy at baseline included statins for 99.0% of patients (1.0% of statin-intolerant patients) and ezetimibe for 56.4% of patients. The proportions of participants achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL at 6−16 weeks and 18−30 weeks were 56.3% and 58.7%, respectively. Among 307 patients on maximally tolerated statins at baseline, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 171 (55.7%) patients. Among 397 patients on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe at baseline, physicians did not have an intention to intensify the lipid-lowering treatment regimen of 299 (75.3%) patients. Physician disagreement with the guidelines was the main reason for not adopting the recommended intensified LDL-C-lowering therapy for secondary prevention in patients at very high risk of ASCVD. The add-on use of non-statins based on the guidelines, including ezetimibe or evolocumab, significantly increased the proportion of patients achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL.

Randomized intervention trials have consistently demonstrated that lower LDL-C levels after acute coronary syndrome (ACS) are associated with a reduced risk of ASCVD events proportional to the absolute reduction in LDL-C and the cumulative duration of exposure to lower LDL-C.15 Recent American guidelines on dyslipidemia emphasize lowering LDL-C levels by using maximally tolerated statins and/or non-statin therapy for secondary prevention in patients at very high risk of ASCVD, including those with ACS.3 In European guidelines, the recommended goal for secondary prevention is to lower LDL-C levels to ≥ 50% from baseline and < 55 mg/dL for patients at very high risk of ASCVD.4 Both recent American and European guidelines emphasize more intensive LDL-C reduction for patients with ACS. However, LDL-C-lowering strategies based on the best available evidence have been poorly applied in real-world settings.7,8,9,10,11,12 Navar et al.10 analyzed 1,567 ACS patients from Asia-Pacific centers and demonstrated that approximately 80% of patients were discharged on a high-intensity statin regimen, and only 63.3% of patients had at least one LDL-C measurement during the follow-up period of 6 months, of whom 52.9% of patients had LDL-C levels of > 70 mg/dL. A retrospective observational study involving 10,071 patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention in the United Kingdom between 2012 and 2017 demonstrated that just under half (47.8%) of the patients achieved LDL-C ≤ 70 mg/dL (2016 target).9 Another retrospective analysis of data from an Australian ACS registry involving 2,671 eligible patients between 2009 and 2018 revealed that 45% of patients did not achieve an LDL-C goal of < 70 mg/dL within 12 months after an ACS event.11 Most recently, Khatib et al.12 analyzed data from 12 hospitals involving 10,589 patients with ACS in the United States between 2013 and 2019 and demonstrated that 49% of patients filled a high-intensity statin prescription at discharge, and only 36% of them were adherent at 1 year. In addition, 50% of eligible patients did not reach the LDL-C goal of < 70 mg/dL at 1 year.

In the present study, the proportions of patients achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL were 56.3% and 58.7% at 6–16 weeks and 18–30 weeks, respectively. The main reason for not adopting the recommended LDL-C-lowering therapy was physician disagreement with the guidelines, followed by patient refusal for reasons other than the cost of the drug, medical limitations such as comorbidities, and drug costs. The Guidelines Oriented Approach to Lipid Lowering (GOAL) Canada is an implementation science study on lipid-lowering therapy performed in Canada between 2015 and 2018.13 During the follow-up at visit 2 (141 ± 133 days after enrollment) and visit 3 (176 ± 145 days after visit 2), the proportions of patients achieving the Canadian Cardiovascular Society recommended LDL-C level of < 2.0 mmol/L were 41.7% and 50.8%, respectively. The top 3 reasons for not adopting the recommended LDL-C-lowering therapy with ezetimibe were patient refusal in 33% of cases, not needed by the physician in 22% of cases, and intolerance in 20% of cases; for therapy with PCSK9 inhibitors, the reasons were cost in 26% of cases, not needed by the physician in 27% of cases, and patient refusal in 25% of cases. In comparison with the GOAL Canada involving patients with clinical ASCVD or familial hypercholesterolemia, the present study enrolled patients at very high risk of ASCVD based on the 2018 American dyslipidemia guidelines. For these very high-risk ASCVD patients, the add-on use of non-statins, such as ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors, significantly increased the proportion of patients achieving LDL-C < 70 mg/dL or < 55 mg/dL. These findings highlight the importance of non-statins in achieving optimal LDL-C targets for patients at very high risk of ASCVD.

Barriers to adopting guidelines can exist at the patient, physician, and healthcare system levels.3 Recent guidelines on dyslipidemia highlight the importance of guideline-directed medical therapy implementation and focus on patient-related factors; however, physician disagreement with the guidelines has not been well addressed.3,4 Recently, a survey by the Korean Society of Myocardial Infarction on the existing lipid guidelines for patients with acute myocardial infarction was conducted in July 2022, and a total of 407 members of the Korean Society of Cardiology responded.16 The findings showed that although most of the participants were aware of the changes in the recent Korean lipid guidelines, more than half of them did not agree with LDL-C targets of < 55 mg/dL, and a quarter of them did not use > 50% LDL-C reduction from baseline as a target. The main reason for not following the new LDL-C targets was suggested to be a lack of data on efficacy and safety for Korean patients. In this regard, one of the potential reasons for physician disagreement with the guidelines in the present study may include the perceived lack of local evidence on Korean patients. Furthermore, it might be partly attributed to substantial changes in the recent guidelines on dyslipidemia and considerable discrepancies between American and European guidelines.3,4,17,18 In addition, physicians may experience clinical inertia, where they are skeptical about the overall benefits of aggressive lipid-lowering therapy for their patients due to concerns about adherence and side effects.19 The publication of guidelines does not guarantee their implementation. Healthcare delivery is highly complex, and physicians are at the forefront of therapy provision based on the best available evidence. Consequently, physician disagreement with the guidelines may have a significant impact on guideline implementation, leading to large gaps in care.

Certain limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of the study. First, the present study was conducted in South Korea, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, selection bias at the center and physician level may also reduce the generalizability of the results. Although the participating centers were tertiary university hospitals, which are regarded as having higher levels of guideline awareness, physician disagreement with the guidelines was the main reason for not adopting guideline-recommended lipid-lowering therapy. Third, the duration of previous ASCVD, baseline LDL-C levels, and percentage reduction from baseline after the index clinical event were not included. Finally, patient-clinician discussions and lifestyle changes were not considered in the study, which might affect the decision-making between physicians and patients.

In conclusion, this implementation science study involving a population at very high risk of ASCVD in South Korea demonstrated that physician disagreement with the guidelines was the main reason for not adopting the recommended intensified LDL-C-lowering therapy for secondary prevention. Given that physicians are at the forefront of therapy provision based on the best available evidence, greater effort is needed to reduce physician disagreement with the guidelines on lipid-lowering therapy to improve clinical outcomes for patients at very high risk of ASCVD.
