Liver metastases of hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative metastatic breast cancer patients: an ominous sign
Authors: Peng-Hui Wang
Published: 2025

Besides metabolic syndrome-associated diseases, such as diabetes mellitus, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, which pose major global health challenges,1–4 cancer has become the leading cause of disability and mortality in both genders.5,6 The incidence of cancers, regardless of origin, has continued to rise steeply, and the trend appears unending, except for certain types of cancers, particularly oncovirus-related cancers, such as cervical cancer,7–9 liver cancer,10 and other rare tumors, which be more effectively controlled or even eradicated through effective screening strategy, preventive vaccination, and accurate early diagnosis, particularly before occurrence of invasive cancer status, blocking transmission of infectious microorganisms, and precise targeted therapies. Additionally, cancers not only cause direct damage to the affected organ but also lead to organ dysfunction and, ultimately, the loss of life. In this editorial, we discuss how recent advances—including improved understanding of tumor molecular biology, the tumor microenvironment, and the importance of multimodality treatment strategies—may have begun to change the outlook for cancer care.11–16 However, there is still a proportion of cancer patients who are resistant to treatment and eventually die of the disease. Many hypotheses have been proposed to overcome therapeutic resistance, and one of the most feasible strategies is combination therapy—a multimodality approach.

Recently, Dr. Kao published an important article in the Journal of the Chinese Medical Association (JCMA) addressing the real-world outcomes of everolimus-based treatment for hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative metastatic breast cancer (HR+/HER2− mBC).17 Although the outcomes of HR+/HER2− mBC patients are expected to be poor—because more than half had received at least three prior lines of systemic therapy, were in relatively poor overall physical condition, and faced a high risk of treatment resistance—the study still provides valuable insights.17 The authors investigated factors associated with worse prognosis in these mBC patients who had received everolimus-based therapy.17 Multivariate analysis was used to adjust for other confounding factors and identify significant and independent predictors for both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).17 The results showed that both liver and central nervous system (CNS) metastases were strong independent risk factors associated with shorter PFS, with hazard ratio (HR) of 1.43 (95% CI, 1.08-1.90) and 2.16 (95% CI, 1.34-3.50), respectively.17 Additionally, liver metastases were the single most dominant risk factor associated with worst OS (shortening OS) with HR of 1.50 (95% CI, 1.06-2.11) whereas, in terms of OS, CNS metastases did not have a statistically significant adverse impact on OS of HR+/HER2− mBC patients (HR: 1.65, 95% CI, 0.93-2.94).17 Although the authors concluded that “patients with liver and CNS metastases had significantly shorter PFS and OS,” this conclusion warrants clarification. Based on our understanding, we believe that liver metastases may be the only factor truly associated with therapeutic failure and mortality in HR+/HER2− mBC patients. This interpretation is supported by several considerations. In fact, we believe that liver metastases represent a strongly unfavorable prognostic indicator in HR+/HER2− mBC patients. Because the authors did not elaborate on this finding, we believe it is important to further address the clinical significance of liver metastases in the course of metastatic and recurrent (m/r) cancer.

As in our previous editorial comment,18 the authors found that metastatic/recurrent (m/r) sites were a strong predictor of worse prognosis in HR+/HER2− mBC patients treated with everolimus-based therapy.17 Although both CNS involvement and liver metastases are clinically important, their impacts on outcomes may differ substantially. Additionally, unlike brain—which are protected by the neurovascular unit and the blood–brain barrier (BBB), limiting drug penetration—liver metastases lack such barriers and are fully exposed to anticancer therapies. However, the outcome of liver metastases appear to be as poor as, or even poorer than, those of brain metastases, suggesting that additional factors may influence treatment response. Moreover, compared to HER2-positive mBC, HER2-negative mBC is less frequently associated with brain metastases; therefore, our discussion focuses on liver metastases in HR+/HER2− mBC patients. A separate editorial will address brain metastases in detail.

Liver metastases result in significant cancer-related death across nearly all primary malignancies, because they contribute to extremely worse clinical outcomes with a sharp decline in survival rates after diagnosis (up to three-quarters of patients will die within 1 year).19 Therefore, it is not surprising to find the worst outcomes of HR+/HER2− mBC patients reported in the study. Conventionally, surgical resection of liver metastases has long been considered the therapy of choice associated with the highest 5-year OS rates, and up to 65% of selected mBC patients undergo surgical resection of liver metastases.19 Unfortunately, Dr. Kao did not mention this part, and the audience may be interested in the data on how many patients have been treated with surgical resection or other evolving local treatment options, such as thermoablation using radiofrequency (RFA) or microwave ablation (MA), or stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), through the lens of efficacy, limitations, and clinical applicability. In addition, information on other modalities, including hepatic artery embolization, alcohol injection, and chemoembolization therapy, and many others, is simultaneously obtained when these HR+/HER2− mBC patients have been treated with everolimus-based therapy. In fact, the addition of liver-directed therapy to systemic treatment, such as chemotherapy, target-guided agonist-based therapy or immunotherapy, or even antibody-drug conjugates, and of course, inclusive the authors’ mentioned everolimus therapy, has been shown to significantly enhance the therapeutic efficacy and subsequently prolong patient survival, because a combined modality approach has demonstrated a marked improvement in both PFS and OS over systemic therapy alone (HR: 0.58).19

Another issue about liver metastases is the compliance of the cancer patients. Nearly all randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have excluded patients with cancer and abnormal liver function. The cutoff value of liver function is strictly limited to ≤3 times and ≤5 times of upper limit of normal (ULN) in the enrolled cancer patients with and without liver metastases and most importantly, if bilirubin level is above 1.25 × ULN, these cancer patients cannot participate in RCTs,19 suggesting that the data from RCT, although the evidence level is strong and the results will change the guideline for treating cancer patients, may not be really good in clinical routine practice. These cancer patients in the RCTs are much healthier than the general population. By contrast, in our clinical routine practice, these cancer patients may be complicated with many unhealthy problems, and of course, laboratory abnormalities are very common. Therefore, Franks et al20 estimated time-to-treatment discontinuation (TTD) and OS for patients with and without laboratory abnormalities receiving a targeted therapy for mBC patients (n = 1585 for everolimus therapy). The results showed mBC patients without any liver laboratory abnormality have a longer mean OS of 7.0-month survival (95% CI, 4.4-11.2 months) compared to those mBC patients with at least one laboratory abnormality of liver with a mean OS of 5.3 months (95% CI, 3.2-8.7 months), and the HR was 0.75 (95% CI, 0.64-0.89), meaning that only liver function impairment of the similar mBC patients will increase the risk of death up to 33%. It is reasonable to believe that liver metastases are strongly and positively correlated with liver laboratory abnormalities and it is not only directly correlated with the choice of drugs (some effective drugs are very effective but cannot be applied due to the need for hepatic metabolism or the risk of liver toxicity) but also directly involved in dosage reduction and even discontinuation of the application of these effective drugs. This hypothesis is also supported by studies investigating other targeted agents, such as CDK4/6i (cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 5 inhibitors, including palbociclib, abemaciclib, or ribociclib, n = 5239) and alpelisib (n = 601) with dramatically reducing OS rate with HR of 0.56 (95% CI, 0.48-0.64) and 0.61 (95% CI, 0.47-0.79), respectively.20 Additionally, the mean OS months are also reflective of this critical issue (laboratory abnormality of liver), showing 19.3 months (95% CI, 14.6-25.2 months) vs 10.8 months (95% CI, 7.9-14.7 months) in mBC patients treated with CDK4/6 inhibitor and 5.2 months (95% CI, 3.4-8.0 months) vs 3.2 months (95% CI, 1.9-5.2 months) in mBC patients treated with alpelisib.20 All result in worse prognosis of liver metastases in the mBC patients compared to other site metastases in the mBC population, as shown by everolimus-based real-world data.17

Taken together, following our previous comment congratulating Dr. Kao’s contribution to managing HR+/HER2− mBC patients by multicenter real-world experience,18 management of liver metastases in HR+/HER2− mBC patients, although they account for a small proportion, represents an unmet clinical need. The updated information and consensus for treating HR+/HER2− mBC patients at the highest risk of cancer-related death, such as those complicated with liver metastases, is urgently needed. However, all of the aforementioned changes need tight cooperation among all healthcare institutes, not only depending on RCTs, but also needing real-world clinical practice. We are looking forward to seeing much experience-sharing on this topic. With these, we believe that patients’ care may be continuously becoming better and better.
