Hells protects mitochondrial integrity via Nr2f2 suppression during osteoclast differentiation
Authors: Myoung Jun Kim, Sung Kyung Choi, Bitnuri Yu, Minho Kim, Un Yung Choi, Sunsook Hwang, Wahn Soo Choi, Jueng Soo You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoclasts are specialized bone-resorbing cells essential for skeletal homeostasis. While chromatin remodelers play critical roles in gene regulation, their specific contributions to osteoclastogenesis remain largely unexplored. In particular, the chromatin remodeler Hells has not been previously investigated in osteoclast differentiation.MethodsTo identify key chromatin remodelers involved in osteoclast differentiation, we performed RNA-seq analysis and compared the differentially expressed remodelers with publicly available human osteoclast RNA-seq data. Among them, Hells was significantly upregulated, which was validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis. To investigate the functional role of Hells, we assessed the effects of Hells KD via siRNA on osteoclast differentiation using TRAP staining, as well as on mitochondrial function by measuring membrane potential, ATP levels, mitochondrial biogenesis, and mitophagy. Bioinformatic analyses were conducted to characterize the Hells-dependent transcriptome. ChIP-qPCR and FAIRE-qPCR were employed to evaluate Hells binding and chromatin accessibility at the Nr2f2 promoter, respectively. Rescue experiments using the Nr2f2 inhibitor and Nr2f2-specific siRNA confirmed the regulatory axis between Hells and Nr2f2 during osteoclastogenesis. Furthermore, baicalin hydrate was applied at varying concentrations to examine its effects on Hells expression, osteoclast differentiation, and mitochondrial integrity. Lastly, we performed ectopic expression of Hells under conditions where its expression was suppressed by baicalin hydrate treatment.ResultsHells expression increased early during osteoclast differentiation, and KD of Hells impaired this process, accompanied by downregulation of key osteoclastogenic markers. Notably, the Hells-dependent transcriptome highlighted mitochondria as the most vulnerable organelle. Mechanistically, Hells directly represses Nr2f2 transcription, thereby maintaining mitochondrial quality and quantity through coordinated regulation of biogenesis and mitophagy. Inhibition of Nr2f2 restored mitochondrial function and osteoclast differentiation in Hells-deficient cells, confirming the presence of a crucial Hells–Nr2f2 axis in osteoclastogenesis. Furthermore, the effect of baicalin hydrate on osteoclast differentiation depended on Hells expression and correlated with mitochondrial integrity.ConclusionsOur study identifies Hells as a key epigenetic regulator of osteoclast differentiation, acting through direct repression of Nr2f2 to ensure proper mitochondrial biogenesis and function. This Hells–Nr2f2 axis establishes a vital connection between nuclear epigenetic regulation and mitochondrial homeostasis, highlighting a promising therapeutic target for osteoclast-related disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02851-x.Keywords: Osteoclast differentiation, Chromatin remodeler, Hells, Nr2f2, Mitochondria

Background

Bone functions as the body's structural framework and a crucial mineral reservoir, primarily for calcium and phosphorus [1–3]. Bone health hinges on a dynamic equilibrium between bone formation and resorption. Osteoblasts, which are responsible for bone formation, and osteoclasts, which handle bone resorption, work together to maintain this equilibrium [4, 5]. Osteoclasts, which are large multinucleated cells, differentiate from mononuclear hematopoietic myeloid progenitor cells found in the bone marrow by the growth factors, including macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-κB) ligand (RANKL) [6, 7]. Given that mature osteoclasts release acidic and proteolytic enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinase-9 (Mmp9) and cathepsin K (CtsK), which allow them to dissolve the bone matrix, elevated osteoclast activity or number plays a central role in the pathology of bone diseases such as osteoporosis, osteonecrosis, and Paget's disease [8].

Osteoclastogenesis is tightly regulated by a coordinated network of transcription factors (TFs) and epigenetic modifiers, including writers, erasers, and readers of chromatin marks. Among DNA methylation writers, the de novo methyltransferase DNA methyltransferase 3 alpha (Dnmt3a) plays a pivotal role during RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation by repressing interferon regulatory factor 8 (Irf8), a known negative regulator of osteoclastogenesis [9]. This methylation-dependent silencing facilitates the metabolic reprogramming required for osteoclast function. Histone modification erasers also contribute significantly to this process. The histone demethylase lysine (K)-specific demethylase 4b (Kdm4b), together with cofactors cell division cycle and apoptosis regulator 1 (Ccar1) and mediator complex subunit 1 (Med1), is recruited to the promoters of osteoclast-related genes upon RANKL stimulation [10]. This leads to demethylation of H3K9me3, enhancing transcriptional activation and promoting osteoclast formation. Likewise, histone deacetylase 2 (Hdac2) facilitates osteoclast differentiation by activating the protein kinase b (Akt) signaling pathway, which in turn phosphorylates and inhibits forkhead box o1 (Foxo1), a transcriptional suppressor of osteoclastogenesis [11]. Among the reader proteins, bromodomain-containing 9 (Brd9), a bromodomain-containing protein, interacts with forkhead box p1 (Foxp1) at the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (Stat1) promoter, promoting Stat1 transcription [12]. This activates the interferon beta (IFN-β) signaling pathway, which acts as a negative feedback loop to restrain excessive osteoclastogenesis. While covalent chromatin modifications are well recognized in osteoclast biology, ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers remain less studied. Hells (helicase, lymphoid-specific), an ATP-dependent chromatin remodeler of the SNF2 family, functions as a key regulator of chromatin accessibility and epigenetic stability across diverse biological contexts [13–19]. Hells cooperates with CDCA7 and DNA methyltransferases to organize nucleosome architecture and maintain DNA methylation, particularly at heterochromatic and repetitive genomic regions, and further contributes to transcriptional regulation and DNA double-strand break repair within compact chromatin domains [13–17]. Consistent with these molecular activities, Hells regulates lineage-specific transcriptional programs during development, including epigenetic repression of pluripotency-associated loci in embryonic stem cells and the maintenance of self-renewal and proliferative capacity in neural stem and progenitor cells [20–22]. These studies establish Hells as a versatile chromatin remodeler that integrates nucleosome organization, epigenetic regulation, and genome maintenance to support cell identity and differentiation. Despite these well-established roles in other contexts, whether Hells exerts a comparable chromatin-based regulatory function during osteoclast differentiation remains unexplored.

Due to the high energy demand during differentiation and mature osteoclast activity, osteoclasts appear to have unique mitochondrial strategies. RANKL stimulation increases peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivators-1 β (Pgc1β) expression, which then promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, resulting in enhanced mitochondrial mass and function [23–26]. Conversely, mitochondrial defects disrupt osteoclast differentiation. Mice deficient in transcription factor A, mitochondrial (Tfam) or NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit S4 (Ndufs4) show reduced osteoclast differentiation and increased bone mass [24, 27, 28]. In fact, treatment with ATP synthase inhibitors or mitochondrial complex inhibitors hampers osteoclast maturation [29]. Overall, these studies highlight that osteoclast differentiation and activity significantly depend on mitochondrial function and ATP.

In this study, we investigated the role of the chromatin remodeler Hells in osteoclast differentiation. Our data demonstrate that RANKL-induced upregulation of Hells is essential for effective osteoclastogenesis. Loss of Hells impaired osteoclast differentiation by downregulating mitochondrial-related gene expression, leading to disrupted mitochondrial function, impaired biogenesis, and altered mitophagy. Mechanistically, this was attributed to the upregulation of nuclear receptor subfamily 2 group F member 2 (Nr2f2) following Hells depletion, as confirmed by rescue experiments using an Nr2f2 inhibitor and Nr2f2-specific siRNA. These findings highlight the role of the Hells–Nr2f2 axis in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and osteoclast function, offering new insights into the interplay between chromatin remodeling and mitochondrial integrity during osteoclastogenesis.

Methods

Isolation and differentiation of osteoclasts

C57BL/6 male mice, aged 5 weeks, were purchased from Orientbio Inc. (Orientbio, Gyeonggi-do, Korea). Following the sacrifice of mice, whole bone marrow cells were extracted from the femurs and tibias. All bone marrow cells were cultured in complete minimum essential medium alpha (α-MEM) medium, consisting of α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Welgene, Gyeongsangbuk-do, Korea), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), and 100 units/mL penicillin–streptomycin (GenDEPOT, Barker, TX, USA) for 24 h (h). Suspended cells were cultured in complete α-MEM medium supplemented with 30 ng/mL M-CSF (ProSpec, Ness Ziona, Israel) for 72 h. After 72 h, the adhered cells were utilized as bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) and plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well and in 35 mm plates at a density of 5.0 × 105 cells per plate. They were subsequently cultured in complete α-MEM medium supplemented with 30 ng/mL M-CSF and 100 ng/mL RANKL (ProSpec, Ness Ziona, Israel), along with glutamine (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) for 96 h.

TRAP staining

Multinucleated mature osteoclasts were confirmed by staining for tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) positive cells. The culture medium was removed from a 96-well cell culture plate containing mature osteoclasts, and each well was carefully rinsed with PBS. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, 200 μl of a 1:1 mixture of acetone and ethanol was added to each well. The wells were gently washed with PBS three times, and cells were stained with 50 μl of the staining reagent for 30 min at room temperature. The composition of the staining reagent for 96 wells is as follows. 416.5 μg of Naphthol AS-MX phosphate (N4875, Sigma-Aldrich), 41.65 μl of N, N-Dimethylformamide (D4551, Sigma-Aldrich), 5 μl of acetic acid, 25 mg of anhydrous sodium acetate (791,741–100 G, Sigma-Aldrich), 57.5 mg of sodium tartrate dibasic dihydrate (S4797, Sigma-Aldrich), and 4.9 ml of deionized water.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (#17061, iNtRON, Gyeonggi-do, Korea). The RNA samples were standardized to an equivalent concentration and reverse-transcribed using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (#4368813, Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The cDNA samples were analyzed utilizing a Cycler® 480 II with LightCycler® 480 SYBR Green I master mix (#04887352001, Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland), in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines. The RT-qPCR analysis proceeded with an initial denaturation phase of 5 min at 95 °C, followed by 45 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 10 s. The RNA values were quantified automatically using the LightCycler® 480 program (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The following primers were used in this study: Hells_forward: 5’-TCA CAG GAG GAG TAA TGA GGT G-3’, Hells_reverse: 5’-CAA ACA AGA AAA GGT CCA GGT-3’, Nfatc1_forward: 5’-GCC TTT TGC GAG CAG TAT CT-3’, Nfatc1_reverse: 5’-TCA TAG TGA GCC CTG TGG TG-3’; CtsK_forward: 5’-AGG CAT TGA CTC TGA AGA TGC T-3’, CtsK_reverse: 5’-TCC CCA CAG GAA TCT CTC TG-3’, Mmp9_forward: 5’-TGT CAT CCA GTT TGG TGT CG-3’, Mmp9_reverse: 5’-AAT GGG CAT CTC CCT GAA C-3’, Nr2f2_forward: 5’-CCA AGA GCA AGT GGA GAA GC-3’, Nr2f2_reverse: 5’-TTC CAA AGC ACA CTG GGA CT-3’, Pink1_forward: 5’-CCT TTC AAC AGC TCC AGT GT-3’, Pink1_reverse: 5’-GCC CAA AGA TTT CAT AGG CGA-3’, β-actin_forward: 5’-AAG TGT GAC GTT GAC ATC CG-3’, β-actin_reverse: 5’-GAT CCA CAT CTG CTG GAA GG-3’.

Western blot

The BMMs were lysed using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and 1 mM EDTA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were subsequently transferred to a new tube. According to the experiment, 30 μg of whole-cell extract was subjected to SDS-PAGE on 10 ~ 12% gels and subsequently transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were incubated with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. Following blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were rinsed with TBS-T and incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody in 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with TBS-T, the membranes were visualized utilizing an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit and subsequently exposed on a LAS-3000 image detection system (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). This study utilized the following antibodies: anti-Hells (A300-226A, Betyl, 1:1,000), anti-Nfatc1(sc-7294, Santa Cruz, 1:500), anti-Nr2f2 (sc-271940, Santa Cruz, 1:500), anti-total oxidative phosphorylation (ab110413, abcam, 1:500), and anti-β-actin (4967S, Cell Signaling, 1:2,000).

Mitochondrial protein extraction

Mitochondria were extracted using the Mitochondria Isolation Kit for cultured cells (#89874, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For mitochondrial protein extraction, isolated mitochondria were lysed with RIPA buffer for 20 min at 4 °C. According to the experiment, 10 μg of lysate was analyzed by Western blot assay. This study utilized the following antibodies: anti-LC3B (ab48394, abcam, 1:2,000), anti-Mtco1 (ab203912, abcam, 1:1,000).

mRNA-sequencing

mRNA for mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) was extracted according to the manufacturer's instructions utilizing the miRNeasy Mini Kit (217,004, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), followed by treatment with DNase (79,254, Qiagen). The library was constructed and sequenced using the NovaSeq platform by Illumina Inc. The paired-end method was employed. Trimmomatic 0.39 was utilized to eliminate adapters and low-quality sequences. The filtered reads were aligned to the mouse genome reference (GRCm38; mm10) utilizing STAR (2.7.3a). RSEM (v1.3.1) was employed to quantify and transform the mapped reads into TPM values. Fold-change and statistical significance for identifying differentially expressed genes were computed using DESeq2 (1.26.0). The dataset was submitted to the NCBI database.

Gene knockdown (KD) by siRNA transfection

The Hells-specific siRNA, the Nr2f2-specific siRNA, and the negative siRNA were obtained from Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea). BMMs were seeded at the mentioned densities and incubated for 24 h with M-CSF. After 24 h, for 96-well plates, 100 nM of siRNA was combined with 0.25 μl of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (#13778150, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 25 μl of Transfection Optimized Medium (#TR 004–01, Welgene), and for 35 mm dishes, 100 nM of siRNA was combined with 5 μl of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX in 250 μl of TOM. The mixtures were gently mixed and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Following incubation, siRNAs were transfected into the cells for 12 h. Subsequently, the media was substituted with one containing M-CSF and RANKL. The following sequences were used in this study: siHells #1: 5’-GAG UCU UGG UUU GAU AUC A dTdT-3’, siHells #2: 5’-UGA UAU CAA ACC AAG ACU C dTdT-3’, siNr2f2: 5’-AGU GUG UGA AGU UUC UCU A dTdT −3’.

ChIP-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions (Upstate Biotechnology, Inc., Lake Placid, NY, USA). DNA was fragmented by sonication to obtain segments ranging from 200 to 500 base pairs. The sheared DNA underwent pre-clearing with protein A magnetic beads (92,590, EMD Millipore), followed by immunoprecipitation of 50 µg DNA using ab-Hells (sc49437, Santa Cruz). Subsequent to immunoprecipitation, the isolated chromatin fragments were subjected to PCR analysis. IgG control experiments were conducted for all ChIPs and integrated into the IP/Input (1%) by expressing the results as (IP-IgG)/(Input-IgG). The PCR primer sequences used were as follows: Nr2f2 proximal promoter, forward, 5′-AGT GAG AGC GAG CGA GATC-3′ and reverse, 5′-TTG TCG ATT GCT TGT CTG GC-3′.

FAIRE-qPCR

Formaldehyde-assisted isolation of regulatory elements (FAIRE)-qPCR was conducted as previously delineated [30]. Cells were crosslinked with formaldehyde, and FAIRE and Input DNA were subsequently prepared from the cells. The results are presented as the mean and range of variation from triplicate RT-qPCR assays conducted on the identical DNA sample. Results are presented as the percentage of input chromatin (Input DNA) and are obtained from a single experiment that is representative of a minimum of two independent experiments. The PCR primer sequences used were as follows: Nr2f2 proximal promoter, forward, 5′-AGT GAG AGC GAG CGA GATC-3′ and reverse, 5′-TTG TCG ATT GCT TGT CTG GC-3′.

Mitochondrial mass measurement

Mitochondrial mass was assessed using MitoTracker staining following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, BMMs were transfected with siHells for 2 days, after which they were incubated with 250 nM MitoTracker Green (M7514, Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in cell culture medium at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator for 30 min. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde PFA. The fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min and blocked in 3% goat serum (#005–000–121, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, Inc., West Grove, PA, USA) for 30 min. The cells were stained with Ac-Hells (A300-226A, Betyl, 1:100) and Brilliant Violet™ 421-AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (#111–545-003, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, Inc., 1:500) for 30 min at room temperature and mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade reagent. Fluorescent signals were detected and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC-1 kit (T3168, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, BMMs were transfected with siHells for 2 days, followed by incubation with 1 µg/mL JC-1 diluted in cell culture medium at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator for 30 min. Cells were then fixed with 4% PFA, followed by Hells staining using the same procedure applied for Mitotracker, and subsequently mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade reagent. Fluorescent signals were detected, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Mitophagy measurement

BMMs were seeded in 12-well plates at the indicated densities and incubated for 24 h with M-CSF. After 24 h, cells were transfected with the pCLBW cox8 EGFP mCherry (#78520, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) expressing plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. 6 h post-transfection, RANKL was added to induce osteoclast differentiation. After 48 h, cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA. Hells was stained using the same procedure applied for Mitotracker. Samples were mounted using ProLong Diamond Antifade Reagent for confocal microscopy. Confocal microscopy was used to detect fluorescent signals, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. The ratio of EGFP to mCherry fluorescence intensity was analyzed to evaluate mitophagy activity.

Mitochondrial biogenesis measurement

Mitochondrial biogenesis was assessed using the MitoTimer plasmid (#52659, Addgene). The procedure was identical to the mitophagy assay, except that cells were transfected with MitoTimer instead of pCLBW-COX8-EGFP-mCherry. Fluorescent signals were detected, and the red-to-green fluorescence ratio was analyzed using a fluorescence microscope.

Intracellular ATP measurement

Intracellular ATP levels were assessed using the ATeam1.03-nD/nA/pcDNA3 plasmid (#51958, Addgene). The procedure was identical to the mitophagy assay, except that cells were transfected with ATeam1.03-nD/nA/pcDNA3 instead of pCLBW-COX8-EGFP-mCherry. Fluorescent signals were detected, and the fluorescence intensity ratio was analyzed using a fluorescence microscope.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

At 48 h after Hells KD, cells were washed twice with Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, H6648, Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated with 20 μM 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA, D399, Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in HBSS for 10 min at 37 °C in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with HBSS and further incubated in fresh HBSS for an additional 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Then, 100 μl of cell suspension was transferred to a black 96-well plate. DCF fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader at 485 nm excitation and 535 nm emission wavelengths. Fluorescence intensity was used as an indicator of intracellular ROS levels.

Results

Upregulated Hells promotes osteoclast differentiation

To identify the critical chromatin remodelers involved in osteoclast differentiation, we analyzed transcriptomic data from RANKL-stimulated bone marrow-derived osteoclast early differentiation and human osteoclast differentiation [31, 32]. The analysis revealed that Hells, Smarca1, and Actr6 were among the chromatin remodelers consistently upregulated in both datasets (Fig. 1A). Among these, Hells has been implicated in multiple differentiation contexts, including embryonic stem cells and neural lineages, where it plays roles closely linked to cell fate regulation [20–22]. However, its role in osteoclast differentiation has not been reported previously. We therefore decided to investigate the potential relationship between Hells and osteoclastogenesis. To this end, we first analyzed its expression during RANKL-induced differentiation and observed an upregulation of Hells, as confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1B) and Western blotting (Fig. 1C and D). To determine whether this RANKL-induced upregulation of Hells is essential for osteoclast differentiation, we performed Hells KD using specific siRNAs. Silencing Hells significantly reduced the expression of nuclear factor of activated T cell 1 (Nfatc1), a master regulator of osteoclastogenesis, along with the osteoclast functional markers Mmp9 and CtsK (Fig. 1E, F, and G). Additionally, TRAP staining, a marker of mature osteoclasts based on TRAP activity, revealed a marked decrease in mature TRAP-positive osteoclasts upon Hells depletion (Fig. 1H). These findings indicate that Hells is required for RANKL-mediated osteoclast differentiation.

Hells is a hidden mitochondrial regulator in osteoclastogenesis

To profile the Hells-dependent transcriptomic landscape during RANKL-mediated osteoclast differentiation, we performed mRNA-seq following siRNA-mediated Hells KD (Supp Fig. 1A). The mRNA-seq data indicated that there were 2,183 significantly upregulated and 2,067 downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (absolute fold change ≥ 1.5 and P value < 0.05) in the Hells KD osteoclast compared to the control (Fig. 2A). Gene ontology (GO) analysis demonstrated that the upregulated genes were primarily enriched pathways related to the extracellular matrix (ECM) and collagen. In contrast, the downregulated genes showed significant enrichment in mitochondrial-related pathways, such as the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, respiratory electron transport, ATP synthesis, and mitochondrial protein synthesis (Fig. 2B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that gene sets associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition and cytokine production were overrepresented, while those connected to mitochondrial processes were significantly underrepresented (Fig. 2C). A heatmap of energy source gene sets further illustrated significant suppression of mitochondrial TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) following Hells KD (Supp Fig. 1B), while only modestly affecting a few glycolysis-related genes localized to the cytoplasm (Supp Fig. 1C). As multiple analyses consistently indicated suppression of mitochondria-associated pathways, and given the central role of mitochondria during osteoclastogenesis, we examined whether these transcriptomic changes were mirrored at the subcellular level. Subcellular localization analysis revealed that approximately 20.9% of the downregulated genes were localized to the mitochondria, which is markedly higher than the approximately 5.5% expected from all mouse genes [33], indicating a selective repression of mitochondria-localized genes (Fig. 2D). A Fisher’s exact test showed that mitochondrial localization was significantly enriched among the downregulated genes (P value < 2.22 × 10⁻1⁶; odds ratio = 4.8). Based on the consistent and specific suppression of mitochondrial pathways by Hells KD, we propose that Hells serves as a key regulator of mitochondrial function during RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation.

Nr2f2 is a direct transcriptional target of Hells

To identify upstream TFs potentially mediating the mitochondrial effects of Hells during osteoclast differentiation, we utilized the Gene Transcription Regulation Database (GTRD), which integrates ChIP-seq-based transcription factor binding profiles across multiple datasets [35]. Among the top 10 candidate transcription factors associated with genes downregulated by Hells depletion, Nr2f2 exhibited a significant change in expression in our mRNA-seq data (Fig. 3A). We confirmed the upregulation of Nr2f2 following Hells depletion through RT-qPCR (Fig. 3B) and Western blot analyses (Fig. 3C and D). Subsequent GO analysis, based on the overlap between genes significantly downregulated following Hells KD and GTRD-predicted Nr2f2 target genes, revealed a significant enrichment for mitochondrial-related pathways, indicating that Nr2f2 serves as a critical regulator of mitochondrial gene networks. (Supp Fig. 2A). The inverse expression patterns observed for Hells and Nr2f2 during osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 3E and F) strongly suggest that Hells negatively regulates Nr2f2. To investigate this hypothesis, we conducted ChIP-qPCR analysis to assess Hells binding at the Nr2f2 promoter. After 48 h of RANKL stimulation, we observed significant Hells binding to the Nr2f2 promoter (Fig. 3G). Additionally, FAIRE-qPCR analysis, which assesses chromatin accessibility by measuring nucleosome-depleted DNA regions, revealed increased chromatin accessibility at the Nr2f2 promoter following Hells depletion (Fig. 3H). Together, these findings indicate that RANKL-induced Hells acts as a negative regulator of Nr2f2 expression by decreasing chromatin accessibility at its promoter region.

Nr2f2 is a critical mediator of mitochondrial function as a downstream effector of Hells.

To investigate whether downregulated mitochondrial gene expression impairs mitochondrial integrity and whether Nr2f2 mediates this dysfunction following Hells loss, we performed mitochondrial functional analyses in Hells-depleted osteoclasts treated with CIA1, a selective Nr2f2 inhibitor [36], during RANKL-induced differentiation for 48 h (Supp Fig. 3A). Mitochondrial membrane potential, assessed using the JC-1 assay based on red/green fluorescence shifts, was significantly reduced upon Hells KD, indicating mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 4A). Consistently, intracellular ATP levels, monitored in real-time using fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based ATeam biosensors, were also markedly decreased, reflecting impaired cellular bioenergetics (Fig. 4B and Supp Fig. 3B). Notably, CIA1 treatment restored both mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production in Hells-depleted cells (Fig. 4A and B). In addition, mitochondrial content, evaluated via MitoTracker staining (Fig. 4C) and Western blotting (Fig. 4D and Supp Fig. 4A) of mitochondrial proteins, was substantially reduced by Hells loss but recovered upon Nr2f2 inhibition. Similar to CIA1 treatment, Nr2f2 KD restored the levels of mitochondrial proteins suppressed by Hells depletion (Fig. 4E, Supp Fig. 4B).

To determine whether this restoration of mitochondrial quality and quantity contributes to osteoclast differentiation, we analyzed osteoclast maturation. CIA1 treatment and Nr2f2 KD rescued the impaired osteoclastogenesis caused by Hells depletion (Fig. 4F and Supp Fig. 4C), along with the restoration of Nfatc1, Mmp9, and CtsK expression (Fig. 4D, E, and G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Hells regulates RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation by modulating mitochondrial function through Nr2f2.

Hells-Nr2f2 axis governs mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy in osteoclast differentiation

To further clarify the regulation of mitochondrial mass during osteoclast differentiation, we examined mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy. Since mitochondrial biogenesis during osteoclast differentiation is primarily driven by Pgc1β [23, 24, 26], and Nr2f2 is a known transcriptional repressor of Pgc1β [37–39], we hypothesized that the Hells–Nr2f2 axis regulates mitochondrial biogenesis by modulating Pgc1β expression. Consistent with this, Hells KD markedly suppressed Pgc1β expression, which was significantly restored by CIA1 (Fig. 5A). To quantitatively evaluate mitochondrial biogenesis, we employed the MitoTimer system, which tracks mitochondrial turnover via the green-to-red fluorescence shift of MitoTimer proteins. Hells depletion significantly decreased the green-to-red fluorescence ratio, indicating impaired mitochondrial biogenesis (Fig. 5B), while CIA1 treatment effectively rescued this defect. These findings underscore the role of the Hells–Nr2f2–Pgc1β axis in controlling mitochondrial biogenesis during osteoclast differentiation.

In addition to mitochondrial biogenesis, the removal of damaged mitochondria through mitophagy is essential for maintaining mitochondrial integrity [40–42]. To quantify mitophagy, we employed a fluorescent reporter system based on pH sensitivity: under neutral cytosolic pH (~ 7.2), mitochondria emit yellow fluorescence (green and red merged), whereas in acidic lysosomes (pH < 4.9), green fluorescence is quenched, leaving only red—indicating mitophagic flux. Hells KD significantly increased red fluorescence intensity and red puncta, reflecting elevated mitophagy, which was reversed by CIA1 treatment (Fig. 5C). This was supported by increased LC3B-I to LC3B-II conversion observed in both whole-cell and mitochondrial fractions (Fig. 5D, E, Supp Fig. 5A, and Supp Fig. 5B). To determine whether the elevated LC3B-II levels resulted from enhanced autophagosome formation or impaired lysosomal degradation, we treated cells with bafilomycin A1, a lysosomal inhibitor [43]. Following bafilomycin A1 treatment, LC3B-II accumulation was further increased in Hells-depleted cells, indicating that autophagic flux was active and that the elevated LC3B-II levels were due to enhanced autophagosome synthesis (Fig. 5F and Supp Fig. 5C). These findings confirm that Hells depletion induces mitophagy in response to mitochondrial dysfunction. PTEN-induced kinase 1 (Pink1), a key regulator of mitophagy, has been shown to promote osteoclastogenesis by increasing ROS levels when it is efficient [44, 45]. To determine if Hells depletion–induced mitophagy involves Pink1 regulation, we measured Pink1 expression and ROS levels after Hells KD. Depleting Hells significantly increased Pink1 and decreased ROS, while CIA1 treatment reversed these effects (Fig. 5G and H). These findings suggest that the Hells–Nr2f2 axis controls mitophagy in a Pink1-dependent way during osteoclastogenesis. Together, these findings demonstrate that the Hells–Nr2f2 axis plays a central role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis during RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation by orchestrating both mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy.

Baicalin hydrate impairs osteoclast differentiation with reduced Hells expression

To explore the pharmacological modulation of Hells in osteoclasts, we examined small molecules previously reported to regulate Hells expression. Baicalin hydrate (BH), a natural flavonoid, has been shown to suppress Hells in nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells [46] and exerts a biphasic effect on osteoclast differentiation, enhancing at low concentrations and inhibiting at higher concentrations [47]. We investigated whether BH directly regulates Hells expression and osteoclast differentiation. Consistent with previous findings [47], low-dose BH (≤ 4 μM) slightly enhanced TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclast formation, while high-dose BH (≥ 8 μM) markedly suppressed it without affecting cell viability (Fig. 6A and B). Hells expression remained unchanged with low-dose BH (Fig. 6C and Supp Fig. 6A). Still, it was significantly reduced at high doses, accompanied by decreased Nfatc1 and mitochondrial protein levels (Fig. 6D and Supp Fig. 6B). Consistent with these observations, RT-qPCR analysis of osteoclastogenic markers revealed a slight reduction at low-dose BH. In contrast, their expression was significantly suppressed at high-dose BH (Fig. 6E). To investigate whether the effects of high-dose BH were mediated through the Hells-Nr2f2 axis, we first overexpressed Hells. This restored both mitochondrial proteins and Nfatc1 expression suppressed by high-dose BH (Fig. 6F and Supp Fig. 6C). Furthermore, treatment with CIA1 induced a similar rescue effect (Fig. 6G and Supp Fig. 6D). Notably, Hells overexpression reduced Nr2f2 expression (Fig. 6F), supporting that Nr2f2 acts as a key downstream mediator of Hells in osteoclast differentiation. While the mechanism behind low-dose enhancement without changes in Hells remains unclear, the inhibitory effect of high-dose BH on osteoclastogenesis is mechanistically linked to Hells suppression and its downstream targets. These findings suggest a potential therapeutic approach to modulate osteoclast activity by inhibiting Hells.

Discussion

This study identifies Hells as an epigenetic regulator that contributes to osteoclast differentiation by controlling mitochondrial quantity and quality (Fig. 7). Although Hells has been extensively studied as an ATP-dependent chromatin remodeler involved in DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, and transcriptional regulation across diverse biological systems, its role in coordinating epigenetic regulation with mitochondrial homeostasis has remained undefined. In this study, we identify a previously unrecognized regulatory axis in which Hells directly represses Nr2f2 transcription to maintain mitochondrial integrity during osteoclast differentiation. We demonstrate that RANKL-induced Hells binds the Nr2f2 promoter and restricts chromatin accessibility, thereby preventing Nr2f2-driven repression of mitochondrial programs. Loss of Hells derepresses Nr2f2, impairing mitochondrial biogenesis by suppressing Pgc1β and enhancing mitophagy by upregulating Pink1. Consequently, disruption of this Hells–Nr2f2 axis compromises mitochondrial quality and quantity, leading to defective osteoclastogenesis. Together, these findings suggest a chromatin-based regulatory relationship in which Hells modulates Nr2f2 transcription through promoter accessibility, consistent with a link between chromatin remodeling and mitochondrial regulation during osteoclast differentiation.

Schematic illustration of the mechanism by which Hells suppresses Nr2f2 to promote Pgc1β-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis and osteoclast differentiation, while its depletion leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and mitophagy

Chromatin remodelers are increasingly recognized as critical regulators of osteoclast fate. For instance, AT-rich interaction domain 1a (Arid1a), a core subunit of the canonical BAF complex, stabilizes the commitment to the osteoclast lineage during the proliferation-to-differentiation transition [48]. It achieves this by acting as a scaffold, facilitating the formation of transcriptional condensates with bromodomain-containing 4 (Brd4) and PU.1 at the Nfatc1 super-enhancer. This scaffolding maintains H3K27ac enrichment and sustains Brd4 occupancy within the super-enhancer. Furthermore, Arid1a collaborates with Jun/Fos transcription factors to enhance chromatin accessibility at the activator protein 1 (AP-1) binding motif of the sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin 15 (Siglec15) promoter [49]. This interaction increases H3K27ac levels and stabilizes AP-1 complex recruitment, driving Siglec15 transcription—a gene crucial for osteoclast maturation and cell–cell fusion. Conversely, Brd9, a component of the non-canonical BAF complex, functions as a negative regulator of osteoclast differentiation [12]. By interacting with Foxp1, Brd9 induces Stat1 transcription and activates IFN-β signaling, establishing a negative feedback loop that limits excessive osteoclastogenesis. Consequently, Brd9 deletion leads to enhanced osteoclast differentiation and increased bone resorption. While prior investigations have thoroughly characterized the roles of Arid1a and Brd9 in early osteoclast differentiation using robust methodologies, their explanation has remained focused on the nucleus. This study identifies the Hells-Nr2f2 circuit, a potential nexus connecting nuclear and mitochondrial regulation, offering valuable insights into the interplay between epigenetics and metabolism during osteoclastogenesis. However, the comprehensive genomic occupancy of Hells throughout osteoclast differentiation remains unknown, as does its potential for cooperative function with other epigenetic regulators. Consequently, a more integrated epigenetic analysis is crucial to elucidate the role of Hells.

The Nr2f family, which includes Nr2f1, Nr2f2, and Nr2f6, is part of the COUP-TF family [50–53]. These orphan nuclear receptors can form homodimers or heterodimers—often with the retinoid X receptor (RXR)—functioning as both activators and repressors. Each Nr2f transcription factor displays specialized functions in distinct cellular contexts. Nr2f2 plays significant roles in cellular differentiation across various biological processes, ranging from neuronal and immune cell differentiation to organogenesis and adult tissue homeostasis [54]. Beyond its role in differentiation, Nr2f2 also regulates mitochondrial function, though its effects vary across physiological and pathological conditions. In white adipose tissue and 3T3-L1 adipocytes, Nr2f2 represses Pgc1α transcription by binding to its promoter, and its deficiency enhances mitochondrial gene expression and activity [37]. Similarly, in models of diabetic heart failure, Nr2f2 overexpression contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress by downregulating Pgc1α [39].

In contrast, in adipocyte stem-like cells derived from diabetic mice, Nr2f2 activation increases Pgc1α expression, restores fatty acid oxidation, and improves mitochondrial function [38]. These varying outcomes highlight the highly context-dependent role of Nr2f2 in mitochondrial regulation, acting either as a repressor or an activator, depending on the cellular state and metabolic demands. Of note, while much is known about the downstream roles of Nr2f2, particularly in metabolic regulation, its upstream transcriptional control remains poorly defined. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that Nr2f2 expression dynamically changes during osteoclast differentiation and identify Hells as an upstream regulator that modulates chromatin accessibility at the Nr2f2 promoter.

Mitochondrial homeostasis in osteoclasts is maintained through a finely tuned balance of mitochondrial dynamics, biogenesis, and mitophagy. RANKL-mediated mitochondrial fission, which promotes osteoclast differentiation via the c-Fos–Nfatc1 axis, whereas inhibition of dynamin 1-like (Drp1) attenuates osteoclastogenesis and bone loss [55]. Mitofusin 2 (Mfn2)-dependent mitochondrial fusion and endoplasmic reticulum tethering facilitate calcium signaling and Nfatc1 activation [56]. In this context, AMP-activated protein kinase alpha 1 (AMPKα1) negatively regulates osteoclast activity by upregulating Drp1 and suppressing Mfn2 [57]. Although balanced mitochondrial dynamics are essential for osteoclast differentiation, our data show no significant changes in related genes upon Hells modulation, suggesting that Hells is unlikely to serve as a primary regulator of this process.

Mitochondrial biogenesis has generally been reported to occur around 48 h after RANKL stimulation [23, 58, 59]; however, recent studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial complex subunit expression increases as early as 6 h [60, 61], indicating an earlier onset of mitochondrial activation during osteoclast differentiation. This process is primarily governed by Pgc1β [26], and depletion of Pgc1β or mitochondrial genes markedly suppresses osteoclast differentiation, formation, and resorptive function [23, 26, 59, 61]. Collectively, these studies highlight mitochondrial biogenesis as a key process supporting osteoclastogenesis. Given its central role, Pgc1β expression has been shown to be regulated by upstream factors such as peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ (Pparγ), additional sex combs like transcriptional regulator 2 (Asxl2), and cAMP response element–binding protein (CREB) [24, 25]. However, the involvement of chromatin remodelers in this pathway remains unexplored. In this study, we identify Hells as a key epigenetic regulator that represses Nr2f2 to induce Pgc1β, thereby establishing a mechanistic connection between chromatin remodeling, mitochondrial biogenesis, and osteoclast activity and differentiation in vitro.

PTEN deficiency induces Pink1, a key regulator of mitophagy, leading to excessive calcium influx and elevated mitochondrial ROS levels that hyperactivate Nfatc1 and drive excessive osteoclastogenesis [44]. Under inflammatory conditions, hyperactivation of Stat3 and the NLRP4 inflammasome promotes the release of proinflammatory cytokines, further enhancing osteoclast differentiation [45]. Conversely, in the context of Pink1-independent mitophagy, the hyperacetylated ATP synthase inhibitory factor subunit 1 (Atpif1) impairs mitophagy in osteoclasts from aged Sirt3-deficient mice and suppresses osteoclast formation, even under elevated ROS conditions [62]. In our study, Hells depletion triggered Pink1-dependent mitophagy, promoting the clearance of damaged mitochondria and reducing ROS accumulation, thereby attenuating osteoclast differentiation. While we did not directly inhibit mitophagy to confirm its causal role in this process, our findings suggest a functional link. Future studies using Pink1 KD or pharmacological inhibitors will help further validate this mechanism. Collectively, these findings identify Hells as a previously unrecognized regulator of osteoclastogenesis that coordinates mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, which is essential for osteoclast differentiation.

An important conceptual question raised by our findings is whether the Hells–Nr2f2 regulatory relationship represents an osteoclast-specific adaptation or reflects a more broadly conserved chromatin-based regulatory principle operating across cell types. The present study does not resolve this distinction. Our experiments were designed to mechanistically interrogate chromatin accessibility at the Nr2f2 promoter and its functional consequences during osteoclast differentiation. Within this context, the data consistently support a model in which Hells-dependent repression of Nr2f2 coordinates mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy to sustain osteoclast differentiation. However, whether a similar chromatin-based regulatory relationship between Hells and Nr2f2 operates in other cellular lineages remains an open question. Addressing this issue will require systematic cross-lineage analyses of Hells-dependent chromatin accessibility and Nr2f2 transcriptional control in additional cellular contexts.

Another important limitation of the present study is that the functional conclusions are derived primarily from in vitro osteoclast differentiation systems. While these models provide a powerful framework for mechanistic dissection of chromatin accessibility, transcriptional regulation, and mitochondrial dynamics, they do not fully capture the complex physiological environment in which osteoclast function is regulated in vivo. Consequently, the current data do not establish whether the Hells–Nr2f2 regulatory axis is strictly required for osteoclast function under physiological conditions, where systemic cues, intercellular signaling, and compensatory pathways may influence chromatin and mitochondrial regulation. Future studies employing in vivo genetic models or bone-targeted modulation of Hells will be necessary to determine the physiological contribution of this regulatory axis to bone remodeling and osteoclast activity. Such studies will be important for determining whether this chromatin–mitochondrial regulatory relationship represents a lineage-adapted mechanism in osteoclasts or a more broadly conserved principle across cell types.
