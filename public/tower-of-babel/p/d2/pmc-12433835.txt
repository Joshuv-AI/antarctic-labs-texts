Gestational diabetes mellitus and its association with newborn thyroid screening: a population-based study
Authors: Dor Shoshan, Avivit Brener, Eyal Cohen-Sela, Orian Raviv, Michal Yackobovitch-Gavan, Shlomo Almashanu, Ronella Marom, Liran Hiersch, Yael Lebenthal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) affects maternal–fetal metabolism, but its impact on neonatal thyroid function remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between maternal GDM and total thyroxine (TT4) levels in newborn screening (NBS) and to identify contributing maternal and neonatal factors.MethodsThis observational cohort study linked national NBS thyroid data with hospital medical records. The cohort included 101,450 mother-infant dyads, comprising 4,643 GDM and 96,807 non-GDM singleton pregnancies with term liveborn offspring. Maternal GDM status was the primary exposure variable, and neonatal TT4 levels were assessed as the main outcome measure.ResultsThe GDM prevalence was 4.6%. The median NBS TT4 levels (mg/dL) were 14.9 (12.7–17.3) in the GDM newborns compared to 14.6 (12.4–17.2) in the non-GDM newborns, with similar proportions of newborns requiring reflex-TSH testing. GDM mothers were older than non-GDM mothers (34.2 ± 4.8 vs 32.5 ± 4.8 years), had higher prepregnancy body mass indices (25.6 ± 5.5 vs 22.5 ± 4.0 kg/m2), and increased odds of hypertension (OR 3.39) and proteinuria (OR 2.40). GDM pregnancies had higher odds of Cesarean delivery (OR 2.21), large-for-gestational-age infants (OR 1.51), and neonatal intensive care admissions (OR 1.52). Multivariable analysis identified parity, neuraxial anesthesia, oxytocin use, Cesarean delivery, maternal fever, newborn sex, gestational age, and birth weight percentiles as factors associated with TT4 levels (P < 0.001 for all).ConclusionGDM was not associated with clinically significant differences in NBS TT4 levels. While GDM pregnancies showed increased risks of adverse maternal-neonatal outcomes, the modest thyroid differences observed in this large cohort of term singleton pregnancies suggest that thyroid alterations in the newborns may not be a matter of concern in GDM pregnancies.Significance statementThis study examines the relationship between GDM and newborn thyroid function using a large, population-based dataset. By focusing exclusively on term singleton pregnancies, we eliminated confounding factors such as prematurity and multiple gestations. While previous research raised concerns about potential thyroid dysfunction in neonates of mothers with GDM, our findings show only minimal differences in thyroxine levels, providing reassurance that GDM has little impact on neonatal thyroid function. GDM remains strongly linked to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including higher rates of Cesarean deliveries and neonatal intensive care admissions. These findings provide reassurance about neonatal thyroid function in GDM pregnancies while emphasizing the importance of monitoring other perinatal risks.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy, affecting 7–10% of pregnancies worldwide. The rising prevalence of GDM is driven by increasing maternal risk factors, such as advanced age and obesity, together with changes in diagnostic criteria and demographics (1, 2, 3, 4). GDM is associated with maternal and neonatal complications, including preeclampsia, Cesarean delivery, persistent maternal diabetes, macrosomia, and neonatal hypoglycemia (5, 6, 7).

Thyroid dysfunction has been increasingly implicated as both a contributor to and a consequence of GDM. Several studies have demonstrated that subclinical hypothyroidism, low free T4 levels, or a high fT3/fT4 ratio during early pregnancy are associated with an elevated risk of developing GDM, particularly in the presence of thyroid autoantibodies (8, 9). These findings suggest a bidirectional interplay, possibly mediated by shared metabolic pathways, inflammatory processes, and placental function (10). Conversely, insulin resistance and hormonal shifts characteristic of GDM may alter maternal thyroid hormone dynamics and feedback regulation (11).

The relationship between in utero exposure to maternal hyperglycemia and newborn thyroid dysfunction has been a subject of ongoing debate. Some researchers have reported elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels in cord blood of GDM pregnancies, attributing this rise to fetal stress and correlating it with lower Apgar scores (12, 13, 14). Other studies have suggested that neonatal hyperthyrotropinemia may result from fetal stress or suppression of fetal thyroid function by maternal hyperglycemia (14, 15, 16, 17). Gutiérrez-Vega et al. demonstrated that GDM pregnancies were characterized by elevated maternal TT3 levels together with reduced fetal fT4 and TT3 levels, findings that correlated with altered placental deiodinase expression and activity. Those authors postulated that the hypoxia and oxidative stress present in GDM might contribute to these alterations (18). However, those studies may have notable limitations, such as the inclusion of preterm deliveries (12, 13, 14) and small sample sizes (16, 18). In addition, most of them relied upon cord blood measurements rather than standardized newborn screening protocols, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about the impact of GDM on neonatal thyroid function. Furthermore, thyroid hormone levels in preterm infants are influenced by the physiological immaturity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis, reduced hormone synthesis, and decreased binding protein concentrations, independent of maternal glycemic status (19).

The developmental origins of health and disease hypothesis underscores the influence of maternal health and intrauterine environment on fetal and neonatal health, while alternatively, fetal and placental characteristics can impact maternal well-being (20, 21, 22, 23). These reciprocal interactions highlight the need to view maternal and infant health as a dyad. As such, environmental, social, and medical factors, including increased use of obstetric interventions, such as assisted reproductive technology (ART), medications, and Cesarean sections, may affect hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis function (24, 25, 26). In this study, we hypothesized that total thyroxine (TT4) levels are significantly lower in term singleton infants of mothers with GDM compared to those in the general population, reflecting the influence of GDM on maternal–fetal thyroid hormone metabolism.

Newborn screening (NBS) programs facilitate the early detection of congenital hypothyroidism, thereby improving long-term outcomes (27). We utilized thyroid NBS data to examine the impact of GDM on neonatal thyroid function by comparing thyroxine levels and maternal-infant dyad characteristics in term singleton infants of mothers with GDM versus those in the general population.

Materials and methods

Study design

The Israeli NBS program’s thyroid database was queried to generate a list of all the neonates born at Lis Maternity and Women’s Hospital between January 1, 2011 and December 31, 2021. As a major public hospital, Lis Maternity Hospital serves a diverse population, making the study cohort broadly representative of the general population in Israel. The MDClone platform (MDClone© Ltd, Israel) (28) was utilized to access the medical records of infants and their mothers in order to link pertinent data with the thyroid data to generate a unified mother–infant dyad dataset. Included were term (gestational age (GA) ≥37 weeks) singleton liveborn infants with available NBS thyroid test results. Excluded from the analysis were infants with conditions that could impact the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis function and resultant study outcomes, such as being preterm (GA <37 weeks), multiple pregnancies, and having been diagnosed with chromosomal abnormalities, as well as maternal thyroid disorders. Data were validated and preprocessed, and cases with insufficient data and corrupt data resulting from faulty measurements or documentation were excluded from the analysis.

The mother-infant dyads were categorized into the study and control groups. The study group included women with documented pregestational diabetes or GDM diagnosed during the current pregnancy. We defined GDM by abnormal glucose levels in the glucose challenge test (≥200 mg/dL) or subsequent oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) (5, 29, 30). Diagnostic thresholds of 95 mg/dL, 180 mg/dL, 155 mg/dL, and 140 mg/dL plasma glucose values for fasting, and at 1, 2, and 3 h after 100 g OGTT, according to the Carpenter and Coustan criteria, were employed when two glucose values were elevated (31). The control group included women with documented glucose levels within the normal range.

Ethics

Our Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol (approval number TLV-0213-23) and waived the need for informed consent for this retrospective and anonymized analysis. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the guidelines of Good Clinical Practice (32), and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (33).

Participants

The NBS program search yielded 124,238 total T4 results of infants born at Lis Maternity Hospital. The MDClone platform was utilized to link these results to the medical records of the mother-infant dyads. Out of a total of 124,238 mother-infant dyads, 22,788 dyads were excluded (preterm infants (n = 14,544), multiple pregnancies/gestations (n = 2,157), maternal thyroid disease (n = 2,731), and data preprocessing (n = 3,356)). Categorization of mother-infant dyads into the GDM group resulted in 4,643 (4.6%) dyads in the maternal GDM group and 96,807 dyads in the control group. Methods

Clinical and demographic data were extracted by MDClone (MDClone© Ltd, Israel), a big-data platform that generates deidentified datasets through advanced algorithms. The Israeli NBS program conducts centralized screening for congenital hypothyroidism for all newborns, measuring TT4 via heel prick blood samples collected on filter paper between 36 and 72 h after birth for term infants. TSH testing follows when TT4 values are below the 10th percentile. This approach of measuring TT4 first with reflex-TSH enhances sensitivity for detecting both primary and central congenital hypothyroidism, while minimizing false-positive findings by means of targeted TSH follow-up (34). A subset of infants underwent a second TT4 screening based on clinical practices, which may have included factors such as clinical concerns, NICU admission, or other indications. Maternal vital signs were measured using Vital Signs Monitor 300 Series (Welch Allyn®, USA), and neonatal measurements by the IntelliVue MP70 Patient Monitor (Philips Medizin Systeme Böblingen GmbH, Germany). Neonatal glucose was measured from whole blood by means of the POC StatStrip® Glucose Hospital Meter System (Nova® Biomedical Corporation, USA).

Data collection

Data on mother-infant dyads were collected from Lis Maternity and Neonatology electronic medical records, encompassing self-reported patient information, physicians’ notes on diagnoses, management, and surveillance, as well as laboratory results and clinical measurements from conception throughout pregnancy, labor, delivery, and perinatal periods. The information obtained from the maternity hospital database included maternal age, gravidity, parity, previous live births, reported prepregnancy height and weight, measured weight at admission for delivery, and the calculated pregnancy weight gain. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (in kilograms) divided by height (in meters squared). Methods of conception were classified as spontaneous, in vitro fertilization (IVF), and non-IVF ART (including intrauterine insemination, ovulation induction medications, or a combination of the two). Health conditions affecting gestation included smoking status, hypertension, proteinuria, chronic medication use, and chronic medical conditions.

Labor and delivery data included medications (Pitocin® (Oxytocin Injection, USP), antibiotics), anesthesia type (none, neuraxial, general), mode of delivery (spontaneous vaginal, vaginal-assisted, elective/urgent Cesarean), PROM, stained (meconial or bloody) amniotic fluid, placental anomalies (incomplete (requiring intervention) and abnormal (size/color/shape/general appearance)), and maternal vital signs (heart rate, temperature, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure). Elective Cesarean sections in our cohort were performed based upon routine maternal and fetal indications. These included, but were not limited to, a history of previous Cesarean delivery, non-vertex presentation, maternal request after counseling, placenta previa, and suspected macrosomia (estimated fetal weight >4,250 g in the presence of maternal diabetes or estimated fetal weight >4,500 g regardless of maternal diabetes status). Neonatal data included sex, GA, birth weight, and corrected birth weight percentiles (calculated with PediTools Electronic Growth Chart Calculators, based upon the World Health Organization growth chart for term infants) (35). Classifications were: appropriate for gestational age (AGA, z-scores −1.28–1.28), small for gestational age (SGA, ≤ −1.28), and large for gestational age (LGA, ≥1.28) (35). Newborn presentation at birth was vertex, breech, face/brow, diagonal/transverse, and compound. Additional neonatal parameters included Apgar score (1 and 5 min), vital signs (temperature and heart rate), glucose levels at birth, neonatal hospitalization department (nursery/neonatal intensive care unit), and duration of hospital stay. The first measured glucose level was used for analysis. Neonatal hypoglycemia was defined according to the American Academy of Pediatrics and Pediatric Endocrine Society guidelines (<25 mg/dL at 0–4 h, <35 mg/dL at 4–24 h, and <50 mg/dL at 24–48 h after birth for term infants) (36).

Socioeconomic status (SES) was determined using home addresses and based upon the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics’ 20-cluster classification system (1 = lowest, 20 = highest), derived from 16 population variables calculated for each statistical area (37).

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was NBS thyroid function levels (total thyroxine (TT4) levels) in term singleton infants born to mothers with GDM (study group) in comparison to infants in the general population (control group). We also explored secondary maternal outcomes (ART, pregnancy weight gain, comorbid conditions, pre-labor rupture of membranes (PROM), anesthesia, and Cesarean section) and neonatal outcomes (sex, adjusted birth weight, Apgar score (appearance (skin color), pulse, grimace (reflex), activity (muscle tone), and respiration), and hospitalization in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU)). The tertiary outcome was the identification of factors associated with NBS TT4 levels.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed, and violin plots were generated by the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software version 29 (SPSS Inc., USA). All statistical tests were 2-sided. The normality of continuous data was assessed by using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Q–Q plots. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while skewed data are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) bounds. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. For the primary outcome, the comparison of NBS TT4 levels between the study and control groups was performed by applying the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences in categorical variables were analyzed by the chi-squared test, and continuous variables were compared by either the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending upon data distribution. Odds ratios (ORs) were adjusted for confounders by using binary logistic regression models.

Contingency tables were performed in order to explore associations between GDM and selected outcomes through the estimation of ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A forward multivariable linear regression model was used (entry stepping method criteria P ≤ 0.05) to identify factors contributing to NBS TT4 levels, adjusted for confounding factors. Candidate variables were selected based on clinical relevance and the prior literature suggesting potential associations with neonatal thyroid function, or factors that could confound the relationship between maternal GDM and neonatal TT4 levels. The following independent variables were included in the model: SES cluster, maternal age, pre-gestational BMI, parity, IVF, maternal GDM, gestational hypertension, smoking status, GA, neuraxial anesthesia, administration of oxytocin, Cesarean section, maternal fever, newborn female sex, and birth weight percentiles. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, retaining only mother-infant dyads with complete data for all variables in the multivariable regression analysis. A P-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered significant, and Bonferroni corrections were used for multiple comparisons.

The sample size was calculated based upon the expected mean (SD) difference of 0.25 μg/dL in NBS TT4 levels (the primary outcome of this study) by means of WinPepi software version 11.65. The required sample size of 3,439 infants born to mothers with GDM and 76,538 cases from the general population was calculated to achieve a significance level (α) of 5% and a power of 80%. The calculation considered the proportion of GDM in the Israeli population (4.3%) (1).

Results

A total of 4,643 (4.6%) women out of 101,450 available mother-infant dyads were diagnosed with GDM.

NBS thyroid function

The median NBS TT4 levels (μg/dL) were 14.9 (IQR 12.7, 17.3) in the GDM group compared to 14.6 (IQR 12.4, 17.2) in the general population (P < 0.001). Graphic visualization of the NBS TT4 level distribution revealed remarkable similarity between the two groups (Fig. 2). The proportions of newborns requiring TSH testing due to relatively low TT4 levels were similar.

Distribution of newborn thyroxine levels. Violin plots (distributions of numeric data using probability density curves) illustrating the distribution of NBS TT4 levels of newborns of mothers with GDM compared with those from the general population.

Maternal and pregnancy characteristics

The maternal and pregnancy characteristics of the mother-infant dyads of the GDM group compared with the general population are presented in Table 1 and Fig. 3. Mothers with GDM were older compared to mothers in the general population (mean age 34.2 ± 4.8 years vs 32.5 ± 4.8 years, respectively, P < 0.001). The cohort’s collective SES was above average, with a median SES cluster of 15 (range 1–20). Mothers with GDM belonged to one SES cluster lower compared with the general population (14 vs 15, P < 0.001). Mothers with GDM were found to be on average 1 cm shorter (1.63 ± 0.06 vs 1.64 ± 0.06, P < 0.001), had a higher pre-gestational BMI (25.6 ± 5.5 vs 22.5 ± 4.0 kg/m2, P < 0.001), and underwent a significantly lower weight gain during pregnancy (10.6 ± 5.9 kg vs 13.0 ± 5.1 kg, P < 0.001). Methods of conception differed significantly, with more mothers in the GDM group having undergone ART overall (13.1 vs 7.8%, P < 0.001; OR 1.77 (95% CI 1.62–1.94), P < 0.001). The prevalence of smoking in mothers with GDM was also significantly higher (4.4 vs 3.6%, P = 0.004; OR 1.23 (95% CI 1.07–1.42), P = 0.005). The pregnancies of mothers with GDM were complicated by comparatively increased rates of hypertension (3.4 vs 1.0%, P < 0.001; OR 3.39 (95% CI 2.79–4.13), P < 0.001) and proteinuria (1.9 vs 0.8%, P < 0.001; OR 2.40 (95% CI 1.85–3.13), P < 0.001).

Labor and delivery outcomes

The labor- and delivery-related characteristics of the mother-infant dyads with GDM in comparison to the general population are shown in Table 2 and Fig. 4. There was a significant group difference in the mode of delivery, with higher rates of Cesarean section observed in women with GDM (32.3 vs 17.9%, P < 0.001; OR 2.21 (95% CI 2.08–2.36), P < 0.001). Similar rates of PROM and abnormal or incomplete gross placental appearance were observed in the two groups; however, lower rates of meconium-stained amniotic fluid were observed in mothers with GDM compared with the general population (13.2 vs 17.5%, P < 0.001; OR 0.71 (95% CI 0.65–0.78); P < 0.001).

Newborn outcomes

The characteristics of term infants stratified according to GDM status are presented in Table 3 and Fig. 5. The infants in the GDM group had significantly higher birth weights, with higher birth weight percentiles (P < 0.001) and a higher proportion of infants categorized as LGA (9.6 vs 6.6%, P < 0.001; OR 1.51 (95% CI 1.37–1.67), P < 0.001) compared to the general population. A graphic visualization of birth weight percentiles showed that the two groups had a similar spread and distribution, with an upward shift of the center in the GDM group (Fig. 6). There were no significant group differences in presentation at delivery or Apgar scores. Infants in the GDM group had higher rates of NICU hospitalizations (3.2 vs 2.1%, P < 0.001; OR 1.52 (95% CI 1.29–1.79), P < 0.001) and experienced a longer duration of hospital stay (2.4 days (IQR 1.8, 3.1) vs 2.2 days (IQR 1.7, 2.8), P < 0.001). They also had higher rates of neonatal hypoglycemia (4.3 vs 2.9%, P < 0.001; OR 1.48 (95% CI 1.22–1.80), P < 0.001) compared to controls.

Discussion

Our large-scale nationally representative cohort study sought to clarify the relationship between maternal GDM and neonatal thyroid function in term, singleton pregnancies. We found only modest differences in NBS TT4 levels between infants born to mothers with GDM and those born to mothers without GDM. Although this difference reached a level of statistical significance (P < 0.001), the absolute change was minimal, with substantial overlap in interquartile ranges, and is unlikely to be clinically meaningful given our large sample size (38, 39). Importantly, we found no differences between groups in the proportions of newborns requiring TSH testing due to low TT4 levels, suggesting that these small alterations likely hold minimal clinical relevance.

These findings contrast with those of Shanmugam et al. (16), who reported suppressed thyroid function in a substantially smaller sample size (n = 30) and focused upon gestational impaired glucose tolerance rather than GDM. Similarly, while Yang et al.'s (40) larger cohort study (n = 15,236) demonstrated elevated TSH levels in GDM offspring, their inclusion of preterm infants, a condition known to alter thyroid function, limits comparability with our findings. Our results also differ from Lao and Lee’s (17) observation of increased hyperthyrotropinemia in GDM pregnancies in their analysis that was restricted to diet-treated GDM and that included preterm births.

While our findings suggest minimal impact of GDM on neonatal thyroid function when controlling for major confounders, this study confirmed substantial adverse outcomes associated with GDM even in this relatively low-risk cohort of term singleton pregnancies. Notably, we observed distinct maternal characteristics in the GDM group, including advanced age, higher pre-gestational BMI, and increased utilization of ART. The 1.77-fold higher ART rate in our GDM group compared to controls warrants special attention, especially given Israel’s globally high ART utilization (9% versus 2–6% in European countries) (41, 42). This association likely reflects shared risk factors between infertility and GDM, including advanced maternal age, polycystic ovary syndrome, elevated BMI, and metabolic complications (43, 44, 45). The relationship between GDM and ART might be bi-directional, with several studies having identified ART, particularly IVF, as a risk factor for developing GDM later in pregnancy (46).

Consistent with previous research, our study found that maternal smoking, both preconception and during pregnancy, is associated with an increased risk of GDM (47, 48). This association may stem from shared socio-demographic risk factors, such as lower SES, education level, and occupation, which are common to both smoking and GDM risk (49, 50, 51).

ART, particularly IVF, has been linked to increased risk of euthyroid hyperthyrotropinemia and subclinical hypothyroidism in offspring, potentially through epigenetic modifications affecting thyroid gland development (52, 53). Similarly, maternal smoking has been shown to impact fetal thyroid function through multiple mechanisms, including altered TSH levels, disrupted thyroid hormone transport, and exacerbation of iodine deficiency (54, 55, 56). The higher prevalence of these factors in our GDM cohort suggests that the observed alterations in thyroid function might reflect more than a direct effect of GDM alone.

Our analysis revealed increased odds of maternal hypertension and proteinuria in GDM pregnancies (OR 3.39 and 2.40, respectively), exceeding rates reported in previous studies (57). The underlying pathophysiology likely involves GDM-induced pro-inflammatory states, cytokine imbalances, and placental inflammation, coupled with shared predisposing factors, including insulin resistance and dyslipidemia (58). The Cesarean section rate of 32.3% in GDM pregnancies (OR 2.21) that we observed aligns with previous research and can be partly attributed to adherence to guidelines recommending Cesarean delivery for estimated fetal weights exceeding 4,500 grams (5, 59).

Some complications associated with maternal GDM have been proposed to be associated with preterm birth (60). Thus, our focus on term pregnancies allowed us to demonstrate that the association of GDM with adverse outcomes persists independently of prematurity. The increased incidence of LGA infants (OR 1.51) and elevated NICU admission rates (OR 1.47) remained significant after controlling for potential confounders.

Multiple factors were identified as being associated with NBS TT4 levels, including parity, neuraxial anesthesia, oxytocin administration, Cesarean delivery, maternal fever, newborn sex, GA, and birth weight percentiles. These findings suggest that neonatal thyroid function reflects a complex interplay of maternal, gestational, and neonatal factors. While Herbstman et al. (61) reported associations between GDM and elevated neonatal free T4 levels – as well as between maternal age and hypertensive disorders and lower TT4 – these findings were based on a smaller cohort of 300 infants, including 11 cases of GDM and a proportion of preterm deliveries. Notably, our current findings indicate that the impact of GDM appears more modest than previously thought when major confounders are accounted for.

We acknowledge several limitations to this study. The observational design prevents establishment of causal relationships between GDM and mother-infant dyad outcomes. The self-reporting of some data, including obstetric history, health conditions, and smoking during pregnancy, and pregestational weight, may introduce a degree of information bias. Moreover, the lack of data on maternal glycemic control, including insulin use and achievement of glycemic targets, limited our ability to explore how variations in GDM severity might influence pregnancy and neonatal outcomes. Since Cesarean delivery was not the focus of this study, we have no data on the specific indications for those who underwent Cesarean delivery in each group. Our study cohort primarily consists of white participants, which may limit the generalizability of findings to more diverse populations. In addition, data on maternal diet and physical activity, which are known to impact pregnancy and neonatal outcomes, were unavailable. Furthermore, the exclusion of mothers with known thyroid disease – intended to reduce confounding – may have introduced selection bias if exclusion rates differed between groups and could have lowered the rate of abnormal neonatal thyroid screening results, potentially minimizing the observed differences between the GDM and control groups. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited to healthcare systems that utilize TT4 for newborn thyroid screening. The study’s strengths include its large, nationally representative cohort, uniform obstetric protocols implemented across a single medical center, and rigorous validation processes. By focusing upon singleton pregnancies of term liveborn offspring, we eliminated the confounding effects of prematurity and multiple gestations. Moreover, access to comprehensive electronic medical records linking maternity and neonatology data, including hospitalizations, laboratory results, and medications, helped mitigate information bias.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that maternal GDM was associated with a significant difference in NBS TT4 levels, albeit the absolute difference was minimal, with substantial overlap in distributions between groups, suggesting that this finding may reflect a byproduct of the large sample size rather than a true clinically meaningful effect. By specifically examining singleton pregnancies of full-term offspring, our findings reveal that the metabolic impact of GDM on neonatal thyroid function is less pronounced than suggested by previous studies. While the effect of GDM on thyroid function appears minimal, its significant impact on maternal, gestational, and neonatal outcomes persists even in what might be considered a lower-risk GDM population with fewer confounding effects. The complex interplay of factors affecting neonatal thyroid function identified in this study emphasizes the need for longitudinal research to elucidate the long-term developmental and metabolic consequences of these alterations. Understanding these associations may contribute to optimizing screening and follow-up protocols for infants of mothers with GDM.
