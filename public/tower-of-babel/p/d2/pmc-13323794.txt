Prolactin receptor overexpression in pregnancy-associated Cushing syndrome and functional implications
Authors: Masamitsu Naka, Kazuki Ishiwata, Sawako Suzuki, Yusuke Kouchi, Takashi Kishimoto, Koutaro Yokote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCushing syndrome (CS) during pregnancy is rare but is associated with substantial maternal and fetal risks. Although aberrant activation of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), particularly the luteinizing hormone/human chorionic gonadotropin receptor, has been implicated in pregnancy-related hypercortisolism, non-GPCR mechanisms remain incompletely defined. We report a case of adrenocorticotropic hormone-independent CS during pregnancy caused by a cortisol-producing adrenocortical adenoma. Integrative molecular analyses of the resected tumor, including ribonucleic acid sequencing and immunohistochemistry, demonstrated increased expression of the prolactin receptor (PRLR) compared with adjacent normal adrenal tissue. PRLR is a member of the type I cytokine receptor family and is expressed in normal adrenal glands; however, its potential role in adrenal tumors has not been well characterized. To examine its functional relevance, primary cell cultures derived from independent cortisol-producing adenomas from nonpregnant women were treated with recombinant prolactin (PRL). PRL induced a dose-dependent increase in both cortisol secretion into the culture medium and intracellular cortisol levels. Following unilateral adrenalectomy, hypercortisolism resolved, and maternal and neonatal outcomes were favorable. These data provide molecular and functional evidence supporting a contributory role of PRLR in pregnancy-associated hypercortisolism, potentially triggered by physiological PRL elevations during gestation.

Introduction

Cushing syndrome (CS) during pregnancy is rare, with approximately 220 cases reported to date [1]. Despite its low incidence, untreated CS is associated with serious maternal and fetal complications, including hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes mellitus, cesarean delivery, preterm birth, low birth weight, and fetal demise [1, 2]. Early recognition and appropriate management are therefore essential.

In pregnancy-associated CS, aberrant expression of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) in adrenal tumors can activate the adenylyl cyclase–cAMP–protein kinase A pathway independently of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thereby stimulating cortisol production [3]. Most reported cases involve the luteinizing hormone/human chorionic gonadotropin receptor (LHCGR), in which elevated human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) during pregnancy stimulates cortisol secretion [3, 4]. A single case has also described cortisol stimulation through activation of the ACTH–melanocortin 2 receptor (MC2R) axis [5]. However, pregnancy is also associated with marked elevations of other pituitary and placental hormones, including prolactin (PRL), raising the possibility that additional ligand–receptor interactions may contribute to cortisol excess in adrenal tumors. This hypothesis has not been systematically investigated.

We previously reported a pregnancy-associated CS caused by an adrenocortical adenoma that was successfully managed with pharmacological treatment followed by unilateral laparoscopic adrenalectomy [6]. Here, we further performed integrative molecular and functional analyses of the resected tumor, including ribonucleic acid sequencing (RNA-seq), immunohistochemistry, and primary tumor cell culture experiments, to investigate a potential non-GPCR receptor mechanism underlying cortisol overproduction during pregnancy.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old primigravida with no significant past medical history developed cushingoid features at 12 weeks of gestation. At 18 weeks, she exhibited moon face, acne, central obesity, violaceous abdominal striae, subcutaneous ecchymosis, lower extremity edema, and proximal muscle weakness (Fig. 1A). Her blood pressure was 146/97 mmHg. Laboratory evaluation revealed hypokalemia with a serum potassium level of 2.9 mmol/L (reference range (RR), 3.6-4.8 mmol/L), and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) was diagnosed based on a fasting blood glucose level of 93 mg/dL (SI: 5.2 mmol/L; RR, 73-109 mg/dL [SI: 4.1-6.1 mmol/L]) and a 2-hour postprandial glucose level of 201 mg/dL [SI: 11.2 mmol/L], exceeding the target level of <120 mg/dL [SI: <6.7 mmol/L].

Diagnostic assessment

At 18 weeks of gestation, laboratory findings at presentation (Table 1) demonstrated suppressed morning plasma ACTH levels and absence of the normal diurnal cortisol rhythm. The 24-hour urinary free cortisol (UFC) level was more than 3 times the upper limit of normal, exceeding the physiological increase expected during pregnancy [7]. Serum PRL and estradiol (E2) levels were elevated, whereas luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels were suppressed; however, these findings were consistent with normal gestational hormonal changes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a left adrenal mass measuring 38 × 33 × 19 mm, without evidence of contralateral adrenal atrophy (Fig. 1B). Taken together, suppressed ACTH, loss of circadian cortisol rhythm, markedly elevated UFC, and the presence of an adrenal mass supported the diagnosis of ACTH-independent CS during pregnancy due to an adrenal tumor.

Treatment

A block-and-replacement regimen was initiated with metyrapone and oral hydrocortisone. The doses were gradually titrated to 1000 mg/day and 30 mg/day, respectively, achieving UFC levels slightly above the physiological range expected during pregnancy. Blood pressure was controlled with methyldopa, and glycemic control improved with basal-bolus insulin therapy. Laparoscopic left adrenalectomy was performed at 23 weeks and 3 days of gestation during the second trimester, generally considered the safest period for nonobstetric surgery in pregnancy. No maternal or fetal complications were observed. Histopathological examination confirmed a cortisol-producing adrenocortical adenoma composed predominantly of eosinophilic cells, with a Weiss score of 1, consistent with low malignant potential.

Outcome and follow-up

Postoperatively, the patient's cushingoid features and hypertension improved, and metyrapone and methyldopa were discontinued. Insulin requirements markedly decreased as glycemic control stabilized. At 38 weeks and 2 days of gestation, cesarean delivery was performed because of non-reassuring fetal status. No neonatal complications were observed. Hydrocortisone supplementation was discontinued 15 weeks postpartum, indicating earlier recovery of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis than typically observed after adrenalectomy for CS.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the development of CS during pregnancy, we performed RNA-seq on the resected tumor and adjacent normal adrenal tissue. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted as previously described [8, 9] using the Illumina QuantSeq 3' mRNA-seq kit (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria) and NextSeq 500 platform. Although this analysis was based on a single tumor and adjacent adrenal sample, 61 of 787 annotated GPCR genes were expressed in the tumor (>2 reads per million mapped reads [RPM]), of which 12 were upregulated more than 2-fold relative to normal adrenal tissue (Table 2). Previously reported GPCRs implicated in pregnancy-associated CS, particularly LHCGR, were not identified in this tumor. Although MC2R expression was high, suppressed plasma ACTH levels supported an ACTH-independent mechanism of cortisol production. We next expanded the analysis to non-GPCR genes and identified 26 transcripts upregulated in the tumor (Table 3). Among these, the prolactin receptor (PRLR) was selected for further investigation, as PRL levels increase during pregnancy due to lactotroph hyperplasia [10] and PRLR is expressed in normal human adrenal tissue [11]. PRLR mRNA expression was 2.73-fold higher in the adrenocortical adenoma than in adjacent normal adrenal tissue (Fig. 2A), and immunohistochemistry confirmed increased PRLR protein expression in the tumor (Fig. 2B). To evaluate the functional roles of PRLR overexpression, we examined PRL-induced cortisol production in primary cultures derived from cortisol-producing adenomas obtained from two nonpregnant women. Adrenocortical adenoma cells were collected immediately after adrenalectomy and cultured as previously described [8, 9]. The cells were treated with recombinant human PRL (1-100 nM) for 48 or 72 hours (Fig. 2C). PRL induced a dose-dependent increase in cortisol secretion into the culture medium, as measured by chemiluminescent immunoassay (Fig. 2D), and elevated intracellular cortisol levels determined by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these findings support the functional relevance of PRLR signaling in cortisol production.

Discussion

We report a case of pregnancy-associated CS in which an adrenocortical adenoma exhibited increased PRLR expression, accompanied by functional evidence that PRL stimulates cortisol production. These findings suggest that PRL–PRLR signaling may contribute to pregnancy-associated cortisol excess in susceptible adrenal tumors.

PRLR is expressed in normal adrenal tissue and has been implicated in adrenal development and steroidogenesis. In PRLR-deficient female mice, adrenal mass and circulating corticosterone are reduced, indicating a physiological role in adrenal function [12]. In humans, PRL at concentrations observed during pregnancy (10−9 to 10−7 mol/L) stimulates cortisol production in a dose-dependent manner in primary cultures of normal adrenal cells [11]. While these observations support a physiological role for PRLR signaling in normal adrenal tissue, its pathological relevance in adrenal tumors has not been well characterized. In the present case, PRLR expression was increased in the adenoma compared with adjacent normal adrenal cortex, and PRL enhanced cortisol production in primary cultures derived from independent cortisol-producing adenomas. These findings support a potential mechanistic link between PRLR signaling and cortisol overproduction. Our findings broaden the spectrum of hormone receptor–mediated mechanisms beyond GPCRs and suggest that evaluation of non-GPCR pathways, such as PRLR signaling, may provide additional insight into the pathophysiology of pregnancy-associated hypercortisolism. In addition, targeting the PRL–PRLR–cortisol axis may represent a potential therapeutic strategy and could, in selected cases, offer an alternative to surgery. Mechanistically, PRLR activates intracellular pathways, including JAK/STAT, MAPK, and PI3K/AKT [13]. Rodent studies have shown that PRL-induced steroidogenesis is attenuated by JAK2 inhibition, suggesting partial dependence on the JAK/STAT pathway [14]. Whether similar downstream pathways mediate PRL-driven cortisol production in human adrenal tumors remains to be elucidated and warrants further mechanistic investigation.

The clinical course further supports a relatively rapid onset of hypercortisolism during pregnancy. Cushingoid features developed after conception, imaging demonstrated an adrenal mass without contralateral adrenal atrophy, and postoperative glucocorticoid replacement was required only for a relatively short duration. These findings are more consistent with pregnancy-triggered cortisol overproduction than with longstanding hypercortisolism. Gestational PRL elevation may have triggered cortisol excess through PRLR signaling in a previously silent adrenal adenoma. This study has limitations. We were unable to demonstrate PRL-dependent cortisol secretion in primary cultures of cells from this patient's tumor, and no in vivo dynamic testing was performed to confirm PRL responsiveness. In addition, RNA-seq analysis was based on a single tumor and adjacent adrenal sample, and the use of adjacent adrenal tissue as a control may not fully represent normal adrenal cortex in the setting of ACTH-independent hypercortisolism. Larger case series and mechanistic studies using human adrenal tumor models are required to determine the clinical relevance of PRLR signaling in pregnancy-associated hypercortisolism and to determine how gestational hormonal changes may unmask hormone-responsive adrenal tumors.
