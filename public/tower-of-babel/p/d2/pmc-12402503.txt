Developing a plant based hand sanitizer using antibacterial Apium graveolens leaf extract
Authors: Fouzia Shafi, Samiya Iram, Aabida Jabeen, Sobiya Manzoor, Bazila Naseer, Tashooq Ahmad Bhat, Tawheed Amin, Faheema Mushtaq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndigenous medicinal plants, enriched with bioactive compounds such as alkaloids, flavonoids, and polyphenolic components, exhibit significant disinfectant, antiseptic, and antimicrobial properties. This study aimed to explore the antibacterial potential of celery leaf (Apium graveolens L.) extract for its application in hand sanitizer formulations. The extraction process was optimized under the following conditions: a solvent-to-sample ratio of 50:1, 80% solvent concentration, an extraction temperature of 80 °C, and an extraction time of 1 h. Under these optimized conditions, the total phenolic content was determined to be 62.70 mg GAE/g, with an antimicrobial activity of 11.02 mm. The study further investigated the antibacterial efficacy of various concentrations of celery leaf extract (10%, 25%, 50%, and 75%). The highest inhibition zone of 12.83 ± 0.29 mm was observed at 75% concentration, followed by 10.68 ± 0.32 mm at 50% and 7.5 ± 0.50 mm at 25%. These findings highlight the potential of celery leaf extract as a natural and effective ingredient in antimicrobial formulations, particularly for hand sanitizers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18033-7.Keywords: Antimicrobial activity, Extraction, Hand sanitizer, Medicinal plantSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Immunology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Medicinal plants have been widely utilized across the globe for their inherent antioxidant and antimicrobial properties, particularly in light of the rising prevalence of drug-resistant microorganisms1. However, the misuse and overuse of antibiotics have become a significant contributor to the emergence and spread of multidrug-resistant strains among various classes of pathogens2. Extensive research has validated the antimicrobial potential of numerous medicinal plants and their by-products, leading to the identification of several clinically significant bioactive compounds3. For centuries, plant extracts and their derivatives have been employed in traditional and alternative medicine, pharmaceuticals, food preservation, and natural remedies, underscoring their versatility and broad applicability across diverse fields4. Various vegetables, fruits, spices, herbs, and plant extracts have been recognized for their potential antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, anti-aging, anticancer, and antioxidant properties5. These beneficial effects are largely attributed to the presence of key bioactive compounds, including alkaloids, terpenes, phenolic acids, glycosides, and flavonoids6. Previous studies have also highlighted the antibacterial and antifungal properties of various plants against a range of enteric bacteria and common fungi.

Celery (Apium graveolens L.) is an aromatic plant cultivated worldwide, primarily for its fresh herbs, which are used as a salad crop. Its dried fruits are also widely used as a spice. In India, celery is commonly referred to as ‘Karnauli’ or ‘Ajmod,’ while its wild form is known as smallage. Celery is consumed globally as a vegetable and is often used as a flavoring agent in soups and pickled as a side dish. It is particularly renowned for its distinctive aroma and essential oil content3. The plant is rich in tocopherols, carotenes, vitamins, and secondary metabolites such as alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenolic acids. Additionally, it is a valuable source of choline, potassium, folate, and vitamins A and K. These compounds exhibit antinociceptive, anti-inflammatory, and tranquilizing properties. The essential oil and extracts of celery contain numerous biologically active compounds that have demonstrated significant antimicrobial effects7.

The leaf extract of celery has shown potent antimicrobial activity against a range of pathogens, including Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, Hafnia alvei, Salmonella typhimurium, Enterobacter aerogenes, Enterococcus faecalis, and Staphylococcus aureus8. Due to its antimicrobial efficacy, celery leaf extract has been explored as a potential active ingredient in hand sanitizers. Studies have suggested its use as an alternative to alcohol in sanitizer formulations, given its effectiveness against various microorganisms9. Staphylococcus aureus, a common skin pathogen, rapidly colonizes the superficial layers of the skin and is a primary component of the skin’s microbial flora10. This bacterium is classified as a pathogenic organism that can be transmitted through hand contact, making it a critical target for antimicrobial interventions.

The primary objective of this study was twofold: (1) to optimize the extraction conditions (solvent ratio, concentration, temperature, and time) for maximizing phenolic yield and general antimicrobial activity from celery leaves using response surface methodology, and (2) to formulate a hand sanitizer from the optimized extract and validate its efficacy specifically against Staphylococcus aureus, a clinically relevant skin pathogen.

Materials and methods

Raw material collection and preparation

Celery leaves (Apium graveolens L.) were collected from the experimental fields of the Department of Vegetable Science at Sher-e-Kashmir University of Agricultural Sciences and Technology, Shalimar, Kashmir, India (Voucher No. 574, Dated: 13/11/2022) and all the methods used in this work are in compliance with institutional guidelines. Healthy, disease-free leaves were harvested in the morning, transported immediately to the laboratory, and washed thoroughly to remove surface debris. Fresh leaves were air-dried (25 °C, 48 h) until achieving 5% moisture content, verified by gravimetric analysis (AOAC 934.01) and infrared drying (Sartorius MA35). Dried leaves were ground and stored in airtight containers.

Process for extraction of phenols from celery leaves

Shade-dried celery leaves were ground into a fine powder and extracted with methanol (99.98%) under varying solvent-to-sample ratios, temperatures (50–90 °C), and extraction times (0.5–3.0 h) as outlined in Table S1. The mixture was agitated in a laboratory shaker (120 rpm), followed by centrifugation (REMI-8 C, 5000 rpm, 15 min) to separate aqueous and organic phases. The dried extract (250 mg per 1 g initial powder) was reconstituted in 2 mL methanol, yielding a 125 mg/mL stock. This stock was diluted as needed for TPC (0.5 mg/mL) and antimicrobial assays (12.5–93.75 mg/mL)12.

Experimental design

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize four independent variables: solvent-to-sample ratio (A), solvent concentration (B), extraction temperature (C), and extraction time (D). Each variable was tested at five coded levels (−2, −1, 0, + 1, +2) across 30 experimental runs, including 12 center points (Table S1). Total phenolic content (TPC) determination

TPC was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu method13.

Antimicrobial activity assessment

Antimicrobial activity was assessed exclusively against Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC FST 1) via agar well diffusion14, as it is the primary target for hand hygiene efficacy.

Optimization

ANOVA was utilized to employ the significance of individual independent variables, and multiple linear regression was employed to investigate the significant impact of these variables on the response variables. The statistical significance of the parameters in regression models was assessed at a 5% significance level. Process optimization was conducted using the desirability function approach15.

Preparation of sanitizer from optimized celery leaf extract

The leaves of celery were dried in the shade. The dried leaf material was finely powdered using a pestle and mortar, and extraction was performed using optimized conditions utilizing methanol as the solvent (solvent sample ratio: 50:1, solvent concentration: 80%, extraction temperature: 80 °C and extraction time: 1 h). The solvent extracts underwent filtration using four layers of muslin cloth, followed by additional filtering through Whatman filter paper (No.1). Subsequently, the filtrate underwent centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 20 min. Concentrated solid extract (1 g/mL in methanol) was diluted with 200 mL distilled water to prepare working solutions (22, 55, 110, 165 mL). These were blended with 5 g CMC-Na, 10 mL glycerin, and 5 mL propylene glycol, yielding final volumes of ~ 242–365 mL. Final celery extract concentrations were 9.9–45.2% (v/v) in the aqueous phase (extract + water). Then, this solution was dissolved in CMC-Na and hot distilled water. Subsequently, the solution was kept at ambient temperature for 15 min and stirred until a uniform gel mass was formed. The gel mass was then mixed with propylene glycol and glycerin16. pH testing was done by using pH meter. The pH values were adjusted to the pH requirement of topical preparation i.e., 4.5–6.5. Testing of prepared celery leaf-based extract sanitizer

Test microorganism

Bacterial cultures were obtained by serial dilution method. Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC strain FST 1) was obtained through isolation of pure culture using continuous streaking method. The strains were preserved on agar slant at 4 °C and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h on nutrient agar prior to any susceptibility testing.

Antibacterial assay of the prepared sanitizer

The antimicrobial efficacy of the extracts was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method, following the protocol outlined by Bauer et al.17 with slight modifications. Staphylococcus aureus strains cultured on nutrient agar at 37 °C for 24 h were suspended in a saline solution (0.85% NaCl) and adjusted to a turbidity of 0.5 on the McFarland scale (equivalent to 108 CFU/ml). Petri plates were allowed to solidify in a laminar hood. Wells, 5 mm in diameter, were created and filled with 100 µl of each celery leaf extract at concentrations of 10%, 25%, 50%, and 75%. A 100 µL aliquot of 70% ethanol (v/v, commercial hand sanitizer, Sterillium®) was applied as the positive control, while distilled water served as the negative control. All treatments were tested in triplicate wells (5 mm diameter). The petri plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 18 to 24 h. Following incubation, the plates were inspected for zones of inhibition, and the antibacterial effectiveness was determined by measuring the diameters of the inhibition zones in millimeters (mm) using a standard measuring scale.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were replicated, and the results were presented as the mean of three separate determinations.

Proximate analysis of celery leaves

The proximate composition of celery leaves (Apium graveolens) is summarized in Table 2. The moisture, crude fiber, crude fat, ash, crude protein and carbohydrate contents were recorded as 78.96%, 1.80%, 1.60%, 7.94%, 1.57%, and 8.13%, respectively. The high moisture content of celery leaves influences their absorption and assimilation rates in biological systems. The protein content (1.57%) aligns with the findings of Ashoush et al.18, who reported a similar value of 1.94%. The ash content, indicative of mineral composition, was consistent with data reported by Ifon and Bassir19 for Nigerian leafy vegetables. The crude fat content (1.60%) suggests that celery leaves are low in plant lipids, classifying them as low-fat foods, as noted by Lintas20. The crude fiber content (1.80%) underscores the dietary importance of celery leaves, as fiber is essential for digestion (Oliver, 1986). Adults are recommended to consume 18–32 g of fiber daily21, and celery leaves, with their 8.13% carbohydrate content, serve as a valuable energy source in normal diets22. These findings are consistent with those of Facciola23 for L. sativum.

Effect of extraction conditions on total phenol content of celery leaves

Total phenol content (TPC) was used to quantify the phenolic compounds in celery leaves. Phenolic compounds, naturally occurring in plants, exhibit redox properties that confer antioxidant activity24. The TPC of celery leaf extracts ranged from 39.68 to 63.33 mg GAE/g (Table 3). The highest TPC (63.33 mg GAE/g) was achieved at a methanol: celery ratio of 50:1, 80% methanol concentration, 80 °C extraction temperature, and 1-hour extraction time. Conversely, the lowest TPC (39.68 mg GAE/g) was observed at a methanol: celery ratio of 40:1, 60% methanol concentration, 70 °C extraction temperature, and 3-hour extraction time.

Effect of extraction conditions on antimicrobial activity

Antimicrobial activity, influenced by secondary metabolites like tannins and flavonoids27, ranged from 7.71 to 12.24 mm inhibition zones (Table 3). The highest activity (12.24 mm) occurred at a 30:1 solvent ratio, 80% methanol, 60 °C, and 1 h, whereas the lowest (7.71 mm) corresponded to a 40:1 ratio, 20% methanol, 70 °C, and 1.5 h.

The fitted regression model (Eq. 4) and response surface analysis (Fig. 1B) revealed significant (p ≤ 0.01) positive linear effects of solvent: sample ratio (A) and solvent concentration (B) on antimicrobial activity, whereas extraction temperature (C) and time (D) exerted negative linear effects. Among interaction terms, AC (solvent ratio × temperature) and AD (solvent ratio × time) enhanced activity, while AB (solvent ratio × concentration), BC (concentration × temperature), and CD (temperature × time) suppressed it. Maximal antimicrobial activity (12.24 mm inhibition zone) was achieved at 80% methanol concentration and a 30:1 solvent: sample ratio, coupled with moderate extraction conditions (60 °C for 1 h). These findings underscore that higher solvent ratios and concentrations amplify antimicrobial efficacy, likely due to enhanced mass transfer driven by polarity gradients and solute-solvent affinity25. Conversely, prolonged extraction durations and elevated temperatures diminished activity, potentially due to thermal degradation of thermolabile antimicrobial compounds28. For instance, temperatures exceeding 60 °C may disrupt plant cell integrity, triggering premature release and oxidation of bioactive constituents29. Similarly, extended extraction times (> 1 h) correlated with reduced efficacy, likely attributable to polyphenol and pigment degradation30. This aligns with Rostagno et al.31, who demonstrated that optimal phenolic extraction requires balancing solvent efficiency and time-dependent compound stability.

Optimization of extraction parameters

The desirability function identified 40:1 solvent: sample ratio, 80% methanol, 70 °C, and 1 h as the global optimum, balancing TPC (58.21 mg GAE/g) and antimicrobial activity (11.82 mm). While 50:1/80°C maximized TPC (63.33 mg GAE/g), and 30:1/60°C maximized antimicrobial activity (12.24 mm), the selected conditions ensure > 90% efficacy for both parameters—critical for sanitizer formulations.

Efficacy testing of celery leaf-based sanitizer

Hand hygiene remains a cost-effective strategy for mitigating infectious disease transmission, with sanitizers broadly categorized as alcohol-based or non-alcoholic alternatives32. In this study, a novel sanitizer was formulated from Apium graveolens Leaf extract, incorporating distilled water, sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na), propylene glycol, and glycerin. Celery-derived polyphenolic flavonoids, known for their broad-spectrum bioactive properties—including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities33 were hypothesized to underpin the sanitizer’s efficacy.

Antimicrobial performance

The sanitizer exhibited dose-dependent antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus (Table 4). At 45.2% concentration (165 mL), the inhibition zone measured 12.83 ± 0.29 mm, surpassing lower concentrations (10.68 ± 0.32 mm at 21.6%; 7.5 ± 0.50 mm at 9.9%) and the negative control (distilled water: no inhibition). While slightly less potent than the alcohol-based positive control (70% ethanol: 14.98 ± 0.07 mm; Fig. 2), the celery formulation demonstrated significant bactericidal action, suppressing S. aureus proliferation even at 25% concentration. 3. Synergistic activity

Mechanistic insights

The observed efficacy likely stems from synergies between celery’s bioactive constituents—polyphenols, carotenoids, and antioxidants—which disrupt microbial cell membranes and metabolic pathways34. Statistical analysis confirmed the sanitizer’s broad-spectrum potential, with activity escalating proportionally to extract concentration. This aligns with prior studies linking phenolic density to antimicrobial potency24.

Practical implications

Though marginally less effective than ethanol-based sanitizers, the celery formulation offers a natural alternative with reduced risks of adverse effects (e.g., skin irritation, dryness) associated with prolonged alcohol use. Its dose-responsive activity further supports scalability for diverse hygiene applications. Celery’s efficacy (12.83 mm) is comparable to neem/tulsi (~ 11–14 mm), outperforming aloe/olive (~ 9–10 mm), despite lower phenolic density than neem. Conclusion

This study demonstrates the viability of Apium graveolens (celery) leaf extract as a natural, sustainable alternative to conventional alcohol-based hand sanitizers. Proximate analysis revealed celery leaves to be a nutrient-rich resource, characterized by high moisture content (78.96%) and bioactive compounds such as polyphenols and crude fiber, which align with their potential for hygienic applications. Optimized extraction parameters—a 40:1 solvent: sample ratio, 80% methanol, 70 °C, and 1-hour duration—yielded extracts with balanced efficacy: 58.21 mg GAE/g TPC and 11.82 mm inhibition zone. While a 30:1 ratio at 60 °C maximized antimicrobial activity (12.24 mm), the selected conditions ensure > 90% of peak performance for both metrics, critical for industrial scalability. The formulated celery-based sanitizer exhibited dose-dependent efficacy, achieving a maximum inhibition zone of 12.83 ± 0.29 mm at 75% concentration. While slightly less potent than alcohol-based controls (14.98 ± 0.07 mm), its performance underscores the therapeutic potential of celery-derived polyphenols and carotenoids, which disrupt microbial proliferation through membrane interference and oxidative stress mechanisms. Critically, this natural formulation mitigates risks associated with alcohol-based sanitizers, such as skin irritation, dryness, and systemic side effects (e.g., headaches, nausea). By leveraging celery’s phytochemical richness, this work advances eco-friendly hygiene solutions that balance efficacy, safety, and accessibility—a vital step toward addressing global demand for sustainable antiseptic products.
