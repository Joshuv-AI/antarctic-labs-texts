A Label-Free blocking immunoassay to evaluate Anti-Adalimumab antibody activity in clinical samples
Authors: Judi Abdelrazik, Naomi Iwai, Yonghua Liu, Nathaniel Taylor, Ruben Y Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A novel LF-BIA using BLI to quantify the blocking activity of ADAs against ADL.•The LF-BIA detected blocking activity of ADAs in serum samples.•The LF-BIA may complement the existing traditional assays for total ADAs.Keywords: Label-Free Blocking Immunoassay, Biolayer Interferometry, Adalimumab, Anti-Drug Antibodies

Introduction

Adalimumab (ADL) is a monoclonal antibody (mAb) drug against tumor necrosis factor-⍺ (TNF-⍺) for the treatment of autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, Crohn’s disease, psoriasis, and juvenile arthritis [1]. As one of the top-selling anti-TNF-⍺ mAb therapeutics, ADL has been widely used, and patients generally respond well to ADL therapy [2,3]. However, it was observed that initial response was lost over time in up to 70 % of patients, mainly due to the development of antidrug antibodies (ADAs), which could neutralize ADL and reduce its efficacy [3,4]. ADAs are categorized into two categories: neutralizing ADAs and non-neutralizing ADAs. The majority of neutralizing ADAs are blocking ADAs, which bind to the mAb drug’s active site and block its pharmacological function. Non-neutralizing ADAs bind to other regions of the mAb drug, allowing it to remain active [5].

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), in which serum concentrations of a mAb drug are measured and doses are optimized accordingly, can improve response rate and disease management and has been shown to be a more cost-effective approach for mAb therapies [6,7]. A large randomized clinical trial (NOR-DRUM) on the TDM of TNF-α inhibitors confirmed that proactive TDM for timely detection of ADAs reduced negative consequences in comparison to standard treatment for maintaining disease control, supporting proactive TDM as a general strategy during maintenance therapy [8,9]. Immunoassay methodologies, such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) have been reported to measure ADA concentrations, but they require reporter molecules to generate a readout and do not directly measure antibody-antigen interactions. Optical sensing-based label-free immunoassay platforms, such as biolayer interferometry (BLI, or thin-layer interferometry, TFI), enable real-time measurement by directly detecting changes in physical parameters caused by antibody-antigen interactions rather than relying on reporter molecules, providing a fast, direct alternative solution [10,11]. On the other hand, immunoassays for mAb drug TDM typically measure ADA concentrations rather than their functional impact. It is useful to also measure the blocking or neutralization activity of ADAs. Building on the application of BLI to quantify ADL and ADAs in serum samples [12], this article describes an attempt to establish a label-free blocking immunoassay (LF-BIA) to measure the ADA blocking activity in serum samples.

Methods

Samples and reagents

A cohort of 42 patient samples was acquired from a reference clinical laboratory (Mayo Clinic Laboratory, Rochester, MN) for assay development purposes. Within the cohort, 20 samples tested positive for anti-ADL ADAs by an ELISA performed at the reference clinical laboratory. The remaining 22 samples had active ADL concentrations in the therapeutic range (>8 μg/mL) and were considered negative for anti-ADL ADAs, though ELISA testing was not performed. The samples were fully anonymized remnant specimens, not obtained specifically for use in this study through an interaction or intervention with living individuals; therefore, neither informed consent nor IRB review was required.

BLI sensing probes pre-coated with streptavidin and the running buffer PBST (phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.4 with 0.02 % Tween 20) were obtained from Gator Bio (Palo Alto, CA). An ADL biosimilar was purchased from Ushelf (Hopkinton, MA), and biotinylated using an EZ-Link HPDP-biotin reagent kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). The LF-BIA was applied to 42 patient samples.

Experiment Protocol

A blocking immunoassay is a type of immunoassay designed to measure the ability of antibodies to block or inhibit the interaction between the antigen and its binding partner [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. In this case, following the model of a label-free surrogate virus neutralization test [17], the LF-BIA was designed to measure the ADA blocking activity against the interaction between ADL and its therapeutic target TNF-⍺. The assay was performed on a Gator Plus BLI analyzer (Gator Bio, Palo Alto, CA). The complete time course of ADL-ADA and ADL-TNF-⍺ immune complex formation on each sensing probe was recorded as a sensorgram.

Each serum sample was serially diluted (1:10, 1:25, 1:50, 1:100, 1:250, 1:500, 1:1000) in PBST for analysis. Negative control samples (N = 9, from healthy individuals), a set of negative samples with severe hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia (HIL) (N = 5, 1H-index > 1000 mg/dL, 2 I-index > 60 mg/dL, 2 L-index > 2000 mg/dL), blank buffer, and a positive control – a blocking rabbit monoclonal anti-ADL antibody (Yurogen, Worcester, MA) – were also included. The LF-BIA was implemented by sequentially dipping sensing probes into reagents and diluted samples in a 384-well plate orbitally shaken at 1000 rpm.

The BLI analyzer operates eight sensing probes simultaneously. After dipping the sensing probes in 10 μg/mL biotinylated ADL in PBST to immobilize ADL onto the probe surfaces, the assay was carried out in measurement cycles, each consisting of two processes. The first process included (1) dipping a sensing probe into PBST for 2 min to acquire a baseline, (2) dipping the sensing probe into a diluted sample for 10 min to form ADL-ADA complexes on the sensing probe, (3) dipping the sensing probe into PBST for 2 min to dissociate non-specifically bound molecules, and (4) dipping the sensing probe into 2 μg/mL TNF-⍺ in PBST (TNF-⍺ purchased from Acrobiosystems (Newark, DE), reconstituted in deionized water) for 10 min to form ADL-TNF-α complexes on the sensing probe. The second process included only the first and last steps of the first process, determining the full binding capacity of ADL to TNF-⍺ on each sensing probe for the calculation of ADA blocking rate. Between the processes and between the cycles, the sensing probes were regenerated in 0.01 % formic acid to strip molecules bound to ADL on the probe surfaces. Each cycle measured one sample dilution. Regeneration performance was confirmed by reproducible sensorgrams in the second process across all cycles. The LF-BIA workflow and example sensorgrams are illustrated in Fig. 1. In each experiment, the probes were first primed in the 1:10 samples and regenerated to reduce non-specific binding to background serum proteins, and measurements then proceeded from higher dilutions (1:1000) to lower dilutions (1:10).

Illustration of the LF-BIA workflow and example sensorgrams. In each cycle of measurement, (A) the first process measuring the binding capacity of ADL to TNF-⍺ after blocking by ADAs, and (B) the second process measuring the full binding capacity of ADL to TNF-⍺. The example sensorgrams of the dilution series of a serum sample (1:50, 1:100, 1:250, 1:500, 1:1000, and 1:2000) in the first process and the second process are displayed in (C) and (D), respectively. The “jumps” in the sensorgrams were caused by refractive index change when the sensing probes were transferred between PBST and samples, which did not affect the data analysis.

In each cycle of measurement (corresponding to one dilution of samples), an ADA blocking rate was calculated as one minus the ratio of the readout in the first process to that in the second process, presented as a percentage.

The ADA blocking activity in patient samples was evaluated using the fold of SD, calculated as the difference in blocking rate between a patient sample and the mean of the negative controls, divided by the SD of the negative controls. Student’s t-test was used to assess statistical significance between the blocking rates of different samples. A cutoff value (fold of SD) could be set to differentiate positive and negative LF-BIA results. To compare the LF-BIA and ELISA results, a contingency table was made, and the parameters including Pearson’s chi-square, clinical sensitivity, clinical specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), were calculated using formulae in a textbook [18], while P-value was calculated using was calculated in Python using the “chi2_contingency” function from the “scipy.stats” module. In all statistical analysis, a P-value less than 0.05 was considered to conclude concordance between the two sets of results.

Results

The BLI signal increase during the TNF-⍺ loading step in the first process of each cycle was proportional to the residual binding capacity of ADL to TNF-⍺ and inversely proportional to the quantity of ADL-ADA complexes on the sensing probe. As shown in Fig. 2A, ADA blocking rates were plotted against dilution factors for negative controls (N = 9), negative samples with severe HIL, blank buffer (PBST, 8 replicates), and a positive control (8 replicates). Round dots indicate mean values, and error bars represent the standard deviation (SD) values. The near-zero blocking rates of the blank buffer confirmed the reproducibility of on-probe ADL binding to TNF-⍺ across regeneration steps, as well as the performance of the BLI analyzer. The blocking rates of the HIL samples and negative controls were aligned, indicating that HIL conditions did not significantly increase LF-BIA background (t-test, t-score ranged from −1.0 to 1.5, P > 0.05). The blocking rates of the negative controls and the positive control could be well differentiated at lower dilution factors (1:10, 1:25, 1:50), though background signals were observed in the negative controls likely due to the impact of non-specific binding of background serum proteins as reported in other blocking immunoassays [14,15]. The blocking rates for the 42 patient samples are plotted in Fig. 2B. Because the ELISA-not-performed group remained distinct from the negative controls, it is reasonable to suspect that some of these samples contain ADAs.

ADA blocking rates plotted against dilution factors for ELISA-positive patient samples, ELISA-not-performed patient samples, a positive control (8 replicates), negative controls (N = 9), HIL samples (N = 5), and blank buffer (8 replicates). For clarity, the plots for the positive control and the patient samples are shown separately in (A) and (B). Round dots indicate mean values, and error bars represent standard deviation.

The dilution factor 1:25 was chosen to determine the ADA blocking activity because the background in negative controls was insignificant (average blocking rate about 5 %) [14,15], while the difference between the negative controls and the positive control was significant (t-test, t-score = 9.1, P < 0.05). The LF-BIA results are summarized in the box-and-whisker plot in Fig. 3A. The overlap between the ELISA-positive and ELISA-not-performed sample groups can be explained by methodological differences: LF-BIA measures blocking ADAs, whereas ELISA quantifies total ADAs. The ELISA-positive patient samples with relatively low blocking activity may contain only a small proportion of blocking ADAs within the total ADA pool, and the ELISA-not-performed patient samples with relatively high blocking activity may have blocking ADAs while ADL concentrations remain in the therapeutic range (>8 μg/mL). Because no ADA testing service using blocking immunoassays or neutralization assays is currently available, the LF-BIA results could not be compared with an assay of the same type. Assuming the ELISA results are used as the reference for positive/negative classification, the cutoff value for LF-BIA results could be set between 3- and 4-fold SD of negative controls, determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis [16], as shown in Fig. 3B.

(A) Box-and-whisker plot of ADA blocking activity in patient samples (20 ELISA-positive, 22 ELISA-not-performed). In each sample group, whiskers represent the full range, the box spans the second and third quartiles, and the central line marks the median. LF-BIA results are shown as dots on the right. (B) ROC curve of the LF-BIA using ELISA results as the positive/negative reference. The values next to each round dot denote the LF-BIA cutoff in fold of SD. Parameters to compare the LF-BIA and ELISA results: cutoff at 3-fold SD, Pearson’s chi-square 4.97, P = 0.026, clinical sensitivity 75 %, clinical specificity 59 %, PPV = 63 %, NPV = 72 %; cutoff at 4-fold SD, Pearson’s chi-square 6.04, P = 0.014, clinical sensitivity 60 %, clinical specificity 77 %, PPV = 71 %, NPV = 68 %.

Discussion

The LF-BIA may provide an efficient approach to specifically measure blocking ADAs rather than total ADAs. Preparing a batch of samples takes about one hour, and a BLI analyzer can handle multiple sensing probes (8–32) to test several patient samples simultaneously, allowing decent throughput. In patient samples, the LF-BIA results showed partial concordance with ELISA, as illustrated by the ROC curve in Fig. 3B. These findings suggest that LF-BIA may capture a different characteristic of ADAs (blocking activity), complementing ADA concentrations measured by immunoassays. However, fully assessing the LF-BIA performance remains challenging due to the lack of established reference assays for detecting blocking or neutralizing ADAs. An alternative approach to verify its performance would be to correlate the measured ADA blocking activity with patient responses to ADL, though this would likely require additional resources. In addition, the LF-BIA did not include an acid-dissociation step to dissociate bound ADAs from the mAb drug, similar to some commercial ELISAs and other reported ADA assays [7,12,19]. In these assays, measuring free ADAs may miss patients who are beginning to generate low amounts of ADAs, but it will not miss those with relatively higher levels of ADAs.

Optical sensing-based label-free immunoassays enable real-time, automated measurement of immunoreactions without chemical labels. By capturing the complete time course of immune complex formation, these methods facilitate efficient analysis and ease of troubleshooting. Traditional blocking ELISA methods typically evaluate the competitive binding of serum antibodies and a specific antibody to an antigen, with either the antigen or the specific antibody immobilized on a substrate surface [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. These methods require chemical labeling of the antigen or the specific antibody, or addition of a secondary antibody for signal generation. Chemical labels or secondary antibodies can interfere with immune complex formation and compromise assay performance. The LF-BIA adopts a similar competitive binding format, measuring the ability of ADAs to block the interaction between a binding partner (TNF-⍺) and an antigen (ADL). Unlike ELISA methods, the LF-BIA uses an optical sensing-based label-free approach that preserves biomolecules in their native forms to better reflect the immunoreactions. Alongside previously reported applications of label-free immunoassays in clinical testing [10,12,17,20], the LF-BIA highlights a promising application area in which label-free technologies such as BLI can play an important role.
