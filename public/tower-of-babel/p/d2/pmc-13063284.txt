Progress of transgenic pigs in liver xenotransplantation
Authors: Xing-Ming Xie, Guo-Ying Wang
Published: 2026

Liver transplantation, a medical procedure in which a diseased or failing liver is replaced with a healthy liver from a donor, is an effective method for resolving end-stage liver diseases. The primary source of livers is voluntary donation, resulting in a severe and long-term shortage of available human donor organs, and leaving many patients unable to receive timely treatment. Indeed, different regions or cultures may exhibit different demands for liver transplantation.1 Xenotransplantation provides a potential solution to overcome the organ shortage. Pigs are considered ideal donor animals due to their physiological characteristics similar to humans.2,3

Pigs with knockout of three genes, which are 1,3-galactosyltransferase(GGTA1)/beta-1,4-N-acetyl-galactosaminyltransferase 2 (β4GalNT2)/cytidine monophospho-N-acetylneuraminic acid hydroxylase(CMAH), have demonstrated their potential in reducing hyperacute rejection and acute humoral rejection in xenotransplantation.4 Until 2024, liver xenotransplantation from pig to human had achieved significant progress, as evidenced by three clinical cases conducted by Chinese scientists.

On March 10, 2024, Professor Dou Kefeng's team from Xijing Hospital of Air Force Medical University transplanted a whole liver of a gene-edited pig into a brain-dead person. This marked the first attempt to transplant a gene-edited pig liver into a brain-dead patient, with the patient's original liver left in place.5 The donor pig liver was provided by Chengdu Clonorgan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and underwent six gene edits including the knockout of three pig antigens (GGTA1, β4GalNT2, and CMAH) that can cause hyperacute rejection and acute humoral rejection, as well as the knock-in of human complement regulatory proteins (hCD46, hCD55) and the human coagulation regulatory protein human thrombomodulin (hTBM). After 96 hours of surgery, the donor liver functioned well with no hyperacute rejection reaction observed. The transplanted pig liver survived for 10 days in the recipient. On January 7, 2025, Dou Kefeng's team successfully implanted a gene-edited pig liver in situ into another brain-dead patient, achieving complete replacement of the human liver.6 However, no reports on the survival time of this transplanted liver have been released.

On May 17, 2024, Professor Wei Hongjiang's team from Yunnan Agricultural University, in collaboration with the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, performed a genetically modified pig liver transplantation in a 71-year-old patient with severe liver cancer. After 7 days of surgery, the patient was able to move around freely, and no hyperacute or acute rejection reactions were detected. The coagulation system functioned normally, and liver function returned to normal. Unfortunately, repeated upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage ultimately led to the patient's death on day 171.7

On May 22, 2024, the Chinese scientific team led by Professor Dong Jiahong successfully completed the world's first combined xenotransplantation of gene-edited pig liver and kidney into brain-dead patients in Kunming, China.8 On January 31, 2026, Professor Zeng Zhong's team at the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University successfully transplanted the liver and kidneys from a six-gene-edited pig into a brain-dead recipient. Postoperatively, the transplanted xenograft liver and kidneys functioned continuously for 11 days.9

Chinese scientists have successfully transplanted gene-edited pig livers into humans. The knockout of GGTA1, CMAH, and β4GalNT2 significantly inhibited hyperacute rejection and acute humoral rejection. The knock-in of hCD46, hCD55, and hCD59 effectively alleviated acute rejection caused by activation of the human complement system. These inhibitory effects of the above genes on hyperacute or acute rejection reactions were verified in pig-to-human liver xenotransplantation, demonstrating a promising gene-editing strategy to prolong the survival of pig livers in humans.

Dou Kefeng's team used a gene-edited pig overexpressing hTBM against coagulation. Wei Hongjiang's team used a gene-edited pig overexpressing hTBM, hCD39, and the human endothelial protein C receptor (hEPCR) against coagulation. Dong Jiahong's team used a gene-edited pig overexpressing hTBM and hCD39 against coagulation. TBM expressed on the endothelial cell surface inhibits thrombosis and blood coagulation. Porcine TBM, however, is unable to effectively inhibit blood coagulation in humans, leading to hypercoagulability and graft failure in xenotransplantation.10 The EPCR is a membrane-bound receptor involved in the regulation of blood coagulation. Baboons that received a pig kidney overexpressing human EPCR had a longer survival time compared to those that received a pig kidney overexpressing human TBM.11 The knock-in of hCD39 in pigs also inhibited platelet activation and thrombus generation in xenotransplantation.12 Taken together, the knock-in of human coagulation regulatory proteins in pigs is a feasible strategy to alleviate the coagulation dysregulation in pig-to-human liver xenotransplantation.

In xenotransplantation, genetically modified pigs still cannot prevent the transmission of porcine endogenous retrovirus (PERV) to the recipient. In addition to PERV, porcine cytomegalovirus, hepatitis E virus, porcine lymphoherpesvirus, and porcine circovirus may cause infections. Most viruses in pigs can be eliminated through vaccination or antiviral drugs; however, PERV is an endogenous retrovirus integrated into the pig genome that is difficult to eliminate by these methods. After xenotransplantation, PERV can integrate into the human genome, resulting in cross-species virus transmission. Nowadays, PERV is considered the virus with the highest risk level in xenotransplantation.13 Therefore, pigs with no expression of PERV-C and low expression of PERV-A and PERV-B are recommended as transplant donors to reduce the risk of viral transmission.

In order to reduce the risk of pathogen transmission to humans, the pigs should grow in an environment without designated pathogens. In order to establish and maintain a designated pathogen-free environment, isolating and raising donor pigs, providing sterile feed, and conducting regular pathogen testing on their living environment are adopted.

Some issues require special attention. Once a pig liver is transplanted into the human body, PERV may integrate into the human genome or recombine with human endogenous retroviruses, potentially generating novel viruses that could pose a disaster to humans. Although scientists have reported inactivating PERV using CRISPR-Cas9, the risks associated with such cross-species intervention cannot be fully foreseen at present. Digital droplet PCR and next-generation sequencing technologies have been utilized for the detection of PERV. More effective and sensitive detection methods should continue to be developed, which will contribute to the detection of PERV.

In recent years, porcine cytomegalovirus (PCMV) has been commonly found in pigs. PCMV transmission was documented in a notable case of pig heart xenotransplantation. Despite the challenges posed by PCMV, it has not been shown to infect human cells, and research has confirmed its lack of cross-species infectivity.14 This offers a degree of reassurance that PCMV does not pose a major zoonotic threat. Methods such as early weaning of pigs and the use of PCR-based tests have reduced the likelihood of PCMV transmission.

Allotransplantation of the liver in humans has achieved great success in saving the lives of patients with end-stage liver diseases and liver cancer, allowing them to survive for extended periods. In contrast, only a few cases of pig-to-human liver xenotransplantation have been performed, with short-term survival benefits observed. However, the long-term survival benefits need to be investigated. Due to the shortage of donor livers, a large number of patients with end-stage liver disease cannot receive allotransplantation. Although artificial liver support improves metabolic disorders and clears most hazardous substances caused by end-stage liver diseases, the patient's condition may deteriorate again due to the underlying diseased liver. In such cases, pig-to-human liver xenotransplantation can provide patients with a healthy liver, quickly relieving the critical condition and leaving patients with more time to wait for allotransplantation. It has been reported that a pig embryo with kidney development knocked out by CRISPR gene editing technology was implanted with human pluripotent stem cells to produce a humanized mesonephros.15 The chimeric humanized mesonephros from pigs may significantly alleviate the rejection reaction in xenotransplantation. Chimeric livers can provide a liver source for patients awaiting xenotransplantation. Nevertheless, the development of a chimeric humanized liver in pigs still needs substantial experimental verification.
