Is Too Noisy as Bad as Too Quiet? The Inverted U-shaped Relationship between Noise and Sleep Duration
Authors: Yuqi Chen, Le Chang, Zhiying Huang, Ninger Lou, Yijing Chu, Juan Tu, Han Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Sleep is a crucial behavioral determinant of physical and psychological well-being. With the rapid urbanization of China, noise pollution has become a growing concern, and its impact on sleep has raised substantial public health attention. This study aims to investigate the potential non-linear relationship between environmental noise and individual sleep duration, and the moderating roles of urban green space and city size.Methods:We conducted a longitudinal analysis using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. City-level noise exposure was quantified using the Baidu Noise Index, a novel approach in this context. Data on urban population and green space coverage were obtained from the National Bureau of Statistics of China. Fixed-effects regression models were employed to estimate the impact of noise on sleep duration, and moderation analysis was applied to explore the roles of green space and city size.Results:An inverted U-shaped association was observed between noise levels and sleep duration (linear β = 0.746, P < 0.001; quadratic β = –0.955, P < 0.001), with an inflection point at 0.391 [95% confidence interval (CI): [0.320, 0.566]. Moreover, this inverted U-shaped pattern was attenuated in cities with higher green space coverage (quadratic interaction β = 0.822, P < 0.05) and larger population (quadratic interaction β = 1.072, P < 0.001).Conclusion:This study underscores the multifaceted relationship between noise exposure and sleep duration, providing a theoretical foundation for urban planning and health policy formulation, particularly in optimizing urban sound environments and promoting green space development.

INTRODUCTION

Sleep plays a critical role in modulating immune function and metabolic processes, serving as a key determinant for both physical and psychological well-being.[1] Adequate and uninterrupted sleep is vital for daily vigilance, job efficiency, overall health and life satisfaction.[2] Substantial experimental evidence has demonstrated that inadequate or poor-quality sleep is closely linked to health issues, such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease.[345] The detrimental effects extend beyond physical morbidity to encompass critical neurological outcomes and increase all-cause mortality.[67]

Concurrently, rapid urbanization in China has rendered environmental noise a considerable public health challenge.[8] Noise pollution, primarily stemming from transportation, industrial activities, and community sources, has become a prevalent environmental stressor.[9] The World Health Organization attributed a significant portion of the related disease burden specifically to noise-induced sleep disturbance.[10]

Sleep disruption caused by environmental noise remains a pivotal public health concern. Accumulating evidence indicates that noise exposure compromises sleep quality through multiple pathways, including prolonged sleep latency, frequent awakenings, and diminished sleep efficiency.[10] These effects manifest across diverse noise sources. Aircraft noise, with its distinct temporal patterns and prolonged exposure duration, is a potent disruptor of sleep continuity.[11] Parallel findings reveal that occupational noise exposure similarly exacerbates sleep impairments.[12] Interestingly, certain noise types like white noise can paradoxically enhance sleep quality by masking disruptive sounds, suggesting a non-pharmacological intervention.[13]

However, prevailing studies predominantly focus on the linear relationship between noise and sleep outcomes, largely overlooking potential nonlinear effects. Major reviews and meta-analysis synthesizing evidence on noise and sleep explicitly note a substantial gap regarding the investigation of potential nonlinear relationships between noise exposure and sleep outcomes.[1014] Furthermore, while ambient environmental factors like air pollution and noise considerably impact sleep health,[15] few studies have examined the combined effects of multiple urban environmental factors. Another limitation lies in the assessment of noise exposure. Existing studies primarily relied on noise data from fixed monitoring stations or a single noise source, resulting in a lack of comprehensiveness in noise assessment. Advances in big data offer new avenues for the assessment of noise exposure. The Baidu Index, utilizing real-time population search behaviors as a proxy for perceived noise, has emerged as a novel tool for environmental health research. Internet search data, reflecting public information-seeking behaviors, have been proved to be correlated with health outcomes and hold significant potential for epidemiological studies due to their real-time nature and massive sample size.[1617] Critically, by integrating the dimension of human perception and annoyance, which is often absent in pure acoustic data, the Baidu Noise Index provides a scientifically valid complement to traditional metrics.[1819]

To address these limitations, this study investigated the non-linear relationship between environmental noise (using the Baidu Noise Index) and sleep duration (including both nighttime sleep duration and daytime nap duration). Furthermore, it examined the moderating roles of green space coverage and city population in this relationship. By integrating multi-sourced data and employing quantitative analyses, the research aims to elucidate the complex interplay between noise, urban factors, and sleep. The findings are expected to provide theoretical support for urban planning and health policy, particularly in optimizing urban sound environments and green space development to foster healthier urban spaces.

METHODS

Data Sources

Multi-sourced data were obtained to offer empirical evidence. Individual-level data were derived from three waves (2013, 2015, and 2018) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a comprehensive national survey covering respondents from 28 provinces, 150 districts, 450 communities, and 12,400 households across China. Recognized for its high quality and broad coverage, it has been widely applied in multitudinous studies.[20] The ethical approval was granted by Peking University. Noise exposure was measured by the Baidu Index. The National Bureau of Statistics of China collects a wide range of data covering the economy, population, society, and environment. Population data and the information on urban green space coverage were both extracted from it. Individual records from CHARLS were matched with city-level noise data based on community ID. The initial sample consisted of 28,926 observations. After excluding 229 records with missing values in sleep duration or covariates, the final analytical sample comprised 28,697 individual-year observations from 125 cities. Sampling weights provided by CHARLS were not applied in this study.

Variables

Dependent Variable

The restorative effects of sleep are influenced by various factors, including its quality, structure, and daily timing. While multi-faceted metrics exist, the duration of sleep or time spent in bed remains a fundamental measure.[21] Data for sleep duration used in our analysis were obtained from the lifestyle and health behavior section of the CHARLS questionnaire, which offers the data on sleep duration at night and nap duration during the day. The data are derived from the participants’ responses to the following two questions. One is “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night),” and the other is “During the past month, how long did you take a nap after lunch?” We calculated a 24-hour total sleep duration by summing these two self-reported measures.[22] Notably, nap duration during the day refers to the total hours that participants have slept after lunch each day. Although based on self-reported data, its reliability has been validated in the field of sleep epidemiology.[23]

Independent Variable

The core explanatory variable was noise, assessed through public attention to urban noise issues. The study leveraged the unique capacity of the Baidu Index to capture genuine public perception by analyzing online search big data to construct an evaluation system. Based on the Baidu Index platform, we collected search frequency data for the representative Chinese terms for “noise” across PC and mobile channels for 125 major Chinese cities (2013, 2015, and 2018). Daily data for each city were aggregated into an annual city-level noise attention index. This social perception method, capturing spontaneous public search behavior, provides an innovative proxy for urban environmental noise. Related research has supported the effectiveness of such data for social perception studies.[24] To mitigate dimensional differences, we standardized the core explanatory variable (Baidu Noise Index) before squaring it and conducting further analysis.

Moderating Variables

To further explore the relationship between noise and sleep duration, we incorporated two moderating variables as follows: city size and green space. City size was measured by the total population, referring to the total number of individuals who permanently reside in a particular geographic zone.[25] The assessment of green space relied on the green coverage rate in urbanized regions, which was determined by the proportion of urban built-up areas covered by greenery to the total built-up area,[26] including transportation routes, open plazas, and constructed facilities.

Control Variables

Several potential confounding factors, including age, gender, residence, chronic diseases, and assets, were controlled for in the analysis. Age referred to the actual age of the respondents, which was based on participants’ self-reported information. Both gender and residence were classified into two categories for analysis, with 0 representing female or rural residents and 1 representing male or urban residents. Chronic diseases represented the total count of chronic diseases of the participants, which was based on their self-reports. It can reflect the health situation of the residents and further influence their sleep. Assets reflected the current personal economic status, which were collected through face-to-face interviews about the total value of crash and financial products held by the respondents.[27]

Statistical Modeling

The Hausman test between the fixed effects (FE) model and the random effects (RE) model was firstly conducted to test whether individual heterogeneity is related to explanatory variables. The result (P < 0.001) significantly rejected the null hypothesis, indicating a violation of the RE model’s assumption that individual effects are uncorrelated with the regressors. The violation of the RE model’s assumption supported the adoption of the FE model. To account for temporal trends (such as changes in urban noise policies) across the study period (2013–2018), we incorporated year-FE into the final FE model specification.

The relationship between the noise index and sleep duration was examined through a series of nested regression models. The baseline model was progressively extended by incorporating first the linear term of the noise index, then its quadratic term, and finally the moderating variables. The hypothesized inverted U-shaped relationship between noise exposure and sleep duration was formally tested and validated using a U-test, and the significance of the moderating effects of green space and city size was assessed using t-tests. All statistical analyses, including the generation of tables and figures, were performed using Stata 19.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Model 1 established the reference specification by exclusively integrating sociodemographic covariates. With the introduction of the noise index in Model 2, a positive association between noise index and sleep duration was observed (β = 0.1663, P < 0.05), indicating that sleep duration initially increased with higher noise levels.

Model 3 incorporated the mean-centered quadratic term of the noise index. The results revealed statistically significant coefficients for both linear (β = 0.746, P < 0.001) and quadratic terms (β = −0.955, P < 0.001). The positive sign of the linear term and the negative sign of the quadratic term are consistent with an inverted U-shaped relationship, indicating that sleep duration initially increases with rising noise levels but eventually decreases after a certain point. To formally test for the inverted U-shape, we conducted a U-test following the method of Lind and Mehlum.[28] As delineated in Table 4, a significant inverted U-shaped relationship was observed between the variables within the noise index interval [1]. The slope at the lower bound was 0.746 (t = 3.851, P < 0.01), while at the upper bound it was −1.164 (t = −2.759, P < 0.01), respectively. The slopes at both ends were statistically significant at the 1% level with opposing signs, satisfying both the necessary and sufficient conditions for establishing an inverted U-shaped relationship. The inflection point was estimated at 0.391 (95% CI: [0.320, 0.566]. Specifically, when noise levels fell within an optimal range, sleep duration demonstrated a positive correlation with noise index. However, exceeding this critical threshold triggers a reverse transition in sleep duration with continued elevation of noise levels, as evidenced by the visualization in Figure 1.

The Moderating Effects of City Size and Green Space

The size of the urban population plays a role in moderating noise adaptation. Larger cities typically offer a greater diversity of social activities, which have been associated with positive mental health outcomes,[29] thus potentially buffering the impact of noise on individual sleep. Additionally, green space, as an important component of the urban environment, can mitigate noise disturbance through biophilic soundscape effects, thereby contributing to a more livable environment for residents. Previous research indicates that green spaces can effectively alleviate the adverse health impacts of noise pollution, with particular benefits for sleep quality.[30] To verify the moderating effects of city size and green space, the study respectively introduced two variables to Model 3. The analytical outcomes are summarized in Table 3 (Models 4 and 5). In Model 4, we observed that both the linear (β = 1.723, P < 0.001) and the quadratic noise components (β = −2.340, P < 0.001) exhibited significant correlations with sleep duration. More importantly, the interaction term between city size and the quadratic noise attained statistical significance (β = 1.072, P < 0.001), which exceeds 62% of the noise’s primary linear effect and represents 46% of the core quadratic noise effect, further confirming the existence of the moderating effect. The positive sign of this interaction coefficient implied an attenuation effect of urban dimensions on the curvilinear noise–sleep association. A parallel analytical approach was applied to green space in Model 5, which similarly confirmed its moderating effect, thereby consolidating evidence for the role of these environmental and urban-scale moderators.

To intuitively illustrate these interactions, the relationships are visualized in Figures 2 and 3. First, based on city population data as a measure of city size, cities were dichotomized through ±1 standard deviation partitioning: large cities (Mean +1 SD) and small cities (Mean −1 SD). The relationships between sleep duration and noise for both groups are illustrated in Figure 2, where both curves exhibit an inverted U-shaped pattern. As shown in Figure 2, both city types exhibit an inverted U-shaped relationship between noise and sleep duration. However, the curve for larger cities displays a reduced concavity compared to that of smaller cities, indicating a weaker influence of noise on sleep duration in more populous urban settings. This differential pattern suggests that residents of larger cities are less susceptible to noise-induced sleep changes. One possible explanation for this attenuated effect could be that the vibrant and dynamic social environment of larger cities can help reduce feelings of loneliness, thereby improving mental well-being and lowering psychological distress.[31] Existing studies have pointed out that daily activity can positively influence the sleep quality.[32] Neighborhood conditions, such as safety and walkability, have also been proved to have benefits on sleep.[15] Large cities tend to have more various daily activities and more ordered neighborhood with more comprehensive infrastructure, which contributes to the mental health of the residents,[33] thus mitigating the noise interference with sleep. Additionally, residents in large cities may develop a higher tolerance for noise due to prolonged exposure, making them less sensitive to its impact on sleep. Furthermore, better infrastructure, such as soundproofing in buildings, advanced urban planning, and stricter noise regulations, may also help reduce sleep disruption.

Moreover, access to green space encourages nature engagement, which is linked to improved mental well-being and more resilient sleep patterns amid noise exposure.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the complex relationship between environmental noise and sleep duration among middle-aged and older Chinese adults, with a specific focus on the moderating roles of urban characteristics. By integrating macro-level data from the Baidu Noise Index with micro-level individual data from the nationally representative CHARLS survey, our analysis yielded several key findings.

The primary finding revealed a significant inverted U-shaped association between noise exposure and sleep duration. This non-linear pattern suggests a dual-pathway mechanism: both excessively quiet (hypo-acoustic) and excessively loud (hyper-acoustic) environments may be detrimental to sleep integrity.

Contributions

The study offers three key contributions to the existing literature. First, it introduced a methodological innovation in noise exposure assessment by utilizing Baidu Index data. This approach leveraged search behavior patterns from a large digital user cohort, providing an indirect measure of noise issues with advantages in spatial coverage, temporal span, and update frequency over traditional monitoring. Crucially, Baidu Noise Index integrated objective noise indicators with public subjective perception,[19] enabling a more holistic understanding of noise pollution’s impact on resident well-being.

Second, the analysis of 28,657 observations empirically verified a nonlinear, inverted U-shaped relationship between the Baidu noise index and sleep duration. This finding demonstrated that moderate environmental noise levels correlate with optimal sleep maintenance, offering new theoretical support for optimizing urban soundscapes.

Third, the research identified green space coverage and city size as significant contextual moderators of the noise–sleep relationship. Larger urban populations were associated with greater acoustic adaptability, potentially buffering noise impacts. Green space mitigates adverse noise effects through bio-acoustic regulation (reducing traffic noise, providing natural masking sounds) and creating acoustically buffered environments.[30] These findings underscored the importance of integrating green infrastructure in urban planning.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. Primarily, the investigation’s scope was confined to Chinese individuals aged 45 years and older, potentially restricting the applicability of the results to younger demographics or diverse ethnic populations. The CHARLS sample data also exhibited an imbalance at the city level, leading to considerable disparities in participant distribution across cities. The analysis also did not explicitly examine potential differences in the noise–sleep relationship between urban and rural residents.

Second, while the Baidu Index provides extensive temporal and spatial coverage, it cannot serve as an entirely objective measure of urban noise levels. Meanwhile, more concrete forms of noise, such as occupational noise exposure and household noise levels, were not taken into consideration in our study due to data unavailability. Our analysis also did not account for other environmental factors known to co-vary with noise pollution and influence sleep, like air pollution and light pollution.

Third, the use of objective tools, such as accelerometers, is often impractical in large-scale studies. Although empirical evidence has shown reasonable correlation between subjective sleep reports and instrument-based measurements, supporting the reliability of self-reported data in this context,[34] the reliance on self-reported sleep data would introduce potential subjectivity in measurement.[35] Similarly, the macro-level measures of green space may not accurately reflect individual accessibility or exposure. Future research would benefit from incorporating device-based sleep tracking and more precise, individual-level environmental exposure assessments.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the relationship between community noise and sleep duration among middle-aged and older adults is non-linear and context-dependent. The identified inverted U-shaped relationship is moderated by city size and green space, revealing the complex interplay between the acoustic environment and urban fabric. These findings underscore the importance of creating moderately quiet environments with appropriate background soundscapes and expanding green spaces to improve sleep health. Effective public health and urban planning strategies should aim not merely for noise abatement but for the creation of optimized soundscapes. Prioritizing the expansion of urban green infrastructure and fostering moderately stimulating acoustic environments emerge as key avenues for enhancing residents’ sleep health and overall well-being.
