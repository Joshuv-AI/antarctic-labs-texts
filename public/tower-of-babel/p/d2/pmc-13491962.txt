Comparative evaluation of tigecycline susceptibility testing methods for carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Yu Shi, Shaozeng Li, Lijing He, Haibin Wang, Yupeng Shang, Kang Zhang, Xinwei Han, Yuan Wei, Wanli Chu, Yulin Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn this study, we aimed to evaluate the performance of different tigecycline susceptibility testing methods for CRKP and CRAB using the broth microdilution (BMD) method as the gold standard, under different breakpoint criteria.MethodsA total of 53 CRKP and 47 CRAB clinical isolates, collected from December 2021 to January 2023, were tested for tigecycline susceptibility using six clinically common methods: Vitek2 (using the Vitek2 Compact system), the Kirby–Bauer (KB) disk diffusion susceptibility test, the modified KB (mKB) test with a buffer solution, the Epsilometer test (E-test), minimum inhibitory concentration test strips (MTS) and BMD (as the reference standard). The susceptibility test results were interpreted based on the standards issued by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing(EUCAST), the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and the British Society for Antimicrobial Chemotherapy(BSAC). The performance of each method was assessed through essential agreement (EA), categorical agreement (CA), error rates and Kappa (κ) values.ResultsIn terms of methodology, the mKB significantly increased the inhibition zone diameter compared with the KB for both CRKP (3.2 ± 0.8 mm) and CRAB (3.6 ± 0.8 mm) (P < 0.05, respectively). Using BMD as the gold standard and following FDA criteria, the mKB demonstrated an accuracy rate significantly higher than the KB in interpreting CRKP susceptibility results (62.3% vs. 1.9%, P < 0.05). The agreement rate between the E-test and the MTS for CRKP was as high as 84.9%, indicating no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). However, the E-test yielded significantly higher accuracy than the mKB (98.1% vs. 60.4%, P < 0.05). For CRAB, the accuracy of the E-test was lower than that for CRKP (68.1% vs. 98.1%). When compared with BMD, the E-test had a high EA value of 92.5% for CRKP and a lower EA value of 68.1% for CRAB. Under the FDA standard for CRKP, the E-test yielded a CA value of 98.1%, with very major error (VME) and major error (ME) rates of 0%, a minor error (mE) rate of 1.9% and a κ-value of 0.658, indicating substantial agreement. In contrast, when applied to CRAB, the performance of the E-test declined markedly, with a CA value of only 68.1%, VME and ME rates still at 0%, but a much higher mE rate of 31.9% and a κ-value of just 0.082, indicating poor agreement.ConclusionThe mKB improves the detection performance of the KB, suggesting that pH variation is one of the factors affecting the accuracy of KB-test-based TST. Among the five clinical verification methods included in this study, the E-test demonstrates the best overall performance under all interpretive criteria, making it more suitable for confirming the tigecycline susceptibility of CRKP. However, this method appears less reliable for CRAB, indicating the significant impact of interspecies differences on TST performance. Additionally, the verification methods aligned better with the FDA interpretive criteria. Future research should focus on methodological optimisation based on various influencing factors and on refining interpretive breakpoints specific to each method, which will be key to improving TST accuracy.

Background

In the World Health Organization’s global priority list of antibiotic-resistant bacteria (WPAP), carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) and Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) are classified as pathogens of critical priority based on criteria such as incidence, mortality and treatment difficulty. Their multidrug-resistant (MDR) characteristics pose significant challenges in clinical treatment and are associated with markedly increased mortality in bloodstream infections (BSIs) compared with infections caused by non-resistant strains [1]. Laura C. Antochevis et al. reported that ward-acquired pneumonia accounted for 38.8% of all isolated strains, with carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) being the most prevalent (19.3%). Notably, CRKP was found to be the most frequently detected pathogen among the CRE strains, responsible for 14.2% of all BSIs, followed by CRAB, which accounted for 9.6% [2]. A similar upward trend has been observed across various provinces in China. As reported by Lijun Yin et al., from 2017 to 2021, a total of 5,078 CR-GNB-positive cases were identified at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University. Specifically, CRE accounted for 58.1% (2,590 cases) of all cases, with CRKP being the most common (74.1%, 2,186 cases), followed by CRAB (26.6%, 1,350 cases) [3].According to data from the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET), between 2005 and 2025, the isolation rate of Klebsiella pneumoniae increased from 14% to 19%, while the isolation rate of Acinetobacter baumannii decreased from 13.2% to 10.4%. The resistance rates to imipenem and meropenem in Klebsiella pneumoniae rose from 3% to 2.9% to 24.6% and 25.5%, respectively, whereas in Acinetobacter baumannii, the resistance rates increased from 31% to 39% to 76.5% and 76.4%, respectively.In our hospital in 2025, pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing results showed that 171 CRKP isolates were detected, accounting for 15.4% of all Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates (n = 1110), and 172 CRAB isolates were detected, accounting for 29.7% of all Acinetobacter baumannii isolates (n = 580). In a 10-year study, Yili Chen et al. reported that among 29 patients diagnosed with CRKP bloodstream infection (BSI), the 28-day mortality rate was 48.3%, which was significantly higher than that in the carbapenem-susceptible Klebsiella pneumoniae (CSKP) group (5.83%) [4]. Leyla Ferliçolak et al. followed 57 ICU patients with confirmed CRAB infection and found a 30-day mortality rate as high as 56.1% [5].

Tigecycline, a broad-spectrum glycylcycline antibiotic, is one of the key therapeutic agents used to treat such infections. The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST), the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and the British Society for Antimicrobial Chemotherapy (BSAC) have each established interpretive breakpoints for tigecycline susceptibility (defined in terms of inhibition zone diameter [IZD, mm] and minimum inhibitory concentration [MIC, mg/l]) for a range of bacterial groups, including Enterobacteriaceae, Staphylococcus spp., Enterococcus spp., groups A/B/C/G Streptococcus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Clostridioides difficile, Acinetobacter spp. and Pseudomonas spp. In contrast, the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) has not yet provided corresponding interpretive criteria for tigecycline, as indicated in CLSI M100-34. Broth microdilution method (BMD) is the gold standard but given its complexity, it is rarely used in clinical laboratories. Other susceptibility testing methods are more commonly applied in clinical settings. Notably, there are significant discrepancies among different tigecycline susceptibility testing (TST) methods such as automated systems, disk diffusion, gradient strip diffusion and BMD, particularly when applied to CRKP and CRAB. Studies have suggested that factors such as the composition and pH of the culture medium, species-specific characteristics and methodological differences may affect the accuracy of susceptibility interpretation. This study evaluated and compared the performance of BMD with five common clinical TST methods for CRKP and CRAB to provide practical guidance for clinical laboratories in selecting reliable methods.

Materials and methods

Sources of bacterial isolates

Clinical isolates of CRKP (n = 53) and CRAB (n = 47) were collected from the Fourth Medical Center of the People’s Liberation Army General Hospital(No. 51 Fucheng Road, Fengtai District, Beijing) between 8 December 2021 and 20 January 2023. This study has been approved by Institutional Review Board(IRB )and other ethics research institutes in the local hospital.In the CRKP group, specimens from patients with pneumonia accounted for approximately 66% (35/53), those from patients with trauma or extensive burns accounted for approximately 21% (11/53), those from patients with urinary tract infections accounted for approximately 11% (6/53), and those from patients with bloodstream infection (BSI) accounted for approximately 2% (1/53).In the CRAB group, specimens from patients with pneumonia accounted for approximately 83% (39/47), while those from patients with trauma or extensive burns accounted for approximately 17% (8/47).All isolates exhibited intermediate or resistant results to tigecycline based on initial automated system testing. Escherichia coli (E. coli) ATCC 25,922 was used as the quality control strain for susceptibility testing.

Instruments and reagents

Bacterial identification was performed using the VITEK-MS (bioMérieux, France) automated microbial identification spectrometry system. For bacterial susceptibility testing, the DensiCHEK Plus nephelometer (bioMérieux, France) was used. The automated susceptibility testing was conducted using the Vitek2 Compact 60 (bioMérieux, France) automated microbial identification and susceptibility testing system, with N334 cards for CRKP and N335 cards for CRAB, respectively. All instruments and reagents for Vitek2 were sourced from bioMérieux, France. Tigecycline susceptibility disks (15 µg/disk) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The tigecycline buffer solution used in the modified Kirby–Bauer (mKB) disk diffusion susceptibility test was provided by Shanghai Yuanke Industrial Development Co., Ltd. Tigecycline strips for the Epsilometer test (E-test) were supplied by Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, China), while minimum inhibitory concentration test strips (MTS) were obtained from Liofilchem (Italy). Reagents and inoculation media for BMD were supplied by Wenzhou Bio-Kont Co. Ltd. Mueller–Hinton (MH) agar plates were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The concentration range was 0.016–256 µg/ml for the E-test and the MTS method and 0.032–32 µg/ml for BMD.

Methods and requirements

All procedures were performed in accordance with the CLSI guidelines. For the quality control strain E. coli ATCC 25,922, test results were considered valid if the IZD for the 15 µg tigecycline disk fell within 20–27 mm and the MIC was within the range of 0.03–0.25 µg/ml. Manual susceptibility testing was carried out independently by experienced researchers in the microbiology laboratory at different time points. Each test used freshly prepared culture media and susceptibility reagents from recent batches. To minimise the potential for human error, if inconsistent results were obtained for the same isolate using the same method, the test was repeated until consistent results were achieved.

Vitek2

The Vitek2 Compact 60 automated microbial identification and susceptibility testing system was employed, with N334 and N335 antibiotic susceptibility cards for CRKP and CRAB, respectively. The tests were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The MIC values were automatically generated and interpreted by the built-in software and the Advanced Expert System.

KB and mKB test

A bacterial suspension was prepared and adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard. A sterile cotton swab was dipped into the suspension and gently pressed against the inner wall of the test tube above the liquid level to remove excess fluid. The swab was then used to evenly inoculate the surface of an MH agar plate by streaking in three directions, rotating the plate 60° between each streak, followed by a final sweep around the inner edge of the plate to ensure uniform coverage. The inoculated plate was left at room temperature for 3–5 min to allow the surface to dry. Two tigecycline disks (15 µg each) were placed on opposite sides of the agar plate, with a centre-to-centre distance of > 24 mm and a minimum distance of > 15 mm from the plate edge. Each disk was gently pressed to ensure firm contact without further repositioning. For the mKB test, 6 µl of tigecycline buffer solution was added directly onto each disk. Plates were incubated at (35 °C ± 2 °C) for 16–20 h. Following incubation, the IZD value was determined using a Vernier calliper against a dark background. The inhibition zone was defined as the area with no visible bacterial growth, and this measurement was recorded as the test result.

E-test

Bacterial suspension preparation and agar plate inoculation were performed as described above. After the inoculated plate had dried, the E-test strip at the labelled end was grasped using a pair of sterile forceps. The strip, with the scale facing upward, was carefully placed onto the agar surface and gently pressed from the low-concentration end to the high-concentration end to remove air bubbles and ensure full contact with the agar. Once placed, the strip was not moved. The plate was incubated at (35 °C ± 2 °C) for 16–20 h. Results were interpreted by reading the MIC value at the point where the elliptical zone of inhibition intersected the strip at approximately 80% inhibition. This was determined visually by identifying the point of complete growth inhibition, disregarding any trailing growth or small colonies that might extend beyond the obvious zone edge.

MTS

The MTS method followed the same operational steps and interpretation criteria as the E-test.

BMD

A bacterial suspension was adjusted to the standard 0.5 MFU turbidity. Then, 10 µl of the suspension was added to and thoroughly mixed with 2 ml of antibiotic inoculation broth (bacterial suspension-to-broth ratio of 1:200). A volume of 100 µl of the prepared inoculum was added to each well of the antibiotic panel plate, with tigecycline concentrations ranging from 0.032 to 32 µg/ml. Incubation conditions were.

the same as described in the E-test section. The MIC values were interpreted by examining the wells from the lowest to the highest concentration, and the MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration at which no visible bacterial growth was observed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the software SPSS 29.0, and statistical plots were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.4. Categorical data are expressed as a frequency or percentage. Paired t-tests and paired chi-square (χ²) tests were used for intergroup comparisons, with a P-value of less than 0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference. Agreement between each TST method and BMD was assessed using Kappa (κ) statistics, calculated using MedCalc 22.0, where a κ-value of 0.8–1.0 was interpreted as very strong agreement, 0.6–0.8 as strong agreement, 0.4–0.6 as moderate agreement, 0.2–0.4 as weak agreement and < 0.2 very weak agreement.

Interpretation guidelines

As shown in Table 1, the current interpretive criteria from the FDA for tigecycline susceptibility are as follows: (1) for CRKP, 14 mm < IZD < 19 mm in the KB test and an MIC value of either 3 µg/ml or 4 µg/ml are interpreted as intermediate and used for classification; (2) for CRAB, an MIC of 3 µg/ml, 4 µg/ml or 6 µg/ml is defined as intermediate and used for classification. According to the BSAC criteria, 19 mm < IZD < 24 mm and an MIC value of 1.5 µg/ml or 2 µg/ml are considered intermediate for CRKP, while for CRAB, an MIC value of 1.5 µg/ml or 2 µg/ml is interpreted as intermediate and used for categorisation. The following criteria were used to evaluate the performance of TST methods. (1) Essential agreement (EA): defined as the MIC values from the test method differing by no more than ± 1 doubling dilution from those of the reference or comparative method. (2) Categorical agreement (CA): defined as the concordance in classification between the test method and the reference or comparative method, including the categories ‘susceptible’, ‘intermediate’, ‘dose-dependent susceptible’ and ‘resistant’. (3) Minor error (mE): occurs when the test method interprets an isolate as intermediate, while the reference method reports it as susceptible or resistant, or vice versa. (4) Major error (ME): occurs when the test method reports an isolate as resistant, while the reference method reports it as susceptible, i.e. false resistance. (5) Very major error (VME): occurs when the test method reports an isolate as susceptible, while the reference method reports it as resistant, i.e. false susceptibility. According to the performance criteria recommended by the CLSI, the acceptable limits for these indicators are EA ≥ 90%, CA ≥ 90%, VME ≤ 1.5%, ME ≤ 3% and mE ≤ 10%.

Results

TST results by Vitek2, E-test, MTS method and BMD

Tigecycline susceptibility of the CRKP and CRAB isolates was evaluated using the Vitek2 Compact microbiology analyser (Vitek2), E-test, MTS and BMD. Results were interpreted based on EUCAST, FDA and BSAC criteria, with the corresponding MIC₅₀ and MIC₉₀ values shown in Table 2. According to the BMD results, for CRKP, the highest tigecycline susceptibility rate was observed under the FDA criteria (100%), followed by BSAC criteria(77.4%), and was the lowest under EUCAST criteria (47.2%). In the CRKP group, under the FDA criteria, the susceptibility rates of the E-test and MTS were 98.1% and 86.8%, respectively, which were close to those of the BMD method, whereas the susceptibility rate of Vitek 2 was 0%, showing a marked discrepancy from BMD.Under the BSAC criteria, the susceptibility rate of the E-test was 84.9%, which was close to that of BMD (77.4%), while the susceptibility rates of MTS and Vitek 2 were 52.8% and 0%, respectively, both showing substantial differences from BMD.Under the EUCAST criteria, the susceptibility rate of the E-test was 39.6%, which was close to that of BMD (47.2%), whereas both MTS and Vitek 2 showed susceptibility rates of 0%, which differed considerably from BMD.In the CRAB group, under both FDA and BSAC criteria, the susceptibility rates of all three methods showed marked discrepancies compared with BMD. Comparison of IZD and MIC values via paired t-tests

CRKP group

The normality of the difference values between each pair of methods was assessed via Q-Q plot analysis and approximated a normal distribution. The statistical analysis revealed that the mKB produced significantly larger IZDs compared with the KB, with an average increase of 3.2 ± 0.8 mm (P < 0.05); MIC values from the MTS method were significantly higher than those from the E-test, with an average increase of 0.72 ± 0.85 µg/ml (P < 0.05); MIC values from BMD were slightly higher than those from the E-test, with a mean difference of 0.16 ± 0.63 µg/ml, lacking statistical significance (P = 0.074 > 0.05). Compared with the MTS, BMD yielded significantly lower MIC values, with an average decrease of − 0.57 ± 0.77 µg/ml (P < 0.05).

These findings indicate that the addition of a tigecycline buffer solution—used to stabilise the pH of the testing environment—enhanced the accuracy of the KB. The differences in MIC values between the MTS and E-test suggest that variations in manufacturing processes and matrix materials between different brands can influence susceptibility testing results. While the MIC values from the BMD and E-test were largely consistent, the MTS tended to yield higher MICs than BMD, indicating a measurable discrepancy between these methods. See Table 3; Fig. 1.

EUCAST criteria

CRKP group

Using the EUCAST criteria, the agreement rate was 28.3% between the mKB and KB, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05), 60.4% between the E-test and MTS, suggesting no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) and 60.4% between the mKB test and E-test, indicating a difference lacking statistical significance (P > 0.05).

In the CRKP group, susceptibility or resistance was determined for each method according to the EUCAST breakpoint criteria. Using BMD as the gold standard, results consistent with BMD were defined as correct, while those inconsistent with BMD were defined as incorrect.A total of 53 isolates were included in the CRKP group. The KB method yielded 32 correct results, corresponding to an accuracy of 60.4%, whereas the mKB method yielded 36 correct results, corresponding to an accuracy of 67.9%.

According to the EUCAST criteria for the CRKP group, compared with BMD, the Vitek2 showed a high ME rate of 47.2%, indicating a tendency for false susceptibility results. Therefore, resistant findings should be confirmed by further verification. Comparing the KB and mKB, the KB exhibited a higher ME rate (39.6% vs. 0%), whereas the mKB showed a higher VME rate (32.1% vs. 0%). This suggests that both methods are unsuitable for confirmatory testing and may mislead clinical decision-making. The E-test and MTS both demonstrated relatively high EA values, with the E-test being higher (92.5% vs. 79.2%). Likewise, CA values were higher for the E-test (69.8% vs. 52.8%). These results all indicate the E-test as being the most reliable among the five test methods. However, the E-test still exhibited a VME of 11.3% and an ME of 18.9%, both exceeding the acceptable thresholds defined by the CLSI guidelines, rendering it unsuitable for confirmatory susceptibility testing. Notably, the MTS showed an ME rate as high as 47.2%, underscoring its lack of reliability in this context.

According to the FDA criteria for the CRKP group, when compared with BMD, the Vitek2 exhibited an ME rate of 47.2% and an mE rate of 52.8%, indicating remarkable discrepancies in classifying intermediate and resistant isolates. Therefore, both intermediate and resistant results require confirmatory testing. The mKB significantly improved the performance of the KB, as it reduced the ME rate from 17.0% to 0% and the mE rate from 83.0% to 39.6% and increased the CA from 0% to 60.4%. These findings demonstrate that the mKB markedly enhanced the interpretive accuracy of the KB. However, the persistently high mE rate suggests the presence of other influencing factors beyond pH changes in the medium. Both the E-test and MTS displayed high EA and CA, with 0% ME and VME rates, and mE rates of 1.9% and 13.2%, respectively. These results are within or close to the CLSI criteria, indicating that both methods are reliable alternatives to the gold-standard BMD. Considering the lower price of the E-test strips, the E-test offers better cost-effectiveness. For the CRAB group, the comparison between the Vitek2 method and BMD yielded results consistent with those in the CRKP group. However, both the E-test and MTS showed lower EA and CA values compared with those in the CRKP group. Notably, these two methods had high mE rates reaching 31.9%, primarily because susceptible isolates were misclassified as intermediate. Overall, the E-test and MTS are more suitable for confirming the presence of CRKP rather than CRAB isolates.

According to the BSAC criteria for the CRKP group, when compared with BMD, the Vitek2 showed an ME rate of 77.4%, indicating a high likelihood of false-resistant results; thus, resistance results require confirmation. Although the mKB reduced the ME rate from 77.4% to 34.0%, the mE rate increased from 22.6% to 62.3%, indicating that neither the KB nor the mKB is suitable for verification under these criteria. The CA values for the E-test and MTS were 77.4% and 71.7%, respectively. The E-test met the CLSI criteria for VME (0%) and ME (1.9%) but yielded a relatively high mE rate (20.8%). The MTS also met the VME criterion (0%) but had a higher ME rate (9.4%) and mE rate (18.9%). Therefore, the E-test is superior under the BSAC criteria. For the CRAB group, comparisons between the Vitek2, KB and mKB vs. BMD produced results generally consistent with those observed in the CRKP group. However, for both the E-test and MTS, the CA values were markedly lower than those in the CRKP group (48.9% vs. 77.4% for the E-test, 12.8% vs. 71.7% for the MTS). The ME rates were 19.1% and 17.0%, while the mE rates reached 31.9% and 70.2%, respectively. These findings indicate neither method as being suitable for verifying CRAB isolates under the BSAC criteria.

The analysis of κ values revealed that when used to verify CRKP under the FDA criteria, only the E-test demonstrated a relatively strong level of agreement (κ = 0.658). For all other methods and criteria, the consistency with BMD was suboptimal. See Table 10.

Discussion

Both CRKP and CRAB have emerged as common nosocomial pathogens that are increasingly difficult to treat. Studies have shown that compared with susceptible strains, drug-resistant Acinetobacter spp. tend to form enhanced biofilm [6]. In MDR A. baumannii, biofilm-associated genes such as csuE, epsA, bap, ompA, blaPER-1 and bfmS are extensively present, implying an exceptional biofilm-forming capacity [7]. Bloodstream infections caused by these pathogens are particularly severe. According to a study by Haitao Zhao et al.calculated the BSI-related mortality rates within 28 or 30 days, CRAB-induced infections yielded a mortality rate exceeding 50%, ranking top three among all drug-resistant pathogens in terms of mortality. The mortality rate for CRKP-related BSIs was also significant at approximately 40%. Multilocus sequence-typing analysis revealed that CRAB strains were predominantly of the ST2 type, most of which carried the blaOXA-23 gene, while CRKP strains were mainly of the ST11 type, with the majority harbouring the blaKPC-2 gene [8]. According to the clinical practice guidelines issued by the Infectious Diseases Society of America in 2022, tigecycline is listed among the recommended treatment options for both CRE and CRAB, and corresponding guidance on its clinical use is provided [9]. However, ,according to Dongmei Lv et al., low-dose tigecycline is insufficient to effectively treat bacterial infections; instead, it enhances bacterial tolerance to antibiotic selective pressure, thereby promoting the proliferation of dominant bacterial populations [10]. Vasudevan A et al. reported that the broad spectrum initial antibiotic treatment remains an important independent modifiable risk factor [11].Therefore, accurate laboratory reporting of TST results is crucial for guiding appropriate clinical therapy.Vasudevan A et al.also reported that the economic burden of RGNB(resistant gram negative bacilli) infections to the patients and the hospital is considerable. Efforts need to be taken to prevent their occurrence by cost effective infection control practices [12].Vasudevan A et al.also reported that GSDCS(Gram Negative bacteria in last 6 months, Surgery during current admission before RGNB, prior Dialysis with end stage renal disease, prior use of Carbapenem within last 6 months, Stay in the ICU for more than 5 days)is a simple bedside clinical score which will help clinicians to choose appropriate, timely targeted antibiotic therapy and avoid exposure to unnecessary treatment for patients at low risk of nosocomial RGNB infection. This will reduce the selection pressure and help to contain antibiotic resistance in ICUs [13].

This study adopted the three most widely used international TST interpretive criteria issued by the FDA, BSAC and EUCAST. Among the five frequently used clinical verification methods evaluated in this study, the E-test demonstrated relatively superior performance. For CRKP, CA was 98.1% under the FDA criteria, 77.4% under the BSAC criteria, and 69.8% under the EUCAST criteria. Specifically, under the EUCAST criteria, the VME rate reached 11.3% and the ME rate was 18.9%, both relatively high. Under the BSAC criteria, the mE rate was as high as 20.8%. In contrast, the FDA criteria produced error rates that all fell within the acceptable limits defined by the CLSI, indicating that the FDA criteria are better suited for methodological validation studies. These findings are consistent with the study by Olympia Zarkotou et al., which concluded that the FDA tigecycline susceptibility breakpoint is more appropriate than that of EUCAST [14]. On this basis, our study also included a comparative evaluation using the BSAC criteria.

Among the five methods included in this study, the KB test represents the most cost-effective verification method in clinical practice, while domestically produced E-test strips are significantly less expensive than imported MTS. All of these factors should be taken into consideration as the selection of cost-effective, yet reliable methods is critical for clinical TST. The KB, E-test, and MTS are all manual testing approaches, which are inherently influenced by a range of complex factors. Peng Wang et al. reported that when testing CRAB using the E-test on MH agar plates produced by BD and Oxoid, a significant difference was observed in their susceptibility rates, with BD yielding a significantly higher susceptibility rate (71% vs. 29.9%). Both methods had an ME rate of 1.9%, while the mE rate was 63.6% for Oxoid, significantly higher than the 23.4% in the BD group [15]. This underscores the potential association between the composition of the culture medium and the accuracy of TST results. In the present study, the E-test performance for CRAB, when interpreted using the FDA criteria, showed lower ME (0% vs. 1.9%) and mE (31.9% vs. 63.6%) rates than previously reported, indicating a higher degree of reliability. Nevertheless, further investigation is needed to clarify the potential influence of medium composition on TST accuracy.

In this study, the KB test was modified by adding a buffer solution containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt and sodium hydroxide to stabilise the pH of the testing environment. Methodologically, this adjustment aimed to improve test accuracy. From the perspective of the testing results, the IZDs for CRKP and CRAB increased by 3.2 ± 0.8 mm and 3.6 ± 0.8 mm, respectively (both P < 0.05). For CRKP, the accuracy rate rose from 1.9% to 62.3%, marking a significant improvement under the FDA criteria. Using BMD as the reference, both the KB and mKB for CRKP yielded a VME rate of 0%. Moreover, the ME rate dropped from 17.0% to 0% following the modification, the mE rate decreased from 83.0% to 39.6%, and the CA rate increased from 0% to 60.4%,showcasing the optimising effect of the buffer solution on test performance.

Differences were found between the materials used for the E-test strip and MTS production, including a significant difference in costs. When evaluated under the FDA criteria for CRKP, the E-test achieved an accuracy rate of 98.1%, and the MTS method exhibited an accuracy rate of 86.8%, demonstrating no statistically significant difference. Using BMD as the reference, the EA, CA, VME, ME, and mE of the E-test for CRKP all meeting the CLSI criteria. Overall, the domestically produced E-test strips demonstrated the highest cost-effectiveness for CRKP susceptibility testing. As to CRAB, both the E-test and MTS method yielded an identical accuracy rate of 68.1%, with no statistically significant difference. When compared with BMD, .the EA, CA, and mE of the the E-test for CRAB didn’t meet the CLSI criteria. These findings suggest that the E-test is not suitable for CRAB verification. This aligns with the report by Stefano Grandesso et al., which also found that both Vitek2 and the E-test produced unreliable results when used for TST of MDR and extensively drug-resistant A. baumannii strains [16]. Therefore, interspecies differences appear to be a significant influencing factor for TST accuracy.

Genotypic classification based on resistance genes is highly valuable for understanding antibiotic resistance patterns. Min Yi Lau et al. reported that clinical isolates collected from 2019 to 2020 at the University Malaya Medical Centre revealed that blaOXA-23 and blaOXA-51 were predominantly in CRAB, while in CRKP, the dominant resistance gene was blaNDM, followed by blaOXA-48 [17], . Similarly, a study by Yun Wu et al. in 2021 analysed isolates collected from 26 hospitals across 19 cities in six regions of China. Among the 440 CRAB isolates, all expressed blaOXA-23-type genes and 11 strains (2.5%) also expressed blaNDM-type genes. In 242 CRKP isolates, the predominant genotype was blaKPC, accounting for 80.17%, followed by blaNDM at 10.74% [18]. These findings highlight the geographical and institutional variability in the distribution of resistance gene types in CRKP and CRAB strains. Notably, blaOXA-23 was the first OXA-type carbapenemase identified in CRAB and remains prevalent worldwide [19]. This gene has been strongly associated with outbreaks of CRAB in healthcare settings, particularly in intensive care units [20, 21]. According to a study by Sakiusa C. Baleivanualala et al., clinical specimens collected from 272 patients across three hospitals in Fiji yielded 131 (48.0%) CRAB and 6 (2.2%) CRKP isolates. Among the 131 CRAB strains,, with three distinct clones all encoding blaOXA-23, blaOXA-66 and blaNDM-1. Among the 6 CRKP isolates, the blaNDM-5 genotype was predominant.Of note, inter- and intra-hospital transmission of different clones was observed across the three hospitals [22]. As reported by Branka Bedenić et al. in the study of A. baumannii isolates collected between 2009 and 2014 from multiple hospitals in Croatia, using the EUCAST interpretive criteria and BMD for TST, OXA-23-positive, metallo-β-lactamase (MBL)-positive strains had a tigecycline resistance rate of 54.5%, while OXA-23-positive but MBL-negative strains showed a lower resistance rate of 29.4%. In contrast, strains positive for blaOXA-24/40 or blaOXA-58 were generally susceptible to tigecycline. When tested using the E-test, all OXA-23-positive, MBL-positive strains, 23.5% of OXA-23-positive but MBL-negative strains, and 4.1% of OXA-24/40-positive strains exhibited resistance to tigecycline. However, under the FDA interpretive criteria, all these isolates were classified as susceptible. Using BMD as the reference, 5 MBL-positive strains (45.5%) and 8 OXA-24/40-positive strains (10.7%) showed MEs with the E-test; the mE rates for isolates with OXA-24/40, MBL, OXA-58, OXA-23 and OXA-24/40 plus MBL-negative and carbapenemase-negative profiles were 58.7%, 45.5%, 33.3%, 29.4% and 27.0%, respectively [23]. According to these findings, we can conclude that the interpretation of TST results can vary significantly depending on the interpretive criteria used, and methodological error rates also differ across bacterial genotypes carrying different resistance determinants. Due to resource limitations, the current study had a modest sample size and did not include genotypic analysis of carbapenemase genes, which underscored a direction for future research.

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this study, the following conclusions and recommendations are drawn: (1) The mKB test can serve as a first-line verification method for TST at primary healthcare laboratories. (2) The E-test is a viable alternative to BMD in clinical laboratories, given its optimal performance and cost-effectiveness in verifying tigecycline susceptibility for CRKP when interpreted under the FDA criteria. (3) Considering that both interspecies variability and differences in interpretive breakpoints significantly affect TST results, in addition to methodological optimisation, it is equally crucial to scientifically adjust interpretive breakpoints and criteria so that clinicians can access accurate results and practical guidance. (4) The screening and classification of antibiotic resistance genes play a critical role in improving the precision of susceptibility testing, which represents a research direction in the field.
