Gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by tibial dyschondroplasia in turn accelerates disease pathogenesis through the gut-bone axis in broilers
Authors: Hualong Song, Jiawei Wang, Yongbo Hao, Xiaoran Li, Shijia Yan, Shaoyang Han, Jiahao Zhao, Yuchen Han, Shuo Sun, Xing Chen, Mhd Adanan Purba, Hui Chen, Chong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTibial dyschondroplasia (TD) is a common and economically significant skeletal disorder in broilers, characterized by unmineralized, avascular cartilage plugs protruding into the metaphyseal region. Despite some evidence connecting the gut microbiota to skeletal disorders, the specific microbial drivers of TD pathogenesis remain unclear. In this study, we performed fecal microbiota transplantation in both healthy and TD model broilers to assess the influence and contribution of gut microbiota dysbiosis to TD pathogenesis. The broilers were allocated into 4 groups: CON (normal control group broilers), TD (TD model broilers), TDRN (TD model broilers that received FMT from normal broilers) and NRTD (normal broilers that received FMT from TD model broilers). Results demonstrated that FMT successfully transferred the TD phenotype from diseased to healthy broilers (NRTD group), whereas transplantation from healthy donors did not reverse the TD phenotype in TD broilers (TDRN group). This to some extent indicates that gut microbiota as a critical pathogenic driver. Microbiome analysis revealed significant depletion of Lactobacillus and enrichment of Streptococcus and Escherichia-Shigella in all TD-affected groups (TD, TDRN, NRTD) compared to controls (P < 0.05). Metabolomic profiling identified seven stably dysregulated metabolites. Among them, chenodeoxycholic acid showed a strong positive correlation with Lactobacillus abundance and tibial mineral content, while 2-methoxyestradiol (an estrogen metabolite) exhibited inverse associations. Collectively, these findings provide evidence that gut microbiota dysbiosis causally contributes to TD and define the Lactobacillus-chenodeoxycholic acid axis and estrogen metabolism as promising targets for preventive and management strategies against TD in broilers.Keywords: Tibial chondrodysplasia (TD), Broilers, Fecal microbiota transplant, 2-Methoxyestradiol, Chenodeoxycholic acid

Introduction

Tibial dyschondroplasia (TD), first described in broilers in (Leach et al., 1965), represents a pervasive and economically consequential skeletal disorder characterized by defective tibial development that manifests as unmineralized cartilage plugs and lameness. Recent epidemiological data indicate an estimated incidence of approximately 1% in commercial broiler production (Liu et al., 2023b). The primary pathological manifestations include impaired vascularization of the proximal tibial growth plate, abnormal proliferation of chondrocytes, and failure of endochondral ossification, ultimately resulting in the formation of an opaque, avascular, and unmineralized cartilaginous masses (Nawaz et al., 2023). Similar cartilage bone abnormalities have been observed in multiple species, including pigs, horses, cattle, dogs, rabbits, rats, and even humans, where they are described as chondrodysplasia (Faienza et al., 2021; Engiles et al., 2022; Corbeau et al., 2024; Bendre et al., 2025). The complex etiologies render traditional single-target treatment strategies largely ineffective, which leads to significant economic losses to the poultry industry’s profitability and compromised animal welfare. Therefore, exploring a new treatment to effectively alleviate TD and elucidating its mechanism is a priority. After years of research, the techniques for TD models have become quite mature. Currently, feeding broilers with thiram can effectively induce the development of TD (Zhang et al., 2019; Amini et al., 2026), which facilitates research on this disease.

The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of skeletal development. Through multiple interconnected pathways, gut microbiota dysbiosis disrupts the processes of bone formation and remodeling, including impairment of the intestinal barrier (Wu et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2024), immune dysregulation (Moon et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024), metabolic disturbances (Zhang et al., 2022b; Liu et al., 2023a; Zhou et al., 2024b; Yu et al., 2025), compromised nutrient absorption and impaired utilization (Elmassry et al., 2020; Ibrahim et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024), and perturbed endocrine signaling (Guan et al., 2023). Through fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), the gut microbiota can be bidirectionally modulated; it can be used to transfer a dysbiotic community and recapitulate disease phenotypes, or to introduce a healthy consortium and restore physiological homeostasis (Xu et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025). Previous studies have extensively investigated the role of microorganisms in this disease, demonstrating that microbiota influence skeletal development (Zhang et al., 2022a, 2022c; Zhou et al., 2024a). However, significant gaps remain in our understanding of the mechanisms underlying TD pathogenesis and treatment.

Given the multifactorial nature of TD and the limited efficacy of conventional single-modality therapies, this study is intended to identify specific gut microbiota and differential metabolites that drive TD pathogenesis using an intervention-based screening approach. By integrating FMT with multi-omics profiling, we elucidate the microbial-metabolic gut-bone axis interactions in TD. The findings are expected to advance fundamental knowledge of skeletal-microbial crosstalk and to pinpoint key metabolites with decisive roles in disease progression, thereby offering novel targets for preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Hebei Agricultural University (Baoding, China; Protocol number: 2025117). The study adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and was carried out in accordance with both the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 and the EU Directive 2010/63/EU.

Experimental animals and materials

A total of 216 one-day-old male Cobb 500 broilers were obtained from Jiuxing Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Development Co., Ltd. (Baoding, China). Tetramethylthiuram disulfide (thiram, purity ≥ 98%) was purchased from Shuer Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Experimental design and FMT

All broilers were under a defined light/dark cycle and controlled relative humidity (60–70%), where the temperature was gradually lowered from 35°C on day 1 to 23°C by day 21 and maintained thereafter, and provided access to water and food. All broilers were acclimated to the housing conditions prior to any experimental procedures. Formulation of the basal diet (Table 1) followed the Chinese broiler feeding standard (NY/T 33-2004).

Calculated nutrient concentrations.

A total of 216 healthy broilers were randomly allocated into 4 experimental groups (n = 54 per group, with 9 replicate pens of 6 birds each): CON (control normal group) and NRTD (normal-to-TD transfer group): broilers received normal diet; TD (TD model group) and TDRN (TD recovery group): broilers received normal diet (supplemented with thiram 100 mg/kg body weight from days 4 to 7 post-hatch) to induce TD. This bidirectional FMT design enabled assessment of both TD transmissibility from diseased to healthy birds and potential therapeutic effects of healthy microbiota on established TD (Xu et al., 2023). Starting on Day 10, fresh fecal samples (20 mL) were collected from the CON and TD groups daily at 7:00 AM within 15 minutes after feeding. The fecal sample was resuspended in sterile, pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 10% glycerol. The samples were mixed at a 1:1 volume ratio and vortexed for 20 seconds to ensure thorough homogenization. Then, the homogenized bacterial suspension was filtered through a sterile stainless-steel mesh (0.25 mm), and administered to the recipient broilers via oral gavage. Broilers in the TDRN group received the bacterial suspension prepared from the CON group, while broilers in the NRTD group received the suspension prepared from the TD group. Over 7 consecutive days, recipient broilers received daily oral gavage of 3 mL fecal microbiota solution. As controls, broilers in the CON and TD groups received intubation of 3 mL sterile saline solution during the same period.

Growth performance and fecal score

Broilers were reared to 21 days of age, a critical period for rapid skeletal development and the manifestation of TD symptoms. After a 12-hour fasting period (with water supply maintained), broilers in individual pens were weighed to determine the average final body weight per pen as the experimental unit. Daily feed intake was measured per pen, with spilled feed collected and weighed to ensure accurate consumption data. Growth performance parameters—including average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR)—were calculated using standard formulas.

ADG (g/day) = (Final body weight - Initial body weight) / Number of days in the period

ADG (g/day) = (Final body weight - Initial body weight) / Number of days in the period

ADFI (g/day) = Total feed intake / Number of days in the period

ADFI (g/day) = Total feed intake / Number of days in the period

FCR = Total feed intake / (Final body weight - Initial body weight)

FCR = Total feed intake / (Final body weight - Initial body weight)

Following the procedure of Hongyu Feng et al. (2024), fecal scoring was conducted on day 20 of age, three days post-FMT (Feng et al., 2024). On that day, feces were collected from trays within 30 minutes after feeding and scored using a 5-point scale (1 = normal, well-formed feces; 2 = soft but formed feces; 3 = pasty, unformed feces; 4 = semi-liquid feces; 5 = watery, liquid diarrhea).

Sample collection

At 21 days of age, 9 broilers with comparable body weights were randomly selected from each treatment group. Blood was aseptically collected from the wing vein, centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, then the upper serum was collected into sterile tubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for serum metabolomics analysis.

Tibial parameters

Tibial weight was measured using an analytical balance with a sensitivity of 0.001 g. Tibial length was measured using digital calipers. Tibial mineral density and mineral content were determined using a small animal dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scanner (iNSiGHT VET DXA, Osteosys Co., Ltd., Korea).

Histopathological analysis

Tibial samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution (pH 7.4) for 48 hours at room temperature, decalcified in 10% EDTA solution (pH 7.4, changed every 48 hours) until complete decalcification was achieved (verified by needle penetration test, typically 14-21 days). Subsequently, the specimens were dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax using a fully enclosed tissue processor (ASP300S, Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA) (Miquelestorena-Standley et al., 2020). The paraffin-embedded samples were then longitudinally sectioned along the sagittal plane to expose the tibial growth plate. Finally, sections were examined histopathologically by staining with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Safranin O to assess pathological changes (Thorup et al., 2022). The thickness of the tibial growth plate was measured using ImageJ software (ImageJ software v1.6.0, NIH, Bethesda, MA, USA).

16S rRNA gene sequencing

To determine the structure of ileal microbiota, DNA was extracted from ileal content samples, and DNA quality and integrity were assessed via agarose gel electrophoresis. PCR amplification targeted the V3–V4 region (approximately 330 bp) of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene using TransStart Fastpfu DNA Polymerase in an ABI GeneAmp 9700 PCR system. PCR products were purified and recovered using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Purification Kit. Quantification was performed with the Quantifluor™-ST Blue Fluorescent Quantification System (Promega) to determine the PCR product concentration, followed by proportional pooling according to the sequencing volume requirements for each sample. PCR products were constructed into sequencing libraries, with adapter sequences added to the outer ends of the target region via PCR. Final sequencing was performed on the MiSeq platform (following Illumina protocols) by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. The resulting paired-end (PE) reads underwent assembly, quality control, and filtering. Following sample differentiation, OTU clustering analysis and species taxonomic analysis were performed.

Widely targeted metabolomics

After thawing serum samples on ice following removal from the −80°C freezer and vortexing to mix, transfer 50 μL to a centrifuge tube. Add 300 μL of 20% acetonitrile/methanol extraction solution containing internal standard, vortex for 3 minutes, centrifuge at 4°C, 12,000 r/min for 10 minutes. Transfer 200 μL of supernatant to a tube, incubate at −20°C for 30 minutes. Centrifuge again under the same conditions for 3 minutes, then transfer 180 μL of supernatant to the liner of the injection vial for instrument analysis.

LC-MS Detection and Data Acquisition A Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column was employed. The mobile phase consisted of ultrapure water (containing 0.1% formic acid) and acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid). Gradient elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and a column temperature of 40°C. Injection volume was 2 µL (broad-targeted) or 5 µL (non-targeted). Data acquisition was performed using the LC-QTOF-MS/MS platform in Information-Dependent Acquisition (IDA) mode for non-targeted detection, and on the LC-MS/MS (QTRAP) platform in Multi-Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode for qualitative and quantitative analysis of broad-targeted detection. Data were processed using Analyst 1.6.3 software. For quality control (QC), QC samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of all sample extracts. One QC sample was inserted every 10 analytical samples during instrument analysis to monitor repeatability. Reproducibility of extraction and detection was assessed by overlaying total ion chromatograms (TICs) from different QC samples. Pearson correlation analysis and internal standard response variation (CV values) were performed on QC samples (CV ≤ 15% indicates stability). Simultaneously, the coefficient of variation (CV) is analyzed for all samples. When over 75% of substances in the QC sample exhibit CV < 0.3, it indicates highly stable experimental data.

The metabolites were evaluated by the PCA and OPLS-DA. Metabolites meeting the criteria of VIP ≥1 and a FC of either ≥2 or ≤0.5 were classified as differentially abundant. For KEGG pathway analysis, significance was assigned to those with an FDR <0.05 as determined by Fisher’s exact test.

Statistical Analyses

The microbiome data were further analyzed bioinformatically on the platform of Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. Other than the gut microbiome, other data were analyzed using SPSS software (SPSS version 26.0; SPSS Inc, IL), reported as mean ± SEM, and significant differences between mean values were determined by Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA. We used GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate graphs and considered results with P < 0.05 to be statistically significant. Levels of significance are indicated by asterisks (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).

In contrast to the CON group, the TD, TDRN, and NRTD groups exhibited characteristic TD symptoms, clinically manifesting as unstable standing, prolonged recumbency, and leg deformities (Fig. 1). Histological examination revealed altered tibial morphology, characterized by an opaque tissue mass at the growth plate. Safranin O staining identified this mass as uncalcified cartilage. Consistent with this observation, H&E staining further delineated the pathological changes, showing blurred boundaries between the hypertrophic and proliferative zones alongside a notable reduction in angiogenesis within the proliferative zone of affected broilers. Tibial parameters (Table 3) revealed significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05). Gut microbial community structure and diversity

16S rRNA gene sequencing of ileal contents generated 2,259,371 high-quality sequences across all groups, clustering into 287 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. The results suggested that microbial richness and evenness were maintained despite compositional shifts (Fig. 2a, 2b). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) of beta-diversity (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) both demonstrated clear separation among the groups (Fig. 2c, 2d). The CON group formed a separate cluster clearly separated from a combined cluster containing TD, TDRN, and NRTD groups. Critically, the three TD-affected groups did not show significant separation from each other, confirming similar microbiota compositions regardless of whether TD was chemically induced or microbiota-transmitted (Fig. 2e).

Screening of differentially abundant taxa

Three major differential bacterial genera—Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, and Escherichia-Shigella—were identified based on linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) (Fig. 2f, 2h). In the TD and NRTD groups, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus was significantly lower (P < 0.05) than that in the CON group, whereas the abundances of Streptococcus and Escherichia-Shigella were significantly higher (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2g, 2i). These shifts were consistent across all three TD-affected groups with no significant inter-group differences, establishing these bacterial genera as reliable biomarkers of TD-associated dysbiosis.

Serum metabolomic analysis and screening of differential metabolites

To investigate the composition of metabolites within the broilers, metabolomic profiling was performed with serum samples. The OPLS-DA revealed a clear separation in metabolic profiles between the TD vs CON groups, as well as between the NRTD vs CON groups (Fig. 3a, 3d). Volcano plot analysis showed 95 differential metabolites in the TD vs CON groups, comprising 35 upregulated and 60 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 3b). In comparison, 285 differential metabolites were identified between the NRTD vs CON groups, of which there were 76 upregulated and 209 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 3e).

For differential metabolites identified in the TD vs CON comparison, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was applied, identifying significant enrichment (P < 0.05) in pathways related to pentose and glucuronate interconversions, phosphonate and phosphinate metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Fig. 3c). In contrast, the differential metabolites in the NRTD vs CON group were mainly enriched in arginine biosynthesis, autophagy-animal, autophagy-other, porphyrin metabolism, and glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3f).

Differential analysis of metabolites was performed using the OPLS-DA model. Metabolites with |log2FC| ≥ 2.5 relative to the reference group were considered differentially abundant. As shown in Table 4, 8 differential metabolites were identified in the TD vs CON comparison, whereas 160 metabolites were identified in the NRTD vs CON comparison. The results identified seven stably dysregulated metabolites across comparisons: loganin, hydroferulic acid, chenodeoxycholic acid, tricarballylic acid, ergothioneine, cortodoxone, and 2-methoxyestradiol (Fig. 3g). Relative to the CON group, the TD and NRTD groups exhibited distinct metabolic shifts: the abundances of loganin, hydroferulic acid, tricarballylic acid, and chenodeoxycholic acid were significantly decreased, while those of ergothioneine, colforsin daropate, and 2-methoxyestradiol were significantly increased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3h, 3i).

Discussion

TD is typified by skeletal deformities, reduced vascular invasion, and growth plate thickening, clinical features that eventually lead to a loss of standing capability in broilers (Zhang et al., 2022c; Xu et al., 2023). The TD group exhibited these characteristic features, confirming the successful establishment of the TD model in this study. To explore the involvement of gut microbiota in the etiology of TD, we employed FMT and integrated data on growth performance, bone parameters, histological evaluation (HE and Safranin O staining), and gut microbial profiling. Notably, the normal broilers that received FMT from TD model broilers developed characteristic TD symptoms, whereas the TD model broilers that received FMT from normal broilers did not show therapeutic improvement. Our experimental approach yielded direct evidence for the causality of gut microbiota in TD. The FMT experiment serves as a critical proof-of-principle: the transfer of a dysbiotic microbiota was sufficient to induce the full TD phenotype in otherwise healthy recipients, without exogenous thiram. This result directly implicates the gut microbiota as an independent pathogenic factor and extends beyond mere association to suggest a mechanistic breakdown in gut-bone signaling. This represents a paradigm shift from previous correlative observations to causal demonstration, and our findings advocate for microbiota modulation as a novel and viable approach to TD intervention.

Under normal conditions, the gut microbiota maintains a balanced and diverse ecosystem, with beneficial bacteria dominating. Gut microbiota dysbiosis refers to an alteration in the original structure of the gut microbiota, characterized by a decrease in beneficial bacteria and an increase in pathogenic bacteria. This has also been confirmed in existing studies (Hou et al., 2025). For example, after H9N2 AIV infection, the gut microbiota in broilers becomes dysbiotic, leading to an increase in intestinal opportunistic pathogens and inhibiting the growth of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus (Zhang et al., 2020); the overuse of antibiotics leads to gut microbiota dysregulation, resulting in an increase in pathogenic bacteria and a decrease in beneficial bacteria (Shi et al., 2020). In this study, 16S rDNA analysis revealed that, compared with the CON group, both the TD and NRTD groups exhibited significant differences in gut microbiota structure at the genus level (characterized by decreased relative abundance of beneficial bacteria and increased relative abundance of pathogenic bacteria), and the fecal score was also significantly higher in these two treatment groups. We therefore concluded that gut microbiota dysbiosis occurred in these two treatment groups. Furthermore, the NRTD group that received FMT exhibited a more pronounced alteration in microbiota structure than the TD group induced by chemical reagents. Given the TD symptoms observed in the NRTD group, we propose that the development of TD is associated with the gut microbiota dysbiosis resulting from FMT treatment.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in skeletal development, with dysbiosis implicated in TD pathogenesis (Hansen et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023). By comparing bacterial genera among the TD, NRTD, and CON groups, we identified three significantly differential genera: Lactobacillus with decreased relative abundance, Escherichia-Shigella and Streptococcus with increased relative abundance. Lactobacillus, as a well-defined probiotic genus, maintains normal intestinal function and promotes gut health through multiple mechanisms, including immune modulation, vitamin synthesis, and the creation of an acidic microenvironment that inhibits pathogen colonization (Hati et al., 2019; Doo et al., 2024; Kuraji et al., 2024). In contrast, Streptococcus and pathogenic Escherichia strains produce inflammatory mediators and metabolic toxins that may directly impair chondrocyte function and hinder skeletal development. Recent studies indicate that Streptococcus species can induce bone resorption by promoting osteoclast differentiation while suppressing osteoblast activity, consistent with our observation of reduced mineralization in TD-affected birds (Ooi et al., 2014; Noble et al., 2020; Jones, 2022; Sun et al., 2024b). Although most Streptococcus species are harmless commensals, pathogenic strains—particularly Streptococcus faecalis—disrupt bone homeostasis by upregulating osteoclast generation markers (c-Fos and NFATc1) while downregulating osteoblast differentiation (Runx2) (Park et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2024; Huband et al., 2025). This imbalance between osteoclasts and osteoblasts ultimately interferes with skeletal development. These microbial alterations collectively contribute to the deterioration of the intestinal microenvironment, leading to metabolic disorders in the body and ultimately affecting skeletal development.

To explore the influence of gut microbiota dysbiosis in regulating metabolic processes in broilers we analyzed the serum metabolome. Through screening, we identified seven metabolites exhibiting significant differences: loganin, hydroferulic acid, chenodeoxycholic acid, tricarballylic acid, ergothioneine, colforsin daropate, and 2-methoxyestradiol. The gut microbiota influences bile acid metabolism through multiple mechanisms: (1) bacterial bile salt hydrolases deconjugate primary bile acids (Chen et al., 2023); (2) 7α-dehydroxylases convert primary to secondary bile acids (Corrêa et al., 2023); (3) intestinal microbes regulate enterohepatic circulation efficiency (Sun et al., 2018); (4) microbial metabolites modulate hepatic bile acid synthesis (Wang et al., 2020). Lactobacillus species, depleted in our TD birds, are major BSH producers and bile acid modulators (Fibi-Smetana et al., 2021). Their loss would reduce chenodeoxycholic acid bioavailability through decreased deconjugation and potentially increased fecal excretion. Simultaneously, pathogenic bacteria may produce metabolites that directly suppress hepatic FXR expression or compete for FXR binding, further impairing bile acid-mediated skeletal protection. Research indicates that chenodeoxycholic acid and 2-methoxyestradiol influence skeletal growth and development. Chenodeoxycholic acid is a bile acid that has been used for the dissolution of cholesterol gallstones. Furthermore, chenodeoxycholic acid plays a regulatory role in skeletal development. As the most potent agonist of the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) (Clifford et al., 2021; Mori et al., 2022), it upregulates Runx2 expression via the FXR/RXR heterodimer and ER homodimer, and enhances ERK and β-catenin signaling (Cho et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2022). This cascade promotes the expression of osteogenic markers like osteopontin and osteocalcin, thereby accelerating osteoblast differentiation and bone formation (Wang et al., 2018; Absil et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2023b). Moreover, chenodeoxycholic acid reduces the RANKL/OPG ratio by inhibiting RANKL, which in turn suppresses osteoclastogenesis (Cho et al., 2013). 2-methoxyestradiol, a pro-apoptotic agent derived endogenously from 17β-estradiol with anti-angiogenic effects that is currently being investigated as an anticancer agent (Samartzis et al., 2016; Hou et al., 2023), appears to mediate TD pathogenesis through dual mechanisms: it simultaneously disrupts growth plate homeostasis by inhibiting chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation while promoting apoptosis of mature chondrocytes, and suppresses bone remodeling through osteoclast inhibition (Turner et al., 2000; Sibonga et al., 2002; Maran et al., 2006; Cicek et al., 2007; Guan et al., 2023), with gut microbiota dysbiosis-induced 2-methoxyestradiol accumulation (Sun et al., 2024a) serving as the potential upstream trigger for these pathological processes. Notably, we found that the abnormal elevation of serum 2-methoxyestradiol closely aligns with the aforementioned microbial shift patterns.

Correlation analysis indicated that chenodeoxycholic acid and 2-methoxyestradiol were significantly negatively correlated (P < 0.05). The former is a primary bile acid and the latter an estrogen metabolite, both of which belong to the steroid class and are centrally metabolized by the liver, itself subject to their regulatory feedback (Qiu et al., 2017; Ren et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2025). Bile acids can influence hormone secretion via the FXR pathway (Yang et al., 2025), and regulate the expression of estrogen-metabolizing enzymes (such as CYP3A4) through pregnane X receptor and constitutive androstane receptor, thereby impeding the degradation of estrogen within the liver (Kodama et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019)Conversely, hormones modulate bile acid metabolism. Excessive estrogen and its metabolites can induce cholestasis, leading to reduced bile flow and the accumulation of toxic bile acids (Dong et al., 2019; Zu et al., 2021; Alaei Faradonbeh et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2023a). In summary, despite the multiplicity of pathways leading to TD, our experimental evidence compellingly indicates that the pathway involving gut microbiota dysbiosis and its subsequent disruption of bile acid and estrogen metabolism should not be overlooked in the development of TD.

While this study has identified potential gut microbiota and metabolites linked to TD in broilers, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, because the main goal was to test whether FMT itself reshapes the gut microbial community, we did not use antibiotic pretreatment to clear the native microbiota in recipient birds—this may have influenced the engraftment and functional effects of the transplanted microbiota. Second, due to current methodological limits, we could only detect microbial taxa at relatively high abundance; thus, potential low-abundance but causative pathogens or consortia might have been missed. Finally, given the complex etiology of TD and the large number of altered metabolites identified, we focused our interpretation on those with the most pronounced fold changes. Whether these metabolites are true drivers of TD, rather than secondary markers, will need further experimental validation.

Conclusion

FMT’s successful transfer of the TD phenotype confirms that specific gut microbiota are mechanistically linked to the induction of skeletal pathology. We discovered a consistent dysbiotic signature marked by Lactobacillus depletion and Streptococcus and Escherichia-Shigella enrichment across all TD-affected groups. The mechanisms underlying these observations center on the dysregulation of bile acid metabolism (specifically reduced chenodeoxycholic acid levels) and the accumulation of the anti-angiogenic agent 2-methoxyestradiol in TD. These findings illuminate promising therapeutic strategies for future TD treatment in broilers: (1) modulation of the gut microbiota, particularly by boosting Lactobacillus abundance and suppressing opportunistic pathogens; (2) supplementation with chenodeoxycholic acid in the diet; and (3) regulation of related metabolism to inhibit endogenous 2-methoxyestradiol synthesis.
