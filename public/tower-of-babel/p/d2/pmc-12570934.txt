Implementation of the Study Participant Feedback Questionnaire to understand participant experience in a phase 3 oncology clinical trial
Authors: Andrzej Nowojewski, Alex Wheal, William Dott, Dario Ruscica, Serban Ghiorghiu, Barbara Valastro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo understand participant experience in the INTERLINK-1 clinical trial using the Study Participant Feedback Questionnaire (SPFQ), explore factors associated with participant experience and evaluate the implementation of the SPFQ.DesignPhase 3, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre trial.Setting79 sites in 21 countries from October 2020 to October 2022.Participants371 adults with recurrent/metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (39% aged >66 years, 69% White and 81% male).Primary outcome measuresSPFQ administered at enrolment (SPFQ-A), during the trial (SPFQ-B) and at trial completion (SPFQ-C).ResultsA total of 223, 64 and 93 participants completed the SPFQ-A, SPFQ-B and SPFQ-C, respectively. Early termination of the INTERLINK-1 study limited the number of SPFQ-B and SPFQ-C responses. For all but one question, over 80% of participants selected positive responses, indicating positive experience with regards to enrolment (SPFQ-A), site (SPFQ-B) and overall trial (SPFQ-C). Individually, quality of life, continent and race were significantly associated with participant experience. Small numbers of negative and neutral responses enabled the construction of multiple ordinal regression models and were used to identify unbiased coefficients associated with patient experience. The models showed that males and participants in Asia reported worse experiences in questions regarding trial enrolment (p<0.05). Recent adverse events and higher Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status were significantly negatively associated with trial site satisfaction (B1; OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.96, p<0.05) and site staff-related questions (B5; OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.93, p<0.05), respectively. Indications of acquiescence bias were found, possibly due to SPFQ question design.ConclusionsOverall, participants reported positive experiences in the INTERLINK-1 study as evaluated by the SPFQ. Specific aspects of participant experience varied by sex, continent, recent adverse events and ECOG status. Further investigation is required to understand the causes of these findings to develop strategies for improved participant experience. Observations from this study will be used to inform future SPFQ implementation and develop strategies for better, more equitable trial experiences.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04590963.Keywords: Patient-Centered Care, Patient Satisfaction, Head & neck tumours, Clinical Trial

Introduction

Understanding participant experience is key to ensuring the success of clinical trials because of its impacts on the ability to recruit and retain participants.1,3 One in five adult cancer trials fails due to low accrual.4 Ultimately, inadequate recruitment can result in negative scientific, budgetary and ethical consequences for clinical trials.5 There have been several recent attempts to develop a participant burden metric to link clinical trial design with participant experience.6,9 These proposed metrics incorporate factors such as frequency of visits, types of procedures, travel, lifestyle restrictions, blinding status and potential treatment effects to determine the overall participant burden.6,9 These metrics are useful for the study team to predict the impact that various study design choices will have on participants, but they do not capture participants’ lived clinical trial experiences. A literature review of participant experience in clinical studies identified that only a small fraction (nine out of thousands) of studies systematically evaluated participant experience. Moreover, only one of the nine used a widely accepted and validated instrument.10

TransCelerate BioPharma aimed to address this by developing the Study Participant Feedback Questionnaire (SPFQ), a standardised, patient-completed instrument that assesses satisfaction and clinical study experience for use across a range of indications and trial designs. The SPFQ was developed in collaboration with industry professionals (clinical operations, development and patient-reported outcomes (PRO), patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs)) in accordance with best practices for the validation of PRO instruments.11 An initial conceptual framework and SPFQ draft was developed from a literature review, patient and site advisory boards, 1:1 patient concept elicitation interviews and industry professional interviews. The SPFQ draft was merged with the Trial Feedback Questionnaire,12 developed for use in oncology in the USA and UK, to create a final version, which was validated through stakeholder engagement, including the US Food and Drug Administration. Content and cross-cultural validity of the SPFQ was further confirmed through concept elicitation and cognitive debriefing in interviews conducted with 80 participants across eight countries (in Europe, South America and Asia), ensuring geographic, cultural and linguistic validity.13 Moreover, a total of 17 therapeutic areas were represented across the countries, reaffirming the use of SPFQ as a generic measure across therapeutic indications. The SPFQ template consists of three brief participant questionnaires administered at enrolment, during and at the end of the study. Domains covered in the SPFQ include concepts such as satisfaction with trial procedures, provision and understanding of key information, impact on daily life, ability to ask questions and the quality of the answers provided, and support following the trial completion.11

There has been no published literature on the use of the SPFQ template to date, although a selection of SPFQ questions was used to assess participant satisfaction in a decentralised study of antibody response to COVID-19 vaccines.14

As the only published instrument for assessing study participant experience, AstraZeneca has adopted the SPFQ for implementation in clinical trials across several disease areas. The SPFQ enables AstraZeneca to identify key barriers and drivers for participant satisfaction and objectively evaluate the outcome of patient centric interventions and trial design features. INTERLINK-1, the first of these trials to be completed, was a clinical trial in patients with recurrent or metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC).15 HNSCC is the seventh most common cancer worldwide, with 890 000 new cases and 450 000 deaths in 2020.16 17 HNSCC accounts for approximately 90% of HN cancers.18 Globally, the incidence of HNSCC is highest in South and Southeast Asia.17 In the USA, HNSCC has three times higher prevalence in men than in women, and the median age at diagnosis is 64 years.17 The highest incidence is observed in non-Hispanic White men.17

Data from INTERLINK-1 provide an opportunity for critical assessment of the implementation of the SPFQ in an oncology clinical trial. The primary aim of this analysis was to understand the overall experience of participants in the trial and to explore the characteristics and clinical variables that are associated with participant experience as reported via the SPFQ. The secondary objective was to evaluate the implementation of SPFQ in a clinical trial and its impact on the data quality to inform future implementation.

Methods

INTERLINK-1 study

INTERLINK-1 (NCT04590963) was a phase 3, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre, global study of monalizumab (a humanised antibody targeting the NKG2A immune checkpoint receptor) in combination with cetuximab (a chimeric antibody that inhibits EGFR) in patients with recurrent or metastatic HNSCC who have progressed on or after treatment with an immune checkpoint inhibitor (programmed cell death 1/programmed cell death ligand-1 and platinum-based therapies. The details of the INTERLINK-1 trial design and primary endpoints have been previously reported,15 and further results will be reported in a separate publication. The protocols were approved by the independent ethics committee at each participating site (online supplemental table S1), and all patients provided written informed consent. Patients were randomised in a 2:1 ratio to active and control arms. Study enrolment was discontinued on 1 August 2022, based on the results of a preplanned futility interim analysis that showed treatment with monalizumab and cetuximab did not demonstrate a clinical benefit compared with placebo and cetuximab, and due to unlikelihood of achieving statistical significance for the primary endpoint overall survival in the HPV (human papillomavirus) unrelated population.19

SPFQ survey data

The SPFQ was developed by TransCelerate BioPharma as a tool to collect feedback from participants on their trial experience.11 As a participant-completed measure, it assesses trial participant satisfaction and understanding of clinical trial procedures and experience. Content and cross-cultural validation support the application of the SPFQ across clinical development programmes in diverse geographies and indications.13 The SPFQ is split into three unique questionnaires (online supplemental table S2) that are completed at different time points in the trial: SPFQ-part A administered at enrolment (questions A1–A4), SPFQ-part B administered to participants during the trial (questions B1–B8) and SPFQ-part C administered to participants on their completion of the trial (questions C1–C5).11 The SPFQ questions assess concepts such as satisfaction with trial procedures, provision and understanding of key information, impact on daily life, ability to ask questions and the quality of the answers provided, and support following the trial completion.

The SPFQ uses a 5-point Likert scale for most questions and binary yes/no scales for others (online supplemental table S2). Four questions (B7, B8, C1 and C2) use a binary answer scale (no, yes) encoded as (0, 1). Question C5 uses a reverse 5-point Likert scale (much less than expected, somewhat less than expected, same as expected, somewhat more than expected, much more than expected) and is encoded as (4, 3, 2, 1 and 0) to match the emotional valence scale of the other questions (ie, answer encoded as 0 is least positive). The remaining questions used a standard 5-point Likert scale (strongly disagree, disagree, neither agree nor disagree, agree and strongly agree) encoded as (0, 1, 2, 3 and 4). The version of SPFQ implemented in INTERLINK-1 comprises 17 questions distributed across the three parts from the TransCelerate BioPharma V. 1.0 template.

In this study, the SPFQ was optional for participants to consent to and complete (using an electronic device), and it was possible for participants to skip whole parts and individual questions within each part. If participants consented to the SPFQ in the main informed consent form, then they were given the opportunity to complete the SPFQ on the study electronic clinical outcome assessment platform. Part A was administered at the screening visit (up to 28 days before randomisation), part B at cycle 11, day 1 (day 141±2 after randomisation visit according to the protocol) and part C at the end-of-treatment visit, which took place 1 month after the last administration of the investigational medicinal product.

Patient and public involvement

Other than patient involvement in the development of the SPFQ,11 patients were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of INTERLINK-1 or this analysis using SPFQ data collected during INTERLINK-1.

Statistical analysis

To make full use of the SPFQ 5-point response ordinal scale, multiple ordinal logistic regression was used, which provides cumulative log odds across all SPFQ levels for each independent variable, confidence intervals (CIs) and Wald’s χ2 estimates. For each model, the proportionality assumption and outcome were tested using nominal_test and scale_test from the ordinal library.20 Outcome variables for which the assumption was violated were examined by comparing the odds ratios (OR) for each group comparison in the ordinal logistic regression. Additional details are shown in online supplemental table S3.

To analyse univariate associations between SPFQ answers and participant and clinical covariance, as well as identify significant differences in the respondent characteristics contingency table, Fisher’s exact test was used. The following participant characteristics and clinical measurements were used as independent variables: age, sex, race, trial arm, early enrolment, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, recent adverse events (AEs), quality of life and continent. These variables were chosen pragmatically, as they are commonly collected in clinical trials, and would therefore allow for future comparisons with other trials that use the SPFQ. Early enrolment was defined as occurring in the first 50% of active days for a given site. A recent AE was defined as one that occurred within 15 days before completing the SPFQ, regardless of its severity or resolution (ie, before or after taking the SPFQ, or did not resolve before the end of the trial). Quality of life was based on question 30 of the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) QLG Core Questionnaire (QLQ)-C30 patient reported outcome.21 Additional details are shown in online supplemental table S4.

The variance inflation factor was used to remove collinear variables in regressions modelling responses to different SPFQ questions. To determine the impact of random effects, we used the likelihood ratio tests for cumulative link models22 and compared the full models for each question with models that included fixed effects only.23 Goodness of fit was assessed with McFadden,24 Cox and Snell25 and Nagelkerke26 pseudo-R2s. We assessed convergence of models by inspecting the condition number of the Hessian (ie, cond.H) for each model.

Finally, participants who might have provided careless responses27 in part C were identified as those who selected the same response option in questions C3–C5 and the same option in the binary questions C1 and C2 that had the same valence (positive or negative) corresponding to questions C3 and C4. All analyses were conducted in R 4.0.328 language.

Results

A total of 371 participants were randomised into the study, of which 223, 64 and 93 participants completed parts A, B and C, respectively (table 1). The INTERLINK-1 study was terminated early, which prevented a large proportion of participants from reaching either cycle 11 of the therapy or end of trial where SPFQ parts B and C were administered, respectively. Furthermore, participants whose condition progressed before cycle 11 completed were not asked to complete part B as this scenario was never considered when designing the study protocol. This resulted in the significant drop-off in number of respondents for parts B and C of SPFQ. Adherence for parts A, B and C was 60.1%, 67.4% and 26.7%, respectively (60.1%, 17.3% and 25.1%, respectively, when calculated as a fraction of all randomised patients). All 371 randomised participants had an opportunity to complete part A, but only 95 participants reached cycle 11 when part B was administered. Due to early termination, it is not known how many participants were given an opportunity to complete part C. Therefore, part C adherence is a lower bound estimate because the denominator includes patients who did not reach the follow-up visit when part C was administered. Participants who died during the trial (n=21) were excluded from the denominator.

Responders and non-responders differed in three significant ways (table 1). Responders to parts A and C were significantly more likely to be White and from European sites than non-responders, and in part C, there were more responders who enrolled early. Specifically, in parts A and C, there were significantly (Fisher’s exact test, p<0.05) more White participants among responders than non-responders (part A: 73.1%, part C: 77.4% vs part A: 62.8%, part C: 65.8%) and European site participants among responders than non-responders (part A: 57.8%, part C: 63.4% vs part A: 43.9%, part C: 47.5%). There were also significantly more responders who enrolled early compared with non-responders in part C (69.9% and 49.8%, respectively). Responders and non-responders did not significantly differ in any other characteristics.

Figure 1 shows the distribution of responses to all SPFQ questions. For all but one question, large majorities of participants (81% to 98%) selected responses with positive valence rather than neutral or negative. Questions B7, B8, C1 and C2 had a binary answer scale (see Methods) and were colour coded using dark blue and dark red dotted texture. Question C5 (the overall commitment required was similar to expectations) had markedly more neutral and negative responses than the other questions. Question C5 was the only question with a Likert scale that had a reverse valence (positive answers were presented in reverse order compared with the remaining questions) and did not use the standard agree/disagree scale. The low number of negative responses to binary questions made it difficult to derive any meaningful associations with the covariates using either univariate or multivariate modelling and therefore will not be discussed further in this paper.

Online supplemental figures S1-S4 show distributions of responses to each SPFQ question by each explanatory variable. Significant univariate associations between a covariate and the responses using Fisher’s exact test at the p<0.05 level are noted. Continent was significantly associated with responses to all SPFQ questions, with European participants reporting more positive answers than those in Asia and Other. Race was significantly associated with the responses to all SPFQ questions apart from B6 and C5, with White participants reporting more positive answers than Asian and non-White participants. Patients reporting higher quality of life were also more likely to report higher patient experience in seven questions: understanding the treatment process (A1), adequacy of pretrial information (A2), comfort with asking questions before the trial (A4), satisfaction with the trial site (B1), organisation of the trial visits (B2), convenience of trial visit scheduling (B3) and satisfaction with information about future support (C3). Early enrolment showed a statistically significant association in five questions with the first half of enrolled participants reporting better experiences compared with respondents enrolled later: understanding the treatment process (A1), adequacy of pretrial information (A2), ease of understanding pretrial information (A3), satisfaction with the trial site (B1) and satisfaction with information about future support (C3). The following question-covariate pairs were also statistically significantly associated with better experience: female sex (ease of understanding pretrial information (A3), satisfaction with information about future support (C3), satisfaction with trial experience (C4)), younger age (satisfaction with information about future support (C3), satisfaction with trial experience (C4)), no recent AE (comfort with asking questions during the trial (B5), satisfaction with answers to questions during the trial (B6)) and Hispanic ethnicity (comfort with asking questions before the trial (A4)).

Question C5 (whether the overall commitment was similar to expectations) had a very different distribution of answers than other questions. There were more neutral responses, and the positive and negative responses were more evenly balanced than observed for other questions. Only continent and quality of life showed significant associations with C5 answers, and higher quality of life was associated with more negative responses to C5, which is an opposite trend compared with all other questions.

Prior to fitting the multiple ordinal regression model, it was necessary to address variables that were highly interrelated (ie, collinear), as this would make the model difficult to interpret. Online supplemental table S5 provides the distribution of responders by race and location, showing that almost all non-White patients were located in Asia. Therefore, location was used as the explanatory variable, rather than race. Furthermore, there were too few respondents in some countries, which prevented some models from converging; and therefore, the country variables were combined to a continent variable with three levels: Europe, Asia and Other. Other consisted of participants from North America (n=29), South America (n=10) and Oceania (n=11). The likelihood ratio test showed that using it as a fixed effect rather than a random effect significantly improved the model quality.

In the following models, quality of life will be used as a control variable as it exhibits high multicollinearity (variance inflation factor greater than 10) in almost all SPFQ questions and therefore it would be challenging to interpret its regression coefficients. In part C, inclusion of quality of life led to unstable models, and therefore it was excluded from the analysis.

Different response distribution in C5 (figure 1 and online supplemental figure S4) could have been caused by careless responders choosing to select the same response to all questions. On closer investigation, we found that 11 responders chose the same pattern of response to all questions in part C, regardless of the valence. This type of response is called ‘straightlining’ and is a common phenomenon in survey research.29 Table 4 shows the results of the ordinal regression models for all part C SPFQ questions, and comparing it to online supplemental table S6 shows very little change.

Discussion

The INTERLINK-1 study was one of the first clinical trials to collect direct feedback from participants on their experience participating in an AstraZeneca ongoing clinical trial using SPFQ and the first opportunity to interrogate the SPFQ responses in combination with clinical variables and participants’ characteristics. The overall results across all SPFQ questions indicated a positive participant experience on the INTERLINK-1 study. Quality of life scores closest to SPFQ completion and recent AEs were significantly associated with the SPFQ answers in the univariate (for almost all questions) and multivariate (for one question) analysis, respectively. This is an important finding because SPFQ is intended to be used as a standardised bank of questions across studies in different therapeutic areas,11 which could differ significantly in baseline quality of life and AE burden. Researchers should, therefore, account for these systematic differences when comparing SPFQ responses between trial populations.

We found that participants in Asia, male participants and participants enrolled later at a site had worse experiences across certain questions compared with those outside of Asia, female participants and participants enrolled early at a site, respectively. We do not yet know which potential underlying factors and context are driving these observed differences; therefore, further investigation is warranted to understand the root causes. Strategies can then be implemented to provide a better and more equitable experience for participants in global studies.30,33 To mitigate the limited depth of root cause analysis offered by the SPFQ, two approaches are being implemented. First, study teams have access to the data in a dashboard during the study, along with a local country expert who can contextualise the data, engage sensitively with sites to uncover root causes and actions (while maintaining good relations) and provide cross-study overview and analysis. Second, we are piloting qualitative one-on-one interviews34 with study participants that focus on participant experience, including those domains covered by the SPFQ and study-specific ones that are not. Using this approach, study teams can use results of the SPFQ during the study to target specific sites for more in-depth root cause analysis and actions.

In this study, the treatment arm to which participants were randomised was not significantly associated with their trial experience. In principle, one might expect patients who receive medicine that improves their condition to report better patient experience.35,38 On the other hand, for treatments with higher adverse event burden, it may have the opposite effect on overall experience. The active treatment arm in INTERLINK-1 was not found to be efficacious vs placebo;19 therefore, we were unable to explore this hypothesis.

The results allow us to make specific and actionable recommendations for how to improve the administration of SPFQ in clinical trials, which will, in turn, enable study teams to improve patients’ experience in clinical trials. First, early termination of the study and late implementation of the SPFQ-B in the schedule of activities significantly reduced the number of responders to parts B and C of SPFQ. As a result, only 64 participants completed the SPFQ-B and 93 completed the SPFQ-C. Consequently, the findings for questions in part A are supported by stronger evidence than in parts B and C due to a higher number of respondents in part A than in parts B and C. Solutions to address this could include administering SPFQ-B much earlier during the trial, administering it several times during the treatment period in long trials, and combining parts B and C administration during the first follow-up visit if a participant progresses or withdraws prior to the scheduled visits for part B and/or C administration.39 Second, our findings are limited to those participants who responded to the SPFQ. At least one-third of participants who had an opportunity to complete the SPFQ at each stage chose not to do so. It is possible that these participants had a more negative view of the trial but chose not to share it, which may have biased the results in favour of positive responses.40 41 To avoid this potential for bias, future trials may implement the SPFQ as a mandatory feedback mechanism.

Two additional limitations could only be solved by significantly expanding the scope of the patient experience research. First, the highly correlated nature of the continent, quality of life and race variables makes it difficult to resolve how each influences participant experience. Quality of life as measured by the QLQ-C30 has been shown to vary across countries in North America and Europe.39 Therefore, it may not be possible to completely disentangle the effects of quality of life from country. However, clinical trials that aim to recruit patients from different races30 32 at the country level may be able to resolve the effects of these two variables. Second, while it was possible to quantitatively evaluate the associations between SPFQ responses and participant and clinical variables, it was not possible to evaluate the reasons or context behind participant responses. To produce more meaningful insights, the SPFQ would need to be followed up with qualitative research including interviews with participants and site staff.

This analysis suggests several ways in which SPFQ design might be improved. First, we will discuss rewording the questions to reduce the potential for acquiescence bias, then removing binary scale questions and finally, removing the reverse valence question (C5) and using other ways to identify careless responders. The positive skew in all questions apart from C5 (ie, the overall commitment was similar to expectations) might suggest the presence of acquiescence bias.42 43 This is a type of response bias in which respondents have a tendency to agree with survey question statements rather than give their own unbiased views.44 It often happens when respondents are presented with agree/disagree, true/false or yes/no survey questions.44 An alternative explanation is that the default response in a very well-run trial where participants have no issues at all would actually be the strongly agree option, except for C5. In that question, same as expected is the default option in a trial where study physicians created realistic expectations about how much commitment the trial would require, and neither overestimated it nor underestimated it. Therefore, respondents had an opportunity to express a wider range of opinions for C5 than a simple agree/disagree scale would allow. In the only other published use of SPFQ, a version of C5 was asked but with the agree/disagree scale, and over 80% of respondents chose the two most positive options.14 That study was a vaccine trial of healthy participants; therefore, it is expected to have a much lower participant burden than an oncology trial, which may explain the high level of agreement with the statement about the overall commitment being similar to participant expectations. Therefore, it could be beneficial in future implementations of SPFQ to minimise acquiescence bias by rephrasing the question and implementing a more balanced response scale.45 Furthermore, the yes/no scale in the four binary questions (ie, B7, B8, C1 and C2) limited the diversity of responses, which prevented us from using the multivariate regression model. In the other questions that did not use a binary scale, multivariate ordinal regression overcame this problem because it takes each response level into account separately. These models led to additional insight about the association of sex with participant experience in the multiple-choice questions. This was obscured in the univariate analysis due to the correlated nature of these variables and a small sample size but became statistically significant in the multivariable ordinal regression model. Therefore, future versions of SPFQ should avoid binary scale questions.46 47

Question C5 was the only one with the reverse valence scale48 49 (ie, the most positive answer of the 5-point scale was on the left side rather than the right side as in the other questions). This allowed us to identify 11 careless responders who answered in a straight line through all SPFQ-C questions.50 51 The ordinal regression model without these responders did not differ significantly from the model with all respondents; however, the fact that C5 answers seemed to show opposite trends in online supplemental figure S3 (ie, C5 responses are more negative for participants with higher reported quality of life) to all other questions, suggests that there could have been more careless responders beyond the 11 we identified. It is difficult to conclude whether question C5 was correctly understood by the responders. In the future, SPFQ could either avoid using reverse scales, use a dummy question with a reverse scale to identify careless responders or add an appropriate explanation that question C5 has a reverse scale.52

The SPFQ provides a consistent measure of participant experience across several patient-informed domains. First, it gives participants an opportunity to become a partner in their clinical trial, which can facilitate patient trust and overall experience.53 Second, it enables cross-study aggregation of data to identify trends, eg, at a site level, that are not present at an individual study level. Finally, it enables a standardised way to track participant experience and benchmark across studies, programmes and indications. In this way, it provides an objective measure to determine which initiatives and study design/delivery features positively or negatively impact participant experience.

The SPFQ template administered in this study did not address domains such as financial motivations/toxicity, expectations around experience, appreciation/gratitude, social determinants of health, motivation to participate in the study and future clinical trials, or likelihood to recommend the study to someone else.10 54 Moreover, several questions grouped together multiple aspects into a single statement based around a central theme. For example, item B1 (‘Overall, I am satisfied with the trial site’) groups comfort and privacy of the treatment area, waiting area, parking and ease of access to the site. In this example, it is not possible to determine exactly which elements of the site participants are dissatisfied with. Future versions of the SPFQ should be adapted to ensure that each statement addresses only one distinct aspect to enable clear interpretation and better root cause assessment.

Although a generic patient experience measure enables cross-study data aggregation and benchmarking, it does limit the ability to address the experience of specific study design elements, eg, digital or decentralised elements, which have increased exponentially since the COVID-19 pandemic.55 56 This is particularly important as these elements have the potential to positively impact participants’ experience.57 To address this, a standardised question bank will be developed to enable studies to customise the SPFQ with relevant questions, while maintaining the core domains of the template. In this way, the benefits of a single template can be maintained, while allowing specific facets of the experience to be explored more deeply.

The addition of questions to the core template questions does have the potential to increase survey fatigue and completion burden. One method to address this is adaptive questioning, which tailors questions based on previous responses, minimising redundancy and cognitive strain.56 For example, if a participant answers that they are dissatisfied with the trial site, only then would more probing questions be administered to assess which aspects resulted in dissatisfaction.

In conclusion, this retrospective analysis of participant experience on the INTERLINK-1 study has allowed us to identify specific trends, related to sex, continent, recent adverse events and ECOG status, that will support the development of strategies for improved participant experience in future studies. These results also allow us to make specific and actionable recommendations for improving the design of the SPFQ and its implementation in clinical trials.

Our research supports the implementation of a standardised measure of participant experience in clinical trials as an important tool for sponsors to monitor satisfaction and quantify the benefits of patient centric initiatives, not only within trials but also across the drug development process and between different drug development programmes. This will inform changes to improve the trial participants’ experience and allow researchers to prioritise and promote adoption of those initiatives that provide the highest impact to patients and the organisation.58 Implementation of SPFQ in ongoing studies is an important step to create an optimised and more equitable experience for trial participants and ultimately improve trial participant retention and recruitment.
