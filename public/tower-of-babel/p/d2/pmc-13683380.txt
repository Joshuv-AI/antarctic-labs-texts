Ketogenic diet alleviates acute radiation-induced intestinal injury through JAK2/STAT3/RORγt/IL-17A signaling pathway via gut microbiome
Authors: Jingjing Yang, Zhi Ling, Ming Zhou, Mingyang Tao, Jingxian Mao, Huaijuan Guo, Jiaxin Wang, Xiao Qu, Ying Wang, Yefei Zhu, Kun Zhang, Xuebing Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence suggests dietary interventions regulate inflammatory signaling through gut microbiome modulation, yet their therapeutic potential in radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) remains underexplored. This study demonstrates that ketogenic diet (KD), a high-fat and low-carbohydrate dietary regimen, exerts protective effects against RIII through dual mechanisms involving microbial regulation and inflammatory pathway inhibition. Using high-salt diet (HSD) as a dietary control, KD significantly attenuated intestinal inflammation by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines while enhancing barrier integrity through tight junction protein upregulation in radiation-exposed murine model. 16S rDNA sequencing showed KD enriched Akkermansia and reduced Enterobacteriaceae, whereas HSD exhibited inverse patterns. Mechanistically, RNA sequencing revealed that KD uniquely suppressed the JAK2/STAT3 pathway in RIII mice. In vitro studies demonstrated that β-hydroxybutyrate, a key ketone metabolite, effectively suppressed RORγt expression and subsequent downregulation of IL-17A gene transcription via the inhibition of JAK2/STAT3 pathway, thus mitigate inflammatory damage. Fecal microbiota transplantation validated that KD-modified microbiome directly inhibited JAK2/STAT3 signaling activation, as well as the downregulation of RORγt and IL-17A. These findings establish KD as a promising dietary strategy mitigate acute RIII through synergistic modulation of gut microbiota and inflammatory signaling, providing novel insights into nutritional approaches targeting microbial-host crosstalk in radiation injury.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with its incidence rapidly increasing across many countries each year1. Standard treatments for most cancers include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, with over 50% of patients with cancers benefiting from the latter. Advances in digital techniques have enabled more precise targeting of tumors during radiotherapy2. However, despite its oncological effectiveness, radiotherapy is associated with significant side effects, including skin, cardiac, and gastrointestinal toxicity, which can disrupt treatment continuity and negatively impact patients’ quality of life3,4. For patients with abdominal or pelvic cancers, the most common complication of radiotherapy is radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII), which manifests as abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss, and, in severe cases, life-threatening intestinal perforation5. The pathogenesis of RIII is complex and multifactorial, involving changes in gut microbiota composition, increased inflammatory cytokine production, and disrupted intestinal barrier function6,7. Current clinical management of RIII primarily involves antibiotics, probiotics, nutritional support, and, when necessary, endoscopic or surgical intervention8. However, these treatments often prove insufficient, highlighting the need for a deeper mechanistic understanding to develop novel and effective preventive strategies.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on the role of dietary interventions in modulating intestinal health, particularly in conditions like colorectal cancer and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)9. For instance, one study demonstrated that a high-salt diet (HSD) exacerbates intestinal inflammation, potentially by reducing beneficial Lactobacillus populations and butyrate production in mouse models of chemically induced colitis10. The ketogenic diet (KD), characterized by high-fat and low-carbohydrate intake, has shown promise in improving clinical outcomes in a range of diseases, from metabolic disorders to malignancies11. For instance, KD has been shown to elevate serum levels of taurodeoxycholic acid and tauroursodeoxycholic acid in mice, both of which are essential for regulating body weight and fasting glucose levels12. Additionally, β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) produced by KD inhibits cancer cell proliferation by inactivating mammalian target of rapamycin signaling and glycolysis13. Our previous research has demonstrated that KD alleviates colitis development and protects the intestinal barrier by modulating gut microbiota and reducing colonic group 3 innate lymphoid cells14. Both KD and BHB can protect against intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury through inflammatory suppression and barrier protection15. A recent study has found that the preventive role of KD in colitis may be correlated with dietary fatty acid composition16. KD likely plays critical roles in alleviating intestinal diseases. However, its specific impact on RIII has not been extensively studied.

In this study, a mouse model was first established to evaluate the effects of KD on RIII development; meanwhile, HSD was selected as an adverse dietary control since it has been shown to alter intestinal microbiota and accelerate inflammation. A cellular model receiving ionizing radiation (IR) combined with BHB or high-salt (HS) treatment was also used to partly mimic the effects of KD and HSD in vitro. Transcriptome sequencing was then performed to explore diet-mediated molecular mechanisms in RIII, followed by biological validation in both in vivo and in vitro settings. Microbiome and metabolomic analyses were employed to identify diet-altered bacteria and their metabolites during RIII development. Furthermore, the impact of diet-modified gut microbiota on RIII was assessed using a mouse model that underwent fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). This study not only uncovers the mechanistic links between dietary interventions, gut microbiota, and inflammatory signaling in RIII but also identifies several novel therapeutic targets for this disease.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples and patient information

Intestinal tissues were collected from 20 patients with cancers clinically diagnosed with RIII who underwent colonoscopy at the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University between 2021 and 2023. The median age of the patients was 64.5 years, with a range from 47 to 83 years. Among the participants, 8 were male, and 12 were female. The cancer types included colorectal cancer (n = 12) and cervical cancer (n = 8). None of the patients had a prior diagnosis of chronic intestinal diseases, such as Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis. According to follow-up records (updated before June 2024), 18 patients remained alive, and none of them had experienced tumor recurrence.

Animal experiment

All mice (C57BL/6, male, 8-week-old, weighing 20–25 g) were sourced from the Experimental Animal Center of Yangzhou University and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature (20–25 °C), humidity (50-60%), and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Mice’s weight, food, and water intake were monitored weekly during the first 16 weeks and daily after IR treatment. Following a 7-day acclimatization period, 24 mice were randomly divided into four groups: normal diet (ND) group (mice fed ND without IR treatment, n = 6); ND + IR group (mice fed ND and treated with IR, n = 6); HSD + IR group (mice fed HSD and treated with IR, n = 6); KD + IR group (mice fed KD and treated with IR, n = 6). The detailed compositions of ND, KD, and HSD are provided in Table S1. Mice were co-housed before dietary interventions to minimize cage effects. After 16 weeks of dietary intervention, the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and received total abdominal irradiation (TAI, 12 Gy, 200 cGy/min). The irradiation area covered the abdomen from the xiphoid to the pubic symphysis, with the remaining body parts shielded by a lead plate. Six days post-irradiation, the mice were euthanized, and blood, fecal, and tissue samples were collected.

Definition of Lee’s index and disease activity index (DAI)

Lee’s index was calculated as follows: Lee’s index = [Weight (g) × 1000](1/3)/Length (cm), where length was defined as the distance from the tip of the nose to the anus. The DAI was calculated based on the percentage of body weight loss, diarrhea, and hematochezia, using the following scoring system: body weight loss (0, no loss; 1, 1–5% loss; 2, 5–10% loss; and 3, ≥10% loss); hematochezia (0, no blood; 1, weak; 2, moderate; and 3, strong); stool consistency (0, normal; 1, mild soft; 2, soft; and 3, diarrhea).

Cellular model

The human normal colon epithelial cell line NCM460 was obtained from the INCELL Corporation (San Antonio, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. To partly simulate the effects of HSD or KD in vitro, NCM460 cells were first treated with sodium chloride solution or β-hydroxybutyric acid (BHB) for 2 h13,17. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to IR at a dose of 8 Gy (2 Gy/min) using a linear accelerator (Varian, UK). Cellular assays were performed 6 h after IR treatment. For drug intervention experiments, the Stat3 agonist Colivelin (MedChemExpress, USA) and the Stat3 inhibitor C188-9 (MedChemExpress, USA) were added to the medium following treatment with sodium chloride solution or BHB.

Plasmid construction

The plasmid (pcDNA3.1-RORC) (Obio Technology Co., Ltd, China) was constructed to enhance RORγt expression, and the empty vector plasmid pcDNA3.1 was used as a control. NCM460 cells were seeded into 12-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Afterward, pcDNA3.1 or pcDNA3.1-RORγt was transfected into NCM460 cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 or P3000 (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

To deplete the gut microbiota, mice were treated with an antibiotic solution (Abx) for 1 week. The antibiotic mixture, consisting of ampicillin (1 mg/mL), neomycin (1 mg/mL), metronidazole (1 mg/mL), and vancomycin (0.5 mg/mL), was dissolved in sterile drinking water and administered daily until the end of the experiment. For FMT, feces from mice treated with 16 weeks of ND, KD, or HSD were collected and suspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 15% glycerin (100 mg/mL), then stored at −80 °C. Mice were administered 200 μL of the microbial transplant solution orally once daily for 7 consecutive days. The mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): F_ND (mice receiving microbial transplant solution from the ND group without IR treatment), F_ND + IR (mice receiving microbial transplant solution from the ND group with IR treatment), F_HSD + IR (mice receiving microbial transplant solution from the HSD group with IR treatment), and F_KD + IR (mice receiving microbial transplant solution from the KD group with IR treatment). After 1 week of FMT, the mice underwent IR treatment as previously described and were subsequently housed for 6 days before being sacrificed.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Tissue samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5-μm-thick slices. The sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated using an ethanol gradient, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined under a light microscope. For crypt quantification, at least 10 well-oriented crypts were assessed per mouse from 3 non-overlapping regions per section, with 6 mice per group analyzed.

Immunohistochemistry

The paraffin-embedded tissue samples were cut into 5 μm sections. The sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated in alcohol, and subjected to antigen retrieval using a microwave oven. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with a 0.3% hydrogen peroxide solution. After washing the sections three times with PBS, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies. Following a 1-h incubation with the secondary antibody, protein staining was performed using a diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The details of the primary and secondary antibodies are provided in Table S2. For each mouse, two non‑adjacent sections were evaluated with three non‑overlapping fields/section at 200 × (3 fields/mouse). Two board‑certified pathologists, blinded to group, independently scored each field using an immunoreactivity score (IRS): intensity 0–3 (0 negative, 1 weak, 2 moderate, 3 strong) × positive‑area grade 1–4 (1: 0–25%; 2: 26–50%; 3: 51–75%; 4: 76–100%), yielding 0–12. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The mean IRS of fields per‑mouse was kept as an integer and was used for one‑way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test (n = 6 for each group).

Western blot

Total protein extraction from cells or tissues was performed using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein samples were loaded onto a 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel, transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA), and blocked with 5% skim milk powder. The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by a 1.5-h incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature. Protein visualization was carried out using a Chemiluminescence Detection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). β-actin was used as an internal control. The details of the primary and secondary antibodies are provided in Table S2.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using MMLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, China). The reaction was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Kit (Takara Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primer sequences of the target genes and internal control genes (GAPDH or β-actin) are provided in Table S3. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔT method.

16S rDNA sequencing and data analysis

Fecal DNA extraction was performed using the MagPure Soil DNA LQ Kit (Magan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and integrity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The DNA samples were used as templates for PCR amplification of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene using Takara Ex Taq (Takara, Japan). The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 343F (5′-TACGGRAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 798R (5′-AGGGTATCTAATCCT-3′). The PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman, USA) and quantified with the Qubit dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 250 bp paired-end reads (Illumina Inc., CA). Species diversity was assessed using alpha diversity indices, including Chao1 and Shannon indices. Beta diversity was estimated using principal coordinates analysis. The taxonomic abundance spectra among groups were compared using the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

Fece samples were placed into Eppendorf tubes and incubated with a methanol solution. After ultrasonic treatment in an ice-water bath and overnight incubation, the samples were centrifuged, and the methanol solution was added again. Following another centrifugation, the supernatants were collected, filtered, and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. The metabolomic data were analyzed using an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus (Waters Corporation, USA) coupled with a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). An ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column was employed for both positive and negative ionization modes. The raw data were processed using Progenesis QI V2.3 software (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK). Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to assess the overall distribution of the samples. Differential metabolites between groups were identified based on the following criteria: (1) Variable Importance of Projection values > 1; (2) p value < 0.05 (two-tailed Student’s t-test).

Transcriptome sequencing

RNA sequencing was performed on whole intestinal tissue samples, rather than isolated epithelial fractions. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity, concentration, and integrity were assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), respectively. Libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit, and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on the criteria: p value < 0.05 and |Log2 fold change| > 1. The bioinformatics analysis results were visualized using R software (v. 3.6.1).

Serum metabolic detection

The serum levels of BHB and glucose were measured using a ketone body assay (Sigma, USA) and a glucose assay (Sigma, USA), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Intestinal permeability measurement

Intestinal permeability was assessed using the FITC-Dextran method. Briefly, after a 6-h fasting period, mice were administered FITC-Dextran (0.6 mg/g, Sigma, USA) via oral gavage. Blood samples were then collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm to isolate plasma. The plasma was mixed with PBS solution in an equal volume. Finally, the concentrations of FITC in the samples were measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

TdT-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) analysis

For the detection of apoptosis, paraffin-embedded tissue samples were sectioned into 5-μm-thick slices and hydrated after dewaxing. The sections were incubated with DNase-free proteinase K solution at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by staining with TUNEL solution (Roche Applied Science, Switzerland) and 4′,6-diamidino-2- phenylindole (DAPI, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 10 min. The positively stained cells were counted using a fluorescence microscope.

EdU pulse-chase assay

To assess intestinal epithelial proliferation and renewal, an EdU pulse-chase assay was performed. Mice from each group (post‑IR, n = 6 per group) received an intraperitoneal injection of 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU; 50 mg/kg in sterile PBS, Invitrogen, USA) on day 6 after irradiation. Mice were sacrificed 2 h after EdU injection. The intestinal tissues were collected, fixed, embedded in paraffin, cut into sections, washed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. The sections were washed with Click iT reaction mixture (Yeasen, China) at room temperature in the dark for 30 min, followed by washing with 3% BSA/PBS. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Sections were visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). For another independent steady state (non-IR) baseline comparison among ND, KD, and HSD, n = 3 mice per group were used. For all the statistical analysis, at least 10 well-oriented crypts per mouse were quantified from 3 non-overlapping regions per section of two sections per mouse. EdU-positive cells per crypt were counted by a blinded observer in ImageJ. The mean percentage of EdU-positive cells per crypt of each mouse was used for statistics.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The levels of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-17A in the serum of mice and cell supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits (Absin, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm for each well.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

For cell viability, cells were seeded into a 96-well culture plate and incubated with Cell Counting Kit-8 (Solarbio Life Sciences, China) for 2 h. The optical density at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected by flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were washed, re-suspended, and incubated with Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (Beyotime, China) or Dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescent probe (Beyotime, China) at room temperature. After staining, the samples were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Live/Dead cell double staining assay

For assessing cell viability and death, cells were washed and re-suspended in a staining buffer containing Calcein-AM and propidium iodide reagent (YEASEN, China). After incubation for 30 min at 37 °C, live (green) and dead (red) cells were counted using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To investigate whether RORγt binds to the IL-17A promoter, potential binding sites were predicted using the JASPAR database. The wild-type (WT) and mutant-type (MT) IL-17A promoter sequences containing the binding site were designed and provided in Table S4. A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed as described in our previous study18. The relative luciferase activity was calculated by normalizing the firefly luciferase fluorescence value to the Renilla luciferase fluorescence value.

IL-17A neutralization experiment

To evaluate whether IL-17A blockade alone can partly recapitulate the protective effects of KD, mice were randomly assigned to the following groups (n = 6 per group): ND, ND + IR, ND + IR + anti-IL-17A antibody, HSD + IR, HSD + IR + anti-IL-17A antibody. The dietary intervention was maintained for 16 weeks as described previously. All mice received TAI at 12 Gy on week 17. The anti-IL-17A antibody (Bio X Cell, USA) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 200 μg per mouse 1 day before irradiation and repeated on day 3 after irradiation. Mice were euthanized on day 6 post-IR.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6 software (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA) or IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0 software (IBM Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). The differences between the two groups were determined using an unpaired or paired two-tailed Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. The differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Games-Howell post-hoc comparison test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Results

KD inhibited mucosal inflammation and enhanced the intestinal barrier integrity in mice with RIII

The effects of KD on RIII were evaluated in mice fed ND, HSD, or KD for 16 weeks. At the start of week 17, the mice underwent IR treatment, and general signs were monitored over 6 consecutive days before sacrifice. The experimental flowchart is presented in Fig. S1A. Compared to the ND and HSD groups, the KD group exhibited the least weight gain by the end of the 16-week dietary intervention (Fig. S1B). The body weight of the KD group was significantly lower than that of both the ND and HSD groups (Fig. S1C). Additionally, the Lee index of the KD group was markedly reduced compared to the ND group, with no significant difference observed between the ND and HSD groups (Fig. S1D). The KD group also showed significantly higher BHB and lower serum glucose levels compared to the ND or HSD groups (Fig. S1E, F). In another independent experiment (3 mice per group), no significant difference was observed in the proportion of EdU-positive epithelial cells among the ND, KD, and HSD groups before IR (Fig. S1G, H). Post-IR treatment, weight loss was significantly reduced in the KD + IR group, whereas it accelerated in the HSD + IR group (Figs. 1A, B and S1I–L). IR exposure resulted in a continuous increase in DAI over 6 days (Fig. 1C). The DAI in the KD + IR group was significantly lower than in the ND + IR or HSD + IR groups, while the HSD + IR group exhibited a significantly higher DAI (Fig. 1D). Representative images of the harvested colon tissues are shown in Fig. 1E. Statistical analysis revealed that the colon length in the KD + IR group was significantly longer than that in the ND + IR and HSD + IR groups (Fig. 1F). KD treatment notably reduced serum concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in RIII mice, whereas HSD increased their levels (Fig. 1G–I). Intestinal permeability measurements showed that serum FITC concentration was significantly lower in the KD + IR group than in the ND + IR group, while the opposite was true for the HSD + IR group (Fig. 1J). Consistent with these observations, expression levels of intestinal tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin1, and Claudin3) were measured in colon tissues, confirming the effects of KD and HSD on barrier function at both mRNA (Figs. 1K and S1M–O) and protein levels (Fig. 1L). H&E staining revealed that the KD + IR group had more intact crypts in the intestinal epithelium compared to the ND + IR group, whereas the HSD + IR group displayed the opposite pattern (Fig. 1M). Further analysis showed that the KD + IR group had significantly more crypts than the ND + IR or HSD + IR group (Fig. 1N). TUNEL assay results indicated a lower number of apoptotic epithelial cells in the KD + IR group compared to the ND + IR group, while the HSD + IR group showed an increase in apoptotic cells (Fig. 1O, P). KD reshaped the gut microbiome and related metabolites in mice with RIII

Dietary interventions influence biological outcomes by modulating the gut microbiome. To investigate this, 16S rDNA sequencing was employed to analyze fecal microbiomes from RIII mice. As illustrated in Figs. 2A and S2A, the Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, observed_species, ACE, and PD_whole_tree indices were significantly lower in the HSD + IR and KD + IR groups compared to the ND + IR group, whereas the goods_coverage index exhibited the opposite trend. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) revealed significant differences in microbial community composition across the three groups (Fig. 2B). The total and unique OTU counts for each group are presented in Fig. S2B. At the family level, KD treatment notably increased the relative abundance of Akkermansiaceae and Atopobiaceae, while decreasing that of Desulfovibrionaceae and Monoglobaceae in RIII mice compared to ND treatment. In contrast, HSD treatment significantly elevated the relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, with a concurrent decrease in Butyricicoccaceae and Akkermansiaceae relative to ND treatment (Figs. 2C and S2C). At the genus level, KD treatment enhanced the abundance of Akkermansia, Alloprevotella, Muribaculaceae, Clostridia_vadinBB60_group, and Allobaculum, and reduced levels of UBA 1819, NK4A214_group, and Bilophila. Conversely, HSD treatment increased the relative abundance of Escherichia-Shigella while reducing the levels of Muribaculaceae, Akkermansia, Parasutterella, and Monoglobus relative to ND treatment (Fig. 2D). In the HSD + IR group, dominant species included Gammaproteobacteria, Enterobacterales, Enterobacteriaceae, and Escherichia-Shigella, whereas in the KD group, dominant species were Verrucomicrobiae, Verrucomicrobiales, Akkermansiaceae, and Akkermansia (Fig. 2E). To assess the effects of HSD and KD on fecal metabolites, LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted. Partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) revealed clear separation of metabolites across the ND + IR, HSD + IR, and KD + IR groups (Fig. S3A). Differential metabolites between the KD vs. ND and HSD vs. ND comparisons were identified and summarized in Fig. S3B and Fig. S3C, respectively, with a comprehensive overview of differential metabolites across multiple comparisons shown in Fig. S3D. The correlation analysis of the top 20 differential metabolites among the three groups is displayed in Fig. S3E, and detailed information on the top 50 differential metabolites is provided in Fig. S3F. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis highlighted cholesterol metabolism, GABAergic synapse, and taurine and hypotaurine metabolism as key pathways influenced by dietary interventions (Fig. S3G). Correlation analysis further revealed that Akkermansia was positively correlated with metabolites such as allantoxanamide, 5-butyl-2-methylpyridine, benzofuran ketone, L-histidinol, and methylenediphosphonic acid, while negatively correlated with alpha-muricholic acid and beta-muricholic acid (Fig. S3H). In contrast, Escherichia-Shigella showed a strong positive correlation with 3alpha-hydroxy-5beta-chola-7,9(11)-dien-24-oic acid but was significantly negatively correlated with 5-butyl-2-methylpyridine. Bilophila exhibited positive correlations with several metabolites, including chenodeoxycholic acid, alpha-muricholic acid, and beta-muricholic acid, while displaying negative correlations with metabolites such as allantoxanamide, dimethyl sulfoxide, benzofuran ketone, L-histidinol, and methylenediphosphonic acid.

Next, inflammation-related functional genes were screened in both comparisons. A total of 169 inflammation-associated DEGs were identified in the KD + IR vs. ND + IR comparison, while 221 inflammation-associated DEGs were found in the HSD + IR vs. ND + IR comparison (Fig. 3D). These genes were further analyzed using protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks. In the KD + IR vs. ND + IR comparison, key inflammation-regulating genes such as RORC, CXCL2, ICAM-1, and Vcam1 were connected with neighboring genes, which are known to play pivotal roles in inflammation (Fig. 3E). In the HSD + IR vs. ND + IR comparison, key genes included RORC, IL6, and FCGR2b (Fig. S4E). GO and KEGG pathway analyses revealed that genes in the KD + IR vs. ND + IR comparison were enriched in pathways such as the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, and TNF signaling (Fig. 3F), while the HSD + IR vs. ND + IR comparison revealed enrichment in pathways like the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and IL-17 production (Fig. S4F). To test this hypothesis, the mRNA levels of IL-17A in colon tissues from RIII mice were measured. The results revealed that HSD + IR increased, while KD + IR decreased, the IL-17A mRNA levels (Fig. 3H). A similar pattern was observed for serum IL-17A levels in RIII mice (Fig. 3I). These results collectively suggest that HSD + IR and KD + IR modulate IL-17A levels via the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. A previous study has shown that RORγt binds to the IL-17A gene promoter to enhance its transcription in human bronchial epithelial cells19. Transcriptome sequencing revealed that RORC was a leading functional DEG in both KD + IR vs. ND + IR and HSD + IR vs. ND + IR comparisons. Western blot analysis confirmed that KD decreased RORγt protein expression in the intestinal tissues of RIII mice, while HSD increased it (Fig. 3G). Finally, the expressions of JAK2, RORγt, and IL-17A, together with an epithelial marker (E-cadherin), were detected in the colonic tissues from ND, ND + IR, KD + IR, and HSD + IR mice using immunohistochemistry. Compared with the ND + IR group, both RORγt and IL-17A expression were significantly reduced in the KD + IR group, while the opposite was observed in the HSD + IR group (Fig. S5A, B).

IL-17A neutralization recapitulates partially the beneficial role of KD in irradiated mice receiving ND or HSD

To evaluate whether IL-17A blockade alone can recapitulate the protective effects of KD, mice were randomly assigned to the following groups (n = 6 per group): ND, ND + IR, ND + IR + IL-17A blockade, HSD + IR, HSD + IR + IL-17A blockade. The dietary and irradiation interventions were performed as described previously, and the IL-17A blockade was administered intraperitoneally 1 day before irradiation and 3 days after irradiation. After IR treatment, body weight loss was significantly reduced in the ND + IR + IL-17A blockade or HSD + IR + IL-17A blockade group, as compared to the matched control groups (Fig. S6A, B). Anti-IL-17A antibody treatment resulted in a continuous decrease in DAI over 6 days (Fig. S6C). The final DAI in the ND + IR + IL-17A blockade group was significantly lower than in the ND + IR groups, while a similar tendency was observed in the comparison between the HSD + IR + IL-17A blockade group and the HSD + IR group (Fig. S6D). The histological detection revealed that the ND + IR + IL-17A blockade group had more intact crypts and longer villi in the intestinal epithelium compared to the ND + IR group, and a similar tendency was observed in the comparison between the HSD + IR + IL-17A blockade group and the HSD + IR group (Fig. S6E, F). Representative images of the harvested colon tissues are shown in Fig. S6G. The statistical analysis revealed that IL-17A blockade treatment significantly increased the colon length of the dietary-treated group after IR intervention (Fig. S6H). The result of the FITC-dextran assay demonstrated that IL-17A blockade treatment reduced the intestinal permeability of the dietary-treated group after IR intervention (Fig. S6I). The TUNEL assay indicated less apoptotic epithelial cells after IL-17A blockade treatment (Fig. S6J, K). Finally, the ELISA assays demonstrated IL-17A blockade treatment decreased the serum level of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in irradiated mice receiving ND or HSD (Fig. S6L). Taken together, these results suggest that IL-17A neutralization partially recapitulates the beneficial role of KD in irradiated mice.

Discussion

Using RIII models both in vitro and in vivo, this study, for the first time, investigates the differential effects of KD and HSD on RIII and their associated molecular mechanisms. As depicted in Fig. 7C, in intestinal epithelium damaged by radiation, KD alters the gut microbiota and related metabolites, which in turn inactivates the JAK/STAT/RORγt signaling pathway and suppresses the transcription of the downstream IL-17A gene. This leads to reduced IL-17A production, resulting in improved barrier function and inhibition of the inflammatory response. In contrast, HSD exerts opposing effects by elevating IL-17A levels through activation of the JAK/STAT/RORγt signaling pathway. These findings underscore the significant role that dietary interventions play in the development of RIII and suggest several potential molecular targets for disease detection and treatment.

The HSD group was included as a representative pro-inflammatory diet to contrast the protective effects of KD, given its established role in promoting gut dysbiosis and exacerbating intestinal inflammation10,22. In the RIII mouse model, KD was found to protect barrier function, alleviate inflammation, and improve clinical symptoms such as weight loss and hematochezia, while HSD had the opposite effect. These results align with previous studies on their role in inflammation. For example, in a mouse model of experimental colitis, KD prevented weight loss, reduced inflammatory cytokines, and increased expression of TJ proteins16. In a rat model of irritable bowel syndrome, KD reduced intestinal inflammation by downregulating COX-2 and TLR-4 expression23. In a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, KD was associated with lower levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in patients24. In contrast, HSD promoted peritonitis in mice by increasing TNF-α, IL-6, and monocyte chemotactic protein-1 levels25. In the IL-10-deficient murine model of colitis, HSD increased the mRNA expression of several inflammatory cytokines, including IL-12b, IL-23a, and IL-1b26. Since BHB, a key component of ketone bodies produced by KD, is central to its effects, it was further examined in vitro27. In IR-treated normal intestinal epithelial cells, BHB treatment not only promoted cell growth and inhibited apoptosis and ROS release, but also upregulated TJ protein expression and reduced inflammatory cytokine production. In contrast, HSD treatment had the opposite effects. These observations align with a previous study indicating that BHB reduces IL-1β and IL-18 production by inactivating the NLRP3 inflammasome28. Taken together, our animal and cellular data collectively suggest that KD exerts a protective role in RIII, while HSD has a detrimental impact.

Accumulated evidence has suggested that dietary interventions can influence disease development through modulation of the gut microbiome and associated metabolites29. In this study, the results of 16S rDNA sequencing revealed that the ND + IR group showed a higher Shannon index but also a higher Simpson index compared to the KD + IR group. This reflects a greater overall richness of rare taxa and a more balanced community structure in ND + IR, while KD + IR shifted the community toward enrichment of a few beneficial dominant taxa, such as Akkermansia, leading to reduced overall richness and a more uneven but functionally protective microbiota composition. Akkermansia is well known for its anti-inflammatory properties and its ability to protect barrier integrity30. Akkermansia muciniphila, the most common species of Akkermansia, has shown therapeutic potential in RIII. For instance, it has been demonstrated to enhance intestinal epithelial barrier integrity in RIII mice by producing propionic acid31. Akkermansia muciniphila also contributes to the recovery of intestinal stem cells following radiation in mice32. On the other hand, HSD increased the abundance of several pro-inflammatory bacteria, such as Enterobacteriaceae, and decreased the abundance of beneficial taxa, such as Lactobacillus. Enterobacteriaceae, a family of Gram-negative bacteria, is known to produce endotoxins like lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which can activate inflammatory signaling pathways and impair barrier function, leading to intestinal inflammation and obesity-associated diseases33. Increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae may also delay intestinal recovery following chemotherapeutic injury34. The Lactobacillus strains, well-known probiotics, play a pivotal role in immune regulation and the maintenance of tight junctions35. For example, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG has been shown to inactivate the inflammatory cGAS/STING pathway, reducing M1 pro-inflammatory macrophage and CD8 + T cell infiltration in RIII mice36. Additionally, Lactobacillus plantarum promotes DNA damage repair in irradiated intestinal epithelial cells through activation of the farnesoid X receptor–fibroblast growth factor 15 signaling pathway37. A subsequent correlation analysis based on metabolomics revealed potential associations between these bacteria and various metabolites. For instance, Akkermansia was positively correlated with an increased level of L-histidinol, a compound that has been shown to enhance anti-cancer efficacy and reduce the toxicity of chemotherapeutics38,39. This bacterium was also negatively correlated with the level of alpha-muricholic acid, which may be linked to the occurrence of toxin-induced colorectal cancer40.

To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, transcriptome sequencing was performed, revealing that KD and HSD regulated the JAK-STAT and IL-17 signaling pathways. Given the established link between the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and intestinal inflammation, it was selected for further validation41–43. The results indicated that KD significantly reduced the expression of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 in the colon tissues of IR-treated mice, whereas HSD had the opposite effect. These findings were also confirmed in the cellular model treated with BHB or HS. In an experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis mouse model, KD reduced the ratios of p-JAK2/JAK2 and p-STAT3/STAT3, partially supporting our observations44. Additionally, our results showed that KD decreased IL-17A levels in both the colon tissues and serum samples of IR-treated mice, while the opposite effect was observed with HSD. IL-17A, produced by group 3 innate lymphoid cells, exacerbates inflammation in an experimental colitis mouse model45. It also increases epithelial permeability by recruiting neutrophils, thus accelerating colonic injury45. In a colitis model, KD decreased IL-17A levels, protected barrier function, and alleviated mucosal inflammation14. Conversely, HSD exacerbated colitis in an IL-17A-dependent manner22. Notably, several studies have suggested that activated JAK2/STAT3 signaling contributes to increased IL-17A production46–48. Therefore, KD and HSD may modulate JAK2/STAT3 signaling to regulate IL-17A production, ultimately exerting their inhibitory or promoting effects on RIII. To further elucidate the mechanism, RORγt, a key transcription factor for the IL-17A gene, was examined, which was also identified as a significant DEG in our transcriptome analysis49. In salt-sensitive rats, HSD promoted the enrichment of RORγt on the promoter region of the IL-17A gene, leading to increased IL-17A expression and subsequently inducing hypertension50. Both RORγt and IL-17A expression were significantly elevated in human normal intestinal lamina propria mononuclear cells treated with sodium chloride51. In our study, KD decreased RORγt expression in the intestinal tissues of RIII mice, while HSD increased it. Furthermore, RORγt overexpression significantly enhanced the transcriptional activity of the IL-17A gene in NCM460 cells. These findings suggest that HSD increases IL-17A levels through RORγt-mediated transcriptional activation, which can be inhibited by KD. Since the JAK2/STAT3 pathway has been shown to upregulate RORγt expression and increase IL-17A production in renal fibrosis, this study validated this in IR-treated NCM460 cells51. Colivelin, a STAT3 agonist, rescued BHB-induced suppression of RORγt and IL-17A, while C188-9, a STAT3 inhibitor, antagonized HS-induced RORγt upregulation and IL-17A production. Taken together, these findings suggest that HSD activates the JAK2/STAT3 pathway to upregulate RORγt expression, which in turn promotes the transcription of the IL-17A gene and increases its levels. This process can be inhibited by KD.

Finally, the role of diet-altered gut microbiomes on RIII was confirmed using an FMT mouse model. These findings suggest that KD and HSD likely alter the gut microbiome and related metabolites, which, in turn, modulate pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and influence the development of RIII. However, to our knowledge, few studies have specifically addressed the role of particular bacteria and their metabolites in RIII, highlighting the need for further mechanistic investigations.

This study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. Firstly, it is important to note that the present study focused on the acute injury phase, with evaluation at only 6 days post-irradiation. Accordingly, our conclusions are restricted to the acute phase. Future studies will extend the observation window to 2 weeks or longer to capture the regenerative phase and to assess the durability of effects. Meanwhile, KD was administered before irradiation as a preventive intervention, and whether initiating KD or BHB treatment after radiation exposure has any therapeutic potential needs further investigation. Secondly, our diet comparisons lack an isocaloric/isomacronutrient control diet matched to KD. Such non‑ketogenic high‑fat comparators, with matched fat content but adjusted carbohydrate ratios and defined fatty‑acid composition, are needed to distinguish effects of the ketogenic metabolic state from high‑fat intake per se. Thirdly, this study focused on the suppressive role of KD in the JAK2/STAT3 pathway; however, it may also influence other inflammatory signaling cascades such as NF-κB and MAPK, which are known to contribute to intestinal injury. Further studies should focus on these pathways to provide a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding. Fourthly, our cellular experiments indicated increased IL-17A expression or production in irradiated intestinal epithelial cells, while IL-17A is typically produced by Th17 cells and innate lymphoid cells rather than epithelial cells. Whether radiation induces IL-17A primarily in immune or epithelial compartments and whether KD regulates IL-17A in immune cells in a similar mechanism remains unclear and is hoped to be clarified by utilizing single-cell sequencing in our future work. Fifthly, our mechanistic validation was performed in the NCM460 cell line, while human colonic organoid models are more suitable because of their ability to recapitulate epithelial heterogeneity and function, enhancing translational relevance. Moreover, only BHB was selected in cellular validations, and other KD-associated metabolites may also contribute to the protective effects, which need further investigation. Lastly, our current design lacks a non-irradiated normal diet group as a healthy baseline to more fully characterize gut microbiota diversity. Future studies should incorporate this group to provide a clearer understanding of baseline microbial composition prior to irradiation. Also, while our correlation analyses suggest that KD-modified microbiota may drive protective metabolite profiles, we did not directly test causality through mono-colonization or metabolite supplementation, which should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that KD alters the gut microbiota to inhibit the JAK2/STAT3/RORγt signaling pathway, which in turn reduces IL-17A production and mitigates acute RIII. In contrast, the opposite effect was observed with HSD. These findings not only provide novel insights into the role of dietary interventions in RIII but also suggest potential therapeutic targets for the disease, such as gut microbiota and IL-17A signaling.
