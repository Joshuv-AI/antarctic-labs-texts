First report of carbapenems encoding multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria from a pediatric hospital in Gaza Strip, Palestine
Authors: Nabil Abdullah El Aila, Nahed Ali Al Laham, Swapnil Prakash Doijad, Can Imirzalioglu, Mobarak Abu Mraheil
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe worldwide prevalence of multi-drug resistance (MDR) in Gram-negative bacteria (GNB), particularly related to extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) and carbapenemases, poses significant global public health and clinical challenges.ObjectivesTo characterize ESBL-producing Gram-negative bacilli, within a pediatric hospital in Gaza using whole genome sequencing (WGS).MethodsA total of 158 clinical isolates of Gram-negative bacilli were collected from Al-Nasser Pediatric Hospital. These isolates were tested for ESBL production using the double disk synergy test. The antibiotic susceptibility profile was determined using the Kirby Bauer method following the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute guidelines. Selected 15 phenotypically MDR isolates were whole-genome sequenced and characterized for their genome-based species identity and antibiotic resistance gene profile.ResultsOf the 158 isolates, 93 (58.9%) were positive for ESBL production. The frequency of Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Proteus mirabilis, and Serratia marcescens was 50%, 22.7%, 22.7%, 1.8%, 1.2%, and 1.2% respectively. The prevalence of ESBL among urine, pus, blood, and sputum was 64%, 44%, 23%, and 63.6%, respectively. Chloramphenicol, Imipenem, and Meropenem were the most effective antibiotics against ESBL producers.In sequenced isolates, an average of six anti-microbial resistance (AMR) genes were noted per isolate, where one of them carried up to 13 antibiotic resistance genes. Carbapenem resistance genes such as blaKPC-2(6.6%), blaPDC-36/12 (6.6%), and blaPOM-1 (6.6%) were detected. All the sequenced E. coli isolates (n = 8) showed multiple resistance genes, mainly against β-lactamase (25.0%), aminoglycosides (37.5%), sulfonamides (37.5%), and genes conferring resistance to tetracyclines (25.0). ConclusionOur results showed a high prevalence of ESBL-producing GNB isolated from a pediatric hospital in the Gaza Strip. Various antibiotic resistance genes were identified, including those encoding ESBL and carbapenems. The results highlight the significant challenge posed by MDR in GNB and emphasize the need for effective antibiotic strategies. Given the high endemicity observed in various studies from Palestine, it is important to conduct clinical and molecular epidemiology research to identify risk factors, transmission patterns, and clinical outcomes associated with GNB strains that carry ESBL and carbapenem resistance genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03550-8.

Introduction

The emergence of infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens has become a major problem within the hospital and community, especially in children [1, 2]. Pediatric patients are more susceptible to infectious diseases worldwide primarily due to MDR or extremely drug-resistant (XDR) gram-negative bacteria (GNB) [3]. Increased morbidity and an estimated 40% mortality in developing countries are attributed to MDR among clinically significant GNB such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter spp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii [4, 5]. Resistance mechanisms to a specific antibiotic may be intrinsic (when a microorganism lacks target sites for the antimicrobial or encodes innate resistance capabilities) or acquired. The acquired resistance involves the presence of enzymes that inactivate the antimicrobial, post-transcriptional, or post-translational modifications of the target site, or reduced uptake and active efflux of the antimicrobial; these mechanisms originate from the acquisition of exogenous DNA or mutation of endogenous DNA [6, 7]. Due to the limited availability of antibiotics, indiscriminate drug use, poor living conditions, inadequate dietary intake, and lax government oversight, MDR or XDR GNB are more prevalent in developing countries [8, 9].

Extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL) are enzymes produced by many bacterial species as a means of defense against β -lactam drugs, with the genes encoding for those enzymes being mainly located on mobile genetic elements. ESBL enzymes confer antimicrobial resistance to a wide spectrum of β-lactams such as penicillin, aztreonam, as well as first, second, and third-generation cephalosporins [10, 11]. β-lactamases are widespread and were detected in a wide range of Enterobacterales [10] predominantly in E. coli, and K. pneumoniae, as well as in other non-Enterobaterales, such as P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii [12–15].

β-lactamases fall into four different molecular classes: A, B, C, and D. While most β-lactamases have a serine in their active site, class B β-lactamases are metalloenzymes that require a Zinc molecule [16, 17]. CTX-M, TEM and SHV belong to class A beta-lactamases. CTX-M is the most prevalent ESBL family in Enterobacterales but TEM and SHV enzymes in addition to members of the three classes of β-lactamases (B, C, D) are also common [18].

Carbapenems are commonly used to treat infections caused by ESBL-producing Enterobacterales. High prevalence rates of ESBL-producing isolates have forced the use of carbapenems, which subsequently resulted in an elevated number of carbapenem-resistant isolates. Among acquired resistance, carbapenems resistance is associated with the production of carbapenemases including class A KPCs, class B metallo- β-lactamases (VIM, IMP, or NDM-1) or the class D OXA-type enzymes [19–21].

In recent years, a high prevalence of ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing GNBs has been observed worldwide, posing a concern for developing countries. A higher rate of infectious complications and death occurs in children with MDR bacteremia, who are also more likely to receive inadequate initial antibiotic therapy [22]. These findings point to the urgent need to encourage antimicrobial stewardship, decrease unnecessary antibiotic use, and avoid horizontal gene transfer in pediatric patients.

Little is known, however, about the various ESBL and carbapenemase genes linked to these phenotypes in Gram-negative bacilli isolated in Gaza pediatric hospitals. Additionally, little information comes from this area about the correlation between these genotypes and the observed phenotypes. Such information is crucial for evaluating the impact of combined phenotypic and genetic tests on clinical care and for improving our knowledge of how resistance genes affect the epidemiology of circulating pathogens.

A potentially fruitful step towards a better understanding of the mechanism underlying antibiotic resistance in GNB isolated from our pediatric hospitals would be identifying genes involved in resistance and virulence factors. This work could also lay the groundwork for future clinical management and treatment options.

The aim of the present study was to investigate the prevalence of antibiotic resistance genes in GNB isolated from different clinical samples from a pediatric hospital in Gaza Strip using whole genome sequencing.

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted using 158 clinical isolates of Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) obtained from Al-Nasser Pediatric Hospital, the primary referral pediatric hospital in the Gaza Strip. The samples were obtained from urine, pus, sputum, blood, and ear discharges, collected between August and December 2021. Informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in this study prior to their inclusion. The samples were meticulously collected from Al-Nasser Hospital, following stringent ethical standards and securing the required approval from the respected ethical committee. This hospital, known for its dedication to patient care and research excellence, was a crucial source for the collection of clinical isolates. The study received approval from the Department of Human Resources and Development in the Ministry of Health – Gaza, as well as from the local Helsinki committee, with approval number PHRC/HC/766/20.

Culture and identification of clinical isolates

All collected clinical isolates were sub-cultured on MacConkey agar and incubated aerobically at 37 °C overnight. The bacterial isolates were then identified based on cultural characteristics and various biochemical reactions, including the API 20E test.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was carried out using a modified Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method according to CLSI guidelines [23] on Mueller-Hinton agar. Before inoculation, a swab stick was dipped into a bacterial suspension with turbidity matching the 0.5 McFarland standard. The antibiotics tested in this study included amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (20/10 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), amikacin (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), imipenem (10 µg), amoxicillin (30 µg), cefuroxime (30 µg), co-trimoxazole (25 µg), aztreonam (30 µg), meropenem (10 µg), piperacillin/tazobactam (100/10 µg), piperacillin (100 µg), and chloramphenicol (30 µg) (Himedia, India). The inhibition zones for each antibiotic were interpreted and reported as resistant, intermediate, or susceptible according to the CLSI 2017 guidelines.

Phenotypic detection of esbls using double disk synergy test

A phenotypic confirmatory test for ESBL producers was performed using the double disc synergy test. The test organism was spread onto a Mueller-Hinton agar plate, and the antibiotics ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), and ceftazidime (30 µg) were placed 20 mm apart (edge to edge) from an amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (20/10 µg) disc in the center of the plate. After a 24-hour incubation period, a positive result was indicated by an enhanced zone of inhibition between any of the cephalosporin antibiotics and the amoxicillin/clavulanic acid disc, demonstrating synergistic activity with clavulanic acid and the presence of an ESBL. Quality assurance was conducted weekly using K. pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 (ESBL-producing isolate) and E. coli ATCC 25,922 (susceptible isolate) as positive and negative controls, respectively [24].

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from cultured isolates using the alkaline lysis method as previously described [25]. In short, a single bacterial colony was suspended in 20 µl of lysis buffer (0.25% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 0.05 N NaOH) and heated at 95 °C for 15 min. The cell lysate was then diluted with 180 µl of sterilized distilled water. The cell debris were pelleted by centrifugation at 16,000xg for 5 min, and the supernatants were frozen at -20 °C until further use.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and bioinformatic analysis

Fifteen of the 158 phenotypically characterized isolates (Table 1) were subjected to whole-genome sequencing and subsequent analysis. DNA for WGS was isolated from overnight cultures using the PureLink® Pro 96 Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, Dreieich, Germany). For short-read sequencing, an Illumina Nextera XT library was prepared and sequenced on a NextSeq 500 device (Illumina, Eindhoven, Netherlands) using the NextSeq 500/550 Mid Output v2 kit (2 × 150 nucleotides read length). Reads were filtered for adapter sequences and low-quality bases by trimmomatic v0.36 [26], and assembled de novo using Unicycler v0.4.8 assembler [27]. Only contigs > 500 bp were considered for further analysis. Annotations were carried out using PROKKA v1.11 [28].

Isolates were identified by BLAST of 16 S rDNA sequence obtained from assemblies against the 16 S rRNA sequence database of NCBI) and based on the raw reads by the Kraken2 tool. Furthermore, isolates were cross-checked for their identity using the TYGS server (https://tygs.dsmz.de/), which compares the genomes to type-strain and calculates the in-silico DNA-DNA hybridization score (Meier-Kolthoff and Göker, 2019). The MASH distance-based [29] phylogeny was constructed using GGRaSP package [30]. Virulence gene profiling was performed by aligning amino-acid sequences of all genes (using 70% query coverage and 80% nucleotide identity) against the ‘Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [31] using the Diamond v0.8.36.98 tool [32]. Antibiotic resistance gene profiling was carried out using NCBI’s AMRfinder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathogens/antimicrobial-resistance/AMRFinder/) as well as by ‘Resistance Gene Identifier’ v5.05.5 against CARD database (v3.0.3) [33]. Plasmid contigs were predicted by using platon tool v1.6 [34] and plasmid types were identified using https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PlasmidFinder/service (DOI: 10.1007/978-1-4939-9877-7_20 ). The MLST was carried out using https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MLST/ service ( doi: 10.12.0/JCM.06094-11). All the bioinformatics tools were run with default parameters unless specified.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20. Frequencies, cross-tabulations, and the Chi-square test were performed to determine statistical significance, with a threshold set at a P-value less than 0.05.

Results

Phenotypic characterization

During the study, 158 isolates of Gram-negative bacilli were obtained from Al-Nasser pediatric hospital in Gaza Strip. Out of the 158 Isolates, 93 (58.9%) were positive for ESBL production whereas 65 (41.1%) were non-ESBL.

The frequency of E. coli, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, P. mirabilis, and S. marcescens was 50%, 22.7%, 22.7%, 1.-9%, 1. 3%, and 1. 3% respectively (Table 1). K. pneumoniae was the leading ESBL-producing bacteria isolated from Al-Nasser pediatric hospital, with a prevalence rate reaching 83%. The prevalence rate of ESBL production was 62% among E. coli, 33% among P. aeruginosa, and the two isolates of P. mirabilis were ESBL producers (Table 1).

The distribution of isolates by type of specimen was 71.5% for urine, 13.9% for pus, 7.6% for blood and 7% for sputum (Table 1). The prevalence of ESBL among these specimens was 65.6%, 45.5%, 16.7% and 63.6%, respectively. As represented in Table 1, the bacterial isolates that recovered from urine samples had the highest prevalence of ESBL (65.5%), followed by the bacterial isolates that recovered from sputum samples (63.3%).

Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns

Chloramphenicol, Amikacin, Imipenem, and Meropenem were the most effective antibiotics against ESBL producers, respectively effective in 51%, 49%, 47%, and 40% of ESBL isolates. ESBL-producing microorganisms showed high resistance against cefuroxime (96%) and cefotaxime (91%). Also, they were highly resistant to amoxicillin (92%) and piperacillin (92%).

Compared to non-ESBL, piperacillin/tazobactam and aztreonam also showed high rates of sensitivity (74% and 71%, respectively), while the highest rate of resistance was obtained with amoxicillin, piperacillin, and amoxicillin/ clavulanic acid (78%, 74% and 63% respectively; S1 Table). Moreover, in comparison with non-ESBL isolates, ESBL isolates showed a significantly higher increase of resistance for most of the tested antimicrobial agents in this study (P-value ˂ 0.05), except for amoxicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, amikacin, cephalexin, and meropenem (S1 Table).

Out of 158 bacterial isolates, 122 (77.2%) were classified as MDR isolates, and one was pan-resistant (S2 Table).

The overall resistance percentage of Amoxicillin, piperacillin, cefuroxime, and cefotaxime among all isolates was 86%, 84.1%, 80.3%, and 79.1%, respectively. Moreover, the least resistance rate was among piperacillin/tazobactam, imipenem, and meropenem at 22.7%, 27.8 and 37.3%, respectively (Fig. 1). _.

Genome-sequence-based identification of the isolates

To explore the genomic features of the resistant isolates, we randomly selected fifteen isolates from the top three genera (Escherichia, Klebsiella, and Pseudomonas) prevalent in our study. All the sequencing data is deposited in the NCBI database and available publicly under the project accession number PRJNA984638.

The 16 S rDNA, Kraken2, and isDDH-based genomics approaches concordantly identified isolates to a consistent genus, while the is isDDH approach could determine the precise allocation of the species. Four bacterial species were identified: Escherichia coli (n = 8), Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 4), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n = 2), and a single strain of Pseudomonas furukawaii (Table 2). Screening of genomes to detect antimicrobial resistance genes (args)

Genomic assemblies were screened for antibiotic-resistance genes using the AMRFinderPlus tool. Twenty-nine genes belonging to 14 subclasses and 10 classes of antibiotic resistance were observed across the fifteen isolates (Fig. 2). Up to thirteen antibiotic-resistance genes per isolate were detected. Genes conferring resistance to β-lactam such as blaSHV and blaTEM, for cephalosporins such as blaOXA and blaCTX−M−3/33/15, and for carbapenems such as blaKPC−2, blaPDC−36/12, and blaPOM−1 were observed (Fig. 2). Besides β -lactams, antibiotic resistance genes for important antibiotics such as Streptomycin or Gentamycin (aph(3’’)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, and aadA5), Tetracycline (tet-A, -B and -D) trimethoprim (dfrA), sulfonamide (Sul) and macrolide (erm(B) and mph(A)), fosfomycin (fosA) and chloramphenicol (catB) were also noted among these isolates. E. coli isolates 130, 137, 139, 132, 67, and 32 carried gyrA mutation D87G and/or S83L, while S80L parC mutation was observed among the isolates 137, 139, 132, and 67. Observed mutations were known to cause resistance toward fluoroquinolone. Typically, The gyr and par genes are known to be located on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids. Given that the genomes were sequenced through a short-read sequencing technique, next, we looked at the evidence for plasmid contigs. Screening for the contigs using the Platon tool revealed that all carried plasmid contigs except for one isolate (id-09, identified as Pseudomonas aeruginosa). Further, plasmid typing revealed that E. coli and K. pneumoniae carried different types of plasmids that included replicon types IncFIB, IncFII, IncHI1B, IncX3, repB, Col156, and incQ1. Plasmids of isolates 149, 87, and 52 did not be assigned to any types. Notably, plasmid types were consistent among MLST type 8, i.e., IncFIB, IncFII.

Screening of the virulence genes

We conducted a comprehensive screening of the virulence genes in the sequenced isolates. A high number of virulence genes were noted among E. coli and Pseudomonas (S3 Table). In brief, E. coli showed a considerable presence of genes associated with the type-6 secretion system (including II, III, and ACE), flagellar structures (encompassing lateral, peritrichous, polar, and chemotaxis-related components), and various fimbriae (AFA-I and -II, Type-1, P, SFP, and F9). Additionally, genes linked to heme/iron utilization (such as chu, hemin uptake proteins, heme Biosynthesis, ferrous iron transport system, and siderophores- yersiniabactin, enterobactin, and aerobactin) were notably abundant in E. coli.

Klebsiella isolates harbored a distinct set of genes, primarily featuring a single T6SS variant, namely T6SS-III. Unlike E. coli, Klebsiella exhibited relatively lower abundance in flagellar and fimbrial genes, being characterized mainly by peritrichous and polar flagellar genes. In terms of fimbriae, Klebsiella species carried genes specific to Type-I fimbriae, while the exclusive presence of type-III fimbrial genes was observed. Noteworthy genes related to yersinibactin, ferrous iron transport system, heme biosynthesis, and enterobactin were also identified in Klebsiella species.

Pseudomonas demonstrated a distinct virulence gene profile in comparison to both E. coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Among the Pseudomonas species, namely aeruginosa, and furukawaii, genes related to the Hxc-, Hcp- and Xcp-secretion systems, HIS-I, and characteristic T3SS were prevalent. Pseudomonas species, akin to E. coli, exhibited a diverse array of flagellar genes. P. aeruginosa specifically displayed genes encoding CU and type-IV fimbriae. Notably, genes associated with pili (Type IV and Flp) were exclusively identified in Pseudomonas. Furthermore, P. aeruginosa displayed an exclusive presence of pyocheline and pyoverdine, has A-type proteins, and a direct heme uptake system, all associated with heme/iron utilization. Gene cassette for alginate biosynthesis was present in isolates of both species.

Across all species, we consistently observed the presence of capsular and lipopolysaccharide genes. It is important to note that individual isolates carried unique virulence gene profiles (S3 Table).”

Discussion

Gaza Strip is a small territory on the Mediterranean coast. It is a part of Palestine and is considered one of the lower middle-income developing regions according to the World Bank classification [36]. Historically, the Gaza Strip has suffered and is still suffering for more than 50 years. Since 2006, Gaza has been a semi-autonomous region, subject to border and travel restrictions enforced by Israel and Egypt and subject to many attacks by Israel. Accordingly, the regional countries imposed a severe siege and blockade on the Gaza Strip and almost complete closure of all the borders that control the transport of people and goods, except for infrequent humanitarian purposes. The Rafah border crossing in Egypt frequently acts as a gateway for the transfer of individuals and commodities. However, its closure has become more frequent in recent times. This has led to a situation where the inhabitants of Gaza are somewhat more secluded, and consequently, they can be described as a population enduring a significant humanitarian crisis. The entire Gaza Strip has been under a strict blockade and siege for the past 19 years [37]. The blockade on Gaza has severely restricted access to essential medical supplies, including antibiotics and other crucial medications. This scarcity forces healthcare providers to ration treatments, often leading to sub-dosing or the use of less effective antibiotics. Such practices can exacerbate the treatment of infectious diseases, as incomplete or improper dosing may not fully eradicate infections, allowing bacteria to survive and potentially develop resistance. Over time, this can lead to the emergence of more drug-resistant strains, making infections harder to treat and increasing the burden of disease in the region.

Gram-negative bacilli infections are the most serious and potentially life-threatening causes of infectious disease in hospitalized patients [38]. Hospitalized patients, especially in neonates and pediatric wards, are highly susceptible to nosocomial infections. The environmental and work conditions of the neonatal intensive care units impose another risk factor and can be the major cause of infection. The prevalence of many nosocomial infections in our hospitals in the Gaza Strip has been reported in the last decade, but few of them have been documented [37, 39–42]. Data regarding multidrug-resistant GNB isolated from hospitals in Palestine are generally scant [43–47]. Previous reports have mostly focused on Northern Palestine and the West Bank [44, 48–51], with relatively little detail regarding the molecular epidemiology of GNB from Gaza Strip hospitals [41–43, 52].

In this study, we phenotypically and genotypically characterized the Gram-negative bacteria isolated from a pediatric hospital in the Gaza Strip, Palestine. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing the molecular epidemiology of MDR Gram-negative bacteria isolated from a pediatric hospital in the Gaza Strip, Palestine using whole genome sequencing. Using phenotyping methods, the prevalence of ESBL-producing GNB isolates was 58.9% which is in accordance with our recent findings (51.6%) in a study conducted in four pediatric hospitals in 2019 in the Gaza Strip [43]. Our results were higher in comparison with previous reports from both parts of Palestine, the Gaza Strip, and the West Bank [15, 48, 49, 53, 54], however, a higher prevalence was found in a recent previous study that investigated the prevalence of ESBL among K. pneumoniae and E. coli in the Gaza Strip [46]. In the present study, there was variation in ESBL production according to the type of isolated bacteria and the clinical specimens that were used. The leading ESBL producer was K. pneumoniae, with a prevalence rate of 83% followed by E. coli with 62% (Table 1). This result was a little bit higher than our recent findings from a pediatric patient population in the Gaza Strip, where the prevalence was 63.4% and 55.3% among K. pneumoniae and E. coli, respectively [43]. However, lower prevalence was found in many studies around the world, especially from developed countries such as the US and Europe [12]. Regarding the specimen type, Urine was the highest specimen where we isolated ESBL-producing GNB with a prevalence rate reaching 65.5%. It was followed by sputum specimens with a prevalence rate of 63.3% (Table 1). In our recent previous study in the Gaza Strip among four pediatric hospitals, the highest rate of ESBL was recovered from pus specimens (55.1%), and in another study in Oman, the urine specimens were the main source of ESBLs (70.8%), followed by blood specimens (15%) [55]. These variations may be due to the percentage of the specimen types in comparison to others in each study.

In this study, carbapenem antimicrobial agents such as imipenem and meropenem, chloramphenicol, and piperacillin/tazobactam were the most effective antimicrobials against both the ESBL and the non-ESBL isolates. However, a high resistance rate was noticed to amoxicillin (92%), cefuroxime (96%), and cefotaxime (91%) by the ESBL isolates (S1 Table). These findings were in agreement with our recent study, where we demonstrated that the carbapenem agent meropenem and amikacin were the most effective antimicrobial agents against ESBL-positive isolates (83.1% and 82.5%, respectively), while the least effective were amoxicillin and cephalexin with resistance rates reaching 96.9% and 86.8% respectively [43]. We noticed that ESBL producers, in comparison to non-ESBL, have a significantly higher resistance rate for almost all tested antimicrobial agents. Many studies from Palestine and other countries showed that carbapenems as imipenem, and meropenem, and amikacin were the most effective antimicrobial agents against the isolated ESBL producers in these studies, and most of these studies also demonstrated higher resistance of ESBL in comparison with non-ESBL GNB isolates [43, 45, 46, 48, 56–59].

In recent years, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) has become more accessible and widely used in fields like clinical microbiology. WGS allows for detailed characterization of bacterial genomes, aiding in accurate diagnosis, understanding bacterial transmission, and guiding prompt therapies [60]. It also improves taxonomic identification, especially among closely related bacterial species that are difficult to differentiate by traditional methods [61] In this study, WGS was used to precisely identify the species of 15 multidrug-resistant bacterial isolates, enhancing the accuracy of phenotypic identification, which was previously limited to genus-level identification. The observed diversity in MLST sequence types among the isolated multidrug-resistant GNB highlights the complexity of bacterial populations within the pediatric hospital setting. The prevalence of distinct STs in P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli raises questions about the potential transmission pathways of these multidrug-resistant bacteria. The varying MLST profiles suggest mostly non-clonal spread within the hospital environment. It is crucial to conduct further epidemiological investigations to elucidate the routes of transmission, identify potential reservoirs, and establish effective containment strategies. Next, we screened genomes for antibiotic resistance genes. Isolates of K. pneumoniae, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa carried several antibiotic resistance genes, and carried up to 13 antibiotic resistance genes. Carbapenem resistance genes such as blaKPC-2, blaNDM-19, and blaPOM-1 were observed. This is the first time such carbapenem resistance genes from Palestine, and specifically from the Gaza Strip, have been found in clinical GNB. Previous reports published from the Gaza Strip by Al Laham et al., reported VIM metallo-β-lactamase-producing Alcaligenes faecalis clinical strains and OXA-48-producing multidrug-resistant Proteus mirabilis strain [41, 42]. Tayh et al., detected VIM metallo-β-lactamase production in E. coli and K. pneumoniae clinical isolates [47] while Sjölander et al., detected NDM-2-producing A. baumannii. VIM-producing Pseudomonas aeruginosa clinical strains were isolated from Gaza Strip and West Bank of Palestine [52]. This is the first time to detect a carbapenem-resistant KPN K. pneumoniae (blaKPC-2) in a pediatric hospital in the Gaza Strip. This isolate (no. 142) also harbored a variety of antibiotics resistance genes, including blaSHV-11, blaTEM-1, blaCTX-M-15, fosA7, mph(A), catB3, arr-3, sul1/2, tet(A)/(B)/(D), dfrA14, and aac(3)-IId. In recent years, the emergence of carbapenem-resistant GNB in the Mediterranean Basin has become an alarming problem [62], , where carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae is designated as one of the microorganisms that pose an urgent threat to public health worldwide that can spread rapidly in healthcare settings, and cause numerous human infections such as urinary, respiratory, and blood-stream infections [63]. In a study conducted in Lebanon characterizing an ESBL-producing K. pneumonia by using WGS revealed that the isolate harbored different types of β-lactamase genes, including blaoxa−1, blaCTX−M−15, blaSHV−11, and blaTEM−1b. Moreover, the isolate was also characterized by the concomitant presence of other resistance determinants, most notably acc(6′)-lb-cr and qnrb1 [64]. In a study conducted in Mexico, thirty-eight strains of K. pneumoniae isolated from the blood of pediatric patients were characterized by whole-genome sequencing and genomic clustering methods. Genes encoding β-lactamase were found in all the bacterial isolates, among which the blaSHV variant was the most prevalent in the sequenced genomes. However, oqxA or qnrB genes, which conferred resistance to quinolones and genes that conferred resistance to tetracycline [tet(A), tet(B), tet(C), tet(D), ortet(E)] were present in 97% and 45% of the strains, respectively [60]. In South Africa, a WGS study was performed to molecularly characterize nine K. pneumoniae isolates. They harbored several β-lactamase genes, including blaCTX-M-15, blaTEM-1b, blaSHV-1, blaOXA-1 concomitantly with many other resistance genes as acc(6′)-lb-cr, aadAI6, oqxA and oqxB that confer resistance to aminoglycosides and/or fluoroquinolones, respectively [4]. In this study, all the E. coli isolates tested by WGS showed multiple resistance genes, mainly β-lactamase, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, sulfonamides, and genes conferred resistance to tetracyclines. In a recent study conducted for Enterobacterales isolates from children in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, WGS was performed on 42 fluoroquinolone resistant-ESBL producing isolates and revealed that 90.5% of the isolates carried one or more plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) genes, where the most frequent PMQR genes were aac(6’)-lb-cr (74%), followed by qnrB1 (40%), oqx, qnrB6 and qnS1 [65]. Our findings using WGS were almost in agreement with those aforementioned studies from developing and developed countries on multidrug-resistant GNB.

The comparative exploration of the virulence gene profiles across E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas species offers a rich perspective on the genetic diversity underlying their pathogenic strategies. This investigation elucidates the genetic mechanisms that underpin their ability to cause infections and interact with their respective host environments. E. coli, a quintessential member of the Enterobacteriaceae family, exhibited a remarkable array of virulence genes. The conspicuous prevalence of genes encoding the type-6 secretion system (T6SS) components, including variants II, III, and ACE, underscores the potential significance of this secretion apparatus in E. coli’s pathogenicity. The T6SS’s pivotal role in translocating virulence effectors into host cells enhances bacterial survival and colonization. The wide spectrum of flagellar and fimbrial genes implies adaptability to diverse niches, facilitating motility, attachment, colonization, and adhesion. Moreover, the abundance of heme/iron utilization genes highlights the bacterium’s evolved mechanisms for thriving within the iron-restricted host environment (10.1093/femsre/fuac031).

In stark contrast, K. pneumoniae, also belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family, showcased a unique virulence gene repertoire. The presence of a singular T6SS variant, T6SS-III, suggests a specialized role, potentially in interbacterial competition or host interactions. The limited abundance of flagellar and fimbrial genes in Klebsiella aligns with its lessened motility and adhesion potential. Meanwhile, the virulence gene landscape of Pseudomonas species, including P. aeruginosa and P. furukawaii, stands apart from that of the Enterobacteriaceae members. The prevalence of genes related to Hxc-, Hcp-, and Xcp-secretion systems, along with HIS-I and T3SS, highlights their likely roles in Pseudomonas pathogenicity. These secretion systems empower the delivery of effectors that manipulate host cellular processes and evade immune surveillance. The presence of a diverse array of flagellar genes in Pseudomonas underscores its adaptability to various environmental niches. Notably, the exclusive presence of genes governing CU and type-IV fimbriae in P. aeruginosa suggests potential contributions to heightened adherence and robust biofilm formation. The distinct heme/iron acquisition mechanisms within Pseudomonas species underscore their specialized approaches to nutrient acquisition and adaptation to iron-deprived settings (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10534-022-00480-8).

This comparative virulence gene analysis underscores the distinct strategies these bacterial species employ in establishing infections and navigating their specific host interactions. The variations in their virulence gene profiles mirror the intricate interplay between bacterial pathogens and their host environments. Further elucidating the functional roles of these virulence factors will deepen our comprehension of bacterial infections and inform the development of targeted therapeutic interventions. By unraveling the genetic intricacies behind pathogenicity, we move closer to addressing the challenges posed by these diverse bacterial pathogens.

Conclusions

Clinical and molecular epidemiology studies should be undertaken to determine risk factors, transmission patterns, and clinical outcomes for GNB strains harboring ESBL and CRE resistance genes, given the high rate of endemicity observed in multiple studies from Palestine including this report.
