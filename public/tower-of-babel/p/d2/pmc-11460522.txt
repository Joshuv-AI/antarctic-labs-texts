Screening and identification of linear B-cell epitopes on structural proteins of African Swine Fever Virus
Authors: Haiyan Lu, Junjun Shao, Wei Liu, Shandian Gao, Guangqing Zhou, Xiaoyu Ning, Haiyan Huang, Yijia Liu, Huiyun Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•66 potential linear B-cell epitopes from 12 structural proteins of African Swine Fever Virus (ASFV) were selected.•Among the 22 identified epitopes, 13 were novel and exhibited both immunoreactivity and immunogenicity. The remaining 9 epitopes complemented the previously known ASFV epitopes.•By systematically comparing the immunoreactivity and immunogenicity of these 22 epitopes from different ASFV structural proteins, we have updated and supplemented significant information on ASFV epitopes.Keywords: Screening, Linear b-cell epitopes, African Swine Fever Virus

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF) caused by the African swine fever virus (ASFV) is a highly contagious and lethal disease affecting domestic pigs. The disease can lead to massive losses in the pig industry and even impact food security in affected countries (Gallardo et al., 2015).

ASFV, whose genome consists of about 170 ‒ 190 kb, has sophisticated structures and contains a large number of viral and host proteins (Alejo et al., 2018; Blome et al., 2020; Heimerman et al., 2018). Although various potential vaccine candidates and drugs have been studied, few products have been used practically to control the disease due to limitations in their safety and efficacy (Zhou et al., 2023; Chathuranga et al., 2023; Blome et al., 2014; Urbano et al., 2022).

B-cell epitopes are the smallest units on antigen surfaces to bind specific antibodies, and specifically activate the immune responses in humoral immunity (De Groot et al., 2020). Verification of B-cell epitopes could provide deep insights into immunological studies (Purcell et al., 2007), including recognition of immune processes, determination of antibody characteristics. The linear B-cell epitopes have continuous primary amino acid sequence and can be recognized readily in vitro (Kumar et al., 2023). Furthermore, linear B-cell epitopes can be utilized for disease diagnosis, designing of peptide-based vaccine, and production of antibody (Brun et al., 2011; Xu and Zhao, 2022). In addition, vaccines based on epitopes may contribute to avoid some ingredients involved in immune escape and immunosuppression (Dixon et al., 2019; Hernáez et al., 2004), especially for ASFV composed of more than 150 proteins with complicated mechanisms. In recent, the epitopes located in several structural proteins of ASFV have been extensively identified, and their immunological function were also investigated. However, the based-epitope ASF vaccine still cannot offer complete protection to pig against virulent challenge. The report showed that a robust T-cell response and high-level antibody response against ASFV were evoked in animals by a nanoparticle vaccine based on dominant B and T cell epitopes from p72, CD2v (pEP402R), pB602L and p30 (pCP204L) of ASFV. However, the nanoparticle vaccine based on these epitopes has not yet been subjected to further experimental validation, so its potential to confer protection remains to be established (Song et al., 2023).

Although recombinant ASF vaccines based on major structural proteins and their epitopes have been tested in several laboratories, these vaccines still cannot offer effective protection to pigs against virulent challenges (Neilan et al., 2004; Angulo et al., 1993). It is necessary to identify more effective immune-protective antigenic epitopes for the development of ASF vaccines. Previous reports have indicated that ASFV structural proteins, including p72, pH108R, p17, pE248R, pE120R, pE199L, p54, p30, p12 (pO61R), p22, p49 (pB438L), and pEP402R, are key components of the capsid, encompassing the capsid, outer envelope, and inner envelope, and play an important role in viral infection and reproduction (Alejo et al., 2018). This suggests that identifying dominant protective antigens among ASFV structural proteins could contribute to the successful design and development of ASFV vaccines.

In this study, to identify dominant protective antigenic epitopes for the development of an ASFV vaccine, ASFV structural proteins were selected, and their epitopes were investigated using bioinformatics and immunological methods. A total of sixty-six epitope candidates from 12 structural proteins of ASFV were identified through bioinformatics, and their immunological functions were subsequently verified by ELISA, Western blotting, and testing in mice vaccinated with recombinant epitope proteins. The results showed that 22 out of 66 epitopes could be recognized by ASFV standard positive serum, and mouse antisera raised against each epitope reacted well with inactivated ASFV. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, nine of these epitopes are neoantigens that have been discovered for the first time, which will be valuable for further investigating the immune mechanisms against ASFV and for developing recombinant ASFV vaccines in the future.

Materials and methods

Mice, inactivated virus, and serum samples

Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) BALB/c female mice, 6–8-week-old, were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute. The ASFV (ASFV/II/CN/SC/2019 strain) was inactivated at 60 °C for 30 min in the Animal Biosafety Level 3 facilities (ABSL-3) at Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute and was then stored at - 20 °C until use. Anti-ASFV positive serum was purchased from China Institute of Veterinary Drug Control.

Prediction of B-cell epitope

A total of twelve proteins were selected for screening and identifying potential antigenic epitopes: ten from the ASFV Pig/HLJ/2018 strain (pB646L, pH108R, pE248R, pE199L, pE183L, pCP204L, pO61R, and pKP177R) and two from the 47/Ss/2008 strain (pD117L and pE120R). Their amino acid sequences were retrieved from the NCBI protein database, and the corresponding annotations were listed in Table S1.

Linear B-cell epitopes of the abovementioned proteins were predicted using SVMTriP (http://sysbio.unl.edu/SVMTriP/prediction.php), and then epitope candidates consisting of 12, 16, or 20 amino acids were determined. To further verify the predicted epitope candidates, the IEDB Analysis Resource application Antigen Sequence Properties (Bepipred Linear Epitope Prediction 2.0 Results) and ElliPro (http://tools.iedb.org/ellipro/) were also used to screen for linear B-cell epitopes. The epitope candidates were selected according to the default parameters with a score no lower than 0.5 and a distance of 6.

In addition, taking into account the difficulties in expressing hydrophobic amino acid residues in prokaryotic systems, the predicted epitopes located within the transmembrane regions were excluded from further identification. TMHMM 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/) was used to predict the protein transmembrane regions and eliminate epitopes located within them.

Construction of recombinant expression plasmids with epitope

For the design and construction of recombinant expression plasmids with epitopes, the gene sequences of each potential epitope were incorporated into a flexible linker (GGGGSS) at the 3′ end. The corresponding coding sequences were synthesized by GenScript Biotech Co., Ltd. (China) and subsequently cloned into the BamH I and Xho I sites of the pGEX-4T-1 vector. Additionally, pGEX-4T-1 has only a linker sequence and does not contain any candidate epitopes, serving as a negative control named Tag-linker.

Expression of fusion proteins

All constructed plasmids and a negative control were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The recombinant bacteria were grown in LB liquid medium containing 50 μg/mL ampicillin for 16 h at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The cultures were then diluted 1:100 into fresh LB medium with 50 μg/mL ampicillin and incubated at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 0.4–0.6. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM IPTG, and the culture was incubated for an additional 16 h at 16 °C. The expression of recombinant proteins was analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

Purification of fusion proteins

The recombinant proteins were purified using the BeyoGold™ GST-tag Purification Resin (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), according to the instructions provided with the kit. Briefly, 100 mL of induced bacterial cultures were harvested by centrifugation. The bacterial pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The dosage can be increased appropriately according to the bacterial count and the sample was then subjected to sonication on ice for 15 min (3 s bursts at 220 W with a 3 s cooling period between bursts). The lysed cells were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was combined with 1 mL of pre-balanced resin in a column and incubated for 60 min at 4 °C. After washing five times with phosphate buffer, the GST-tagged proteins were eluted with 2 mL elution buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM reduced l-glutathione, pH 8.0). The proteins were then analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and their concentrations were determined using the Bradford Protein Assay (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) in combination with IMAGEJ software.

Analysis of immunoreactivity of potential epitopes

To identify potential epitopes that specifically react with anti-ASFV positive serum, each well of a 96-well ELISA plate was coated with 1 µg/mL of individual protein in a total volume of 100 µL for 1 hour at 37 °C. Each protein sample was set up with six replicates. The plates were washed three times with PBST (phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween-20), followed by separate distribution of 100 μL ASFV standard positive and negative serum, each diluted with serum diluent buffer at a ratio of 1:100. The plates were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C and then washed three times with PBST. HRP-conjugated goat anti-pig IgG (1:10,000) (Abcam, Shanghai, China) was added to each well. After incubation for 30 min at 37 °C, the plates were washed again, and 100 µL of TMB (SurModics IVD Inc.) was added and incubated for 10–15 min at 37 °C. After stopping the reaction with 2 M H₂SO₄, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.).

The optical density (OD) ratios for immunoreactivity, determined by the formula [Pa/Na = OD value of positive serum/OD value of negative serum] to express the results.

Assay of immunogenicity

Vaccination of mice

To validate the immunogenicity of the epitope candidates, 190 female 6–8-week-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) mice were used. These mice were randomly divided into 24 groups, with 8 mice in each group. The mice were vaccinated subcutaneously with 50 µg of individual fusion protein emulsified in Freund's complete adjuvant (Sigma, Shanghai) during the primary immunization. On days 14 and 28 after the first vaccination, a booster was administered with the same dosage of antigen emulsified in Freund's incomplete adjuvant. Additionally, two control groups were separately administered with Tag-linker fusion protein (50 µg/mouse) and PBS (100 µL/mouse), and all protocols were the same as those for the test groups.

Sera samples were collected before and after the primary immunization, as well as after each of the two booster immunizations, and were subsequently analyzed using ELISA. The ELISA procedure was carried out according to the previously described method with minor modifications. Briefly, sera were diluted at 1:200, and HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG antibody was diluted at 1:10,000 (Abcam).

The antibody response levels within each group were evaluated by comparing the optical density (OD) measurements of post-vaccination sera to those of pre-vaccination sera. This ratio was expressed using the formula: Relative antibody levels (Pab/Nab) = Pab (OD value of post-vaccination serum) / Nab (OD value of pre-vaccination serum).

Analysis of immunogenicity of potential epitopes

To assess the antibody response specifically attributed to the epitopes rather than the carrier protein GST, we conducted an indirect ELISA using whole inactivated ASFV antigens. The optimized antigen coating dilution solution (1:100), mouse serum diluent (1:100), and HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG antibody (1:10,000) were used, and the ELISA was performed according to the previously described procedure. Finally, the potential epitopes were considered immunogenic if the Pi/Ni value (OD value with added positive serum/OD value with added negative serum) showed significant differences between experimental groups and control groups (P < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

The immunoreactivity of potential epitopes was evaluated using Duncan's multiple range test for multiple comparisons in SPSS 25.0 software. Epitopes with a Pa/Na ratio higher than 2.0 were selected as candidate epitopes for further evaluation of their immunogenicity. The immunogenicity of these potential epitopes was also assessed using Duncan's multiple range test.

Conservative analysis of the epitope candidates

To evaluate the conservation of epitopes in gene II of ASFV, 13 strains of this gene from recent years in China were selected for multiple sequence alignment using the Clustal W method (DNAStar, USA). Variations among the isolates were compared and analyzed. The ratio of the number of matched amino acids with the identified epitope in each strain to the total number of amino acids in the identified epitope was calculated. A ratio of 100 indicates a perfect sequence match at the corresponding position, with values closer to 100 reflecting higher homology. The conservation analysis was conducted using BioEdit and MEGA software.

Results

Immunoreactivity of potential epitopes

Sixty-six potential epitopes were divided into two groups using an immunoreactivity ratio (Pa/Na) of 2.0 as the critical value (Table S3). The results showed that there were 6 potential epitopes of p72, 5 potential epitopes of p17, 3 potential epitopes of pE248R, 2 potential epitopes of pB438L, and 1 potential epitope each of pH108R, pE183L, pE120R, pE199L, p30, and p12, respectively, showing immunoreactivity (Fig. 2). For potential epitopes with values greater than 2, a comparative analysis was conducted using Duncan's multiple range test. The results indicated that the amino acid residues (aa) 132–180 of p54, the 5–20, 15–34, 63–86, 85–104, and 71–113 aa regions of p17, and the 65–117 aa region of pE120R were significantly higher than those of other potential epitopes, with immunoreactivity ratios of 9.0561, 8.1517, 8.2874, 5.9085, 5.5121, 12.235 and 7.0289, corresponding to the codes epit3–3, epit4–1, epit4–2, epit4–9, epit4–4, epit4–5, and epit6–3, respectively (Fig. 3 and Table S4).

Immunogenicity of potential epitopes

The immunogenicity verification analysis was further conducted on 22 candidate epitopes exhibiting an immunoreactivity ratio greater than 2. To avoid false negatives in the detection of immunogenicity, it was necessary to measure the antibody levels in the serum of mice immunized with the potential epitope vaccine. The results showed that antibody levels in the group were significantly different on the 14th day after the first immunization. The antibody level increased on the 28th day after the first immunization (14 days after the second immunization) and continued to rise on the 38th day after the third immunization (10 days after the third immunization). There was no difference in antibody levels within the group on the 38th day (Fig. S2, Table S5). Therefore, the sera collected on the 38th day were used as test samples for evaluating the immunogenicity of the potential epitopes.

Duncan's multiple range test was conducted on the immunogenicity ratio data for the potential epitopes. The results revealed a significant difference in the immunogenicity ratios between the experimental groups and the negative control groups, suggesting that 22 candidate epitopes displayed immunogenicity. In these epitopes, the amino acid residues were the 36–56 of p12, the 211–230 aa of pB438L, the 352–363, 362–372 aa of p72, and the 5–20, 63–86, 71–113 aa of p17, whose immunogenicity ratios (Pi/Ni) were the highest, corresponding to the code epit9–1, epit12–4, epit1–7, epit1–8, epit4–1, epit4–9 and epit4–5, their values were 5.5567, 4.39, 4.1917, 4.0367, 4.1933, 4.0283, 4.3317, respectively (see Fig. 4). For the amino acid residues 4–15 of p30, the 132–180 aa of p54, the 15–34, 85–104 aa of p17, the 496–530 aa of p72, and the 51–73 aa of pB438L, corresponding to the code epit8–1, epit3–3, epit4–2, epit4–4, epit1–14 and epit12–2, their immunogenicity ratios were next and the values were 3.45, 3.245, 3.11, 3.0333, 2.7983, 2.5983, respectively (see Table S6).

Comparative analysis of candidate epitopes

In this study, 22 epitopes with immunoreactivity and immunogenicity were screened from 66 candidate epitopes, 13 of which were newly verified epitopes. To be exact, these epitopes newly were verified as follow: the amino acid residues 352–363, 416–434, 424–439, 496–530 of p72; the 33–46 aa of pH108R; the 63–86 aa of p17; the 121–136, 138–169, 158–185 aa of pE248R; the 65–117 aa of pE120R; the 175–189 aa of pE199L; the 36–56 aa of p12; the aa 211–230 of pB438L. In these epitopes, the amino acid residues 65–117 of pE120R and the aa 63–86 of p17 have the highest value of immunoreactivity ratio, which were 7.0289 and 5.9085, respectively, and the remaining values were between 2 and 3. The 352–363 aa of p72, the 63–86 aa of p17, the 36–56 aa of p12, and the 211–230 aa of pB438L had the highest value of immunogenicity ratio, which exceeded 4 (Table 1 and Fig. 5).

In addition, the remaining 9 potential epitopes partially coincided with previous studies. Specifically, the amino acid residues 244–262 of p72 partially overlap with the 249–258 aa of the previous study (Miao et al., 2023) and the 362–372 aa of p72 correspond to the previous 364–395 aa (Ivanov et al., 2011). The aa 132–180 of p54 corresponded slightly to the previous aa 175–184 (Cao et al., 2021). Moreover, Petrovan et al. have utilized five monoclonal antibodies to react with expressed peptides of the amino acid residues 60–178 of p54, and the monoclonal antibody recognizing amino acids 93–113 has shown good neutralizing activity (Petrovan et al., 2020). The 4–15 aa of p30 corresponded to the previous 12–18 aa (Zhou et al., 2023). The 51–73 aa of pB438L corresponds to the previous 51–71 aa (Ivanov et al. 2011), (Table S7). Previous studies have primarily identified epitopes by monoclonal antibodies, but some epitope sequence ranges have not been precisely determined. Unlike the monoclonal antibody method, Ivanov et al. identified epitopes by immunizing mice and pigs with synthetic peptides coupled to the carrier protein streptavidin; however, they also encountered issues with imprecise epitope sequence ranges.

For the immune characteristics of p17 epitopes, we conducted supplementary experiments. The results showed that the immunoreactivity of the amino acid residues 5–20, 15–34, 85–104, and 71–113 was better than that of the residues 8–20 (Li et al., 2023) and 88–111 (Ivanov et al., 2011) previously studied for p17. However, there was no significant difference in their immunogenicity (Table S8). Based on the comparative analysis of our epitope identifications with previous reports, we can conclude that the epitope sequences we identified are highly credible. Furthermore, our findings have expanded and refined the known range of ASFV epitopes.

Conservative analysis

The conservation of 22 epitopes identified in 13 isolates of genotype II from China was analyzed (Table S9). These epitopes showed 100% homology among the 13 isolates, except for three epitopes found in 2 isolates (C8 and C13). Two epitopes in the C8 isolate, epit3–3 (p54) and epit6–3 (pE120R), were 98% homologous to those in other isolates, indicating that these epitopes are also highly conserved in ASFV genotype II. Although epit8–1 (p30) had lower homology (58%), the remaining epitopes in the C13 isolate had 100% homology. The data indicate that the overwhelming majority of the screened epitopes are highly conserved in ASFV genotype II, which may be useful for designing and developing vaccine-based epitopes (Table 2).

Discussion

The immune protection conferred by vaccines primarily relies on neutralizing antibodies that obstruct the virus's attachment and entry, inhibit viral replication, and cell-mediated immune responses that target and eliminate the virus, thereby preventing or clearing infections (Kenneth M. and Weaver, 2016). Consequently, identifying and developing protective antigens and their epitopes, which can stimulate the production of neutralizing antibodies, is essential for advancing research in genetic engineering and subunit vaccines. The African swine fever virus, characterized by its complexity, involves numerous structural proteins with functions that remain poorly understood. Among these, the p72 protein is the major structural component, accounting for over 30% of the viral capsid and is the most immunogenic ASFV protein (Wang et al., 2019). Many linear neutralizing epitopes of this protein have been identified, and some studies also indicate that p72 has abundant epitopes (Ivanov et al., 2011; Heimerman et al., 2018; Miao et al., 2023). p49 (pB438L) (Epifano et al., 2006) and pE120R are essential as minor capsid proteins for the assembly of the viral structure (Alejo et al., 2018; Epifano et al., 2006; Andrés et al., 2001). Both p30 and p54 (pE183L) are located on the inner membrane of the viral capsid and are also important antigenic proteins (Gómez-Puertas et al., 1998); studies have shown that these two proteins can induce neutralizing antibodies (Barderas et al., 2001; Cao et al., 2021).

Some ASFV capsid inner membrane proteins include p17, p12, pE199L, pE248R, pH108R, and p22 (pKP177R). Among these, p17, as a membrane protein, can stimulate pigs to produce neutralizing antibodies (Muñoz and Tabarés, 2022). This study shows that the p17 epitopes indeed have good reactivity and immunogenicity. The p12 protein can mediate the attachment of viral particles to specific receptors and induce specific antibodies against p12 in vivo (Angulo et al., 1993). The high immunogenicity of the p12 epitope identified in this study indicates that the serum antibodies produced in mice can effectively bind with ASFV particles. Research shows that the inner membrane proteins pE199L and pE248R are involved in the fusion step of virus invasion (Cuesta-Geijo et al., 2022). pH108R is associated with the virulence of African Swine Fever (Vuono et al., 2022). Some studies suggest that p22 can elicit the production of partially protective antibodies (Díaz et al., 2022).

Although existing studies indicate that many of the proteins mentioned can induce neutralizing antibodies against ASFV, antibodies produced against one or several of these proteins are insufficient to fully protect the host from the virus. For instance, antibodies targeting the p72, p54, p30, and p12 proteins fail to neutralize the virus's infectivity (Neilan, et al., 2004; Angulo, et al., 1993). This suboptimal immunogenicity may stem from the presence of immunosuppressive antigenic epitopes within these proteins (Dixon et al., 2019). Research has shown that these structural proteins are rich in antigenic epitopes. To identify antigenic epitopes with effective immune protective capabilities—specifically those that inhibit viral adsorption, entry, and replication—this study focused on epitope screening and identification of these structural proteins. The findings not only complement and refine existing research but also provide a technical platform for screening B-cell linear neutralizing epitopes of other ASFV proteins. This lays the groundwork for the development of more effective multi-epitope vaccines.

In this study, 22 epitopes with demonstrated immunoreactivity and immunogenicity were screened from a pool of 66 candidates. Of these, 13 were newly validated epitopes, while the remaining 9 partially overlapped with those identified in previous studies (see Section 3.5 Comparative Analysis of Candidate Epitopes). The 9 epitopes identified in this study show some variations from those reported previously, likely due to differences in amino acid sequences and identification methods. Nevertheless, the overall findings are consistent, and the epitopes identified here exhibit high conservation. Moreover, some epitopes identified exhibited a clear discrepancy between their immunoreactivity and immunogenicity. For example, multiple epitopes of p17, such as epit4–1, epit4–2, epit4–4, and epit4–9, (5–20, 15–34, 85–104, 63–86 aa) as well as epit6–3 (65–117 aa) of pE120R, showed high immunoreactivity but relatively low immunogenicity (see Fig. 3, Fig. 4). This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that immunoreactivity was assessed by evaluating the interaction of expressed antigenic epitopes with serum from virus-infected individuals (Stanfield and Wilson, 1995), whereas immunogenicity was measured by assessing the interaction of sera from immunized mice with ASFV particles. The difference in animal species used for the sera—pigs, which are sensitive hosts for ASFV, and mice, which are not ideal models for ASFV—could account for this discrepancy (Lunney et al., 2009). Additionally, some structural proteins are located inside the viral particles, where the binding of sera can be significantly hindered by spatial constraints(Lok, et al., 2008), which may also contribute to the lower immunogenicity of p17. In contrast, some epitopes of p72, p12, and pB438L (p49), such as epit1–7 (352–363 aa), epit9–1(36–56 aa), and epit12–4 (211–230 aa), demonstrated higher immunogenicity. This may be due to the use of epitope fusion proteins, which, after multiple immunizations, could induce higher antibody levels due to the larger molecular weight of the carrier protein, affecting the antibody levels against smaller epitope antigens. Moreover, the ability of different epitopes to stimulate antibody production can vary based on their intrinsic properties (Braden and Poljak, 1995), and some epitopes are less affected by such interfering factors.

Currently, there are a variety of technologies for identifying B-cell linear epitopes, such as peptide microarray technology (Desmet et al., 2024), synthetic peptide technology (Ivanov et al., 2011), X-ray crystallography (Toride King et al., 2018), phage peptide library presenting technology (Guo et al., 2023), prokaryotic expression presenting systems, yeast surface presenting systems (Park et al., 2012), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), among others. However, some of these methods are inapplicable due to inconvenient devices, complex technologies, or high costs. It is undeniable that the method by monoclonal antibody to identify linear B cell epitopes is the most accurate. At present, some epitopes have been identified by this method. These epitopes are from p72, p30, p54, p17, p22, pA137R, pC129R, pCP312R and other proteins (Yin et al., 2022; Heimerman et al., 2018; Zhang, X et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022, 2023; Cao et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2024; Embury-Hyatt C et al., 2023; Wang, et al., 2023; Hagoss et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang, S et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). However, its operation process is quite cumbersome and it is easy to lose some epitopes.

Although computational predictions have certain limitations due to defects in parameters and algorithms, which result in challenges for physicochemical ramifications and practical applications in vaccination (Caoili, 2010), linear B-cell epitopes can be predicted rapidly and conveniently. In this study, after employing computational approaches to predict linear B-cell epitopes, an Escherichia coli expression system and an indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay were primarily used for screening these epitopes. This combined method is relatively simple and yields accurate results.

Conclusion

In this study, 22 epitopes were identified from 66 potential epitopes by computational immunological techniques, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, and immune mouse experiments. In these epitopes, 13 epitopes were newly identified and 9 epitopes were partially coincident with the previous research. The B-cell linear epitopes identified in this study supplement and update the existing knowledge of ASFV epitopes, contributing to a deeper understanding of ASFV and providing additional epitope information for the development of safe and effective African swine fever vaccines and diagnostic reagents
