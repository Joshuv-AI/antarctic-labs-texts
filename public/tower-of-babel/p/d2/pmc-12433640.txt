Journeying Towards Excellence in the Care of Patients with Haemophilia and Other Inherited Bleeding Disorders From a Developing World: Insights From Tanzania
Authors: Stella Samson Rwezaula, Samson D Mtoba, Rashid A Gosse, Rebecca W Mwakichako, Abdallah Raphael Makalla, Martha Loiseyeki Bruckman, Luhongedzo Gerson Matandala, Linda Alphey Shao, Hedwiga Francis Swai, Michelle Sholzberg, Jerome Teitel, Amos Rodger Mwakigonja, Oluyemi Akinloye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHaemophilia, an X-linked recessive bleeding disorder caused by mutations in the F8 and F9 genes, remains profoundly underdiagnosed in resource-limited settings. In Tanzania, it is estimated that fewer than 10% of individuals with haemophilia are diagnosed. Centralized services, limited diagnostic capacity, and underutilization of available therapies pose major barriers to timely and effective care. This review reports on the activities undertaken to establish and expand haemophilia care in Tanzania, charting the journey toward excellence.MethodsThis is a comprehensive review of national program reports, registry data from the Haemophilia Society of Tanzania, and interviews with key personnel directly involved in the development of haemophilia services between 2021 and April 2025.ResultsBetween 2021 and 2025, Tanzania made significant strides in strengthening haemophilia care. A multidisciplinary workforce was trained both locally and internationally in specialized diagnosis, treatment, and management of haemophilia and other inherited bleeding disorders. Fourteen haemophilia clinics were established, including a national comprehensive clinic at Muhimbili National Hospital and satellite clinics across zonal and regional hospitals, providing screening, diagnosis, and comprehensive care. As of 17th April 2025, a total of 473 patients had been registered and were receiving structured care through this network.ConclusionThe integration of specialized haematological training, stakeholder engagement, decentralization of services through 14 clinics, and the development of a national registry and clinical guidelines have markedly enhanced Tanzania’s capacity to diagnose and manage haemophilia and other inherited bleeding disorders. Moving forward, it is imperative to expand regional coverage, intensify community-based awareness and screening initiatives, strengthen capacity building and research efforts, and establish a specialized centre of excellence dedicated to advancing haemophilia care, professional training, research, and innovation.Keywords: journeying, excellence, patient care, haemophilia, inherited bleeding disorders, Tanzania

Introduction

Haemophilia is an X-linked recessive bleeding disorder caused by mutations in the F8 and F9 genes, which encode coagulation factor VIII (FVIII) and factor IX (FIX), respectively.1 The deficiency of these factors disrupts the intrinsic pathway of the coagulation cascade, leading to prolonged bleeding episodes.2 Globally, haemophilia affects an estimated 815,100 individuals; however, only 347,026 have been clinically diagnosed, with the vast majority of undiagnosed cases occurring in developing countries.3 Haemophilia A (HA) is more common than Haemophilia B (HB), affecting approximately 1 in 10,000 males and 1 in 30,000 males, respectively,1,4 with about one-third of cases arising from de novo mutations.5,6

The burden of haemophilia in Africa remains extremely high due to profound underdiagnosis, scarce availability of clotting factor concentrates, and severely under-resourced healthcare infrastructure.7–9 Although fewer than 3% of the world’s haemophiliacs live in Africa, only 11.4% of expected cases are diagnosed, and of those in need, merely 2% have access to clotting factor concentrates.10,11

Tanzania, with an estimated population of ~61.7 million in 2022, exemplifies the stark gap between estimated and confirmed haemophilia cases. While prevalence estimates suggest between 6,200 and 12,000 individuals may be affected, only 463 have been diagnosed to date (HA: 421; HB: 42), (National Statistics of Haemophilia and Other Inherited Bleeding Disorders: Haemphilia Society of Tanzania. National Registry of Bleeding Disorders, April 2025).12 Until recently, diagnostic and treatment services were centralized at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) in Dar es Salaam. This model necessitated long-distance referrals, contributing to delayed diagnosis, limited access to care, and preventable complications, including disability and death.13 To address this gap, the Haemophilia Society of Tanzania (HST) was established in 2009 as a multi-stakeholder, not-for-profit organization and became an official member of the World Federation of Haemophilia (WFH) in 2013. Since its formation, HST has collaborated with MNH to develop a national registry to enhance diagnosis, classification, and tracking of cases. By April 2025, the registry had documented 473 individuals with inherited bleeding disorders in Tanzania, including HA (421), HB (42), Factor X deficiency (3), and von Willebrand disease (7). This study aimed to review Tanzania’s journey toward excellence in haemophilia care by describing the processes, milestones, and collaborative efforts that led to the establishment of decentralized haemophilia clinics, identifying key challenges and mitigation strategies, and proposing future directions to scale and sustain comprehensive care of haemophilia nationwide.

Methods

This paper presents a review of the key activities undertaken to establish and advance care for haemophilia and other inherited bleeding disorders in Tanzania. The study involved a comprehensive review of documents from specialized clinics and the HST, as well as interviews with key personnel involved in the launch and implementation of specialized haemophilia services. In addition, patient records were reviewed using data collected from the newly established clinics.To illustrate the typical care journey, challenges, and outcomes for individuals with inherited bleeding disorders in Tanzania prior to the expansion of services, two exemplar cases were purposefully selected from the MNH clinic, the first haemophilia treatment centre established in the country. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the MNH Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of the individuals whose clinical histories were included as illustrative case studies.

Results

Training for Healthcare Providers

Between 2021 and 2025, a multidisciplinary team of healthcare providers including specialists, general practitioners, pharmacists, nurses, laboratory technicians/scientists, and physiotherapists, received comprehensive training in the care of patients with haemophilia and other inherited bleeding disorders. In-service training was delivered through targeted workshops and symposia held at health facilities where the haemophilia clinics were established. The training covered a wide range of topics aimed at equipping participants with the essential knowledge and practical skills required to deliver comprehensive haemophilia care (Table 1).

Healthcare Providers Trained and Courses Covered

In addition, fourteen (14) healthcare professionals underwent targeted intensive training and mentorship at established Comprehensive Haemophilia Treatment Centres (HTCs) in Canada, Kenya, India, and South Africa. This group comprised four specialists, one general practitioner, four laboratory scientists/technologists, three nurses, one pharmacist, and one physiotherapist (Table 2). The skills obtained in these trainings were crucial in advancing haemophilia care, including the application of point-of-care ultrasound for diagnosis and treatment, circumcision for male patients with haemophilia, factor assay, physiotherapy, and surgical and non-surgical treatment of haemophilia musculoskeletal complications such as haemophilic arthropathy. However, some advanced procedures such as radioisotope synovectomy, regular prophylaxis guided by pharmacokinetic (PK) profiling, and advanced genetic diagnostics were not easily translatable in our context due to limited infrastructure, lack of specialized equipment, high costs, and insufficient availability of clotting factor concentrates. Additionally, while family-based genetic counselling and cascade screening are routine in developed settings, application of these practices faces challenges in our setting due to limited awareness, sociocultural barriers such as stigma, and a lack of well-trained genetic counsellors. Despite these barriers, the training provided valuable foundational skills that were adapted using locally available resources, for example, using physiotherapy techniques to prevent joint deterioration, and adopting simplified family tree mapping tools to identify at-risk individuals.

Establishment of Haemophilia Satellite Clinics

Between 2021 and 2025, a total of fourteen Haemophilia clinics were launched across Tanzania. The first clinic was launched at MNH in 2021, serving as the national Comprehensive Haemophilia Treatment Clinic. This was followed by the launching of four satellite clinics at zonal referral hospitals (Bugando Medical Centre, Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Centre, Benjamin Mkapa Hospital and Mbeya Zonal Referral Hospital) and eight satellite clinics at regional referral hospitals in Tanzania mainland and one district hospital in Zanzibar (Figure 1). These satellite clinics have been capacitated to conduct haemophilia screening and confirmatory testing, as well as to provide treatment, physiotherapy and other rehabilitative services. MNH, as the hub for comprehensive haemophilia care, plays a central role in coordinating and supporting key functions, including the distribution of clotting factor concentrates and diagnostic reagents to other clinics and as the tertiary Centre of referral for complicated cases (Table 3).

National Registry and Guidelines for Management of Haemophilia

The national haemophilia registry was established in 2021 at MNH and has since served as a vital tool for the identification, monitoring, and evaluation of services provided to individuals with haemophilia and other inherited bleeding disorders. As of 17th April 2025, a total of 473 patients (463 with haemophilia and 10 with other inherited blood disorders) had been registered across various treatment centres in the country. Among the patients with haemophilia, four were female, all diagnosed with haemophilia A. Among those with other inherited bleeding disorders, only one out of nine was female. A total of 133 suspected cases presented with haemophilia symptoms and prolonged aPTT; however, their diagnoses had not yet been confirmed by Factor Assay (Figure 3).

Identified cases of haemophilia and other inherited blood disorders in Tanzania.

In addition, national treatment guidelines for the management of haemophilia have been developed (awaiting endorsement by the Ministry of Health). These guidelines are expected to serve as a standardized reference for healthcare providers for the diagnosis, treatment, and long-term care of patients with haemophilia. They are expected to enhance consistency and quality of care across all levels of the healthcare system, support clinical decision-making, and promote best practices in line with international standards.

Stakeholders Engagement

Multiple stakeholders at the local, national, and international levels were actively engaged from the outset of this journey toward excellence in haemophilia care. WFH, Novo Nordisk Foundation (NNF), Novo Nordisk Haemophilia Foundation (NNHF), and Roche Kenya Limited provided financial support to facilitate capacity building and equip clinics with essential supplies, including clotting factor concentrates and diagnostic reagents necessary for effective service delivery. The Tanzania Ministry of Health has been involved at every stage, not only to support the smooth implementation of haemophilia care services but also to ensure the long-term sustainability of the established clinics. Additionally, the ministry of health has appointed a focal person who coordinates the implementation of haemophilia services in the country.

At the community level, engagement efforts included outreach programs, awareness campaigns through local media and public gatherings, printed educational materials, and mobilization of community health workers. Free haemophilia screening services were provided at each site during the clinic inauguration, followed by linkage to care for individuals identified with bleeding disorders. In addition, HST, a non-profit organization composed of patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers, was established in 2009. The Society aims to improve the quality of life for individuals and families affected by haemophilia and other inherited bleeding disorders. The HST was officially registered under the Ministry of Home Affairs in 2009 and affiliated with the WFH in 2013 and it is sustained through members’ annual subscription fees and periodic donations from international partners. It continues to play a leading role in advocating for equitable access to high-quality care across the country.

Exemplar Cases of Haemophilia and Factor X Deficiency

To illustrate the journey from suffering to diagnosis and care in the Tanzanian context, we present a summary of two patient cases diagnosed with haemophilia and Factor X Deficiency at the MNH clinic.

Case 1: A 9-year-old boy presented to our clinic in 2018 with compartment syndrome of the left arm and haemarthropathy of the left knee. His perinatal and postnatal periods were uneventful. His first bleeding episode occurred at the age of three following trauma to the upper gingiva. The bleeding persisted for one week, necessitating blood transfusion at a regional hospital and suturing at the site of injury. However, no further investigations were conducted to identify the underlying cause of the prolonged bleeding.

Over the following years, he experienced recurrent joint and muscle swelling. At the age of five, he underwent a traditional circumcision at home, which resulted in persistent bleeding for two weeks. He was transfused with two units of packed red blood cells at a regional hospital but was again discharged without a definitive diagnosis.

In July 2018, following corporal punishment at school, he sustained an injury to his left shoulder. Due to progressive swelling, he was referred to MNH two weeks later. He was diagnosed with severe Haemophilia A (Factor VIII activity <1%). Despite medical intervention, he was discharged with a diagnosis of left arm compartment syndrome and left knee Haemathropathy, which were not fully reversible despite multiple physiotherapy sessions (Figure 4).

A 9-year-old patient with left arm compartment syndrome and left knee haemathropathy.

Case 2: A 15-year-old girl presented with a history of a bleeding tendency, primarily manifested as prolonged menstrual bleeding. Her bleeding history dates back to the neonatal period, when she experienced prolonged umbilical cord stump bleeding lasting two days, necessitating re-admission. She was managed with vitamin K administration and re-tying of the umbilical cord.

At two months of age, she underwent traditional removal of “plastic teeth” and subsequently bled for one week. She was referred to a regional hospital where she received two units of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) and suturing at the site. Once again, the underlying cause of bleeding was not investigated. By five months of age, she began experiencing intermittent joint and muscle swelling.

At the age of 15, she attained menarche, after which she began experiencing excessively heavy menstrual bleeding. This led to monthly hospital admissions at MNH for transfusions with PRBCs and fresh frozen plasma (FFP) due to severe anemia.

The patient has a significant family history of bleeding disorders. Four of her nine siblings died from excessive bleeding, and one surviving sibling experiences recurrent joint and muscle bleeding. In March 2023, both the patient and her sibling were admitted to MNH and diagnosed with severe Factor X deficiency. However, the unavailability of factor X concentrate led to suboptimal management, relying on FFP, tranexamic acid, and regular transfusions. She is currently maintained on hormonal therapy and iron supplementation.

Besides the physical challenges, the patient and her family have endured significant psychosocial distress. The father abandoned the family due to the chronic illness burden, leaving the mother, a petty trader, as the sole provider (Figure 5).

Provision of Haemophilia Care

Currently, the care for patients with haemophilia is provided at the healthcare facilities and community settings. Provision of education on haemophilia starts at the community level, where trained HCPs conduct public awareness and mobilize individuals and families through Family tree tracing to undergo screening tests for those who provide informed consent. The screening tests include activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and Mixing studies. Those who test positive are linked to the nearby haemophilia clinic for confirmatory tests, diagnosis and care. The confirmatory diagnosis is done by Factor Assay, and those who are confirmed to have haemophilia are enrolled for a treatment program, including On-demand or prophylaxis with Factor concentrates (for those who have not developed complications) and Emicizumab prophylaxis for those who have developed complications such as Inhibitors. The patients and families are engaged in care, and besides the medical treatment, they receive ongoing education on self-infusion, home therapy and genetic counselling.

Discussion

Haemophilia, a rare inherited bleeding disorder, has gained little attention in the low- and middle-income countries, including Tanzania, where diagnosis, treatment, and long-term management are often compromised by systemic health system limitations.14 Our review highlights the critical steps and recent progress that have been made in Tanzania to advance care for haemophilia and other inherited bleeding disorders.

One of the key findings from this study is the delayed diagnosis and inadequate initial management of haemophilia in children, which led to severe complications such as joint damage, compartment syndrome, and chronic transfusion dependence. The two case narratives illustrate how children with early signs of bleeding, such as umbilical stump hemorrhage or post-trauma bleeding, repeatedly presented to health facilities without being diagnosed. In both cases, multiple hospital admissions and transfusions occurred before appropriate diagnostic workup confirmed severe haemophilia A and factor X deficiency, respectively. These cases show the low clinical suspicion, limited diagnostic capacity, and poor referral systems that delay care and contribute to avoidable morbidity, which is also evident in other developing countries.3,13,15,16

The establishment of satellite haemophilia clinics and provision of training for healthcare providers represent important strides toward decentralizing care and improving early detection.17 These clinics had improved access to services, enabled free screening during launch events, and fostered linkage to care for newly identified patients. Even though, despite this progress, comprehensive care remains a challenge for many patients, particularly in rural areas. This is influenced by inconsistent supply chains of essential equipment and supplies such as diagnostic tests and medications (factor concentrates), inadequate of skilled healthcare providers in the clinics, and high treatment costs.18

Globally, haemophilia care is characterized by a striking disparity. While prophylactic factor replacement therapy is the standard of care in high-income countries. As a result of near-normal life expectancy, on-demand treatment remains the norm in resource-limited settings like Tanzania due to resource constraints.18,19 According to the WFH, less than 12% of expected haemophilia cases are diagnosed across Africa, and factor concentrate usage remains below 0.1 IU per capita, far below the global recommendations.3,20 Moreover, in these developing countries, systemic challenges such as limited public awareness, inadequate funding, lack of routine laboratory diagnosis in both primary and tertiary health facilities greatly hinders progress in haemophilia care.21,22 Recent achievements observed in Tanzania is majorly contributed by as strong collaboration with local international partners such as the WFH, NNF, NNHF, Roche Kenya Limited and HST which have played a central role in addressing some of these barriers such as funding and advocacy from the local to policy-levels. The HST has been particularly instrumental in advocacy, patient mobilization, and awareness creation which has progressively contributed to policy-level recognition of haemophilia care, despite persisting systemic challenges persist.23

Our findings reaffirm that for haemophilia care to be truly comprehensive, interventions should extend beyond clinical management to include rehabilitation services, psychosocial support, and community engagement to dispel myths (eg “plastic teeth” removal) and reduce stigma. Additionally, greater investment is required to integrate haemophilia care into the national health system through inclusion in health insurance packages, expansion of diagnostic infrastructure, and local training of multidisciplinary care teams.

Future Goals for Excellence

Our future goals are centered on expanding access to sustainable, comprehensive haemophilia care across Tanzania. A key priority is to build strategic partnerships to support capacity building and decentralization of care by establishing more satellite treatment and rehabilitation centres at the regional and district levels in Tanzania. This expansion aims to improve early diagnosis, enhance access to essential services, and prevent avoidable complications and deaths.

To address the low diagnosis rate of haemophilia and other bleeding disorders, we plan to intensify screening efforts through family tree tracing, genetic counselling and nationwide awareness campaigns that leverage HCPs, stakeholders, and policy makers from the local community to the national level.

To prevent disability among the identified patients, we are ensuring that every launched haemophilia treatment centre has well-trained physiotherapists and essential physiotherapy equipment for the provision of physiotherapy services.

Recognizing the current lack of tailored services for women, we aim to develop specialized programs for women and girls with bleeding disorders, in collaboration with obstetrics and gynaecology departments, which often serve as the first point of contact for women, especially those of reproductive age.

Long-term plan, we envision establishing a Centre of Excellence for Haemophilia and Inherited Bleeding Disorders to provide high-quality care and serve as a hub for training, cutting-edge research and innovation in Tanzania and the East African region.

Conclusion

Addressing the challenges of haemophilia care in Tanzania requires a multifaceted approach that encompasses early diagnosis, comprehensive management, and systemic integration into the national health framework. Despite recent progress, including the establishment of satellite clinics and collaborations with local and international partners, significant barriers remain, particularly in rural areas where access to care is limited. To achieve sustainable improvements, it is essential for the Tanzanian government to actively involve haemophilia care in the National Strategic Plan for Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs), ensuring that it receives adequate funding and support. Future initiatives should prioritize capacity building for HCPs, the decentralization of services, increase funding to support diagnosis, care and research in haemophilia, enhance public awareness, and develop targeted programs for women and girls with bleeding disorders, ultimately aiming to establish a Centre of Excellence for Haemophilia and other Inherited Bleeding Disorders that will serve as a beacon of quality care, training, and research in Tanzania and the East Africa region.
