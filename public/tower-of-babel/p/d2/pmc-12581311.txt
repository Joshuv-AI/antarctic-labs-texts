Risk stratification and healthcare disparities in COVID-19 outcomes: a retrospective cohort study of treatment pathways before and after infection
Authors: Xin Jiang, Yaoxin Wei, Junlian Li, Songhua Wang, Rui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCOVID-19 infections continue globally post-pandemic. Analyzing treatment pathways and clinical outcomes remains essential for understanding prognostic factors.MethodsWe reviewed medical records of COVID-19-positive outpatients and inpatients from Wenling Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (January 2022-April 2024). Logistic regression (Python) evaluated hospitalization predictors. Paired t-tests (SPSS) and ICD-11-coded disease comparisons assessed pre/post-infection changes.ResultsAmong 664 patients, outpatients (mean age 41.99) were younger than inpatients (mean age 74.48), with more females in outpatient care (57.69% vs. 43.45% inpatient). Patients aged 71–85 years comprised over 50% of hospitalizations. Women showed more mild cases while men had more severe infections. Logistic analysis associated hospitalization with age and urban residency, not gender. Rural residents had higher hospitalization rates than urban populations. Hospitalization odds increased with patient age (0–80 years). Outpatients demonstrated increased digestive system disorders post-infection, while inpatients showed 13.42% more total diseases, particularly neurological disorders. Outpatient disease outcomes remained stable pre/post-infection (P = 0.924), whereas inpatients showed significant changes (P = 0.031).ConclusionsHospitalization correlated with age and urban/rural status, peaking in 71-85-year-olds (predominantly male). Rural residents faced higher hospitalization risk. Age showed linear relationship with hospitalization likelihood (50–70 years). Post-infection neurological disorders in inpatients and digestive issues in outpatients require clinical attention. More females sought outpatient care, though gender didn’t predict hospitalization.Keywords: COVID-19, Epidemiology, Hospitalization, Neurological disorders, Urban-rural disparity

Background

COVID-19 spread globally as a pandemic, as of 8 March 2023, more than 6.82 million people had died globally from COVID-19 since it was first detected in December 2019 (according to the World Health Organization, COVID-19 Weekly Epidemiological Update Edition 133 published 8 March 2023). Following China’s discontinuation of its zero-COVID policy on 7 December 2022, a surge of new cases emerged that month [1–3], with an estimated 90%−97% of the Chinese population being infected within December 2022 [3]. While primarily a respiratory pathogen, SARS-CoV-2 demonstrated multiorgan tropism affecting the digestive, cardiovascular, neurological and reproductive systems [4, 5]. Acute COVID-19 infection was frequently followed by neuropsychiatric symptoms, with some persisting for 6–12 months [6–9]. During this time period, both moderately severe COVID-19 patients, as well as mild or asymptomatic patients [10], had been shown to be in poor health or associated with COVID-19 viral infection, which caused severe influenza-like symptoms and promotes the release of, among other things, inflammatory factors [11], increased thromboembolism and thrombosis of the systemic microvasculature [12, 13], and, through d - dimer elevated levels of d-dimer, and a procoagulant state in the body [14]. Japanese populations demonstrated BP elevations of 1–2 mmHg (systolic) and 0.4–1.4 mmHg (diastolic), while US studies reported increases of 1.5–2.5 mmHg and 1.1–1.5 mmHg, respectively [15, 16]. Psychologically, it increased the risk of depressive symptoms, anxiety, suicidal ideation and suicide rates [17–22]. Several gender- and economy-specific studies had found a greater effect of COVID-19 on suicide rates or suicidal ideation [23–25], anxiety [23, 26], or depression [23, 27] during the COVID-19 pandemic, and even poor mental health symptoms had been documented in patients with COVID-19 up to 6 months after discharge from hospital [28–30].

In recent years, with the rapid development of information technology, compared with the traditional statistical model, machine learning demonstrated superior predictive power, accuracy, modeling capability, and large-scale data analysis capacity [31], especially artificial neural networks and deep learning applications have been expanding across multiple domains [32]. Machine learning has proven clinically effective in medical applications [33–35]. Currently this new technique was often applied to epidemiology as well. In this study, we employed Python-based logistic regression modeling, in order to more efficiently and rationally refer to epidemiological studies to provide reference value for clinical treatment.

While prior studies examined hospitalization predictors, few analyzed post-infection comorbidity shifts in the same cohort, particularly in rural Chinese populations. A critical clinical question involves optimizing treatment pathways through analysis of pre- and post-infection epidemiological changes. The aim of this study was to establish an evidence-based framework for COVID-19 treatment strategies, and to inform policy development and guideline updates. This study had two principal aims: (1) as a primary objective, to develop and validate a predictive model for COVID-19 hospitalization risk based on demographic factors (age, gender, and urban/rural status); and (2) as a secondary objective, to characterize differences in post-infection disease patterns between hospitalized and outpatient cohorts.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wenling Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine affiliated to Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (Approval No. 2024-032; 27/01/2024). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Medical record data screening

All patients included in this cohort were confirmed to be SARS-CoV-2 positive via reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) testing of nasopharyngeal swab samples. This method was consistently used throughout the study period (January 2022 - April 2024) as the standard diagnostic criterion in our hospital system, ensuring a uniform and reliable case definition. The assay results were interpreted by evaluating the exponential amplification pattern and cycle threshold (Ct) values across fluorescence channels: a sample was deemed positive in the FAM or HEX channel when an exponential amplification curve was observed with a Ct value < 38, while negative results were assigned when either no exponential growth occurred or the Ct value was ≥ 40; for the ROX channel, positivity required exponential amplification with a Ct < 35, and negativity was defined by the absence of exponential growth or a Ct ≥ 35. This multi-channel analysis framework ensures standardized interpretation of viral nucleic acid detection outcomes while accounting for channel-specific amplification efficiencies. We extracted medical records for all outpatients and inpatients with confirmed COVID-19 (1 January 2022–30 April 2024) from the medical record system of Wenling Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. We selected patients with: (1) a hospital visit prior to their first positive COVID-19 test, and (2) at least one subsequent visit post-diagnosis. Duplicate records were then eliminated based on the patient’s registered identification number at the time of the visit (which is a unique identification number for each person in China), and patients who had cross-visited the outpatient and inpatient settings were also eliminated. A total of 0 patients were actually found to be eligible for outpatient and inpatient crossover visits, i.e., no patients were found to have been seen in both outpatient and inpatient settings sequentially during that time period. After that, since the date of the emergency room visit routinely defaults to 3 days, we then kept the two visits that had the last and first dates of the visit greater than or equal to 4 days apart. For patients with > 2 visits, we analyzed only the earliest and most recent visits. The Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) diagnosis was excluded from the diagnosis of all enrolled patients. Then the patients were divided into three groups according to whether they attended outpatient or inpatient before or after COVID-19 infection: outpatient patients, inpatient patients, and outpatient and inpatient cross-visit patients. Status of software used

The specific version number of Python was 3.10.5 (tags/v3.10.5:f377153, Jun 6 2022, 16:14:13) MSC v.1929 64 bit (AMD64). Python utilized several key libraries: NumPy (large-scale mathematical computations), Pandas (data processing/analysis), scikit-learn (machine learning/data mining), and statsmodels (statistical modeling). The “scikit-learn” library for machine learning and data mining, and the “statsmodels” library for estimating many different statistical models, as well as for statistical testing and statistical data exploration. We implemented logistic regression models using these Python packages. The software SPSS (version 22.0) was used to analyse paired t-tests and other tests of significance between different factors before and after the different COVID-19 diseases.

Urban or rural demarcation method

Patients included in this analysis were divided into rural and urban groups according to whether they lived in the countryside or town. Patients with addresses containing plots, roads, or streets were classified as urban. Village addresses with limited road infrastructure defined rural classification [36].

Clinical diseases were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 11th edition (ICD-11), which was developed by the World Health Organisation (WHO) to provide a comprehensive and detailed classification of diseases, with the main categories listed below. ‘Tumors’ encompassed all neoplasms, both benign and malignant. Immune system disorders refer to abnormal reactions or dysfunctions involving the immune system, such as autoimmune diseases (e.g. rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus) and immunodeficiency diseases. Endocrine, nutritional or metabolic disorders refer to disorders affecting the function of endocrine glands, the absorption and utilisation of nutrients or energy metabolism processes, such as diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism and obesity. Neurological disorders refer to those affecting the brain, spinal cord, or peripheral nerves, such as stroke, epilepsy, and Parkinson’s disease. Circulatory disorders refer to dysfunctions involving the heart and blood vessels, such as hypertension, coronary heart disease, and heart failure. Respiratory disorders involved pulmonary and airway pathologies, including COVID-19, asthma, and pneumonia. Diseases of the digestive system refer to dysfunctions involving the oesophagus, stomach, intestines, liver, gallbladder and other digestive organs, such as gastritis, gastric ulcer, hepatitis, etc. Initial severity stratification attempts yielded insufficient cases (n = 29 severe) for robust subgroup analysis according to oxygen/ICU requirements.

Methods of analysis

We selected predictors (age, gender, and urban/rural status) based on clinical relevance and prior evidence while avoiding automated selection methods to minimize overfitting risks, and confirmed the absence of multicollinearity through correlation coefficient analysis (all < 0.3) and by verifying logical independence between variables such as age and gender. By extracting the medical record information of COVID-19 virus test-positive patients in the medical record system of Wenling Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine during the period of 1 January 2022-30 April 2024, we applied the logistic regression model based on the Python software to analyse the results of the need for hospitalisation after COVID-19 infection in relation to the genders, age and “Urban residents or not " the correlation between these three factors. The logistic regression model was implemented using Python’s statsmodels library. The optimization process converged successfully after 7 iterations. The final model for predicting the log-odds of hospitalization is given by the formula: logit(p) = 5.547 - (0.122 × age) - (0.246 × gender) + (1.011 × urban_residency), where p represents the probability of hospitalization. The full statistical details of the model, including standard errors and confidence intervals, are available in Table 2. Paired t-tests and other significance analysed between different factors before and after COVID-19 infection were performed using SPSS to study the effect of COVID-19 on patients’ disease states and access to care.

Results

Basic distribution of medical records

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of our 664 COVID-19 patients. The outpatient cohort (n = 130) comprised a higher proportion of females (57.69%, n = 75) and was younger (mean age 41.99 ± 18.04 years). In contrast, the inpatient cohort (n = 534) included a lower proportion of females (43.45%, n = 232) and was significantly older (mean age 74.48 ± 12.70 years). Secondly, the total number of cases in both urban and rural areas was 645, excluding 19 patients who could not be identified as either rural or urban. Residency distribution was identified for 645 patients (97.1% of the cohort). Among outpatients, 50.00% (n = 65) resided in rural areas, compared to 60.67% (n = 324) of inpatients. We systematically analyzed antiviral medication use across our cohort (n = 664). Among hospitalized patients (n = 534), 246 (46.1%) received antiviral therapy, including COVID-19-specific agents: molnupiravir (n = 137, 55.0%), azvudine (n = 83, 33.3%), nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (n = 15, 6.0%), and monulpiravir capsules (n = 14, 5.6%). Twenty-seven inpatients received other antivirals, primarily hepatitis B medications (entecavir/tenofovir, n = 16) and anti-herpetics (n = 8). Outpatient antiviral use was less frequent (8/130, 6.2%), predominantly anti-herpetics (n = 5) and influenza antivirals (n = 2).

Logistic modelling results

Overall results

The logistic regression model converged successfully after 7 iterations, indicating stable estimation of the parameters.

Model performance metrics results

The model demonstrated high accuracy (0.922), correctly classifying 92.2% of cases. The model demonstrated high precision (0.85), sensitivity (0.708), and an F1-score of 0.773, indicating a strong balance between positive predictive power and case identification. A higher value indicated that the model striked a better balance between precision and recall. Specificity was high (0.971), and the excellent area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.974 confirms the model’s strong overall discriminative capacity. The Confusion Matrix values were as follows: the True Negatives (TN) value of 102 indicated that the model correctly identified 102 negative examples. A False Positives (FP) value of 3 meant that the model incorrectly identified 3 true negatives as positive examples. A False Negatives (FN) value of 7 meant that the model incorrectly identified 7 positive examples as negative. True Positives (TP) value of 17 indicated that the model correctly identified 17 positive examples.

Logistic model analysis

In order to investigate which factors are associated with the outcome of hospitalisation after COVID-19 infection, a logistic regression model was constructed with age, gender and “Urban residence or not” as potential predictors. We constructed the logistic model through standard steps of data preprocessing, training, and validation. The results of the analyses showed that the model was well designed and that the factors " Urban residents or not " and age were significantly correlated with the outcome of “need for hospitalisation”, while gender was not correlated. Age showed a very significant correlation (P<0.001), as well as " Urban residents or not " with P = 0.001(P<0.05), while gender did not show a correlation with P = 0.410(P>0.05).The results predict that older people, compared to younger people, and rural residents, compared to urban residents, were more likely to become more ill and require hospitalisation when infected with the COVID-19 virus. Discussions

Some of the phenomena following a pandemic of COVID-19 infection, such as sequelae, might be related to Long COVID infection [37]. Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) is the causative agent of COVID-19 and was usually associated with long-term symptoms known as the post-COVID-19 state or LONG covid [38]. According to the WHO, Long COVID virus is defined as persistent or new symptoms 3 months after the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection and symptoms persisting for more than 2 months [39]. Long COVID was associated with all ages and acute-phase disease severity, with the highest proportion of diagnoses occurring between the ages of 36 and 50 years, and most Long COVID cases occured in ambulatory patients with mild acute disease [40]. A study of immune dysregulation in Long COVID patients with mild acute COVID-19 found that elevated PD1 expression in central memory cells persisted for more than 13 months [41]. Our findings align with these reports and warrant further study.

Logistic model precision and room for improvement. Overall, the model performed relatively well on the test set, with high accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score and specificity, and high AUC-ROC values, and the confusion matrix showed that the model performed well in identifying both “positive examples” and “negative examples”. The confusion matrix showed that the model performs better in identifying both “positive” and “negative” cases. While model performance was strong, limitations included limited simulations and opportunities to reduce false negatives/positives. At the same time, further adjustment of the model parameters or the use of other advanced techniques could be considered to improve the performance of the model, so as to enhance the accuracy of the results.

Models such as Logistic regression should be valued in clinical epidemiological studies. In clinical epidemiological research, Logistic regression was used as a widely used statistical method for problems such as binary classification. Through the application of Logistic regression model, some key factors affecting the occurrence and development of diseases could be explored, so as to provide a scientific statistical basis for the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of diseases. Logistic regression model can deal with the situation in which the dependent variable is dichotomous, which is especially common in clinical research, such as the presence or absence of diseases, and the effectiveness or ineffectiveness of treatments. This application is important for developing better treatment plans and formulating disease coping strategies. Excellent models like Logistic regression should be used more often in epidemiology.

The field of infection should apply artificial intelligence and databases such as Python and its diverse “libraries”. Python, as an efficient and flexible programming language, is increasingly being used in the field of medicine, including epidemiological research. Its powerful data processing capabilities, rich scientific computing libraries, and ease of learning and use make Python one of the preferred tools for pharmaceutical data analysis, model building and optimisation. At the same time, the rapid development of artificial intelligence models, especially deep learning and machine learning technologies, has brought unprecedented changes to the research field. These models are capable of handling large-scale medical data, mining hidden disease patterns, predicting disease progression, and so on.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, its retrospective design at a single center, while providing detailed clinical data, inherently introduces the potential for unmeasured confounding and limits the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings and populations. The patient cohort, drawn from a specific regional hospital in China, may not be representative of national or international demographics and healthcare practices. Second, as with all studies reliant on administrative data, the accuracy and completeness of ICD-11 coding are paramount. Misclassification or under-coding of diagnoses, particularly for milder or asymptomatic conditions, could bias the reported prevalences of post-COVID sequelae. Furthermore, individuals with mild or asymptomatic infections who did not seek medical care are absent from our dataset, potentially leading to an overestimation of the severity and frequency of complications in the broader infected population. Third, while our logistic regression model identified significant predictors of hospitalization, its performance and the relevance of these predictors should be validated in independent, prospective cohorts. The model’s predictors (age, residency) are also non-modifiable, offering limited mechanistic insight despite their strong associative value. Finally, the study period (2022–2024) encompasses the emergence of various Omicron subvariants and evolving vaccination rates. These temporal factors, which we could not fully adjust for, likely influence infection severity and outcomes, affecting the temporal validity of our estimates. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as robust within the context of this specific cohort and time period but require confirmation through larger, multi-center prospective studies designed to account for these limitations.

Conclusions

Outpatients (mean age 41.99 years) were significantly younger than inpatients (mean age 74.48 years), with females comprising 57.69% of outpatient cases compared to 43.45% of inpatient admissions. While logistic analysis confirmed established epidemiological risk factors for hospitalization (older age and rural residency), this study revealed critical post-infection clinical divergences: Outpatients exhibited a 71.43% increase in digestive system disorders despite overall stable disease burden (P = 0.924), suggesting that even mild cases may warrant clinical vigilance for gastrointestinal sequelae. Conversely, hospitalized patients demonstrated a 13.42% increase in total disease burden (P = 0.031) with marked progression of neurological disorders. These findings highlight an association between severe COVID-19 and neurological sequelae, suggesting a potential need for long-term neurological and mental health surveillance in this patient population. These observational insights warrant further investigation in prospective studies to confirm causality and establish clinical guidelines. Integration of machine learning-driven models into clinical epidemiology could be essential for advancing data-driven, personalized post-COVID care.
