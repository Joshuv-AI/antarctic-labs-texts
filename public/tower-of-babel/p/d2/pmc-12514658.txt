The value of using deep learning to predict proliferative hepatocellular carcinoma based on multiphasic magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Fengxi Chen, Yuxin He, Lizhen Liu, Jie Cheng, Yiman Li, Yang Xu, Xiaofeng Li, Huarong Zhang, Kaiyi Yuan, Wei Chen, Ping Cai, Xiaoming Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProliferative hepatocellular carcinoma (PHCC) is an aggressive subtype of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) characterized by high recurrence rates and poor prognosis. Accurate preoperative identification of PHCC is essential for prognostic assessment and individualized treatment planning. However, conventional imaging methods often fail to reliably diagnose PHCC. This study aimed to develop an effective deep learning (DL)-based model using multiphasic gadoxetate disodium (Gd-EOB-DTPA)-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to preoperatively predict PHCC.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study included 1,111 HCC patients who underwent Gd-EOB-DTPA-enhanced MRI followed by curative resection between January 2015 and June 2021. Patients were allocated to training (n=818), internal (n=150), and external (n=143) validation cohorts. A hybrid model combining DL and clinical-radiological features was developed using the AutoGluon framework. The clinical-radiological model was constructed using multivariate logistic regression, whereas the DL model was developed utilizing DL confidence scores derived from arterial and venous phase images. Model performance was assessed by the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and recurrence-free survival (RFS) was analyzed using the log-rank test.ResultsThe hybrid model demonstrated superior performance in both the training and internal validation cohorts, achieving AUCs of 0.922 (95% CI: 0.900–0.939) and 0.894 (95% CI: 0.834–0.934), respectively. In the external validation cohort, the hybrid and DL models demonstrated comparable performance (AUC: 0.771 vs. 0.788; P=0.065). Notably, the DL model outperformed the clinical-radiological model in predicting PHCC, with AUCs of 0.857 (95% CI: 0.781–0.912) vs. 0.747 (95% CI: 0.652–0.825) in the internal validation cohort and 0.788 (95% CI: 0.686–0.865) vs. 0.625 (95% CI: 0.505–0.735) in the external validation cohort (all P<0.05). Furthermore, patients classified as high-risk by the hybrid model had significantly shorter RFS compared to those in the low-risk group (P<0.05).ConclusionsThe hybrid model showed potential for predicting PHCC, which may assist clinicians in making personalized treatment decisions.Keywords: Proliferative, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), deep learning (DL), gadoxetate disodium, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common primary malignant tumors (1). The 5-year recurrence rate is approximately 70% after curative liver resection (2). HCC demonstrates heterogeneity at both molecular and histological levels, leading to diverse prognostic outcomes following treatment (3,4). Recently, Calderaro et al. (4) proposed dividing HCC into proliferative and non-proliferative classes based on their molecular and histological characteristics. Proliferative HCC (PHCC), which accounts for 30–50% of all HCCs, includes macrotrabecular-massive HCC (MTM-HCC), sarcomatoid HCC, sclerosing HCC, and cytokeratin 19 (CK19) positive HCC, and is mainly associated with TP53 mutation, chromosomal instability, and abnormal epigenetic changes (5). Compared to non-proliferative HCC (non-PHCC), PHCC is often poorly differentiated, prone to vascular invasion, and linked to high recurrence (4,6). Previous studies have shown that patients with PHCC generally have a worse prognosis than those with non-PHCC after surgery or conventional transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) (7,8); however, they may benefit from targeted therapy or immunotherapy (9). Consequently, accurate identification of PHCC before treatment has significant therapeutic and prognostic implications.

Current clinical diagnosis of aggressive PHCC mainly relies on histopathological evaluation. However, biopsy is invasive and carries risks such as bleeding, tumor seeding, and insufficient sampling for accurate diagnosis (10,11). Therefore, accurate preoperative diagnosis of PHCC using noninvasive methods is an urgent clinical need. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging, offers a noninvasive approach for detecting HCC. It enables detailed evaluation of tumor vascularity, reflecting angiogenesis and potentially aggressive tumor behavior (12,13). Several studies have demonstrated the correlation between MRI features and HCC subtypes (7,12,14). For example, one study (7) reported that the rim arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE) was more common in PHCC. Another recent study reported that PHCC exhibited low stiffness and high fluidity based on magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) (15). However, these studies have focused mainly on qualitative features of imaging and lacked features extracted by automated methods, such as deep learning (DL).

DL is an emerging diagnostic tool that extracts hidden information from medical images, aiding cancer diagnosis and treatment. Unlike traditional methods, DL does not rely on predefined features and can uncover subtle patterns beyond the scope of conventional radiology (16). Numerous studies have demonstrated the application of DL in various aspects of HCC, including the diagnosis of liver lesions (16-19), prognosis after treatment (20,21), and microvascular invasion (MVI) (22-24). Qu et al. (25) employed a DL model using DCE-MRI, showing excellent performance in predicting PHCC and early postoperative recurrence risk. However, the limited sample size might reduce its clinical applicability.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a model based on DL to predict PHCC using a large-scale MRI dataset. To overcome the limitations of manual feature selection and model tuning, we employed the AutoGluon framework (Amazon Web Services, Seattle, WA, USA), which enabled a fully automated pipeline that enhanced classification accuracy, reproducibility, and clinical relevance. Ultimately, the constructed model is designed to assist clinicians in the preoperative identification of PHCC and the personalization of treatment strategies. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD+AI reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2024-2656/rc).

Methods

Study population

This retrospective multicenter study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and was approved for data use by the ethics committees of all three participating institutions: Southwest Hospital, Army Medical University (approval No. (B)KY2023107); The Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2021-KeLunShen-240); and The Third Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (approval No. 2023-KeLunShen-023). Given the retrospective design and the use of de-identified data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committees of all participating institutions.

A total of 4,647 consecutive HCC patients confirmed by surgical pathology at the three institutions were included between January 2015 and June 2021. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients with complete clinical data, and pathological reports, including pathological classification, MVI, and CK19 status; (II) gadoxetate disodium (Gd-EOB-DTPA) MRI was performed within 1 month before surgery, with a laboratory test interval of <7 days; (III) solitary intrahepatic lesion; (IV) no visible vascular invasion on imaging, and without intrahepatic or extrahepatic metastasis; (V) no prior history of antitumor therapy before surgery. According to the inclusion criteria, a total of 1,111 patients were enrolled in the final analysis. Among them, HCC patients from Southwest Hospital, Army Medical University (institution I) were randomly divided into a training cohort (n=818) and an internal validation cohort (n=150) in an 8:2 ratio. Patients from The Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (institution II) and The Third Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (institution III) were used as the external validation cohort (n=143) (Figure 1).

General clinical and laboratory data of the patients were collected, including gender, age, presence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity, total bilirubin (TBIL), albumin, white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, and α-fetoprotein (AFP) level.

Histopathologic analysis

An abdominal pathologist (H.Z., with 15 years of experience in liver pathology) reviewed all surgical specimens from the three institutions and recorded the CK19 status, MVI status, and histomorphological subtype of each tumor according to the 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) classification (26,27). HCCs with CK19 expression in more than 5% of tumor cells were defined as CK19-positive and classified as PHCC (7). Other subtypes such as MTM, neutrophil-rich, scirrhous, and sarcomatoid were also categorized as PHCC, whereas steatohepatitis, clear cell, lymphocyte-rich, and CK19-negative conventional HCC were designated as non-PHCC.

MRI image acquisition

MRI was performed using 3.0 T systems (MAGNETOM TrioTim, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany; GE Discovery MR750W, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) and a 1.5 T system (Signa, GE Healthcare, USA). Initially, axial T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), in-phase and opposed-phase imaging, and unenhanced T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) were performed. DCE scanning was then conducted using Gd-EOB-DTPA (Primovist, Bayer Schering Pharma, Berlin, Germany), which was injected into an elbow vein at a rate of 1.0 mL/s with a dose of 0.1–0.2 mL/kg. When the contrast agent reached the level of the lower aorta, patients were instructed to hold their breath to initiate the arterial phase (AP). The portal venous phase (VP) was acquired 55–70 seconds after injection, followed by the transitional phase at 150–180 seconds post-injection. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) was performed using a breathing-triggered single-shot echo-planar imaging technique, with b-values of 0, 400, and 800 s/mm2. The b-values of 0 and 800 s/mm2 were used to calculate the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map using a monoexponential model. Finally, the hepatobiliary phase (HBP) was acquired 15 minutes after contrast administration (28).

Image analysis

Two independent abdominal radiologists (Y.L. and J.C., with 11 and 12 years of experience in liver imaging, respectively), who were blinded to the clinical, pathological and follow-up results, interpreted all magnetic resonance (MR) images. In cases of disagreement regarding the interpretation of imaging features between the two radiologists, a third senior radiologist (P.C., with 20 years of experience in liver imaging) was consulted to reach a consensus. Image segmentation and DL confidence (DLC) extraction

Tumor boundaries were manually delineated on all individual sequence images (AP, VP, and HBP) to define volumes of interest (VOIs) by two radiologists (Xiaoming Li and Xiaofeng Li, with 13 and 15 years of experience in liver imaging, respectively) using the Deepwise research platform (version 1.6.3.7, Beijing Deepwise Borui Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, http://www.deepwise.com).

For each volume across all cohorts, images were first resampled to a voxel size of 1.25×1.25×2.5 mm using bilinear interpolation. Based on the manual segmentation, the VOI was extracted from the MRI sequences and centered on the tumor lesion. To capture surrounding tissue context, the bounding box was expanded by 10 pixels in all three dimensions. The resulting volume was then resized to 48×48×32 using trilinear interpolation, and the image intensity was normalized to have a zero mean and unit standard deviation.

We trained a 3D-ResNet-18 model with pretrained weights for each MRI sequence to generate a PHCC risk score. This score, referred to as the DLC, reflects the reliability of the single-sequence volume, with higher DLC values indicating greater reliability. Further methodological details are available in Appendix 1. Among all sequences, only the AP and VP achieved validation areas under the curve (AUCs) above 0.80 in the training cohort. Therefore, only AP-DLC and VP-DLC were retained for further analysis.

The model construction and validation

In the training cohort, both univariate and multivariate regression analyses were conducted to identify clinical and radiological variables associated with PHCC. For multivariate analysis, a backward stepwise selection method was employed, and only variables with a P value below 0.05 were retained for model development.

We conducted automated machine learning (ML) modeling using the AutoGluon framework (29), an open-source framework capable of automating ML modeling for images, text, time series, and tabular data. It includes various commonly used ML models, with fast training speeds and no need for manual parameter tuning. This process incorporated AP-DLC, VP-DLC, and selected clinical-radiological factors as inputs. A total of 13 baseline ML models were trained, along with a weighted ensemble model that adaptively integrated the outputs of the base models to generate a final diagnostic probability at the patient level. The overall workflow is shown in Figure 3. Based on this process, three models were developed: (I) clinical-radiological model using only clinical and radiological variables; (II) DL model based on AP-DLC and VP-DLC; (III) hybrid model, combining the clinical-radiological and DL models. The model’s classification performance was evaluated using metrics including AUC, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Finally, the best-performing model was further validated in the internal and external validation cohorts.

Patient follow-up after surgical resection

All patients followed a standardized surveillance protocol after curative resection, with follow-up every 3–6 months. Assessments included serum AFP measurements, enhanced computed tomography (CT), or MRI. Tumor recurrence was confirmed by definitive evidence of new lesions on CT or MRI scans. The final follow-up was completed on 1 February 2024. The primary endpoint was recurrence-free survival (RFS), defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the first documented recurrence, metastasis, or last follow-up (30).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed with the software SPSS 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc version 20.0.22 (MedCalc, Ostend, Belgium). Categorical variables were compared between groups using the χ2 test, and continuous variables using the Mann-Whitney U or t-tests. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent risk factors associated with PHCC. Model performance was assessed by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). DeLong’s test was performed for the comparison of AUCs. Patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the optimal cutoff determined by the Youden index. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to analyze RFS in PHCC and non-PHCC groups, with statistical significance assessed by the log-rank test. A P value <0.05 (two-tailed) was considered significant.

In the training cohort, there were statistically significant differences in AFP levels, tumor size, and MVI positivity between PHCC and non-PHCC (P<0.001). PHCC was more likely to exhibit irregular shape, incomplete capsule, arterial phase peritumoral enhancement, and peritumoral HBP hypointensity compared to non-PHCC (Table 1).

Clinical-radiologic model development and evaluation

Univariate analysis identified AFP levels, tumor size, irregular shape, incomplete capsule, arterial phase peritumoral enhancement, and peritumoral HBP hypointensity as significant predictors of PHCC in the training cohort (all P<0.05). Multivariate analysis further demonstrated that AFP levels, tumor size, incomplete capsule, and peritumoral HBP hypointensity were independent risk factors for predicting PHCC. These four variables were used to construct the clinical-radiological model (Table 2), which yielded an AUC of 0.625 (95% CI: 0.505–0.735) in the external validation cohort (Table 3).

DL model development and evaluation

The AutoGluon framework showed that Weighted_ensemble_model_L2—a stacked ensemble model of 13 ML models, was the best performer. The AUCs for all models in the training cohort are listed in Table S1. The DL model integrating AP-DLC and VP-DLC demonstrated strong performance, with AUCs of 0.901 (95% CI: 0.875–0.922), 0.857 (95% CI: 0.781–0.912), and 0.788 (95% CI: 0.686–0.865) in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively (Table 3).

Hybrid model development and evaluation

The AP-DLC, VP-DLC, and the clinical-radiological variables (AFP levels, tumor size, incomplete capsule, and peritumoral HBP hypointensity) were considered as input variables to build the hybrid model. This model achieved AUCs of 0.922 (95% CI: 0.900–0.939), 0.894 (95% CI: 0.834–0.934), and 0.771 (95% CI: 0.661–0.854) in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively (Table 3).

Survival and recurrence after surgery for HCC

The mean follow-up was 38.8, 30.0, and 48.7 months in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that patients with histologically confirmed PHCC had a significantly lower RFS than that of those with non-PHCC (P<0.001, P=0.001, P=0.005). A hybrid model score of 0.235 was identified as the optimal cutoff for risk stratification. Patients were classified into either the low-risk group (score <0.235) or the high-risk group (score ≥0.235). Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed that the high-risk group had shorter RFS than the low-risk group in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts (P<0.001, P=0.022, P=0.036) (Figure 6).

Discussion

Accurate identification of PHCC has become increasingly important for guiding treatment decisions and evaluating prognosis. In this study, we developed a hybrid model that integrated clinical-radiological features with DL methods via the AutoGluon framework. The hybrid model achieved high predictive performance, with AUCs of 0.922, 0.894, and 0.771 in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively. Additionally, the hybrid model provided added value in prognostic risk stratification. These findings suggest that it could assist in the preoperative identification of PHCC and guide the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors (9), and thus support more precise diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Previous studies have shown that certain clinical-radiological features aided in the diagnosis of PHCC (7-9,31). In our study, PHCC was commonly associated with elevated AFP levels, larger tumor size, incomplete capsule, and positive MVI—findings that aligned with prior research (7,12,32-34). These features reflected the tumor’s aggressive biology and were associated with poor prognosis. Additionally, our study found that patients with PHCC were more likely to exhibit peritumoral HBP hypointensity, which has never been reported previously. This might be explained by altered peritumoral perfusion due to dysfunction of organic anion-transporting polypeptides in the surrounding hepatocytes (28). In this study, the clinical-radiological model was built using four variables: AFP level, tumor size, incomplete capsule, and peritumoral HBP hypointensity. However, its performance in the external validation cohort was suboptimal (AUC =0.625), which differs from previous reports (9,35). This discrepancy might have resulted from differences in the evaluation of imaging features between our study and previous reports. Moreover, the interpretation of radiological features was often subjective, and inter-observer consistency could vary considerably (36), which might further limit the reliability of such models in clinical practice.

DL can automatically extract subtle features from multimodal imaging data, enhancing image analysis and diagnostic accuracy (37). Consistent with the findings of Qu et al. (25), our DL models based on AP-DLC and VP-DLC showed strong performance in preoperative prediction of PHCC, with AUCs of 0.857 and 0.788 in the internal and external validation cohorts, respectively. The AP and VP represented key time points in contrast-enhanced MRI for capturing tumor hemodynamics. In clinical practice, HCC typically exhibits “wash-in” during AP and “wash-out” during VP. Previous studies (38,39) have shown that as tumor grade and invasiveness increased, hepatic arterial supply escalated while portal venous supply declined, indicating that hemodynamic patterns reflect tumor aggressiveness. This correlation likely explains why the combination of AP and VP features in our study achieved superior predictive performance for PHCC. Other MRI sequences (e.g., T1WI, T2WI, DWI) were excluded due to limitations such as redundancy, low contrast, and artifact susceptibility. Notably, the DL models outperformed the clinical-radiological model, supporting their potential to offer more discriminative biomarkers for PHCC prediction.

In this study, we further developed a hybrid model combining the clinical-radiological model with the DL model using the AutoGluon framework. The utilization of AutoGluon likely contributed to the strong performance of the hybrid model. Its ability to automatically train and synthesize multiple ML models (13 foundational models plus a weighted ensemble in our case) potentially captured complex patterns in the data more effectively than relying on a single, manually-tuned model (40). This automated approach also enhances reproducibility and reduces the potential for bias associated with manual model selection and hyperparameter tuning (41). Although data imbalance could pose challenges for model training, the robust ensembling strategies employed by AutoGluon are often resilient to moderate imbalances (29). Our study showed that the hybrid model outperformed the DL model in the training and internal validation cohorts. However, in the external validation cohort, the performance of the hybrid and DL models was comparable (P>0.05), which was consistent with findings from a previous study (25). This discrepancy was likely due to two main factors: (I) heterogeneity in MRI scanners and parameters across centers (42), and (II) distributional shifts in clinical-radiological features, such as tumor size, between the external and training cohorts, which limited model generalization. When the clinical-radiological model was combined with the DL model, it did not enhance performance synergistically and might have even reduced the predictive efficacy of the hybrid model (43). Notably, our findings highlighted the robust complementary value of the DL model in augmenting the predictive efficacy of the clinical-radiological model across all cohorts. However, the black-box nature of the DL model posed a major challenge for clinicians in interpreting its complex statistical algorithms (44).

Previous research has identified PHCC as an independent risk factor for recurrence after surgery, significantly affecting survival outcomes (7); our findings further validated that PHCC was associated with poorer RFS compared to non-PHCC. Additionally, risk groups were defined using the hybrid model’s optimal cutoff, stratifying patients into high- and low-risk PHCC groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that the high-risk group consistently exhibited significantly shorter RFS compared to the low-risk group across all cohorts. By enabling preoperative risk stratification, the hybrid model might serve as a useful tool for screening patients with potentially high-risk PHCC. This could assist clinicians in considering the possible use of targeted therapies or immunotherapies (45), planning more tailored postoperative surveillance schedules to enable earlier detection and prompt intervention upon recurrence, and conducting more informed prognostic evaluations (25).

This study had several limitations. First, its retrospective design may have introduced selection bias. Second, the study population was limited to Chinese patients, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other regions or ethnic groups. Third, certain MRI features, such as intratumoral ischemia or necrosis, and rim APHE, were not included in the analysis. Finally, the DL model was not applied to predict specific histopathological subtypes of HCC due to the imbalance in data distribution across subgroups.

Conclusions

Our study explored a hybrid model that combined a clinical-radiological model with a DL model for the preoperative prediction and prognostic assessment of PHCC, which might have potential to assist in identifying patients at higher risk and support clinical decision-making. Further prospective validation is warranted to confirm its clinical applicability.
