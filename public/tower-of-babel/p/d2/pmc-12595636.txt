Lessons from the pandemic: an interrupted time series analysis of food and non-food household expenditures in Iran
Authors: Mohammadreza Zakeri, Amirreza Radfar, Mohsen Bayati, Kamran Bagheri Lankarani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic disrupted household consumption worldwide. Evidence from middle-income countries remains limited. We examined changes in Iranian household expenditures before, during, and after the pandemic.MethodsWe used nationally representative Household Income and Expenditure Survey data (2014–2023; 381,302 households). Expenditures were CPI-deflated, and interrupted time series regression was applied to six major categories (food, housing, transport, health, education, and other) across three phases: pre-COVID (2014–2019), during COVID (2020–2021), and post-COVID (2022–2023). Budget shares and subgroup analyses (urban–rural) were also examined.ResultsBefore the pandemic, most real expenditures were declining. At the onset of COVID-19, sharp contractions were observed in transport, health, and non-essential categories, while food and housing remained relatively stable. Education showed no significant immediate decline. In the post-COVID period, food and housing increased but did not return to pre-pandemic trajectories. Health and non-essential spending remained depressed, whereas education recorded a significant but incomplete rebound. Budget shares shifted toward essentials, with food and housing rising, and education and other non-essentials declining. Urban–rural patterns were broadly consistent, although levels were higher among urban households.ConclusionsThe pandemic caused immediate and lasting structural shifts in household spending. Reallocation toward food and housing reflects pressure on discretionary categories such as education and health. Limitations include the absence of detailed income analysis and the overlap with other macroeconomic shocks. Targeted policies are needed to protect vulnerable groups and sustain investment in human capital.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25230-6.Keywords: COVID-19, Household expenditure, Food spending, Non-Food spending, Iran

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted economic activity worldwide, with households facing sharp reductions in income, heightened uncertainty, and altered consumption patterns [1, 2]. In response, many governments implemented stringent public health measures—such as lockdowns, quarantines, and social distancing—that, while necessary to limit transmission, had far-reaching consequences for daily life, including household spending behavior [3–5].

Global evidence shows heterogeneous impacts on household expenditures. In the United States, food and grocery spending remained stable or rose, while most discretionary categories declined sharply [6–8]. In the Philippines, overall household spending declined, yet expenditure on food, health, and digital services rose, particularly among rural and low-income populations [9]. Similarly, European evidence highlights significant declines in consumer confidence and household spending, with the most pronounced reductions observed in sectors such as tourism, hospitality, and retail, influenced by varying containment measures and socio-economic factors across countries [10, 11]. These heterogeneous effects highlight the need for region-specific analysis to inform evidence-based policy interventions.

In Iran, the pandemic coincided with long-standing macroeconomic vulnerabilities. Economic strain and access constraints reduced overall household spending, while dietary diversity also declined [12–15]. High inflation, repeated episodes of rial depreciation, and international sanctions further limited imports and fiscal capacity. Government measures—such as cash transfers and subsidy reforms—were unevenly implemented and only partially mitigated price shocks [16–18]. These concurrent pressures suggest that Iranian households may have experienced pandemic-related expenditure changes that differed in both magnitude and composition from those observed elsewhere.

While numerous studies have documented household expenditure shifts during COVID-19 in other settings, evidence from Iran remains limited and fragmented. To date, no study has systematically applied an interrupted time series design to nationally representative data to quantify both the immediate shock of COVID-19 and subsequent recovery in household expenditures. To address this gap, our study applies Interrupted Time Series (ITS) regression models alongside compositional budget share analysis on nationally representative, inflation-adjusted quarterly household expenditure data spanning 2014 to 2023. This integrated approach enables the quantification of both immediate shocks and longer-term trend changes in key consumption categories, offering a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of household financial behavior and expenditure recovery trajectories during and after the pandemic.

Method

Data source and sample

This study draws on microdata from the Household Income and Expenditure Surveys (HIES), compiled and published by the Statistical Center of Iran, the official national statistical authority. The HIES is conducted annually using a stratified multistage probability sampling design to ensure full representativeness across all provinces, including both urban and rural households. Following standard data cleaning procedures—such as the exclusion of records with incomplete, inconsistent, or extreme values—the final analytic sample comprised 381,302 households, observed across 40 quarterly periods spanning from the first quarter of 2014 (Q1 2014) through the fourth quarter of 2023 (Q4 2023).

Inflation adjustment and expenditure classification

To ensure temporal comparability, all nominal household expenditures were deflated using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) with 2016 as the reference year (CPI = 100), based on category-specific inflation rates published by the Central Bank of Iran. Real expenditures were classified into two primary domains: food and non-food. The non-food domain was further disaggregated into five key categories: housing, transport, health, education, and a residual “other” category, which includes semi-essential and discretionary items such as clothing, furnishings, recreation, communication, restaurants, and miscellaneous services.

Empirical strategy: interrupted time series regression

To assess the evolving effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on household expenditure trajectories, we employed an Interrupted Time Series (ITS) regression framework. This quasi-experimental design is well suited for evaluating the impact of external shocks that occur at clearly defined points in time, as it allows for the estimation of both immediate shifts in expenditure levels and longer-term changes in trend. Separate ITS models were estimated for each of the six major household expenditure categories: food, housing, transport, health, education, and a composite “other” group, which includes discretionary and semi-essential goods and services.

In addition to the main expenditure categories, we also applied the same ITS specification to the disaggregated non-essential (‘other’) subcategories (tobacco, clothing, furnishings, communication, recreation, restaurants, and miscellaneous goods and services). These results are reported in Supplementary Table 2.

The analysis was structured across three distinct phases that aligned with the progression of the pandemic and corresponding policy responses in Iran. The pre-COVID period, spanning from the first quarter of 2014 (Q1 2014) through the first quarter of 2020 (Q1 2020), served as the baseline for trend estimation. The COVID period, from Q2 2020 to Q1 2022, captured the initial shock of the pandemic and the period of associated restrictions and economic volatility. The post-COVID period, extending from Q2 2022 to Q4 2023, represented the recovery phase, in which household behaviors began to adjust to new structural conditions.

In this model, Yₜ represents the average real household expenditure in quarter t. The variable Time is a continuous quarterly trend ranging from 1 (Q1 2014) to 40 (Q4 2023). The variable COVID is a binary indicator equal to 1 during the pandemic period (Q2 2020 to Q1 2022), and 0 otherwise. Post is a binary variable equal to 1 during the post-COVID period (Q2 2022 to Q4 2023), and 0 otherwise. The interaction terms Time × COVID and Time × Post capture slope changes during and after the pandemic, respectively. The error term εₜ captures unexplained variation.

Each coefficient in the model has a distinct interpretation. The parameter β₁ reflects the underlying pre-pandemic trend in expenditures. β₂ captures the immediate change in the expenditure level at the onset of the COVID period, while β₃ estimates the change in trend during the pandemic relative to the baseline. β₄ measures the level shift occurring at the start of the post-COVID period, and β₅ reflects the slope change in expenditure trends during the recovery phase.

To ensure valid statistical inference, we applied Newey–West robust standard errors to correct for autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity commonly observed in quarterly economic data. This approach also mitigates potential inference issues related to non-stationary time series, which are typical in macroeconomic household data. We tested for unit roots using the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test with constant and trend specification. Results indicated that all real expenditure series were non-stationary in levels (Supplementary Table 1). To address this, we applied CPI deflation, included linear time trends, and estimated models with Newey–West corrected standard errors. All nominal expenditures were deflated using the national consumer price index (CPI), rebased to 2016 (CPI = 100), which is the official base year adopted by the Statistical Center of Iran during our study period. This provides consistency across categories and ensures comparability of real expenditures from 2014 to 2023. In addition, we controlled for seasonal variation by including quarterly dummy variables (Q2, Q3, and Q4) in each model, as spending patterns for several categories (such as education and transport) exhibited clear seasonal fluctuations. This model specification enables a detailed decomposition of the pandemic’s economic impact and helps distinguish between transitory disruptions and persistent changes in household consumption behavior.

Structural break diagnostics

To evaluate whether household expenditure series experienced a significant change in trajectory at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, we conducted a Chow test for a structural break. The interruption point was specified as the second quarter of 2020, consistent with the timing of widespread economic and social impacts of the pandemic in Iran. The Chow test formally compares model fit before and after the break and provides an F-statistic to assess whether the coefficients differ significantly across periods. Applying this procedure to all major expenditure categories—food, housing, transport, health, education, and the non-essential “other” group—enabled us to validate the appropriateness of the second quarter of 2020 as the interruption point for the interrupted time series analysis.

Using the household-level data, we calculated mean real expenditures by category for each phase (pre-COVID: 2014–2020, during COVID: 2021–2022, post-COVID: 2022–2023). Software and statistical implementation

All analyses were conducted using Stata version 17. Interrupted Time Series (ITS) models were implemented using standard OLS regression with segmented time variables and interaction terms to capture level and slope changes across the pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID phases. Newey–West standard errors were applied to address autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity. Quarterly seasonal dummies were included to adjust for periodic effects. Data visualization—including actual vs. fitted trends, structural break lines, and budget share dynamics—was performed using Python (matplotlib) and finalized in Microsoft Excel.

Food expenditures exhibited a modest pre-COVID decline (− 1.12 million rials per quarter). The estimated level shift at the onset of the pandemic was small and not statistically significant (− 1.2 m; −1.9% of the pre-COVID mean), and the post-COVID shift was similarly negligible (+ 1.2 m; +1.9%). In contrast, expenditure trajectories steepened significantly, with trend increases of + 5.8 m per quarter during COVID-19 and + 2.3 m per quarter thereafter.

Housing expenditures also declined prior to the pandemic (− 0.69 m per quarter). Both the immediate level change at onset (− 1.7 m; −3.5%) and the post-COVID shift (− 0.7 m; −1.4%) were not significant. Nonetheless, trend coefficients indicate sustained growth, rising by + 3.7 m per quarter during and + 5.6 m per quarter after the pandemic.

Transport expenditures fell sharply at COVID onset (− 2.8 m; −13.6%), while the subsequent post-COVID level change was small and non-significant (+ 1.6 m; +7.8%). Slope changes were positive across both phases (+ 1.3 m during; +1.2 m after).

Health expenditures recorded one of the steepest immediate contractions (− 5.1 m; −24.8%). A further non-significant decline was observed post-COVID (− 0.72 m; −3.5%). As with other categories, positive trend adjustments emerged during (+ 1.6 m per quarter) and after (+ 0.78 m per quarter) the pandemic.

Education displayed a small, non-significant increase at onset (+ 1.2 m; +19%) and a larger significant post-COVID rise (+ 2.9 m; +47%), though associated trend shifts were negligible (− 0.01 m during; +0.1 m after).

Expenditures on “Other” non-essential items were most affected, with an immediate contraction of − 14.2 m (− 16.7%), followed by a non-significant additional reduction post-COVID (− 7.4 m; −8.7%). Notably, trend effects were strongly positive, with increases of + 7.09 m per quarter during COVID-19 and + 5.3 m thereafter.

For reference, in the pre-COVID period (2014–2019), average quarterly household expenditures amounted to approximately 64 m rials on food, 49 m on housing, 21 m on transport, 21 m on health, 6 m on education, and 85 m on other categories (all CPI-adjusted, per household).

Disaggregated analyses of the “other” subcomponents confirmed broadly consistent dynamics across tobacco, clothing, furnishings, communication, recreation, restaurants, and miscellaneous goods and services (Supplementary Table 2). The magnitude of the COVID shock, however, varied: contractions were most pronounced in clothing (− 40%), recreation (− 20.9%), restaurants (− 15.9%), and miscellaneous goods (− 11.8%), while tobacco also declined by − 12%. In contrast, furnishings and communication showed negligible immediate changes. Despite positive post-COVID trend adjustments in most categories, these increases did not offset the level losses incurred at the onset of the pandemic. These disaggregated results are wholly consistent with the aggregate pattern observed for the combined “Other” category.

The share of food expenditures rose during the pandemic (from 26% to 28.2%) and remained above pre-COVID levels in the post-pandemic period (27.6%). Housing’s share increased slightly during and after COVID (19.9% → 21.4% → 22.6%). Transport’s share declined slightly during the pandemic (8.5% → 8.4%) and remained stable in the post-COVID period (8.4%). Education’s share fell steadily during and after COVID (2.5% → 1.5% → 1.1%). The “Other” non-essential category also contracted during COVID (34.7% → 33.1%) and decreased further in the post-COVID period (32.9%), underscoring the reallocation of household budgets away from discretionary consumption.

Urban–rural differences

Table 4 reports mean real household expenditures by category across urban and rural households. Several clear patterns emerge. First, food and housing consistently dominated household budgets in both groups. Urban households spent more in absolute terms, particularly on housing (68 m pre-COVID vs. 28.5 m in rural areas), reflecting structural differences in housing markets. Both categories remained stable during the COVID period in both groups and experienced a large increase in the post-COVID phase.

Second, the pandemic shock was evident across both groups. Transport and health expenditures declined during COVID in urban households (− 3.0 m and − 4.7 m, respectively) and a similar decrease in transport and a marginal increase in health among rural households (− 3.3 m and + 0.1 m, respectively), while education fell most sharply among urban(− 3.8 m) and rural households (− 1.4 m). Also, post-pandemic trajectories show increase in all three categories among both urban and rural households.

Third, the non-essential “Other (combined)” category exhibited the deepest contractions, dropping from 100.6 m to 87.6 m among urban households and from 70.2 m to 63.8 m among rural households. In the post-COVID phase, both groups displayed strong rebounds (urban: 124.1 m; rural: 117.6 m).

Overall, while the magnitude of spending was consistently higher among urban households, the direction and timing of changes were remarkably similar across groups.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the COVID-19 pandemic’s heterogeneous effects on household expenditure patterns in Iran over a ten-year period spanning pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic phases. Using Interrupted Time Series regression on inflation-adjusted data from over 380,000 nationally representative households, we identified significant but varied disruptions across six major spending categories: food, housing, transport, health, education, and discretionary “Other” items. Our findings reveal that while food expenditures experienced no significant immediate drop at the pandemic onset and showed strong positive trend increases during and after the pandemic, other categories, particularly discretionary expenditures, faced sharper immediate declines with slower recovery trajectories.

The pandemic was associated with sharp and immediate contractions in health, transport, and non-essential (“Other”) expenditures. Health spending fell by nearly one-quarter relative to pre-COVID levels, reflecting both reduced service utilization during lockdowns and heightened barriers to access. The “Other” category contracted by around 17%, driven largely by declines in clothing, recreation, restaurants, and miscellaneous consumption. Transport expenditures also dropped by about 14%, consistent with mobility restrictions and reduced travel demand. By contrast, food and housing were less affected in the short term, and both categories exhibited sustained positive trends in the post-COVID period. Education expenditures did not show a significant immediate decline during the pandemic. However, their budget share contracted, and although a significant post-COVID rebound in levels occurred, spending remained below pre-pandemic shares and lacked a sustained trend recovery.

Several studies have documented substantial reductions in total household expenditures during the pandemic, particularly in lower- and middle-income countries, including Nigeria, Iran, the Philippines, India, South Africa, and the United States [8, 9, 14, 19–22]. These findings reflect widespread economic disruptions, income losses, and heightened uncertainty that constrained household spending across diverse contexts. In line with this broader literature, our study also observed significant declines in real non-food expenditures during the pandemic period in Iran, underscoring the vulnerability of discretionary and semi-essential spending categories to external shocks. In contrast to the mixed evidence globally, where food expenditures either declined or shifted unevenly, food spending in Iran remained stable during the pandemic and rose thereafter—likely reflecting subsidy support, domestic production capacity, and household reprioritization under economic strain.

The reallocation of spending away from non-essential sectors toward necessities like food and housing illustrates the heightened financial pressure faced by households during the pandemic, consistent with previous findings [23–26]. Our results reveal a contraction in discretionary sectors such as health and other non-essential during the COVID period and remained below pre-pandemic levels in the post-COVID period, without meaningful rebound. These dynamics highlight the importance of well-targeted social protection mechanisms to buffer the economic impact of such systemic shocks and to support equitable recovery.

The Iranian experience parallels global patterns of household adjustment during the pandemic. Consistent with findings from the United States, South Africa, and Southeast Asia, households in Iran initially prioritized essential consumption, particularly food and health, even amidst overall declines in total spending [8, 21, 27, 28]. The rise in food’s budget share during the pandemic period signals a reactive shift toward perceived necessities. However, cumulative shortfall trends and subsequent reversals in budget al.location suggest that this shift was temporary and, in some cases, insufficient to offset broader welfare losses. The case of food expenditures is particularly illustrative: although post-COVID trends were positive, they were not strong enough to close the gap with pre-pandemic projections.

In addition, the protracted disruption in discretionary sectors—especially those captured under the “Other” category—aligns with international evidence on long-term declines in non-essential consumption [8, 29]. Iranian households, like their global counterparts, did not fully revert to pre-pandemic consumption patterns in education, recreation, or apparel. Structural changes in budget al.location and the magnitude and persistence of declines in these categories suggest not only temporary reduction but potential long-term underinvestment. This likely reflects a mix of income suppression, rising inflation, and a more cautious household budgeting behavior that may persist, particularly among financially constrained groups.

Subgroup analyses revealed important but parallel patterns. Urban households spent more overall, particularly on housing, but both urban and rural households experienced similar declines in transport, health, education, and non-essential spending. “Other” expenditures fell by 13% in urban and 9% in rural households, followed by strong rebounds in both groups. These findings suggest that while absolute levels differed, the direction and timing of shocks were broadly shared across household types. Comparable evidence from other settings, including Poland and African countries, likewise shows that while rural and urban households differ in absolute spending levels, the overall direction of pandemic-related shocks was largely consistent across groups [30, 31]. This parallelism underscores the universal nature of the COVID-19 shock, while also highlighting the need for context-specific policy responses to address structural gaps in expenditure resilience.

While this study offers valuable insights into household adjustment mechanisms during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of repeated cross-sectional data, rather than true panel data, restricts our ability to track individual households over time or to capture within-household coping strategies. Second, although we report descriptive differences by urban–rural status, we were unable to conduct a full distributional analysis by income quintile or household size due to data constraints; a detailed exploration of inequality dynamics therefore lies beyond the scope of this paper. Third, informal income flows, intra-household decision-making, and non-monetary coping mechanisms—especially among the most vulnerable—remain beyond the observable scope of the dataset. Finally, other concurrent shocks, including sanctions, subsidy reforms, and chronic inflation, cannot be fully disentangled from pandemic effects. As such, our interrupted time series estimates should be interpreted as capturing the combined influence of the pandemic and concurrent macroeconomic pressures.

Despite these limitations, the findings have important policy implications. Targeted measures are needed to protect household investment in health and education during economic shocks. Specific interventions could include maintaining or expanding health and food subsidies, indexing cash transfers to inflation, and providing tuition support or scholarships to sustain educational access. Protecting these categories is critical to preventing long-term erosion of human capital and to strengthening household resilience in the face of future crises.

Conclusion

Using interrupted time series regression and budget share analysis, this study examined the COVID-19 pandemic’s impact on household expenditures in Iran across food, housing, transport, health, education, and non-essential categories. Food spending remained stable during the crisis and rose thereafter but did not close pre-pandemic gaps. Housing and transport showed initial declines with partial recovery, while health fell sharply and remained below pre-COVID levels. Education rose insignificantly during the pandemic and signficantly post-COVID. Non-essential items, especially clothing and recreation, faced severe, prolonged declines. Urban households spent more overall, but both urban and rural groups experienced parallel shocks, highlighting the broad-based nature of the crisis. Budget shares shifted temporarily toward essentials, with partial post-crisis reversion. These findings underscore the need for targeted policies—such as inflation-indexed cash transfers, subsidies, and education support—to protect essential consumption and strengthen household resilience.
