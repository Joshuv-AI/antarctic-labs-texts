Understanding the consequences of being RhD immunized during pregnancy 10 years after introduction of targeted routine antenatal anti‐D prophylaxis: A retrospective nationwide cohort study
Authors: Anna Parhamaa, Riina Jernman, Mika Gissler, Katri Haimila, Inna Sareneva, Kati Sulin, Tiina Teivaanmäki, Suvi Toivonen, Susanna Sainio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTargeted routine antenatal anti‐D prophylaxis (RAADP) was introduced in Finland in 2014. The aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of anti‐D immunizations among pregnant women and the severity of hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) in affected pregnancies 10 years after RAADP was added to the national prevention program in the context of a rapidly declining birthrate in Finland.Material and MethodsA nationwide, retrospective cohort study included all RhD‐negative pregnancies in Finland between 2014 and 2023. Information on antenatal screening was obtained from the Finnish Red Cross Blood Service database, and obstetric and neonatal data from hospitals' records. Primary outcomes were prevalence of anti‐D immunization and severity of HDFN defined as severe (intrauterine transfusion, IUT), moderate (neonatal exchange transfusion/intravenous immunoglobulin, IVIG/top‐up transfusion), and mild (phototherapy). The secondary outcome was the effect of declining birth rates on the absolute numbers of anti‐D pregnancies.ResultsThe study included a total of 518 pregnancies of 383 women with anti‐D (367 pregnancies with RhD‐positive fetus/neonate). In 10 years, the prevalence of anti‐D among RhD‐negative pregnant women declined from 1.33% to 0.48% (reduction of 64%), and of severe HDFN from 0.20% to 0.06% (reduction of 72%). For an RhD‐positive fetus, the risk of severe HDFN was 17.7% (95% CI 13.9% to 22.0%), with an overall survival rate of 90.8%. For a RhD‐positive neonate not treated with IUTs, the risks of moderate and mild HDFN were each 33.0% (95% CI 24.4% to 42.6%). In addition to the effect of RAADP, the actual number of anti‐D cases decreased by 34.0% due to the 24% fall in the birth rate during the study period.ConclusionsTen years after the introduction of targeted RAADP, the prevalence of anti‐D immunizations was reduced by more than half. Declining birth rates further reduce overall case numbers but do not lessen the complexity of care needed. A significant proportion of anti‐D pregnancies still require close monitoring or intervention. Optimizing screening strategies, referral pathways, and readiness for neonatal treatment remains essential in the evolving landscape of HDFN prevention and management.

INTRODUCTION

Hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) is nowadays rare, but a potentially serious complication of pregnancy. Despite well‐established preventive measures used for more than 50 years, the most severe forms, leading to fetal or neonatal death or disability, are still related to anti‐D immunizations. 1 Approximately 9%–32% of pregnancies affected by anti‐D alloimmunization require intrauterine transfusions (IUTs). 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Reported live birth rates after IUTs vary from 88.9% to 100%. 6

In comparison to the clinical burden related to the treatment of severe HDFN, primary prevention of the disease is relatively easy and highly efficient. This includes administering anti‐D immunoglobulin (anti‐D prophylaxis) to RhD‐negative women in pregnancy and postpartum and matching for RhD in blood transfusions. Before the era of anti‐D immunoglobulin, anti‐D immunization occurred in 13%–16% of pregnancies of RhD‐negative women. 7 , 8 Introduced in the late 1960s, postpartum prophylaxis reduced this risk to approximately 1%–2% and is still the most effective intervention to prevent RhD‐related HDFN in subsequent pregnancies. 7 , 9 Routine antenatal anti‐D prophylaxis (RAADP) at the beginning of the third trimester, introduced in the 1990s in many countries, has further reduced antenatal immunization to 0.2%–0.3%. 10 , 11 , 12

In Scandinavia, RAADP was introduced only after 2010. By that time, large‐scale screening methods for determining fetal RhD type from maternal blood had become available, and RAADP was from the very beginning targeted at women carrying an RhD‐positive fetus. 4 , 13 As studies tend to focus on fetal RhD typing with excellent results, there are less data on the clinical effectiveness of these national programs. 4 , 14 Moreover, since the 1990s, when the original clinical trials of RAADP were performed (not to mention the postnatal trials from the late 1960s), Europe has faced major changes in population demographics. 15

In Finland, targeted RAADP was implemented effectively in the national maternity care in 2014, with over 98% of the RhD‐negative women participating in the new program by the end of 2016. 14 The high accuracy of the screening test for fetal RhD status enabled discontinuing cord blood typing to guide postpartum prophylaxis in 2018, further improving cost‐efficiency. Expectedly, the prevalence of pregnancies with anti‐D antibodies decreased from 1.52% in 2014 to 0.88% in 2017. 16 By that time and over the next few years, no reduction in the number of pregnancies requiring IUTs was seen. Reducing the burden of severe HDFN is, however, the main goal in any changes to national prevention programs and needs to be carefully evaluated.

Our aim was to assess (1) the prevalence of anti‐D immunizations among pregnant women, and (2) the severity of HDFN in affected pregnancies 10 years after RAADP was added to the national prevention program in the context of rapidly declining birth rate in Finland.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This was a retrospective, nationwide cohort study including all pregnant women with anti‐D antibodies (alone or with other antibodies) detected in the Finnish Red Cross (FRC) Blood Service database between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2023. During the study period, the total number of births was 490 288 with the annual birth rate decreasing 24% from 57 019 in 2014 to 43 121 in 2023. The total fertility rate declined by 26% from 1.71 to 1.26, which is below the European Union average of 1.38 and the lowest rate in the Nordic countries. 15 , 17 , 18

National screening and prevention program

All antenatal screening tests were performed in the FRC Blood Service laboratory. The national antenatal screening and anti‐D prophylaxis program has been described earlier. 14 Briefly: RhD‐negative women were screened at 10–12 weeks of gestation (1st screening), 24–26 weeks (2nd screening with testing of fetal RhD status), and 36 weeks (3rd screening). When indicated by the fetal RhD result, anti‐D prophylaxis was given to non‐immunized women at 28–30 weeks and at delivery. In potentially sensitizing events (PSEs), anti‐D prophylaxis was given to all women in case the fetal RhD status was not yet tested.

In cases with anti‐D detected in the 1st screening, to assess whether the fetus is RhD‐positive, typing of the father was performed as the first step. If the father was heterozygous (Dd), fetal RhD was determined from maternal plasma at 12 weeks of gestation or low‐titer anti‐Ds (<4) were monitored only by the titer. Antibody levels were titrated by using the tube saline indirect antiglobulin test with the conventional tube technique. Titers of ≥16 or a four‐fold rise were considered critical, indicating a pregnancy requiring clinical monitoring with ultrasound Doppler measurement to assess the risk of fetal anemia.

The nature of anti‐D detected for the first time was based on information on anti‐D prophylaxis. In uncertain cases, follow‐up tests were requested. Distinguishing between low‐titer immune anti‐D (true antibody) and passive anti‐D (anti‐D prophylaxis) was not possible at the 3rd screening, where only anti‐D with a titer of 4 or more was classified as immune.

Data source

The FRC Blood Service database was searched for the results of antenatal screening including information on anti‐D prophylaxis. Clinical patient data included the following variables: maternal age; gravidity (G); parity (P); antenatal treatment for HDFN (IUT); neonatal treatment for HDFN (exchange transfusion (EXT), top‐up transfusion, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), phototherapy); HDFN‐related fetal loss or neonatal death. Clinical data regarding pregnancies where IUT was performed were collected for the entire study period. Data on pregnancies where no antenatal treatment was required were available for newborns born in 2014–2017. After that, due to the EU General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR (and its strict interpretation in the 2019 Act on the Secondary Use of Health and Social Data in Finland), data collection from patient records has become unfeasible due to high costs and uncertainties in the data extraction. 19 , 20

National data on parturients and deliveries in 2014–2023, as well as data on causes of death were obtained from the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare and Statistics of Finland. The registration of births is complete in Finland with 99% of pregnant women participating in antenatal screening. As there is no register on spontaneous abortions in Finland, around 10% of registered pregnancies (with at least one screening sample available) are generally presumed to have ended in spontaneous or induced abortion. 21

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the prevalence of anti‐D immunizations among RhD‐negative pregnant women after introduction of RAADP (in 2014), and the severity of HDFN in affected pregnancies. The secondary outcome was to evaluate the accuracy (sensitivity, specificity) of the predefined cut‐off level for anti‐D titer ≥16 to detect all cases of severe and moderate HDFN.

Statistical analyses

The data were analyzed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0, IBM Corporation) and RStudio (4.2.2). Trends were tested using linear regression analysis and the Cochran‐Armitage test for trend. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. We applied an epidemiological decomposition approach to separate the individual contributions of RAADP and declining pregnancy rates to the overall change in anti‐D prevalence.

RESULTS

During the study period, there were 516 852 pregnancies with at least one antenatal screening test taken, of which 61 458 were RhD‐negative (11.9%). Altogether 518 pregnancies of 383 women were affected with alloanti‐D, which presented in combination with other Rh‐antibodies (anti‐C and anti‐E) in 188 (36%) pregnancies. In addition to anti‐D, another high‐risk antibody, anti‐K, was detected in four (0.8%) pregnancies and another moderate‐risk antibody in 12 (2%) pregnancies (anti‐Fya being most common). The mean parity of the affected pregnancies was 4.1 ± 2.6 (range 1–15), the proportion of nulliparous women was 9.7%, and of multiparous women with at least four previous births 13.3%. Notably, 7.5% of the women were primigravidae, meaning the affected pregnancy was their first pregnancy.

Prevalence of anti‐D immunizations

The prevalence of anti‐D among RhD‐negative pregnant women declined from 1.33% (96/7176, 95% CI 1.08% to 1.63%) in 2014 to 0.48% (26/5398, 95% CI 0.31% to 0.71%) in 2023 with a significantly decreasing trend (p < 0.001) over the years. The corresponding prevalence of severe HDFN requiring IUTs in RhD‐negative pregnancies declined from 0.20% (14/7176, 95% CI 0.11% to 0.33%) to 0.06% (3/5398, 95% CI 0.01% to 0.16%) with a significant decreasing trend (p < 0.05). Hence, 10 years after the introduction of RAADP, the prevalence of all and severe anti‐D immunizations decreased by 64% and 72%, respectively (Figure 2).

Altogether 37 of 518 pregnancies (7.1%) were lost to follow‐up and removed from the subsequent analyses. The majority of these were inferred to have undergone a spontaneous or induced abortion (31 before 16 weeks, six between 16 + 0–21 + 6).

In the 481 pregnancies with anti‐D, the fetal/neonatal RhD type was positive in 367 of 481 (76.3%) pregnancies and negative in 91 of 481 (18.9%). In 23 of 481 (4.8%) pregnancies, the fetal/neonatal RhD type was unknown despite heterozygous/unknown father. Fetal RhD typing was not performed due to constant low‐titer anti‐D (≤2 in 15 cases, titer of 4 in five, and of 8 in three cases). After exclusion of pregnancies with a negative or unknown fetal/neonatal RhD type, 367 pregnancies remained for the HDFN severity analysis.

The anti‐D exposure time was estimated by evaluating the screening results in each pregnancy. In 278 of 367 (75.7%) pregnancies, the fetus was exposed to anti‐D from the beginning (anti‐D detected in the 1st screening); in 57 of 367 (15.5%) pregnancies, anti‐D was detected in the 2nd screening; and in 30 of 367 (8.17%) pregnancies after 24–26 weeks (anti‐D detected in the 3rd screening).

Risk of severe HDFN

Intrauterine transfusions were performed in 65 pregnancies (including two twin pregnancies) of 60 women (three women had IUT in two pregnancies and one woman in three pregnancies). Nineteen fetuses (19/65, 29.2%) were hydropic at the first IUT or at some point. The risk of severe HDFN (requiring IUTs) in an anti‐D immunized pregnancy with an RhD‐positive fetus was 17.7% (65/367, 95% CI 13.9% to 22.0%). This risk remained unchanged (p = 0.5165) over the study period.

There were three fetal deaths and three neonatal deaths. Two fetal deaths occurred within a week of the IUT for a hydropic fetus (at 21 and 24 weeks) and one after IUT for a non‐hydropic fetus (at 20 weeks). Two neonatal deaths were caused by HDFN in combination with extremely premature birth at 25 and 26 weeks following IUT for a hydropic fetus, and in one case, intrauterine therapy was discontinued due to progressive intracranial bleeding, and the newborn died shortly after birth. The overall survival after IUTs was 90.8% (59/65, 95% CI 80.1% to 96.5%). The median gestational age at birth among neonates who survived was 35 + 0 weeks (range 27 + 6–36 + 6). In addition to IUTs, 13.6% (8/59) received EXT, 64.4% (38/59) IVIG, 28.8% (17/59) top‐up transfusions, and 69.5% (41/59) received phototherapy.

No additional deaths related to HDFN could be identified on the basis of Statistics Finland's data on causes of death and the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare's data on parturients and deliveries. Total extended mortality due to HDFN among RhD‐negative pregnancies was 0.1/1000.

In 60 of 65 (92%) of pregnancies with severe HDFN, anti‐D was present already from the 1st screening. In addition, in four pregnancies, the antibody was identified for the first time in the 2nd screening of the ongoing pregnancy (three G1P0, one G5P4) and in one pregnancy (G2P1) at 20 weeks in investigations carried out for a hydropic fetus. The predefined cut‐off level for anti‐D titer ≥16 was found 100% sensitive with a specificity of 54% and a positive predictive value (PPV) of 32% to detect severe HDFN.

Of the 65 pregnancies receiving IUTs, anti‐D was presented in combination with other Rh‐antibodies (anti‐C or anti‐E) in 42 (65%) pregnancies. In two pregnancies of the same women, anti‐D and anti‐E with extremely high titers from 256 to 2000 were presented in combination with anti‐K with low titers (≤1).

Risk of moderate or mild HDFN

Information on neonatal treatment was obtained from 141 of 185 (76.2%) pregnancies with RhD‐positive neonates born in 2014–2017 (including one set of twins).

Neonatal treatment (any form) was given for 74 of 112 (66.1%) neonates not treated with IUTs: five (4.5%) received EXT, 23.2% IVIG, 24.1% top‐up transfusions, and 64.3% received phototherapy. No treatment was needed for 38 of 112 (33.9%) neonates. Hence, for RhD‐positive neonates not treated with IUTs, the overall risk of moderate HDFN (EXT, IVIG, top‐up transfusion) was 33.0% (37/112, 95% CI 24.4% to 42.6%) and mild HDFN (phototherapy) was 33.0% (37/112, 95% CI 24.4% to 42.6%). The median gestational age at birth in the moderate risk group was 36 + 5 weeks (32 + 1–40 + 1) and 38 + 1 weeks (35 + 2–40 + 6) in the mild risk group.

Among the 37 neonates with moderate HDFN, anti‐D was first identified in the 2nd screening in six pregnancies and in the 3rd screening in two pregnancies. A cut‐off titer level ≥16 detected all pregnancies with a neonate requiring EXT. With a maximum titer of 8, one neonate received a top‐up transfusion, and with a maximum titer of 4, one neonate received IVIG after failure of intensive phototherapy. To detect moderate HDFN, the cut‐off level for anti‐D titer ≥16 was found 95% sensitive with a specificity of 65% and a PPV of 57%.

Impact of a rapidly declining birth rate on anti‐D‐related HDFN

To distinguish the impact of declining birth rate from the effect of RAADP on the number of anti‐D, we used a standard epidemiological decomposition approach. First, we applied the prevalence in 2014 (1.33%) to the 2023 cohort of RhD‐negative pregnancies (N = 5398), yielding 72 expected cases. The comparison of this estimate with the actual reduction in cases (from 96 in 2014 to 26 in 2023) showed that 34% of the decline (from 96 to 72) is attributable to fewer births, while 66% (from 72 to 26) is a result of the improved prophylaxis program. DISCUSSION

This is a large nationwide study of the current state of HDFN due to anti‐D immunizations in Finland. Ten years after targeted RAADP was added into the national prophylaxis program, the prevalence of anti‐D among RhD‐negative pregnant women declined from 1.33% to 0.48%, representing a 64% reduction. This decrease is comparable to other studies evaluating the effect of targeted RAADP. 10 , 11 , 12 Most importantly, the corresponding prevalence of severe HDFN requiring IUTs declined from 0.20% to 0.06%; this being the main concern in any use of anti‐D prophylaxis, and to our knowledge, the result has not been reported earlier. Due to the combined effect of targeted RAADP and the declining national birth rate, the absolute number of HDFN cases has decreased, and severe cases are now rare in Finland. Clearly, there is a need for updated risk assessment and a better understanding of disease severity and treatment.

In our cohort, almost one‐fifth (17.7%) of RhD‐positive fetuses required IUTs, conferring a significant patient and economic burden to the health system. In terms of survival outcomes, the overall survival after IUTs was 90.8%, with the majority of the deaths occurring in hydropic cases undergoing the first IUT before 24 weeks of gestation. Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the highest risk of mortality being related to fetal hydrops and addressing the importance of timely treatment of fetal anemia. 1 , 3 In turn, the risk of fetal demise was reported to be 20% even in the most experienced hands when IUTs were performed before 20–22 weeks. 1 Thus, it is of utmost importance to centralize IUTs to an expert fetal medicine center and to a limited number of specialists who also perform other invasive procedures on a regular basis. Since 2015, IUTs have been performed in two centers in Finland instead of five to develop and maintain the skills of a reasonable number of operators to care for this small but challenging patient group. 22 However, monitoring these high‐risk pregnancies is equally important and includes patient counseling regarding the ongoing pregnancy and possible pregnancies to follow. In Finland, where distances can be long and traveling to follow‐ups may be arduous for pregnant women with small children, it is fundamental that noninvasive monitoring can be undertaken in the nearest delivery hospital and that those in need of treatment are referred early to the experienced centers. To ensure effective collaboration, continuous education of healthcare staff working with immunizations is necessary at all levels.

The purpose of antenatal screening is to identify RhD‐negative pregnancies eligible for anti‐D prophylaxis and to detect pregnancies at risk of severe HDFN requiring clinical follow‐up and possible IUTs. In excess, knowing which cases will likely need postnatal EXT or top‐up transfusions is essential to obstetric and neonatal care providers. In our cohort, two‐thirds of neonates (66%) not treated with IUTs received some treatment postnatally, indicating that interventions ranging from phototherapy to IVIG, top‐up transfusions, or, most importantly, EXT need to be available at the delivery hospital. In the latest study to date, a selected cohort of neonates receiving any postnatal treatment, EXT frequencies were similar between neonates treated with IUT (24%) and those not treated with IUTs (23%), but with significant practice variations between centers (range 0%–78%). 23 In our study, 14% of neonates treated with IUTs received EXT, but practices varied even in a small country like Finland. Moreover, even at the beginning of the 10‐year study period, only 4% of neonates not treated with IUTs received EXT, and since then, this highly invasive measure has become increasingly uncommon. On the other hand, approximately one‐third of our neonates not treated with IUTs required no therapy at all, further underscoring the importance of predicting the need for postnatal therapy.

Delivery hospitals rely on their antenatal screening laboratories to differentiate high‐risk cases from low‐risk ones, not only with 100% sensitivity, but also with acceptable specificity, minimizing unnecessary clinical monitoring and possible interventions. In many countries, a titer of ≥16, measured with the conventional tube technique, is typically used as the critical titer for anti‐D. 24 Titer measurements vary between laboratories, also with similar techniques, and comparisons between studies should be dealt with caution. At our predefined cut‐off of ≥16 for anti‐D titer, the specificity was 54% and the PPV 32% for severe HDFN, indicating that IUTs are needed in one out of three pregnancies with an RhD‐positive fetus. For moderate HDFN, the corresponding specificity and PPV were 67% and 56%, respectively, implying that half of the neonates will need transfusion therapy or at least IVIG. Hence, a cut‐off of ≥16 was found optimal, not only for identifying those at risk of severe HDFN, but also for predicting any need for intensive therapy for neonates not treated with IUTs. This may also be an argument for choosing the optimal delivery hospital providing all necessary treatments.

Due to the underlying mechanism of maternal alloimmunization, severe HDFN typically appears in subsequent pregnancies with an RhD‐positive fetus and tends to worsen with each affected pregnancy. 25 , 26 Expectedly, in our cohort, over 95% of severe HDFN cases occurred in subsequent pregnancies, with anti‐D detected in the 1st screening. 4 , 5 Unexpectedly, IUTs were also required in five pregnancies with a negative 1st screening. For the three primigravidae (G1P0) with anti‐D detected at the 2nd screening of the ongoing pregnancy, no other explanation than the role of individual immune response in antibody formation could be identified. For the two other women with negative 1st trimester screening (G5P4 and G2P1), a possible explanation is that sensitization may have happened in the previous pregnancy or delivery, with the antibody remaining undetectable until boosted by the RhD‐positive fetus. These rare cases emphasize the importance of a highly sensitive antenatal screening, including the 2nd trimester sample. Instead, as a result of the introduction of RAADP, the 3rd trimester screening was removed from the screening programs in the Nordic countries except Finland, 27 where information on late‐onset antibodies affecting transfusions or postnatal care was still considered important for remote delivery hospitals with limited stock of blood products and pretransfusion testing capacity restricted to antibody screening and crossmatching only. Given the infrequency of moderate HDFN in these 3rd trimester immunizations even at the beginning of the study period when full compliance with the new program was not yet achieved, our findings may be an argument for re‐evaluating the value of the 3rd trimester screening in Finland, as well.

Since the introduction of RAADP, the incidence of new immunizations has decreased to 0.1%–0.2%. 10 , 11 , 16 In our study, a historically low birth rate explained one‐third (34%) of the actual reduction in anti‐D cases. However, the additional impact of declining fertility rate on the absolute number of anti‐D pregnancies and severity of HDFN is much more complex. First, immunized women represent a special population, of which a notably high proportion are multiparous, at least in Finland. In our cohort, 13.3% of women had at least four previous births—a significantly higher proportion than in the general parturient population (10%, p < 0.05), and among the highest rates reported in the European Union. 15 This may reflect underlying attitudes toward contraception, which in some religious groups is prohibited, contributing to continued pregnancies despite known sensitization. 28 Intriguingly, we have three population extremities of women of fertile age; those who do not want a child, those who do not want a second child, and those who will have as many children as possible despite known risks. The true effect of RAADP will be seen once there are no more anti‐Ds detected by the 1st screen in the latter group. Second, fewer first‐born children, accounting for 75% of the observed birth rate decline in Finland, will most likely further decrease the number of anti‐D cases. Third, the expected effect of fewer subsequent pregnancies on HDFN severity is obscure and, so far, no tendency toward less serious disease could be observed in our cohort with a relatively stable risk of severe HDFN (17.7%). Thus, in addition to RAADP, declining birth rates will significantly influence the number of cases requiring monitoring and treatment and must be considered when evaluating the effectiveness of the prophylaxis program.

The strength of this study is the large, nationwide cohort, representing all RhD‐negative pregnancies in Finland from 2014 to 2023. All anti‐D cases were identified through the FRC Blood Service database and data on clinical outcomes through our five university hospitals. The proportion of miscarriages, induced abortions or possible loss to follow‐up (7.1%) in the study cohort did not differ from the general parturient population according to statistics based on registered pregnancies. 17

This study has some limitations. First, almost 5% of the ongoing pregnancies with a possibly RhD‐positive fetus were excluded from the HDFN severity analyses. In these pregnancies, despite a heterozygous father, fetal genotyping was not performed due to low‐titer anti‐D in the 1st screening remaining constant either due to a RhD‐negative fetus or a low‐responder mother. In all cases, the titers were monitored up to 36 weeks of gestation and no cases of severe HDFN were missed. Second, distinguishing weak immunizations from traces of anti‐D prophylaxis is impossible; hence, the number of anti‐D immunizations in the 3rd screening is likely an underestimation, but the true number cannot be confirmed until the next pregnancy. On the other hand, it is very unlikely that these weak immunizations with titers ≤2 have any clinical consequences to the neonate. Third, but most importantly, detailed individual patient data on neonatal treatment for neonates who were not treated with IUTs were only available for the years 2014–2017; therefore, we could not analyze trends over time. The register data from the whole study period suggest that EXTs have become uncommon, but unfortunately, data on IVIG and late top‐up transfusions are not included. Thus, the results, especially regarding the need for EXT, should be interpreted in the context of the long study period.

CONCLUSION

Ten years after RAADP was introduced to Finnish guidelines, the prevalence of anti‐D among RhD‐negative pregnancies was reduced by more than half. Due to the concomitant decline in fertility rates, the actual number of cases requiring clinical monitoring and IUTs is even lower. Despite becoming rare, severe HDFN still confers a major risk for an RhD‐positive fetus. Future prospects include the potential shown by nipocalimab in the treatment of alloimmunized pregnancies. 29 Furthermore, long‐term follow‐up of IUT‐treated children is needed and this is addressed in an ongoing Nordic multicenter study.
