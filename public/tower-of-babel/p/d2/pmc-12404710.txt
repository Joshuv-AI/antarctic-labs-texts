Redox metabolism and signalling in plants
Authors: Alexandre Boscari, Pierre Frendo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants must continuously adapt to their biotic and abiotic environment in order to survive, grow, and reproduce. Redox reactions play a central role in these processes, influencing numerous aspects of plant physiology, from transcriptional regulation to environmental perception, through the modulation of cellular metabolism. Redox regulation is driven by changes in the concentrations of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), as well as antioxidants, which impact plant functioning. In this Special Issue, a collection of reviews and articles cover current work in this field, exploring the role of redox molecules in plant metabolism and adaptation, spanning from detailed molecular mechanisms to whole-plant responses.

Redox metabolism and signalling are universal features across life. In plants, they underpin critical processes including photosynthesis, adaptation to abiotic stress (e.g. hypoxia), and plant–microbe interactions with symbiotic or pathogenic microorganisms. While many redox-active molecules and pathways have been characterized (Del Río, 2015; Noctor et al., 2018), others remain poorly understood. The field is being transformed by innovative approaches—ranging from omics technologies to biosensors—that offer an integrative perspective on redox dynamics. This topic was the subject of the 16th International Conference on Reactive Oxygen and Nitrogen Species in Plants, hosted in Antibes in 2024, and forms the basis of this Special Issue. The meeting brought together researchers working on a range of molecular and physiological processes to present different aspects of the redox regulation in plant metabolism and adaptation to the environment. This Special Issue develops topical issues and emerging fields discussed during the meeting.

The conceptual landscape of redox signalling in plants has evolved significantly since the pioneering work of Foyer and Noctor (2003, 2005), who fundamentally redefined ROS from toxic metabolic byproducts to central signalling molecules that regulate plant physiology. This paradigm shift was further advanced by the comprehensive work of Mittler et al. (2004, 2011), who detailed the complex ROS networks involved in plant cellular communication. More recently, Kolbert et al. (2024) expanded this framework by showing that nitric oxide (NO), along with related RNS and reactive sulfur species (RSS), mediates not only interorgan and intraorgan communication but also, remarkably, interplant communications—highlighting a previously underexplored dimension of plant communication. While these foundational studies laid the groundwork for our current understanding of redox biology, recent advances have revealed a level of complexity that extends far beyond these initial discoveries.

Redox regulation of plant response to environmental constraints

The accelerated pace of climate change in recent years serves as a critical warning, underlining the urgency of research focused on plant resilience and the development of new crop varieties to safeguard global food security (Rhee et al., 2025). Environmental fluctuations invariably lead to local or systemic disturbances in cellular redox homeostasis (Fichman and Mittler, 2021; Angelovici and Mittler, 2024). Abiotic stresses alter the level of oxidant and/or antioxidant molecules, thereby shifting the redox state of numerous proteins or metabolites, and triggering signal transduction pathways associated with protein phosphorylation, calcium perception, and protein degradation. ROS play central roles in plant responses to stress combinations such as simultaneous heat and drought, or cold and freezing, whose effects cannot be simply inferred from individual stress responses. In such situations, ROS and the antioxidant network are deeply involved in coordinating the signalling pathways that underpin plant adaptation (Hendrix et al., 2025). Beyond immediate stress responses, ROS also mediate stress priming, a process through which plants acquire enhanced tolerance to future stress episodes (Hendrix et al., 2025). This priming effect represents a promising alternative strategy to conventional breeding, aimed at improving plant survival under increasingly adverse environmental conditions. Amongst the various priming agents, NO, whether endogenously produced or exogenously applied, functions as potent regulator of both seed pre-treatment and vegetative priming. It contributes to the mitigation of stress-specific damage while also inducing broader antioxidant mechanisms (Fejes et al., 2025). For instance, seed or vegetative priming with sodium nitroprusside (SNP), a widely used NO donor, has been shown to alleviate the detrimental effects of salinity, cold, drought, and heavy metal toxicity. However, due to known cytotoxic effects associated with SNP, there is a growing need to evaluate alternative NO donors (e.g. S-nitrosoglutathione) as well as new substances with more favourable properties (e.g. NO-releasing nanomaterials, plasmas, plasma-treated liquids, or combined donors). These innovative approaches represent critical future directions for advancing fundamental research in stress physiology and plant biotechnology (Fejes et al., 2025).

Plant response to environmental conditions also involves interactions with the biotic environment including mutualistic associations with soil fungi and bacteria (Lebreton and Keller, 2024). These microorganisms are particularly important for a sustainable agriculture, as they offer an efficient means to enhance plant nutrient uptake and productivity, thereby reducing the need for fertilizer inputs (Singh et al., 2020). However, global warming is reshaping plant–environment interactions, with profound effects on agricultural systems worldwide. In this context, redox-active molecules such as ROS, RNS, and RSS orchestrate legume responses to various (a)biotic stresses and symbiotic relationships, offering promising strategies to improve crop resilience in changing environments (Johansson et al., 2025).

Inside the plant cells, peroxisomes are an important source of both ROS and RNS, which play key roles in the plant response to environmental stresses (Molina-Moya et al., 2025). Advances in -omics technologies, along with the availability of mutants specifically altered in peroxisomal ROS metabolism, enabled the identification of peroxisome-specific transcriptomic signatures. In addition to redox signalling, peroxisome contribute to stress responses by producing signalling molecules such as phytohormones (jasmonic acid, auxins, and salycilic acid) and polyamines. Furthermore, recent progress in microscopy now allows the visualization and analysis of peroxisome dynamics under stress conditions (Molina-Moya et al., 2025). Beyond ROS/RNS production in peroxisomes, NO can also be generated from nitrite by mitochondrial alternative oxidase (AOX) under low O2 conditions, such as flooding (Igamberdiev and Bykova, 2025). AOX is induced under hypoxia and contributes to NO turnover via the phytoglobin–NO cycle. This mechanism supports glycolysis by reoxidizing the glycolytically formed NADH and rerouting glycolytic carbon toward the synthesis of alanine and other amino acids (Igamberdiev and Bykova, 2025). Thus, AOX is essential under O2-limiting conditions for maintaining cellular redox balance (Igamberdiev and Bykova, 2025).

Redox signalling in plant biology: an integrated network of molecular communication

Recent studies have revealed an extraordinarily sophisticated and interconnected redox signalling network that extends beyond the classical ROS and NO pathways to include hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and melatonin as integral components of a comprehensive cellular communication system (Corpas et al., 2025). These molecules interact dynamically, forming what can be described as ‘redox codes’—specific combinations of signalling molecules that translate environmental and developmental cues into coordinated cellular responses. The realization that these signals operate within a unified and context-dependent network, rather than in isolation, has significantly reshaped our understanding of plant redox biology.

The emergence of nitroxyl (HNO) as a distinct redox signal marks a major advance in plant redox biology (Suarez et al., 2025). Unlike its closely related counterpart NO, HNO displays unique chemical properties and biological functions, establishing it as a key player in the redox signalling network. Its interconversion with NO is modulated by plant compounds, such as H2S, ascorbic acid, and salicylic acid, which ensures widespread HNO bioavailability across plant tissues. This discovery has revealed that the HNO/NO balance is subject to fine-tuned regulation through non-enzymatic processes, creating additional layers of signalling complexity that can respond rapidly to changing cellular conditions (Suarez et al., 2025). In parallel, the identification of protein persulfidation as a major post-translational modification has shed light on the signalling role of H2S (Zhou et al., 2025). This modification, characterized by the addition of sulfur atoms to cysteine residues, represents a fundamental mechanism through which H2S regulates protein function and cellular processes. Proteomic studies have revealed that persulfidation affects numerous proteins involved in stress responses, metabolic pathways, and developmental processes, highlighting its central role in plant adaptation mechanisms (Zhou et al., 2025). The specificity and reversibility of the protein persulfidation position it as a sophisticated regulatory tool that allows plants to fine-tune their responses to environmental challenges (Zhou et al., 2025).

The technological advancement of genetically encoded biosensors has profoundly transformed our ability to investigate redox signalling in real time within living plant tissues (Ugalde and Meyer, 2025). These fluorescent protein-based sensors facilitate dynamic monitoring of redox-related physiological parameters with unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution, uncovering the complex patterns of redox signal propagation throughout plant organs. The application of these biosensors has uncovered the existence of redox microdomains and hotspots, particularly in association with plasma membrane-localized NADPH oxidases, highlighting the spatial precision with which redox signals orchestrate cellular responses (Kritsiligkou et al., 2023). The practical implications of these insights are particularly notable in horticultural contexts, especially within the economically important Solanaceae family. Exogenous treatments with H2O2, NO, H2S, and melatonin have been shown to significantly influence fruit physiology—modulating ripening processes, enhancing antioxidant profiles, and improving stress resilience (Corpas et al., 2025). These findings point to promising avenues of biotechnological applications for crop improvement. However, the complexity of the redox signalling networks necessitates careful optimization to harness their benefits without triggering unintended physiological consequences (Corpas et al., 2025).

The study of legume–rhizobia symbiosis has uncovered striking paradoxes in redox signalling and regulation, where O2 and its reactive derivatives (ROS and NO) appear to exert opposing influences (Meilhoc et al., 2025). On one hand, nitrogenase—the key enzyme responsible for nitrogen fixation—is highly sensitive to O2 and requires an extremely low-O2 environment to function (Appleby, 1992). On the other hand, the nitrogen fixation process is energetically demanding, relying on robust respiratory metabolism (Vance, 2008). The resolution of this apparent contradiction lies in finely tuned regulatory systems involving plant phytoglobins and bacterial haemoglobins, which precisely modulate O2 diffusion and consumption to sustain both low-O2 conditions for nitrogenase activity and high-energy yields from respiration. These mechanisms also contribute to maintaining redox homeostasis within nodules, ensuring efficient symbiotic performance. Perhaps the most compelling recent development in this field is the identification of nitro-oxidative modifications of nucleotides, which extends redox signalling into the realm of genetic information processing (Chmielowska-Bąk et al., 2025). These modifications, including nitrated and oxidized forms of nucleic acids and cyclic nucleotides, challenge our understanding of the boundary between cellular signalling and molecular damage. The discovery of nitrated cGMP as a novel second messenger suggests that such modifications function not merely as byproducts of oxidative stress, but as integral components of sophisticated sensing and regulatory networks.

Redox control of the leaf metabolism

Photosynthesis is a central biological process that is the source of energy for plants and, via the food chain, for almost all other forms of life. It depends on redox reactions that provide reducing power and energy for the assimilation of inorganic carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur, allowing the generation of organic compounds and O2 production. However, the photosynthetic redox metabolism must constantly adapt to prevent oxidative damage caused by excessive ROS production (Cejudo et al., 2021; González et al., 2021; Foyer and Hanke, 2022). Central to this regulation are thioredoxins (TRXs), which control the activity of key enzymes via thiol–disulfide exchanges (Meyer et al., 2009; Pérez-Ruiz et al., 2017). The use of Arabidopsis mutants lacking parts of the NADPH-dependent TRX-reductase C (NTRC) or light-dependent TRX systems has proved to be decisive in uncovering their roles in photosynthetic acclimation to fluctuating light (FL), with delayed relaxation of non-photochemical quenching in the low-light phases of FL (Dziubek et al., 2025). Results show that NADPH-dependent NTRC is balancing chloroplast redox systems and Calvin–Benson cycle activity during prolonged light variability, to optimize PSI, PSII and water-use efficiency (Dziubek et al., 2025). In addition to TRXs, ascorbate scavenges ROS and enables the rapid and full induction of photoprotective non-photochemical quenching (NPQ). NPQ depends on zeaxanthin (Zx), which requires ascorbate as an electron donor during its synthesis by violaxanthin de-epoxidase (Hager and Holocher, 1994; Niyogi et al., 1998). Using Arabidopsis vtc2 mutants depleted in ascorbate, the effects of development on photoprotection and stress resistance were analysed in FL (Kappel et al., 2025). When the plants were placed in natural conditions with highly fluctuating light and temperature, the ascorbate-deficient mature leaves of vtc2 turned white, showing developmental and environmental effects on VTC2-dependent ascorbate accumulation and function (Kappel et al., 2025). Beyond photosynthesis and respiration, recent evidence indicates that the mitochondrial NTR/TRX system is also involved in the regulation of photorespiratory metabolism (Hou et al., 2021; Lima et al., 2021; Porto et al., 2022). The use of mitochondrial TRX o1 (trxo1) or both NADPH-dependent TRX reductases A/B (ntrab), enables it be to shown that the level of citrate and glutamate was higher in illuminated leaves from Arabidopsis mutants than in the wild type (WT) (Souza et al., 2025). The lack of TRX o1 decreased the content and activity of glutamine synthetase (GS), which leads to a lower level of glutamine, and exacerbated the increases in GS activity triggered by high light, when compared with the wild type. The results indicate that the metabolic fluxes from the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) to the GS/GOGAT cycle are regulated by the mitochondrial TRX system, probably associated with an increased substrate availability (Souza et al., 2025).

In addition to the physiological analysis of Arabidopsis mutants, a broad bioinformatics genomic analysis has identified 17 atypical TRX-like proteins in A. thaliana, which have not been considered before despite having both a TRX fold and the CxxC/S signature typical of redox-active TRXs (de Bont et al., 2025). The in silico sequence and structure analyses revealed that they are distributed in eight distinct classes with unique active site signatures and structures, some with bacterial disulfide isomerases A signature and peroxiredoxin-like folds. The distinct subcellular localizations (plastids, mitochondria, and extracellular space) and gene expression profiles suggest that these proteins are involved in diverse cellular processes (de Bont et al., 2025).

In conclusion, redox biology emerges as a unifying paradigm that interconnects diverse facets of plant metabolism, development, and stress adaptation. From the dynamic regulation of photosynthesis and photorespiration through TRXs and ascorbate to the enhancement of stress resilience via redox-based priming strategies and symbiotic interactions, redox signalling enables plants to finely calibrate their responses to both internal cues and environmental stimuli. The discovery of novel redox-active proteins, the expanding repertoire of reactive species—including H2S, melatonin, and HNO—and the recent breakthroughs in our understanding of nucleotide modifications all underscore the increasing complexity and functional versatility of redox signalling networks. Technological advances, especially in real-time biosensing, are now shedding light on the spatial and temporal dimensions of redox regulation. Together, these findings highlight redox signalling as a multidimensional, deeply embedded regulatory system that orchestrates plant physiology across all scales—from molecules to ecosystems. In the face of accelerating climate change and the urgent need for sustainable agriculture, deepening our knowledge of this intricate network is not only a scientific challenge but also a strategic priority for building resilient crop systems and food security.
