Key considerations for advancing chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): a multi-partner/disciplinary working group perspective
Authors: Elena W Y Hsieh, James B Chung, Afsah Amin, Anca D Askanase, Beatrix Bartok, Paul Brunetta, Marlene J Carrasco-Alfonso, Cindy Coney, Jennifer C Cooper, Maria Dall’Era, Nikolay Delev, Ann Eldred, Richard A Furie, Sandra Garces, Katie Groover, Monica Harrington, Jonathan J Hogan, Gavin H Imperato, Meredith Jackson, Shaun W Jackson, Artemio M Jongco, Elaine Karis, Ashley Koegel, Maximilian F König, Gary Koretzky, Vijay Kumar, Eric Morand, Aimee S Payne, Vaishali Popat, Naiyer Rizvi, Patricia Rohan, Michel Sadelain, Georg Schett, Aman P Singh, Ronald F van Vollenhoven, Debra Zack, Lupus ABC CAR T Working Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly data have shown the potential of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies to expand the therapeutic landscape in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). While many CAR T-cell therapy learnings can be drawn from the experience of this modality in oncology, key questions remain regarding clinical development considerations unique to lupus. To assess and discuss these issues, the Lupus Accelerating Breakthroughs Consortium, a public–private partnership, convened a multi-partner working group to collect the diverse perspectives of academics/clinicians (including rheumatologists and oncologists), industry representatives (including SLE as well as CAR T-cell clinical development experts), regulators and people living with lupus on this potentially ground-breaking therapy. The working group considered the risk/benefit considerations for eligibility criteria in lupus, early-phase dosing and dose-limiting toxicity challenges, incorporation of comparator arms in late-phase registrational trial design, SLE-specific issues in conditioning therapy and immune monitoring and the limitations of SLE pre-clinical models for studying cell therapies. The key future ‘calls to action’ for the field include the need for well-defined severity/refractoriness-based eligibility criteria, the need for long-term monitoring infrastructure and the need for educational and logistical support for rheumatologists and patients.Keywords: Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic; Biological Therapy; Clinical Trial; T-Lymphocytes; Lupus Nephritis

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study uniquely integrates perspectives from academic researchers, clinicians, industry scientists, regulators, and individuals living with SLE, offering a comprehensive view of the translational and clinical challenges in advancing CAR T-cell therapy for SLE. It highlights the need for uniform definitions of severity and refractoriness for eligibility criteria that focus on patient quality of life, underscores the need for long-term infrastructure for monitoring safety, efficacy, and fertility outcomes, and identifies other key clinical development challenges including the role of conditioning regimens, comparator arms for registrational trials, and safety assessments tailored to SLE.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy—a breakthrough for patients with haematological malignancies—could change treatment paradigms in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and other autoimmune diseases.1 2 Early data have shown the efficacy of CAR T-cell therapy in refractory SLE, lupus nephritis (LN) and other autoimmune diseases that can be durable (>1 year follow-up) without ongoing immunosuppressive therapy.3 4 In order to solicit input from multiple partners on the advancement of CAR T-cells for patients with SLE, the Lupus Accelerating Breakthroughs Consortium (Lupus ABC), a public–private partnership, convened a working group comprising patients living with lupus, clinicians and researchers from academia and governmental agencies, industry sponsors and US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) representatives.5 The working group identified key considerations in the development of CAR T-cell therapy for SLE, focusing primarily on autologous CAR T-cell therapies and incorporating the diverse perspectives of members of each sector. While other immune effector cell therapies with different risk and benefit characteristics, including bispecific T-cell engagers and engineered natural killer cell therapies, are now being applied to the treatment of patients with SLE, these modalities were not considered by the working group for this perspective. Here, we present the considerations of the working group in six key areas: risk/benefit considerations for eligibility for CAR T-cell therapy in SLE, early- and late-phase clinical development, conditioning therapy, occurrence and management of CAR T-cell toxicities, measurement of clinical and immunological response parameters and the role of pre-clinical models in advancing immune effector cell therapies for SLE. Risk/benefit considerations for eligibility for CAR T-cell therapy

Eligibility: organ specificity

Traditionally, there is a separation between LN and extra-renal SLE in clinical trial design, with different standards of care and endpoints.6 However, studying cell therapies in LN alone may not provide a sufficient patient population for trials and would not address major unmet needs in extra-renal SLE. Additionally, patients with SLE with renal involvement may also have other organ involvement, and hence, studies on ‘independent organ involvement’ may draw artificial disease demarcations that are more complex in the disease pathophysiology and reality of clinical practice. The working group therefore favoured expanding enrolment beyond LN for lupus CAR T-cell trials, when possible. CAR T-cell therapy trials are an opportunity to move away from the organ-specific/non-organ-specific dichotomy in SLE to identify patients with severe disease who could benefit from CAR T-cell therapy, whether they have skin, central nervous system, haematological or other organ involvement.

The working group also recognised that there are challenges associated with broader enrolment based on the severity of SLE, independent of which organs are involved, compared with a focus on LN. A combined population necessitates well-defined criteria for capturing disease manifestations and appropriate efficacy measures to show benefits, which has proven to be challenging in the lupus field. Future efficacy endpoints that incorporate a multi-domain responder classification system or that relate to treatment-free remission (discussed below) may further enable the inclusion of combined trial populations. Alternatively, if a combined population (eg, patients with both LN and SLE) is included in a trial, two (or more) cohorts could be created, each with well-defined eligibility criteria, outcome measures, dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) and stopping rules to account for the different needs of each population. However, for early-phase trials, where the primary goals are to demonstrate safety and establish dose through analyses of pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics, broadened eligibility criteria are warranted within appropriate risk/benefit assessments.

Eligibility: disease activity and severity

Instead of focusing on organ-specific manifestations, inclusion criteria may be viewed on two axes, especially for early-phase trials: (1) disease activity and (2) disease severity (ie, organ-threatening vs non-organ-threatening). Traditional assessments of activity like Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) and British Isles Lupus Assessment Group (BILAG) have limitations for assessing severity. SLEDAI does not assess severity in a given organ system, and similar scores can be obtained in patients with single organ pathology that is highly weighted or those with activity in multiple organ systems assigned lower weights. BILAG may be considered somewhat more of a severity measure as it includes intention-to-treat considerations. In the end, neither is designed for nor is optimally suited for the assessment of severity per se.7 One example of the dissociation of the two is that some patients with SLE may have low SLEDAI scores but have ongoing renal disease with significant proteinuria if they had normal complement and anti-dsDNA levels. It emerged from working group discussions that more efforts are needed to define and measure severity more generally in SLE.

Working group members had multiple perspectives on how to define and consider severity in SLE (table 1). Both industry and academic members suggested that an adjudicated physician assessment may be needed to assess a patient’s severity from multiple dimensions beyond current activity. Patients in the working group suggested that measures of severity should consider the quality of life (using measures such as the Health Assessment Questionnaire). Long-term glucocorticoid use is associated with high toxicity and organ damage and can be evaluated with tools such as the glucocorticoid toxicity index.8 Some symptoms that are not considered clinically organ-threatening (pain and fatigue) can be debilitating to patients. Patients consider severity to include how their disease symptoms impact their ability to perform activities of daily living, to maintain a professional career and to have social interactions, among other quality of life components. Strong consideration should be given to constructing a ‘severity index’ that reflects the perspectives of patients living with SLE, academic researchers, industry partners and regulatory authorities, as described in table 1. A simple prototype for such an index based on the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) SLE classification criteria and subcriteria was developed in 2016 and is currently used in observational SLE studies. We propose the development of an enhanced severity index that is fit for the purpose of determining eligibility for enrolment into a CAR T-cell trial.9 Moreover, the Definition of Remission in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (DORIS) Task Force has recently agreed to develop a lupus severity instrument (RV, personal communication).

Refractoriness and prior treatments

The working group largely agreed that refractoriness can be defined as at least two failed (inefficacy or intolerance) immunosuppressive therapies at optimal dose and duration (not including glucocorticoids or hydroxychloroquine) for early-phase trials. Failure should be defined based on well-accepted clinical criteria. Common therapies such as glucocorticoids, mycophenolate mofetil/sodium, azathioprine, methotrexate and cyclophosphamide (CYC) can have adverse effects that significantly impact patients’ quality of life even if the therapies may not conventionally be classified as failed from an efficacy point of view. The working group considered that the drugs a patient is currently taking to control disease (and their side effects) might be considered as an indicator of both severity and refractoriness.

In later-phase trials, there may be an opportunity to consider CAR T-cell therapy in patients with less refractory SLE. This decision should factor in the potential for the prevention of long-term damage. In addition, as safety data accumulate, the risks of long-term use of immunosuppression could also support the inclusion of earlier stage patients, particularly in the paediatric population. Furthermore, during CAR T-cell development for malignancies, patients with multiple failed lines of therapy were considered first, but the benefit was found to be greater at earlier stages with lower tumour burden.10 This situation could be similar in SLE, such that earlier intervention may offer a favourable benefit–risk profile with the potential for improved organ function. Hence, as CAR T-cell therapy trials advance in SLE, the optimal time for treatment with the CAR T-cell product may change. Inclusion of only patients for whom all other therapies have failed should not be the goal (in later-phase trials) if the efficacy benefits of CAR T-cell therapy in SLE are found to outweigh the risks (including longer-term risks like secondary malignancies).

Age

CD19-targeting CAR T-cell therapy is approved in the USA for children with haematological malignancies.11 As of 2024, three clinical trials (two in China and one in the USA (NCT06465147)) are investigating CAR T-cell therapy in paediatric patients with SLE.12,15 The US trial enrols patients aged 2–30 years in decreasing age cohorts, while the Chinese trials include patients aged ≥3 or 6–19 years. Two additional US trials with paediatric arms opened in 2025 (NCT06839976 and NCT06308978). The working group discussed that paediatric populations could be increasingly considered for trials as initial safety, and efficacy data on adults are obtained. Due to concerns that children with pre-pubertal disease onset may have rare genetic variants driving SLE aetiology and therefore may be less responsive to CAR T-cell therapy, the benefit–risk calculation for the inclusion of these populations may be different from that for children >12 years. Hence, initial CAR T-cell trials in the paediatric population should consider >12 years for inclusion, as their disease pathophysiology most resembles the adult population.16 17

On the other hand, for early-phase trials, a case could be made for younger patients (<12 years), to be considered for inclusion if they have severe and active disease and if there is an opportunity for significant benefits in potentially saving organs or sparing long-term toxicities from cytotoxic agents, assuming that there is no impact of CAR T-cell therapy/conditioning therapy on fertility (discussed below). The value of an SLE-free life for adolescents, young adults and those of childbearing potential cannot be ignored. Potentially halting cumulative organ damage over the lifespan is a significant benefit to consider in the development of paediatric SLE CAR T-cell therapy trials.

On the other end of the spectrum, some working group members expressed that older adults with SLE (>75 years) might be initially excluded until favourable risk/benefit profiles are established in younger adult populations. However, other working group members expressed that age alone may not be an appropriate eligibility criterion for older patients and that overall physiologic status considering factors like co-morbidities, risk for infection and cardio-pulmonary function (eg, cardiac ejection fraction) should inform eligibility.

Clinical development considerations

Patient perspective

Current CAR T-cell therapy trials for SLE are enrolling in only limited medical centres, which raises patient concerns around economic feasibility, including travel expenses and time away from home, family and work, since trial patients generally must be within proximity of the trial site for close follow-up. This limitation also raises patient concerns on trial access and how centre locations can affect the composition of patients in the trial cohort. This is especially concerning for marginalised populations who may have more issues with access to clinical trials in the limited number of medical centres currently offering treatment. Patients being treated in a university setting are more likely to receive information on trials than those seeing community practice rheumatologists and advanced practice providers or those in communities with few or no rheumatologists, so education programmes may be needed to increase awareness among community practitioners and community organisations who reach the diverse and targeted populations regarding enrolling trials. It is important that trial results represent the diversity of the patient population with lupus who may be eligible for the treatment, if it is approved. Logistical support is also needed to enable rheumatology providers to easily access up-to-date information regarding open trials and inclusion/exclusion information and the referral process. In addition, more patient education is needed in all populations with lupus regarding the trial experience (time commitment (especially for long-term follow-up), travel, safety, etc). This education must be delivered at an appropriate educational level and in a language that the patient understands. This education should also clearly distinguish the lupus patient’s experience from the typical oncology patient’s experience.

In addition to the in-patient stay after CAR T-cell treatment, patients usually need to stay in proximity to a specialised care centre during the first month after CAR T-cell infusion. While the commitment is large, patients may feel it is reasonable for very promising outcomes. Most sponsors of CAR T-cell therapy trials offer reimbursement of expenses for patients and caregivers to travel to a study site. However, this reimbursement cannot address all the challenges associated with travel for patients (eg, time off work/school, fatigue, exposure to illness, difficulty keeping track of logistical details, comfort and convenience of accommodations, handicap accessibility and caregiver availability). Follow-up monitoring and associated travel also pose additional financial burdens, time commitments and logistical challenges.

The overall time commitment is a major concern for patients, along with short- and long-term safety and the need to stop taking current medications (although this requirement is trial-dependent and may change as data evolve). Patients weigh the risks of possible short- and long-term side effects and potential for changes in current medication regimens with their quality of life and potential for success of the therapy. Beyond the safety of the treatment itself, patients are also concerned about the ability to prevent and manage SLE flares, as well as pain control during treatment. This information must be communicated to patients so that they can appropriately weigh the potential risks and benefits of trial participation.

The scalability of the CAR T-cell therapy for patients with lupus following clinical trials is also concerning due to the high cost of the treatment, the availability of medical institutions able to administer it to patients with lupus and the risk of insurance coverage or lack of coverage. The questions of cost-effectiveness, access to therapy, and risk vs benefit will be one that patients with lupus will continue to ponder if the therapy is approved.

Early-phase development

Based on available trial registry data, all SLE CAR T-cell therapy studies to date (mainly early-phase) have been open-label, single-arm trials. The goals of early-phase trials are to demonstrate safety, establish a dose through analyses of pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics and evaluate preliminary clinical effects. Dose escalation should take into account safety, tolerability and preliminary treatment effect(s), aiming to select/confirm the optimal CAR T-cell dose. DLTs are established and approved by regulators and may involve consultation with an independent data monitoring committee (DMC).

Impact of prior immunosuppressive therapies on the leukapheresis product

Patients’ therapies may impact the production of the CAR T-cell product and interfere with infused CAR T-cell expansion, phenotype and function. Concerns include the potential for standard lupus therapies to negatively impact T-cell viability, cause metabolic exhaustion or skew T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoires, causing risks to the success of therapy. Relevant therapies are therefore discontinued in advance of leukapheresis and prior to infusion in patients with lupus. Hydroxychloroquine may be continued given the low likelihood of impact and the known potential for disease flares if discontinued.18 Conventional immunosuppressives (eg, azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate, calcineurin inhibitors and CYC) should be discontinued with timing that takes consideration of both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (including delayed effects) factors. The impact of low to moderate dose glucocorticoids on CAR T-cell function is likely minimal and may be necessary to appropriately manage the patient’s disease activity through the treatment period.19

Dose selection

The primary goal of CD19-directed or B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)/CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapies in lupus is the long-term reprogramming of the immune system (also known as ‘immune reset’) that is characterised by a new state of immune homeostasis which does not require further immune suppression and permits durable remission. Reaching such a state may require (i) ‘deep’ B-cell depletion (elimination of B-cells in peripheral blood, lymphoid tissues and autoimmune target tissues), (ii) fundamental revamping of the B-cell repertoire and (iii) effects on other non-B-cell immune lineages, such as T-cells, that are activated during autoimmune disease.20 In addition, BCMA/CD19-directed CAR T-cells, which more completely deplete plasma cells, may further and differentially contribute to the clinical, molecular and cellular immune reset. Optimal dose ranges for achieving ‘immune reset’ and ‘deep‘ B-cell depletion are the subject of study of ongoing trials and are product-dependent.

A traditional dose–response curve may not be observed in patients with SLE based on experiences in oncology.21 CAR T-cell therapy dose response is a complex area of clinical pharmacology that warrants further study specifically in autoimmune disease and a cumulative understanding of datasets for a CAR T-cell product over the course of drug development.22 Due to lower sample sizes and potential for high inter-subject variability associated with cellular kinetics/expansion characteristics of CAR T-cells during early-stage clinical trials, it is often challenging to identify a dose/cell kinetics–response relationship. Response is also affected by different CAR designs and mechanism of action for each construct, modality (autologous vs allogeneic), T-cell phenotype and other host factors. While allogeneic cell therapy products may have less patient-to-patient variability due to inter-consistency of products, optimal dosing for these products is still uncertain and likely to be product-dependent.

Prior clinical experience of a CAR T-cell product in one indication may inform dosing strategies in other indications; however, lack of translational and mechanistic models limits a quantitative extrapolation, leading to empirical use of available clinical and pre-clinical data for dose selection. Furthermore, differences in manufacturing, patient populations and other uncertainties described elsewhere should be considered during dose selection of CAR T-cells, particularly for first-in-human studies.23 When possible, pre-clinical comparative pharmacology studies (including in vitro and ex vivo) should also consider utilising approved/existing CAR T-cell products as positive controls in order to explore relative potency and provide directionality towards appropriate starting dose.

A current key knowledge gap is the importance of CAR T-cell persistence in autoimmune indications. In B-cell malignancies, CAR T-cell persistence is linked to clinical efficacy, durable remission, lower relapse, longer progression-free survival and higher neurotoxicity rates.24 Based on more limited data in SLE, transient CAR T-cell persistence appears sufficient for immune reset and sustained remission without chronic toxicity. Shorter-lived, fast-contracting CARs may be preferred in SLE since prolonged persistence increases the risk of hypogammaglobulinaemia and infection. In SLE, deep depletion of B-cells and drug-free remission can be achieved with rapid contraction of CAR T-cells post-infusion, with remissions lasting months to years without CAR T-cell detectability in the peripheral blood.3 In addition, dual-target BCMA-CD19 CARs lead to rapid B-cell depletion and remission; B-cell recovery occurs in months, and relapse is rare despite transient CAR T-cell persistence.25 More detailed, quantitative and mechanistically linked persistence data in SLE cohorts are needed in order to guide construct selection and therapy duration.

DLTs and stopping/pausing criteria

CAR T-cell therapy-associated toxicities and their management in haematological malignancies are extensively covered elsewhere.2 26 DLTs will initially be based on oncology studies but will likely differ in SLE and other autoimmune diseases, and new DLTs may arise. For SLE trials, the risk of DLTs should be considered in the context of the product construct, indication, eligibility criteria and available safety data from the same or similar products. For example, DLT criteria for the immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome grade may be different for protocols that do or do not include subjects with neuropsychiatric lupus manifestations.

In a traditional regulatory framework, ‘stopping criteria’ are more accurately termed ‘pausing criteria.’ A study or the treatment of an individual subject may be paused until it can be determined what caused a patient to meet a criterion and whether any safety modification is needed. DLTs are considered on a scale from ‘definitely related’ to ‘not related’ to the product. The goal is not to stop a trial or a dose finding due to adverse events unrelated to the product or due to related adverse events that are effectively managed. Rather, stopping criteria are intended to protect the safety of subsequent patients. Considering the diverse clinical manifestations of lupus which may confound the interpretation of CAR T-cell-related events, some latitude to apply clinical judgement to select criteria could be invested in the DMC, which may be able to advise on adding participants to generate further data on these adverse events. Clinical protocols should be written to retain flexibility while still safeguarding the patient and the integrity of research. The flexibility should be written within a framework of pre-specified parameters to facilitate data interpretation and to preserve the ability of the research study to answer the questions for which it was designed.

Stopping rules and DLTs are stricter for autoimmune conditions than for cancer because patients may have other treatment options, differences in risk tolerability and longer life expectancies. Regulatory agencies will continue to learn and adapt as data evolve in this accelerating field.

Late-phase development: registrational study design

Endpoints and comparator arms

The primary and key secondary efficacy endpoints being studied in Phase 1 and Phase 2 studies will inform the aspects of late-phase development trial design for cell therapies in SLE. Conventional endpoints (eg, SLE Responder Index (SRI) and British Isles Lupus Assessment Group-based Composite Lupus Assessment (BICLA)) are currently used in registrational SLE trials and have historically been used for past SLE therapy approvals, but they have numerous limitations and are based on disease activity status measures that were not originally designed to be treatment response measures in clinical trials.27

CAR T-cell therapy trials in SLE may focus on endpoints capturing clinical remission, immunological remission and/or validated treat to target endpoints (eg, lupus low disease activity state and DORIS).28 Other efforts are underway to develop improved response measures, such as the Lupus ABC treatment response measure for the SLE project, which will include complete response criteria for each domain as well as the minimal clinically important difference.29 Future endpoints should capture responses that are clinically meaningful to patients, including withdrawal from immunosuppressive SLE medications, patient-reported outcomes or organ-specific outcome measures (eg, CLASI). Eventually, surrogate endpoints may be developed based on immune metrics or biomarkers that might correlate with longer-term remissions.

For novel registrational endpoints describing durable treatment-free remission, early discussion with regulatory authorities may facilitate regulatory acceptance for use in later-phase trials. For more traditional response-related endpoints, such as SRI or BICLA, a comparator arm may be necessary in later-phase trials. This is challenging in SLE for multiple reasons. CAR T-cell studies are generally difficult to blind and difficult to enrol for trials in which participants may be randomised to receive a potentially less efficacious comparator treatment in the refractory SLE setting. Many patients in the target recruitment population may also have already received an alternative standard of care therapies, such as biologics (eg, belimumab, anifrolumab and rituximab), limiting options for appropriate comparator arm treatments.

Sponsors or investigators could consider allowing patients in a comparator arm, who would be continued on standard of care therapy, to receive CAR T-cell therapy based on lack of improvement or worsening disease after a predefined timepoint. This would enable all participants with active disease to ultimately receive the CAR T-cells. Despite the challenges, incorporation of a comparator arm also provides an opportunity to assess the utility of existing therapies in refractory SLE. Other possibilities are to utilise a synthetic or external comparator arm. While external comparator arms are control cohorts from other studies that may not precisely match the CAR T-cell therapy study population, synthetic comparator arms are cohorts developed from external data that are closely statistically matched to the patients in treatment groups.30 An FDA draft guidance for biologics and drug development indicated that an external comparator arm or a cohort running simultaneously could be implemented, with a number of considerations for assessing the comparability of data.31

The Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics group is considering whether historical longitudinal data could be used as an external comparator arm, with patient populations that might match populations participating in a CAR T-cell therapy and with measurements of long-term outcomes. The Asia Pacific Lupus Collaboration is also undertaking similar studies (pilot synthetic control studies), as well as industry-funded studies of different definitions of severe-refractory SLE to deliver an evidence-based framework focused on long-term outcomes in SLE.

Long-term monitoring

Long-term monitoring in SLE should include efficacy, safety (including secondary malignancies) and fertility monitoring. Monitoring long-term efficacy in SLE is uniquely challenging compared with that in an oncology setting, where monitoring endpoints such as overall survival does not require clinical testing and visits. Questions remain regarding the durability of responses, optimal management of patients after initial treatment (eg, background therapy and laboratory monitoring) and potential for retreatment. A recent report described a patient with idiopathic inflammatory myositis treated with CD19 CAR T-cell therapy who relapsed after an initial response.32 Retreatment with the same CD19 CAR T-cell product was unsuccessful, but subsequent treatment with a BCMA CAR T-cell therapy led to disease improvement, providing insights into potential mechanisms of response, relapse and treatment strategies. Mechanisms of resistance and relapse in lupus after CAR T-cell treatment are unknown given the early stages of this field, but these mechanisms in oncology have been extensively reviewed elsewhere and host- and product-intrinsic factors related to resistance in oncology may apply to patients with lupus in some cases.33,35 Clonal haematopoiesis, a host-intrinsic factor that may be relevant for both safety (cytokine release syndrome and secondary malignancies) and response to CAR T-cell therapy in oncology, may also be an emerging concern for patients with lupus given its prevalence in SLE.36,38 As these mechanisms of remission, relapse, toxicity and resistance in lupus are elucidated, standardised monitoring strategies will need to be developed to anticipate or detect early loss of remission or long-term safety signals.

In the United States, patients who receive a treatment involving gene transfer are required to be monitored for up to 15 years for delayed adverse events (and lifelong for new malignancies).39 Pre-existing clonal haematopoiesis has emerged as a potential risk factor for secondary cancer after CAR T-cell therapy in oncology patients and, given its common occurrence in both oncology and autoimmune patients, is an emerging concern that may indicate risk for secondary cancer after CAR T-cell therapy, impacting product safety. Although this responsibility for safety monitoring lies with sponsors, responsibilities regarding monitoring longer-term efficacy, fertility and other outcomes in SLE are not yet defined by regulatory authorities. Long-term monitoring poses logistical challenges for patients, study sites and sponsors. When possible, long-term follow-up activities should minimise the need for patients to travel to their original study treatment facility. The monitoring obligations of sponsors will realistically have a regulator-defined end date, and the field should consider additional coordinated efforts such as registries or long-term follow-up studies, which could be spearheaded by professional societies, governmental agencies and/or point-of-care providers.

Conditioning therapy

In the oncology setting, the goal of lymphodepleting chemotherapy (conditioning) is to deplete existing patient lymphocytes in order to create a cytokine milieu that favours engraftment and expansion of CAR T-cells.40 Extensive pre-clinical and clinical observations suggest that CYC and fludarabine act in a complementary manner when administered sequentially in the days prior to CAR T-cell infusion. CYC produces a rapid, broad depletion of host lymphocytes, thereby creating immediate immunological ‘space,’ whereas fludarabine deepens and prolongs this lymphopenic window because of its prolonged intracellular half-life.41 42 Additionally, CYC and fludarabine synergistically induce homeostatic cytokines, most prominently interleukin-7 and interleukin-15, delivering survival and proliferation signals that drive CAR T-cell expansion and persistence that have been previously correlated with efficacy in haematological malignancies. While conditioning with CYC and fludarabine is essential in oncology, it is unclear whether it is also necessary in the SLE setting and how much it may contribute to overall effectiveness. It is also currently not known how different approaches to conditioning will be applicable to different types of cell therapies being studied in SLE (eg, autologous vs allogeneic). Conditioning, whether in cancer or SLE, does not come without risks and patient concerns, including those around severe cytopenias, infection and fertility effects. For that reason, the working group is supportive of exploring reduced, removed or alternative (eg, bendamustine) conditioning strategies in SLE.

The impact of conditioning regimens on longer-term, durable treatment-free remissions in SLE is difficult to assess due to the long follow-up time and large patient numbers required. Comparing shorter term clinical efficacy can initially guide the development of an optimised regimen. Additionally, standardisation of assessment time points for metrics like peripheral blood leucocyte phenotypes, cytokine levels pre-cell infusion and CAR T-cell expansion will improve the ability to compare different conditioning strategies across trials. Currently, data on omitting or reducing the dose of conditioning therapy in SLE and other autoimmune diseases prior to autologous or allogenic cell therapy are limited to a handful of very early-stage case reports.343,45

Regulatory authorities face challenges in determining what aspects of the CAR T-cell experience in oncology can translate to non-malignant conditions (eg, dose, conditioning regimen and severe adverse effect profile). The benefit–risk profile of different doses and conditioning regimens may be different in oncology compared with autoimmune diseases like SLE. Additionally, it is unclear how conditioning regimens in one autoimmune condition might translate to other autoimmune conditions. The field is in its early stages, and regulatory authorities are understandably focused on safety. Early-phase studies are being evaluated within the context of ensuring a reasonable benefit–risk profile, but specific considerations will evolve over time as more data are generated. The working group discussed that alternative conditioning regimens can be tested by providing appropriate justification to regulatory bodies and may be done in smaller numbers of patients within a broader trial to enable comparisons of safety and efficacy profiles.

Conditioning therapy and fertility

The potential impact of CAR T-cell therapy treatment (including conditioning therapy) on fertility in patients with SLE needs to be explored and better understood, as it will influence treatment choices made by individuals of childbearing potential. This is particularly important because 90% or more of patients with SLE are women, and the disease is known to disproportionately affect reproductive-age women.46 The working group discussed that many patients with SLE may be reluctant to take conditioning CYC due to fertility concerns.

The traditional conditioning regimen in oncology uses a lower dose of CYC (generally around 900–1500 mg/m2 total cumulative dose) than the cumulative dose given in course of the Euro-Lupus regimen (3000 mg), and data are available on the reduced effects of that regimen on fertility and ovarian reserve.40 47 Additionally, based on the oncology literature, high risk of premature ovarian failure has been associated with total cumulative doses of >7500 mg/m2 CYC, with low risk of premature ovarian failure when the cumulative dose is <4000–5000 mg/m2. This risk is further mitigated in women younger in age, with substantially increased risk occurring in women over the age of 30.48,50 Thus, if a patient has not had prior CYC exposure, their risk of gonadal toxicity is low. However, many patients may already have been exposed to CYC in the past for the treatment of SLE or LN and the dose used for conditioning therapy prior to CAR T-cell administration may contribute to an increased cumulative exposure. The impact on fertility has been difficult to study in CAR T-cell therapy recipients with malignancies due to limited available data and needs to be addressed prospectively.48 The Lupus ABC working group agreed that there is a need for evidence-based guidelines and best practices in this regard for SLE. Investigators should be encouraged to discuss fertility preservation options with patients of childbearing age and include risk in the informed consent document. Alternative conditioning regimens to the traditional fludarabine (Influenza)/CYC (such as bendamustine or bendamustine+Influenza) have been tested in oncology and may provide additional options for patients with lupus with CYC-related fertility concerns.51

Measuring immune profile and response

Immune monitoring in CAR T-cell therapy typically encompasses the measurement of CAR T-cell expansion and persistence, monitoring of the depletion of target cells (B-cells), immunoglobulin levels and reconstitution of B- and T-cells over time. In general, the SLE field can borrow CAR T-cell monitoring timepoints, metrics and techniques from oncology, though some characteristics may be different for patients with SLE . For example, it has been reported that CAR T-cells have a relatively lower magnitude of expansion followed by a reduced persistence profile than in malignancy trials, but it is not known how this may correlate with efficacy or how consistent this is across patients with SLE, trials and cell therapy products.3

In addition to common monitoring instruments of CAR T-cell therapy in malignancies and autoimmune diseases (CAR T-cell counts, B-cell counts and immunoglobulin levels), there are several specific monitoring approaches (eg, autoantibody levels, complement factors and inflammation markers) that may be unique to SLE and other autoimmune conditions compared with malignancy trials. As a primary objective, the field needs a better understanding of the mechanism of action of SLE remissions and ‘immune reset,’ as well as discovery efforts for a potential surrogate efficacy biomarker or endpoint. Such immune monitoring would ideally distinguish between complete responders, partial responders and non-responders.

While the term ‘immune reset’ has not been uniformly applied and remains to be more clearly defined based on emerging data and practical considerations,52 several aspects of this reprogramming have been described: (i) CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy, but not CD20-directed monoclonal antibody-based B-cell depletion, not only depletes B-cells from lymph nodes and other organs but also eliminates germinal centres from the lymph nodes, leading to abrogation of follicular dendritic cells and T-follicular helper cells.20 (ii) B-cells re-emerging after CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy are naive, non-class-switched cells with a broad non-clonal VJ-rearrangement resembling a B-cell compartment with no memory B-cells and also deprived of disease-associated activated memory B-cells (also known as double negative B-cells type 2, DN2 or age-associated B-cells).3 53 Finally, (iii) the T-cell compartment after CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy has been shown to exhibit a down-regulated type-I interferon signature, indicating that B-cell targeting by CAR T-cells has profound effects on the T-cell activation state, presumably by the abrogation of B-cell-mediated antigen presentation function.54

Immune monitoring efforts should focus on these key aspects of reprogramming to further elucidate how CAR T-cell therapy might achieve disease modification in lupus. Immune monitoring may also help clarify the impact of CAR T-cell therapy on both systemic and organ-specific immune cell subsets and activation and whether re-treatment or treatment against a different target may be beneficial. Research-focused immune assessments may include monitoring for antibodies against CAR, other common SLE autoantibody levels, vaccine antibody titres and responses post-therapy (especially for B-cell targeted therapies), immune cell phenotypes (memory vs naive B-cells) before and after CAR T-cell therapy and TCR/B-cell receptor (BCR) repertoire of repopulating cells.

While early SLE trials have included lymph node biopsies to confirm the degree of B-cell depletion in tissues, this has not yet been connected causally to treatment-free remissions. In LN, repeat kidney biopsies post-CAR T-cell therapy may yield valuable scientific information on this connection, especially in the earlier phases of clinical trials. However, as CAR T-cell therapies move toward approval in SLE, repeat kidney biopsies after therapy would only be clinically indicated in non-responders. In addition, we note that the field would benefit from identifying and validating minimally invasive biomarkers that could eventually substitute for biopsies.

When designing an immune monitoring plan, investigators should balance research desires with the burden on patients and their families. Patients may consider risk, benefits and the toll on themselves and caregivers. Trials should be designed in ways that minimise the burden on patients who may have family, work or other obligations to balance.

Role of pre-clinical models

Given the many outstanding mechanistic questions for understanding CAR T-cell therapy effects in SLE, the working group considered the potential role for and limitations of pre-clinical animal models in studying cell therapies for human SLE. T-cells in the commonly used MRL/lpr mouse model lack Fas receptor and therefore have defects in apoptosis, making it difficult to study CAR T-cell persistence.55 Other SLE models have multiple immune abnormalities that lead to SLE phenotypes but may also impact CAR T-cell product quality and characteristics. Understanding these limitations is paramount to the appropriate use of the model and interpretation of the study results. Furthermore, immunocompetent models of SLE necessitate the generation of murine, rather than human CAR T-cells, which requires different binding and structural CAR domains, and antigen expression on murine target cells may differ from human B-cells. Murine CAR T-cells also require different manufacturing methods in regard to retroviral vector and growth media and may demonstrate different expansion and persistence characteristics from their human-derived analogues. These challenges complicate the translational relevance of syngeneic CAR T-cell therapy studies to human products and systems.

A critical need for the field is the development of humanised mouse models of SLE that can better recapitulate human disease and allow for the evaluation of human CAR T-cell therapies against human target cells. Adoptive transfer of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with SLE in immunodeficient mice induces proteinuria and kidney IgG deposition, but the preclinical utility of the model is limited by the large donor blood volumes required and lack of autoantibody induction, a hallmark of SLE pathology.56 Pristane injection of immunodeficient mice reconstituted with human haematopoietic stem cells induces antinuclear antibodies, glomerulonephritis, pulmonary serositis and plasmablast expansion in blood and tissues; however, the model lacks serum type I interferon induction, human myeloid subsets and the full distribution of B-cell subsets that are central to SLE pathogenesis.57 Other animal models (porcine, canine, non-human primate vs murine models) may have increased translational relevance for human disease and allow for improved prediction of both safety and efficacy in human patients. Funders should consider investing more in research on comparative immunology of these systems to better understand their usefulness in autoimmunity and immunotherapy. In the meantime, pre-clinical validation may be substantiated by demonstrating pharmacologic effects against the intended target cells in primary SLE patient samples, combined with in vitro toxicology studies and judicious use of in vivo models for specific pharmacological or toxicological evaluations.

Conclusion

The introduction of CAR T-cell therapy brings an opportunity to make dramatic advances for patients with lupus. Advancing CAR T-cell therapy to patients with SLE will require continued input and ongoing collaborations from multiple partners across academic/clinical research, industry, regulators and individuals with SLE.
