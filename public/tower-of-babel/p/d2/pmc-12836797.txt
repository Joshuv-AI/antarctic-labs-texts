Surrogate to predict overall survival in patients with BRAF V600E-mutant colorectal cancer treated with BRAF inhibitor combinations
Authors: J Ros, V Navarro, G Villacampa, I Baraibar, F Salvà, M Rodriguez, C Vaghi, A Garcia, A Alcaraz, J Tabernero, E Élez, R Dienstmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe BRAF V600E mutation, found in up to 12% of patients with metastatic colorectal cancer, is associated with aggressive disease and poor response to standard chemotherapy. However, the advent of BRAF inhibitors has led to improved clinical outcomes and survival. While surrogate endpoints for predicting overall survival (OS) have been extensively studied in the overall colorectal cancer population treated with chemotherapy, their applicability in patients with BRAF V600E-mutant colorectal cancer receiving either BRAF inhibitor combinations or conventional chemotherapy remains unclear, and needs to be better elucidated. The aim of the study was to evaluate surrogate endpoints to predict OS in patients with BRAF V600E-mutant colorectal cancer treated with either BRAF inhibitor combinations or chemotherapy.Materials and methodsA systematic review was carried out to identify clinical trials or real-world cohorts evaluating patients with BRAF-mutant colorectal cancer treated either with chemotherapy or BRAF inhibitor combinations. A control cohort of melanoma patients treated with BRAF inhibitors in a phase III randomized trial was included. Adjusted R2 (R2adj) values were calculated to quantify the association between surrogate endpoints and median OS.ResultsOverall, a total of 5227 patients included in 29 cohorts were analyzed. Among patients with colorectal cancer treated with chemotherapy, overall response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) showed a high correlation with OS (R2adj > 0.90). Among patients treated with targeted therapy, progression-free survival (PFS) showed the highest correlation with OS (R2adj = 0.90). In the melanoma cohort, PFS was strongly associated with OS (R2adj = 0.92).ConclusionsIn BRAF-mutant colorectal cancer, standard surrogate endpoints for chemotherapy-based treatments accurately predict OS; however, when patients are treated with targeted therapies, both ORR and PFS have proven to be reliable predictors of survival.Key words: surrogate markers, BRAF mutation, colorectal cancer, targeted therapy, BRAF inhibitor

Introduction

The BRAF V600E mutation is a biomarker of aggressive disease, poor response to standard chemotherapy, and short overall survival (OS) in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC).1 Notwithstanding, the development of BRAF inhibitors combined with epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitor with or without a third mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway-blocking drug such as MEK, ERK, or PI3CA inhibitors, chemotherapy, or immune checkpoint inhibitors has improved clinical outcomes.2, 3, 4, 5 However, due to the low prevalence of the mutation, the vast majority of these trials are phase I or phase II clinical trials, and only two phase III trials evaluating the clinical activity of BRAF inhibitors have been developed. In the refractory setting, the phase III BEACON trial (NCT02928224) demonstrated the superiority of encorafenib–cetuximab ± binimetinib over irinotecan-based chemotherapy. The trial was not designed to compare encorafenib–cetuximab (hereafter referred to as doublet) versus encorafenib–cetuximab–binimetinib (hereafter referred to as triplet). In this trial, despite the higher overall response rate (ORR) achieved with the addition of the MEK inhibitor binimetinib compared with the doublet (27% versus 20%), there were no differences in either progression-free survival (PFS) (4.5 and 4.3 months, respectively) or OS (9.3 months in both experimental arms), thus making it difficult to extrapolate surrogates for OS.2 On the other hand, the BREAKWATER trial (NCT02928224) compared encorafenib–cetuximab–FOLFOX versus chemotherapy in the first line. The investigational arm demonstrated higher ORR (60.9% versus 40%), median PFS (mPFS) (12.8 versus 7.1 months), and median OS (mOS) (30.3 versus 15.1 months).6

Although ORR and PFS have extensively been evaluated as an OS surrogate endpoint in the overall CRC patient population, the validation of these surrogates for OS needs to be better portrayed in the BRAF-mutated subgroup.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Besides, considering the discrepancy between ORR and OS observed in the BEACON trial between the doublet and the triplet,2 surrogates for OS need to be better defined. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the association of ORR, disease control rate (DCR), and PFS with OS among patients with BRAF-mutated CRC treated with either chemotherapy or targeted therapy.

Considering the poor prognosis associated with this specific CRC subtype, 4-month PFS (4mPFS) in percentage has also been evaluated as a potential surrogate for OS. However, since there are only two randomized phase III trials in CRC and acknowledging the underlying biological differences, to gain a more accurate and wide perspective of the potential results, we included phase III clinical trials evaluating the clinical efficacy of BRAF inhibitor combinations in melanoma, in which the prevalence of the BRAF mutation is 50%, and several phase III trials have already been published.

Materials and methods

Selection criteria, search strategy, study identification, and data extraction

The literature search included three databases: PubMed, Embase, and Scopus to identify phase I-III clinical trials or real-world cohorts published from the inception of each database to 30 June 2025. In cases where multiple publications were available, the most recent publication was included. Online information from the European Society of Medical Oncology congress, the American Society of Clinical Oncology annual meeting, or other cancer-specific congresses was used to identify unpublished trials or unpublished patient cohorts (see Supplementary Figure S1A and B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100225, for the whole search information). The systematic review was conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis.15

To be included in the systematic review, studies had to satisfy the following criteria: (i) included data with BRAF-mutant CRC, (ii) treated with BRAF inhibitor combinations or chemotherapy, and (iii) clinical outcome information available. Furthermore, another search with patients with metastatic melanoma treated with BRAF inhibitors was also included as a control arm, with the following criteria: (i) included data with BRAF-mutant melanoma, (ii) treated with BRAF inhibitor combinations in a phase III trial, and (iii) clinical outcome information available. Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2025.100225, includes information on how trials/cohorts have been classified.

Endpoint definitions

OS was defined as the time from randomization (in the case of patients enrolled in clinical trials) or treatment start date (in the case of real-world cohorts) to death from any cause. PFS was defined as the time from randomization to progressive disease or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Complete response (CR) was defined as the proportion of patients with complete response by RECIST v1.1 as the best response. ORR was defined as the percentage of patients with confirmed CR plus partial response (PR) by RECIST v1.1. DCR was the percentage of the combination of CR, PR, and stable disease by RECIST v1.1. The 4mPFS was determined based on the survival percentage at 4 months from the time of randomization. DigitizeIt software (Braunschweig, Germany) was employed to analyze Kaplan–Meier curves and extract the precise survival percentage at the 4-month mark.

Statistical analysis

We conducted an arm-level analysis to predict the accuracy of endpoints to predict mOS. These analyses were carried out within the following categories: (i) melanoma treatment arms, (ii) mCRC all treatment arms, (iii) mCRC chemotherapy arms, and (iv) mCRC targeted therapy arms. Within the mCRC targeted therapy arms, different subgroups were analyzed. Four different surrogate endpoints were evaluated: percentage of ORR, mPFS, percentage of DCR, and percentage of 4mPFS. To assess the strength of the association between surrogate endpoints and mOS, we fitted linear regression models while adjusting for the study sample size as a weighting factor. Adjusted R2 (R2adj) values were calculated to quantify the extent to which predictors explained the total variation in OS (a higher R2adj, closer to 1, indicates a stronger association). No imputation was carried out. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software version 4.2.2.

Results

The literature research identified 29 cohorts of patients (24 CRC cohorts and 5 melanoma trials) that met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. The selected studies included a total of 5227 patients (2691 patients with mCRC and 2536 patients with melanoma). Melanoma

Overall, 2536 patients with melanoma treated with BRAF inhibitors ± MEK inhibitors were included. Patients were evaluated in five different phase III randomized clinical trials in the first-line setting. Mean ORR and DCR were 55% and 87%, respectively. mPFS was between 6 and 15 months, and mOS was between 14 and 34 months. In the pooled melanoma cohort, mPFS showed the highest association for mOS (R2adj = 0.92), whereas ORR showed the lowest association for mOS (R2adj = 0.49). Both 4mPFS and DCR had intermediate association with mOS (R2adj = 0.79 and 0.71, respectively). Colorectal cancer

In the mCRC cohort, 2691 patients were included from 20 clinical trials and 4 real-world reported cohorts. Conversely to that observed in the melanoma cohort, only 35% of the patients (n = 1191) received treatment in the first-line setting, whereas the vast majority of patients were included in the refractory setting. Regarding treatments, 876 patients were treated with chemotherapy, whereas 1784 patients received BRAF inhibitor-based combinations. Overall, when considering the whole cohort regardless of treatment type, ORR and mPFS showed the highest association with mOS (R2adj = 0.75 and 0.88, respectively).

However, when analyzed by treatment type, among the 876 patients with BRAF-mutated mCRC treated with chemotherapy, DCR and PFS stand as the most reliable surrogates for mOS followed by 4mPFS (R2adj = 0.96, 0.91, and 0.90, respectively). Similarly, among the 1815 patients who received BRAF inhibitor-based combinations, PFS showed strong correlation with mOS (R2adj = 0.90) followed by ORR (R2adj = 0.83), whereas both 4mPFS and DCR failed to demonstrate OS surrogacy (R2adj = 0.45 and 0.32, respectively). Besides, we evaluated separately those patients who received encorafenib-containing treatments, as it is the BRAF inhibitor with both Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) approval. Among patients treated with encorafenib-containing regimens, PFS and ORR showed better association (R2adj = 0.91 and 0.89, respectively), while 4mPFS and DCR showed poor association with mOS (R2adj = 0.52 and 0.45, respectively). Then, we looked specifically for each encorafenib-based combination, and among patients who received the doublet (encorafenib–cetuximab), ORR and 4mPFS demonstrated strong correlation with OS (R2adj = 0.94 and 0.85, respectively) followed by PFS (R2adj = 0.82). DCR was not associated with OS (R2adj = 0.39). Furthermore, among those patients who received the triplet including binimetinib, ORR showed high accuracy to predict OS, followed by 4mPFS and mPFS (R2adj = 0.83, 0.82, and 0.78 respectively). When combining the doublet with a third partner other than binimetinib (either programmed cell death protein 1 inhibitor or PI3K inhibitor), ORR and DCR did not show OS surrogacy, whereas 4mPFS showed the strongest association with mOS (R2adj = 0.91). Finally, among patients treated with BRAF inhibitor combinations other than encorafenib (vemurafenib or dabrafenib), PFS showed mild association with OS. Discussion

Despite recent therapeutic advances, and the development of prognostic and predictive biomarkers, OS remains poor in BRAF-mutant mCRC.3,6,16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Thus, not only the development of novel biomarkers or new drugs that boost the antitumor effect is needed but also a more accurate and reliable measurement of clinical benefit. In advanced CRC treated with chemotherapy, current evidence indicates that mPFS is the strongest surrogate for mOS, followed by ORR.9,10 Both ORR and mPFS are appropriate surrogate endpoints for mOS for systemic therapy in mCRC but their relative value varies notably with treatment line, type of therapy, and study size.12 Therefore, a better understanding of these OS surrogate endpoints in clinical trials could help to improve trial designs and performance.12

In this scenario, the development of reliable and accurate surrogate endpoints for OS remains paramount. Often, OS may be too complex to be the primary endpoint of a clinical trial, with higher cost related to study size and long follow-up compared with other endpoints such as ORR or PFS.7 Unlike what is observed in BRAF-mutated melanoma, where several phase III randomized trials affirm the robustness of mPFS as surrogate for mOS, in BRAF-mutant mCRC this association still needs to be better elucidated. This suggests that, despite harboring the same mutation, the histological context may influence sensitivity to BRAF inhibitors. In the case of BRAF CRC, the low prevalence of the mutation, the aggressiveness of the disease, and the limited phase III data increase complexity and make it difficult to achieve enough robustness to allow similar extrapolation.

Thus, we carried out a systematic review aiming to assess surrogates for OS among patients with BRAF-mutant CRC and included a control cohort of BRAF-mutant melanoma. In the melanoma cohort, 2536 patients from randomized, phase III trials were included, and PFS was clearly the most robust surrogate for OS. Likewise, among patients with BRAF CRC treated with chemotherapy, not only DCR and PFS showed high correlation with OS but also 4mPFS and ORR demonstrated accurate prediction of OS. On the other hand, among patients who received BRAF inhibitors in mCRC, surrogacy varied widely depending on the BRAF combination. Among patients who received the doublet with encorafenib–cetuximab, currently approved by both the FDA and the EMA, ORR was an accurate surrogate for OS, followed by 4mPFS (R2adj = 0.94 and 0.85, respectively). In contrast, among patients treated with the triplet which includes binimetinib, ORR and 4mPFS demonstrated stronger association with OS, but when other encorafenib–cetuximab-based combinations were evaluated, only 4mPFS showed association with mOS. In other BRAF inhibitor combinations, only PFS demonstrated some association with mOS.

Our analysis reveals that the level of correlation for PFS and ORR varied with treatment line, type of therapy, and trial size, which is in line with previous CRC publications evaluating surrogates for OS.7,15 Thus, the most accurate surrogate for OS in BRAF CRC seems to vary depending on treatment class, but the lack of phase III trials makes it difficult to clearly establish the optimal surrogate. In this scenario, final results of the BREAKWATER trial (NCT02928224) demonstrated the efficacy of combining encorafenib–cetuximab with chemotherapy. Indeed, the combination showed a statistically significant and clinically relevant improvement not only in terms of ORR and mPFS but also in OS, establishing a new standard of care for patients with BRAF V600E-mutated mCRC.6 This synergistic effect was based on preclinical research in tumor xenograft models, which identified that chemotherapy-treated xenografts exhibited up-regulation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and MAPK pathway activation, whereas encorafenib–cetuximab-treated models showed suppressed MAPK signaling. In contrast, when chemotherapy was combined with encorafenib–cetuximab, both EMT and MAPK signaling remained suppressed.21 Based on this rationale, the combination of both treatments seems to overcome the acquired resistance mechanisms that may arise when treating with only one of them, thereby justifying the differences in surrogate biomarkers between treatment with chemotherapy or BRAF inhibitors, compared with treatment with a combination of chemotherapy and BRAF inhibitors.

The major limitation of this work lies in the limited sample size of most of the cohorts analyzed and the treatment heterogeneity. Most of the studies are phase I-II non-randomized trials or real-world cohorts with high heterogeneity regarding BRAF inhibitor combinations. Trying to overcome such limitations, a systematic review was done, and, acknowledging limitations and biological differences with CRC, a control cohort using melanoma was included to portray the clinical activity of BRAF inhibitors in a tumor type with high BRAF mutation prevalence. Despite the obvious biological differences and sensitivity to BRAF inhibitors, this comparison helps to highlight how challenging the treatment of BRAF CRC still is, and that developing clinical trials for this specific CRC subgroup remains complex.

In conclusion, due to the treatment heterogeneity and modest sample size of the different cohorts/trials, there are still insufficient data to accurately establish the best surrogates for OS when treating BRAF-mutated CRC with targeted agents. However, when treating this subtype of patients with chemotherapy-based combinations, conventional surrogates such as PFS may be used. The best surrogate for OS in BRAF-mutant CRC treated with BRAF inhibitors has yet to be fully elucidated, but both ORR and PFS have proven to be reliable predictors of survival when treating with encorafenib-based combinations.
