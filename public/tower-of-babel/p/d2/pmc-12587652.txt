A standardized extract of Centella asiatica (ECa 233) alleviates brain injury and improves brain function in β-thalassemia mice with iron overload
Authors: Paranee Yatmark, Puntita Anutagerngkun, Somkiat Huaijantug, Mayuree H. Tantisira, Sukonthar Ngampramuan, Saovaros Svasti, Noppawan Phumala Morales
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIron overload plays a role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders by inducing oxidative injury in the brain. Centella asiatica (L.) Urb. It is a medicinal plant with neuroprotective effects that exhibits antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. A standardized extract of C. asiatica (ECa 233) has high concentrations of the bioactive compounds madecassoside and asiaticoside. It may have potential for use in iron overload conditions.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the effects of ECa 233 on iron-induced brain injury, memory deficits, and impaired locomotor activity in 12-month-old wild-type (WT) and β-thalassemia (β-globin knockout, BKO) mice with iron overload.MethodsWT and BKO mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with 100 mg of iron, followed by i.p. injection of 20 mg/kg body weight of ECa 233 for 10 days. Iron accumulation and injury in the brain were evaluated via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Prussian blue staining. The Morris water maze (MWM) and open field tests were performed to evaluate spatial learning and memory and locomotor activity, respectively.ResultsAging BKO mice resulted in spontaneous iron accumulation in the brain and poor performance in the MWM test. Iron overload significantly increased brain iron content and worsened brain injury, locomotor activity, and performance in the MWM test in both WT and BKO mice. Treatment with ECa 233 significantly decreased iron accumulation in the brain and reversed brain damage in WT and BKO mice to the same extent. In addition, ECa 233 improved iron-induced memory deficits in WT mice and locomotor activity in iron-overloaded WT and BKO mice.ConclusionThese results suggest that the beneficial effect of ECa 233 may be partially due to decreased iron accumulation in the brain. ECa 233 may be used as an adjuvant with an iron chelator to prevent iron-induced neurodegenerative complications in individuals with β-thalassemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05145-w.

Background

Centella asiatica (L.) Urb. (commonly known as Asian pennywort, gotu kola, mandukaparni, and bua-bok) is a medicinal plant widely used in China, India, Southeast Asia, and Africa. It has been used as a therapy for various conditions in many traditional medicine systems [1]. In Thailand, C. asiatica is listed in the Herbal Medicinal Products section of the National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM) for use in wound healing, scarring, and fever. C. asiatica contains various bioactive compounds, mainly pentacyclic triterpenoids, including madecassoside, asiaticoside, madecassic acid, and asiatic acid. Its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neurogenic, anxiolytic, and neuroprotective effects have been reported both in vitro and in vivo [2]. C. asiatica extract has potential uses in the prevention and treatment of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) because of its protective effect against amyloid β (Aβ)-induced cytotoxicity in glial cells [3] and its ability to improve hippocampus-dependent spatial memory in rats [4] and mitochondrial functions [5–6] and to ameliorate oxidative stress by activating the NRF2-regulated antioxidant response pathway [7–8].

Because the biological activities of C. asiatica are caused mainly by madecassoside and asiaticoside, a standardized extract of C. asiatica with a high content of these compounds, ECa 233, was developed. This extract has been shown to enhance memory retention and increase the expression of proteins related to hippocampal synaptic plasticity in normal mice that received 30 mg/kg ECa 233 for 30 days [9]. Protective effects against rotenone-induced motor deficits and dopaminergic neuronal death in a rat model of Parkinson’s disease were observed [10]. In addition to its therapeutic effects, ECa 233 as well as the extract of C. asiatica have been shown to be safe for use in rodents and humans [11–13].

Beta-thalassemia is a genetic hemoglobinopathy caused by defects in the β-globin gene. The fatal complications of β-thalassemia result from iron overload, which is caused by increased iron absorption in the gut secondary to ineffective erythropoiesis and chronic blood transfusion. With the development of effective iron chelation therapy, the life expectancy of patients with β-thalassemia has improved significantly [14–15]. As a consequence, neurodegenerative complications are a concern in thalassemia due to chronic iron toxicity in the brain.

Iron accumulation in the brain has been reported in numerous neurodegenerative diseases and inflammation of the central nervous system, such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and neuroferritinopathy [16–18]. Becerril-Ortega et al. [19] reported that iron overload accelerated the production of Aβ and cognitive impairment in a transgenic mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease. Recently, Li et al. [20] demonstrated the suppression of hippocampal neurogenesis in an iron overload mouse model. In β-thalassemia patients, iron accumulation has been detected in several brain regions, including the hippocampus, which is implicated in cognitive deficits and learning and memory impairment [21–24].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of ECa 233 on iron-induced brain injury, memory deficits, and impaired locomotor activity in iron-overloaded 12-month-old wild-type (WT) and β-thalassemia (β-globin knockout [BKO]) mice. Heterozygous BKO mice (muβth3/+) exhibit traits of β-thalassemia intermedia, including chronic anemia, splenomegaly, and spontaneous iron overload in the spleen, liver, kidney, and brain [25–26]. Hippocampus-dependent spatial memory was assessed via the Morris water maze (MWM) test, and locomotor activity was monitored via the open field test. The results of this study may elucidate the brain pathology in individuals with iron overload and the benefits of ECa 233 for the prevention and treatment of iron-induced brain damage in individuals with β-thalassemia.

Methods

Animals

Twelve-month-old male and female WT C57BL/6 mice and heterozygous BKO mice (muβth3/+) weighing 25–30 g were obtained from the Thalassemia Research Center, Institute of Molecular Biosciences, Mahidol University, Thailand. The standard diet and water were provided ad libitum. The temperature and humidity were maintained at 22 ± 2 °C and 55 ± 15%, respectively, and the animals were maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle. The protocol used in this study was approved by the Institute of Molecular Biosciences Animal Care and Use Committee (IMB-ACUC), Mahidol University (approval number IMB-ACUC-2020/014).

Experimental design

Twelve-month-old WT and BKO mice were divided into 3 groups: control (normal saline treatment), iron overload, and iron overload with ECa 233 treatment. ECa 233 was extracted from Centella asiatica (L.) Urb cultivated in Ayutthaya Province, central Thailand. ECa 233 was suspended in sterile normal saline at a concentration of 4 mg/ml. The mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with 20 mg/day iron dextran (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) for 5 days. Three days after the last dose, the iron-loaded mice were i.p. injected with 20 mg/kg/day of ECa 233 (lot number MRA0816001, containing 51% madecassoside and 38% asiaticoside; Siam Herbal Innovation Co., Ltd., Pathum Thani, Thailand) for 10 days. After the last dose, the mice were randomly selected for behavioral tests or histopathological studies. At the end of the experiment, all the mice were euthanized with an overdose i.p. injected with 50–60 mg/kg pentobarbital, and the brains were collected for determination of iron content, antioxidant status, and histopathological analysis. Iron content and antioxidant status in brain tissue

The whole brain was homogenized in 1 ml of 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4, with a bead mill homogenizer (HT lysing homogenizer, Ohaus, Parsippany, NJ). The nonheme iron content was determined via a modification of the ferrozine method described by Foy [27], and the details of the procedure were previously described by Yatmak et al. [28]. The absorbance of the ferrozine-iron complexes at 562 nm was measured with a microplate reader (Varioskan™ Flash, Thermo Scientific, Vantaa, Finland).

The homogenate was centrifuged at 9,200 × g for 10 min (Spectrafuge 24D, Labnet International, Inc., Woodbridge, NJ), and the supernatant was used for the determination of total glutathione (GSH) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity via the methods described by Kondo and Sawada [29] and Takahashi K [30], respectively.

Histological analysis

The brains were dissected and preserved for routine histology by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, 5% sucrose, and 150 mM PBS, pH 7.4. The fixed tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Serial coronal sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for general tissue pathological analysis and with Prussian blue for the detection of iron deposition via standard procedures [31]. Nuclear fast red solution was used to counterstain the tissue sections with Prussian blue. Stained tissue sections were examined under a Nikon inverted microscope (Eclipse Ts2R-FL/Ts2R), and images were acquired via NIS-Elements software.

To analyze Perls’ Prussian blue-stained samples, six nonoverlapping fields of the caudal diencephalon (cerebral cortex and hippocampus), choroid plexus, and cerebellar tissue were randomly selected and viewed at a magnification of 200×. The percentage of Prussian blue-positive areas relative to the total area of the tissue sections was calculated via ImageJ Ver.1.47i software.

To investigate the severity of pathological lesions in the cerebrum, hippocampus, and granular and parenchyma layers of the cerebellum, three nonoverlapping fields of H&E-stained tissue sections were viewed at 400× magnification. Neuropathological changes in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum were semiquantitatively evaluated via histopathology scores modified from Edler et al. [32] and Hyman et al. [33] (Table 1).

Open field test

The open field test was carried out on the day before the MWM test. The open field experiment system consisted of an open field activity cage (Plexiglas, size 50 × 50 × 50 cm) with a VDO tracking system placed on the top of the cage. The apparatus was placed in a light- and sound-attenuated room, and the movement of the mice was monitored continuously for 5 min. The total distance traveled and velocity were recorded [34–35].

MWM test

The spatial learning and memory functions of WT and BKO mice were analyzed with the MWM test as described previously [36]. The MWM apparatus consisted of a circular pool of white water (140 cm in diameter and 60 cm in depth) and a platform (5 cm in diameter and 40 cm in height). The temperature of the water in the pool was maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. In the visible platform test (day 1), the platform protruded 1 cm above the water surface, and the mice were randomly placed at each of the four starting positions facing the pool wall and allowed to swim for 60 s per training session. If the mice could not find the visible platform within 60 s, they were guided and left on the platform for 10 s.

In the hidden platform test, the platform was submerged 1 cm below the water surface in the middle of the southwest quadrant of the pool. The acquisition training trials were conducted four times per day for 4 consecutive days (days 2–5), and the cutoff swimming time was 60 s. The time for the mouse to find the platform (latency to target) and the distance to the platform in each trial were monitored to determine spatial learning and memory performance.

The probe trial was performed during the next session of the MWM task with the platform removed. The percentage of time spent locating the original platform area (time in the zone) was analyzed to determine the memory retention of the mice. All the data were analyzed by observers who were blinded to the groups. The movement of the animals was analyzed via a SMART video tracking system, v3.0 (Panlab Harvard Apparatus, Barcelona, Spain).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics software version 27. The data are expressed as the means ± SDs unless otherwise indicated. Comparisons between the treatments were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Tukey test as a post hoc test, except for histopathological scores, which were analyzed by the Kruskal‒Wallis H test. A p value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

The brains of aging BKO mice presented spontaneous iron accumulation, whereas iron accumulation was not detected in the brains of WT mice. Iron loading at 100 mg significantly increased the iron content to the same extent in the brains of both the WT and BKO mice. Treatment with ECa 233 significantly reduced the increase in iron deposits in both WT and BKO mice (Table 2).

Table 2 shows the GSH content and GPx activity in the brains of the WT and BKO mice. The GSH content did not significantly differ between the control WT and BKO mice. Iron loading and ECa 233 treatment did not affect the GSH content in WT mice. However, a significant increase in the GSH content was observed in the brains of BKO mice after iron loading, and the GSH content further increased after treatment with ECa 233.

On the other hand, GPx activity tended to decrease in the brains of BKO mice compared with those of WT mice. Iron loading tended to alleviate GPx activity in BKO mice, but this effect was not significant. Treatment with ECa 233 had no effect on GPx activity in iron-overloaded WT or BKO mice.

H&E staining

Representative images of H&E-stained images of the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum of WT and BKO mice are shown in Fig. 3. In the hippocampus of iron-treated mice, an increase in the number of dark neurons, cell shrinkage, cell necrosis, and vacuolization were observed in the CA1 region of the hippocampus (Fig. 3, 2a and 5a). Iron overload in the cerebral cortex caused progressive brain damage in the WT and BKO mice (Fig. 3, 2b and 5b). Many degenerated neurons, necrotic cells, and vacuolization were distinctly observed at Mop2/3 in the primary motor areas and/or at SSp-tr2/3 in the primary somatosensory areas. Multiple neovascularizations formed in the cerebral parenchyma as well as in the hippocampus. As shown in Table 3, relatively high levels of cerebral and hippocampal damage were detected in iron-overloaded WT and BKO mice. Brown pigment and moderate neurodegeneration in these locations slightly decreased after treatment with ECa 233. In the cerebellum, mice with iron overload presented defects in both the granular layer and the white matter (Fig. 3, 2c and 5c), including an increase in neovascularization, glial cells, and degenerated Purkinje neurons with pericellular space. In iron-overloaded WT and BKO mice, hemosiderin pigments also appeared along the lining of the choroid plexus (not shown). Compared with untreated WT and BKO mice, iron-overloaded WT and BKO mice treated with ECa 233 presented a reduction in brown pigmentation and a minor reversal of cerebellar degenerative lesions (Fig. 3, 3c and 6c). However, vacuoles and numerous glial cells were still present in the cerebellar tissues. Treatment with ECa 233 alone did not cause significant changes in brain morphology in either WT or BKO mice (Supplemental Figure S1 and S2).

Locomotor activity

The total distance traveled and velocity of the BKO mice were not significantly different from those of the WT mice. Iron overload caused both WT and BKO mice to travel shorter distances and significantly decreased their velocity, indicating locomotor activity impairment in iron-overloaded mice. Treatment with ECa 233 slightly improved locomotor activity, as demonstrated by an increase in locomotor velocity in iron-overloaded mice. However, no significant difference was observed (Table 4).

On day 1, there was no significant difference in latency or distance to the target among the WT treatment groups (Fig. 4c and d). However, a significantly shorter latency and distance to the target were observed in iron-overloaded BKO mice treated with ECa 233 (Fig. 4e and f).

Discussion

Iron overload has been implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders because of its ability to generate cytotoxic reactive radicals, leading to the apoptosis and ferroptosis of neurons and neuroglial cell damage. Our study demonstrated that iron accumulation in the brains of mice led to brain tissue injury, which resulted in impaired locomotor activity and a decline in learning and memory ability. Treatment with ECa 233 decreased brain injury and improved brain function at least partially through a reduction in iron accumulation in the brain.

Twelve-month-old mice, which are equivalent to 42.5-year-old humans [37], were investigated in this study. This age group is considered middle-aged in healthy individuals, but 50% of patients who do not receive adequate blood transfusion and chelation therapy survive to less than 35 years of age [14–15]. Aging BKO mice develop spontaneous iron accumulation in the brain, resulting in brain dysfunction, as demonstrated by decreased performance in the MWM and locomotor activity tests. Consistent with the findings of Pholngam et al. [38], 22-month-old BKO mice, which are equivalent to approximately 60-year-old humans, spontaneously develop cognitive decline, as demonstrated by the MWM test, which was correlated with a decreased number of living neurons in the CA3 region of the hippocampus. Although neuronal damage in the primary motor cortex area was not observed, a reduction in locomotor activity was noted.

However, BKO mice do not develop iron overload to the same degree as humans do because of differences in iron metabolism between species. To study iron overload in an animal model, i.p. administration of iron supplements such as iron dextran was used. The increase in serum iron and organ iron accumulation was similar to that in transfusion-dependent thalassemia patients [39]. With this method, the degree and severity of iron overload are adjustable by varying the dose and period of iron loading. In this study, a total of 100 mg of loaded iron, which is considered moderate iron overload, caused a significant increase in brain iron content and progressive brain damage. Therefore, we postulated that the iron overload BKO model could imitate the neurodegenerative conditions of aging thalassemia patients. Jamrus et al. [40] demonstrated that heavy iron overload (200 mg of iron) induced the early development of cognitive impairment in 3-month-old BKO mice. MWM performance was moderately correlated with the number of living neurons in the CA region of the hippocampus and the iron content in the liver and spleen.

As mentioned earlier, iron toxicity plays a crucial role in neurotoxicity in thalassemia. Iron chelation therapy with deferiprone, which can cross the blood‒brain barrier, has been shown to alleviate neurotoxicity in iron-overloaded mice [41]. Recently, the role of iron and iron chelation therapy in the progression of Alzheimer’s disease and cognitive function has been reviewed [42]. Therefore, exploring other compounds that can reduce the iron content in the brain is an interesting area of research.

In this study, treatment with ECa 233 reduced the iron content in the brain. We hypothesized that the bioactive compounds in ECa 233 may have iron-chelating activity. To our knowledge, the effects of madecassoside and asiaticoside on iron chelation and iron metabolism have not been reported. Madecassoside and asiaticoside have cyclic structures that are rich in hydroxyl groups and may be able to chelate with iron. Because these bioactive compounds can cross the blood‒brain barrier [43–45], they may bind to and mobilize redox-active iron from brain tissue.

The open field test is commonly used to measure locomotor activity and anxiety-related emotional behavior [34]. Iron overload reduces movement ability, possibly due to muscle and motor defects. Iron overload may also induce anxiety-like behavior, as suggested by the fact that the mice spent almost 100% of their time in the peripheral zone. Treatment of iron-overloaded BKO mice with ECa 233 increased the amount of time spent exploring the inner zone to a similar extent to that of control WT mice (data not shown). A previous study by Wanasuntronwong et al. [46–47] demonstrated the anxiolytic effect of ECa 233 in mice subjected to chronic immobilization stress. This finding may indicate that ECa 233 has anxiolytic effects in cases of iron overload. However, further experiments with specific pharmacological tools, such as anxiolytics, are needed to determine the effects of iron overload on anxiety.

The hippocampus is involved in learning and memory, and degeneration of this region has been observed in patients with Alzheimer’s disease [48]. In this study, iron accumulation and neuronal damage were observed in the hippocampus of iron-overloaded mice. Analysis of the MWM test results revealed that iron overload tended to decrease performance and alter the learning ability of the mice. Notably, in animal models or patients with neurodegenerative diseases, iron is detected in specific brain regions, whereas thalassemia is associated with systemic iron overload. Therefore, learning and memory impairment due to iron-induced neuronal damage or noncognitive impairment may be confounders in this setting. In fact, systemic iron overload causes multiple organ damage and alterations in muscle strength [49], hormones, and mood, which may affect MWM performance. In addition, aging BKO mice exhibited poorer performance in the MWM test than did WT mice. This result may be explained by chronic neuronal damage due to hypoxia secondary to anemia. The difference observed on day 1 of the visible platform trial may be associated with visual and motor defects in aging BKO mice [50].

Treatment with ECa 233 revealed a promising trend toward the deleterious effects of iron overload, which may be explained by the multiple pharmacological activities of ECa 233. A previous study demonstrated that ECa 233 significantly enhanced memory retention and hippocampal long-term potentiation in rats via a mechanism related to an increase in hippocampal synaptic plasticity and plasticity-related proteins [9]. Moreover, the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and iron chelation activities of ECa 233 may improve iron-induced multiorgan dysfunction in iron-overloaded mice.

The benefits of C. asiatica extract have been demonstrated in patients with cognitive impairment. For example, Farhana et al. [51] reported a statistically significant improvement in the memory domain of patients with vascular cognitive impairment after taking capsules of C. asiatica extract (1000 or 750 mg) every day for 6 weeks. To our knowledge, there have been no studies on the effect of ECa 233 on iron overload. Notably, the safety profiles of ECa 233 have been reported in nonthalassemia patients and may not be applicable to all thalassemia patients, particularly those who have liver and kidney dysfunctions that result in variations in xenobiotic biotransformation and excretion.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrated that treatment with ECa 233 significantly decreased the iron content in the brain and improved locomotor activity in iron-overloaded WT and BKO mice. The iron-chelating activity of the active compounds in ECa 233 may be responsible for its benefits. The chelating activity and mechanism related to iron metabolism need to be elucidated. Although acute toxicity of ECa 233 was not observed in this study, long-term studies are essential. Our findings suggest that ECa 233 may be used as an adjuvant with an iron chelator to prevent iron-induced neurodegenerative complications in individuals with β-thalassemia. However, the efficacy and safety profile need to be evaluated in patients.
